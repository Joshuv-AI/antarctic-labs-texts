Female membrane proteins regulate postmating ovulation in Drosophila melanogaster by ovulin-dependent and -independent pathways
Authors: Mengye Yang, Melissa A. White, Geoffrey D. Findlay, Ryan C. Vignogna, Jennifer Apger-McGlaughon, Nathan L. Clark, Jae Young Choi, J. Christopher Fromme, Mariana F. Wolfner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ovulation is an intricate process that is essential for reproductive success. In Drosophila melanogaster, ovulation increases after mating. This increase is initiated by the male seminal fluid protein ovulin and is executed by female pathways, including octopamine (OA) neuronal signaling. Despite OA signaling’s central role in ovulation regulation, the broader molecular landscape underlying female control of ovulation remains poorly understood. Here, using ovulin as a probe, we performed evolutionary rate covariation and AlphaFold-Multimer prediction screens to identify candidate female ovulation-regulating proteins. Ovulation assays performed on knockdowns or mutants of identified membrane-protein candidates revealed seven important female ovulation regulators: Lgr3, GabaβR1, SIFaR, mthl9, Smog, Cirl, and CG6067. Lgr3 and GabaβR1 function in an ovulin-dependent manner, while SIFaR and mthl9 regulate ovulation independently of ovulin. For proteins with known nervous system expression, we examined their requirement in OA neurons and their expression in female reproductive tract neurons. Tissue-specific knockdown revealed that Lgr3, GabaβR1, SIFaR, and CG6067 act in OA neurons to influence ovulation, highlighting OA neurons as a key signaling hub. Additionally, Lgr3, GabaβR1, SIFaR, Smog, and Cirl are expressed in OA neurons innervating the reproductive tract, suggesting a potential local function. Finally, we identified evidence of recurrent positive selection having acted on residues within Smog’s ligand binding region, which is interesting in light of ovulin’s rapid evolution. Together, these findings significantly expand our understanding of the molecular networks regulating ovulation following mating in Drosophila.

Ovulation is a complex reproductive process through which oocytes are released from the ovary into the oviduct. It can be spontaneous or induced (1). Spontaneous ovulation happens cyclically, while induced ovulation requires external triggers, such as mating. Some mammals, including humans, mice, and pigs, are spontaneous ovulators, whereas others, such as rabbits, cats, and camels, are induced ovulators. In certain insects such as Drosophila melanogaster, ovulation involves features of both spontaneous and induced ovulation (2).

Mammalian and Drosophila ovulation are regulated by hormonal and neuronal controls that show strong parallels (3–10). For example, follicle rupture, a key step in the ovulation process, relies on matrix metalloproteinases in both mammals and flies (11,12). Of particular importance here, in the case of induced ovulation, male seminal proteins transferred during mating stimulate ovulation in both evolutionary clades. In camelids, seminal plasma contains an ovulation-inducing factor (OIF), identified as the highly conserved protein β-NGF (13–15). In D. melanogaster, the male seminal fluid protein (Sfp) ovulin stimulates the release of mature oocytes from the ovaries in the initial hours following mating (16).

After its transfer to females, most ovulin remains in the female reproductive tract (FRT) for several hours where it undergoes sequential cleavage following mating, while a small amount enters the circulation (16–19). Full-length ovulin, as well as two different C-terminal pieces (CP3N and CP3C), are each sufficient to stimulate ovulation when ectopically expressed in unmated females (20). Ovulin persists in the mated female for only 4 to 6 h, and this transience likely explains why ovulin’s effects on reproduction are only detectable in the first 24 h following mating (18, 21). Females mated to males lacking ovulin release fewer mature oocytes into their lower reproductive tracts, with the largest effect occurring at 3 h after mating (16). This ovulation defect decreases the odds of finding an egg in the uterus and decreases egg laying by 8 to 18% during the first 24 h postmating (16,21). The subtlety of this effect on ovulation is likely the result of redundancy with other egg-production stimulating Sfps or other endogenous ovulation promoting pathways.

Previous studies have also uncovered female contributions to postmating ovulation regulation. Female octopamine (OA) signaling plays a pivotal role in this process, as the absence of OA or its receptors, Oamb and Octβ2R, leads to the retention of mature oocytes in the ovaries (7, 22–24). OA and its receptors have also been shown to stimulate contraction of the ovarian peritoneal sheath, as well as both contraction and relaxation of the oviduct muscles (25, 26). Notably, ovulin acts through female OA neuronal signaling to relax the oviduct musculature and facilitate ovulation (27, 28). Other physiological processes within females also influence ovulation, including secretions from the reproductive tract glands (29, 30), juvenile hormone intracellular signaling (9), and fat-to-ovary communication (3–33). These disparate clues are significant but nonetheless have not yet coalesced into a coherent picture of the broader molecular mechanisms governing ovulation in females.

Here, we first identified female proteins whose rates of evolution covary with that of ovulin, as evolutionary rate covariation (ERC) has proven to be a powerful approach for detecting proteins that function in the same process (34–36). Previously, we used this approach to identify new members of the same pathway as another Sfp, sex peptide, in Drosophila (36). In the present study to search for ovulation regulators, we focused on GPCRs because they are the largest family of transmembrane receptors, and because OA receptors of this class (Oamb and Octβ2R) are known to affect ovulation. SPR, the female receptor for sex peptide (37), is also a member of this receptor family. To extend our search beyond GPCRs, we complemented ERC with AlphaFold-Multimer screens (38, 39), predicting ovulin-interacting proteins across the entire transmembrane receptor repertoire, the FRT proteome, and the OA neuron proteome. We then used genetic techniques to determine the effects of candidate proteins on ovulation. In this way, we identified seven previously unknown female ovulation-regulating proteins (Lgr3, GabaβR1, SIFaR, mthl9, Smog, Cirl, and CG6067), four (Lgr3, GabaβR1, SIFaR, and CG6067) of which are required in OA neurons to impact ovulation. We further investigated the genetic interactions between these ovulation regulators and ovulin, revealing both ovulin-dependent and ovulin-independent pathways. Lgr3 and GabaβR1 function in the same pathway as ovulin to regulate ovulation, whereas SIFaR, along with its known ligand SIFa, and mthl9 operate independently of and thus in parallel with ovulin. For regulators with recognized nervous system expression, we showed that Lgr3, GabaβR1, SIFaR, Smog, and Cirl are present in OA neurons innervating the FRT, indicating a localized role in modulating ovulation. Finally, we explored the molecular evolution of these ovulation-regulating proteins and found positive selection acting on Smog, a result that could be consistent with interactions with rapidly evolving ligands. Altogether, this study reveals complex orchestration of ovulation by identifying critical female proteins and signaling pathways that govern ovulation.

Results

ERC and AlphaFold-Multimer Screens Identified Female Membrane Proteins Potentially Involved in Ovulation Regulation.

Two ERC (34–36) screens were performed to identify Drosophila GPCRs that coevolve with ovulin. ERC scores (ranging from −1 to 1) measure the correlation of amino acid substitution rates across phylogenetic branches between protein pairs, with higher scores suggesting greater potential for a functional relationship. Based on a positive ERC score (r-value > 0) and expression in the FRT and/or nervous system, tissues likely involved in ovulation regulation (7, 25, 40), we selected 11 genes as strong candidates for further study (Table 1). Most of these candidates (Fz4, CG15744, Rickets, Lgr3, SIFaR, GabaβR1, ETHR) exhibited egg-laying defects upon knockdown at 24 h after mating, consistent with potentially reduced ovulation rates. In addition, several of these genes and/or their ligands have known roles in reproductive physiology and behavior, further bolstering our confidence that the ERC screen identified physiologically relevant genes (Table 1). Notably, ETHR is expressed in OA neurons in the FRT and stimulates OA release and ovulation as a consequence of binding its known ligand ETH (10).

We then performed AlphaFold-Multimer (38, 39) screens to identify putative ovulin interactors. Pairwise structural predictions were generated for ovulin and three sets of candidate proteins: Drosophila transmembrane receptors (including GPCRs) (flybase.org), the FRT proteome (46), and the OA neuron proteome (47). The FRT and OA neurons were included as they are probable sites of ovulin action (18, 27). The resulting predictions were ranked by ipTM (interface predicted template modeling) score, AlphaFold’s intrinsic measure of confidence in a predicted interaction (ranging from 0 to 1). Overall, ipTM scores were relatively low (Fig. 1 A–C), possibly due to the young evolutionary age of ovulin (48) and the low confidence in its predicted structure (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). This is consistent with the modest ipTM score of 0.38 for ovulin dimer (0.4 when the signal peptide is removed), despite ovulin’s known self-interaction (49). Notably, no GPCR emerged as a strong candidate (Fig. 1A), and we observed that well-characterized ligand–GPCR pairs such as Dilp8-Lgr3 (ipTM = 0.28), ETH-ETHR (ipTM = 0.22), and fog-Smog (ipTM = 0.20) (Table 1) also yielded low ipTM scores when tested independently (mean ipTM score from top three ranked models), indicating that GPCR–ligand interactions may be difficult to predict with AlphaFold. However, one promising hit, CG6067, emerged from our screen of ovulin with the FRT proteome, with an ipTM score of 0.65 (Fig. 1B). CG6067, a membrane-bound S1A serine protease homolog, may facilitate ovulin cleavage postmating (18, 19, 50), influencing ovulation rates. The interaction between ovulin and CG6067 is predicted with high confidence, indicated by the overall low predicted aligned error (PAE) values for the interacting residues (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and C). Since ovulin’s signal peptide is expected to be cleaved for secretion, we performed an additional prediction with the cleaved form, which produced a similar result (mean ipTM score from top three ranked models: 0.59; see SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). Together, these findings identify CG6067 as a candidate for further investigation in the context of ovulation.

In summary, ERC and AlphaFold-Multimer screens identified 12 membrane proteins expressed in female flies, that are candidates for involvement in ovulation regulation. With ETHR already known for its role in ovulation (10), 11 candidates remain for further validation.

Seven Candidates from Our Screens Regulate Postmating Ovulation.

To confirm the roles of ERC and AlphaFold-identified hits in ovulation regulation, we conducted functional ovulation assays in knockdown/knockout and control females, measuring the probability of finding an egg in the uterus 3 h postmating, when ovulin’s effect is strongest (16). This metric reflects ovulation rates, as ovulated eggs are rapidly transported to the uterus following mating. To rule out oogenesis defects as a potential cause of any reduced egg presence in the uterus, we also assessed the number of mature oocytes in the ovary. To limit possible indirect developmental effects, knockdown was induced by RNAi during the late pupal stage using the ubiquitous temperature-manipulable driver Tubulin-Gal80ts; Tubulin-Gal4 (TG4ts).

One UAS-Lgr3 RNAi line (BDSC:28789) induced almost complete knockdown of Lgr3 (measured by RT-PCR) when expressed ubiquitously in adult females (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). Lgr3 knockdown females exhibited reduced ovulation rates post-mating, with a significantly lower probability of finding an egg in the uterus compared to controls, alongside an unexpected increase in ovulation in unmated females (Fig. 2A). This suggests that Lgr3 may play dual roles, enhancing ovulation after mating and suppressing it in the unmated state. Knockdown did not affect mature oocyte number in the ovary (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B), ruling out obvious oogenesis defects.

Whole-body knockdown of GabaβR1 revealed comparable effects on postmating ovulation rates. By qPCR, one UAS-GabaβR1 RNAi line (VDRC:101440) induced ~25% knockdown when driven in adult unmated females with TG4ts (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). GabaβR1 knockdown females exhibited reduced ovulation rates postmating but had no effect on premating ovulation or oogenesis (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 D and E).

Ubiquitous SIFaR knockdown with two independent RNAi lines (VDRC:1783, BDSC:25831; see SI Appendix, Fig. S3A) also significantly reduced ovulation rates after mating (Fig. 2C and SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). Limiting RNAi construct expression to tissues that endogenously express SIFaR, using SIFaR-Gal4, produced consistent results (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 C and G), confirming that SIFaR is required for increased ovulation after mating. SIFaR has one known ligand, the neuropeptide SIFa (42). To test whether SIFa mediates SIFaR’s role in early ovulation, we asked whether ubiquitous, adult-specific SIFa knockdown recapitulates the ovulation defects seen upon SIFaR knockdown. Indeed, SIFa knockdown females from two different RNAi lines (BDSC:29428, BDSC:60484; see SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B) also showed a reduction in ovulation rate (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S4D). Importantly, none of the knockdowns of SIFaR or SIFa impaired oogenesis (SI Appendix, Figs. S3 B, D, F, and H and S4 C and E) or affected ovulation in unmated females (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A–F). Altogether, these data indicate that SIFa-SIFaR pathway is involved in postmating ovulation regulation.

Knockdown of mthl9 yielded comparable outcomes. Females with mthl9 knockdown using two separate RNAi lines (BDSC:51695, VDRC:108967; see SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B) exhibited a significantly reduced probability of having an egg in the uterus after mating compared to control females, while premating ovulation remained unaffected (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 D, F, and G). A mild oogenesis defect was observed in unmated knockdown females using line BDSC:51695 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6C). However, this defect was not detected in experiments with the VDRC:108967 line (SI Appendix, Fig. S6E), indicating that the ovulation defect observed in mthl9 knockdowns is independent of oogenesis deficiencies.

Mutant or knockdown analysis implicated Smog (mutant; see SI Appendix, Fig. S7A), Cirl (mutant; see SI Appendix, Fig. S8A), and CG6067 (knockdown; see SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A and B) as regulators of postmating ovulation as well (Fig. 3 A–C and SI Appendix, Figs. S7B, S8B, and S9D). Lack of these proteins did not affect ovulation in unmated females or impair oogenesis (SI Appendix, Figs. S7 C and D, S8 C and D, and S9 C and E–G). For Smog, the probability of finding an egg in the uterus was highest in control (SmogΔ/+) females mated to wild-type males, lower in hemizygous SmogΔ/df mutant females (generated by crossing the mutant strain to a deficiency strain that uncovers the Smog locus), and lowest in homozygous SmogΔ/Δ mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). This pattern is likely influenced by the genetic background of homozygous mutants; for example, modifiers linked to the chromosome carrying the Δ allele may enhance the ovulation defect when present on both homologous chromosomes, as in the homozygous condition, compared to the hemizygous combination. Comparisons between hemizygous Smog Δ/df females and two controls (Smogdf/+, SmogΔ/+) confirmed a significant reduction in ovulation rates postmating (Fig. 3A). Likewise, Cirl mutant females exhibited severely reduced ovulation rates (Fig. 3B and SI Appendix, Fig. S8B). Knocking down CG6067 using two different RNAi lines also resulted in analogous outcomes, with a marked reduction in the probability of detecting an egg in the uterus compared to controls (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S9D).

By contrast, knockdown of Fz4 and Rickets impaired oogenesis, significantly reducing mature oocyte numbers in the ovary (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 E and F), thereby precluding ovulation assays. Additionally, CCHa1R knockout and CG15744 knockdown had no impact on oogenesis or postmating ovulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A–D). Interestingly, however, CG15744 knockdown significantly increased ovulation in unmated females (SI Appendix, Fig. S10C), suggesting a role in suppressing ovulation before mating. Based on these phenotypes, these four proteins were excluded from further analyses.

These findings reveal that multiple female membrane proteins, including Lgr3, GabaβR1, SIFaR, mthl9, Smog, Cirl, and CG6067, play important roles in regulating postmating ovulation. Together, these results highlight the extensive female contributions to ovulation control and demonstrate the utility of ovulin-based screening to identify key regulators of this process. We next investigated the genetic interactions between these female regulators and ovulin to further elucidate the molecular mechanisms of ovulation.

Lgr3 and GabaβR1 Work in the Same Pathway as Ovulin to Affect Ovulation, While SIFaR and mthl9 Function Independently of Ovulin.

Unlike the previous section, which compared control and knockdown/knockout females to assess ovulation effects, here we evaluated the genetic interactions and ovulin dependencies of female regulators by comparing ovulation rates within knockdown female groups, specifically between those mated to control males and those mated to ovulin-null males. If a female protein functions in the same pathway as ovulin, ovulation rates should be similarly reduced regardless of whether or not the female receives ovulin from the male. Conversely, if the candidate protein functions independently, ovulation rates should be significantly lower in females mated to ovulin-null males relative to those mated to control males. This analysis revealed genetic interactions with ovulin for four female membrane ovulation-regulating proteins.

Knockdown females of Lgr3 and GabaβR1 mated to ovulin-null males showed no further reductions in ovulation relative to those mated to control males (Fig. 2 A and B). This lack of response to the presence versus absence of ovulin suggests that Lgr3 and GabaβR1 likely function within the ovulin pathway.

By contrast, SIFaR knockdown females (TG4ts > VDRC:1783) mated to ovulin-null males exhibited significantly greater reductions in ovulation compared to those mated to control males (Fig. 2C), indicating that SIFaR functions independently of ovulin in a parallel pathway. Similar trends were observed with the BDSC:25831 line, though the results were not statistically significant (SI Appendix, Fig. S3E). SIFa knockdown females also showed a further reduction in ovulation when mated to ovulin-null males (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S4D), indicating that SIFa-SIFaR pathway increases postmating ovulation in parallel to ovulin.

For mthl9, knockdown females (TG4ts > BDSC:51695) mated to ovulin-null males displayed significantly reduced ovulation compared to those mated to control males (Fig. 2E). However, for line VDRC:108967, ovulation rates in knockdowns were too low to compare between matings with control and ovulin-null males (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). Nonetheless, these findings suggest that mthl9 also operates independently of ovulin in postmating ovulation regulation.

Our results show two distinct functional modes of these female ovulation regulators. Lgr3 and GabaβR1 act in the same pathway as ovulin, while SIFa-SIFaR and mthl9 regulate ovulation independently of ovulin, intricately modulating the process following mating. These findings underscore the complex orchestration of ovulation regulation and provide a foundation for future mechanistic studies.

Lgr3, GabaβR1, SIFaR, and CG6067 Are Required in OA Neurons to Regulate Female Postmating Ovulation Rates.

Several receptors identified here as ovulation regulators are expressed in the nervous system: Lgr3, GabaβR1, SIFaR, Smog, and Cirl (Table 1). Additionally, although CG6067 was initially identified from the FRT proteome, FlyAtlas2 data suggest that it is also expressed in the nervous system, so we included it in our subsequent analyses. To further investigate their functions, we assessed whether these proteins are required in neurons to mediate ovulation. Ovulation is known to depend on input from OA neurons, which respond to the receipt of ovulin in female flies after mating (7, 27). Therefore, we also examined whether these proteins’ roles in ovulation are specifically required in OA neurons. Due to the lack of RNAi lines that achieved sufficient knockdown efficiency for Smog and Cirl, these genes were excluded from the tissue-specific knockdown ovulation assays.

We performed a pan-neuronal Lgr3 knockdown using elav-Gal4 (51) and found it led to a significant decrease in the probability of finding an egg in the uterus 3 h after mating to wild-type males (Fig. 4A). This result indicates that neuronal Lgr3 is needed to enhance ovulation in the hours immediately following mating. Furthermore, we found that Lgr3 influences ovulation through OA neurons, as Tdc2-Gal4-driven Lgr3 knockdown in females also resulted in a significantly reduced probability of finding an egg in the uterus 3 h after mating to a wild-type male (Fig. 4B). Similarly, GabaβR1 is required in OA neurons to elicit the ovulatory response after mating, with OA neuron-specific knockdown markedly reducing the ovulation rate (Fig. 4C). SIFaR expression in neurons, and more specifically in OA neurons, is also necessary to increase ovulation rates shortly after mating, as SIFaR knockdown with elavGal4 and Tdc2Gal4 both caused a decreased probability of finding an egg in the uterus (Fig. 4 D and E). CG6067 showed similar effects: Its knockdown in all neurons or specifically in OA neurons significantly reduced ovulation rates (Fig. 4 F and G), supporting its role in OA neuron-mediated regulation of ovulation. Neuronal knockdowns of each of these four genes did not affect oogenesis or detectably impact ovulation in unmated females (SI Appendix, Figs. S11 A–G and S12 A–G), indicating that their roles are specific to postmating ovulation. Interestingly, in the case of Lgr3, this finding contrasts with the effects of ubiquitous Lgr3 knockdown, which significantly increased the probability of finding an egg in the uterus of unmated females (Fig. 2A); this contrast suggests that Lgr3 may influence premating ovulation through non-neuronal mechanisms. It is worth noting that the phenotype of ubiquitous Lgr3 knockdown resembles that reported for ETHR knockdown in OA neurons (10): while ETH treatment normally increases ovarian peritoneal sheath contractions to facilitate ovulation, ETHR knockdown abolishes this response, resulting in reduced contraction frequency after treatment compared to controls. However, these knockdown females also exhibit elevated baseline contraction frequency before treatment, which may result from disinhibition of OA neurons, a state in which neural activity increases due to the removal of inhibitory signals. Thus, although both Lgr3 and ETHR knockdowns affect premating/pretreated ovulation, the underlying mechanisms appear distinct, highlighting the complexity of this regulatory process.

Our finding that expression of Lgr3, GabaβR1, SIFaR, and CG6067 in OA neurons is critical for regulating ovulation in response to mating highlights the central role of OA neurons as integrators of multiple signals to regulate postmating ovulation.

Lgr3, GabaβR1, SIFaR, Smog, and Cirl Are Expressed in OA Neurons Innervating the FRT.

Neurons innervating the FRT, including a subset of OA neurons that project from the abdominal ganglion, play a large role in regulating the release of oocytes from the ovary (7, 10, 27, 52, 53). To investigate whether the ovulation-regulating genes Lgr3, GabaβR1, SIFaR, Smog, Cirl, and CG6067, which have known neuronal expression (FlyAtlas2), are expressed in neurons innervating the FRT, we used Gal4 drivers under their endogenous promoters to express membrane-bound UAS-mCD8:GFP, thereby visualizing their expression patterns. Negative controls were also included to ensure that any observed GFP signal was not due to background fluorescence (SI Appendix, Fig. S13). Different Gal4 lines for the same gene can vary in specificity and expression patterns depending on the sequences they include and their chromosomal location. Therefore, all Gal4 lines used here were driven by endogenous promoters, with constructs either tagged at the C-terminus of the endogenous gene (54), inserted into intronic regions (55), or integrated at or near the gene’s 3′ end (56), to minimize potential concerns about misexpression. We were unable to analyze CG6067 because only one publicly available driver line was identified, and it showed no FRT expression.

Lgr3-Gal4-driven mCD8:GFP expression revealed labeling that colocalizes with neurons in the FRT, as marked by HRP staining (Fig. 5 A-i). This neuronal expression in the FRT prompted us to investigate whether Lgr3 is expressed in FRT OA neurons, which are known to regulate ovulation (7, 10, 27, 52). As shown in Fig. 5 A-ii, we observed an overlap between GFP reporter expression, the neuronal marker HRP, and OA neurons labeled with anti-Tdc2, indicating that Lgr3 is expressed in FRT OA neurons. This finding is consistent with our previous results showing that knockdown of Lgr3 in OA neurons reduces ovulation rates (Fig. 4B), suggesting that Lgr3 may act locally within the FRT to regulate ovulation. In addition, we noticed structures labeled with GFP that resembled neuronal cell bodies (arrows in Fig. 5 A-i). To confirm whether these structures are indeed neuronal cell bodies, we performed costaining with Hoechst to label nuclei and used higher magnification imaging. Hoechst staining was localized centrally, marking nuclei, while the GFP signal and HRP staining surrounded it (Fig. 5 A-iii), confirming that Lgr3 is expressed in neuronal cell bodies. Moreover, these cell bodies did not overlap with anti-Tdc2 staining (Fig. 5 A-iii), suggesting that they are likely sensory neurons whose cell bodies reside in the FRT. Similarly, GabaβR1, SIFaR, and Smog showed significant overlap between their Gal4-driven GFP expression, HRP-labeled reproductive tract neurons, and Tdc2-positive OA neurons, indicating expression in OA neurons innervating the FRT (Fig. 5 B–D-i and ii). These genes are also expressed in the peripheral neuronal cell bodies (Fig. 5 B–D-iii). These results suggest that, like Lgr3, GabaβR1, SIFaR, and Smog may act locally in FRT OA neurons and possibly in peripheral sensory neurons to regulate ovulation. Interestingly, while Cirl showed similar neuronal expression as the other receptors (Fig. 5 E-i to iii), it also exhibited extensive expression within the FRT itself (Fig. 5 E-i), including in muscles, as evidenced by overlap with phalloidin staining and the staining pattern of the GFP reporter (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 A and B). Because relaxation and contraction of FRT musculature are essential for successful ovulation (10, 25, 27), these findings suggest that Cirl may function both in FRT OA neurons and in muscle tissue to regulate ovulation locally.

The expression of Lgr3, GabaβR1, and SIFaR in reproductive tract OA neurons, along with their requirement in OA neurons for postmating ovulation regulation, raises the question of whether they influence ovulation through reproductive tract OA neurons. Further identification and characterization of these neurons would provide deeper insight into the neural mechanisms governing ovulation.

Smog Is Under Recurrent Positive Selection, Whereas the Other Proteins We Tested Are Not.

Ovulin is among the most rapidly evolving proteins in Drosophila, showing significant amino acid divergence even between closely related species such as D. melanogaster and Drosophila simulans (48, 49, 57–59). We hypothesize that proteins in the same pathway or regulatory network as ovulin may also experience an accelerated rate of amino acid sequence divergence. This could result from a generally relaxed constraint on the pathway, leading to elevated evolutionary rates in multiple proteins, including ovulin. Alternatively, these proteins may have to adapt to ovulin’s rapid evolution, at least in part, to maintain effective ovulation regulation. To investigate this, we examined whether any of the female ovulation regulators exhibit recurrent positive selection at specific sites using PAML, which detects positive selection in protein-coding DNA sequences through maximum likelihood–based phylogenetic analyses (60). We also included ETHR in these analyses because it was previously shown to affect postmating ovulation (10) and was identified in our ERC screens (Table 1). Our analyses focused on species within the melanogaster group to avoid synonymous site saturation (SI Appendix, Fig. S15). The dN/dS ratio (ω) measures the rate of protein-coding gene evolution by comparing nonsynonymous to synonymous substitutions. We used model M0 to estimate the overall ω for each protein and applied models M7, M8, and M8a to test for recurrent positive selection and identify specific amino acid sites predicted to be under positive selection (61, 62). The M0 ω estimate for Smog is notably higher than that for the other tested proteins. Furthermore, Smog exhibits significant evidence of positive selection (Fig. 6A). Although Cirl and certain isoforms of ETHR also show a class of sites under positive selection in the model M7/M8 comparison, this significance is lost when using the model M8a/M8 comparison (Fig. 6A). The differential results of these models suggest that the evolution of these sites in Cirl and ETHR is more likely driven by neutral divergence rather than positive selection.

The amino acid sites under positive selection in Smog are predominantly clustered in unstructured loops (Fig. 6B). In Smog, a Class C GPCR, these sites are all located in the N terminus (Fig. 6B), which harbors the ligand-binding domain in this receptor class (63, 64). Their localization suggests that these regions may have evolved to optimize ligand-binding interactions. This raises the intriguing possibility that Smog may bind to ovulin in vivo, which requires further investigation. On the other hand, many of these female ovulation-regulating proteins have well-characterized other ligands and play pleiotropic roles in other important biological processes. These factors may constrain their evolutionary potential, thereby limiting the occurrence of positive selection in the other proteins we tested.

Discussion

While previous studies reported an ovulin-OA neuronal signaling axis (27) in ovulation control, the extent to which additional pathways contribute to ovulation remained unclear. In this study, we identified six female receptors and one additional membrane protein that play pivotal roles in regulating postmating ovulation (Fig. 7), expanding on existing knowledge and highlighting the complexity of the regulatory network governing postmating ovulation. Interestingly, many of the female GPCR ovulation regulators identified in this study have mammalian orthologs. Lgr3’s predicted mammalian ortholog, RXFP2, also plays a key role in ovulation (65) as the receptor for both protein hormone relaxin and insulin-like peptide 3. Thus, our work may illuminate conserved mechanisms across species as well.

By leveraging ERC and AlphaFold screens to identify candidates, we demonstrate a strategy that successfully identifies genes involved in ovulation, whose roles were previously unknown. ERC detects shared functionality rather than direct interactions, while AlphaFold predicts physical interactions, offering complementary insights into possible signaling networks. Although ERC signals can sometimes arise from similarities in expression patterns (34), this is unlikely in our case, as ovulin is a male accessory gland-specific protein, whereas the identified candidates are female-expressed, or at least not male accessory gland-enriched. This supports the idea that a positive ERC signal is more likely to reflect involvement in a common pathway. AlphaFold predictions incorporate both evolutionary features and structural considerations (38, 66). Given that ovulin is a rapidly evolving protein and detectable in only a few Drosophila species (48, 49, 57–59), its evolutionary divergence may affect AlphaFold’s ability to accurately predict its structure and interactions, potentially explaining the relatively low ipTM scores observed in our screens. Most candidates in this study were identified from ERC screens focused on GPCRs. While our results demonstrate the efficacy of this approach for identifying female proteins involved in ovulation, focusing on a single receptor family may overlook key regulators from other protein families. Future ERC screens on the entire transmembrane proteome may reveal additional, non-GPCR, candidates and provide opportunities for future research into ovulation regulation. Nonetheless, our work highlights the effectiveness of ERC and AlphaFold in pathway discovery, particularly for complex physiological processes such as reproductive regulation.

Our work shows that there are at least two functional pathways controlling ovulation: ovulin-dependent or ovulin-independent (Fig. 7). Lgr3 and GabaβR1 are involved in the same pathway as ovulin. Interestingly, Dilp8, the known ligand for Lgr3 (67, 68), was also reported to influence ovulation (41). Thus, it would be intriguing to explore the relationship between ovulin and the Dilp8-Lgr3 pathway in regulating ovulation. GabaβR1 is our top ERC hit (Table 1), and its known ligand, GABA, is a γ-amino acid remaining identical in all assessed species. Notably, the other GABAB receptors, GabaβR2 and GabaβR3, have much lower ERC scores (–0.80 and 0.08, respectively). This strong covariation in evolutionary rate between GabaβR1 and ovulin supports its functional role in the ovulin pathway. Lgr3 and GabaβR1 both exhibit expression in neurons innervating the FRT (Fig. 5 A and B), thus they are likely to act locally with ovulin in the reproductive tract to elicit the ovulation response. SIFaR and mthl9, on the other hand, regulate ovulation independently of ovulin. We also confirmed the involvement of SIFaR’s ligand, SIFa, in ovulation regulation (Fig. 2D). SIFa expressing neurons project throughout the brain and into the posterior thoracicoabdominal ganglion (69, 70), a neural hub that is anatomically close to the FRT and exposed to hemolymph. This could suggest putative crosstalk between SIFa releasing tissues/neurons and ovulin pathway. While SIFaR is not directly linked to ovulin, it interacts with downstream OA neuronal signaling, as it is required in OA neurons to permit ovulation (Fig. 4E). This underscores the central role of OA signaling in ovulation control and bolsters the intricacy of the regulatory network. Future work should explore mthl9’s potential interactions with OA signaling or other downstream pathways to better define its position within this network. We also identify three membrane proteins, Smog, Cirl, and CG6067, that regulate ovulation but whose relationships to ovulin remain unclear and require further investigation. Notably, many of the female ovulation regulators we identified have known ligands. To further complete the regulatory network, it would be of interest to examine whether these ligands, as well as other known upstream or downstream targets are involved in controlling ovulation. Finally, we were unable to assess the effects of Fz4 and Rickets on ovulation using our standard ovulation assay due to oogenesis defects in their knockdowns (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 E and F). For candidates with similar limitations, future studies could use alternative metrics such as ovary contraction (2, 10) or OA neuron bouton counts (27) as proxies for ovulation.

Although we have identified receptors involved in the same ovulation-regulating pathway as ovulin, the female receptor(s) for ovulin remain unknown. Among seminal fluid proteins (Sfps), only sex peptide (SP) has a known receptor, the highly conserved GPCR sex peptide receptor (SPR) (37). Interestingly, although SPR was initially identified as the receptor for SP, its ancestral ligands are the conserved neuropeptides myoinhibitory peptides (MIPs) (71, 72). SPR and MIPs are conserved throughout insects, while SP is only present within Drosophilinae subfamily (71–73), suggesting that SP may have co-opted endogenous SPR function. Whether this SP–SPR relationship reflects a general pattern for all Sfp–receptor pairs remains unclear. Thus, identification of ovulin receptor(s) will provide further insight into how Sfp function evolves, by determining whether ovulin has co-opted the activity of endogenous receptor(s) that modulates OA neuron activity. Since the female membrane ovulation regulators we identified are expressed in the nervous system and/or FRT, the likely action sites of ovulin, and some show genetic interactions with ovulin, these findings support the potential for direct physical interactions with ovulin, making them promising candidates for ovulin receptor(s). Given that ovulin is rapidly evolving (48, 49, 57–59), we might expect elevated rates of amino acid sequence divergence in its receptor(s). Indeed, we observed this in Smog (Fig. 6A). Although further investigations are needed to definitively identify ovulin receptor(s), most female membrane regulators, including Smog, have documented ligands, increasing the likelihood that ovulin may also hijack endogenous receptor(s) to mediate ovulation. Additionally, it is known that several Sfps that mediate female postmating response (PMR) experience elevated rates of evolution, driven by sexual selection or sexual conflict (74). However, the identification of female genes mediating PMR has been limited. Among those identified, to our knowledge, only two female proteins involved (in as-yet uncharacterized ways) in the SP network (36) were found to be under positive selection (75). Our results raise the intriguing possibility that similar evolutionary pressures could drive the rapid evolution of female genes regulating PMR, offering insights into the coevolution of male and female reproductive proteins.

The activity of FRT OA (Tdc2+ dsx+) neurons responds to a number of signals that help integrate internal state with reproductive output. In unmated females, OA neuron activity is inhibited by acetylcholine release from SP sensory neurons (SPSNs), which project from the reproductive tract to the abdominal ganglion and express pickpocket (ppk), a marker for proprioceptive neurons (76–78). Receipt of SP during mating reduces the activity of SPSNs (77, 78), such that after mating OA neurons are no longer inhibited (76). Interestingly, several octopamine receptors are expressed in ppk+ neurons that innervate the reproductive tract (26, 79), suggesting another potential mechanism by which OA neurons might modulate peripheral neurons and influence ovulation. The ability of ovulin to increase activity of reproductive tract OA neurons (27) presents an additional mechanism coupling increased ovulation to mating. Moreover, ETH-ETHR signaling in OA neurons was reported to coordinate reproductive arrest during times of stress (10), showing that OA neurons are able to integrate environmental information into ovulation decisions. Here, we show that OA neurons also receive signals from Lgr3, GabaβR1, SIFaR, and CG6067 to manipulate ovulation, as OA neuronal knockdown of these four proteins individually reduces ovulation at 3 h after mating (Fig. 4 B, C, E, and G). This demonstrates the role of OA neurons as a central signaling hub that integrates diverse inputs to tightly regulate ovulation. Since our study shows Lgr3, GabaβR1, and SIFaR expression in reproductive tract OA neurons (Fig. 5 A–C-ii), future work should investigate whether these receptors stimulate ovulation through reproductive tract OA neurons. Given recent findings showing that OA neurons exhibit heterogeneous projection patterns and innervate distinct reproductive tract regions (80), it will be important to determine whether Lgr3, GabaβR1, and SIFaR are expressed in specific OA subpopulations that differentially regulate ovulation in the future. In addition, we observed labeling of peripheral cell bodies for several receptors (Fig. 5 A–E), which may indicate expression in reproductive tract sensory neurons such as SPSNs. These neurons could potentially form a functional network with OA neurons to coregulate ovulation, an intriguing possibility for future studies. Such findings would further emphasize the precise control of female ovulation and pinpoint the specific subset of neurons essential for this process.

Materials and Methods

ERC Screen and Candidate Prioritization.

ERC screens (34–36) were used to identify female proteins with shared functionality with ovulin. Since we limited our ovulation-regulating receptor search to GPCRs, we compiled a comprehensive list of D. melanogaster GPCRs from Flybase (flybase.org) by selecting all genes annotated with “G-protein-coupled receptor.” For the first ERC screen GPCR and ovulin orthologs were identified from 12 Drosophila species (melanogaster, sechellia, simulans, yakuba, erecta, ananassae, pseudoobscura, persimilis, wil-listoni, grimshawi, virilis, and mojavensis) using InParanoid and aligned using MUSCLE (81–83). For the second ERC screen orthologs were calculated for an additional 10 Drosophila genomes (biarmpies, bipectinada, elegans, eugracilis, ficusphila, kikawaii, miranda, suzukii, takahashii, rhopaloa). Amino acid divergence of each branch in the phylogenetic tree of orthologous protein alignments was done using “aaml” of the PAML package (36, 60). To control for correlation due to the underlying species tree, raw branch lengths were transformed into rates of evolution relative to the expected branch length (34, 84). These rates of evolution were then used to compute the correlation coefficients for ovulin with each GPCR. Since ovulin orthologs were not detected in all 12/22 species, pairwise analyses excluded species and phylogenetic branches inferred to lack ovulin when calculating the ERC values. From the first screen, six GPCR candidates were selected based on positive ERC scores and their expected expression patterns. The second ERC screen identified five additional GPCRs, resulting in a final list of 11 candidates (Table 1).

AlphaFold-Multimer Prediction Screen and Candidate Selection.

AlphaFold-Multimer screens (38, 39) were used to predict female proteins physically interacted with ovulin. The D. melanogaster transmembrane receptor list was compiled by selecting all genes in the “transmembrane receptors” gene group from Flybase (flybase.org). D. melanogaster OA neuron proteome was determined by selecting all gene markers of the cluster annotated as octopaminergic/ tyraminergic neurons from the FlyCellAtlas data (47) in Scope (scope.aertslab.org), and FRT proteome was established by including all proteins identified in dissected lower FRTs (46). Three independent AlphaFold-Multimer prediction screens were performed with ovulin against the above protein lists using the LocalColabFold (github.com/YoshitakaMo/localcolabfold) implementation of AlphaFold (ColabFold v1.5.5 and AlphaFold v2.3.2) (38, 39, 66). Three predictions were generated for each input, with default settings except for the inclusion of templates and five recycles. Predictions were ranked by average ipTM scores. Top predictions were manually inspected by viewing PAE plots and PDB files (PyMol v2.5.5). Pairwise predictions were performed using the ColabFold notebook (https://colab.research.google.com/github/sokrypton/ColabFold/blob/main/AlphaFold2.ipynb) for known ligand–GPCR pairs, as well as for signal peptide-cleaved ovulin and CG6067.

Fly Husbandry and Ovulation Regulator Candidate Knockdown/Knockout.

Flies were reared on either a dextrose yeast or sucrose yeast diet with a 12:12 light/dark cycle. For candidate knockdown experiments, UAS-RNAi lines for candidate proteins were obtained from the Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center (BDSC) and the Vienna Drosophila Resource Center (VDRC). Knockdown and control females were generated by crossing driver line unmated females to either RNAi line males or the appropriate background control males. For candidate knockout experiments, mutant and deficiency lines were obtained from BDSC. Knockout females were generated by crossing mutant lines with deficiency lines, while control females were produced by crossing either mutant or deficiency lines with the w1118 line. For experiments utilizing temperature-manipulable drivers, crosses were switched from room temperature to 29 °C when black pupae became visible. Ovulin-null and control males for ovulation assays were generated as described (27). Unmated females were collected at eclosion and aged in single-sex groups of 15 to 20 for 3 to 5 d on food supplemented with ~20 μL yeast paste. Males were also aged in single-sex groups of 15 to 20 for 3 to 5 d. To examine candidate receptors’ expression in FRT neurons, candidate Gal4 lines, in which Gal4 expression is controlled by the candidate’s endogenous promoter, were crossed to UAS-mCD8:GFP. Unmated Gal4>UAS-mCD8:GFP females were collected upon eclosion and aged 3 to 5 d on yeast paste supplemented food. The Lgr3-T2A-Gal4 line was kindly provided by Drs. Shu Kondo (Tokyo University of Science) and Hiromu Tanimoto (Tohoku University). And flies from BDSC and VDRC are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1. In contrast to the other genes, for which we used two independent RNAi lines to help rule out off-target effects, we were only able to obtain a single RNAi line for Lgr3 and GabaβR1 that generated sufficient knockdown. However, we expressed these RNAi constructs using several different Gal4 drivers and observed consistent phenotypes across drivers. Nevertheless, although unlikely, we cannot entirely exclude the possibility that the phenotypes resulted from gain-of-function effects or off-target activity of the RNAi transgenes.

RT-PCR and qPCR.

In RNAi experiments, target knockdown was confirmed using either semiquantitative RT-PCR or qPCR. Total RNA was isolated from either 10 to 15 whole bodies or 20 to 30 heads of 3 to 5 d old unmated females using TRIzol (Invitrogen) or RNAzol (Sigma-Aldrich), and the manufacturer’s recommended protocol. RNA was resuspended in nuclease free water and stored at −80 °C. Then 1 μg of RNA was treated with DNase (Promega) to eliminate DNA contamination. SMARTscribe (Clontech) was used to generate cDNA. When testing the knockdown of lowly expressed genes (SIFaR, GabaβR1), a molar ratio of 1.65 μM oligo dN9 to 1.25 μM oligo dT was used in the first strand priming step of the RT reaction. All other cDNA synthesis reactions used only oligo dT. For semiquantitative RT-PCR knockdown analysis, cDNA template was diluted 20×, and the gene of interest and actin/Rpl32 (loading control) were amplified using GoTaq Green Master Mix (Promega). PCR products were visualized on a 2% agarose gel with ethidium bromide/GelRed. To gauge the amount of candidate knockdown, the intensity of the knockdown female’s gene of interest band was compared to that of control females, normalized by actin/Rpl32 signal. For qPCR, primers were first validated to ensure amplification efficiencies in the range of 90 to 150%. Amplification was done using SsoAdvanced Universal SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) on a Bio-Rad CFX-Connect instrument. Actin was used as a reference gene to which gene of interest Ct values were normalized. Differential expression analysis was performed using the ΔΔCt method. Primers used are listed in SI Appendix, Table S2.

Genomic DNA PCR.

In candidate knockout experiments, gene disruption was confirmed by genomic DNA PCR. Genomic DNA was extracted by homogenizing five 3 to 5 d old unmated females in squishing buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH8, 1 mM EDTA, and 25 mM NaCl) supplemented with proteinase K (Invitrogen) and incubating at 37 °C for 30 min. Following incubation, proteinase K was inactivated by heating to 95 °C for 3 min, and the supernatant containing fly genomic DNA was then collected as template for PCR. The gene of interest and loading control were amplified using GoTaq Green Master Mix (Promega). PCR products were visualized on a 2% agarose gel with GelRed. Primers used are listed in SI Appendix, Table S2.

Counts of Mature Oocytes in Ovaries.

Unmated 3 to 5 d old candidate knockdown/knockout and control females were flash frozen, and stored at −80 °C. Ovaries were removed from females and placed in 20 μL 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The number of mature oocytes within each pair of ovaries was counted, and statistical differences between knockdown/knockout and control groups were determined using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Two biological replicates were done for each experiment.

Ovulation Assays.

Ovulation assays were adapted from Heifetz et al. (16). 3-to 5-d-old candidate protein knockdown/knockout and control unmated females were either placed with age-matched males in single pair matings or placed singly in vials as an unmated control group. To enhance female receptivity, ~20 μL of yeast paste was added to each vial prior to the experiment. For experiments using a temperature-manipulable driver, all matings were kept at 29 °C to maintain candidate knockdown. Vials were checked for mating every 10 min, and a pair’s mating start time was recorded. Pairs that did not mate within 4 h of the experimental start time were discarded. 3 h after the start of mating, females were flash frozen. Freezing of unmated control females was interspersed with the freezing of mated females. Frozen flies were stored at −80 °C until dissection. Females were then placed in 1X PBS, and the ventral abdominal cuticle was carefully peeled back to reveal the FRT. The presence of an egg in the uterus was assessed.

For ovulation assays using mates of control and ovulin-null males, data were analyzed in R using the package lme4 to construct a mixed effects logistic regression with female genotype and male genotype included as fixed effects and experimental replicate included as a random effect. Male genotype × Female genotype interaction terms were included in models if the interaction term was significant and its inclusion significantly improved model fit. For assays involving a single male genotype, female genotype was included as a fixed effect, and experimental replicate was included as a random effect. In cases where no mated female in a certain group had an egg in the uterus, we used the package logistf, which applies Firth’s bias reduction method, to address complete separation and provide more reliable, bias-reduced logistic regression estimates. The package emmeans was then used to select relevant pairwise comparisons, and P-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Holm–Bonferroni correction. Negative control unmated females were excluded from statistical analysis for two reasons: 1) To assay whether a candidate exhibited ovulation regulatory activity, we were only interested in comparisons of mated control females to mated candidate knockdown/knockout females. 2) Probabilities of finding an egg in the uterus of unmated females were often 0 or close to 0, which caused problems of separation or quasi-separation in our models. Unmated females were only included in statistical analysis when candidate knockdown significantly affected unmated female ovulation.

FRT Imaging and Staining.

Reproductive tracts were dissected in 1X PBS from unmated 3 to 5 d old candidate Gal4>UAS-mCD8:GFP females. Tracts were immediately placed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences) diluted in 1X PBS for 50 min. After fixation, ovaries were briefly rinsed with 5% Triton-X in 1X PBS and then given five 10-min washes in 0.5% Triton-X in 1X PBS (PBST). After washing, tracts were blocked in 5% normal goat serum (NGS) for 5 h. Reproductive tracts were then treated with mouse anti-GFP (1:50, Invitrogen), rabbit anti-Tdc2 (1:200, Covalab), anti-HRP:Cy3 (1:250, Jackson ImmunoResearch), and Hoechst (1:1,000, Invitrogen) in 5% NGS in PBST overnight. Samples were washed five times and then incubated with goat anti-mouse Alexa 488 (1:500, Invitrogen) and goat anti-rabbit Alexa 633 (1:500, Invitrogen) overnight. Afterward, samples were given five 10-min washes in PBST, and then were mounted on slides in 6 μL AF1 mounting medium (Electron Microscopy Sciences). For Cirl, reproductive tracts were also stained with anti-GFP:AlexaFluor 488 (1:250, Invitrogen), anti-HRP:Cy3 (1:250, Jackson ImmunoResearch), and phalloidin:647 (1:500, Invitrogen). After ~36 h, samples were given five 10-min washes in PBST, and then were mounted on slides in 6 μL AF1 mounting medium (Electron Microscopy Sciences). Images were taken on a Zeiss LSM 710 microscope (Cornell University Biotechnology Resource Center Imaging facility) with 10×, 25×, and 63× objectives.

Evolutionary Analysis.

The membrane protein orthologs in other species were identified using the NCBI Gnomon prediction tool, which leverages available transcriptome data. Hits were verified to be the best reciprocal tBLASTn hit to D. melanogaster. Protein sequences from different species were then aligned using Clustal Omega (85), and inferred maximum likelihood trees were obtained with Simple Phylogeny from ClustalW2 package (85) and adjusted with a high-confidence reference phylogeny of 155 Drosophila genomes (86). DNA alignments were obtained by PAL2NAL (87), with positions containing more than 50% gaps removed. For our evolutionary analyses, we used the codeml program in PAML to estimate overall dN/dS ratio for the entire sequence (model M0) and to run the site tests (60). The model M8, which allows a proportion of sites to undergo positive selection, was compared to null models M7 and M8a, using likelihood ratio tests (61, 62). For gene showing model M8 as a significantly better fit, Bayes empirical Bayes predictions were then used to infer specific sites under positive selection at a 0.9 confidence level.
