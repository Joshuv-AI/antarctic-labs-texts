Neuroendocrine tumor mimicking a recalcitrant vocal fold granuloma: Appearances can be deceiving
Authors: Matteo Lazzeroni, Giuditta Pini, Lorenzo Salvatore Solimeno, Carlo Pescia, Letterio Runza, Lorenzo Pignataro, Giovanna Cantarella
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Key Clinical MessagePosterior glottic lesions resembling granulomas unresponsive to conservative treatment should raise suspicion of a neoplastic condition. Although surgery is rarely recommended for arytenoid granulomas due to their high recurrence rate, histological evaluation is mandatory in cases of uncertain diagnosis. Clinicians should be aware that, although very rare, a laryngeal neuroendocrine tumor occurring in the posterior glottis can mimic the appearance of an idiopathic granuloma, presenting a diagnostic challenge.Keywords: granuloma, larynx, neuroendocrine neoplasm, neuroendocrine tumor

INTRODUCTION

Benign vocal fold lesions are often the result of phonotraumatic forces or chronic irritation on the vocal folds and their treatment is generally a combination of voice therapy and phono‐microsurgical techniques. 1 Arytenoid granulomas are commonly ascribed to laryngopharyngeal reflux, voice abuse, or endotracheal intubation trauma, but their pathogenesis is still controversial. 2

The aim of this paper is to describe a peculiar case of an uncommon laryngeal neuroendocrine (NE) tumor, with a macroscopic aspect and an indolent clinical course mimicking those of an arytenoid granuloma, and to raise awareness on the importance of a thorough histopathological evaluation of apparently benign laryngeal lesions of the posterior glottis unresponsive to conservative measures. Indeed, laryngeal NE neoplasms rarely present with specific clinical symptoms, especially at early stages, thus possibly leading to incorrect diagnosis and improper treatment. 3

When assessing granuloma‐like lesions in the larynx, several possible diagnoses should be considered: tumors, tuberculosis, laryngeal papillomatosis, Wegener's granulomatosis, sarcoidosis, and foreign body reactions. Accurate identification is key for proper treatment planning.

CASE HISTORY/EXAMINATION

A 67‐year‐old male seeked outpatient phoniatric assessment in June 2012, complaining of a foreign body and a burning sensation in his throat associated with voice fatigue and occasional heartburn. He had no chronic cough or postnasal drip. His voice was slightly breathy. He had been a smoker until the age of 63 (40 pack/year) and suffered from an acute myocardial infarction at the age of 62. He underwent a flexible videolaryngoscopy that detected a lesion of the medial surface of the left arytenoid cartilage (Figure 1). The lesion resembled a typical arytenoid granuloma, and the patient's symptoms were thought to be related to laryngopharyngeal reflux and voice straining. His vocal folds' mobility was normal. His breathiness was interpreted as presbiphonia, due to age‐related deterioration of vocal muscles' tone and bulk. Supraglottic hyperfunction, compensating for vocal fold hypotonus, was considered a possible cofactor in the development of a reactive lesion on the arytenoid medial surface.

A slow progressive enlargement of the lesion over the years can be seen. (F) Videolaryngoscopic view 1 month after left medial arytenoidectomy.

METHODS

The patient received vocal hygiene advices, prescription for proton‐pump inhibitor (PPI) therapy for 3 months (repeated every 6 months), and he underwent voice therapy. He was subsequently evaluated every 6 months for 2 years and then once a year. For the following 7 years his symptomatology remained essentially unchanged and was not causing any concern. A slight enlargement of the lesion was noticed over time by videolaryngoscopy, as shown in Figure 1. In October 2019, at the age of 74, at his annual examination, he complained of a progressive voice quality deterioration, of persistent foreign body sensation and a feeling of discomfort in his throat. Despite repeated cycles of voice therapy and PPI treatment, he had not obtained any clinical improvement. Due to the worsening of his symptoms and to the enlargement of the lesion, surgical treatment was therefore proposed. The preoperative videolaryngoscopy demonstrated an increased vascularization of the lesion by narrow band imaging (NBI) light, without atypical patterns.

The granulomatous‐like lesion was removed in general anesthesia under suspension microlaryngoscopy with cold instruments and monopolar cauterization of its base of insertion on the medial surface of the arytenoid; concurrently an injection of a long‐acting steroid (Triamcitolone acetonide) was performed on the medial surface of the left arytenoid. Figure 2A,B shows intraoperative images. The histopathological examination (Figure 3) revealed a nonulcerated submucosal lesion arranged in sheets, cords, and trabecular growth pattern on low‐power examination, with an extremely narrow tumor‐free zone in the submucosa. The tumor was composed of cells with a uniform appearance mixed with a hyalinized fibro‐vascular connective tissue stroma.

(B) Tumor demonstrated strong and diffuse positivity for chromogranin A (×40 magnification). (C) The proliferation index (MIB1/Ki67) was low (×40 magnification). (D) Submucosal lesion growing in sheet, cords and trabeculae, composed of cells with a uniform appearance mixed with a fibro‐vascular stroma (H&E stain; A, ×4 magnification; B, ×10 magnification).

These cells (Figure 3) showed centrally located round or oval nuclei, with finely stippled chromatin (salt‐and‐pepper) and inconspicuous nucleoli, and abundant granular and eosinophilic cytoplasm. Cellular pleomorphism, mitotic activity, and necrosis were not observed. Additionally, there was no evidence of vascular, lymphatic, and perineural invasion. Immunohistochemistry revealed strong positivity for the epithelial markers cytokeratin AE1/AE3, cytokeratin 19, and cytokeratin CAM5.2. The neoplastic cells exhibited immunoreactivity for NE markers, with strong and diffuse staining for chromogranin A and synaptophysin, and mild and focal staining for CD56. The proliferative index, evaluated with the MIB1/Ki67 antibody, was low (1–2%). Finally, TTF1, thyreoglobulin, LH, ACTH, PRL, FSH, TSH, and HMB45 were all negative. Based on these features, the diagnosis of a NE tumor, grade 1 (G 1), was made.

Due to the histological findings, a neck magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) study with contrast medium was performed; it did not show signs of residual laryngeal neoplastic infiltration nor locoregional spread. The case was discussed at the Multidisciplinary Board for Head and Neck Cancer at our Institute (Fondazione IRCCS Ca′ Granda, Ospedale Maggiore Policlinico) and radicalization of the base of implant of the neoplastic lesion was advised. 1 month after the first surgery, the patient underwent a left medial arytenoidectomy in suspension microlaryngoscopy with Digital Acublade carbon‐dioxide laser. Figure 2C,D shows intraoperative images. Histological examination was negative for residual neoplastic disease. The patient tolerated well both endoscopic procedures; he did not complain of any swallowing impairment after the surgeries and manifested only a mild voice deterioration after the medial arytenoidectomy, with complete recovery in about 15 days.

The clinical case was subsequently discussed again at the Multidisciplinary Board and trimestral clinical videolaryngoscopic and annual radiological follow‐up by MRI were prescribed.

CONCLUSION AND RESULTS

The patient has been followed up for 4 years since the second surgical procedure and has not shown any sign of recurrence of laryngeal lesions. His voice quality has been stable over the follow‐up and is currently within normal limits for his age; he does not perceive any more the previous globus sensation.

DISCUSSION

Neuroendocrine neoplasms are heterogeneous malignancies that can occur in any organ of the body with different biological behavior, depending on histological features. According to the latest WHO classification 4 laryngeal NE neoplasms are divided into NE tumors and NE carcinomas, to reflect the WHO/IARC terminology applied also to other tumor sites. 5 NE tumors are well‐differentiated neoplasms, which can be furtherly subclassified according to a three‐tiered grading system: NE tumors G1 are defined by a low mitotic count (<2 mitoses/2mm2), low proliferative index (MIB1/Ki67 < 20%), and no necrosis, NE tumors G2 show necrosis (generally punctate) and/or 2–10 mitoses/2mm2 with a low proliferative index (MIB1/Ki67 < 20%), and NE tumors G3 exhibit necrosis or a mitotic count >10 mitoses/2mm2 with a high proliferative index (MIB1/Ki67 > 20%), in the absence of histological features consistent with NE carcinoma. However, the NE tumor G3 category is still provisional for the upper aerodigestive tract and needs further study.

The group of NE carcinomas includes small‐cell NE carcinomas and large‐cell NE carcinomas, both characterized by a typical cytomorphology paired with a mitotic count higher than 10 mitoses/2mm2, a proliferative index higher than 20% and an unfavorable prognosis.

Laryngeal NE neoplasms are mostly represented by either NE carcinomas (mainly small cell NE carcinomas) or NE tumors G2, with NE tumors G1 and G3 cases being extremely rare. Both NE tumors G2 and NE carcinomas show an aggressive disease course with a high rate of nodal and distant metastasis. 6 , 7 Surgical excision is the mainstay of treatment for NE neoplasms; the treatment is generally conservative and based on transoral CO2 laser surgery. Neck dissection is not usually performed, and radio/chemotherapy has not proved to be effective. 3 , 5

The peculiarity of the described case is the laryngoscopic aspect of his arytenoid lesion, which was undistinguishable from a typical contact granuloma from a macroscopic point of view. The clinical history was also consistent with this diagnosis. Idiopathic granulomas of the posterior glottis are heterogeneous lesions in terms of dimensions and macroscopic appearance. Therefore, during initial and subsequent evaluations, the lesion did not raise suspicion of a neoplastic nature. The nonspecific symptoms were suggestive for laryngeal chronic irritation due to laryngopharyngeal reflux. Voice fatigue was considered mainly as a consequence of presbyphonia. The videolaryngoscopic assessment over the 8 years follow‐up showed no remarkable macroscopic changes of the lesion: the color, the laryngeal site of the lesion, the reported symptoms, the coexisting gastroesophageal reflux symptoms responding at presentation to PPI therapy seemed to confirm the diagnosis of a granuloma.

Surgery is rarely indicated as a primary treatment for a vocal fold granuloma as typically a conservative treatment can be effective in most cases and recurrence is very common after granuloma removal. 8 , 9 Consequently, a conservative approach was pursued for the described patient and only the worsening of symptoms and the enhanced vascularization of the lesion prompted the decision to remove it. Yet, the histologic report was unexpected, due to the extreme rarity of laryngeal NE neoplasms.

Vocal fold granulomas are considered uncommon lesions typically located in the posterior third of the vocal folds, in the area defined as the respiratory glottis; their prevalence is higher in males and this predisposition is attributed to damage during phonation due to a lower vocal pitch in comparison with females. 9 Several etiological factors have been suggested such as intubation trauma, hard glottal attack, vocal misuse, laryngopharyngeal reflux, habitual throat clearing, low‐pitched voice, psychosomatic disorders, smoking, postnasal drip, and throat infections. 8 Granulomas are thought to occur owing to inflammation that produces granulation tissue at the site of an ulceration of the mucosa, though their pathogenesis pathway is not entirely clear. Although the lesion is benign, it can recur in most patients irrespective of treatment. 10 For this reason, surgical removal of the granuloma‐like lesion encountered in our patient was not advised over the 8‐year‐follow‐up. Arytenoid granulomas are usually treated conservatively by medical and rehabilitative treatment; surgery is usually advised for lesions causing airway obstruction or persistent severe dysphonia, or when the nature of the lesion is in doubt. 1 , 2 , 10

In the described case, the persistence of symptoms not responding to PPI and conservative rehabilitative therapy and the increased vascularization of the lesion led to the decision of surgical excision. The diagnosis of NE tumor was totally unexpected considering the small dimensions and the site of the lesion, the indolent course of the disease, and the vague symptoms reported by the patient. Transoral endoscopic CO2 excision of the neoplasm and subsequent medial arytenoidectomy proved to be effective and allowed to spare all laryngeal functions.

In conclusion, laryngeal NE neoplasms may present a considerable diagnostic challenge as their clinical presentation can be nonspecific. The described case shows that a rare NE tumor G1 can mimic a typical contact granuloma not only in the macroscopic aspect but also as far as the symptoms and clinical history are concerned. Clinicians should be aware of this rare condition that can be a diagnostic challenge.

Laryngeal NE tumors are extremely uncommon and heterogeneous neoplasms. The biological behavior and consequent prognosis greatly vary depending on the pathological features. Therefore, an accurate evaluation of the specimen by experienced pathologists is mandatory, and follow‐up steps should be determined on the basis of a multidisciplinary tumor board evaluation.
