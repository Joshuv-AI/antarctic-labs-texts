TwySHE—an mHealth peer-navigator intervention to increase PrEP and contraceptive use among female university students in Zambia: study protocol of a hybrid effectiveness-implementation randomized controlled trial
Authors: K. Hampanda, A. Amstutz, L. Nayame, M. Sehrt, T. Hamoonga, J. A. Menon, I. Mumbula, M. Mulawa, E. Nkole, F. J. Matenga, M. Mugode, O. Mweemba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHIV and pregnancy complications are the leading causes of death among adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) in Eastern and Southern Africa (ESA). In this region, AGYW aged 15–24 years accounted for over 60% of new HIV infections in 2022. This region also has the highest rates of unintended pregnancy globally and an adolescent birth rate twice the global average. Female students in ESA are a neglected, high-risk subpopulation for HIV and unintended pregnancy. Despite high-risk sexual behaviors among female university students in ESA, few behavioral interventions have targeted the prevention of HIV and unintended pregnancies in this population. This study aims to determine the effect and implementation of an mhealth peer navigator intervention on female students’ pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) and contraception use at a large public university in Lusaka, Zambia.MethodsAfter a campus-wide recruitment survey, we will 1:1 randomize 324 female university students aged 18–24 at high risk for HIV to the TwySHE intervention, consisting of one-to-one peer navigator support (HIV/sexual and reproductive health (SRH) education, risk awareness, informed decision making, and accessing health services) for 6 months facilitated by a mobile application; or the control (receipt of a list of local SRH services). Behavioral surveys will be done at baseline, 3, 6, and 12 months. The primary outcome is self-reported PrEP use by month 6 verified by a health provider. Secondary outcomes are modern contraception use by month 6, and PrEP and contraception persistence. Study records and exit interviews will capture factors related to the intervention’s reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, and maintenance.DiscussionTrainings for the research team and peer navigators delivering the intervention are complete. The trial began enrolling participants on March 14th, 2025. As of May 6, 2025, 105 participants have been enrolled. The findings of this study will establish the effectiveness and implementation of a novel peer navigator mhealth intervention to increase PrEP and modern contraception use and persistence. If effective, the intervention has the potential to ultimately contribute to efforts in Zambia and elsewhere to reduce unintended pregnancy and HIV acquisition among high-risk sub-populations of in-school AGYW.Trial registration {2a; 2b}The study has been prospectively registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06852508). Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Adolescent girls and young women (AGYW) in Eastern and Southern Africa (ESA) are extremely vulnerable to both HIV infection and unintended pregnancy, leading to adverse health outcomes and increased socioeconomic vulnerabilities across the life course. In Zambia, the study setting, there were an estimated 4 new HIV infections per 1000 uninfected people aged 15 to 24 in 2021, the seventh highest incidence among youth globally [1]. In Zambia, HIV prevalence is 3 times higher among AGYW aged 15–24, compared to their male counterparts (6% versus 2%) [2]. This trend peaks in the age group of 20 to 24-year-olds, where HIV prevalence is four times higher among females (9%) compared to males (2%) [2]. In addition to the high burden of HIV, more than 28% of girls aged 15–19 have given birth in Zambia, and unsafe abortions contribute to 15% of maternal deaths in the country [2, 3].

Young women at Higher Learning Institutions (HLIs) in ESA are a neglected, high-risk sub-population of AGYW [4–7]. In Zambia, HIV prevalence is higher among women with more education: HIV prevalence is over 15% among women with secondary education or higher compared to less than 11% among women with no education [8]. In South Africa, a survey with over 1200 unmarried female university students found that the prevalence of unplanned pregnancy was over 12% [9]. Despite high levels of sexual activity among university students, studies from the ESA region report suboptimal condom and family planning utilization [10, 11]. Our prior observational research {18a.1} with over 400 sexually active female university students found that more than 30% were not using any form of contraception to prevent pregnancy and < 1% were using oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)—one of the most efficacious biomedical HIV prevention interventions currently on the market [12, 13]. In 2023, a qualitative study from 12 HLIs across three provinces in Zambia found that students reported that sexual and reproductive health (SRH) healthcare services in their educational institutions were inaccessible, had limited comprehensive care, experienced negative interactions with providers, and lacked privacy/confidentiality. Health care providers in the study further reported inadequate youth-friendly spaces, lack of medications, and a shortage of trained workers, which compromised the quality of care for students [14]. Female university students in our preliminary research have cited stigma, fears of being seen as promiscuous, lack of awareness on where to find specific products, and inadequate youth-friendly services as barriers to HIV/SRH services [15].

There is an urgent need for innovative solutions to increase access, uptake, and persistence of PrEP and modern contraception among female university students in settings like Zambia [12]. Many factors contribute to AGYW’s access to and decision to use PrEP or modern contraception [16] but peer influence is especially important at this stage of life [17–22]. Utilizing peers to reinforce key messages about PrEP and support access to reliable SRH information and services for AGYW is one of the key recommendations to emerge from the 2021 Think Tank meeting held by UNICEF, the Global Fund, and other partners on ‘Improving the Quality of PrEP Implementation for Adolescent Girls and Young Women in Eastern and Southern Africa’ [23]. Qualitative studies from Malawi, Kenya, and Uganda additionally have found that peer support can facilitate both PrEP and contraceptive use among AGYW [22, 24]. The recent proliferation of mobile platforms in this region and high acceptance among youth/adolescents additionally has tremendous potential to enhance HIV/SRH interventions targeting AGYW [25–27].

Choice of comparators {6b}

While mobile health (mhealth) technologies and peer-delivered interventions are promising approaches to promote HIV/SRH among AGYW in ESA, no randomized controlled trial (RCT) to our knowledge has tested the effect of an mhealth peer navigator approach to promote PrEP and contraception use among female university students in ESA [26, 28]. To fill this gap, we co-designed an mhealth peer navigator intervention. The intervention will be compared to treatment as usual to increase PrEP and contraception use among female university students in Lusaka, Zambia.

Objectives {7}

The overall objective of the study is to evaluate whether the TwySHE peer-navigator intervention—facilitated by a mobile app—will improve PrEP and contraception use and persistence among female university students who are sexually active and at high HIV risk in Lusaka, Zambia. This will be accomplished through the following specific aims: (1) determine the effectiveness of the mHealth peer navigator intervention to promote PrEP and contraception use among high-risk female students in Zambia; (2) establish underlying mechanisms on the pathway between the intervention and SRH outcomes; and (3) explore implementation factors of the mHealth peer navigator intervention based on the RE-AIM framework [29].

Trial design {8}

We are conducting a 1:1 individual-randomized, open-label single-site hybrid type 1 effectiveness-implementation trial to determine the effectiveness of the TwySHE intervention to increase the use of PrEP (primary outcome) and modern contraception (secondary outcome) among sexually active female university students in Lusaka, Zambia, along with persistence and implementation-related factors (exploratory outcomes). We will follow all participants for 12 months with regular data collection. All intervention activities will end at 6 months (primary endpoint window), but we will continue to follow and survey participants in both study arms to 12 months to determine long-term post-intervention effectiveness to maintain behaviors. Exit interviews with peer navigators who delivered the intervention, and a sub-sample of participants will explore the implementation outcomes of the trial, along with review of study documents.

Methods

Participants, interventions, and outcomes

Study setting {9}

Zambia has a generalized HIV epidemic with a high prevalence of 11.0% among those 15–59 years and an annual incidence of 0.3% (0.1% among men and 0.6% among women) [2]. Zambian AGYW experience a high rate of unintended pregnancy (45%) [30], due to a high unmet need for contraception (50–70%) [8]. In 2016, the Zambian Ministry of Health adopted the 2015 WHO guidelines recommending daily oral PrEP for people at substantial risk of HIV infection, including AGYW in ESA [8, 31]. Gilead’s Truvada daily oral pill was approved in Zambia in 2017. In 2018, the national roll-out and scale-up of oral PrEP in Zambia began. ViiV’s Apretude long-acting cabotegravir (CAB-LA injectable) was approved in 2023. All government-run health centers in Lusaka offer modern contraceptives, oral PrEP, and are increasingly able to offer CAB-LA.

The proposed study will take place at the largest university in Zambia, the University of Zambia (UNZA), a public university located in the capital city of Lusaka with a student body of over 17,000. The university has two campuses separated by about 8 km: the Great East Road Campus, which houses most undergraduate programs, and the Ridgeway Campus (i.e., medical campus), which houses most medical and public health programs for both undergraduate and graduate students. Both campuses have on-site SRH/HIV health services. The Great East Road Campus has a comprehensive health center while the Ridgeway campus offers a smaller, more limited set of services at a “sick bay.” There are more than a dozen health centers, including government and private facilities, and numerous pharmacies within 10 km of the campuses. During our preliminary research, we established relationships with the nearby clinics to (a) ensure their buy-in and support of the intervention and (b) create a comprehensive list of youth friendly HIV/SRH services and the clinic provider contacts for our peer navigators delivering the intervention.

Eligibility criteria {10}

To participate in the trial, individuals must be female, 18 to 24 years of age, currently enrolled as university students at the recruitment site, meet the criteria for being high HIV-risk, and not currently using PrEP. “High risk” is defined as having three or more risk factors on a validated HIV Risk Index [32] (Table 1), which is significantly associated with the WHO epidemiologic HIV high-risk threshold [33, 34]. Individuals who are male, less than 18 years of age, older than 24 years of age, not currently enrolled at the university, currently using PrEP, or who score low risk (2 or fewer risk factors) on the HIV Risk Index are not eligible for participation. HIV status is not part of the inclusion criteria. While the focus of the trial is on promoting PrEP for individuals not living with HIV who are high HIV risk, we are not excluding individuals living with HIV from participation in an effort to be anti-discriminatory, HIV status neutral, and inclusive. Our team felt that individuals living with HIV could also benefit from the peer navigator intervention (e.g., navigating the healthcare system and receiving social support for antiretroviral therapy (ART) use, adherence, and persistence). Participants living with HIV, however, will not be included in the primary statistical analysis (described in detail below).

Interventions {11a}

Control

Participants randomized into the control arm will receive a list of local youth-friendly HIV/SRH service locations. They will also be informed of the longitudinal data collection process and their participation in data collection activities through 12 months.

TwySHE peer navigator intervention

In the intervention group, peer navigators (n = 14) will promote PrEP and contraception use/persistence among participants using the TywSHE mobile apps (described in detail below) as a platform to facilitate education, warm referrals, navigating the health care system, tracking participant uptake and persistence of PrEP/contraception, alerts for check-ins, and to send notifications to study participants. After enrollment and assignment into the intervention arm, peer navigators will be randomly assigned to each participant. The peer navigator will reach out to the participant to coordinate a time and place to meet. During the first visit (in-person), peer navigators focus on building rapport and a trusting relationship with the participant. The session includes getting to know the participant; describing the project in further detail; the role of the peer navigator; explaining expectations of the relationship, including the importance of maintaining confidentiality, and behaving ethically; and introducing the participant to the TwySHE Messenger app.

During the second visit (in-person) and subsequent sessions (in-person or mobile communication), the peer navigators will focus on encouraging, supporting, and helping build the skills of their participants to address their HIV/SRH needs, provide referrals and linkage to available services, and follow up to ensure participants access those services. The focus of the second visit is to complete the comprehensive SRH intake (i.e., “needs assessment”) in the main peer navigator app so the peer navigator understands the needs of the participant and best ways to support her. The needs assessment includes history of contraception use and contraception interest, as well as history of HIV testing, and PrEP use and interest if HIV-negative or ART use if HIV-positive. The needs assessment also enables the peer navigator to assess and capture participant HIV/SRH preferences, including their interest in receiving more information about certain products, their intention to use PrEP and contraception products, and if interested, service locations to obtain services. Lastly, the peer navigator will use the app to assess and document each participant’s desired support from the peer navigator in the future.

Subsequent visits will be conducted as needed, depending on the participant, but some form of communication needs to occur at least twice per month during the six-month intervention period. TwySHE mobile applications and control panel

Main TwySHE mobile app: this version of the mobile app is only accessible to peer navigators and includes the needs and preferences assessment, tracking client appointments and HIV/SRH product uptake, follow-up reminders, HIV/SRH information, and the location and direct contact of providers at local youth-friendly SRH clinics, including the specific services offered. Screen usage analytics are captured in the app. These capture the screens the peer navigator visits in the app and the amount of time they spend on that screen. This information is captured automatically and there is no input or action required from the peer navigator. The main app stores data in a secure database (i.e., control panel, described below) locally which cannot be accessed by other apps or programs. The app is protected with a custom PIN and only communicates with an authorized server when uploading data captured by the peer navigator. The communication is done over a secure connection (HTTPS) using the latest security standards such as SSL Certificates and API tokens.

Messenger TwySHE app: The messenger version of the app is for use by both the peer navigators and participants. The messenger version includes one-on-one private messaging between peer navigators and their assigned participants, anonymous discussion boards for all participants and peer navigators, HIV/SRH information, and the location of local youth-friendly SRH services. Each week, one peer navigator is assigned to oversee and monitor a relevant SRH discussion in the app to promote user engagement. The messenger app requires login via phone number verification. The verification, database and chat features of the app are secured using Googles Secure Firebase Platform. Firebase offers multiple security features including data encryption, audit logging, and role-based access control. This makes Google’s Firebase platform one of the most secure cloud-based development tools for modern applications.

TwySHE control panel: the control panel is a web-based application that is designed to allow research team members to view all data being captured by the main TwySHE app and messenger version in one place. It populates a central online and secure database with the ability to export Excel files. The source code for the control panel is published on GitHub [35, 36]. Functions in the control panel include seeing all registered peer navigators, active devices, enrolled participants, SRH services accessed by participants, future appointments and medication refills for participants, interactions between participants and peer navigators, chats and discussions within the app, and app usage and analytics. From the control panel, notifications and messages can also be sent to participants and peer navigators.

Criteria for discontinuation {11b; 21b}

The only criteria for study discontinuation would be at the participant’s request or if they became unable to continue participation due to relocation or severe physical or psychological morbidity or mortality.

Strategies to improve adherence to intervention protocols {11c; 18b}

In December 2023, we recruited and interviewed 20 applicants for the peer navigator role. In January 2024, eighteen applicants progressed and participated in a 4-day online training, including a pre- and post-knowledge and perceptions test. After scoring and assessing participation in the training, fifteen women were invited to proceed in the process and participated in an additional 3-day online training in March 2024. We hired 14 of the most dedicated and top-performing women as peer navigators, who delivered the intervention and used the mobile apps in a pilot study from April to June 2024. The UNZA HIV Response Unit Program Manager, along with our Study Coordinator, oversaw the day-to-day study activities of the peer navigators during the pilot study. In July 2024, the peer navigators participated in an extensive 2 week in-person training to debrief on their pilot study experience, review refinements to the intervention and apps, and to prepare for delivering the intervention in the RCT. The Study Medical Cards we will use to verify participant uptake of PrEP were created in partnership with health providers at the clinics near campus that students regularly attend. During trial start-up activities, providers were contacted and sensitized about the use of the cards. Participants will be provided instructions on the use of the card during the enrollment visit. Participants will be reimbursed for their participation in all data collection activities.

Procedures for monitoring adherence {11c}

We will track intervention fidelity by measuring the number of interactions (in-person, phone, mobile messaging) that each participant in the intervention receives (there should be at least two interactions/check-ins per month for 6 months for a total of at least 12 interactions). Tracking will be done via the TwySHE main app, which is stored and accessible in the control panel, and through peer navigator reports/logs, which are reviewed weekly by the Study Coordinator. The Data Manager provides weekly reports on the number of participants in the database, which is cross-checked with the Study Coordinator’s logs.

Relevant concomitant, ancillary, and post-trial care {11d; 30}

Participants are allowed to receive any concomitant care. This is a behavioral intervention that seeks to help women access existing SRH/HIV standard of care services; no medical care is offered directly through the intervention. There is no provision for ancillary and post-trial care, or for compensation to those who suffer harm from trial participation given the low risk of this behavioral intervention trial.

Sample size {14; 14.1}

We estimate that 5% of participants in the control arm will be using PrEP by month 6, based on recent data from the setting [37–40], and hypothesize that this proportion will increase by 10% to 15% in the intervention. Based on a 2-sided type I error of 5% for two independent groups and an inflation of 15% for potential drop-out across both arms [41], the trial requires an initial sample size of 324 participants and a final sample size of 282 participants [42–47]. With 141 per arm, and a small to moderate standardized effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.34), the sample will achieve 80% power. To achieve our initial target sample size of 324 high-risk participants, we will sample 810 participants for the initial recruitment survey, assuming that 40% will meet the criteria for being high-risk and eligible for randomization based on prior estimates [48, 49]. All ineligible low-risk female participants will be offered a list of SRH services in the area but not invited for further participation.

Recruitment {15}

Beginning in March 2025, currently enrolled female-identifying UNZA students ≥ 18 years of age will be invited to complete an online survey in English. We will partner with the university’s medical services and existing peer educators on campus to distribute flyers with the survey QR code and REDCap website link. A virtual flyer will also be distributed to student WhatsApp groups accessed through university administration. After survey postcard consent and screening questions (age, gender, current student), participants will complete a brief online survey (~10 min). All data will be collected and stored in REDCap. Survey participants will provide their contact number and indicate their interest in being contacted for participation in additional research (i.e., RCT). The Data Manager will review all new survey responses on a weekly basis and provide a list—on a password protected document—to the Zambian Study Coordinator each week with the names and contact information of eligible participants based on their score on the HIV Risk Index and other inclusion criteria. The Study Coordinator and research assistants will then contact each eligible participant and coordinate a time for the informed consent process (described in detail below in the “Ethics and dissemination” section).

Assignment of interventions

Allocation, sequence generation, and implementation {16a; 16b; 16c; 17a; 17b}

Each eligible participant will be randomized 1:1 into either (1) the peer navigator mHealth TwySHE intervention (n = 162) or control (n = 162). For randomization, we first created a list of 324 unique participant identification (PID) numbers. Each PID number was randomly assigned to the control or intervention arm using a random assignment generator software, GraphPad [50]. Each unique PID number is written onto one of 324 Study Medical Visit Cards (described in detail below). The PID number written on the card does not indicate which arm the participant is assigned. After informed consent and enrollment, the research assistant randomly selects a Study Medical Visit Card from the pile of all cards, which have been mixed, and gives the card to each participant. The research assistants are blinded and do not know which PIDs are affiliated with the study arms at the time the card is selected and given to the participant. After randomly selecting a card, the research assistant then enters the specified PID on the card into a REDCap Enrollment and Randomization Log using a mobile device. The research assistant is then unblinded and informed within the REDCap form (based on a programmed skip pattern) which study arm the participant is assigned. If the research assistant is not able to be online due to internet/wifi problems, they record the participant PID onto a physical log and later enter it into REDCap and follow up with participants to inform them of the study arm and next steps, including sending them the online baseline survey. After enrollment and study arm randomization, participants and research staff are no longer blinded; however, the data analyst will be blinded. Unblinding of the data analyst will occur after primary analysis is complete.

Data collection, management, and analysis

Data collection and management {18a; 18b; 19}

Behavioral surveys: At baseline, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months, all participants will be asked to complete online (REDCap) surveys to capture indicators of interest (Fig. 2). The baseline survey is administered in-person immediately following informed consent and enrollment on a tablet. After the research assistant has entered the participant details into the Enrollment and Randomization Log, the tablet is given to the participant to complete the baseline survey, which is linked to their longitudinal study record. For the follow-up surveys, an online link to the survey will be sent via WhatsApp message to participants’ phone numbers using the TwySHE control panel. Follow-up surveys will be sent for each longitudinal time point (3 months, 6 months, and 12 months). Automated weekly reminders to complete the survey will be sent for 4 weeks after each time point. Participants will be reimbursed a small amount after completion of each survey to promote high response rates. All data from the surveys will be stored on a REDCap database housed on a secure server. Exit interviews—Using RE-AIM [58] as a guiding implementation framework, we will interview all peer navigators (n = 14) and a subsample of participants from the intervention arm (n = 20) to explore factors related to the intervention’s reach, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, and maintenance within our trial and considerations for future studies. Experienced local qualitative interviewers will lead the interviews in English, the official language in Zambia, using a semi-structured interview guide. Participants will be reimbursed a small amount after completion of the interviews {28}. All interviews will be audio recorded, with express consent of the participants, and transcribed verbatim in Avidnote [59]. Transcripts will then be checked for accuracy by our qualitative analysis team, deidentified, and analyzed in Avidnote using a summary table template.

Analytic plan {20a; 20a.1; 20b; 20c}

Data collected from the app (SRH service and peer navigator support preferences, number and type of interactions with the peer navigator, etc.) will be merged with the survey data to create a final dataset. Baseline characteristics are presented by randomized group with medians and interquartile range for continuous variables and numbers and percentages for categorical variables.

Primary outcome: The primary endpoint (PrEP use by month 6) will be analyzed according to the intention-to-treat principle. We formulate our overall target question as well as intercurrent events during the follow-up following the estimands framework [60]: (1) a composite strategy for study withdrawals and deaths (i.e., “worst case imputation”), (2) a principal stratum strategy for lost-to-follow-up, which matches our sample size calculation, (3) a principal stratum strategy for those acquiring HIV during the follow-up, which matches our outcome definition.

We will use a modified Poisson regression model with a log link, adjusted for age, to estimate the risk difference and risk ratios with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals using robust standard errors. We prespecified the following sensitivity analyses on the primary endpoint: [1] we will run the same analysis but only among participants with a verified Study Medical Visit Card indicating PrEP dispensing; [2] we will explore the impact of using a principal stratum strategy for lost-to-follow-up by using a hypothetical strategy assuming no one was lost-to-follow-up; [3] we do not expect missing data on the primary endpoint using the outlined estimands framework analysis strategy; however, if there is, we will use multiple imputation by chained equations assuming missing at random [61]. We will conduct a per-protocol analysis on the primary endpoint using appropriate causal inference methods, such as inverse probability weighting, to account for non-adherence to the intervention over time [62].

Secondary effectiveness outcomes: We will use the same analysis strategy as outlined for the primary outcome.

Secondary implementation outcomes: These outcomes will be analyzed descriptively and using appropriate regression models with 95% confidence intervals to support interpretation but without formal hypothesis testing. Thus, we will not do any further adjustments for multiple testing.

Exploratory structural equation modeling (SEM): To explore potential variables on the pathway between the intervention and study outcome, Bootstrap mediation tests through structural equation modeling (SEM) will estimate the direct and indirect effects between intervention exposure, hypothesized underlying mechanisms (e.g., knowledge, health beliefs, stigma, social norms and support, behavioral intentions and self-efficacy) and the study outcomes. SEM model fit statistics will include model chi-square; root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA); comparative fit index (CFI); and Tucker-Lewis index (TLI).

Qualitative analysis: Transcripts from the exit interviews will be analyzed using a rapid thematic analysis process in Avidnote [63]. First, we will create a templated summary table with pre-specified domains based on pre-identified research questions along the RE-AIM framework, key points, and illustrative quotes. The table will be programmed into Avidnote for AI data abstraction and summaries. After each interview, the templated summary table will be used to generate one summary for each transcript. We will establish intercoder reliability in Avidnote by comparing templated tables completed by our qualitative analyst with tables generated from Avidnote. Revisions will be made within Avidnote until > 90% intercoder reliability is achieved. After completion of summaries for each transcript, we will consolidate all the summaries for visual display in a prioritization matrix to organize broad themes/categories, brief sub-theme/descriptors, and illustrative quotes, and to draw conclusions.

Monitoring

Data monitoring {21a}, confidentiality {27}, and access to data {29}

Recruitment goals will be carefully monitored by the Study Coordinator and the PI. We will also monitor the presence of any missing data and loss to follow-up to ensure the validity and integrity of the data. We will closely track incoming numbers of participants via ongoing recruitment activities, and weekly and monthly reports will be compiled by the study staff. We will utilize similar procedures to monitor any missing data or missed follow-up assessments. All study data will be coded with a participant study ID to maximize confidentiality. The codes that link the name of the participant and the study ID will be kept confidential in a secured database accessible only by the PI and limited research staff. All survey study data will be collected and managed using REDCap. REDCap is a secure web application designed to support data capture for research studies, providing user-friendly web-based case report forms, real-time data entry validation (e.g., for data types and range checks), audit trails and a de-identified data export mechanism to common statistical packages. The database is hosted at the University of Colorado–Denver Development and Informatics Service Center, which will be used as a central location for data processing and management. Laptops and handheld devices will be encrypted, password protected and backed up daily. Tablets will be used to collect much of the data via the REDCap, which allows data to be entered and securely stored on the device, and later, upon internet connectivity, sent to the host server. The PI and Study Coordinator will provide ongoing monitoring of the study progress. All data will be kept confidential, only accessible to limited study members (PI, Study Coordinator, Data Manager, Statisitician), with exception of limits to confidentiality legally required to be reported to law enforcement in Zambia, such as child abuse. The trial has been determined by the funder and institutional review boards to be low risk; thus, a data monitoring committee (DMC) is not needed. There are no planned interim analyses with stopping guidelines {21b}.

Harms {22}

Safety-related risks to participants (female university students) could consist of (1) emotional discomfort or distress resulting from sensitive questions being asked pertaining to HIV, pregnancy, HIV prevention medication, contraceptives, and related social situations and/or relationships; (2) social risk (e.g., risk to reputation) if confidential information revealed during the course of the study were to be disclosed outside of the research team; (3) tension, conflict or violence; and (4) risks associated with participation in the intervention activities (clinic visits, meetings with peer navigators). Detailed protocols will be in place regarding the possibility of tension, conflict, or violence between participants and intimate partners should the partners discover the study participation of the participants. Protocols will be in place to monitor and respond to any instances reported by the participants of any type of negative relationship or other social event, and proper action will be taken, including the provision of referral for services depending on the specific event and participant preferences.

Auditing {23}

All safety-related risks will be monitored routinely, including during recruitment and enrollment, intervention sessions, and data collection. The intervention has been carefully designed to ensure privacy, including protecting confidential information (e.g., HIV status, medication use) from others outside of the study and to monitor all confidential information to protect subject privacy and inadvertent social harms. During study procedures, at any sign of participant discomfort or distress, or by request, activities will be halted and applicable support and referrals (e.g., mental health services) will be provided.

Discussion

While peer-delivered interventions appear to have tremendous potential, there remains a need for rigorous evaluation of the effectiveness of peer-navigator SRH approaches, with well-understood components, on health outcomes among AGYW [64–67]. To our knowledge, no study has successfully integrated mHealth technology into an HIV/SRH peer navigator intervention package and rigorously evaluated outcomes and underlying mechanisms in a high HIV prevalence low- and middle-income country setting. mHealth technologies have traditionally been used for self-management of behavior change at the individual level; yet there are numerous potential applications for use by the health workforce, including lay health workers/peer-navigators [68]. A recent systematic review highlighted high acceptability of mHealth among health workers and evidence that these tools can improve communication with clients, health worker compliance with protocols, and the efficiency and quality of data collection [68]. The results of this study will establish whether an innovative mHealth technology to support a peer-delivered PrEP and contraception intervention for young women is an effective and sustainable HIV/SRH model.

A potential risk to the study is if we encounter challenges in achieving our desired sample size during the projected timeline. If this occurs, we will explore with our Zambian university collaborators additional avenues of recruitment, including presentations during large class lectures and other events on campus. We have at present surveyed 805 female students in the campus-wide survey, of whom 384 meet the eligibility criteria. Our target is to enroll 15 participants each week. Of the 200 who have been contacted, none have declined participation during informed consent. Thus, we do not anticipate major challenges to enrollment. Loss-to-follow-up could impact study success; however, we have accounted for this in our power and statistical analysis and have designed the trial to maximize acceptability and retention through an extensive human-centered design process and refinements after a pilot study [69]. Contamination among study arms could be an issue; however, we have carefully designed the study and have trained all peer navigators delivering the intervention to ensure adherence to protocols. While it is feasible that some participants in the control arm may interact with those in the intervention arm and learn information about HIV/SRH, those in the control arm do not have access to the TwySHE apps or peer navigators.

Recent studies outside of Zambia have documented the separate benefits of peer-navigators and mHealth technologies to improve SRH-related behaviors among AGYW [22, 24, 28]. This RCT builds upon previous studies [70] and explores whether a peer-navigator intervention, supported through a mobile platform, can improve longitudinal uptake and persistence of both PrEP and modern contraceptives among a subpopulation of young women at high risk of HIV and unintended pregnancy in Zambia. If effective, our peer-navigator mHealth intervention has the potential to be a model to promote other PrEP products among AGYW that are in the pipeline, including antiretroviral implants, long-acting injectables, and intra-vaginal rings, as well as multiple-purpose technologies (MPTs) that offer combined protection against unintended pregnancy and HIV [71].
