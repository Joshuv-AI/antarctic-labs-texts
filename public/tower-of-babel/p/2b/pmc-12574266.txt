Novel EP300 and NSD1 variants in Chinese pediatric patients with Rubinstein-Taybi syndrome: evidence for oligogenic inheritance and phenotypic expansion
Authors: Xu Ningan, Wang Yefeng, Wu Xinghan, Zhao Sha
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRubinstein–Taybi syndrome (RSTS) is an autosomal-dominant neurodevelopmental disorder caused by heterozygous loss-of-function variants in CREBBP or EP300. EP300-related cases often show milder clinical features, but their full phenotypic spectrum and the potential role of oligogenic inheritance remain unclear.MethodsWe retrospectively studied seven Chinese pediatric RSTS patients. Genomic analyses included whole-exome sequencing (WES), chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) and Sanger validation. Variants were classified by ACMG/AMP guidelines. Protein structural impacts were assessed by homology modeling. Phenotypic frequencies in our EP300-related patients were compared with published European/American cohorts using Fisher’s exact test or χ² test (p < 0.05 as significant).ResultsTwo de novo EP300 nonsense variants (c.3934 C > T, p.Arg1312Ter; c.2749 C > T, p.Gln917Ter) were identified; one of these patients also harbored a de novo NSD1 missense variant (c.5843G > A, p.Arg1948His). Five other patients carried pathogenic CREBBP variants or deletions. The patient with both EP300 and NSD1 variants exhibited additional anomalies (cryptorchidism, congenital cataracts, strabismus), suggesting synergistic epigenetic dysregulation. Homology models predicted truncation of the EP300 HAT and bromodomain regions and disruption of the NSD1 SET domain. Compared with European/American cohorts, our Chinese EP300-related patients had significantly higher rates of arched eyebrows, downslanting palpebral fissures and low-set ears (p < 0.05). To our knowledge, this is the first clinical evidence of an oligogenic inheritance pattern in RSTS involving both EP300 and NSD1.ConclusionsOur findings expand the genotypic and phenotypic landscape of RSTS in a Chinese population, delineate distinctions between EP300- and CREBBP-related cases, and underscore the value of comprehensive genomic analysis for accurate diagnosis, genetic counselling and personalized management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12920-025-02220-6.

Introduction

Rubinstein–Taybi syndrome (RSTS) is a rare autosomal dominant neurodevelopmental disorder characterized by distinctive facial dysmorphisms, broad thumbs and halluces, intellectual disability, and postnatal growth retardation, with an estimated incidence of approximately one in 100,000 to 125,000 live births [1–3]. The disorder arises from heterozygous loss-of-function variants in the histone acetyltransferases CREBBP and EP300. Approximately fifty to sixty% of cases result from CREBBP variants and an additional five to ten% from EP300 variants [2, 4–6]. These enzymes regulate chromatin remodeling and transcription by acetylating lysine residues on histone N-terminal tails, thereby modulating genes essential for craniofacial, skeletal, and neurological development [7, 8].

Although CREBBP and EP300 share considerable functional homology, accumulating evidence indicates they perform distinct, non-redundant roles during development. EP300-related RSTS often manifests a milder and more variable phenotype than CREBBP-related cases, with subtler facial features and less severe skeletal anomalies [6, 9]. However, precise genotype–phenotype correlations for specific EP300 variants—especially in non-Caucasian populations, remain poorly defined. Moreover, emerging data suggest that oligogenic inheritance involving additional epigenetic regulators like the histone methyltransferase NSD1, may further modulate disease severity and phenotypic diversity [10].

To address these gaps, we conducted a comprehensive clinical and genomic analysis of seven Chinese pediatric patients with RSTS. Our aims were to characterize the spectrum of EP300 and CREBBP variants, refine genotype–phenotype correlations within this cohort, and explore the potential role of oligogenic inheritance in RSTS heterogeneity. These findings will expand the variants and phenotypic landscape of RSTS in an underrepresented population and provide a foundation for improved diagnostic and management strategies.

Methods

Patient cohort

This retrospective case series study included seven Chinese pediatric patients diagnosed with RSTS at Hunan Children’s Hospital between January 2022 and December 2023. The inclusion criteria were: (1) a clinical diagnosis of RSTS based on established criteria (characteristic facial features, broad thumbs/halluces, intellectual disability, and/or growth retardation) [1, 10]; (2) confirmation of the diagnosis through genetic testing (identification of pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in CREBBP, EP300, or a relevant chromosomal region like 16p13.3); and (3) Han Chinese ethnicity. Patients with other genetic syndromes or unclear diagnoses were excluded. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Hunan Children’s Hospital (HCHLL-2023-16). Written informed consent to participate was obtained from the parents/legal guardians of all minor participants.

Clinical data collection

Basic information of the patients was collected using the electronic medical record system, including the age at first diagnosis, clinical manifestations, growth and development history, maternal pregnancy and delivery history, physical examination results, laboratory test results, gene sequencing results, treatment, and prognosis.

Genetic analysis

DNA Extraction: Peripheral blood samples (2 mL, EDTA-anticoagulated) were collected from each patient and their parents. Genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA quality and quantity were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and agarose gel electrophoresis.

Whole-Exome Sequencing (WES): WES was performed by WuXi NextCODE (Shanghai, China). Exome capture was carried out using the Agilent SureSelect Human All Exon V6 kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Paired-end sequencing (150 bp read length) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to achieve an average sequencing depth of at least 100x.

Bioinformatic analysis and variant filtering

Raw sequencing data were processed using a standardized bioinformatics pipeline. Sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19/GRCh37) using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA-MEM, version 0.7.17) [11]. Duplicate reads were marked using Picard tools (version 2.20.2). Variant calling (single nucleotide variants [SNVs] and small insertions/deletions [indels]) were performed using the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK, version 4.1.4.1) HaplotypeCaller, following GATK Best Practices recommendations. Variant quality score recalibration (VQSR) was applied to filter for high-confidence variants [12]. Variants were annotated using ANNOVAR [13] and dbNSFP (version 4.3) [14] to obtain information from various databases, including: OMIM (Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man), ClinVar, HGMD (Human Gene Mutation Database), gnomAD (Genome Aggregation Database), Ensembl.

Variants were filtered based on the following criteria: Variants located in exonic or splicing regions (± 2 bp). Minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.01 in gnomAD (overall and East Asian populations) [15]. Variants predicted to be deleterious by at least three of the following in silico prediction tools: SIFT, PROVEAN, PolyPhen-2, MutationTaster, and CADD (with a CADD score ≥ 20).

Sanger sequencing validation

Candidate variants identified in EP300 and NSD1 were validated by Sanger sequencing using the primers listed below. Primers were designed using NCBI Primer-BLAST (sequences provided below). PCR amplification was performed using Taq DNA Polymerase (Takara Bio, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan). PCR products were purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen) and sequenced on an ABI 3730xl DNA Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Sequencing results were analyzed using SeqMan Pro software (DNASTAR, Madison, WI, USA) and compared to the reference sequences.

Copy number variation (CNV) analysis

CNVs were detected from WES data using CNVkit software (version 0.9.9)​ [16]​, which leverages on-target and off-target read depth to infer copy number changes. CNVkit outputs were visualized and manually reviewed in IGV (Integrative Genomics Viewer)​ [17]​. In Patient 3, a heterozygous deletion of CREBBP exons [14–16] was further confirmed by chromosomal microarray analysis (CMA) using the Affymetrix CytoScan HD platform (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Variant interpretation

Variants were classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) guidelines (2015) [18]. Variants were categorized as Pathogenic, Likely Pathogenic, Variant of Uncertain Significance (VUS), Likely Benign, or Benign. We also considered recommendations from the ClinGen Sequence Variant Interpretation Working Group, where available.

Protein structure prediction

To assess the potential structural impact of the identified variants, protein structure prediction was performed. Wild-type and mutant protein sequences for EP300 and NSD1 were obtained from the UniProt database (accession numbers: EP300 - Q09472, NSD1 - Q96L73). Three-dimensional protein models were generated using the SWISS-MODEL server (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/), a homology-modeling server [19]. The resulting models were visualized and analyzed using PyMOL (version 2.5, Schrödinger, LLC) to identify potential structural changes caused by the amino acid substitutions or truncations.

Statistical methods

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS v.26. Comparative phenotype frequencies between the Chinese cohort and reference cohorts were analyzed using Fisher’s exact test or chi-square (χ²) test, as appropriate. Fisher’s exact test was applied when any expected cell count was < 5. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical and genetic findings in a Chinese RSTS cohort

All seven patients exhibited core features of RSTS, with considerable genetic heterogeneity. Ages at diagnosis ranged from 2 months to 11 years, and birth weights varied between 2.1 kg and 3.4 kg; two patients were born prematurely (Table 1). WES, complemented by CMA for Patient 3 and Sanger sequencing for variant validation, identified a range of pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants in EP300 and CREBBP, a variant of uncertain significance in NSD1, and large deletions involving CREBBP or the 16p13.3 region (Table 2; Fig. 1). EP300-related RSTS: distinct phenotypes and an oligogenic inheritance

Two male patients harbored de novo nonsense variants in EP300. Patient 1, diagnosed at 10–11 years old, carried the c.3934 C > T (p.Arg1312Ter) variant in exon 24, as well as a de novo missense variant in NSD1 (c.5843G > A; p.Arg1948His). Although this NSD1 variant is currently classified as a variant of uncertain significance, its rarity and predicted deleteriousness suggest it may act as a genetic modifier. Patient 2, diagnosed at 2–3 years, carried a novel c.2749 C > T (p.Gln917Ter) variant in EP300 exon 14. Both variants were predicted to introduce premature stop codons, leading to truncated proteins lacking essential functional domains, including the HAT and bromodomain regions (Fig. 3A–C).

Clinically, both patients exhibited growth retardation and microcephaly, hallmarks of RSTS. However, their phenotypic presentations diverged. Patient 1, with the combined EP300 and NSD1 variants, displayed a more complex phenotype, including cryptorchidism, congenital cataracts, and strabismus (Fig. 2A–C). In contrast, Patient 2, with the isolated EP300 variant, presented primarily with growth retardation, microcephaly, and downslanting palpebral fissures, a phenotype more consistent with the milder end of the RSTS spectrum often associated with EP300 variants (Fig. 2D–F).Fig. 2Clinical features of two patients with EP300-related RSTS. Furthermore, although heterozygous NSD1 variants are classically associated with Sotos syndrome, Patient 1 did not exhibit any of the syndrome’s cardinal features. His height and head circumference remained below − 2 standard deviations for age, bone age assessment matched his chronological age without evidence of advanced skeletal maturation, and there was no history of neonatal hypotonia or feeding difficulties. In addition, his facial gestalt lacked the coarse morphology typical of Sotos syndrome; he did not display frontal bossing, downturned mouth corners, or macrocephaly. These observations indicate that the NSD1 p.Arg1948His variant in this patient does not drive a full-blown Sotos syndrome phenotype but is more consistent with a role as a genetic modifier in the context of EP300 haploinsufficiency.

CREBBP-related RSTS: phenotypic variability and genotype-phenotype correlations

Variants in CREBBP were identified in five patients, illustrating the broad phenotypic variability associated with this gene. Patient 3, a female diagnosed at 2–3 years, had a heterozygous deletion of exons 14–16 confirmed by CMA. Her features included inability to walk independently, overlapping toes, high-arched palate, growth retardation, and microcephaly; these overlapped partially with Menke-Hennekam syndrome. Patients 4 and 5, both males under 1 year of age, had larger CREBBP deletions (exons 5–30 and 1–27, respectively) detected by WES-based CNV analysis. Patient 4 exhibited hypertelorism and limb anomalies, while patient 5 presented with failure to thrive, recurrent pneumonia, and hypertelorism. Such extensive deletions likely caused more severe CREBBP haploinsufficiency, leading to complex malformations.

Patient 6, a male aged 3–4 years, harbored a frameshift variant in CREBBP (c.2068del; p.Thr690ArgfsTer20), classified as pathogenic per ACMG guidelines, and presented with growth retardation, developmental delays, and feeding difficulties. Patient 7, a male under 1 year, had an approximately 2.0 Mb heterozygous deletion encompassing the entire 16p13.3 region, including CREBBP, and manifested cardiovascular anomalies, recurrent respiratory infections, hearing loss, and retinal abnormalities, reflecting the multisystem involvement of large genomic deletions.

Protein structure modeling: insights into variant impact

To elucidate the potential effects of the detected variants on protein function, we performed three-dimensional structural modeling (Fig. 3). The truncating variants in EP300 (Patients 1 and 2) resulted in predicted loss of the HAT and bromodomains, which are critical for histone acetyltransferase activity and gene regulation (Fig. 3A–C) [20]. The CREBBP frameshift variant identified in patient 6 was also predicted to truncate key functional domains, consistent with a loss-of-function effect. Comparative phenotype analysis: Chinese vs. European/American cohorts

We compared the clinical characteristics of our EP300-related RSTS patients, together with previously reported Chinese cases, against published cohorts of primarily European/American ancestry (Table 3) [21]. While frequencies of most common RSTS features were comparable, arched eyebrows, downslanting palpebral fissures, and low-set ears were observed significantly more frequently in the Chinese cohort (p < 0.05). These results suggest potential population-specific modifiers influencing the phenotypic expression of EP300-related RSTS, meriting further investigation in larger, diverse cohorts.

Discussion

RSTS is a rare neurodevelopmental disorder primarily caused by pathogenic variants in CREBBP or EP300. While previous studies have described the genetic and clinical features of RSTS, data on EP300-related cases and the potential contribution of oligogenic inheritance remain limited in non-European populations. In this study, we performed detailed clinical evaluations alongside whole-exome sequencing, chromosomal microarray analysis, and Sanger validation in seven Chinese pediatric patients with RSTS. We identified both previously reported and novel pathogenic variants in CREBBP and EP300, and observed one patient carrying concurrent variants in EP300 and NSD1. These results broaden the understanding of the variants spectrum and phenotypic variability of RSTS within the Chinese population and raise the possibility that multi-gene interactions may influence clinical presentation. Our findings highlight the importance of comprehensive genomic diagnostics combined with careful clinical assessment to improve the recognition and management of RSTS.

Our findings further reinforce and extend previous reports indicating that EP300-related RSTS represents a clinically distinct subgroup when compared to classical CREBBP-related cases [2, 3, 6, 22]. In our cohort, patients carrying EP300 variants generally exhibited milder intellectual disability, subtler skeletal anomalies, and lacked the hallmark “grimacing” facial appearance and pronounced digital abnormalities frequently seen in CREBBP-driven RSTS. These differences in clinical presentation are supported by molecular studies demonstrating that, although both EP300 and CREBBP encode histone acetyltransferases involved in chromatin remodeling, they perform partially non-overlapping functions during development. CREBBP is critically involved in early embryonic events such as neural crest cell migration and differentiation, underpinning its association with more severe craniofacial and cardiac defects [8, 23–25]. In contrast, EP300 appears to function predominantly at later developmental stages, particularly in tissues like skeletal muscle and the central nervous system [7, 26]. The two truncating EP300 variants we identified are predicted to abolish protein function via haploinsufficiency, yet the resultant phenotype remains less severe than those caused by large CREBBP deletions or frameshift variants, highlighting the unique and partially overlapping biological roles of these genes. Collectively, our findings support the current consensus that EP300-related RSTS should be recognized as a unique clinical entity, which has important implications for both accurate diagnosis and appropriate genetic counseling.

A major novel contribution of our study is the identification of a patient harboring co-occurring de novo pathogenic variants in both EP300 and NSD1. To date, RSTS has largely been considered a monogenic disorder; however, our findings provide compelling preliminary evidence for an oligogenic mechanism, whereby multiple rare variants act synergistically to shape disease expressivity [9, 22]. Notably, while NSD1 variants are classically associated with Sotos syndrome, a disorder characterized by overgrowth, macrocephaly, advanced bone age, frontal bossing, and intellectual disability, our patient did not display these cardinal features. Review of his clinical records showed that his height and head circumference were persistently below − 2 SD, bone age was concordant with chronological age, and there was no evidence of neonatal hypotonia or feeding difficulties. Facial gestalt lacked the coarse features and frontal bossing typical of Sotos syndrome. Most pathogenic NSD1 variants causing classic Sotos syndrome are truncating or splice-site changes within critical functional domains, leading to profound loss of H3K36 methylation activity. In contrast, the p.Arg1948His missense variant identified in our case is located at the periphery of the SET domain, and structural modeling suggests it may only subtly alter enzymatic function rather than completely inactivating the protein.

Nonetheless, in the context of concomitant EP300 haploinsufficiency, this NSD1 variant may exert a modifier effect, broadening the clinical spectrum with features such as cryptorchidism, congenital cataracts, and strabismus, findings not typically observed in isolated EP300-RSTS. This highlights the increasingly recognized importance of gene–gene interactions and their capacity to drive blended or atypical neurodevelopmental phenotypes. Our results add to growing literature suggesting that oligogenic and epistatic effects, particularly among genes encoding chromatin regulators—may underlie a subset of complex neurodevelopmental disorders. Although functional validation is needed to clarify the precise molecular interplay between EP300 and NSD1, our work provides valuable clinical evidence supporting the existence and significance of oligogenic inheritance in RSTS.

An additional notable observation from our study was the increased frequency of certain craniofacial features, including arched eyebrows, downslanting palpebral fissures, and low-set ears, in EP300-related RSTS patients of Chinese ancestry compared to published European/American cohorts. While these differences may reflect genuine population-specific modifiers or background genetic variation, alternative explanations such as ascertainment bias and subtle discrepancies in clinical evaluation cannot be ruled out. Our study also has several limitations: the small sample size impedes robust statistical analysis and generalizability, especially regarding the oligogenic hypothesis based on a single index case; large deletions in some patients were detected only by WES-based CNV analysis without further orthogonal validation, which may limit breakpoint resolution; and the lack of functional assays or direct epigenetic studies, such as DNA methylation profiling, restricts our ability to confirm the proposed gene–gene interactions at the molecular level. Future multi-center research involving larger, ethnically diverse cohorts and integrated functional genomics will be essential to validate our findings, clarify the mechanisms underlying phenotypic variability, and assess the prevalence and impact of oligogenic inheritance in RSTS and related disorders.

Conclusion

In summary, our systematic analysis expands the genotypic and phenotypic spectrum of RSTS in the Chinese pediatric population, delineates the distinctiveness of EP300- versus CREBBP-related cases, and presents the first clinical evidence implicating oligogenic inheritance involving EP300 and NSD1. These insights highlight both the complexity and heterogeneity of chromatinopathies and emphasize the need for comprehensive genomic evaluation in unexplained or complex cases. Ultimately, our findings lay the groundwork for improving diagnostic strategies, genetic counseling, and individualized care for RSTS, while underscoring the value of collaborative and functional research to unravel the full spectrum of gene–gene interactions in neurodevelopmental disease.
