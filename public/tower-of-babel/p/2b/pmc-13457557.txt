Plate tectonic history and ocean oxygenation shaping biogeography of hydrothermal bacterial community
Authors: Weiguo Hou, Hailiang Dong, Hongyu Chen, Hanhui Liu, Yidi Zhang, Fangru Li, Xiqiu Han, Shang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe influence of plate tectonics on microbial distributions remains poorly understood. Here, we demonstrate that the global biogeography of hydrothermal vent-endemic chemoautotrophic microbiota is structured by the tectonic history of the global major oceans. These microbiota, particularly anaerobic ones, are significantly more abundant in early-origin Pacific, Arctic, and Mediterranean oceans, whereas they are notably scarce in late-formed Atlantic and Indian Oceans. This pattern was attributed to the timing of ocean formation and its interplay with global redox evolution. Fully oxygenated conditions in the Phanerozoic during the formation of the latter two oceans imposed a dual-dispersal barrier among oceans: toxicity of molecular oxygen to anaerobes and depletion of energy sources (especially reduced chemicals) for aerobes, but such a barrier didn’t exist before the Phanerozoic, when the former three oceans started. These results integrate microbial biogeography into a geodynamic framework, revealing that even microbial life is subject to planetary-scale geological constraints.Subject terms: Evolutionary ecology, Microbial ecology

Introduction

The determinants of geographic range size represent a central question in macroecology. Syntheses of studies across plants and animals confirm that dispersal ability is a foundational, albeit variable, predictor of species’ distributions1. For microorganisms, however, the classic tenet that ‘everything is everywhere, but the environment selects’ has long implied that their immense population sizes and dispersal potential negate the influence of geographic barriers2. However, this paradigm is increasingly challenged by evidence of endemism in isolated habitats3–7, raising a critical question: can large-scale geological processes impose dispersal limitations sufficient to structure the global distribution of microorganisms?

Hydrothermal vent systems, emerging along mid-ocean ridges and subduction-related back-arc basins, discharge reducing fluids rich in energy sources such as H₂, H₂S, CH₄, and reduced metals. These fluxes establish steep physicochemical gradients that sustain prolific chemosynthetic ecosystems8,9. Among the key microbial colonists are chemolithoautotrophic members of the phyla Campylobacterota (former class Epsilonproteobacteria), Aquificota, and Thermosulfidibacterota10. These taxa, collectively termed the CAT phyla, include anaerobic and microaerophilic lineages that are highly adapted to vent conditions11,12. Their ecological fidelity is profound: CAT abundances track vent activity, declining precipitously upon vent quenching13–16. Such strict habitat dependence and limited dispersal potential make the CAT phyla ideal model organisms for probing biogeographic controls in the deep sea.

The distribution of vent habitats is a product of planetary-scale tectonic processes. Plate motions continuously create and destroy vent systems over millions of years17, while concurrent shifts in global ocean redox state18, especially the global oxygenation of the deep ocean19, fundamentally altered the biogeochemical context for anaerobic life20. Yet, the extent to which these deep-time geological events have imprinted the contemporary biogeography of vent microbiomes is entirely unknown.

Here, we synthesize global microbial community data from deep-sea vents with paleo-oceanographic reconstructions to test the hypothesis that plate tectonics has shaped modern distributions of CAT lineages. We demonstrate that their biogeography is not random but reflects the timing of ocean basin formation and oxygenation, providing evidence that geological history mediates microbial dispersal and diversification across the oceans.

Results and discussion

A brief review of the tectonic and geochemical evolution history of the global oceans

The Wilson Cycle posits that periodic opening and closing of ocean basins, driven by plate tectonics17, continuously generate and eliminate hydrothermal vent habitats. The modern Pacific Ocean formed at ~ 190 million years ago (Ma) within the vast Panthalassic Ocean (the proto-Pacific Ocean, 650–200 Ma; Fig. 1; supplementary material Fig. S1)21–23, which itself originated from the breakup of Rodinia during the Neo-Proterozoic (around 700–600 Ma)24. The Mediterranean Sea represents a remnant of the Paleo-Tethys Ocean, which opened during the Neoproterozoic (around 650 Ma)25,26. The Arctic Ocean is bounded by continental shelves and cratonic blocks, including the Canadian Arctic Islands, Greenland, North America, the Barents Shelf, the Baltic Shield, and Siberian27. It comprises the Amerasia and Eurasia Basins, which formed at ~ 140 Ma and ~ 56 Ma, respectively28,29. Prior to the Arctic Ocean, the extinct South Anyui Ocean existed within the circum-Arctic region, which opened around 400 Ma, associated with palaeo-Pacific subduction and arc magmatism23,28,30. In contrast, the histories of the Atlantic and Indian Oceans are comparatively short, having formed between ~ 195 Ma and 130 Ma following the breakup of the Pangaea Supercontinent (Fig. 1)31–33. The Red Sea—the youngest ocean hosting hydrothermal vents—initiated rifting ~ 25–30 Ma with the separation of the Arabian-Nubian Shield34. Thus, although the modern oceans appear to have similar ages, they can be classified into two groups: (1) the older group (the Pacific, Mediterranean, and Arctic Oceans) formed from pre-existing oceans, whereas the younger group (the Atlantic and Indian Oceans) formed from the breakup of Pangea (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Material Fig. S1). We therefore hypothesize that microbial communities in hydrothermal systems of the first group were inherited from those of the pre-existing oceans, whereas those in the second group (the Atlantic, Indian, and Red Sea) came from adjacent oceans via dispersal by ocean currents after the formation of the oceans.

Since the Proterozoic, ocean chemistry has undergone dramatic changes as a result of oxygenation18. Before the Proterozoic Eon (4.0-2.5 billion years ago, Ga), the global oceans were pervasively anoxic. The Proterozoic (2.5-0.54 Ga) marked the onset of surface ocean oxygenation, followed by stepwise increases throughout the Phanerozoic (540 Ma), culminating in fully oxygenated deep oceans by the early Paleozoic (~ 440–420 Ma; Fig. 1)19. This trend was episodically interrupted by Mesozoic oceanic anoxic events (183 to 85 Ma), each lasting ~ 1.5 Ma35. These redox shifts progressively transformed seawater from a reducing chemistry rich in Fe2+, H2S, H2, S0, CH4, through a stratified state with oxic surface and anoxic deep waters, to a fully oxygenated system dominated by O2, SO42-, and NO3−18,19. Temporally, the Pacific Ocean, Arctic Ocean, and the Mediterranean Sea originated prior to full ocean oxygenation, whereas the Atlantic, Indian oceans, and the Red Sea opened after deep ocean oxygenation. However, when the opening of the Atlantic Ocean ended in the middle and northern regions around 120 Ma, the deep ocean was anoxic and connected to the Pacific Ocean and the Tethys Ocean to the west and east, respectively19. This temporal dichotomy may exert first-order control over the assembly and evolution of vent microbial communities over geologic time.

The evolution of the energy metabolism of the CAT phyla

An evolutionary relationship among the families within the CAT phyla was inferred from the topography of the 120-concatenated-protein phylogeny, which showed that the Aquificota and Thermosulfidibacterota were the two closely related phyla (Fig. 2 and Supplementary material Fig. S2). The Campylobacterota phylum is predicted to have diverged from the Aquificota-Thermosulfidibacterota phyla at around 4.1 Ga, according to previous prediction (Fig. 2)36. At the family level, the early-diverged Thermosulfidibacteraceae, Desulfurellaceae, and Hippeaceae formed at 2.08–2.85 Ga. Then Nautiliaceae and Desulfurobacteriaceae formed at around 1.6 Ga. At around 1.0 Ga, aerobic families, including Nitratiruptoraceae, Hydrogenimonadaceae, Sulfurovaceae, Sulfurimonadaceae, Hydrogenothermaceae, and Aquificaceae, formed. Temporally, all these CAT lineages at the family level formed earlier than the modern oceans and their predecessors (Figs. 1, 2).

The divergence times were retrieved from the online TimeTree resource and references therein36.

From early-diverged families to late-diverged families, energy metabolism pathways become increasingly complex, as indicated by the isolate descriptions (Supplementary material Table S1, and references therein; Fig. 3). Elemental sulfur-reduction with hydrogen as the electron donor was the only chemolithoautotrophic pathway for these earliest evolved families, including Hippeaceae, Desulfurellaceae, and Thermosulfidibacteraceae. Then, dissimilatory nitrate reduction to ammonium (DNRA), with hydrogen as the electron donor, was adopted as an additional energy acquisition pathway by the families Nautiliaceae and Desulfurobacteriaceae. These two families evolved around 1.5 Ga. The remaining families formed around 1 Ga acquired the ability to respire oxygen and oxidize sulfur compounds (such as H2S, S0, S2O32-), but switched from DNRA to denitrification with N2 or N2O as product.

The presence of functional genes in metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs; Fig. 4 and Supplementary material Fig. S3–S5) supported the evolution of the energy metabolism pathways. Genes responsible for H2-utilizing enzymes, either [FeFe]- or [NiFe]-type hydrogenases, were observed in all the CAT families (Fig. 4a). The [FeFe]-dependent hydrogenases are sensitive to O2, but [NiFe]-dependent hydrogenases are more tolerant to O237. Interestingly, only those MAGs from the earliest-evolved representatives, i.e., Thermosulfidibacterota and Hippeaceae, contain [FeFe]-dependent hydrogenases. However, [FeFe]-dependent hydrogenases are less abundant in the other anaerobic families, including Desulfurobacteriaceae, Desulfurellaceae, and Nautiliaceae. [FeFe]-dependent hydrogenases were totally replaced by [NiFe]-dependent hydrogenases in the facultatively aerobic members.

The heme- and copper-containing terminal oxidases (including A1, A2, B, and C types)38 enable these aerobic CAT families to perform oxygen respiration (Fig. 4b). Interestingly, the anaerobic Nautiliaceae genomes were found to contain cytochrome bd-type oxygen-reducing genes (Fig. 4b), but the cultivated isolates are described as anaerobes within the Nautiliaceae family, with one exception that Caminibacter profound CRT could grow under microaerophilic conditions39. Cytochrome bd oxygen reductases have a high affinity for oxygen but with a low energy efficiency and are often regarded as an enzyme in oxygen detoxification40, which might be the case for the anaerobic members of the Nautilaceae. The Nautiliaceae family represents an intermediate lineage that evolved during the transitional redox stage.

The genes encoding various sulfur-reductases (including psrBC, sudB, hydA, hydB, and shyA)41 may be responsible for the elemental sulfur respiration throughout the CAT families (Supplementary material Fig. S3). Gene sudB (encoding iron-sulfur flavoenzyme sulfide dehydrogenase), responsible for sulfur-reducing reactions, is found in most anaerobic CAT MAGs. Gene psrBC (encoding quinone reductase, responsible for polysulfide reduction) was preferentially found in aerobic CAT MAGs. The gene nirB, a marker gene for DNRA encoding enzymes for the reduction of nitrite to ammonium, is ubiquitous in early-evolved anaerobic CAT families (Supplementary material Fig. S4). The nirF and nirS genes, responsible for nitrite reduction to nitrogen monoxide (NO), are marker genes of denitrification and are ubiquitous in late-evolved aerobic families.

The gene sqor, sulfide:quinone oxidoreductase responsible for oxidizing sulfide to S0, is ubiquitous in the CAT except for certain members in the early-evolved lineages of Thermosulfidibacterota and Hippeaceae (Supplementary material Fig. S5). The gene fccB, another sulfide-oxidizing gene, is found in Aquificaceae and Nitratiruptoraceace. Gene soxC, encoding sulfur dehydrogenases and responsible for sulfur oxidation, is found in the families of Sulfurovaceae and Sulfurimonadaceae. The SoxABXYZ gene set, responsible for thiosulfate oxidation, is observed in most facultatively aerobic members of the CAT families.

Global distribution pattern of the CAT families

Microbial community compositions, based on 16S rRNA gene amplicon data at the family level, revealed a pronounced divergence in the abundance of CAT families between the ancient and younger oceans. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) confirmed that the relative abundances of CAT families were significantly higher in early-formed oceans (Pacific and Arctic Oceans, and Mediterranean Sea, or their ancestor oceans) compared to the late-formed ones (Atlantic and Indian Oceans, and Red Sea, or younger oceans) (Supplementary material Figs. S6–S15). This disparity was visually evident in abundance-projected global maps (Fig. 5), which showed Campylobacterota were ubiquitous and highly abundant (> 50% relative abundance) across most sampling sites, except in the Indian Ocean, South Atlantic Ocean, and Red Sea (Fig. 5a). Aquificota were prevalent (> 10% relative abundance) in the Pacific, Atlantic and Arctic Oceans, and Mediterranean Sea, while Thermosulfidibacterota occurred at lower abundances (> 1%), peaking in the Mediterranean Sea around 12.5%.

The red dots denote the hydrothermal vents with no CAT families detected. The areas of the purple circles denoted 100% relative abundances.

Obligately anaerobic lineages (Thermosulfidibacterota, Desulfurobacteriaceae, Nautiliaceae, Hippeaceae, and Desulfurellaceae) exhibited highly restricted distributions, with Desulfurobacteriaceae concentrated in the eastern Pacific; Hippeaceae and Desulfurellaceae largely confined to the Gulf of California (Fig. 5b). Notably, negligible anaerobic CAT members were detected in the Indian or South Atlantic Oceans. Lower than 1% of Thermosulfidibacterota and Nautiliaceae were found in the South Atlantic Ocean. Thermosulfidibacterota, Desulfurobacteriaceae, and Nautiliaceae (at 0.001% level) were found in the Indian Ocean at much lower abundance. In contrast, facultatively aerobic families (Sulfurovaceae, Sulfurimonadaceae, Nitratiruptoraceae, Aquificae, and Hydrogenothermaceae) showed broader distributions (Fig. 5c), consistent with their respective phyla (Fig. 5a). Among them, Sulfurimonadaceae and Sulfurovaceae were the most abundant CAT families globally.

Alpha diversity analysis further revealed systematic differences in richness and phylogenetic diversity of the CAT taxa across the oceans. Hydrothermal vent systems in the Pacific Ocean exhibited the highest species richness (SR) and phylogenetic diversity (PD) for all three CAT phyla (Fig. 6). Campylobacterota (Fig. 6a, d) and Aquificota (Fig. 6b, e) showed significantly greater SR and PD in the Pacific compared to all other regions. Specifically, the Pacific Ocean hydrothermal samples had an average Campylobacterota SR of 754, with the highest SR of 3294 from the Pescadero Basin. The average value of Campylobacterota SR was 115, 104, 75, and 58 from the Mediterranean Sea, Atlantic Ocean, Arctic Ocean, and Indian Ocean, respectively, significantly lower than that from the Pacific Ocean (Fig. 6a). The average SR value of Aquificota from the Pacific Ocean was 60, with the highest value of 805 from the Brothers volcano. The average value of Aquificota SR was 22.7, 9.2, 9.8, and 17 from the Atlantic Ocean, Indian Ocean, Mediterranean Sea, and Arctic Ocean, respectively, also significantly lower than that from the Pacific Ocean (Fig. 6b). Similar comparisons were observed with PD of Campylobacterota and Aquificota between the Pacific and the remaining oceans (Fig. 6d, e). Thermosulfidibacterota diversity was also relatively higher in the Pacific Ocean and the Arctic Ocean (Fig. 6c, f).

Venn diagram directly exhibited overwhelmingly high diversity of the CAT phyla in the Pacific Ocean, which contained the most OTUs, and encompassed most of the OTUs from the other oceans (Fig. 7). In contrast, the late-formed oceans—the Atlantic and Indian Oceans—consistently hosted relatively fewer Campylobacterota, Aquificota, and Thermosulfidibacter OTUs (Fig. 7b, c). Together, these results exhibited a clear biogeographic pattern across the global oceans.

The mechanism of the uneven distribution of the CAT families in the oceans

To disentangle the uneven distribution of the CAT families across global oceans, redundancy analyses (RDA) were conducted with relative abundances of the CAT families and the geological factors (the times of ocean opening, tectonic setting, depth, host rock type, and sample type), as well as the geochemical factors of different hydrothermal vents (including temperature, pH, and the concentrations of the energy substrates for the CAT families) from open database and references23,42,43. Among these factors, the oceanic basin opening time accounted for the largest proportion of the variance in the uneven distribution of CAT families (Fig. 8), followed by SO42-, H2S, water depth, NO3-, and tectonic settings. The remaining factors, including rock type, were insignificant.

Redundancy analysis (RDA) of the CAT community at the family level with the geological factors (a) and geochemical factors (b).

The tables list the explanatory percentages of the geological and geochemical factors to the variations in the CAT communities.

This result demonstrates a clear biogeographic imprint of ocean origination ages on the global hydrothermal microbial community, in contrast to previous findings in terrestrial hot springs, where microbial biogeographical distributions were determined mainly by geochemical parameters3–7. The mechanisms may include the following: (1) endemic CAT families formed in local oceans due to long-term separation or (2) dispersal limitation of these vent-specific dwellers to hydrothermal vents in newly formed ocean basins. The first potential mechanism can be ruled out because the formation of the CAT families all occurred before the timing of the modern ocean basins (Figs. 1, 2).

Dispersal limitation may be imposed by the progress of oxygenation, as the deep ocean became fully oxygenated by the late Paleozoic (~ 420 Ma)44,45. Molecular oxygen is toxic to anaerobic microorganisms, damaging biomolecules via reactive oxygen species and oxidizing low-redox-potential metalloenzymes46. Consistent with this, anaerobic CAT lineages encode significantly fewer antioxidant enzymes in their MAGs than aerobic counterparts (Supplementary material Fig. S16), indicating poor oxygen tolerance and constrained dispersal through oxic waters. This explains the near-absence of anaerobic CAT families in the younger Indian Ocean and Red Sea (Fig. 5c). Most anaerobic lineages are instead confined to the Pacific, Arctic, and Mediterranean Oceans (Fig. 5b), consistent with their origins predating deep-sea oxygenation44,45 (Figs. 1, 2).

Theoretically, the fully oxygenated Atlantic should also exclude anaerobic taxa, yet we detected Nautiliaceae in the North and Central Atlantic Ocean (Fig. 5b). We propose that only the family members of Nautiliaceae contain cytochrome bd-type O2 reductase (Fig. 4b), an enzyme involved in oxygen detoxification40 that may have endowed them with further dispersal capacity than the other anaerobic CAT families at a low level of oxygen. Furthermore, the northern and central parts of the Atlantic Ocean opened earlier than the southern part23,33. The opening of the North-Central Atlantic Ocean broke the Pangea Supercontinent into northern and southern halves at around 100–120 Ma, forming a geographic pattern in which the North-Central Atlantic Ocean connected to the Pacific and Tethys Oceans simultaneously19,23,33. At the same time, the oxygen in the Atlantic deep ocean was assumed to be at a low level19. So, the unexpected distribution of the Nautilacea family in the Atlantic Ocean might be the joint result of the intrinsic characteristics of Nautiliaceae, ocean connectivity, and regional deep-ocean oxygen evolution. Occurrence of minor Thermosulfidibactota in the South Atlantic (Fig. 5b) and minor Thermosulfidibacterota, Desulfurobacteriaceae, and Nautiliaceae in the Southwest Indian Ocean may be attributed to the regional tectonic histories. These two regional oceans might have evolved from a part of the Proto-Pacific Ocean, similar to the Arctic Ocean (Supplementary material Fig. S1).

Oxygenation also diminishes the availability of reduced compounds (e.g., H2, H2S, S0, SO32-, S2O32-) that serve as energy sources for facultatively aerobic CAT families, likely limiting their dispersal in the presence of oxygen. Indeed, low abundances of the facultatively aerobic Hydrogenothermaceae, Sulfurovaceae, and Sulfurimonadaceae were observed in the Central and Southwest Indian Ocean (Fig. 5c), and the Nitratiruptoraceae were sparse in the Northwest Indian Ocean, South Atlantic Ocean, and Red Sea. Further support comes from the Loihi hydrothermal field (~ 400 ka) in the Pacific, where newly formed vents host low abundances of facultative aerobic CAT families47, highlighting their slow colonization under modern oxidizing conditions. Nevertheless, specific lineages such as Sulfurimonadaceae (mainly genus Sulfurimonas) and Sulfurovaceae (mainly genus Sulfurovum) can disperse via particle-rich hydrothermal plumes that contain micro-niches with reduced compounds, providing a mechanism for long-range dispersal in oxic waters48–50.

Distribution of CAT taxa through Earth’s redox history

The evolutionary trajectory of CAT lineages reflects major transitions in ocean redox chemistry, which can be divided into anoxic, intermediate, and oxic stages18. During the anoxic stage (prior to ~ 2.45 Ga), the global ocean was rich in reduced compounds such as Fe2+, H2, S0, H2S, and NH4+ (Fig. 9a)20,51, resembling the chemical environment of contemporary hydrothermal vent fluids. High ocean temperatures (~ 65 − 80 oC)52 were near the optimal growth temperature for the early-evolved CAT lineages (Supplementary data Fig. S17), implying that ancestral CAT taxa (analogs of Thermosulfidibacterota, Hippeaceae, and Desulfurellaceae) were likely ubiquitous in the open ocean rather than vent-restricted. However, these ancient hydrothermal vents were enriched in the specific lithoautotrophic energy sources, i.e., H2 and S0, for these early-evolved CAT lineages (Fig. 9a), indicating that hydrothermal vents were favorable environments for them.

The Great Oxygenation Event (GOE) marked the onset of an intermediate phase, oxidizing surface waters and driving the accumulation of NO2-, NO3-, and SO42- from reduced precursors (e.g., NH4+, S0, and H2S), while simultaneously decreasing H2 availability in the deep ocean (Fig. 9b)20,51. Episodic deep-ocean oxygenation53,54 likely promoted metabolic diversification within the CAT lineages, favoring taxa capable of utilizing a wider array of electron acceptors. Lineages relying exclusively on H₂ as a sole electron donor (e.g., Desulfurobacteriaceae, Hautiliaceae, and Hydrogenimonadaceae) became increasingly restricted to hydrothermal vents, where H₂ remained abundant. In contrast, sulfur-oxidizing CAT families continued to proliferate in broader marine settings due to the persistence of S⁰, H2S, and S2O32⁻ in seawater.

By the Phanerozoic (~0.54 Ga onward), full ocean oxygenation55 dramatically reduced the pool of reduced chemicals in the open ocean (Fig. 9c). As a result, both anaerobic and facultatively aerobic CAT taxa became largely confined to hydrothermal vent systems, where reductants remained plentiful. The sharp redox gradients between oxidized seawater and reduced vent fluids sustained high energy availability, fueling diverse microbial ecosystems at modern vents. Thus, the progressive oxygenation of Earth’s oceans systematically narrowed the ecological niches of CAT lineages, transforming them from widespread marine taxa into specialists of hydrothermal systems.

The modern oceans were derived from either the Proto-Pacific and Proto-Tethys Oceans or the breakup of the last supercontinent, i.e., Pangea (Fig. 1). The well-established evolutions of the global oceanic geography and oxygenation during the Phanerozoic make it possible to further infer the redistribution of the CAT families from these ancestral oceans to the modern oceans (Fig. 10a–f). During the Phanerozoic, the Pacific Ocean and its ancestor were always the largest oceans on the planet, and all CAT families inhabited the Pacific Ocean as indigenous dwellers (Fig. 10a–f). The Mediterranean Sea was also in a similar situation except for its smaller size. These CAT families inhabited the oceans, from the Proto-Tethys, Paleo-Tethys, and Neo-Tethys Oceans, to the Mediterranean Sea (Fig. 10a–f). The origin of the Arctic Ocean was thought to be from a part of the Proto-Pacific Ocean (Fig. 10b), and all CAT families were proposed to have existed in the Arctic Ocean as indigenous dwellers. Undeniably, the shrinking of the Mediterranean Sea and the Arctic Ocean during their evolution might have caused the loss of some CAT members. As brand-new oceans in the Phanerozoic, the Atlantic and Indian Oceans opened under oxygenic conditions, preventing the anaerobic CAT families from propagating into them. As a result, only the aerobic and oxygen-tolerant CAT families (i.e., Nautiliaceae) inhabited the Atlantic and Indian Oceans.

On the other hand, the reduction of S0 by H2 is a common energy-metabolic pathway of the CAT families, even as the sole lithoautotrophic energy source for the early-evolved CAT families. Such a situation aligns with the evolutionary trajectory of life, from simple to complex, suggesting that the common ancestor of the CAT families, also perhaps the earliest form of life, harnessed this reaction to obtain energy. From this point, these ancient hydrothermal vents, enriched with H2 and S0, were favorable environments for the origination of life.

Such an energetic pathway expectation could also extrapolate life beyond our planet. Europa and Enceladus are the two prospective moons in the solar system that may harbor life in their oceans. In particular, molecular hydrogen found in the plumes of Enceladus indicates a water-rock reaction with H2 as a product through hydrothermal processes56, as occurs in hydrothermal systems on Earth. Sulfur is predicted to be sufficient in Enceladus’s ocean57. Thus, it is possible to find life harnessing the reaction of S0 reduction by H2 as an energy acquisition pathway in the solar system.

Methods

A total of more than 2000 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) affiliated with the phyla Campylobacterota, Aquificota, and Thermosulfidibacterota from references13,48,58–61 and unreferenced MAGs were retrieved from the NCBI database using the “Download Genome Taxon” function in the dataset tool on January 5, 2026. The taxonomy of the MAGs was verified using the GTDB-tk v0.3.3 tool61. Non-hydrothermal vent-derived MAGs were excluded from further analysis. The qualities of all retrieved MAGs were evaluated with CheckM v1.2.2. Any MAGs with completeness > 0.9 and contamination < 0.1 were selected for subsequent analyses. After quality filtering, 813 MAGs were targeted for further analysis (see the Source Data 1). The optimal growth temperatures of the MAGs were predicted using the TOME software62. The phylogenetic relationship of the CAT families was inferred from the concatenated 120 marker genes of the MAGs, which were identified, concatenated, and aligned using the GTDB-tk v0.3.3 tool61. A Maximum Likelihood tree was constructed using MEGA-12 software with the Jones-Taylor-Thornton model substitution, with a bootstrap value of 100063.

Open reading frames (ORFs) of the MAGs were predicted with Prodigal v2.6.3. The predicted ORFs were searched against the NCBI nr protein database (2019/07) and the eggNOG database with the BLASTP algorithm (coverage of > 75%, e-values of < 1 × 10−20) to check their protein identities to the most closely related sequences using DIAMOND sequence aligner version 0.9.30.131 (http://github.com/bbuchfink/diamond). Then, the genes related to energy metabolism pathways, including nitrogen cycling64, sulfur cycling41, oxygen reductases38, and hydrogenases37, as well as the antioxidases in each MAG, were counted using a Linux searching tool, grep. The types of oxygen-respiration enzymes and hydrogenases were also analyzed using DIAMOND by searching against the Heme-Copper Oxidases (HCOs) and Hydrogenase databases37,38. These targeted genes were also verified using the online Conserved Domain Search Service (CD Search) in the NCBI website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Structure/cdd/wrpsb.cgi).

The hydrothermal vent-related high-throughput 16S rRNA gene sequence datasets with read numbers more than 2000 from references9,13–16,59,60,65–68 and unreferenced sequences were retrieved from the Sequence Read Archive (SRA) in NCBI with the prefetch SRA-downloading tool within the SRA toolkit on December 10, 2025. The sequences associated with mussel, shrimp, and snail symbionts were excluded from further analysis. A total of 1209 high-throughput sequence datasets were used for microbial community analyses, which mapped to V3 to V9 hypervariable regions of the full-length 16S rRNA gene sequence (see the Source Data 2). To analyze all the sequences as a pool, they were combined and clustered using a reference-based method, i.e., the Usearch v11 pipelines69 with the SILVA Release 138.2 full-length 16S rRNA gene database as reference70. Usearch fastx_trim_primer was used to trim primers. Subsequently, a collection of high-quality sequences was retained through the usearch fastx_filter script. The usearch closed_ref script was used to cluster sequences into taxonomies with similarity thresholds of 0.75, 0.865, and 0.945 at phylum, family, and genus levels, respectively71. The relative abundances of the microbial compositions for each sample were calculated based on coverage of the total read using the usearch -sintax_summary script. For these high-throughput V4 variable region sequences, an OTU table was constructed using the usearch -cluster_fast script at a 0.97 similarity threshold. Alpha diversity indices, including species richness (SR) and phylogenetic diversity (PD), were calculated with the Vegan R script and plotted with the Picante R script. Any overlapping OTUs of the CAT phyla across different oceans were visualized using an online Venn diagram maker72. LEfSe (linear discriminant analysis effect size) was performed to detect differentially abundant taxa across the oceans using the “microeco” R package.

Redundancy analysis (RDA), a linear canonical community ordination method, was used to visualize the relationships between the response variables (the relative abundances of the CAT families) and explantary variables, i.e., geological factors (the times of ocean opening, tectonic setting, depth, host rock type, and sample type) and geochemical factors (including hydrothermal vent temperature, pH, and the concentrations of the energy substrates for the CAT families). The basin ages adopted the opening times of the preceding oceans if they have. For example, the opening time of the Proto-Pacific Ocean (650 Ma)24 was set as the basin age of the Pacific Ocean. The Mediterranean Sea adopted the opening time of the Paleo-Tethys Ocean (650 Ma)25,26. The Arctic Ocean adopted the opening time of the South Anyui Ocean (400 Ma)23,28,30. The Atlantic Ocean opened in the middle and northern parts first, then opened in the south23. So, the basin ages of the Mid-Atlantic and the North-Atlantic were both conferred with 190 Ma; and the South-Atlantic was conferred with 110 Ma33. Likewise, the Southwest Indian Ocean adopted 150 Ma, and the Central and North Indian Ocean adopted 110 Ma31. The Red Sea adopted 25 Ma34. The tectonic settings of the hydrothermal vents included mid-ocean ridge and subducting settings from a global database43, which were assigned values 1 and 2, respectively. Rock types, including basalt, andesite, ultramafic, dacite, rhyolite, peridotite, and basaltic andesite, were transformed by their silica contents73. The sample types, including chimney, deposit, sediment, mat, and fluid, were assigned with 1-5, respectively. The geochemical parameters were retrieved from a reference42. To reduce the effect of low CAT abundances, only the samples with the total CAT relative abundances greater than 10% were included in the DCA analysis. A total of 366 matched CAT family-geological parameter data, and 116 matched CAT family-geochemical parameter data were used in DCA analyses with Canoco 5.0 software (Microcomputer Power, New York, USA).

The significance of differences in gene copy numbers between anaerobic and aerobic MAGs or in diversity indices between the two groups of the microbial community was tested with a T-test using the “ggplubr” package in R. The differences were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05.
