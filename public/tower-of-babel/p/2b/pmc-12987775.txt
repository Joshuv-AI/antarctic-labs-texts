SENP3 drives colorectal cancer progression by enhancing GDF15 expression
Authors: Yang Yu, Wenfang Bao, Jingde Chen, Yandong Li, Yong Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSUMO-specific peptidase 3 (SENP3), as a de-SUMOylating enzyme, renders the process of protein SUMOylation reversible. It has been demonstrated to play either promoting or inhibitory roles in several cancers. However, research on its function in colorectal cancer (CRC) remains absent.MethodsTissue samples from CRC patients were analyzed to measure the expression level of SENP3. Proliferation and viability assays were performed to assess the role of SENP3 in cell growth. The mouse model was established to evaluate the effect of SENP3 on the growth of xenografts derived from CRC cells. To investigate the mechanisms of SENP3 in CRC, proteomic analysis was conducted.ResultsWe identified an elevated expression of SENP3 in cancerous tissues, which correlated with a reduced survival rate in CRC patients. SENP3 promoted cell growth in vitro and boosted tumorigenicity in vivo as a potential oncogenic factor. In terms of mechanism, proteomic analysis revealed that growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) is a downstream effector of SENP3. Treatment with MG132 confirmed that SENP3 enhances the stability of GDF15. Through a series of cellular functional experiments and mouse model establishment, we demonstrated that SENP3 regulates CRC progression by acting on GDF15.ConclusionBased on our findings, we define a key role for SENP3 in colorectal cancer progression and suggest it as a viable target for therapeutic intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00384-026-05120-y.Keywords: SENP3, GDF15, Colorectal cancer, Tumor growth

Introduction

In today’s world, it is widely recognized that cancer remains a considerable challenge to human health. Global cancer statistics indicate that colorectal cancer holds the position of the third most frequent among all types of cancer, and its mortality rate ranks as the second highest [1]. Moreover, this trend is anticipated to worsen with ongoing socioeconomic development [2]. Therefore, enhancing the treatment efficacy of colorectal cancer and exploring relevant healthcare services can significantly alleviate the substantial social burden imposed by colorectal cancer [3].

With the in-depth understanding of the human genome through scientific study, the notable impact of proteomics on the onset and progression of cancer has been unveiled [4]. In recent years, post-translational modifications (PTMs) have made significant breakthroughs in the field of cancer research [5]. Scientists have identified more than 650 types of protein modifications to date; these include ubiquitination, phosphorylation, glycosylation, methylation, and SUMOylation, and the expanding repertoire reflects ongoing discovery in the field. PTMs alter the function and interactions of target proteins by modifying specific residues, rather than by altering the DNA sequence [6–9]. Among the diverse array of post-translational modifications, SUMOylation has emerged as a particularly significant process, owing to its crucial role in dynamically modulating protein stability, subcellular localization, and functional activity—mechanisms often disrupted in cancer pathogenesis. SENP3, as a member of the SUMO-specific protease family, regulates target proteins by removing SUMO modifications. There is clear evidence indicating that SENP3 modulates the process of occurrence and development of triple-negative breast cancer through YAP1 [10]. Some scholars have also confirmed that SENP3 enhances the efficacy of chemotherapy in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma by regulating the process of fatty acid oxidation [11]. In addition, SENP3 enhances ESCC progression via IRF4 de-SUMOylation-mediated macrophage polarization [12]. Interestingly, in our previous work, it was discovered that SENP3, via its de-SUMOylation activity, impedes the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of lactotransferrin (LTF), thereby inhibiting ferroptosis in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [13].

However, the roles of SENP3 in CRC remain unclear. Building upon our previous findings, we conducted a study to define the contribution of SENP3 in CRC and its underlying mechanisms. In this study, we assessed whether SENP3 is upregulated in CRC tissues and whether it influences patient prognosis. Functional assays in vitro and in vivo were conducted to determine whether SENP3 regulates the growth of CRC cells and tumors. Furthermore, we investigated the molecular mechanisms and downstream targets through which SENP3 regulates CRC progression by employing proteomic analysis.

Method and materials

Plasmids, siRNAs, and shRNA

The plasmid p3*FLAG-CMV-SENP3 (for transient transfection) and the plasmid pWPXLd-FLAG-SENP3 (for lentivirus construction) that were utilized in this study were procured from a public protein/plasmid repository in Nanjing, China. The LV-shGDF15 plasmids for GDF15 knockdown (#P36081 and #P36054) were provided by MIAOLING PLASMID in Wuhan, China. The siRNAs of SENP3 were purchased from GenePharma Co., Ltd. (China). The sequences of siRNAs for SENP3 were as follows: siSENP3-1:

Cell lines and cell culture

The culture medium for HCT116, RKO, SW620, and 293 T cell lines was Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM), procured from Sigma (USA). The LoVo cell line was maintained in RPMI-1640 medium (Sigma, USA). We supplemented the culture media with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Sigma, USA) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Beyotime, China). The same lot of FBS was used throughout the study to minimize variability. All cell cultures were conducted under conditions of 5% CO2 and 37 °C. All cells were guaranteed to be passaged less than 20 passages. All cell lines were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Shanghai Institute of Life Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences. The cell lines used in this study were identified by STR analysis prior to the start of the experiment with a frequency of 2–3 years. All cell lines were to be tested free of mycoplasma contamination.

Human CRC specimens and tissue microarray (TMA)

The clinical tissue samples from CRC patients utilized in our study were sourced from Shanghai East Hospital, China. All participants provided informed consent and signed the documents. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Shanghai East Hospital. Freshly collected tissues were put into labeled cryovials and snap-frozen with liquid nitrogen. Subsequently, the samples were preserved at −80 °C for an extended period. The study utilized tissue microarrays (HColA180Su24) provided by Shanghai OUTDO Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Immunohistochemical staining methods were employed for detection. Two pathologists performed double-blind scoring of immunohistochemical staining [14, 15]. The scoring was based on staining intensity (0: none; 1: weak; 2: moderate; and 3: strong) and positive tumor cell percentage (0: < 10%, 1: 10–25%, 2: 25–50%, 3: 50–75%, and 4: > 75%). The specific categorized criterion is as follows: The “staining intensity” × “positive tumor cell percentage” ≤ 6 was defined as low expression and > 6 as high expression. Patient pathological information for clinical samples is provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Cell transfection and establishment of stable transformation lines

In this study, the transfection reagent used was Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, USA), and plasmids (as well as empty vectors) or siRNA (including non-specific control siRNA) was transfected as recommended by the protocol. The lentivirus used for the knockdown of SENP3 was provided by GenePharma (designed based on the siSENP3-2 sequence) and was used to infect RKO, HCT116, and SW620 cell lines (RKO/HCT116/SW620: LV-shNC, LV-shSENP3), followed by selection with 2.5 µg/mL puromycin for a minimum of 72 h. Furthermore, a lentivirus for the overexpression of SENP3 was constructed in this study using the following method: pWPXLd-FLAG-SENP3 (as well as empty vector) and the vector plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G were transfected into 293 T cells. Following 48 h of transfection, the culture supernatant was harvested and centrifuged to obtain the lentivirus, which was subsequently used to infect the HCT116 and RKO cell lines (HCT116/RKO: LV-VEC, LV-SENP3), followed by monoclonal selection. The knockdown cell lines for GDF15 were constructed by transducing LV-shGDF15 lentiviral particles packaged from psPAX2, pMD2.G, and LV-shGDF15 plasmids in 293 T cells.

Proteomic analysis

The proteins from pre-detected RKO-LV-shNC and RKO-LV-shSENP3 cell lines (with three replicates per group) were collected and submitted to LC-Bio Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) for proteomic analysis using Astral-DIA. The process involved the collection of raw data, protein database searching, data quality control, protein quantification analysis, and the generation of protein expression profiles. In summary, protein samples are extracted and digested into peptides. The peptide samples were reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid and separated using a Thermo Scientific Vanquish Neo UHPLC system equipped with a 150-mm ES906 HPLC column. Mobile phases consisted of A (0.1% formic acid in water) and B (80% acetonitrile). A gradient elution was performed over 8 min at a flow rate of 2.5 µL/min as follows: 4–25% B (0–4 min), 25–35% B (4–6.9 min), 35–99% B (6.9–7.3 min), and 99% B (7.3–8 min). MS analysis was conducted on a Thermo Astral mass spectrometer in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode with the following settings: scan range 380–980 m/z; resolution 240,000; normalized collision energy 25%; AGC target 500%; and maximum injection time 3 ms. DIA windows were set as 300 variable-width segments across the m/z range. The results were filtered by 1% FDR, and only those protein groups which passed this filter criteria were used in downstream analysis. Subsequently, a combination of P-value and fold change (FC) was used to select proteins with significant differences. The statistical results of differential proteins are shown in Supplementary Material 1. The raw data of proteomic analysis in this study are available in the ProteomeXchange database [16] with accession number IPX0013759000.

Cell colony formation assay

After digesting the cells, they were counted and subsequently seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 103 cells per well. Each sample was tested in at least three replicate wells. The culture medium was replaced approximately every 72 h. The colony formation was observed approximately 3 to 4 weeks post cell adhesion. At the end of this period, the supernatant was discarded, and the cells underwent fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde and staining with 0.5% crystal violet. The colonies were first documented by imaging and then enumerated. The assay was repeated in at least three independent experiments.

Cell proliferation assay

CRC cells were digested and plated in a 96-well plate with 3 × 103 cells in each well in triplicate. We observed the adherence of the cells after 6–12 h. Subsequently, add 10 µL of CCK-8 solution (CK04, Dojindo, Japan) to each well. The 96-well plate was incubated for a period of 75 min under conditions of 37 °C. After incubation, the OD value at 450 nm was recorded by the spectrophotometer. Organize the data and perform statistical analysis. Each assay was repeated at least three times.

EdU cell proliferation detection

Cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 8 × 103 cells per well. A minimum of three replicate wells were included for each sample. According to the product instructions, first dilute the EdU reagent (RiboBio, China) in complete culture medium at a ratio of 1000:1. Incubate the plate for 2 h at 37 °C after pipetting 100 µL of the solution into each well. After discarding the culture medium, fix the cells with PBS containing 4% paraformaldehyde, and sequentially perform staining with Apollo and DNA dyes. Fluorescence microscopy imaging will be conducted, followed by statistical analysis of the data. The experiment was independently repeated at least three times.

Western blot assay

Initially, preparation of RIPA lysis buffer (20–188, Merck Millipore, USA) was carried out, incorporating protease inhibitors (100:1) and phosphatase inhibitors (100:1). The required volume of the mixed solution was treated with cell or tissue, and the samples were placed on ice for approximately 30 min to ensure complete lysis (tissue samples required grinding using a homogenizer). Following centrifugation, only the supernatant was retained, to which a suitable amount of loading buffer was added, and the mixture was denatured at 100 °C for 5–10 min. SDS-PAGE gels were prepared, and an adequate amount of protein was loaded for electrophoresis. The gel was subsequently transferred to the PVDF membrane under constant current conditions. After blocking the membrane for 1 h, primary and secondary antibody incubations were performed in sequence. The primary antibody targeting SENP3 protein was commercially available from Cell Signaling Technology (5591S, MA, USA), and the primary antibody targeting GDF15 protein was purchased from Selleck (G3D13, China). The dilution ratios of the two antibodies for western blotting are 1:1000. Finally, detection of protein bands was carried out on the Odyssey infrared imaging system (LI-COR Biosciences, USA). For the protein stability assay, cells were treated with cycloheximide (CHX; S7418, Selleck) at 10 µg/mL or MG132 (HY-13259, MedChemExpress) at 40 µM, respectively. Each sample should be loaded at least three times repeatedly. Each assay was repeated at least three times.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

An appropriate amount of TRIzol (T9424, Sigma, USA) was added to the sample, and after centrifugation to obtain the supernatant, we added chloroform and isopropanol in proportion sequentially. RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA after quantification using the RR037A kit (Takara, Japan). We conducted RT-qPCR using a commercially acquired rapid qPCR kit (RR820A, Takara, Japan) with the QuantStudio6 real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The data analysis was performed using the formula 2−ΔΔCt. Each sample should have at least three replicate wells. Each assay was repeated at least three times. Mouse xenograft model

This study established a xenograft mouse model. The male athymic nude mice (4–5 weeks old) were sourced from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Prior to the experiment, all mice were randomly assigned to different groups using a random number generator. Based on a power calculation, the sample size was determined to be five mice per group. Human colorectal cancer stable cell lines were injected into the subcutaneous axillary region of each mouse at a concentration of 2× 106 cells. One week after the inoculation with CRC cells, the size of the subcutaneous tumors in the mice was measured and recorded every 3 days, along with an assessment of the health status of the mice (calculated using the formula: (length × width2)/2). According to the ARRIVE guidelines and animal welfare regulations, all mice were euthanized by carbon dioxide asphyxiation, and the maximum diameter of subcutaneous tumors did not exceed 20 mm before the mice were sacrificed. Tumors were excised, and the tumor mass was weighed and imaged. Approval for the animal experiments was obtained from the Animal Ethics Committee of Tongji University.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis and graphical construction of the data in this study were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 software. Unless otherwise stated, data are given as mean ± SD. Student’s t-test is used to compare the statistical differences between two groups, while analysis of variance (ANOVA) is used to compare multiple groups, respectively. Analysis of the correlation between SENP3 and CRC prognosis was performed via the Kaplan–Meier Plotter online tool (https://kmplot.com/analysis/)[27]. The median expression level of SENP3 mRNA was used as the cutoff; samples above the median were classified as high expression and below as low expression. The statistical method utilized for survival analysis was the Log-rank test. In correlation analyses, both Spearman and Pearson statistical methods were applied. A P-value below 0.05 was deemed statistically significant. In figures, significance is denoted as follows: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ns (not significant).

Result

SENP3 is highly expressed in CRC and is associated with poorer prognosis

To investigate the expression of SENP3 in CRC, tumor specimens and their matched adjacent normal tissues were harvested from patients with CRC and subjected to further analysis. We evaluated the expression of SENP3 at the mRNA level in 39 pairs of clinical samples, and the data indicated its high expression in cancerous tissues (Fig. 1A). Subsequently, we randomly selected seven pairs of samples from the cohort to assess the protein expression levels of SENP3, and the western blot results supported the conclusion obtained from the qPCR data (Fig. 1B). Furthermore, analysis of immunohistochemical results from a CRC tissue microarray also yielded similar observations: SENP3 was more abundant in tumor tissues compared to normal adjacent tissues (Fig. 1C, D). However, due to the absence of follow-up information for some patients, we were unable to statistically analyze the association between the survival outcomes of these patients and the expression of SENP3. Therefore, we conducted survival analyses using Kaplan–Meier Plotter [17], and the result corroborated our hypothesis. SENP3 served as a predictor for adverse outcomes, with high levels indicating both reduced overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) (Fig. 1E). These findings suggested that the upregulation of SENP3 in CRC contributes to the deterioration of patient conditions.

SENP3 plays a role in promoting the proliferation of CRC cells

To investigate the effect of SENP3 on the proliferation of CRC cells in vitro, we transiently overexpressed exogenous SENP3 in two distinct human-derived CRC cell lines. Similarly, we knocked down SENP3 transiently in two additional CRC cell lines. The results from western blot analysis verified the overexpression or knockdown efficiency of SENP3 as indicated in Fig. 2A, B. The results from cell viability assays demonstrated that SENP3 upregulation accelerated the rate of cell growth, whereas downregulation of SENP3 exhibited an inhibitory effect (Fig. 2C). To further validate the oncogenic potential of SENP3 in CRC cells, we proceeded to establish stable SENP3 knockdown CRC cell lines and confirmed the knockdown efficiency through Western blotting (Fig. 2D). Cell colony formation assays and EdU proliferation assays were then carried out in these stable cell lines. The results indicated that downregulation of SENP3 impaired the proliferation of CRC cells (Fig. 2E, F). In contrast, stable overexpression of SENP3 in HCT116 cells significantly enhanced cell proliferation (Fig. S2). The data above indicated that SENP3 may function as a potential oncogene in CRC.

E Colony formation assays were conducted following SENP3 knockdown, and the results are presented in bar graphs. F EdU proliferation assays were performed after SENP3 knockdown, and the results are displayed in bar graphs. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01

Decreasing SENP3 expression can inhibit tumor growth in mice

To further investigate SENP3’s oncogenic role in vivo, we subcutaneously injected SENP3 stable knockdown cells (RKO-LV-shNC and RKO-LV-shSENP3) into the axillary regions of nude mice (n = 5). Images of tumors in mice revealed that the knockdown of SENP3 significantly impaired xenograft growth in comparison to the control group (Fig. 3A). The xenograft tumors exhibited a lighter weight (Fig. 3B) and a slower growth rate during the formation process (Fig. 3C). In the meantime, we obtained consistent results in the xenograft mice model established with SW620 cells (Fig. 3D-–F). These in vivo experimental results strongly supported our in vitro findings that SENP3 exerts pro-carcinogenic effects in CRC.

SENP3 regulates GDF15 expression in CRC cells

Given that SENP3 has been shown to influence post-translational protein modification processes [18], we hypothesized that SENP3 may affect the stability of its downstream target proteins, thereby altering the growth capability of CRC cells. Accordingly, we conducted a proteomic analysis in RKO cells from both the control group and the SENP3 knockdown group and identified a total of 1046 differentially expressed proteins, which included 469 downregulated proteins and 577 upregulated proteins (Fig. 4A). We selected several proteins, including growth differentiation factor 15 (GDF15), using the criteria of larger fold change and greater statistical significance (smaller P-value) (Fig. 4B). GDF15 is highly expressed in various gastrointestinal tumors and is considered a potential pro-carcinogenic factor [19]. Consequently, we hypothesized that GDF15 might be a key downstream protein through which SENP3 exerts its biological functions. Furthermore, we conducted Western blot analysis in the established stable cell lines and confirmed that the protein expression level of GDF15 was downregulated following the knockdown of SENP3 (Fig. 4C). Moreover, we confirmed that GDF15 was not regulated by SENP3 at the mRNA level (Fig. 4D). We also measured GDF15 in patient tissues and found that GDF15 was highly expressed in cancer tissues, consistent with SENP3 expression trends (Fig. S3). Interestingly, the result showed that MG132 treatment inhibited the degradation of GDF15 in the control group and the SENP3 knockdown group (Fig. 4E). Meanwhile, by treating cells with cycloheximide (CHX) to inhibit protein biosynthesis, we found that the knockout of SENP3 led to a shortened half-life of GDF15 (Fig. 4F). These data indicated that GDF15 might be a downstream target protein of SENP3.

SENP3 promotes the progression of CRC through GDF15

Next, we explored whether the pro-cancerous effects exerted by SENP3 are mediated by GDF15. Stable cell lines with SENP3 overexpression and GDF15 knockdown were established, and the successful establishment of these cell lines was confirmed by western blot analysis (Fig. 5A). Colony formation assays were then conducted using these established CRC cell lines, and the results revealed that knocking down GDF15 decreased the enhanced colony formation ability driven by SENP3 overexpression (Fig. 5B, C). To further substantiate this finding, cell viability assays were performed. The data also implied that downregulation of GDF15 could counteract the pro-proliferative effect of SENP3 (Fig. 5D). Subsequently, we conducted validation studies in vivo using mouse models. As expected, the results obtained from the mouse xenograft model were consistent with those from in vitro experiments. Knockdown of GDF15 partially attenuated the accelerated tumor growth driven by SENP3 overexpression (Fig. 5E, F). To further substantiate our conclusion, we overexpressed GDF15 in SENP3-knockdown CRC cells and subsequently repeated a series of cellular functional validation experiments. The results indicated that exogenous overexpression of GDF15 reversed the inhibition of cancer progression caused by SENP3 knockdown (Fig. S5). These results implied that GDF15 might mediate the oncogenic role of SENP3 in CRC.

Discussion

Colorectal cancer has emerged as a significant public health issue in contemporary society, characterized not only by its rising incidence and mortality rates but also by the limited efficacy of current chemotherapy, targeted therapies, and immunotherapy in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer diagnosed at advanced stages [20]. To date, numerous studies in the field of PTMs have identified numerous targets that influence the biological processes and treatment of CRC.

Protein SUMOylation refers to the dynamic targeting of numerous protein substrates at lysine residues by small ubiquitin-like modifiers under the coordination of ligases, a process that can be reversed by SUMO proteases (SENPs). SENP3 is one of the six SENP isoforms and primarily catalyzes the hydrolysis of SUMO-2/3. The process of de-SUMOylation may disrupt homeostasis within the organism, potentially leading to tumorigenesis [21, 22]. Existing studies have indicated that SENP3 interacts with SIX1 and mediates its deSUMOylation, thereby altering protein stability and promoting the progression of prostate cancer [23]. Some scholars have elucidated that SENP3 is a critical regulatory factor for Treg cells, controlling the SUMOylation of BACH2 through nuclear localization. Its deficiency leads to enhanced T cell activation and an augmented anti-tumor T cell response [24]. Conversely, some studies have confirmed that the deficiency of SENP3 in dendritic cells inhibits STING-dependent type I interferon signaling and diminishes anti-tumor immune responses [25]. It is evident that SENP3 may play a multifaceted role in the process of tumor development. We have observed that there is a lack of detailed reports regarding the specific mechanisms by which SENP3 influences the biological processes of colorectal cancer, which consequently became the focus of our investigation.

In our patient cohort, SENP3 was upregulated in a subgroup of colorectal cancers, and analysis of public cohorts confirmed an association between high SENP3 expression and worse survival. To confirm the oncogenic role of SENP3 in colorectal cancer, we conducted a series of in vitro cellular functional experiments, which demonstrated that SENP3 enhances the growth capacity of tumor cells. Furthermore, in the xenograft subcutaneous tumor model, SENP3 also exhibited the ability to accelerate tumor growth. Considering the modifying effect of SENP3 on proteins, we conducted a proteomic analysis. Thus, we identified several differentially expressed proteins, including GDF15. Research has indicated that the transforming growth factor-beta superfamily (TGF-β superfamily), as a group of peptide signaling molecules that play critical regulatory roles in processes such as cell proliferation and differentiation, includes GDF15 as one of its members. GDF15 participates in various biological processes such as inflammatory injury, hypoxic stress, and tumorigenesis by modulating its own levels in response to stress signals [26]. GDF15 has exhibited a certain role in promoting tumor progression or metastasis in melanoma [27], cervical cancer [28], prostate cancer [29], and various gastrointestinal tumors [30, 31]. Based on its significant fold change and low P-value among the differentially expressed proteins, we propose that GDF15 is highly likely to be a potential target protein through which SENP3 exerts its pro-cancer effects.

We validated that the knockdown of SENP3 resulted in a downregulation of GDF15 expression levels in CRC cells. Interestingly, our research data indicated that SENP3 enhances the stability of the GDF15 protein. This phenomenon may be related to the interactions between SUMOylation and ubiquitination that have already been reported [32, 33]. Subsequently, a series of rescue experiments, both in vitro and in vivo, confirmed that GDF15 knockdown reverses the promoting effect of SENP3 on CRC progression.

Conclusions

This study has elucidated the phenomenon of elevated SENP3 expression in CRC and confirmed that SENP3 acts as an oncogenic factor by a combination of molecular, cellular, and animal model approaches. We have proposed that the underlying molecular mechanism responsible for this phenomenon involves SENP3 stabilizing the target protein GDF15, thereby promoting tumor progression. This study advances our understanding of the molecular mechanisms by which SENP3 drives CRC progression and identifies the SENP3/GDF15 axis as a promising therapeutic target, offering new avenues for optimizing CRC treatment strategies.
