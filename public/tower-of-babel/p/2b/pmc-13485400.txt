Candida glabrata Empyema Unmasked After Antibacterial Therapy: Evidence of Early Polymicrobial Pleural Infection
Authors: Paolo Romero Merino, Oscar R Rivera, Anabel Huaman Boza, Ruth G Díaz Pérez, Alison Cristina Jimenez Ordoñez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Invasive candidiasis is a significant cause of morbidity and mortality, particularly among elderly patients and those with multiple comorbidities. In recent years, a broader spectrum of clinical presentations has been recognized, including localized infections and rare extrapulmonary manifestations [1].

Candida empyema is an uncommon presentation of this pathogen, characterized by high mortality and limited evidence regarding its management due to the scarcity of available data. Diagnosis is particularly challenging, as it typically presents as part of a polymicrobial infection, making identification of the fungal etiology difficult and often resulting in delays in the initiation of appropriate treatment [2]. Furthermore, fungal empyema has been associated with worse clinical outcomes compared to bacterial empyema, including longer hospital stays and higher mortality rates [3].

Multiple risk factors have been described in the development of fungal empyema, including prolonged use of broad-spectrum antibiotics, chronic diseases, advanced age, recent invasive procedures, and a history of malignancy. Complex pulmonary infections, such as parapneumonic empyema, may predispose to secondary fungal invasion, particularly in patients who exhibit clinical deterioration despite optimal antibacterial management [1].

We present the case of a 79-year-old patient with a history of advanced kidney disease and malignancy, who developed a complicated empyema in the context of community-acquired pneumonia, with an unfavorable clinical course despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy, highlighting the importance of considering fungal etiologies such as Candida glabrata in cases of therapeutic failure.

Case presentation

A 79-year-old man with stage III chronic kidney disease, prior total laryngectomy with permanent tracheostomy for laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma, and advanced cognitive impairment presented with five days of increased tracheal secretions, malaise, and worsening baseline mental status.

On initial evaluation, vital signs were consistent with a systemic inflammatory response and mild hypoxemia (Table 1). Laboratory studies demonstrated marked leukocytosis with neutrophilia, significantly elevated inflammatory markers, and acute kidney injury compared to baseline (Table 2). Chest radiography revealed a right-sided pleural effusion without cavitation or loculation (Figure 1).

Homogeneous opacity in the right lower hemithorax (black circle) with blunting of the costophrenic angle and hemidiaphragm, consistent with pleural effusion without evidence of cavitation or loculation. Left lung field is clear.

He was diagnosed with community-acquired pneumonia complicated by parapneumonic effusion and initiated on empiric broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy. Given a weakly positive urinary antigen test for Legionella pneumophila, antimicrobial coverage was expanded to include atypical pathogens. No confirmatory microbiologic testing for Legionella was obtained.

During the initial 48-72 hours of hospitalization, the patient demonstrated transient clinical and laboratory improvement, with partial reduction in inflammatory markers. However, on hospital day 3, he developed worsening dyspnea, increased oxygen requirements, and recurrent systemic inflammation.

On hospital day 6, repeat imaging revealed right middle and lower lobe atelectasis with a new air-fluid level (Figure 2). Point-of-care ultrasonography demonstrated a loculated pleural effusion with internal septations. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the chest confirmed a loculated pleural collection containing an air-fluid level and an adjacent cavitary lesion, findings consistent with progression to complicated empyema with associated parenchymal necrosis (Figure 3).

Ultrasound-guided placement of a pigtail catheter yielded purulent fluid. Pleural fluid analysis was consistent with empyema, and Gram stain and bacterial cultures were negative. Several days after drainage, pleural fluid cultures grew C. glabrata, susceptible to echinocandins, azoles, and amphotericin B. Given the isolation of C. glabrata from purulent pleural fluid in the absence of bacterial pathogens, along with the patient’s clinical deterioration and subsequent response to antifungal therapy, this finding was considered consistent with true fungal empyema rather than colonization or contamination.

Antibacterial therapy was discontinued, and targeted antifungal therapy with an echinocandin (micafungin) was initiated. The patient also underwent continued pleural drainage and intrapleural fibrinolytic therapy with tissue plasminogen activator and DNase, resulting in improved drainage and radiographic resolution of loculations.

Over the subsequent days, the patient demonstrated progressive clinical improvement, including resolution of leukocytosis and inflammatory markers, decreased oxygen requirements, and radiographic reduction of the pleural collection. He was transitioned to oral antifungal therapy and discharged with outpatient follow-up.

Discussion

Fungal empyema thoracis is an uncommon but highly morbid form of pleural infection, most frequently caused by Candida species, particularly Candida albicans and C. glabrata [4-6]. Compared to bacterial empyema, it is typically associated with thoracoabdominal surgery, esophageal perforation, or direct pleural contamination, and only rarely arises as a complication of primary pulmonary infection [2,4]. However, emerging evidence suggests that fungal empyema may be underrecognized in non-surgical patients, particularly those with multiple comorbidities or chronic airway alterations [5,6].

The clinical course observed in this case supports early fungal involvement preceding the overt development of empyema. Initial imaging demonstrated a simple pleural effusion without loculation, followed by rapid progression to a complex, loculated empyema with air-fluid levels and adjacent parenchymal necrosis. This accelerated evolution suggests that fungal infection may have been present at an early stage but remained clinically and radiographically unapparent. While fungal empyema has often been described as indolent, this case highlights that disease progression can be significantly more aggressive in susceptible hosts [5].

The temporal relationship between antibacterial therapy and subsequent clinical deterioration further supports a dynamic and evolving infectious process. Early transient improvement likely reflected partial control of a bacterial component, whereas the later progression may be explained by fungal predominance. Polymicrobial pleural infections, including bacterial-fungal coinfection, have been increasingly recognized, with Candida species isolated alongside bacteria in a substantial proportion of cases [2,7]. In this context, initial antibacterial therapy may have reduced bacterial burden while simultaneously altering the microbial ecosystem, allowing fungal organisms to proliferate and become clinically dominant.

This mechanism is biologically plausible, as broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure is a well-established risk factor for invasive candidiasis due to disruption of normal microbial flora and facilitation of fungal overgrowth [7,8]. C. glabrata, in particular, is characterized by reduced intrinsic virulence compared to C. albicans, but enhanced capacity for persistence, adaptation, and resistance under selective pressure [8]. These features may contribute to delayed clinical recognition followed by rapid progression once fungal proliferation becomes established.

The absence of bacterial growth in pleural fluid cultures does not exclude an earlier polymicrobial process, particularly in the setting of prior antibiotic exposure. Instead, it likely reflects microbiological suppression of bacterial pathogens, unmasking the fungal component at a later stage of disease. Similar diagnostic patterns have been described in fungal empyema, where delayed isolation of Candida may obscure the initial complexity of infection [2,6].

Importantly, this case occurred in the absence of classical predisposing factors such as recent thoracic surgery or esophageal perforation. The patient’s advanced age, chronic kidney disease, prior malignancy, and permanent tracheostomy likely contributed to increased susceptibility. Chronic airway alteration may impair mucociliary clearance and promote recurrent microaspiration, facilitating translocation of colonizing organisms, including Candida species, into the lower respiratory tract and pleural space [4,5]. Additionally, comorbid conditions such as diabetes, malignancy, and renal disease have been consistently associated with worse outcomes in fungal empyema [4,6].

The rapid progression observed contrasts with prior reports describing a more protracted clinical course, with a mean time to diagnosis extending over several weeks [5]. This discrepancy underscores the heterogeneity of fungal empyema and suggests that host factors, microbial characteristics, and prior antimicrobial exposure may significantly influence disease trajectory.

Establishing the diagnosis of fungal empyema requires careful interpretation, as isolation of Candida species from respiratory specimens frequently represents colonization. In this case, the identification of C. glabrata from purulent pleural fluid, in conjunction with clinical deterioration despite appropriate antibacterial therapy and subsequent improvement following antifungal treatment, strongly supports true invasive infection rather than contamination or colonization [1,2].

Management of fungal empyema remains challenging and is not standardized. Adequate pleural drainage is a critical component of therapy and has been consistently associated with improved outcomes [3]. Systemic antifungal therapy is also essential, with echinocandins recommended as first-line agents for invasive candidiasis, particularly in infections caused by C. glabrata, given their favorable activity and resistance profile [7]. However, optimal antifungal selection in pleural infections remains uncertain due to variable drug penetration and limited comparative data [2]. In this case, treatment with micafungin combined with effective pleural drainage resulted in favorable clinical and radiologic outcomes.

Fungal empyema is associated with high mortality, with reported rates ranging from approximately 30% to over 60%, particularly among immunocompromised or critically ill patients [4-6]. Despite these unfavorable outcomes, early recognition, prompt source control, and timely initiation of appropriate antifungal therapy can significantly improve prognosis.

This case underscores that fungal empyema may evolve earlier and more aggressively than traditionally recognized, potentially emerging within a polymicrobial infectious context and becoming clinically dominant following antibacterial therapy. Recognition of this pattern is essential, particularly in patients with persistent or worsening pleural infection despite appropriate antibiotic management.

Conclusions

This case highlights C. glabrata empyema that was initially masked by transient clinical and biochemical improvement following broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy, leading to a delay in recognizing the true fungal etiology. Early response likely corresponded to partial treatment of a concomitant bacterial component, while the fungal infection persisted and progressively evolved, becoming clinically evident only after deterioration at the time of planned discharge. This temporal pattern reflects the slower and often less apparent progression of fungal infections compared to bacterial processes.

From a clinical standpoint, this case reinforces the need for caution when interpreting early therapeutic responses, as they may be misleading in polymicrobial or evolving infections. Clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion for fungal involvement in patients with persistent or recrudescent pleural disease despite adequate antibiotics. Further studies are warranted to better define the natural history and optimal management of fungal empyema. Early consideration of fungal pathogens in non-resolving pleural infections may represent a critical step in improving outcomes in this high-mortality condition.
