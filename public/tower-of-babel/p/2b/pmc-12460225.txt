From texture analysis to artificial intelligence: global research landscape and evolutionary trajectory of radiomics in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Xiong Teng, Quan-Neng Luo, Yi-Di Chen, Tao Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) poses a substantial global health burden with high morbidity and mortality rates. Radiomics, which extracts quantitative features from medical images to develop predictive models, has emerged as a promising non-invasive approach for HCC diagnosis and management. However, comprehensive analysis of research trends in this field remains limited.MethodsWe conducted a systematic bibliometric analysis of radiomics applications in HCC using literature from the Web of Science Core Collection (January 2006–April 2025). Publications were analyzed using CiteSpace, VOSviewer, R, and Python scripts to evaluate publication patterns, citation metrics, institutional contributions, keyword evolution, and collaboration networks.ResultsAmong 906 included publications, we observed exponential growth, particularly accelerating after 2019. A global landscape analysis revealed China as the leader in publication volume, while the USA acted as the primary international collaboration hub. Countries like South Korea and the UK demonstrated higher average citation impact. Sun Yat-sen University was the most productive institution. Research themes evolved from fundamental texture analysis and CT/MRI applications toward predicting microvascular invasion, assessing treatment response (especially TACE), and prognostic modeling, driven recently by the deep integration of artificial intelligence (AI) and deep learning. Co-citation analysis revealed core knowledge clusters spanning radiomics methodology, clinical management, and landmark applications, demonstrating the field’s interdisciplinary nature.ConclusionRadiomics in HCC represents a rapidly expanding, AI-driven field characterized by extensive multidisciplinary collaboration. Future priorities should emphasize standardization, large-scale multicenter validation, enhanced international cooperation, and clinical translation to maximize radiomics’ potential in precision HCC oncology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03620-x.Keywords: Radiomics, Hepatocellular carcinoma, Bibliometric analysis, Artificial intelligence, Research trends

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common malignancy globally and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths, with over 900,000 new cases and 830,000 deaths reported worldwide in 2022; China accounts for approximately half of both global incidence and mortality [1]. Although multimodal treatment strategies guided by systems such as the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging system [2] and the China Liver Cancer (CNLC) staging system [3] have improved survival for some patients, late diagnosis and high post-treatment recurrence rates [4] remain critical clinical challenges. Traditional imaging relies on experiential interpretation by physicians and has limited capability for quantitative assessment of tumor microenvironment heterogeneity, characterized by spatial variations in cellular composition, angiogenic activity, and immune cell infiltration across HCC lesions [5, 6]. Such heterogeneity significantly influences patient prognosis and response to treatment [7]. In contrast, although invasive biopsy enables direct tissue characterization, it remains constrained by sampling bias and procedural risks [8]. These limitations underscore the need for noninvasive, quantitative tools—such as radiomics—to capture the complex biological landscape of HCC. Against this backdrop, Radiomics and its key frontier branch, Radiogenomics, both offer a revolutionary tool for the diagnosis, treatment, and management of HCC. The former involves the high-throughput extraction of quantitative features from medical images such as CT, MRI, and ultrasound to build predictive models using machine learning; the latter is dedicated to correlating these imaging features with genomic and other multi-omics data, aiming to uncover the biological underpinnings of imaging phenotypes [9].

The core methodology of radiomics, namely texture analysis, has a history in medical imaging dating back to the 1970s [10]. In 2012, Lambin et al. [11] formally introduced the concept of “radiomics,” marking the beginning of a systematic and high-throughput era in this field. This new phase aimed to transform the vast spectrum of imaging features into mineable data to support clinical decision-making. Since then, radiomics has exhibited unique advantages across several key aspects of HCC management. In diagnosis, radiomics facilitates the early differential diagnosis of HCC and stratification of pathological grades [12, 13]. For prognostic assessment, radiomics offers value in predicting microvascular invasion (MVI) and recurrence risk [14, 15]. This predictive capability is clinically significant, as it can guide surgical decisions, such as determining the extent of resection needed to minimize recurrence risk. Moreover, selected studies have explored its utility in characterizing the tumor immune microenvironment and predicting molecular biomarkers, such as PD-1/PD-L1 expression [16, 17]. Regarding therapeutic assessment, radiomics has been applied to evaluate the efficacy of transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) and predict radiation-induced liver injury [18, 19]. Notably, these thematic groupings highlight both the breadth and depth of radiomics research while underscoring the necessity of critical appraisal and selective citation of landmark studies in the field. In recent years, cross-omics integration strategies [20] have revealed correlations between molecular levels and radiomics [21], providing a molecular basis for guiding HCC treatment using radiomics. However, the development of the field is still constrained by bottlenecks such as significant technical variability, difficulties in clinical translation, lack of standardization, and insufficient interpretability [22]. Addressing these limitations is a crucial issue for the continued advancement of the field.

By quantifying the contributions of countries, institutions, and authors and their collaboration networks, bibliometric analysis has become a rigorous method capable of providing in-depth insights into the evolutionary trends of a discipline [23, 24]. Methodologically, it primarily involves performance analysis to evaluate research impact and science mapping to visualize the intellectual and conceptual structures of a field using tools like CiteSpace, VOSviewer and Bibliometrix package (R Package) [25–27]. This analytical approach has proven particularly effective for systematically assessing emerging and interdisciplinary fields, allowing researchers to develop metrics that track their development, identify key players based on impact rather than sheer volume, and map knowledge flows across different domains [28–30].

While previous studies on radiomics in HCC have mostly focused on technical applications, a comprehensive bibliometric analysis of the field has not yet been reported. Given the rapid, AI-driven expansion of this domain and its clinical importance, a systematic assessment is crucial. Therefore, this study aims to apply these established frameworks to answer the following key questions: (1) What is the global research landscape and collaborative structure of radiomics in HCC? (2) How have the core research themes and hotspots evolved over time, particularly with the integration of AI? (3) What constitutes the foundational knowledge base of this interdisciplinary field?

Methods

Data source and search strategy

The Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) was selected as the data source for this study. This decision was based on WoSCC’s provision of comprehensive, standardized citation data, which is essential for co-citation analysis, and its established use as a standard, high-quality data source in bibliometric research, ensuring the comparability of our results.

Our search strategy was designed to be highly sensitive to capture the field’s full evolutionary trajectory, including early foundational studies. On April 1, 2025, we searched articles indexed in the Science Citation Index Expanded (SCIE) from January 2000 to April 2025. The inclusion of terms such as “texture analysis” and “quantitative imaging” was intentional, as they represent the conceptual precursors to modern radiomics, which was formally named around 2012. The following search query was used: TS=(“radiomics” OR “radiogenomics” OR “texture analysis” OR “quantitative imaging” OR “imaging biomarker*” OR “radiomic feature*”) AND TS=(“hepatocellular carcinoma” OR “HCC” OR “liver cancer” OR “hepatic cancer” OR “primary liver cancer” OR “primary hepatic cancer” OR “primary liver carcinoma” OR “malignant hepatoma” OR “liver neoplas*” OR “hepatic neoplas*” OR “liver tumor*” OR “hepatic tumor*”). To clarify the composition of the initial search results, a term-by-term analysis was also conducted, with a detailed breakdown provided in Supplementary Table S3. This analysis revealed that “radiomics” was the primary contributor to the retrieved set.

Literature screening and selection

The search strategy yielded 1305 initial records. These records underwent a rigorous, multi-stage screening process, as detailed in Fig. 1. First, we applied filters to include only “articles” and “reviews” and to exclude non-English publications, resulting in 1195 manuscripts for manual review. To evaluate precision, two reviewers (X.T. and QN.L.) independently reviewed a random sample of 50 titles/abstracts. The inter-rater agreement was substantial (Cohen’s κ = 0.78), with 40 records meeting criteria (80% precision).

Screening flowchart

Subsequently, two independent reviewers (X.T. and QN.L.) screened the titles and abstracts to exclude irrelevant studies based on predefined criteria. Studies were removed if they were: (a) not focused on primary human hepatocellular carcinoma (e.g., metastatic cancers, animal models); (b) preclinical in nature (e.g., phantom studies, in-vitro experiments); or (c) did not involve a complete radiomics workflow of quantitative feature analysis for prediction or diagnosis. Following this, their results were compared to identify any discrepancies in article selection. These disagreements were resolved through a two-step process: first, the reviewers engaged in a discussion to reach a consensus. If a consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (YD.C.) was consulted to make the final determination.

This manual screening excluded 289 articles. After confirming no duplicates remained, a final corpus of 906 publications was established for analysis. Notably, the publication dates of the final included literature ranged from January 2006 to April 2025, which reflects the emergence of the first relevant studies in our dataset and defines the timeframe for our analysis. Finally, the full records and cited references for these publications were exported for subsequent analysis.

Analytical tools and metrics

To conduct a comprehensive bibliometric analysis, we employed a complementary suite of specialized software tools, each selected for its specific strengths in data analysis and visualization. This multi-tool approach ensured a robust and multifaceted examination of the research landscape.

Python (version 3.11), along with the pandas, matplotlib, and seaborn libraries, was employed for data processing, statistical analysis, and visualization. Specifically, Python scripts were used to parse the raw literature data exported from WoSCC, calculate annual publication frequencies, and generate the publication trend graph (Fig. 2A). To quantitatively model the growth trajectory of publications, we compared four common growth models: exponential, logistic, Gompertz, and power function. The best-fit model was selected based on a comprehensive evaluation of multiple statistical metrics: the highest coefficient of determination (R² and adjusted R²) and the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and Root Mean Square Error (RMSE).

CiteSpace [25] (version 6.4 R1) was utilized for its strengths in detecting temporal patterns and identifying pivotal intellectual turning points. Its primary applications in this study were keyword timeline analysis, citation burst detection for both keywords and references, and constructing dual-map overlays of journals. For all CiteSpace analyses, the time period was set from January 2006 to April 2025, with time slices of one-year intervals. To enhance network clarity, all generated maps were pruned using the Pathfinder algorithm. Keyword clusters were identified using the Log-Likelihood Ratio (LLR) algorithm. The structural quality of the generated networks and the credibility of the clusters were quantitatively evaluated using two key metrics: Modularity (Q score) and Mean Silhouette (S score). A Q score > 0.3 indicates a significant community structure, while an S score > 0.7 suggests a highly credible clustering result. To ensure the robustness of the keyword co-occurrence network clustering, a sensitivity analysis was performed. We tested three configurations by varying the g-index selection parameter (k = 3, k = 5, and k = 7). The optimal clustering was selected based on a comprehensive evaluation of Q score and S score. Specific node selection thresholds for each analysis are detailed in the Results section. Following algorithmic clustering and labeling using the LLR algorithm, the resulting clusters were manually reviewed for semantic coherence by two senior authors (T.P. and YD.C.), who are experts in HCC and radiomics. This author-led validation step confirmed that the algorithmically identified keyword groupings represented meaningful and distinct research themes.

VOSviewer (version 1.6.20) [26] was chosen for its superior capability in constructing and visualizing large-scale bibliometric networks. It was used to generate co-authorship, co-occurrence, and co-citation maps for countries, institutions, authors, and journals. For all VOSviewer analyses, the full counting method was applied to assign equal weight to each co-authored or co-cited item. For normalization, the association strength method was applied. The network layout was subsequently generated using VOSviewer’s default algorithm, which positions items based on their co-occurrence weights. To ensure objectivity and reproducibility, we based our network analysis inclusion thresholds on data distribution. For each analysis, we ranked all items by their frequency or co-citation counts. We then selected a threshold designed to capture the top 1–3% of the most significant items. This strategy effectively visualizes the core intellectual structure of the field while maintaining network clarity. The specific thresholds derived from this rule are detailed in the Results section for each corresponding analysis.

Online bibliometric platforms (http://bibliometric.com/ and Biblioshiny [27]) were used to compute descriptive bibliometric indicators, such as the total link strength of countries and institutions, and to generate the three-field Sankey diagram (Fig. 8), which visualizes the relationships between authors, keywords, and countries.

Results

According to the inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 906 publications were included in the analysis. These publications were authored by 5432 authors from 1150 institutions in 42 countries or regions, published in 215 journals, and cited 21,121 references from 13,866 authors in 3311 journals.

Annual publication trend analysis

Research in HCC radiomics has undergone exponential growth since 2006, evolving through three distinct phases (Fig. 2A). The initial exploratory period (2006–2012) was characterized by a slow output of fewer than five articles annually, primarily focused on foundational texture analysis in CT imaging. This was followed by a growth period (2013–2018), where annual publications increased to 10–30 articles. This phase coincided with the formal introduction of the “radiomics” concept, shifting the research focus towards clinical applications. The field entered a rapid expansion period from 2019 onwards, with annual publications surging to over 50, and peaking at 183 articles in 2023. This surge is strongly associated with the integration of machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) technologies. Following a comparison of four growth models, the cumulative publication trend was best described by an exponential function (N(t) = 3.90e^(0.3021t) − 11.07, R² = 0.9961; see Supplementary Table S1), which serves as a descriptive tool to illustrate the rapid acceleration. The steep trajectory suggests the field has not yet reached saturation and is poised for continued high-speed development.

China emerged as the most prolific contributor, accounting for the majority of publications (578 articles), followed by the USA, Italy, and Germany, indicating a significant national focus on this research area.

However, publication volume does not solely define influence. In terms of research impact, measured by Average Article Citation (AAC), South Korea (45.69 AAC) and the UK (43.14 AAC) demonstrated superior performance. This suggests their research, while less voluminous, is highly influential and may prioritize novel methodologies or breakthrough findings that attract significant citations.

The international collaboration network reveals another layer of influence. Metrics such as Total Link Strength (TLS, measuring the total number of collaborations) and betweenness centrality (a measure of a node’s role as a bridge in the network) highlight key players. The USA, with high centrality (0.34) and the strongest link strength (146), functions as the primary collaboration hub. In contrast, France, possessing the highest centrality (0.39), acts as a critical network bridge, facilitating knowledge flow between otherwise disconnected research clusters. Notably, high-impact countries like South Korea and Japan show minimal network centrality (0.00). This pattern suggests their influential research may be produced within focused, less internationally connected research groups, potentially limiting the global dissemination of their innovations.

Journal analysis

Research findings in this field were published across 215 journals, with the top ten journals by publication count shown in Table 5; Fig. 6. Among them, Frontiers in Oncology was the journal with the highest number of related publications (77 articles), with its publication peak around 2022. Following closely were European Radiology (53 articles) and Abdominal Radiology (53 articles), with their publication peaks around 2023 and 2024, respectively. Together, they form the main platforms for publications in this research area. This distribution clearly reflects an interdisciplinary trend, with research outputs primarily concentrated in oncology and radiology journals, highly aligning with the research themes.

Discussion

Our bibliometric analysis shows that radiomics in HCC has rapidly evolved from a technical niche into a major, AI-driven field. This exponential growth is driven by both mature AI technologies and the clinical demand for non-invasive biomarkers. The global research landscape is also distinct. China leads in publication volume, while the United States is the central hub for international collaboration. Research themes have likewise evolved, shifting from basic imaging techniques toward predictive models for clinical problems like MVI. However, this progress also highlights a key challenge: a gap between the field’s demonstrated potential and the real-world barriers to its application. Overcoming issues like standardization and clinical translation is essential to realize the full clinical value of radiomics in HCC.

Interpretation of research trends, landscape, and hotspots

Research on radiomics in HCC shows an exponential growth trend (Fig. 2), consistent with the global acceptance of precision medicine concepts and the widespread application of AI in medical imaging [34–36]. This growth is also related to radiomics’ commitment to addressing key issues in HCC clinical practice. To tackle difficulties in early diagnosis, Guo et al. developed the ALARM model to improve detection rates [37]. To meet the demand for non-invasive risk stratification, multiple studies have explored the non-invasive assessment of MVI [14]. Additionally, individualized treatment response models have been developed to predict and evaluate the efficacy of TACE or PD-1 inhibitors [38, 39, 40] Concurrently, the popularization of open-source radiomics analysis tools (e.g., Pyradiomics) [41] and significant improvements in computational power have provided crucial technical support for research in this field. Notably, the research outbreak period is highly correlated with the widespread application of the “radiomics” concept [11] and the application of DL technology to medical radiomics [42, 43]. This further highlights that the development of this field is jointly driven by clinical demand, technological advancements, and multidisciplinary integration.

China’s dominant position in publication volume (Fig. 3) is underpinned by a confluence of factors: a high domestic disease burden creating urgent clinical demand, strong national strategic support for AI and precision medicine, and access to large patient datasets. This high productivity, however, is channeled through a collaboration network that is predominantly domestic, with the USA as its primary international partner. This structure is also mirrored at the institutional level (Fig. 4). While this model is effective at generating high-volume research, its relative insularity risks limiting methodological diversity and the cross-ethnic generalizability of its findings—a critical challenge for the global clinical translation of radiomics.

In contrast, the global landscape reveals other influential models of collaboration. The USA and several European nations act as open international hubs, distinguished by their high network centrality. This model facilitates the integration of diverse expertise and is structured to enhance the potential generalizability of research findings. On another axis, nations like South Korea and Japan demonstrate a notable discrepancy between high research impact and low network centrality. Our data shows institutions like Sungkyunkwan University achieving the highest average citation impact (Table 3, Table S2), yet their national research ecosystems exhibit minimal network centrality. This discrepancy, likely influenced by factors from linguistic preferences to distinct research funding structures, creates a critical bottleneck. Groundbreaking methodologies or high-quality findings from these regions may face delayed global adoption, potentially slowing overall innovation and leading to redundant research efforts elsewhere.

To understand the nature of the high-impact contributions from low-centrality countries, a qualitative look at their most influential publications is revealing. For instance, a key high-impact study from South Korea by Choi et al. [44] focused on establishing a new prognostic tool, demonstrating that specific MRI imaging patterns could effectively predict tumor recurrence after surgery. Similarly, influential work from Japan [45] centered on advancing new analytical techniques, introducing and evaluating an advanced mathematical approach, Topological Data Analysis, for hepatic tumor classification against standard texture analysis. This focus on proposing novel clinical biomarkers or establishing new research standards contrasts with the work often seen from high-volume, high-centrality centers, which more frequently leverage large datasets to validate existing models or conduct broad application studies. This distinction reveals an important pattern of complementary innovation: global progress in this field appears to be driven jointly by the large-scale validation from high-output hubs and these targeted, high-impact breakthroughs from more focused research groups.

To resolve these structural imbalances and foster a more integrated global research network, a multi-pronged strategy is essential. The core objective should be to build “bridges” connecting high-volume centers with high-impact, low-centrality nodes. This requires concrete actions beyond general calls for cooperation. Firstly, forming international consortia, modeled after successful examples like the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI), is crucial for harmonizing protocols and enabling secure, multi-center data sharing. Secondly, funding agencies should create targeted grant schemes that specifically mandate or heavily incentivize multi-regional collaborations, prioritizing the validation of predictive models on diverse, multi-ethnic datasets. Finally, the research community should leverage digital platforms to facilitate sustained international collaboration and knowledge exchange, effectively mitigating the barriers of geographical distance and cost. Implementing these measures is vital for promoting knowledge complementarity and accelerating the translation of radiomics from a research tool into a reliable component of global precision oncology.

Research hotspots and thematic evolution reflect the field’s focal points and developmental patterns. The keyword timeline (Fig. 7D) depicts a progression from early CT/MRI-based texture analysis to a phase centered on “radiomics,” focusing on clinical applications like MVI and prognostic prediction, and more recently, to a new stage of deep integration with “deep learning,” “machine learning,” and “artificial intelligence” data and algorithms. Among these, “Microvascular invasion” has become one of the core keywords throughout the research (Fig. 7B) due to its critical importance in guiding surgical strategies and determining prognosis, with numerous studies dedicated to non-invasive preoperative MVI prediction using radiomics [31, 32, 46, 47]. The rise of DL (Fig. 7C, cluster #3) has endowed radiomics with powerful end-to-end feature learning and complex pattern recognition capabilities [42, 43, 48], demonstrating superior performance over traditional ML in certain tasks and holding the potential to overcome the bottlenecks of manual feature engineering in conventional methods [49, 50]. However, the “black-box” nature of DL models also presents challenges in terms of poor interpretability; the microscopic meaning of DL features and the causal logic of ML modeling require further investigation. In the keyword burst analysis (Fig. 9), recently bursting terms like “survival,” “biomarker,” “signature,” and “tumor heterogeneity” indicate that future research will increasingly focus on predicting clinical endpoint events [15, 51], identifying imaging biomarkers [52], constructing predictive models, and exploring the heterogeneity within tumors and the peritumoral microenvironment [53, 54]. Multi-omics integration [55, 56], as an emerging direction, by combining imaging features with gene mutations, pathway activities, or pathological phenotypes to reveal the biological mechanisms behind imaging features, will effectively enhance the accuracy of predictive models and represents an important frontier and future direction for the field.

The knowledge base of the field is constituted by co-cited authors/journals, and co-citation analysis (Figs. 10 and 11) reflects the multidisciplinary nature of this domain. Highly co-cited authors, such as radiomics methodology experts Lambin, P [57] and Gillies, RJ [5], laid the foundational theories of radiomics. International liver cancer clinical experts like Llovet, JM and Bruix, J published authoritative BCLC staging [2, 58] guidelines. Additionally, scholars such as Kudo, M [59] and Xu, X [31] made significant contributions to HCC imaging diagnosis/treatment and the development of radiomics application models, respectively. Highly co-cited journals, including radiology journals like Radiology and European Radiology, and hepatology journals like Journal of Hepatology and Hepatology, are the main platforms for knowledge dissemination, representing core sources of the knowledge base in their respective fields. The journal dual-map overlay analysis (Fig. 10C) shows that although research findings in this field are often published in clinical medicine or biology journals, their theoretical underpinnings extensively draw from molecular biology, genetics, clinical medicine, surgery, and even physics, engineering, and computational science. Analysis of co-cited authors, literature, and journals reveals that the successful application of radiomics in HCC is essentially the result of a deep integration of knowledge from multiple domains, including imaging science, clinical medicine (hepatology, oncology), and data science.

Advantages and challenges of radiomics in HCC research

Radiomics research in the HCC field demonstrates significant advantages and potential. Firstly, the research is highly clinically problem-oriented, closely revolving around key issues in HCC diagnosis and treatment, such as early diagnosis [60], differential diagnosis [61, 62], prediction of pathological features [60, 63], treatment efficacy assessment [63–65, 66], and prognostic judgment [67, 68]. Secondly, the field exhibits rapid technological application and iteration, actively integrating various algorithms from traditional ML to advanced DL. Subsequently, numerous retrospective studies have preliminarily confirmed its clinical potential, showing high predictive efficacy (AUC often between 0.8 and 0.95). Finally, its non-invasive nature makes it patient-friendly and repeatable, holding promise as an important supplement or even an alternative to invasive biopsy. However, the field currently faces severe challenges that hinder its transition from research to routine clinical application. Among these, insufficient standardization and reproducibility are recognized developmental bottlenecks in the radiomics field [69–71]. In practical application, this manifests across the entire radiomics workflow. (1) Variability in image acquisition: Radiomic features are highly sensitive to technical parameters. For instance, in CT imaging, differences in slice thickness, reconstruction algorithms, and the timing of contrast enhancement phases can significantly alter texture features. Similarly, in MRI, variations in sequence parameters, contrast agents, field strengths, and scanner vendors introduce non-biological variability that confounds analysis. (2) Inconsistent segmentation: Manual or semi-automated delineation of tumor and peritumoral regions remains a major source of variability, directly impacting the robustness of extracted features. (3) Lack of computational standardization: The diversity of feature extraction algorithms and software packages makes direct comparison of models from different studies nearly impossible [72, 73]. These factors collectively make it difficult to compare and validate research results across centers and studies, severely affecting the reliability of research conclusions. Addressing these issues requires strict adherence to established guidelines. Initiatives like the Image Biomarker Standardization Initiative (IBSI) (69) are critical, as they provide a common lexicon and mathematical definitions for hundreds of radiomic features, ensuring that a feature named ‘skewness’ is calculated identically regardless of the software used. Similarly, following reporting guidelines like TRIPOD-AI [74] enhances the transparency and reproducibility of prediction model studies.

Secondly, the challenge of clinical translation remains immense. Only a few studies have conducted prospective trials, and external validation is often insufficient, leaving the actual efficacy of models in real-world clinical settings questionable [75]. The lack of effective integration interfaces with existing medical information systems (Picture Archiving and Communication System (PACS)/Radiology Information System (RIS)) is another obstacle to practical clinical application. Furthermore, due to the prevalence of data silos and quality issues, high-quality, large-scale multicenter datasets, especially those containing matched multi-omics information, remain scarce resources. Establishing a culture and mechanisms for data sharing is an important method for model validation and improvement [76]. Insufficient model interpretability is another significant barrier to application; understanding the basis of model decisions is crucial for building clinical trust and discovering biological mechanisms [77]. The application of Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) technology plays an important role in enhancing model transparency and overcoming the “black-box” effect of ML and DL [77, 78].

Finally, as previously mentioned, insufficient international cooperation limits the applicability and impact of research findings. Multi-omics integration itself also faces technical difficulties in processing high-dimensional data and interpreting complex associations, necessitating multidisciplinary, multicenter international collaboration.

Implications for future research and clinical practice

To promote the healthy development of radiomics applications in the HCC field and ultimately achieve clinical translation, future efforts should focus on the following aspects. Research should strictly adhere to internationally recognized technical specifications and reporting guidelines, and conduct rigorously designed prospective, multicenter Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs) to evaluate the clinical efficacy of models in real-world settings. Research data should integrate multimodal radiomics and multi-omics data, utilizing advanced algorithms to explore the associations between imaging and biological data, thereby constructing more accurate predictive models. Furthermore, collaborative networks across disciplines/institutions/borders should be actively built to establish data-sharing mechanisms. Concurrently, emphasis should be placed on the research and development of XAI technology to enhance model transparency and trustworthiness while pursuing performance, potentially discovering new biomarkers or mechanisms.

In terms of technological innovation, emerging technologies are essential for overcoming key methodological hurdles. Dynamic radiomics [79] and automated segmentation tools [80], in particular, show significant promise for enhancing the field’s robustness and clinical utility. Automated segmentation can significantly improve reproducibility and efficiency. Manual segmentation is a primary bottleneck for radiomics, causing poor reproducibility and limiting large-scale analysis. Deep learning-based tools (e.g., U-Net, nnU-Net) directly address this by providing rapid, consistent, and accurate tumor delineation [81, 82]. These methods yield higher feature stability and are being integrated into end-to-end workflows to support robust models for predicting MVI, histologic grade, and treatment response (54) [83, 84]. Furthermore, by enabling the precise segmentation of the peritumoral microenvironment, they facilitate advanced heterogeneity analysis [85]. However, rigorous validation and standardization are critical for their widespread clinical adoption [86, 87]. Similarly, dynamic radiomics is vital for capturing tumor evolution. Static, single-time-point radiomics fails to capture the temporal evolution of HCC. Dynamic radiomics overcomes this by analyzing feature changes over time, offering deeper biological insights. Techniques like Dynamic Contrast-Enhanced MRI (DCE-MRI) have proven effective for preoperative MVI prediction and for assessing therapeutic response to treatments like TACE, often outperforming conventional criteria [88]. This longitudinal approach also helps differentiate aggressive tumor subtypes and monitor their evolution under treatment [79]. For clinical translation, key challenges remain in simplifying model complexity and establishing robust, multi-center validation to ensure the generalizability of these powerful techniques [89].

In clinical practice, clinicians need to continuously learn and understand the fundamental principles and inherent limitations of radiomics, and be able to critically interpret relevant research findings to participate in related clinical trials and real-world studies. Moreover, for radiomics models that may enter clinical application in the future, how to integrate them into existing clinical workflows to achieve real-time decision support needs to be a key research and development direction. This involves developing “radiomics-in-a-box” software modules that can be integrated directly into hospital PACS/RIS environments [90, 91]. Such integration would allow clinicians to apply validated models to patient images with ease. To further bridge the gap to routine clinical use, intuitive interfaces are needed. These could present model outputs, like risk scores or MVI probability heatmaps, as transparent overlays on the diagnostic images. Funding agencies should prioritize research projects aimed at standardization and clinical translation, and support the construction of multicenter HCC imaging and clinical/multi-omics databases.

Future radiomics research may extend beyond the hospital setting by integrating with novel data streams. This approach is supported by existing evidence; a recent systematic review demonstrated that wearable-derived metrics, such as circadian rhythms, are associated with overall survival in cancer patients [92]. Building on this, a key direction involves fusing a patient’s baseline radiomic signature with longitudinal data from HCC-specific technologies. The feasibility is demonstrated by emerging tools like wearable bioadhesive ultrasound elastography, which enables continuous tracking of liver stiffness in preclinical models [93]. Such integration could facilitate AI-driven alerts for real-time home monitoring, potentially allowing for more personalized, needs-based follow-up interventions. Another important direction is the development of “interventional radiomics” for direct use in the operating theater. Strong clinical justification exists for this, as studies have established that peritumoral stiffness, measured by preoperative shear-wave elastography, is a significant independent predictor of MVI and postoperative recurrence [94]. This knowledge could be translated into real-time surgical guidance using tools like specialized laparoscopic or robotic ultrasound probes. On-the-fly algorithms could process these images, generating an augmented reality (AR) overlay on the surgeon’s display. This AR map could visualize not only tumor boundaries but also a quantitative “stiffness map” of the surrounding tissue. This would highlight high-risk areas invisible to the naked eye, potentially guiding a more biologically informed and precise resection. While challenges in data heterogeneity and standardization remain, these integrated approaches represent a promising future for the field, aiming to develop radiomics from a static predictive tool into a more dynamic component of personalized patient care.

Limitations of this study

As a bibliometric analysis, this study has the following limitations. Firstly, this study’s reliance solely on the WoSCC database introduces a potential for database bias. Given the multidisciplinary nature of radiomics, which integrates medicine with data science, our analysis may underrepresent relevant literature from other important sources. For instance, valuable contributions in engineering and computer science, especially conference proceedings, are more comprehensively indexed in databases like Scopus and IEEE Xplore. Consequently, our findings primarily reflect the landscape of high-quality, peer-reviewed literature within the WoSCC ecosystem, rather than the entire spectrum of scholarly output in the field. Similarly, by limiting our analysis to English-language publications, we may have overlooked important research from non-English sources. Secondly, our analytical approach has inherent limitations. We did not employ thematic topic modeling techniques, such as LDA or BERTopic, which could have provided deeper insights into the semantic evolution of research themes. Our use of threshold-based filtering in VOSviewer and CiteSpace, while necessary for network clarity, may have introduced bias by potentially obscuring the contributions of smaller or emerging institutions and authors. Furthermore, the co-occurrence clusters were algorithmically generated and subsequently validated internally by domain experts within the author team. While this internal validation confirms the semantic coherence of the themes, we acknowledge that a validation by independent, external experts would have further strengthened the objectivity of our thematic analysis. This remains a limitation of the current study. Thirdly, the metrics themselves have limitations. Quantitative indicators such as citation counts and H-index, while reflecting academic impact to some extent, are not entirely equivalent to research quality. Moreover, due to a time-lag effect, recently published high-quality research may not have yet received sufficient citations. Lastly, the analysis results of the current study rely on keywords provided by authors, which may not fully capture all literature on research topics, especially for emerging concepts or interdisciplinary fields.

Conclusion

This bibliometric study delineates the evolutionary trajectory of radiomics in HCC, revealing a field characterized by explosive growth and a distinct global research landscape. Our analysis quantifies China’s leadership in publication volume and the pivotal role of the USA as a collaboration hub, while also uncovering a critical gap: high-impact research from nations like South Korea and Japan shows limited integration into the core collaboration network. The thematic evolution from foundational texture analysis to AI-driven predictive modeling, particularly for MVI and treatment response, underscores a clear trend toward clinical utility. However, the identified structural imbalances in international collaboration may exacerbate challenges in standardization and the external validation of models, hindering clinical translation.

To accelerate progress, future efforts must be strategically prioritized. First, fostering targeted international collaborations is paramount; specifically, bridging high-volume centers with high-impact, low-centrality nodes could enhance research quality and model generalizability. Second, future research must move beyond retrospective analyses to rigorously designed, prospective multicenter trials that adhere to international reporting standards like IBSI, directly addressing the standardization bottleneck. Finally, as the field increasingly embraces AI, developing and applying XAI is crucial not only for overcoming the “black-box” issue but also for potentially discovering novel, robust imaging biomarkers. By addressing these specific gaps identified in our analysis, radiomics can more effectively transition from a promising research tool to a reliable component of precision oncology for HCC.
