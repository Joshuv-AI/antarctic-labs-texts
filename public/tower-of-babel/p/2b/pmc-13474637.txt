Adenomas and Beyond: Colonoscopy Surveillance, Racial and Socioeconomic Disparities in Lynch Syndrome
Authors: Joseph Atarere, Maria Malak Bilalaga, Ramya Vasireddy, Boniface Mensah, Greeshma N. Gaddipati, Simardeep Singh, Ted O. Akhiwu, Stephanie Woo, Chiranjeev Dash, Priyanka Kanth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsPathogenic mutations in the mismatch repair (MMR) genes cause Lynch syndrome. Adenoma resection can prevent colorectal cancer (CRC), but the occurrence of precancerous lesions is understudied in Lynch syndrome.MethodsUsing retrospective cohort data, we examined racial and socioeconomic differences in the occurrence of adenoma, sessile serrated lesions (SSLs), and CRC during the first colonoscopy. We also estimated the association between MMR subtype and time to adenoma or SSL occurrence during follow-up colonoscopies.ResultsOf the 187 patients included in this study, the most common MMR subtype identified was MSH6 (31.0%). At first colonoscopy, 23% of patients had SSLs, 8.6% had adenomas, and 17.1% of patients had CRC. There was no difference by race or socioeconomic status (SES) in the occurrence of adenoma or SSL at first colonoscopy, but patients of lower SES had greater odds of having CRC (odds ratio: 1.24; 95% confidence interval [CI]: [1.01, 1.52]). During follow-up of patients with normal first colonoscopy, the odds of adenoma or SSL were higher among MSH6 compared to MLH1 (hazard ratio [HR]: 3.71; 95% CI: [1.08, 12.78]) and MSH2 carriers (HR: 9.52; 95% CI: [2.32, 39.11]). Among those with adenoma or SSL at first colonoscopy, MSH6 carriers were more likely to develop adenoma or SSL during follow-up compared to MLH1 carriers (HR: 3.68; 95% CI: [1.02, 13.26]).ConclusionAdenoma and SSL were highest among MSH2 carriers at first colonoscopy, while MSH6 carriers had the highest risk of adenomas and SSLs during follow-up. Patients from lower SES backgrounds had higher odds of CRC at first colonoscopy.

Introduction

Lynch syndrome (LS) is the most prevalent hereditary cancer syndrome, affecting approximately 1 in 279 individuals.1 It results from germ line mutations in mismatch repair (MMR) genes—MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2—and a non-MMR gene, epithelial cellular adhesion molecule (EPCAM), which silences MSH2 expression.1 LS is the leading cause of hereditary colorectal cancer (CRC), accounting for about 3% of all new CRC diagnoses.2 The lifetime risk of LS-associated CRC ranges from 8.5% to 61%, significantly higher than the risk in the general population (4.4% in men; 4.1% in women).3, 4, 5 This risk varies by the specific gene affected, with MLH1 and MSH2 carriers being diagnosed at a younger age (mean 24–46 years) compared to MSH6 (mean 54–63 years) and PMS2 (mean 47–66 years) carriers.5, 6, 7 Although LS was traditionally regarded as a non-polyposis syndrome, contemporary studies have identified multiple colorectal adenomas among MMR carriers.8,9 The accelerated adenoma-carcinoma sequence in MMR carriers makes the detection and resection of precancerous lesions important in reducing CRC incidence in these patients.10

The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and the United States Multi-Society Task Force on CRC recommend screening start at ages 20–25 years or 2–5 years prior to the youngest CRC diagnosis in the family if it occurs before age 25.5,7 For MSH6 and PMS2 carriers, screening can begin at ages 30 and 35, respectively, due to their lower risk and later age of diagnosis.5,7,11 For individuals at average CRC risk, it generally takes 10–15 years for a benign adenoma to progress to CRC.12,13 With LS, this adenoma-carcinoma sequence accelerates to as little as 1–2 years, and invasive cancer can develop from seemingly normal mucosa via a non-polypoid pathway.14,15 There is no international consensus on optimal CRC screening intervals for LS, but the NCCN recommends follow-up colonoscopy every 1–2 years for MLH1 and MSH2 carriers and every 1–3 years for MSH6 and PMS2 carriers.5 For patients with adenomas detected on surveillance colonoscopies, complete endoscopic resection is recommended, but screening intervals remain the same.5 These recommendations are based on lower-level evidence due to sparse data on adenomas among MMR carriers.

Black/African Americans (AAs) exhibit higher overall CRC incidence and mortality and present with more advanced disease at diagnosis compared to non-Hispanic Whites (NHW).16, 17, 18 Data from retrospective studies indicate that, among LS patients, the cumulative lifetime risk of CRC in Blacks/AAs is similar to that in NHW.19 However, there are limited data on racial and socioeconomic differences in precancerous colorectal lesions among LS patients.

The current NCCN guidelines of surveillance colonoscopies among LS carriers are tailored to the MMR subtype due to the difference in their respective risks of CRC. Resection of adenomas can help prevent CRC, but there are limited data on adenoma burden by MMR subtype. Identifying MMR subtypes with higher adenoma occurrence may help to refine current screening guidelines by considering both MMR-specific cancer and adenoma risk. This study aims to (1) estimate the prevalence of precancerous lesions and CRC by MMR subtype at baseline and during follow-up colonoscopies among LS patients; and (2) compare differences in the prevalence of precancerous lesions and CRC by race and socioeconomic status (SES).

Methods

Study Population

In this retrospective cohort study, we extracted data from the electronic medical record (EMR) of patients with a germ line diagnosis of LS in a large mid-Atlantic healthcare system who received colonoscopies between April 13, 2005, and February 27, 2024. The patients were first identified using diagnostic codes for LS, benign or malignant lesions of the colon, and personal or family history of colonic polyps or cancer and from the High-Risk Gastrointestinal Cancer Registry at the MedStar Georgetown University Hospital. A manual review of the EMR for each record retrieved was then performed to identify patients with confirmed germ line LS mutations (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, and EPCAM). All colonoscopies were performed by gastroenterologists with an adenoma detection rate above the 35% standard quality requirement (40% for men and 30% for women)20 and the median bowel preparation quality using the Boston bowel prep score was 9 (indicating adequate preparation).

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the presence of (1) CRC or (2) sessile serrated lesion (SSL) or adenoma during the first colonoscopy in our hospital system. When multiple lesion types were present, patients were grouped based on the highest-risk lesion (ie, patients with CRC were classified as CRC even if adenomas or SSLs were also present). The secondary outcome was the development of adenoma or SSL during subsequent surveillance colonoscopies. A normal colonoscopy was defined as one with no lesions or lesions that were not CRC, adenoma, or SSLs.

Variables

The primary independent variable of interest was the MMR subtype (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, and EPCAM). We collected demographic data, including age at first colonoscopy, sex (male and female), race (White, Black/AA, and others), and area-level SES, using the state-level Area Deprivation Index (ADI) from the Neighborhood Atlas.21,22 ADI ranks neighborhoods by socioeconomic disadvantage based on income, education, employment, and housing quality, with deciles from 1 (least disadvantaged) to 10 (most disadvantaged) within a particular state.

Relevant clinical data collected included a history of other non-CRC cancers, the number of colonoscopies, and intervals between them. Other non-CRCs were grouped as Lynch related (endometrial, ovarian, gastric, duodenal, pancreatic, bladder, and prostate) and non-Lynch related.

Statistical Analyses

Analyses were conducted using Stata 18.0, with statistical significance for multivariable regression set at P < .05. We described the study population characteristics by MMR subtype, presenting numeric variables as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages). Numeric variables were compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test and categorical variables with Fisher’s exact test. Due to small subgroup size (n = 6), EPCAM carriers were excluded from regression and time-to-event analyses.

Logistic regression

Using multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for age, sex, and MMR subtype, we examined racial and socioeconomic differences in the occurrence of (1) the composite outcome of adenoma and SSL and (2) CRC during the first colonoscopy. Differences were assessed separately for race (model 1), SES (model 2), and together (model 3). The regression models for the composite outcome of adenoma and SSL excluded patients who already had CRC. We also examined for the predictors of follow-up colonoscopy using a multivariable logistic regression model adjusted for age, sex, race, state ADI rank, and baseline colonoscopy findings.

Time-to-event analyses

For patients who had at least 2 colonoscopies and with normal findings on the first colonoscopy, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were used to assess time from the first colonoscopy (t) to the first occurrence of adenoma or SSL stratified by MMR subtype. Survivor functions were compared using log-rank tests. Cox proportional hazards (Cox-PH) models adjusted for age and average time between colonoscopies were used to estimate the association between MMR subtype and time to adenoma or SSL occurrence during follow-up. These analyses were repeated for patients with adenoma or SSL during the first colonoscopy who underwent at least 2 surveillance colonoscopies.

Results

Study Population Characteristics

A total of 187 patients with germ line LS mutations who underwent at least 1 colonoscopy during the study period were included. The most common MMR subtypes identified were MSH6 (58 patients; 31.0%) and MLH1 (52 patients; 27.8%), while EPCAM carriers were the least common (6 patients; 3.2%). Most participants were White (74.6%) and female (71.7%), with a median state ADI rank of 3 (IQR: 2-6) (Table 1). Black/AAs were, however, from more disadvantaged neighborhoods compared to NHW (median state ADI rank of 5 vs 3) (Supplemental Table 2). The median age at first colonoscopy was 46 years (IQR: 37-59). This was highest among MSH6 carriers and lowest among PMS2 carriers (51 vs 39 years). At the first colonoscopy, 27.8% of patients were diagnosed with SSLs, 22.5% with tubular adenomas, and 4.8% were diagnosed with villous adenomas. The prevalence of tubular adenomas (31.7%) and SSLs (34.2%) was highest among MSH2 carriers. 17.1% of patients had CRC at baseline, which is similar to the combined burden of other LS-related cancers (18.2%) in this population. Among the 32 patients with baseline CRC, 9 also had SSLs, 4 had adenomas, and 1 had both adenomas and SSLs (Supplemental Table 6). MLH1 carriers had the highest burden of CRC (28.9%). Although MSH2 carriers had the lowest CRC prevalence, they had the highest combined burden of adenoma and SSL (41.5%). The median number of colonoscopies received by participants was 2 (IQR: 1-3), and the median time between colonoscopies for those with at least 2 procedures was 15 months (IQR: 10-24). Racial and Socioeconomic Differences in the Occurrence of Colorectal Cancer During First Colonoscopy

Compared to NHWs, Black/AAs had a lower proportion of SSLs (14.3% vs 26.4%) but a higher proportion of CRC (32.1% vs 13.2%) during the first colonoscopy (Supplemental Table 2). After adjusting for age, sex, SES, and MMR subtype, racial differences in CRC occurrence during the first colonoscopy were not statistically significant. In the fully adjusted model (model 3), patients from neighborhoods with higher socioeconomic disadvantage had greater odds of having CRC (odds ratio [OR]: 1.24; 95% confidence interval [CI]: [1.01, 1.52]) (Table 2).

In model 2 (SES only), MSH2 carriers were less likely to have CRC compared to MLH1 carriers (OR: 0.16; 95% CI: [0.04, 0.72]). This relationship was, however, not significant after adjusting for race (models 1 and 3). Males (OR: 4.41; 95% CI: [1.54, 12.68]) and older adults (OR: 1.04; 95% CI: [1.01, 1.08]) were also more likely to have CRC at baseline, consistent across all 3 models. Further details can be found in Table 2 and Supplemental Table 3.

Surveillance Colonoscopy Follow-Up Rates and Predictors of Follow-Up

Of the 187 participants in the study, 155 (82.9%) did not have CRC during the first colonoscopy and were thus eligible for surveillance colonoscopies. Among these, 86 (55.5%) received a follow-up colonoscopy. Participants with SSL during the first colonoscopy were more likely to undergo a follow-up colonoscopy compared to those with adenoma (OR: 6.36; 95% CI: [1.34, 30.24]). There was no significant relationship between age at first colonoscopy, sex, race, or SES and the likelihood of returning for a follow-up colonoscopy. See Supplemental Table 5 for further details.

Adenoma or Sessile Serrated Lesion Occurrence During Follow-Up Colonoscopies Among Patients With Normal Findings on First Colonoscopy

Of the 96 participants with normal findings on first colonoscopy, only 53 (55.2%) returned for a follow-up colonoscopy. Among these, only 3 (2 MLH1 and 1 MSH6 carrier) developed CRC during the 20-year follow-up period.

Discussion

LS significantly increases the risk of early-onset CRC. Our findings reveal important variations in adenoma and CRC risk based on MMR subtype, race, and SES. We contextualize these results within the existing medical literature and explore their implications for colonoscopy surveillance in LS.

While several studies have examined CRC incidence by race and MMR subtype,23,24 less attention has been given to the occurrence of adenomas and SSLs. One study from the MD Anderson Cancer Center by del Carmen et al found that 21% of LS patients had adenomas at first colonoscopy, mostly tubular adenomas.25 This is very similar to the 22.5% of tubular adenomas found in this study. Their study, however, did not specify the presence of SSLs, which were identified in 27.8% of our study participants. In contrast, Gibson et al reported prevalence rates of 53.5% for adenomas and 16.8% for SSLs.26 It is noteworthy that Gibson et al evaluated the combined occurrence of these lesions over an average of 2 colonoscopies across a mean follow-up period of 4.5 years. In our study, we distinctly characterized the occurrence of these lesions both at first colonoscopy and during follow-up assessments. In our study, SSLs were more prevalent among NHWs, but the prevalence of adenomas between Black/AAs and NHWs was similar. Notably, our regression models revealed no racial disparities in the combined occurrence of adenomas and SSLs at first colonoscopy, consistent with findings reported in previous studies.25,27

A previous study including asymptomatic LS patients in Portugal found no difference in adenoma and SSL burden by MMR subtype among MLH1, MSH2, and MSH6 carriers.28 Gibson et al included PMS2 carriers and found no difference in the odds of SSL by MMR subtype but reported higher odds of adenoma among MSH2 compared to PMS2 carriers.26 Our data indicated that MSH2 carriers had the highest combined burden of adenomas and SSLs at 41.5%, followed by MSH6 carriers at 36.2% and MLH1 carriers at 28.9%. Conversely, PMS2 carriers demonstrated the highest proportion of normal colonoscopies at 73.3%. In one of the regression models, PMS2 was associated with lower odds of adenomas and SSL compared to MLH1 carriers, reflecting their reduced risk of these lesions, which has been noted previously.29 The absence of significant differences in the fully adjusted model is likely due to the limited sample size of our study. Our findings must, however, be interpreted in context, as MLH1 carriers also presented the highest prevalence of CRC during their initial colonoscopy at 28.9%, followed by MSH6 carriers at 15.5% and PMS2 at 13.3%. This suggests that the lower adenoma and SSL burden in MLH1 carriers during the first colonoscopy may be due to their significantly higher CRC risk via non-polypoid mechanisms or accelerated adenoma-carcinoma sequence.

Alarmingly, only 55.5% of participants without CRC on first colonoscopy returned for a repeat colonoscopy, significantly lower than the 68% reported in previous studies, which poses concern given the elevated CRC risk in LS carriers.30 Follow-up rates were particularly low among those with adenomas or SSLs during first colonoscopy, contrary to expectations. Notably, patients with adenomas were significantly less likely to have a follow-up colonoscopy. This may stem from simultaneous referrals for surgical intervention rather than follow-up surveillance for patients with adenomas not amenable to resection, compounded by barriers such as poor health literacy and inadequate communication between patients and providers.31 We were, however, unable to formally evaluate for language barriers, an important factor that may have influenced adherence to follow-up. Although SES was not linked to the likelihood of follow-up colonoscopies in this study, this may be due to our small sample size. Furthermore, we could not ascertain whether participants who did not return for repeat colonoscopy were lost to follow-up or transitioned to care outside our healthcare system.

Data on adenoma occurrence in LS are limited, with existing studies indicating similar rates of adenomas and SSLs across MMR subtypes.28,32 We found that among patients with normal first colonoscopies, MSH6 carriers exhibited a higher likelihood of developing adenomas or SSLs during follow-up compared to MLH1 and MSH2 carriers. In the subgroup of patients with adenomas or SSLs on first colonoscopy, MSH6 were still more likely than MLH1 carriers to develop adenomas or SSLs during follow-up. MSH6 carriers possess a lifetime CRC risk lower than that of MLH1 and MSH2,6,11,23,33 and this is reflected in the current NCCN screening guidelines, which recommend surveillance colonoscopies 1–3 years after adenoma resection.5 Although the burden and characteristics of adenomas and SSLs were not specifically analyzed in previous studies,6,11,23,33 it is plausible that the reduced CRC risk in MSH6 carriers is linked to the lower malignant potential of their adenomas and polyps. This is, however, hypothetical, and the increased adenoma and SSL occurrence among MSH6 carriers noted in this study cannot be ignored. While a later age of screening onset may be appropriate for MSH6 carriers, more frequent surveillance (every 1–2 years) may be more appropriate. It should be noted that the longest follow-up period for any participant in this study was approximately 20 years, leaving uncertainty regarding the long-term validity of these findings.

Our study also underscores the significance of active surveillance in the prevention of CRC. During a follow-up period of 20 years, only 6 total cases of CRC were recorded. This low incidence of CRC during surveillance highlights both the efficacy of current surveillance guidelines and the need for more personalized recommendations to further reduce CRC incidence. These findings are, however, limited by the low follow-up rate of study participants. The observed correlation between longer intervals between colonoscopies and a lower likelihood of adenoma or SSL recurrence in this study is counterintuitive and suggests the potential influence of unmeasured confounding factors. One possible explanation is that individuals with perceived lower risk—perhaps due to better overall health or favorable prior colonoscopy findings—may be more likely to delay surveillance, thereby skewing the association. Additionally, some patients may have undergone interval colonoscopies outside our hospital system, resulting in an artificially prolonged recorded interval between procedures. This could lead to misclassification and further bias in the association.

Approximately 17.6% of our patient population had CRC during the first colonoscopy, with MLH1 carriers presenting the highest proportion (28.9%) and MSH2 carriers the lowest (9.8%). Mixed findings exist regarding the relationship between MMR subtype and the lifetime cumulative risk of CRC, with some studies indicating similar risks for MLH1 and MSH2,34 and others showing an increased risk among MLH1 carriers.23 A closer examination of a multinational study revealed that the difference in cumulative CRC risk between MLH1 and MSH2 carriers was more pronounced at age 50 than at age 75, at which point the cumulative risks between these MMR subtypes became more comparable.24 Hence, at the median age of 46 years at first colonoscopy in our study, MLH1 carriers are exposed to significantly higher cumulative CRC risk compared to MSH2 carriers.

In our study, Black/AAs demonstrated a higher CRC prevalence at first colonoscopy, likely explained by socioeconomic disparities, as these participants were from lower socioeconomic backgrounds (mean ADI of 5 vs 3 for NHW). This aligns with previous literature that associates SES with higher CRC risk.35 On examining the relationship between MMR subtype and CRC occurrence in the context of race, we found that models unadjusted for race indicated lower odds of CRC for MSH2 compared to MLH1 carriers. However, this relationship became nonsignificant upon adjusting for race, suggesting that the higher baseline CRC prevalence among MLH1 carriers is likely driven by racial factors. Indeed, Black/AAs in our study had a higher prevalence of MLH1 compared to MSH2 carriers, corroborating prior findings in studies that also highlighted a predominance of MLH1 carriers (61%) among Black families.19

Current CRC screening guidelines for LS patients do not differentiate by race; however, the NCCN recommends initiating screening 2–5 years prior to the youngest CRC diagnosis in the family if it occurs before age 25.5 It is noteworthy that Black/AAs who undergo CRC screening are often less likely to communicate findings related to colonic polyps to their relatives,36 potentially delaying timely colonoscopy for at-risk family members. Additionally, other unmeasured confounders, such as tumor location, may contribute to the observed higher CRC burden in Black/AAs. Unfortunately, our study did not include data on tumor location, limiting our ability to explore this important factor. While our findings do not warrant modifications to the existing guidelines concerning screening onset and intervals based on race, it is important for physicians to remain cognizant of the heightened risk of CRC among Black/AAs due to the previously discussed factors.

The risk of other LS-associated cancers like gynecological cancers (endometrial and ovarian) and gastric cancers varies by MMR subtype and across studies.6,33,37 A French study reported cumulative lifetime risks for endometrial and ovarian cancer, highest among MLH1 carriers (54% and 20%, respectively).6 Most cases were observed after age 40, with cumulative risks below 2% for both cancers at age 40, regardless of MMR subtype.6 The combined prevalence of LS-associated cancers in our study was 18.2%, which is notably lower than reported in other studies. This figure reflects prevalence at the time of data collection rather than cumulative risk, which explains these discrepancies, as our study population was significantly younger and did not reach the age of 70. Another explanation for the low prevalence of LS-associated cancers noted in this study is loss to follow-up, with only 12.3%–28.3% of participants returning for repeat colonoscopies.

A significant strength of this study was its representation of Black/AA participants. Previous studies on CRC screening in LS carriers have included less than 4% of Black/AA individuals,25,27 despite their increased CRC risk; conversely, 16.2% of our participants were Black/AA. Additionally, there are few studies estimating adenoma and SSL burden across MMR subtypes, making our findings valuable for filling this gap. Our approach to assessing CRC risk during follow-up separately for patients with normal findings and those with adenomas or SSLs during first colonoscopy offers practical implications rarely addressed in other studies.

Despite these strengths, our study has limitations. The short follow-up period restricts comparisons of CRC risk across MMR subtypes over longer durations. The reliability of our results is influenced by the level of detail and accuracy of EMR documentation. For example, data on procedure-specific factors like withdrawal time were not available. It is important to note that the first colonoscopy recorded for patients in this study reflects the first procedure available in our dataset but may not represent their true index (first-ever) colonoscopy. As such, the study findings should be interpreted within the context of this limitation, as prior procedures, particularly those performed outside our health system, could influence outcomes and surveillance intervals. Additionally, our limited sample size and number of outcome events have negative implications for the statistical power and predictive ability of our models. For example, established risk factors for CRC like obesity, tobacco, and alcohol use were not included. Although these variables are potential confounders, the limited number of outcome events constrained the number of covariates that could be included in our models before compromising model integrity. Consequently, residual confounding may remain, and the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Additionally, inherent biases in a single-center study may reduce the external validity of our findings. Finally, we did not have data on individual-level measures of SES, which might be most relevant for guideline-concordant follow-up in Lynch patients. Although we used area-level SES variables, which have been suggested to be valid proxies for urban area residents, caution is warranted in generalizing these findings to non-urban settings. In addition, patients residing in the highest ADI neighborhoods (7-10) were relatively underrepresented in this study, likely reflecting the predominantly urban population served by our health system. As the most socioeconomically disadvantaged communities are more often located in rural areas, our study findings may not be fully generalizable to these populations.

Conclusion

In this large, diverse group of individuals with LS, MSH2 carriers had the highest burden of adenomas and SSLs but the lowest prevalence of CRC during first colonoscopy. In contrast, MLH1 carriers displayed a higher CRC risk, which appears linked to racial factors, particularly among Black/AAs who have elevated CRC rates influenced by socioeconomic factors. CRC incidence was low during active surveillance, with MSH6 carriers having the highest risk of adenomas and SSLs. These findings underscore the need for personalized CRC surveillance guidelines considering MMR subtype, race, and SES.
