Favorable outcomes of epilepsy with gait-induced seizures after resection of the unilateral supplementary motor area

Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•The patient had gait-induced seizures, a rare type of reflex epilepsy.•Intracranial EEG revealed onset in the supplementary motor area.•Resection led to seizure freedom, implicating gait-related motor networks.Keywords: Reflex epilepsy, Intracranial electroencephalogram (EEG), Electrocorticogram, magnetoencephalogram (MEG), SMA

Introduction

Reflex epilepsy is characterized by seizures triggered by specific stimuli or actions. Gait-induced seizures are a rare manifestation of reflex epilepsy, with few reported cases (Iriarte et al., 2001, Saeki et al., 2009, Szűcs et al., 2012). We report a case of epilepsy with gait-induced seizures, successfully treated with the resection of the supplementary motor area (SMA), following intracranial electroencephalogram (EEG), providing pathophysiological insight.

Case presentation

Clinical course

A 28-year-old right-handed woman had longstanding episodes of “falling” preceded by paroxysmal leg stiffness. Her symptoms began in childhood with episodes of leg stiffness during sleep. By age 14, she experienced occasional sudden bilateral leg stiffness while walking, often resulting in falls. These gait-induced events gradually increased in both frequency and severity. In her early 20 s, similar episodes occasionally occurred even without walking and sometimes involved increased muscle tone in the upper extremities. At age 27, she sustained a lumbar fracture in a car accident caused by leg stiffness while driving. Her gait-induced events further worsened, and she began falling consistently within a few steps of initiating gait. To prevent injuries, she started using a wheelchair even for short distances and took medical leave from her job at a preschool nursery.

Initially diagnosed with functional neurological disorder, she was later found to show subtle EEG changes during her episode (described below) and was treated with anti-seizure medications (ASMs) including lacosamide and perampanel, without improvement. Her past medical, family, and birth developmental histories were unremarkable. She had never experienced loss of consciousness, tonic-clonic seizures, or other paroxysmal neurological conditions.

Diagnostic Workup

Upon referral to our clinic for further examination, every time she stood up from a wheelchair and walk, she stopped within several steps and exhibited bilateral leg stiffness resulting in backward falls. The leg stiffness resolved spontaneously within 10–20 s. No associated mental or cognitive changes were observed during the events. Other routine neurological examination was unremarkable except for hyperreflexia in the lower limbs. Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale-III indicated moderate intellectual disability, with a full-scale IQ of 59, Verbal IQ of 64, and Performance IQ of 60.

The brain magnetic resonance imaging was within normal limits, and nuclear imaging studies showed no focal abnormality in cerebral perfusion or metabolism. A routine scalp EEG revealed no obvious epileptic spikes or sharp waves. However, when the patient started to walk while EEG recorded, low-amplitude rhythmic beta activity lasting for about 10 s, predominantly at the Cz electrode, was observed (Fig. 1A). Although this EEG activities were constantly observed by initiating walking, any other stimuli or action which may be related to gait, such as cutaneous stimulation on the soles, stepping movements while lying down, or imagery of walking, did not provoke either clinical symptoms or EEG changes. Continuous EEG also detected occasional brief spontaneous low-amplitude beta activities on the Cz electrode, lasting less than one second, without any clinical symptoms.

Although the rhythmic fast activity over Cz were consistently observed with initiation of walk and the findings raised concern for epilepsy with gait-induced seizures, these EEG changes lacked clear epileptiform morphology or evolving pattern in amplitude or location. To further evaluate her epileptogenicity, magnetoencephalography (MEG) was performed with the ASMs off, and small sharp waves were repeatedly observed, whose sources were estimated in the right lateral primary motor cortex (M1) (Fig. 1B).

Surgical Procedure

The patient continued falling frequently even with sufficient doses of multiple ASMs. We proceeded with intracranial EEG recording to confirm the diagnosis of epilepsy and explore a potential target for resective surgery.

Subdural electrodes were placed over the bilateral motor, premotor, and supplementary motor cortices based on the hypothesis that epileptogenic zones were localized within Rolandic or peri-Rolandic regions. We refrained from using stereo-electroencephalogram because of anticipated need for cortical motor mapping, where subdural electrodes may provide higher special resolution, and concern for the hemorrhagic complication with placing multiple leads over the cortical convexity where abundant bridging vain existed. Electrocorticography captured low-amplitude rhythmic fast activity originating from the electrode over the right SMA greater than right M1 as the patient stood up. This activity showed increasing amplitudes along with a waxing and waning pattern, and persisted after the initiation of gait. It subsequently spread to the surrounding peri-Rolandic areas and contralateral electrodes, coinciding backward falling with tonic extension of the left lower limb. This tonic posture did not extend to other body regions. The rhythmic activity ceased concurrently with the resolution of tonic posturing (Fig. 2A, B).

The findings confirmed a diagnosis of epilepsy with gait-induced seizures. Although the ictal onset appeared nearly simultaneous at both right SMA and M1 contact, the amplitude of the ictal activity was greater in the SMA. We therefore assumed the seizure onset zone was primarily located in the SMA, with partial involvement of M1. The rhythmic fast activity observed over Cz on scalp EEG likely corresponded to ictal discharges in the SMA demonstrated by electrocorticography. The clustering of MEG dipoles over the dorsolateral M1 did not necessarily overlap with the estimated seizure onset zone, likely due to source estimation error from low signal–noise ratio. After thorough discussion with the patient, a decision was made to proceed with resective surgery. Since the initial ictal activity involved not only SMA but the portion of right M1, the resection was extended to the edge of the M1 contact that exhibited ictal onset changes, where electrical stimulation elicited flexion of the left lower knee (Fig. 2A). Given the patient’s severe functional disability caused by seizure-related falls, seizure control was prioritized over the risk of postoperative motor deficit, and informed consent was obtained.

Resection surgery was performed uneventfully, aside from the anticipated partial paralysis involving the left leg (Fig. 2C). Pathologically, the resected specimens showed increased superficial hypermyelination in the cortical surface, as well as disruption of cortical neuronal polarity and clustering features most appropriately described as minor malformations of cortical development (mMCD).

Postoperative Course

After the surgery, the patient became able to walk independently without leg stiffness or collapse, although partial paralysis in the distal left leg persisted. She remained seizure-free on carbamazepine 200 mg for two years post-surgery (Engel classification, class I). Her quality of life improved significantly and she resumed independent daily activities and employment.

Discussion

This case demonstrates the successful management of drug-resistant epilepsy with gait-induced seizures through SMA resection. The patient initially presented diagnostic challenges due to subtle scalp EEG changes and broad differential diagnoses. Subdural EEG provided definitive ictal onset in the SMA, facilitating a targeted resection and seizure freedom. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of gait-induced seizures successfully treated with resective surgery.

Gait-induced seizures are a rare form of seizure seen in reflex epilepsy, with only a few cases documented in the literature (Iriarte et al., 2001, Saeki et al., 2009, Szűcs et al., 2012). In our case, scalp EEG recordings during seizures revealed low-voltage rhythmic fast activity most prominently in the Cz electrode, which was also described in the previously reported cases of gait-induced seizures (Saeki et al., 2009, Szűcs et al., 2012). The other reported rhythmic spike and waves in the unilateral central area (Iriarte et al., 2001). Similarly, seizures triggered by general motor activity (i.e., not limited to gait) (Lombroso, 1995, Lv et al., 2014, Takahashi et al., 1997) also manifested rhythmic beta or alpha-theta activity over the Cz region (Lv et al., 2014, Takahashi et al., 1997). Abnormal activity in these electrodes on scalp EEG suggests underlying pathology in nearby motor-related cortical areas, such as SMA and primary motor cortex. In our case, seizure freedom following resection of the SMA provided confirmatory evidence for its epileptogenic zone. EEG activity in these Rolandic and peri-Rolandic areas, even if subtle as observed in our case, warrants careful attention in patients with this rare seizure phenotype.

Gait-induced seizures have been described as proprioceptive-induced seizures, as prior cases have included instances where seizures were elicited not only by gait but also by somatosensory or acoustic stimuli (Saeki et al., 2009). In our case, however, sensory stimulation of the lower limbs, as well as mimicking gait by moving legs while lying down and simply thinking about gait, failed to provoke seizures. The resected area did not include the sensory cortex, and ictal onset did not involve the sensory cortex on intracranial recordings. Therefore, involvement of the proprioceptive sensory networks was considered unlikely in this case, whereas motor networks responsible for gait initiation or execution presumably played a key role in seizure generation.

This case highlights the importance of careful non-invasive neurophysiological assessment. The successful outcome of resective surgery supports the SMA’s role in gait-induced seizure generation and demonstrates that targeted surgical intervention can be effective for appropriately selected patients.
