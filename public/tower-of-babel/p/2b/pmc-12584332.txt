Perioperative outcomes in lung cancer: transfer vs. outpatient admission routes
Authors: Wei Chen, Qiangqiang Zheng, Yi Shen, Yang Yuan, Yusong Lu, Xiong Liu, Min Liang, Yunfeng Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to compare perioperative features of lung cancer patients admitted via different routes and their short-term prognosis impact.MethodsA retrospective analysis included 116 consecutive patients transferred from other departments for video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) from June 2021 - June 2024 and 353 outpatients who underwent VATS during the same period. A total of 218 patients were obtained through propensity score matching (1:1), including 109 patients in the transferred lung cancer group and 109 patients in the outpatient lung cancer group. Perioperative characteristics and short-term prognosis were compared with multivariate binary logistic regression for risk factors.ResultsA significant proportion of lung cancer patients transferred from the Department of Respiratory Medicine to the Department of Thoracic Surgery for surgical resection exhibited more complex clinical profiles than their outpatient counterparts. These patients were generally older, with poorer lung function, more advanced tumor stages, higher ASA, hypoplastic interlobar fissures, and severe pleural adhesions.​During the perioperative period, transferred patients had a higher incidence of postoperative pulmonary infections, longer hospital stays, greater hospitalization costs, and a higher 30-day readmission rate. Multivariate regression analysis identified two independent risk factors for postoperative pulmonary infection in the transferred group: longer operation time (OR = 1.011, 95% CI = 1.001–1.021, P = 0.038) and lower FEV1 levels (OR = 0.377, 95% CI = 0.156–0.911, P = 0.03).ConclusionsSignificant differences exist in perioperative characteristics and short-term prognosis between transferred and outpatient lung cancer patients. When admitting transferred patients, develop individualized treatment plans, enhance multidisciplinary collaboration, and streamline transfer processes to improve efficiency. Address psychological needs through timely interventions while optimizing preoperative pulmonary function. Without compromising surgical safety or oncological outcomes, minimize operative duration to enhance overall treatment efficacy and postoperative recovery quality.

Introduction

Currently, the incidence of lung cancer remains at a high level, and it ranks as the primary cause of death among all cancer-related mortalities globally [1].With the gradual increase in people’s health awareness and the progressive implementation of low-dose spiral CT screening for high-risk groups, an increasing number of lung cancer patients have achieved favorable treatment outcomes through timely diagnosis and intervention. This has led to a reduction in the mortality rate of lung cancer and an extension of patients’ survival periods [2, 3].

There is a diverse range of treatment options for lung cancer, which can vary depending on patients’ specific conditions and factors such as the type and stage of the cancer. These options include surgical resection, targeted therapy, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, antibody-drug conjugate, and multimodal therapy. Currently, surgical resection remains the standard treatment approach for early-stage lung cancer [4–6].

In China, lung cancer patients who are candidates for surgical resection mainly make appointments through the outpatient pathway and then receive surgical resection treatment in the thoracic surgery department. Simultaneously, some patients are admitted to the thoracic surgery department for surgical treatment via the emergency and inter-departmental transfer pathways. The findings of several studies [7, 8]have indicated that lung cancer patients admitted through the emergency pathway have a lower surgical intervention rate, a higher incidence of postoperative complications, and a lower survival rate, which is unfavorable for the prognosis of lung cancer patients. However, research reports are scarce on the perioperative clinical characteristics and prognosis of lung cancer patients who are admitted through the - hospital inter-departmental transfer pathway and undergo surgical treatment.

This study aims to analyze and compare the differences in perioperative clinical characteristics and short-term prognosis between lung cancer patients admitted through the - hospital inter-departmental transfer pathway and those admitted through the outpatient pathway. The results of this study will provide a reference basis for the perioperative management and subsequent treatment of lung cancer patients admitted through the in-hospital inter-departmental transfer pathway. Materials and methods

Clinical data and grouping

This study encompassed consecutive lung cancer patients transferred from other departments to the thoracic surgery department of our hospital via the in-hospital transfer route for VATS resection treatment between June 2021 and June 2024. Employing stratified sampling analysis, lung cancer patients who underwent VATS resection and were admitted through the outpatient route during the same period were included based on a 1:3 matching principle. The same medical team managed all the above-mentioned patients.

Patient data included in the statistical scope were those with pathologically diagnosed primary lung cancer who underwent thoracoscopic surgical resection with a pathological resection margin indicating R0 resection and were classified as stage MIS to Ⅲb according to the 8th edition of the TNM staging system for lung cancer by the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC). Meanwhile, patients under 18 years old, those with a history of ipsilateral thoracic surgery, and those who had resection of two or more lobes were excluded.

It should be noted that most patients who received neoadjuvant therapy prior to surgery had their subsequent treatment plans determined through multidisciplinary discussion (MDT) at the initial visit and were given fast-track arrangements for their admission routes. Given that these patients could not be clearly differentiated between the outpatient and inter-department transfer routes, they were not included in this study.

Surgery and perioperative management

All patients underwent video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) for pulmonary resection, including wedge resection, segmentectomy, and lobectomy. When segmentectomy was combined with wedge resection, it was classified as segmentectomy; when lobectomy was combined with segmentectomy or wedge resection, it was classified as lobectomy. The methods of lymph node dissection followed relevant guidelines [9, 10]. The peri-operative management was implemented by appropriate diagnosis and treatment specifications, covering aspects such as preoperative preparation, anesthesia, analgesia, and drainage [9, 11].

Data collection and definition

We retrospectively retrieved the baseline data and peri-operative information of these patients from the electronic medical record system of the Department of Thoracic Surgery at the Fourth Hospital of West China.

The baseline data covered: (1) Basic demographic information and health history: age, gender, height, weight, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, long-term alcohol consumption history, comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, pulmonary diseases, history of tumors); forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1), predicted forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1%), predicted diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO%);(2) Tumor - related details: the maximum diameter of the tumor on preoperative CT, lesion composition. The peri-operative information included American Society of Anesthesiologists Physical Status Classification (ASA), surgical resection method, the lung lobe where the primary lesion was located, the degree of fissure development, thoracic adhesion, postoperative pathological type, and tumor TNM stage.

The peri-operative outcome indicators consisted of: (1) Surgical operation metrics: intraoperative blood loss, the number of lymph node dissection stations and the number of lymph nodes dissected during the operation, operation time, conversion to thoracotomy rate, postoperative transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU) rate; (2) Postoperative complications: incidence of arrhythmia, wound infection, pulmonary infection, chylothorax, Extensive subcutaneous emphysema, respiratory failure, persistent air leakage, hydropneumothorax, persistent drainage, and the incidence of other rare complications. Arrhythmia was defined as supraventricular tachycardia, predominantly atrial fibrillation and atrial flutter [12], which may be accompanied by symptoms such as palpitations and chest pain, confirmed by electrocardiogram. Surgical Site Infection (SSI) was defined as the presence of redness, swelling, heat, or pain at the incision site, or purulent discharge requiring suture removal and drainage, or a positive bacterial culture from the discharge. Pulmonary Infection was defined as new pulmonary infiltrates on imaging studies, accompanied by leukocytosis and fever; sputum culture was performed whenever possible [13].However, microbiological confirmation was not specifically sought, and empirical antibiotic treatment was initiated. Chylous Leak was defined as a significant increase in drainage volume or the drainage of milky-white fluid after the patient resumed a normal diet, with biochemical analysis of the pleural fluid revealing a triglyceride concentration ≥ 110 mg/dL [14].Extensive Subcutaneous Emphysema was defined as the entry of intrathoracic air into the subcutaneous space of the chest wall, spreading to the contralateral chest and shoulder, and potentially extending further to the abdomen, head, neck, and limbs [15].Respiratory Failure was defined as the acute onset of hypoxemia, with an arterial partial pressure of oxygen (PaO₂) to fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO₂) ratio (PaO₂/FiO₂) ≤ 200 mmHg, requiring non-invasive positive pressure ventilation or invasive mechanical ventilation for at least 24 h [16].Persistent air leakage was defined as continuous post-operative air leakage for more than 5 days [17].Hydropneumothorax was defined as radiographic evidence of significant atelectasis in at least one lobe and/or moderate or larger pleural effusion, with inadequate response to previous drainage, necessitating reinsertion of a chest tube. persistent drainage was defined as constant post-operative drainage for more than 7 days in the absence of an air leak. (3) Postoperative day 1 pain assessment: Patient pain intensity was assessed daily during morning rounds by nursing staff using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), scored from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst pain possible). (4) Recorded metrics included: total hospital stay, preoperative hospital stay, duration of chest tube drainage, postoperative hospital stay, hospitalization costs, in-hospital mortality, and 30-day readmission rate.

Sample size calculation

With postoperative hospital stay as the outcome variable, we calculated the sample size based on a previous study reporting mean ± SD of 4.7 ± 1.2 d (uniportal group) versus 5.3 ± 1.4 d (triportal group) [18]. The effect size was calculated as 0.460. Setting the type I error at 5% (α = 0.05), type II error at 20% (β = 0.20), and an allocation ratio of 1:1, the minimum required sample size was 150 participants (75 per group).

Propensity score matching (PSM)

To minimize the impact of baseline differences between in-hospital transferred and outpatient-referred lung cancer patients on outcomes, we performed 1:1 propensity score matching. Variables for propensity score estimation included: age, FEV1, DLCO%, maximum tumor diameter, tumor composition, clinical stage, T stage, N stage, ASA, interlobar fissure development, and pleural adhesion severity. A nearest-neighbor matching algorithm was used with a caliper width of 0.02. Post-matching balance was defined as an absolute standardized mean difference (ASMD) ≤ 0.02 for all covariates.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 software (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). First, the Kolmogorov - Smirnov test was used to test the normality of continuous variables. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, and those with a non-normal distribution were expressed as median (P25, P75). Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (%). The Student’s t-test was used to compare continuous variables with a normal distribution, and the Wilcoxon test was used for continuous variables with a non-normal distribution. The chi-square test was used for categorical variables. Fisher’s exact probability method was used when the chi-square test was not applicable. A two-sided P ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Multivariate binary logistic regression was used to further analyze the variables with statistical significance compared to univariate logistic regression analysis to identify the independent risk factors for postoperative complications in transferred patients.

Ethical review

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of West China Fourth Hospital, Sichuan University (HXSY - EC − 2024091), and the requirement for individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Results

A total of 116 lung cancer patients admitted via the inter-department transfer route and 353 corresponding lung cancer patients admitted through the outpatient route were incorporated into the study. The patients’ ages ranged from 24 to 83 years old. Among those admitted through the inter-department transfer route, 59 patients were transferred from the Department of Respiratory Medicine, constituting 50.9% of the total number of transferred patients. In contrast, only eight patients originated from the surgical system, representing merely 6.9% of the total. The detailed sources of transferred patients are presented in Fig. 1.

Comparison of perioperative outcomes

Analysis of risk factors for postoperative pulmonary infection in transferred lung cancer patients

The Transfer Group had a significantly higher rate of pulmonary infections compared to the Outpatient Group (23.9% vs. 10.1%, P = 0.007).Based on clinical data and previous clinical studies, we identified potential perioperative predictors for pulmonary infection in transferred patients: age, sex, Body Mass Index(BMI), forced expiratory volume in 1 s(FEV1), forced expiratory volume in 1 s as a percentage of predicted value(FEV1%), predicted diffusion capacity of carbon monoxide percentage(DLCO%), tumor diameter, tumor composition, diabetes history, smoking history, T stage, tumor stage, ASA, interlobar fissure development, pleural adhesions, surgical resection type, operative time, and intraoperative blood loss [19–21] . Univariate logistic regression analysis (Table 3) demonstrated that age, sex, FEV1, tumor diameter, tumor composition, T stage, tumor stage, interlobar fissure development, and operative time were risk factors for postoperative pulmonary infection in the Transfer Group. Variables with statistically significant differences in univariate logistic regression were included in multivariate analysis. Multivariate logistic regression (Table 4) identified longer operative time (OR = 1.011; 95% CI = 1.001–1.021; P = 0.038) and lower FEV1 (OR = 0.377; 95% CI = 0.156–0.911; P = 0.03) as independent high-risk factors for pulmonary infections in the Transfer Group.

Discussion

Currently, the diagnosis and treatment model of lung cancer has evolved from a single-discipline approach to a multidisciplinary collaborative treatment model (MDT) [22, 23]. This model involves departments such as thoracic surgery, respiratory medicine, oncology, radiology, pathology, and anesthesiology [24, 25].In the outpatient diagnosis and treatment model, patients typically undergo imaging examinations first (such as chest high-resolution CT (HRCT), enhanced chest CT, positron-emission tomography CT (PET-CT), etc.). The radiology department provides an imaging report, after which doctors from the respiratory medicine or thoracic surgery department conduct further clinical diagnoses. A tissue biopsy can be performed initially to obtain pathological evidence, assess the surgical feasibility, and formulate a treatment plan if necessary. For complex cases or those with a lung cancer clinical stage of III or higher, doctors from relevant departments like oncology, radiology, and pathology must participate in the standardized diagnosis and treatment of lung cancer patients to ensure comprehensive care.

In the transfer-to-another-department model, when a patient is “incidentally” suspected of having lung cancer during intended specialist treatment in other departments, a multidisciplinary discussion is held with relevant departments. This aims to formulate a subsequent diagnosis and treatment plan, weigh the order of diagnosis and treatment, and transfer patients with clear surgical indications to the thoracic surgery department for surgical resection. As depicted in Fig. 1, over 90% of the assigned patients are from the internal medicine system, and the Department of Respiratory Medicine is the largest source of transferred patients, accounting for 50.9% of the total, far exceeding other departments in quantity and proportion. This is speculated that this may be associated with the following factors.

Firstly, there is an overlap in the diseases treated by the Department of Respiratory Medicine and the Department of Thoracic Surgery. When patients present with respiratory symptoms, they usually first visit the Department of Respiratory Medicine. When the treatment effect in the Department of Respiratory Medicine is unsatisfactory, or the disease shows signs of requiring surgical intervention, such as in cases of bronchiectasis, empyema, and lung cancer, the Department of Thoracic Surgery is needed for surgical treatment.

Secondly, there is a close connection between the Department of Respiratory Medicine and the Department of Thoracic Surgery [26].For instance, the surgical treatment assessment of various respiratory diseases, peri-operative pulmonary function assessment, and rehabilitation training necessitate the joint participation of the Department of Respiratory Medicine and the Department of Thoracic Surgery. This results in frequent communication and discussion between the two departments. In some large medical centers, the Department of Respiratory Medicine and the Department of Thoracic Surgery are integrated into a respiratory center to strengthen their connection further. Therefore, when lung cancer patients admitted to the Department of Respiratory Medicine need surgical treatment, they can receive a timely response from the Department of Thoracic Surgery and benefit from better inter-departmental communication.

Thirdly, patients in the Department of Respiratory Medicine are more accepting of transfer to another department than non-respiratory medicine patients. Non-respiratory medicine patients usually receive examinations and treatments for specific diseases in their original departments and have no prior psychological anticipation of lung lesions. When informed that they may have lung cancer and advised to transfer to the Department of Thoracic Surgery for treatment, they may need to adapt to a new treatment environment and plan, causing additional psychological stress. In contrast, most patients transferred from the Department of Respiratory Medicine to the Department of Thoracic Surgery still mainly focus on treating their initial disease, and the possibility of surgical treatment is often communicated at the beginning of treatment in the Department of Respiratory Medicine. Thus, patients in the Department of Respiratory Medicine are more accepting of transfer to another department than non-respiratory medicine patients. These factors have made the Department of Respiratory Medicine a source of hospitalized lung cancer patients transferred to the Department of Thoracic Surgery.

During the initial phase of the study, we compared baseline characteristics between 116 lung cancer patients in the transferred group and 353 in the outpatient group. The results showed that the transferred group had poorer baseline health status and more advanced tumor stages than the outpatient group. This greater clinical complexity was anticipated to negatively impact perioperative outcomes. To minimize confounding effects from these baseline differences and better balance intergroup characteristics while reducing selection bias, we employed Propensity Score Matching (PSM). After matching, 109 matched pairs (109 transferred group patients and 109 outpatient group patients) were included for perioperative outcome comparison. The study found a significantly higher incidence of postoperative pulmonary infection in the transferred group compared to the outpatient group (23.9% vs. 10.1%, P = 0.007). Although the difference in operative duration between the two groups did not reach statistical significance [transferred group: 105 (75.5, 135.5) min vs. outpatient group: 96 (69, 120) min, P = 0.057], the notable numerical difference warrants attention. Postoperative pulmonary infections are commonly associated with impaired clearance of respiratory secretions, decreased patient immunity, poor sputum drainage, cross-infection, and anesthetic effects. Notably, patients in the transferred group (particularly those transferred from respiratory medicine) underwent invasive procedures such as fiberoptic bronchoscopy and percutaneous lung puncture at a higher rate, though this study did not systematically record procedure types. These procedures may compromise the airway defense barrier, introduce pathogens, and weaken host resistance. Furthermore, this study revealed that transferred group patients had a longer preoperative hospital stay, increasing the risk of cross-infection. These factors may collectively contribute to the elevated risk of postoperative pulmonary infection in the transferred group.

Multiple studies have identified advanced age, lower FEV1, male sex, diabetes, smoking, squamous cell carcinoma, pulmonary disease, prolonged mechanical ventilation, extended operative duration, preoperative steroids, induction chemotherapy/radiotherapy, missed antibiotic prophylaxis, and resection type as risk factors for postoperative pulmonary infection in lung cancer patients [27–30]. Univariate logistic regression analysis in this study (Table 3) revealed that age, sex, FEV1, tumor diameter, tumor histology, T stage, tumor stage, fissure development status, and operative duration were risk factors for pulmonary infection in the transferred group. Multivariate logistic regression analysis (Table 4) identified prolonged operative duration (Odds Ratio [OR] = 1.011; 95% Confidence Interval [CI] = 1.001–1.021; P = 0.038) and lower FEV1 (OR = 0.377; 95% CI = 0.156–0.911; P = 0.03) as independent risk factors for postoperative pulmonary infection in the transferred group. Gómez-Hernández et al. reported that prolonged surgical time in video-assisted thoracoscopic lung resection is associated with an increased risk of postoperative complications [28]. Dexter et al. indicated that extended operative time in lobectomy for early-stage lung cancer may increase postoperative complications and prolong hospital stay [31]. Brunell et al. demonstrated that lower FEV1 increases the risk of perioperative mortality and cardiopulmonary complications following pneumonectomy [32].Liu.et al. demonstrated that multimodal prehabilitation significantly enhances perioperative functional capacity in lung cancer patients undergoing VATS lobectomy [33].Therefore, shortening operative time and improving preoperative lung function are crucial for enhancing perioperative outcomes.

This study further reveals that the length of hospital stays for patients transferred between departments is significantly longer than that of outpatient-admitted patients. (P<0.001) This is evident in the longer preoperative hospitalization preparation ༈P<0.001༉and postoperative hospitalization recovery time༈P = 0.027༉ for transferred patients. Concurrently, the hospitalization costs for assigned patients are significantly higher. There are primarily several reasons for the extended hospital stay of transferred patients.

Firstly, the inherent clinical complexity of transferred patients contributes to prolonged pathways. These patients often present initially to other departments—for instance, Cardiology for pulmonary symptoms accompanied by cardiac manifestations, or Respiratory Medicine for comprehensive evaluation of symptoms like cough, hemoptysis, fever, and weight loss. Upon suspicion or confirmation of lung cancer, they are then referred to Thoracic Surgery. This multi-departmental journey frequently involves redundant or poorly coordinated investigations, extending the diagnostic phase and delaying definitive treatment. Furthermore, managing underlying comorbidities is essential both preoperatively to optimize surgical candidacy and postoperatively to mitigate complications. This necessitates collaborative care, such as Cardiology managing antihypertensives and antiarrhythmics, or Endocrinology controlling glycemia. Collectively, these factors inevitably extend the treatment timeline and increase the length of hospital stay. Secondly, regarding postoperative recovery, transferred lung cancer patients face disadvantages stemming from poorer baseline status, greater comorbidity burden, and longer operative times. These factors heighten susceptibility to complications such as pulmonary infection, often necessitating antimicrobial therapy and supportive care. Consequently, recovery is impeded, leading to prolonged hospitalization and increased costs.

Notably, prolonged hospitalization not only increases the patient’s financial burden—including direct medical expenses and caregiver costs—but also heightens the risk of adverse psychological effects such as anxiety and depression. These effects can undermine confidence in recovery, further impairing long-term outcomes [34, 35].The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) is now widely used to screen hospitalized patients for signs of anxiety and depression, and its applicability in the general population has been repeatedly validated [36].Cruzado et al. demonstrated that patients undergoing thoracic surgery exhibit a high prevalence of psychotherapeutic needs [37].Ju et al. reported that lung cancer patients scheduled for VATS experience elevated anxiety levels, with longer preoperative hospital stays and greater information needs correlating with higher anxiety scores [38].Chen et al. also found that patients with anxiety had longer hospital stays [39].Given that transferred lung cancer patients experience longer total and preoperative hospital stays, more complex disease and treatment profiles, and frequent interactions with multiple healthcare providers, they may suffer from more severe anxiety. Therefore, addressing patients’ information needs, actively improving disease understanding, and strengthening the therapeutic alliance are crucial to alleviating psychological burden. Unfortunately, this study did not assess transferred patients using anxiety and depression scales, limiting further analysis. Future research should incorporate detailed anxiety and depression assessments.

We also observed a significantly higher 30-day readmission rate in the transferred group compared to the outpatient group (6.4% vs. 0.9%, P = 0.031). Konstantinidis et al. indicated that postoperative pulmonary complications and prolonged hospitalization may increase short-term readmission rates [40], while Deshpande et al. showed that patients with anxiety or depression are more likely to be readmitted shortly after surgery [41] Consequently, for transferred lung cancer patients, reducing hospital length of stay, minimizing postoperative pulmonary complications, and addressing psychological distress are critically important.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective analysis, the sample size remains constrained despite using propensity score matching (PSM) to adjust for baseline differences. Potential selection bias, observer bias, and reliance on medical record completeness persist. Notably, some clinically relevant baseline characteristics—such as detailed comorbidities from referring departments and quantified anxiety assessments—were not systematically captured.Second, the study’s timeframe limits its scope to perioperative outcomes. We did not track long-term survival metrics (e.g., 1-, 3-, or 5-year survival), warranting future investigations to compare longitudinal outcomes between admission pathways.Third, findings apply exclusively to patients undergoing thoracoscopic resection. Generalizability to open thoracotomy cohorts remains unverified.To address these gaps, we propose:

Multi-center prospective cohorts with larger samples;Extended follow-up for survival and quality-of-life metrics;Expanded data collection, including psychosocial variables (e.g., validated anxiety scales) and comorbidity severity;Integration of multidisciplinary methods (e.g., psychology, sociology);Comparative analyses across surgical approaches.

Conclusions

This study confirmed that transferred lung cancer patients requiring surgery (particularly those from respiratory medicine) exhibited more complex clinical profiles compared to outpatients, including older age, lower FEV1, more advanced tumor stage, higher ASA classification, incomplete fissures, and more severe pleural adhesions. These factors may adversely affect perioperative outcomes. After PSM, the transferred group had a significantly higher incidence of postoperative pulmonary infection (23.9% vs. 10.1%). Multivariate analysis identified prolonged operative time and reduced FEV1 as independent risk factors for pulmonary infection in transferred patients. Furthermore, the transferred group demonstrated longer median hospital stays, higher hospitalization costs, and increased 30-day readmission rates. Their greater disease burden and management complexity may also contribute to more severe anxiety/depression, necessitating targeted psychological intervention. Therefore, for transferred lung cancer patients, our institution should: establish individualized protocols; enhance multidisciplinary collaboration; optimize transfer processes to shorten preoperative waiting time; improve lung function through respiratory rehabilitation; develop standardized surgical pathways to reduce operative duration without compromising oncological safety; and implement systematic psychological screening/intervention using anxiety/depression scales. These measures aim to improve overall treatment efficacy and long-term survival outcomes.
