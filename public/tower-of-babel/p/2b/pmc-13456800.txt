Asymptomatic migration of Kirschner wire into the cervical spine: a rare case report complication 32 months post-clavicle fracture fixation
Authors: Hieu Nguyen Dinh, Tuyen Nguyen Trung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importance:Kirschner wires (K-wires) are widely used in orthopedic surgery for fracture fixation and joint stabilization, but complications such as migration, though rare, can have severe consequences.Case presentation:We report the case of a 24-year-old female who presented with asymptomatic migration of a K-wire into the cervical spine, 32 months after clavicle fixation. Imaging revealed that the wire had traversed from the right first rib to the C6–C7 interlaminar space. Surgical removal was performed via a posterior medial approach after an initial posterolateral dissection failed. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, with no complications or neurological deficits.Clinical discussion:This case marks the 14th reported instance of spinal K-wire migration and the first involving an asymptomatic female patient.Conclusions:It underscores the importance of careful surgical technique, routine follow-up, timely hardware removal, and the role of advanced imaging in detecting and managing such rare but potentially serious complications.

Introduction and importance

Kirschner wire (K-wire) fixation is widely used in orthopedic surgery because of its simplicity, low cost, and effectiveness. However, complications such as migration, wire breakage, infection, and neurovascular injury have been reported. Although migration is uncommon, it may result in life-threatening complications involving the thoracic cavity, major vessels, or spinal canal[1,2].

Spinal migration of K-wires is exceedingly rare, with only a limited number of cases reported in the literature. Because of the proximity to critical neural and vascular structures, delayed diagnosis may lead to severe neurological injury[3]. Nevertheless, several asymptomatic cases have also been described, emphasizing the importance of routine postoperative surveillance.

Here, we report a rare case of asymptomatic migration of a clavicular K-wire into the cervical spine 32 months after fixation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of an asymptomatic female patient with cervical spinal migration of a K-wire. This case highlights the importance of long-term follow-up, timely hardware removal, and individualized surgical management.

Case presentation

A 24-year-old female underwent right lateral clavicle fixation using a K-wire tension band technique. Due to the COVID-19 pandemic and subsequent pregnancy, the patient did not attend her scheduled follow-up appointments.

Thirty-two months postoperatively, during a routine radiographic evaluation at a routine general health check-up, migration of the K-wire into the cervical spine was incidentally identified on right shoulder radiographs, revealing a migration of approximately 10 cm (Fig. 1). The patient reported no neck pain, upper limb pain, numbness, weakness, gait disturbance, or bowel/bladder dysfunction. Neurological examination demonstrated full motor strength (grade 5/5) in all extremities, intact sensory function, normal deep tendon reflexes, and the absence of pathological reflexes. No signs of myelopathy or spinal cord compression were identified.

Further evaluation with computed tomography (CT) scans revealed that the K-wire had migrated in a trajectory from anterior to posterior and from inferior to superior. The lateral end of the wire was located adjacent to the right first rib, while the medial tip traversed the right C6–C7 interlaminar region (Figs 2 and 3). CT multiplanar reconstruction demonstrated that the medial tip penetrated the right C6–C7 interlaminar space without clear evidence of spinal cord compression, neural foraminal compromise, or associated vertebral destruction. No epidural hematoma or major vascular injury was identified on imaging.

The patient underwent surgical removal of the migrated K-wire under general anesthesia in the prone position. Intraoperative localization was performed using fluoroscopic guidance. An initial right posterolateral exploration was attempted to identify the lateral tip of the wire near the first rib; however, the wire could not be safely identified as it was deeply embedded within the surrounding soft tissues.

The surgical strategy was, therefore, changed to a right posterior paramedian approach at the C6–C7 level. Under fluoroscopic guidance, the migrated K-wire was identified within the right C6–C7 interlaminar region and carefully extracted. No intraoperative complications occurred.

The operative duration was approximately 90 minutes. No surgical drain was required, and postoperative intravenous antibiotics were administered for 24 hours. The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery and was discharged on postoperative day 5.

The extracted wire measured approximately 8 cm in length (Fig. 4). The discrepancy between the radiographic and extracted wire length may reflect projection magnification and partial embedding within the surrounding soft tissues. At the 24-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic without neurological deficits.

Clinical discussion

K-wire migration into the spine is an extraordinarily rare complication, with profound implications for patient outcomes and surgical management. The first case, reported by Mazet in 1943, described the potential for wire migration into the thoracic cavity, with one instance involving the spinal column at the T2–T3 level[5]. Subsequent case reports and reviews, such as those by N’da et al (2018)[3], have shown that the most commonly reported levels of spinal migration are the cervicothoracic and upper thoracic regions, particularly C7–T1 and T2–T3. This predilection for these regions may reflect their anatomical positioning relative to the clavicle and the mechanical forces exerted during patient movement.

In rare instances, migrated K-wires may penetrate the spinal canal or adjacent structures, as reported by Furuhata et al (2020) and Tsai et al (2009)[3,5,6]. These migration patterns suggest that wire displacement is influenced not only by mechanical factors but also by dynamic biological processes such as tissue remodeling, muscle activity, respiratory motion, gravity, and joint mobility.

To date, only 14 cases of spinal K-wire migration have been reported, including this study (Table 1). Among these, our case is the first involving a female patient presenting asymptomatically, with detection occurring 32 months postoperatively. Comparatively, prior cases demonstrate a broad time range for detection, spanning from 3 weeks (Mian et al, 2012) to 7 years (Mankowski et al, 2016)[7,8]. Notably, symptomatic cases frequently present with neurological deficits, such as Brown-Séquard syndrome (Lonçan, 1998)[9] or paraparesis (Fransen, 2007)[10]. In contrast, asymptomatic presentations, such as those reported by Bennis et al (2008) and Li et al (2013), are uncommon[11,12]. Our patient’s case underscores the potential for K-wire migration to remain undetected in the absence of symptoms, emphasizing the need for vigilant follow-up. This highlights the importance of proactive imaging, particularly in patients with incomplete follow-up due to extrinsic factors like the COVID-19 pandemic.

Computed tomography with multiplanar reconstruction was essential for defining the trajectory of the migrated wire and planning surgical removal. In this case, CT imaging demonstrated penetration of the right C6–C7 interlaminar region without evidence of spinal cord compression or major vascular injury.

Surgical removal of a migrated K-wire is the definitive treatment and demands a tailored approach based on the wire’s anatomical positioning. Posterior medial approaches are generally favored due to their direct access to the spinal column and reduced dissection complexity. Conversely, posterolateral approaches may be hindered by the depth of the wire and muscular obstructions, as encountered in this case. The failure of our initial posterolateral approach illustrates the technical challenges associated with accessing deeply embedded wires.

This case also underscores the utility of a staged surgical strategy, transitioning between approaches as dictated by intraoperative findings. Successful extraction using the posterior medial approach affirms its value, aligning with previous recommendations in the literature[3,5].

The rarity of spinal K-wire migration belies its potential severity. Neurological complications, though infrequent, can have catastrophic consequences, as evidenced by the reports of tetraparesis (Regel et al, 2002) and Brown-Séquard syndrome (Lonçan, 1998)[9,13]. Prevention hinges on the meticulous surgical technique, particularly ensuring secure K-wire fixation. Bending the wire’s ends to reduce mobility, employing locking constructs, and removing hardware promptly post-healing are critical measures. Routine imaging, even in asymptomatic patients, is essential for early detection.

This case uniquely highlights the interplay of patient-specific factors, such as pregnancy and pandemic-related healthcare disruptions, in complicating follow-up and surveillance. Addressing these challenges requires robust systems for patient tracking and telemedicine follow-up to mitigate delays in care. This case provides several clinically important insights beyond the rarity of spinal K-wire migration itself. First, despite penetration into the cervical interlaminar region, the patient remained completely asymptomatic without neurological deficit, emphasizing that the absence of symptoms does not exclude potentially hazardous migration. Second, unlike the majority of previously reported cases involving male patients, our patient was female, further expanding the demographic spectrum of this complication. Third, delayed diagnosis in this case was strongly influenced by the interruption of postoperative surveillance during pregnancy and the COVID-19 pandemic, highlighting the vulnerability of long-term orthopedic follow-up systems during major healthcare disruptions. Finally, this case demonstrates the importance of intraoperative flexibility, as the initial posterolateral exploration failed and a posterior paramedian approach was ultimately required for safe extraction. These factors collectively provide practical diagnostic and surgical lessons for orthopedic and spine surgeons managing migrated fixation devices.

One limitation of this report is the absence of detailed intraoperative exposure photographs, which limits the visual demonstration of the surgical corridor and extraction process. Nevertheless, the diagnosis and operative planning were comprehensively supported by multiplanar CT reconstruction and fluoroscopic localization.

Conclusions

K-wire migration into the cervical spine is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication. This case demonstrates that significant spinal migration may remain completely asymptomatic despite proximity to critical neurovascular structures. Early radiological detection, careful surgical planning, and prompt removal are essential to prevent catastrophic complications. Routine postoperative follow-up and timely hardware removal remain key preventive measures.
