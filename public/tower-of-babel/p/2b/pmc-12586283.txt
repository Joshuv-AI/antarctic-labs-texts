Prevalence of Contact Allergy to Rubber Accelerators From the European Baseline Series in Dermatitis Patients: A Systematic Review and Meta‐Analysis
Authors: Daniel Isufi, Christoffer Kursawe Larsen, Mikkel Bak Jensen, Kian Karimian, Claus Zachariae, Jakob Ferløv Baselius Schwensen, Farzad Alinaghi, Jeanne Duus Johansen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRubber accelerators are chemicals used as vulcanizers in the production of rubber items and may cause contact allergy (CA). The aim of this study was to assess the worldwide prevalence of CA to rubber accelerators from the European baseline series among dermatitis patients. A comprehensive literature search across PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science yielded 106 studies with 826 543 individuals patch tested between 1984 and 2023. Meta‐analysis showed pooled prevalence rates of 2.55% for thiuram mix, 0.86% for mercapto mix, and 0.83% for mercaptobenzothiazole. Marked geographical variations were observed: North America and Asian regions showed the highest prevalences whereas Europe had lower rates. Comparisons before and after the year 2000 revealed a decline in CA to all three accelerators. Sub‐analysis of the extended rubber series revealed the prevalence of CA to tetraethylthiuram disulphide of 3.73% and to zinc diethyldithiocarbamate of 0.60%. The prevalence of CA to carba mix was 3.96%. These findings underscore the public health implications of rubber accelerator‐induced CA. Strengthened preventive measures and regulatory efforts, including improved labelling, safe limits of rubber gloves, and reduced use of high‐risk accelerators are needed to mitigate disease burden. The results emphasise a need for ongoing surveillance, especially concerning extended rubber series.Keywords: allergic contact dermatitis, contact allergy, epidemiology, prevalence, rubber accelerators

Introduction

Rubber accelerators are chemicals used to enhance the vulcanisation process of rubber and may cause contact allergy (CA) and thereby allergic contact dermatitis [1]. CA to rubber accelerators represents a significant public health concern; in Denmark, rubber accelerators are the most frequent cause of recognised occupational allergic contact dermatitis and 87% of these cases are due to rubber gloves [2]. Occupations most often affected by CA to rubber accelerators are those where exposure to rubber products is common, that is, among health care workers, cleaners, mechanics, hairdressers, and food industry workers [1, 3, 4]. Currently, the European Baselines Series (EBS) includes three rubber accelerators: thiuram mix, mercaptobenzothiazole, and mercapto mix [5]. However, extended rubber series exist and include several other rubber accelerators for example single thiurams such as tetraethylthiuram disulphide and dithiocarbamates such as zinc diethyldithiocarbamate (ZDEC) [6].

Some studies have investigated the prevalence of CA to rubber accelerators revealing varying results [7, 8, 9]. However, no comprehensive review of the literature has been conducted to date. This study aims to examine the prevalence of CA to rubber accelerators in adult and paediatric dermatitis patients and according to geographical regions.

Methods

Prior to initiation, a study protocol was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD42025590255). The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐analyses (PRISMA) guidelines were followed [10].

Literature Search

A literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science with the search strings in Table S1. Two authors (DI and MBJ) independently screened articles based on title and abstract. The full‐text version of eligible articles and articles where eligibility was unclear based on title and abstract were retrieved. Discrepancies were resolved through debate with author CKL.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Eligible studies had to: (I) be original, (II) include ≥ 100 dermatitis patients, (III) written in English, (IV) conducted in humans, and (V) report the number of positive patch tests (PPTs) for rubber accelerators.

Exclusion criteria were: (I) no report on CA to rubber accelerators, (II) grey literature (i.e., conference abstract), and (III) studies not conducted in humans. In the case of a population being included in more than one article, the most comprehensive version was included.

Data Extraction and Quality Assessment

Where applicable, the following was extracted to a predefined Excel‐sheet: name of the article, author surname, country, type of rubber accelerator(s) tested including the concentration and vehicle, number of patients undergoing patch testing, number of patients with PPTs towards respective rubber accelerators, relevance of PPTs, sex, and age.

If possible, the following data was extracted specifically for patients with PPTs towards rubber accelerators: number of males, occupational dermatitis, atopic dermatitis, hand dermatitis, leg dermatitis, facial dermatitis, age > 40 years (MOAHLFA‐index) and other locations of dermatitis.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using StatsDirect (version 3.1.4), following established methods [11] previously used in similar systematic reviews [12, 13, 14]. Pooled proportions for PPTs were calculated with random‐effects models (DerSimonian–Laird) and 95% confidence intervals. Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochran Q‐test and the I 2 statistic, with I 2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% denoting moderate, large, and very large heterogeneity, respectively. The Appraisal tool for cross‐sectional studies (AXIS) was employed to evaluate the risk of bias, and publication bias was investigated using Egger's test and funnel plots. Forest plots illustrated study comparisons and confidence interval precision.

For sub‐analysis, PPTs were grouped by geographical region (Africa, East Asia, Southeast Asia, Europe, North America, Oceania, and the Middle East) and by publication year (before vs. after 2000). Studies containing overlapping data for year 2000 were excluded. For the extended rubber series, statistical analyses for rubber accelerators were performed on those reported in ≥ 2 studies, pooling data when different concentrations were used. Complete data are presented in Appendix S1. Graphical comparisons were generated using GraphPad Prism (version 10.4.1).

Results

Qualitative Assessment of the Included Studies

Eligible Studies

A total of 3849 articles (PubMed = 1919, Embase = 272, Web of Science = 1658) were extracted. Of these, 2356 non‐duplicate articles were screened based on title and abstract, yielding 136 full text articles eligible for inclusion. Of these, 30 were excluded with reasons of no report on rubber accelerators (n = 14), not enough data available (n = 10), population already included (n = 5), and less than 100 patients (n = 1), resulting in 106 articles included in the meta‐analysis (Figure S1).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta‐analysis of 106 studies encompassing 826 543 patients who underwent patch testing provides comprehensive insights into the epidemiology of CA to rubber accelerators.

The overall prevalence of CA to thiuram mix, mercapto mix, and mercaptobenzothiazole was 2.55%, 0.86%, and 0.83%, respectively. This is higher compared to other rubber additives from the EBS; for example, the antioxidant N‐isopropyl‐N′‐phenyl‐paraphenylenediamine (IPPD; 0.41% [119]) used in black rubber items such as rubber tires [120]. Further, the found prevalence of thiuram mix is higher compared to, for example, textile dye mix (2.3% [105, 121]) and epoxy resin (1.3% [122]) but still lower than, for example, neomycin (3.2% in adults and 4.3% in children [14]) and preservatives like methylisothiazolinone (MI; 5% [105, 123]).

Clinical relevance found for thiuram mix, mercapto mix, and mercaptobenzothiazole was 51.2%, 64.5%, and 62.9%, respectively. These numbers are low considering the need for clear exposure to often well‐defined sources, as for example rubber gloves, to develop CA to this group of allergens. However, it may underline the need for better surveillance and registration of relevance, as discussed earlier [13, 14].

Regional differences in rates of CA to rubber accelerators were observed, with Europe generally showing the lowest rates for both thiuram mix (2.18%) and mercapto mix (0.66%), while the lowest rates for mercaptobenzothiazole were found in Africa (0.37%). However, this latter finding was only based on three studies and thus should be interpreted with caution. On the other hand, North America and Asian regions revealed tendencies of high rates of CA to rubber accelerators. These regional changes may rely on variability in the use of rubber items and education in, for example, the correct use of rubber gloves. Further, it could be that regions with a robust rubber manufacturing industry, like Southeast Asia, may demonstrate higher rates of CA due to increased occupational exposure among these workers. Also, intercountry differences in the content of rubber accelerators in rubber items may exist. This was discussed in a recent Copenhagen market survey on the content of rubber accelerators in rubber gloves. In this study, 35 of 48 disposable gloves were produced in Southeast Asia, and 13 did not state the country of origin [124]. The article anticipated that no important differences in the content of rubber accelerators in rubber gloves between western countries exists [124] since the majority of gloves are imported from the same geographical region. However, this must be investigated further considering the much higher prevalence of CA to rubber accelerators in North America compared to Europe reported in the current study. Rubber accelerators, such as ZDEC and ZDBC, have recently been identified in accelerator‐free gloves [124, 125]. This is highly problematic, as this glove type is an important preventive measure for patients with CA to rubber accelerators, as demonstrated by Crepy et al. [126]. It is important that clinicians continue to advise patients to use accelerator‐free gloves, bearing in mind their potential content of rubber accelerators, which may be problematic for certain patients.

Based on the study period before versus after year 2000 in any geographical region, a statistically significant decline was observed for thiuram mix (4.34% vs. 2.37%), mercapto mix (1.99% vs. 0.73%), and mercaptobenzothiazole (1.84% vs. 0.67%). These findings are consistent with several studies presenting declining trends for CA to rubber accelerators [8, 9, 77]. For thiurams, the decline may be ascribed to the fact that glove producers began using dithiocarbamates instead of thiurams in the beginning of the current millennium [9].

Carba mix was excluded from the EBS in 1989 as concomitant reactions with thiuram mix were observed [127]. Ever since, dithiocarbamates have not been tested as part of the EBS. Rather, they are tested in supplementary rubber series. However, a few countries test with dithiocarbamates in baseline series; for example, carba mix is part of the British baseline series. The prevalence of CA to carba mix was high (3.96%). However, the diagnostic value of carba mix as a marker of dithiocarbamates can be discussed as it includes the non‐dithiocarbamate diphenylgunaidine that may cause irritant reactions [128].

Regarding the extended rubber series, the most prevalent dithiocarbamates and thiurams were ZDEC and TETD, respectively. As expected, we found a higher prevalence of CA to TETD (3.73%) than ZDEC (0.60%). Besides thiurams being used in gloves before the year 2000, thiurams and dithiocarbamates are chemically related, form a redox pair, and it has been stated that thiurams are the best marker for CA to thiurams/dithiocarbamates [129]. Further cross‐reactivity between ZDEC and TETD has been observed in mouse models [130].

Despite the comprehensive coverage of rubber accelerators in the EBS, the diagnostic value of extended rubber series should not be overlooked. It is vital to note that as many as 40% of patients who will be diagnosed with CA to rubber additives may be overlooked if extended series are not tested additionally [65]. Consequently, the use of rubber series is recommended when CA to rubber accelerators is suspected, for example, when healthcare workers with hand dermatitis use rubber gloves as part of their work.

During time, mercapto mix has been tested as both 1% consisting of N‐cyclohexylbenzothiazyl sulfenamide, dibenzothiazyl disulphide, and morpholinylmercaptobenzothiazole and 2% consisting of N‐cyclohexylbenzothiazyl sulfenamide, mercaptobenzothiazole, dibenzothiazyl disulphide, and morpholinylmercaptobenzothiazole [5]. We stratified PPTs by the two concentrations and found that significantly more patients reacted to 2% compared to 1%. The EBS currently tests with mercapto mix 2% [5], and our study further indicates the important diagnostic values of this.

The strengths of this study are (I) the comprehensive and systematic literature search including 106 studies and data on 826 543 patch tested patients, (II) inclusion of data on extended rubber series, (III) inclusion of data on concentration and vehicle, and (IV) extensive sub‐analyses based on geographical regions and study period, thus highlighting the epidemiology of CA to rubber accelerators. However, some limitations should be noted: (I) estimates of the proportion of CA to rubber accelerators in some geographical regions relied on few studies and should be interpreted with caution, (II) data was not feasible for comparison of temporal trends within specific geographical regions, and (III) variability in applying and reading of the patch test may exist considering the long study period from 1984 to 2023 and inclusion of studies from all over the world.

The high prevalence of CA to rubber accelerators found in this study calls for better worldwide implementation of strategies for primary prevention. Correct use of rubber gloves and substitution of rubber accelerators with alternative substances may contribute to the observed decline in CA to rubber accelerators. For example, the use of accelerator‐free gloves is an important part of the secondary prevention strategy for CA to rubber accelerators.

Gaps in Knowledge

Although the current study attempted to provide a comprehensive overview of the epidemiology of CA to rubber accelerators, knowledge gaps remain in the literature. Currently, few studies report the number of clinically relevant PPTs, although we present promising results on their relevance. A more transparent reporting of this could provide valuable insights and enhance clinical practice.

There exists no regulation on the content of rubber accelerators in rubber gloves. To establish such safe limits, threshold values of rubber accelerators eliciting allergic contact dermatitis are highly needed, especially for thiurams and dithiocarbamates, and require further investigation. Recently, the patch test dose of TETD eliciting allergic contact dermatitis in 10% of patients (ED10) was found to be 0.75 μg/cm2, which should be used as the point of departure in future discussions of safe limits of rubber accelerators in rubber gloves [131].

The highest rate of CA to single rubber accelerators is seen for thiurams even though patients primarily may be exposed to dithiocarbamates by rubber gloves. Future studies on this topic should focus on the chemical properties of these two close chemicals including their chemical behaviour in the skin.

Conclusion

CA to rubber accelerators is frequent in dermatitis patients worldwide, with notable variations across geographical regions. For preventive measures, we recommend regulations on safe limits of rubber accelerators in rubber products such as rubber gloves, improved labelling of, for example, rubber gloves, and introducing alternative accelerators or accelerator‐free options.
