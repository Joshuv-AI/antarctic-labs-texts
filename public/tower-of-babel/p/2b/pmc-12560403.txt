Mechanism by which CAP regulates the “cold–heat” balance in osteosarcoma model mice: an integrative study of metabolomics, transcriptomics, and network pharmacology
Authors: Ziming Wang, Shun Zhang, Shipeng Li, Guosheng Zhao, Yuan Zhang, Xiaohong Zhang, Mingwei Sun, Yun Lu, Enfeng Song, Cantao Quan, Chuanzhu Lv, Yang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOsteosarcoma (OS) is a prevalent malignant bone cancer in children and adolescents, yet the mechanisms driving its progression and therapeutic resistance remain inadequately understood. Current treatment modalities often overlook the significance of the “cold–heat” imbalance as conceptualized in traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), highlighting a critical gap in integrating TCM principles with modern cancer therapies.ObjectivesWe aimed to investigate the therapeutic effects of capsaicin (CAP) on the “cold–heat” imbalance in OS model mice and elucidate the mechanisms involved by utilizing a multiomics approach.MethodsWe employed metabolomics, transcriptomics, and network pharmacology to analyze the impact of CAP on OS progression. Tumor-bearing mice were treated with CAP, and metabolic profiles, tumor volumes, and key molecular targets were assessed.ResultsOur findings revealed that CAP significantly shifted the metabolic phenotype of OS model mice from a “cold” state to a “heat” state, resulting in a reduced tumor volume and increased metabolic activity. Notably, CAP was shown to target key pathways involving AKT1, STAT3, NF-κB, EGFR and Caspase 3.ConclusionCAP effectively modulated tumor growth and restored the “cold–heat” balance in OS model mice, providing valuable insights into the potential integration of TCM with conventional oncologic therapies. These findings underscore the importance of multifaceted strategies in developing innovative cancer treatments and contribute to the advancement of translational medicine.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07238-z.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS), the most common primary malignant bone cancer [1], typically affects children and adolescents, with a median age of 16 years [2]. The 5-year survival rate for children and young adults with localized disease has increased to 78% with combined surgical and adjuvant/neoadjuvant chemotherapy [3, 4]. However, despite advances in treatment strategies for OS management, systemic toxicities often result in suboptimal therapeutic outcomes. The 5-year survival rate for patients with metastatic OS remains dismally low, ranging from 20 to 30% [5–7]. Unfortunately, the survival rate for most OS patients has not increased significantly over the past three decades [8, 9]. As such, there is an urgent need for new treatment strategies to prolong the overall survival of individuals with OS.

Traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), employed in cancer treatment for thousands of years, provides a rich source of bioactive natural compounds characterized by multi-component and multi-target properties [10, 11]. TCM not only serves as a preventive measure against cancer development but also effectively alleviates adverse side effects associated with radiotherapy and chemotherapy. Currently, TCM occupies an important position within integrative oncology as a complementary and alternative therapeutic approach [12, 13]. Central to TCM theory are the concepts of “cold” and “heat,” which are pivotal in disease understanding and therapeutic application. According to TCM, diseases and medications are categorized into “cold” or “heat” classifications, with the principle that “heat” medications are utilized to treat conditions exhibiting a “cold” pattern [14, 15]. Classical TCM texts provide foundational insights into these concepts. For instance, the ancient Chinese medical text Huangdi Nei Jing, composed approximately 2000 years ago, proposed the principle of “Yang transforms into qi, while Yin constitutes form” (yang hua qi, yin cheng xing). Moreover, the Huangdi Nei Jing explicitly states that “Tumors arise due to the presence of cold” (ji zhi shi sheng, de han nai sheng), while the General Treatise on the Causes and Manifestations of Diseases (Zhu Bing Yuan Hou Lun) indicates that “Tumors and masses originate from internal cold” (ji ju zhe, you han qi zai nei suo sheng ye), and the Classic of Difficult Issues (Nan Jing) asserts that “Tumors are of yin nature” (ji zhe yin qi ye). Collectively, these canonical perspectives consistently attribute tumorigenesis to pathological conditions associated with “cold” characteristics.

While many classical TCM texts historically link tumor-like masses to “cold” syndromes, modern TCM research acknowledges that different tumors can present distinct “cold” or “heat” characteristics depending on their clinical manifestations, underlying pathophysiology, and treatment responses. Among these, OS is widely considered a “cold-type” disease in contemporary TCM practice due to its characteristic features [16, 17]. Recent studies have qualitatively and quantitatively examined the “heat” attributes of TCMs, demonstrating that they can increase heat release in the body’s metabolic processes [18]. Meanwhile, researchers have investigated the effects of “cold” and “heat” medications on rats by analyzing their metabolites through metabolomics. These findings suggest that “heat” and “cold” medications may exert their physiological effects by modulating lipid metabolism [19]. This provides a direction for studying the mechanisms by which TCM intervenes during disease by regulating the cold–heat balance [20–22].

Capsicum annuum L. was introduced into China 300 years ago and used in TCM to treat ailments attributed to “cold” in the body, such as abdominal pain and chilblains [23]. Capsaicin (CAP) (8-methyl-N-vanillyl-6-nonenamide, Supplementary Materials Fig. S1), a key component of pepper, has been reported to inhibit the progression of different types of tumors [24, 25].

Our research group has dedicated significant effort to studying the impact of CAP intervention on OS over an extended period [26, 27], establishing a robust research foundation. Previous research has demonstrated the promising role of CAP in inducing apoptosis and cell cycle arrest and inhibiting OS cell invasion. Furthermore, the combination of CAP with cisplatin and the photosensitizer IR780 has synergistic effects on OS [27]. Although the evidence suggests that CAP has potential as an effective antitumor agent, the role of CAP in maintaining the cold–heat balance of the body during the progression of OS remains unexplored. Furthermore, recent advancements in multiomics approaches for investigating interventions in TCM have offered new avenues for research in ethnopharmacology [28–30]. This study represents the first attempt to investigate how CAP regulates the balance of “cold–heat” in OS through metabolomics, transcriptomics, and network pharmacology. These findings provide new insights into the role of CAP in OS treatment.

Materials and methods

Animals and model establishment

A total of 62 male nude mice (4 weeks old) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Sichuan Province People’s Hospital. All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the experimental protocol approved by the Ethics Committee of Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital to ensure adherence to ethical standards for animal welfare (Number 661, 2024, Sichuan Province People’s Hospital). The mice were housed in specific pathogen-free conditions with ad libitum access to food and water. Prior to the commencement of the study, the mice were individually housed for one week to acclimate to the experimental environment. Following the acclimation period, each mouse in the CAP intervention group and model group was subcutaneously injected with 200 μL of sterile PBS(PH 7.4) containing human MNNG/HOS cells (cell density 106 cells/mL) into the right flank to induce the OS model. The experimental intervention began once the tumor volume reached 50 mm3.

Among the 62 mice, 40 were used for cold/heat property identification and to investigate the effects of CAP on the OS model. These mice were divided into eight groups(n = 5 per group): control (CON), model (MOD), CAP-L (low-dose CAP, 10 mg/kg), CAP-M (medium-dose CAP, 20 mg/kg), CAP-H (high-dose CAP, 40 mg/kg), MOD+CAP-L, MOD+CAP-M, and MOD+CAP-H groups. Treatments were administered every 4 days for a total of 5 interventions. Body weights were recorded every 5 days during treatment. The tumor volume was calculated every 5 days using the formula: tumor volume = ½ × a2 × b (where ‘a’ represents the tumor width and ‘b’ represents the tumor length). On day 20, mice were euthanized under anesthesia, with xenograft tumors weighed and analyzed for each subject. The remaining 22 mice were used for transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses. The CAP group received 20 mg/kg CAP in 200 μL PBS (PH 7.4) via oral gavage, while the control group received no treatment.

Cell culture

MNNG/HOS cell line were procured from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC; Manassas, VA, USA) and cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 μg/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

SDH and FAO (fatty acid oxidation) detection

A 50 mg sample of tumor tissue was frozen in liquid nitrogen, ground to a powder on ice, and then added to 500 μL of protein extraction buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4; 1 mM EDTA; and 1 mM PMSF). The samples were sonicated, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 minutes at 4 °C, resulting in the collection of the supernatant. The SDH and FAO test kits were subsequently utilized according to the provided instructions. After incubation at 37 °C for 30 minutes, the absorbance values were measured at specific wavelengths using a spectrophotometer. Finally, the levels of SDH and FAO in the samples were determined by referencing the values to the standard curve.

Metabolite extraction and metabolomic analysis

Approximately 50 mg of tumor tissue was accurately weighed and placed into a 2 mL centrifuge tube, followed by the addition of 1000 μL extraction solvent consisting of 75% (v/v) 9:1 methanol:chloroform and 25% water. After the addition of stainless steel beads, the samples were homogenized using a tissue grinder at 50 Hz for 60 seconds, repeated twice. The homogenate was subjected to 30 minutes of sonication at room temperature, followed by 30 minutes of ice bath incubation. The samples were then centrifuged at 12,000 × g 12,000 rpm for 10 minutes at 4 °C, and the supernatants were collected, concentrated, and dried. The dried residue was reconstituted in 200 μL of 50% acetonitrile containing 2-chloro-L-phenylalanine (4 ppm) as an internal standard, filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, and transferred to autosampler vials for LC–MS analysis.

Chromatographic separation was performed using a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm, Waters, USA) maintained at 40 °C. The flow rate was set at 0.3 mL/min, and the injection volume was 2 μL. For positive ion mode, the mobile phases were 0.1% formic acid in water (A2) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (B2), with the following gradient: 0–1 min, 8% B2; 1–8 min, 8–98% B2; 8–10 min, 98% B2; 10–10.1 min, 98–8% B2; 10.1–12 min, 8% B2. For negative ion mode, the mobile phases were 5 mM ammonium formate in water (A3) and acetonitrile (B3), using the same gradient program as above. Mass spectrometry was performed on an Orbitrap Exploris 120 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization source in both positive and negative modes using Full MS-ddMS2 (data-dependent acquisition). The key parameters were set as follows: spray voltage at +3.50 kV/−2.50 kV, capillary temperature 325 °C, sheath gas 40 arb, aux gas 10 arb, MS1 scan range m/z 100–1000, resolution 60,000 FWHM for MS1, 15,000 FWHM for MS2, normalized collision energy 30%, and dynamic exclusion set to automatic.

The raw data were first converted to mzXML format by MSConvert in ProteoWizard software package (v3.0.8789) and processed using R XCMS(v3.12.0) for feature detection, retention time correction and alignment. The key parameters were set as follows: ppm = 15, peakwidth = c(5, 30), mzdiff = 0.01, method = centWave. Subsequently, further analysis was conducted via the MetaboAnalyst 6.0 online server. Initially, peak data were matched from the raw liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) data, and the peak area data were preprocessed in Microsoft Excel 2019. To ensure the reproducibility and statistical robustness of the metabolomics analyses, raw data were normalized and scaled prior to downstream analysis. Batch effects were identified and corrected using quality control-based robust LOESS signal correction. Principal component analysis (PCA) and quality control samples were used to evaluate data stability. For statistical analysis, univariate and multivariate approaches were employed, and multiple testing was corrected using the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Metabolites with an adjusted p value (FDR) < 0.05 were considered significantly different, ensuring reliable identification of differential metabolites and pathway enrichment results. Metabolite abundance was evaluated using Student’s t-tests, and metabolites with statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) were considered differentially abundant. These differentially abundant metabolites (DAMs) were subsequently visualized using volcano plots to highlight both the magnitude and significance of changes between groups.

Transcriptome sequencing and data analysis

RNA was extracted from the tumor tissues of both the model group and the CAP group, each consisting of 11 samples weighing 50 mg, via, using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted RNA was quantified and its quality was assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). An Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) was used to ensure RNA purity and integrity for sequencing. Eukaryotic mRNA was enriched using magnetic beads containing oligo (dT), followed by fragmentation using a fragmentation buffer (New England Biolabs, USA) at 94 °C for 5–7 minutes. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers and reverse transcriptase, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis. The resulting double-stranded cDNA was purified, end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated with sequencing adapters. After fragment size selection with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, USA), PCR amplification was performed to obtain the final cDNA library. Sequencing was conducted on an Illumina HiSeq platform (Illumina, USA). Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified from raw read counts using the edgeR package (version 3.42.0) in R.

To ensure the reproducibility and statistical robustness of the omics analyses, potential batch effects were corrected using the removeBatchEffect function in the limma package during data preprocessing. Differential expression analysis was performed using the EdgeR package, and multiple testing correction was conducted with the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate. For subsequent GO and KEGG enrichment analyses, an FDR threshold of < 0.05 was applied to identify significantly enriched terms, thereby enhancing the reliability and biological interpretability of the results. DEGs were defined as those with a fold change > 1.2 and adjusted p value < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted using the ClusterProfiler package (version 4.12.1). Terms with adjusted p values < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Network pharmacology analysis

To systematically explore the potential molecular mechanisms of CAP in the treatment of OS, we conducted a network pharmacology analysis using a multi-step integrative approach. First, the SMILES string of CAP was retrieved from the PubChem database [33] (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). This structural data was uploaded to three independent target prediction platforms SwissTargetPrediction [34] (http://www.swisstargetprediction.ch/), SuperPred (https://prediction.charite.de/index.php), and PharmMapper [35] (http://lilab-ecust.cn/pharmmapper/) to predict CAP-related targets. Only human (Homo sapiens)-related targets were retained.

Concurrently, OS-related genes were identified from multiple disease-gene databases, including GeneCards [36] (https://www.genecards.org/), DisGeNET [37] (https://www.disgenet.org/home/), DrugBank [38] (https://go.drugbank.com/), Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) (https://www.omim.org/), and the Therapeutic Target Database [39] (TTD) (http://db.idrblab.net/ttd/). All retrieved targets were standardized using UniProt [40] (https://www.uniprot.org/) to ensure consistent gene/protein nomenclature.

Overlapping targets between CAP and OS were identified using Venn diagram analysis. These common targets were then uploaded to the STRING database [41] (https://string-db.org/) with the organism set as Homo sapiens to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network. The resulting interaction data were exported as a .tsv file and imported into Cytoscape 3.10.2 software for visualization and topological analysis [42].

Core targets were screened using the cytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape based on multiple centrality metrics, including Degree, Closeness, and Betweenness. Subsequently, GO enrichment and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the Metascape platform [43] (https://metascape.org/) to identify statistically significant biological processes and signaling pathways. Enrichment results were filtered using an FDR threshold of < 0.05, and the top pathways were visualized based on enrichment counts and significance.

This workflow adheres to widely accepted standards in network pharmacology research and provides a reproducible framework for analyzing the complex interactions between CAP and disease mechanisms [44].

Verification via molecular docking analysis

The 2D structure of the small-molecule ligand was obtained from the PubChem database (http://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/). This 2D structure was then input into Chem Office 20.0 software to generate its 3D structure, which was subsequently saved as a mol2 file. The RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) (http://www.rcsb.org/) was utilized to screen for protein targets and high-resolution crystal structures to serve as molecular docking receptors. PyMOL 2.6.0 software was used to remove water and phosphate groups from the proteins, and the structures were saved as PDB files. Molecular Operating Environment 2019 software was used for energy minimization of the compounds, pretreatment of the target proteins, and identification of active pockets. Finally, MOE 2019 was used for to perform the molecular docking analysis. The number of operations was set to 50. The binding strength was evaluated on the basis of the binding energy, and the results were visualized via PyMOL 2.6.0 and Discovery Studio 2019 software.

Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay

SPR experiments were conducted using a Biacore T200 instrument (GE Healthcare, USA) to experimentally validate the binding affinities of CAP to Caspase-3 and NF-κB. Recombinant human target proteins (purity ≥90%) were immobilized onto CM5 sensor chips using standard amine coupling according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, proteins were diluted to 25 µg/mL in sodium acetate buffer (pH 4.5,5.0,5.5) and immobilized at approximately 10,000 RU. CAP was dissolved in running buffer (PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20 and 5% DMSO, pH 7.4) and injected over the immobilized proteins at a series of concentrations (15.625, 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250, 500 and 1000 µM) with a flow rate of 30 µL/min at 25 °C. Association and dissociation phases lasted 60 s and 60 s, respectively. Sensorgrams were corrected with reference flow cell subtraction, and kinetic parameters (KD) were determined by fitting the data using Biacore T200 evaluation software (version 3.2).

Metabolomics, network pharmacology and transcriptomics integrative analysis

To comprehensively investigate the impact of CAP on OS, this study integrated metabolomics, transcriptomics, and network pharmacology data. Initially, DEGs and DAMs were identified from the transcriptome and metabolome data, respectively. DAMs associated with “cold and heat” were matched with SMILES in the PubChem database. The targets of these metabolites were subsequently predicted using the Swiss Target Prediction Database. By conducting network pharmacology analysis, common targets (CTs) of CAP and OS were identified. The DAMs, DEGs, and CTs related to “cold and heat” were then compared and a topological analysis was conducted to pinpoint core targets.

Western blot analysis

Following treatment with different protocols for specified durations, the cells were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer containing phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF) and phosphatase inhibitors to extract total intracellular proteins. Protein samples (30–50 μg/lane) were separated by SDS‒PAGE on 8–12% gels and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were then blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by overnight incubation with primary antibodies (1:800) at 4 °C. The membranes were subsequently washed with Tris-buffered saline and Tween-20 (TBST) and incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 hour at 37 °C. Finally, the protein bands were detected using an ECL imaging system (Tanon 5200, Tanon Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC)

Tumor tissues fixed in formalin were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. The sections were dewaxed with xylene and subsequently hydrated using ethanol gradient solutions. Antigen retrieval was achieved through high-pressure treatment with citric acid buffer at pH 6.0. Next, 5% goat serum was added to block nonspecific binding sites. For each protein of interest, the sections were incubated sequentially with a primary antibody followed by a fluorescent-labelled secondary antibody, with washing steps using PBS after each incubation. To prevent interference between the fluorescence signals of different antibodies, distinct fluorescence channels were utilized for detection after each labelling round. Once all target proteins were labelled, the slides were mounted with anti-fluorescence quenching mounting solution, and images were acquired via multispectral fluorescence microscopy.

Immunofluorescence (IF)

The cell suspension was evenly distributed onto coverslips in each well of a 12-well cell culture plate. The cells were fixed with paraformaldehyde for 20 minutes and then washed with PBS. Following membrane permeabilization, BSA was added, and the mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 minutes. The blocking solution was then removed, and the prepared antibody was sequentially applied, followed by incubation at 4 °C overnight in a humidified chamber. The primary antibody was subsequently aspirated, and the slides were washed with PBS. 488-labelled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:200) and Cy3-labelled goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000) were added and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 50 minutes. After removal of the secondary antibody, the slides were washed three times with PBS for 5 minutes each. DAPI staining solution was applied, and the samples were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 10 minutes. An anti-fluorescence quenching reagent was used to block excess fluorescence. A fluorescence microscope (LSM 980, Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) was subsequently used to observe the slides, with three random fields of view selected from each well.

Statistical analysis

All the data are expressed as the means ± standard deviations. Statistical analysis was conducted via SPSS 19.0 software (IBM, IL, United States). Statistical significance was determined through nonparametric tests and one-way ANOVA. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of the “cold–heat” properties of OS model mice and the effect of cap on these properties

Mice were evaluated for their “cold–heat” characteristics by measuring water intake, food consumption, rectal temperature, cold–heat scores, and SDH activity across the following groups (Supplementary Materials Table S1–S5): CON, MOD, CAP high/medium/low-dose (CAP-H, CAP-M, CAP-L), and model combined with CAP high/medium/low-dose (MOD+CAP-H, MOD+CAP-M, MOD+CAP-L) [45, 46]. As shown in Fig. 1A and 1B, compared with the CON group, the CAP-H, CAP-M, and CAP-L groups exhibited “heat-inducing” characteristics, whereas the MOD group displayed “cold syndrome” features. However, following the CAP intervention, the “cold syndrome” characteristics of the model group gradually normalized. Specifically, the mice showed a transformation from white to pink claw color, lip color, and tongue color, an improvement from loose stools to normal feces, and a restoration of activity levels to normal. Metabolomic analysis

A comprehensive metabolomic analysis of tumor tissues from both groups was conducted to further elucidate the mechanism through which CAP regulates the “cold–heat” balance in OS model mice. To investigate the systemic metabolic alterations induced by CAP, we selected the medium dose (20 mg/kg) for metabolomics analysis. This decision was based on a comprehensive evaluation of efficacy and safety. Although the high dose of CAP demonstrated slightly greater effects in modulating cold–heat phenotypes and inhibiting tumor growth in vivo, the difference compared with the medium dose was not markedly significant. Moreover, our previous studies [26, 27] confirmed that the 20 mg/kg dose achieved substantial anti-OS activity with minimal toxicity. Therefore, to ensure data reliability while avoiding potential confounding factors associated with higher doses, we chose the medium dose as the optimal concentration for multiomics investigation.

We evaluated changes in the metabolite profiles of tumors from the different groups via PCA, partial least squares discrimination analysis (PLS-DA) and orthogonal projections to latent structures discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA). The PCA score plots demonstrated that samples from all groups fell within the hotelling’s T² (95%) ellipse (Fig. 3A and B). The PLS-DA score plots showed that intergroup samples fell within the 95% confidence interval, indicating their minimal variation (Fig. 3C and E). Molecular docking and SPR validation of CAP interactions with key targets

To investigate the interaction between CAP and its protein targets, molecular docking was conducted via MOE 2019 software. The binding results indicated that CAP can bind to the binding domains of the target proteins AKT1, Caspase 3, EGFR, NF-κB, and STAT3, with molecular docking energies ranging from −5.3730 to −6.1378 kcal/mol. It is widely accepted that docking energy values below −4.25 kcal/mol suggest a certain level of binding activity, values below −5.0 kcal/mol indicate suitable binding activity, and values below −7.0 kcal/mol signify strong binding activity [53]. The docking results showed that CAP exhibited suitable binding activity with the protein targets, and CAP–EGFR (−6.1378 kcal/mol) and CAP–NF-κB (−6.0954 kcal/mol) presented the lowest docking binding energies.

Molecular dynamics simulations and SPR assays were conducted to further validate the molecular docking predictions. Discussion

The effect of CAP on OS has been studied from the perspectives of TCM and modern molecular biology. Understanding the attributes of “cold” and “heat” in TCM is essential for comprehending disease progression and treatment [54]. In this study, mice with OS exhibited typical “cold” symptoms, including reduced water intake, decreased body temperature, and diminished SDH activity (as shown in Fig. 1). These metabolic changes reflect the energy stagnation often associated with “cold” conditions in TCM pathology. Conversely, CAP alleviated these symptoms and reduced tumor size and weight in OS model mice (as shown in Fig. 2). This shift demonstrates CAP’s ability to correct metabolic disorders linked to OS and underscores its “heating” effect, which is consistent with its established classification as a “heat” agent in TCM [31]. This study is the first to systematically evaluate the impact of CAP on the cold–heat balance of OS model mice and to explore its mechanisms through multi-omics approaches. Our findings revealed significant reductions in tumor volume and weight, as well as alterations in body weight and cold–heat features, in CAP-treated mice, highlighting the role of CAP as an effective therapeutic agent for OS. It not only influences tumorigenesis but also restores the internal temperature equilibrium disrupted by the disease. This research provides a new perspective on the translational value of TCM concepts alongside modern pharmacological approaches in cancer treatment, offering insights into the therapeutic potential of CAP in managing OS.

From a translational perspective, CAP shows promising potential for clinical application in OS therapy, supported by existing evidence regarding its feasibility and favorable safety profile. CAP has a long-established history of dietary and topical use [55, 56], exhibiting excellent tolerability and minimal systemic toxicity in humans. Our prior preclinical studies have consistently demonstrated that a moderate dose (20 mg/kg) of CAP effectively suppresses OS growth while maintaining an acceptable safety margin [26, 27]. These findings are consistent with the clinical experiences of CAP in other therapeutic contexts, such as pain management [57] and anti-inflammatory treatment [58]. Furthermore, given its multi-target mechanisms, including modulation of key cancer-related pathways such as AKT1, STAT3, NF-κB, EGFR, and Caspase-3, CAP represents an attractive candidate for adjunctive or combinatorial therapy alongside conventional chemotherapy or targeted agents. To further advance the clinical translation of these promising findings, future studies will focus on linking capsaicin-mediated modulation of the “cold–heat” balance in OS with the established concepts of “cold” and “hot” tumors in modern oncology. Additionally, we aim to conduct comprehensive investigations into the pharmacokinetics, safety, and clinical efficacy of CAP, exploring its therapeutic potential for osteosarcoma in clinical settings.

Our metabolomics, transcriptomics, and network pharmacology analyses provide a comprehensive understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of CAP on OS. The metabolomic analysis revealed significant differences in the metabolic profiles between the CAP-treated and model groups, revealing 216 DAMs, many of which are involved in pathways associated with tumor progression and immune regulation (as shown in Fig. 3). Notably, purine metabolism and phenylalanine metabolism exhibited significant alterations, both of which have previously been linked to the “cold-heat” balance [19]. These findings suggest that CAP may restore the “cold-heat” balance through its effects on key metabolic pathways, ultimately influencing tumor cell proliferation and survival. The transcriptomic analysis identified an additional 5,548 genes that were differentially expressed between the groups, highlighting the extensive regulatory impact of CAP at the genetic level (as shown in Fig. 5). The integration of network pharmacology revealed 159 common CAP and OS-related targets (as shown in Fig. 4). By combining these targets with those from the DEGs and DAMs, we identified five core targets—AKT1, STAT3, NF-κB, EGFR, and Caspase-3—that play critical roles in CAP-mediated OS inhibition (as shown in Fig. 7). These core targets are involved in essential pathways such as cell cycle regulation, apoptosis, and inflammation, all of which are important processes in cancer progression.

The AKT pathway is one of the signaling pathways implicated in human cancer [59]. All three AKT isoforms are upregulated in human cancer tissues and promote tumor proliferation at varying levels as oncogenes [60, 61]. AKT1 can influence the cell cycle progression and apoptosis of tumor cells [62]. Caspase-3, a widely expressed member of a conserved protein family, is recognized primarily for its proteolytic activation in response to specific exogenous or endogenous inducers of this type of cell death [63]. Cytotoxic drugs, radiotherapy, or immunotherapy can induce tumor cell death by activating Caspase-3 [64]. The increased expression of Cleaved Caspase-3 suggests that CAP has the potential to modulate apoptosis, thereby enhancing its antitumor effects. STAT proteins are transcription factors activated by cytokines, growth factors, and other peptide ligands [65]. Notably, STAT-3 is closely associated with tumor progression and influences tumor behavior by regulating proliferation, cell cycle progression, apoptosis, angiogenesis, and immune evasion [66, 67]. Additionally, the constitutive activation of EGFR and NF-κB, which are the core targets of this study, can synergistically promote carcinogenesis [68]. In our previous study, we demonstrated that AKT1 and Caspase-3 play important roles in the effects of CAP intervention on OS [26, 27]. This study further revealed that these effects may be closely related to the cold‒heat balance in OS model mice. We identified DAMs, DEGs, and key targets through a multiomics approach, with molecular docking studies revealing that CAP can bind to these proteins with high affinity, inhibiting their carcinogenic activity. Western blot, immunofluorescence staining, and immunohistochemistry analyses showed that CAP reduced the phosphorylation of AKT1, STAT3, and NF-κB, and the levels of EGFR, while increasing cleaved Caspase-3. The findings of this study support the anticancer effects of CAP documented in the literature and suggest that CAP may modulate the “cold–heat” balance in OS mice through multiple targets and pathways, thereby affecting the progression of OS.

Although OS is classified as a cold-type disease in TCM due to systemic manifestations such as reduced body temperature, decreased activity levels, pale tongue appearance, and diminished SDH activity, this classification does not necessarily imply uniformly suppressed metabolic activity within the tumor itself. Tumor cells frequently activate compensatory energy-producing pathways, including fatty acid synthesis and β-oxidation, to sustain their high proliferative demands [69, 70], even under conditions characteristic of an overall cold state. Our results demonstrate that CAP exhibits distinct heat-type properties at the systemic level, effectively alleviating cold-related symptoms observed in OS model mice. However, within the tumor microenvironment, CAP exerts a targeted inhibitory effect on lipid metabolism. Specifically, CAP significantly downregulates the expression of FASN and CPT1B, two critical enzymes involved in fatty acid synthesis and fatty acid β-oxidation, respectively, thereby impairing lipid-derived ATP production in tumor cells. Thus, CAP simultaneously functions as a systemic heat-promoting agent while locally suppressing lipid metabolic pathways, ultimately contributing to its antitumor activity.

In modern tumor immunology, tumors are often classified as “cold” or “hot” based on their immune microenvironment [71]. “Cold tumors” are characterized by low levels of T cell infiltration, immune desert or exclusion phenotypes, low expression of inflammatory cytokines, and resistance to immunotherapy [72]. In contrast, “hot tumors” exhibit abundant tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes and active interferon-γ signaling; in addition, they generally show better responses to immune checkpoint blockade [73]. This classification bears a striking resemblance to the TCM concept of “cold–heat imbalance.” In TCM, “cold” syndromes are associated with deficiency of yang-qi, stagnation of circulation, hypofunction of metabolism, and impaired defense mechanisms—concepts that parallel the immunologically silent nature of cold tumors. Conversely, “heat” syndromes in TCM are linked to hyperactivity of yang-qi, exuberant circulation, inflammatory responses, and overactive metabolic states, aligning with the characteristics of hot tumors [74]. This conceptual overlap offers a novel perspective: the “coldness” of a tumor in TCM may reflect a suppressed metabolic and immune status, which could be corrected by “heat” agents to restore dynamic balance.

This study integrates the TCM concepts of ‘cold and heat’ with modern molecular biology to elucidate the multifaceted effects of CAP on OS. By employing a combination of metabolomics, transcriptomics, network pharmacology, and both in vitro and in vivo experiments, we established a comprehensive framework that connects the ‘cold–heat’ balancing effects of CAP to its antitumor mechanisms. Additionally, we identified potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets through multi-omics techniques, which may serve as a foundation for precision medicine approaches in future OS treatments. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that CAP alone does not fully encompass the holistic and individualized nature of TCM therapeutics, which typically involve complex herbal formulations with multiple synergistic ingredients. Instead, in this study, CAP serves as a representative single-agent model, providing an initial step toward understanding how the biological foundations of TCM’s “cold–heat” concept may intersect with modern tumor biology. In future studies, we aim to explore the integration of CAP with classical TCM prescriptions and individualized syndrome differentiation further. By combining these findings with comparative analyses of multi-herb formulations, we hope to more comprehensively validate and elucidate the clinical and mechanistic significance of TCM’s holistic approach in cancer therapy.

Additionally, our study has certain limitations. Although we have demonstrated the effectiveness of CAP in regulating critical signaling pathways in OS, the precise molecular mechanisms by which CAP influences the “cold–heat” equilibrium require further investigation. Future studies should explore upstream regulators of the identified core targets and their interactions with other metabolic and signaling pathways. Furthermore, although our in vivo model offers valuable insights, clinical trials will be necessary to confirm the therapeutic feasibility, safety, and efficacy of CAP in patients with OS. Finally, our investigation focused primarily on the independent antitumor effects of CAP; subsequent studies should also examine its potential synergistic benefits when combined with conventional chemotherapy or targeted therapies. Expanding research in these directions could facilitate the incorporation of CAP into comprehensive, integrative treatment strategies for OS.

Conclusion

This study reveals the role of CAP in regulating the “cold–heat” balance in an OS mouse model, showing that CAP effectively alleviates the “cold” symptoms associated with OS and inhibits tumor growth. These findings provide new insights into CAP as a potential therapeutic agent, highlighting the importance of integrating TCM with modern pharmacology. Future research should focus on the clinical applications of CAP and its synergistic effects with conventional therapies.
