Distinct Movement-Related Cortical Control Following Anterior Cruciate Ligament Injury: A Scoping Review
Authors: Robbe Capelleman, Emile Cloet, Dries Pieters, Jos Vanrenterghem, Evi Wezenbeek, Erik Witvrouw, Tim Lehmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDistinct brain activity in anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) patients has been reported, yet comprehensive evidence on neural differences during movement and the potential impact on motor performance remains limited.ObjectivesThis review aimed to synthesize evidence on cortical activity during motor tasks in ACL patients compared to healthy controls and explored the potential impact on motor performance.MethodsA scoping review was conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute methodological guidance for scoping reviews and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses extension for scoping reviews. Eligible literature was searched in PubMed, Embase, SPORTDiscus, and Web of Science from inception to December 31, 2025, and included primary research assessing brain activity during voluntary motor tasks in ACL patients compared to controls.ResultsOut of 233 identified records, 25 were included, with 15 utilizing electroencephalography, 8 functional magnetic resonance imaging, and 2 functional near-infrared spectroscopy assessing a total of 416 ACL patients on study level. 21 studies reported significant between-group differences in brain activity, demonstrating increased activity in frontal and visual processing regions but inconsistent findings for motor and parietal cortices. Additionally, 14 records described behavioral data alongside neuroimaging results, of which 10 found no significant differences and 4 reported impaired motor performance.ConclusionDistinct patterns of brain activity were demonstrated in ACL patients compared to healthy controls, predominantly depicting increased recruitment of neural resources within the cognitive and visual network. Despite some evidence reporting associated impaired motor performance, the relationship between cortical alterations and motor performance remains hypothetical. Future research should incorporate paradigms including visual and cognitive dual-tasks to explore the relationship between brain activity and behavioral outcomes.

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) ruptures remain one of the most impactful traumatic knee injuries in sports, with an increasing occurrence over the past decades [1, 2]. Despite surgical ACL reconstruction (ACLR) and 9 to 12 months of criteria-based rehabilitation, only 50% of patients successfully return to their preinjury level of sport participation, up to 20% experience a new ACL injury, and they remain at a fourfold elevated risk of developing post-traumatic osteoarthritis [3–5]. These poor clinical results have consistently been attributed to persistent alterations in movement quality observed for months to years after return to sports, with current treatment approaching these biomechanical impairments from a musculoskeletal perspective [6–11]. However, emerging evidence on central nervous system (CNS) differences suggests that ACL injuries should be more comprehensively conceptualized as a neuromusculoskeletal condition [12].

The shift toward a neuromusculoskeletal perspective originates from the function of the ACL in maintaining functional knee stability. Beyond its role as a mechanical stabilizer, the mechanoreceptors located within the ACL transduce proprioceptive information toward the CNS to support dynamic joint stability [13, 14]. At the cortical level, voluntary motor control emerges from coordinated activity across a distributed network of brain regions, since the descending motor commands from the primary motor cortex are modulated by input from adjacent regions [15, 16]. Briefly outlined, the prefrontal cortex contributes to goal-directed and cognitive control of movement, whereas the premotor and supplementary motor areas are involved in motor planning and sequencing [17, 18]. Additionally, the anterior parietal cortex processes somatosensory information, while the posterior parietal cortex integrates these inputs with spatial and visual information relayed from occipital regions [19, 20]. Although anatomical reconstruction restores mechanical stability, the mechanoreceptors of the native ACL are not replaced, resulting in disrupted proprioceptive input from the ACL to the CNS [14, 21]. Consequently, these alterations in afferent signaling could lead to widespread changes in the regional activity across cognitive, motor, and sensory areas, as well as adaptations in connectivity within the sensorimotor network in an attempt to preserve motor control [14, 21].

An initial review by Neto et al. [22] revealed that ACL-injured athletes exhibit dissimilar cortical functioning across several brain regions, characterized by increased activity in the prefrontal, motor, and occipital cortices, as well as decreased activity in the primary sensory cortex, and reduced corticospinal excitability of the motor cortex compared with control subjects. In summary, these brain activity patterns may reflect an increased reliance on cognitive-attentional functions and visual feedback, alongside altered sensorimotor integration [22, 23]. Nevertheless, a substantial volume of neuroimaging studies included in this initial review examined resting-state brain activity, sensory-evoked potentials, or transcranial magnetic stimulation of the motor cortex [22]. Despite providing valuable insights into neurophysiological functioning, these investigations do not directly indicate whether, to what extent, or in which regions movement-related cortical activity differs between ACL-injured and healthy athletes. Consequently, inferences regarding the impact of cortical activity on motor performance are largely theoretical and do not provide direct evidence of a relationship with altered movement quality following ACL injury.

To address these shortcomings, both hard- and software have continuously been innovated to facilitate mobile neuroimaging [23–25]. Recent advances in electroencephalography (EEG) research -including the transition to high-density EEG systems and sophisticated preprocessing algorithms such as the BeMoBIL-pipeline illustrate the fast-paced evolutions within this field [23, 25]. Although these advances may improve the insight into movement-related brain activity and its association with motor performance in more ecologically valid environments, substantial variability in experimental designs, data acquisition, processing, and analysis approaches introduces considerable heterogeneity [26, 27]. Given that such methodological variability may influence neuroimaging outcomes and the derived interpretations, a synthesis of evidence specifically focused on movement-related cortical activity and its association with motor performance is warranted to inform future research and clinical practice guidelines [26]. Therefore, this review aims to provide a synthesis of how cortical brain activity differs between ACL patients and healthy controls while performing voluntary motor tasks and whether these neural differences actually impact motor performance. Considering the substantial heterogeneity in this field of research, a scoping review was deemed the most suitable design for answering the research questions.

Methods

The proposed research was a priori registered on the OSF registries platform (DOI: 10.17605/OSF.IO/7ZBXW), conducted in accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute updated methodological guidance for scoping reviews, and reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension for scoping reviews [28, 29].

Eligibility Criteria

Literature eligible for inclusion in this review consisted of primary research articles, including (non-/pseudo-) randomized controlled trials, cohort, case–control, or cross-sectional studies. Other publication types (i.e., letters, commentaries, or conference abstracts) and study designs (i.e., case reports, case series, reviews, and meta-analysis) were excluded. Study populations had to consist of both an ACL-injured group and a control group, with no prerequisites regarding age, gender, knee function, physical activity, treatment (i.e., conservative or ACLR), graft type, absence of concomitant articular lesions, or time since injury or reconstruction. Studies that recruited ACL patients with associated ligament injuries were not eligible given the potential influence on the outcome measures of interest. Furthermore, experimental procedures required EEG, functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) or functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) neuroimaging of brain activity during voluntary motor tasks, including isometric contractions, isolated knee movements or functional tasks. Consequently, studies that exclusively investigated resting-state brain activity, evoked-brain activity (e.g., somatosensory-evoked potentials), or corticospinal excitability were excluded. Moreover, assessments of cortical activity during evoked or passive movements were excluded. The absence of behavioral outcomes did not constitute an exclusion criterion to avoid missing relevant references solely focusing on alterations in brain dynamics during movement without evaluating its functional impact.

Study Selection

A systematic, non-filtered literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, SPORTDiscus, and Web of Science electronic databases from inception to December 31, 2025, using the following keywords: ACL injury or ACL rupture; EEG, fNIRS, or fMRI; and neuroplasticity or brain activity. Keywords related to motor tasks and behavioral outcomes were deliberately omitted to prevent missing appropriate records that focused on mobile neuroimaging but did not assess motor performance. The complete search strategies can be found in Appendix 1.

Following record identification, duplicates were removed and subsequently eligibility screening was performed using the Rayyan web-based platform (Rayyan Systems, Inc., Cambridge; USA) [30]. Both the title and abstract screening and the full-text screening were conducted blinded by the two main reviewers (EC and RC), applying the predefined eligibility criteria in a predetermined order to increase consistency. A final consensus meeting was held at the end of each stage to resolve remaining conflicts. Data Charting

The data charting process was standardized using a Microsoft Word table (v2508, Build 16.0.19127.20082; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, USA). For this purpose, the main reviewers initially compiled a list of items relevant to the research question independently, after which mutual comparison led to consensus on the data to be extracted. Data extraction of the entire literature pool was first conducted autonomously by the main reviewers. Upon completion, the filled charting form was collaboratively examined by the main reviewers for each source separately to resolve disagreements, ensure accuracy and consistency, and summarize the reported data for those necessary to interpret the key results. Lastly, the included evidence was clustered according to neuroimaging technique (i.e., EEG, fMRI, or fNIRS), outcome measures of brain activity, and implemented motor task, respectively, to provide an overview of commonalities and discrepancies in the current literature relating to the research questions. Results

Study Selection and Design

A total of 233 records were identified through database searching. After duplicate removal (n = 124) and screening based on title and abstract (n = 72), 37 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Twelve references were excluded during the eligibility assessment due to an inappropriate study design (n = 6), population (n = 3), experimental task (n = 2), or outcome measure (n = 1). Ultimately, a total of 25 studies published from 2008 to 2025 met the eligibility criteria and were selected for qualitative synthesis. The systematic search and screening results are depicted in Fig. 1.

All included references were classified as level of evidence 3b. All studies employed a case–control design with cross-sectional assessments, except for Busch et al. [33] and Nyffenegger et al. [34], who additionally conducted longitudinal follow-ups, ranging from one to three and one to six months post-ACLR, respectively. The sample sizes of the included studies were relatively small, ranging from nine to 35 ACL patients with a mean of 16.6 [37, 56].

Patient Characteristics

Since sample independence across the included studies could not be ensured, the subsequent results represent study-level patients characteristics. The total ACL-injured cohort across all references consisted of 416 young adults between 20 and 29 years with a mean age of 24.04 years. The vast majority of studies included both males and females, with the exception of three that exclusively assessed males [35, 41, 47], resulting in a male predominance of 59% across the total sample. The studies by Kapreli et al. [47] and Miao et al. [35] were the only ones that investigated ACL-deficient (ACLD) males (n = 33), whereas the remainder evaluated ACLR patients. Reconstructive surgery was most commonly performed with a hamstrings tendon (n = 144; 36.7%), bone–patellar tendon–bone (n = 86; 21.9%), or quadriceps tendon (n = 52; 13.3%) graft. However, a minority of the reconstructions were conducted with peroneus longus tendon autografts (n = 17; 4.3%), artificial grafts (n = 16; 4.1%) or allografts (n = 7; 1.8%). Four studies, with a total of 70 ACLR patients (17.9%), did not report the surgical technique [41, 44, 52, 55].

The timing of measurement varied considerably across all studies. Kapreli et al. [47] evaluated ACLD patients more than two years post-injury, while Miao et al. [35] did not report time since injury. For the ACLR research, time from surgery ranged from six weeks to over five years post-operatively, with multiple included references having substantial within-study variability assessing participants at both early and late postoperative stages. Based on the average time since surgery, six studies assessed participants within the first six months [33, 34, 45, 46, 54, 55], eight between six months and 1.5 years [32, 36–40, 42, 56], and the remaining nine more than two years post-surgery [41, 43, 44, 48–53].

Physical activity levels were reported in 16 studies, most commonly using the Tegner Activity Scale (n = 14), with post-injury scores ranging from 4.34 to 7.9 (mean: 6.74) [32, 36–40, 42, 48–53, 56]. Other instruments included the Marx Activity Rating Scale (n = 3; scores: 11–14) [45, 48, 51], and the Baecke Questionnaire (n = 1; 7.8) [47]. Knee function was assessed in 19 studies using various patient-reported outcome measures. The International Knee Documentation Committee questionnaire was most frequently used (n = 11; scores: 54.5–88.0; mean: 83.0) [36, 38–40, 42, 46, 48–52], followed by the Lysholm Score (n = 5; 72.5–89; mean: 81.2) [32, 35, 41, 51, 55], the total Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (n = 5; 52.4–90.4; mean: 79.48) [33, 34, 45, 46, 49], its pain subscale (91.7) [52], and the Knee Outcome Survey Activities of Daily Living Scale (69.4) [47]. Although three of the included references reported neither knee function or level of physical activity, the available data from the remaining studies indicate that both physical activity and self-reported knee function remain suboptimal, irrespective of the timing post-injury [43, 44, 54].

Study Characteristics

Across the 25 included studies, 15 utilized EEG [32–46], eight fMRI [47–54], and two fNIRS [55, 56] to capture cortical activity. Although brain activity was assessed during motor task execution in all included studies, motor performance metrics were not reported in 11 of the 25 studies. Among all the fMRI and fNIRS studies, the repositioning error reported in the study by Strong et al. [51] was the only documented motor performance metric alongside neural measures. In contrast, across the EEG studies, only those by Miao et al. [35] and Riehm et al. [40] did not report behavioral outcomes.

Motor Tasks and Behavioral Outcomes

Considerable variability in motor task complexity was observed across the included studies, including analytic tasks (n = 16), postural balance paradigms (n = 4), and dynamic movements (n = 5).

Eight fMRI studies employed an analytic movement paradigm conducted in a controlled supine position. Of these, six evaluated cortical activity during repetitive knee flexion–extension tasks with either the injured leg [47–49], non-injured leg [53] or bilaterally [52, 54], all without reporting motor performance metrics. The remaining two fMRI studies incorporated multi-joint movements using heel slides [50, 51], with Strong et al. [51] utilizing a knee repositioning paradigm. In the latter, no significant between-group differences were observed in repositioning error. An isolated seated knee extension exercise was employed in eight studies, evaluating either force reproduction (n = 4), joint repositioning (n = 3), or knee extension within a Go/NoGo paradigm (n = 1). Among the force reproduction studies, three evaluated force steadiness and/or reproduction error [37–40]. Of these, the study by Sherman et al. [39] was the only one demonstrating an increased reproduction error between ACLR patients and controls, without a difference in force steadiness. During knee joint repositioning [32–34], only one study illustrated increased repositioning errors in the ACLR group [32]. Finally, no significant difference in reaction time but more errant decision-making was retrieved in ACL patients when incorporating seated knee extensions in a Go/NoGo paradigm [42].

Postural balance during single-leg stance was assessed in four papers [36, 43, 44, 46]. While the overall stability index and sway area indicated comparable balance control [43, 44, 46], findings regarding sway velocity were inconsistent. Specifically, the increased sway velocity demonstrated in the ACLR group by Sherman et al. [36] was not supported by the research of Lehmann et al. [46].

Five additional studies assessed a range of functional and dynamic movements, including walking, running, stair ambulation, single-leg squats, jumping, and short-distance kicking [55]. Three did not report a motor performance metric [55]. Among those that did, neither Giesche et al. [41] nor Piskin et al. [45] identified significant differences in countermovement jump performance or kicking accuracy, respectively. However, during soccer kicking, ACL participants demonstrated greater variability in foot external rotation compared with controls [45].

Brain Analysis Techniques

Three EEG studies conducted source-level analysis [36, 38, 45], while the remaining 12 performed channel-level analysis. Power spectral density (PSD) was the most frequently utilized EEG analysis, employed in eight studies [32–37, 39, 41], followed by corticomuscular coherence (CMC, CMcRQA) [39, 40] and event-related desynchronization/synchronization (ERD/ERS) [43, 44], each conducted in two papers, respectively. Furthermore, several measures were only used once, including motor-related cortical potentials (MRCP) [41], lateralized readiness potentials (LRP) [42], multiscale entropy (MSE) [45], event-related spectral perturbation (ERSP) [45], and weighted phase lag index (wPLI) [46]. All eight fMRI studies analyzed the blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal changes. The two fNIRS studies adopted comparable approaches, with Liu et al. [56] reporting block-averaged changes in oxyhemoglobin (HbO) to assess brain activation, while Cao et al. [55] computed general linear model-derived β-values from the HbO changes as primary measure of cortical activation. Fundamentals of neuroimaging techniques and associated outcome measures

Power spectral density (PSD)

[58]

Metric obtained by decomposing EEG signals from the time domain into the frequency domain, quantifying the contribution of each frequency band to the overall energy of the EEG signal

Increased power within a specific frequency band reflects enhanced synchronization of neuronal activity, except in the alpha frequency band, which exhibits an inverse relationship with cortical excitability. Increased alpha power is thus associated with functional inhibition of cortical areas

Corticomuscular coherence (CMC)

[39, 59]

Functional connectivity metric that quantifies the linear frequency synchronization between electrocortical signals of the motor cortex and electromyographic signals of peripheral muscles, most frequently in the beta and gamma frequency band

Increased CMC reflects enhanced corticomotor coupling between activated cortical areas and musculature of interest, with beta-range coherence associated with static force output and gamma-range coherence to dynamic force modulations

Corticomuscular cross-recurrence quantification analysis (CMcRQA)

[40]

Functional connectivity metric that quantifies non-linear, asynchronous coupling between electrocortical signals of the motor cortex and electromyographic signals of peripheral muscles

CMcRQA provides outcome measures that reflect regularity (determinism), duration (mean diagonal line length), complexity (entropy), and intermittency (laminarity and trapping time) of the corticomuscular interaction

Movement-related cortical potentials (MRCP)

[60]

Event-related metric derived from time-domain analysis of EEG signals, quantifying the negative potential in the fronto-central motor regions that precede voluntary movement relative to a pre-movement baseline period

Increased MRCP amplitudes reflects heightened activation of motor-related regions, particularly the SMA and M1, during general movement planning, preparation and initiation, whereas increased MRCP latency reflects slower

Lateralized readiness potentials (LRP)

[61]

Event-related metric derived from time-domain analysis and double subtraction of EEG signals from homologous electrodes over the right and left hemisphere, quantifying negative potentials in the contralateral motor cortex that precede voluntary movement relative to a pre-movement baseline period

Increased LRP latency onset reflects slower response selection (stimulus-locked), response activation (response-locked) or delay between these processes, whereas increased LRP amplitude reflects increased cortical activation in these phases, respectively

Event-related desynchronization/event-related synchronization (ERD/ERS)

[62]

Event-related metric that operates in the time–frequency domain, quantifying spectral power changes in a specific frequency band in response to an event, expressed relative to a baseline period preceding the event of interest

ERD reflects a decrease in power resulting from desynchronized neural activity, whereas ERS reflects an increase in power originating from synchronized neural activity. The functional interpretation of ERS/ERD depends on both the frequency band and region

Event-related spectral perturbations (ERSP)

[63]

Event-related metric that operates in the time–frequency domain, quantifying temporal spectral power changes across a broad frequency range in response to an event

ERSP provides a representation of power fluctuations over time in each multiple frequency bands, with power decreases reflecting neural desynchronization and power increases neural synchronization

Multi-scale entropy (MSE)

[64]

Complexity metric obtained by coarse-graining EEG signals in fine to coarse temporal scales, quantifying moment-to-moment variability of fast (high frequency) and slow (low frequency) neural dynamics

Increased entropy reflects an increased variability and reduced predictability of neural dynamics, whereas reduced entropy reflects reduced variability and increased predictability

Weighted phase lag index (wPLI)

[65]

Functional connectivity metric based on the weighted phase-synchronization between pairs of electrodes, quantifying phase-lag consistency of electrocortical signals across time in the different frequency bands

wPLI range from 0 to 1, with increasing values reflecting more phase-locking, illustrative for a stronger functional interaction between brain regions

Blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal change

[66]

BOLD signal changes originate from a local rise in the oxyhemoglobin-to-deoxyhemoglobin ratio, resulting from an influx of oxygenated blood that surpasses the metabolic demand for oxygen

Increased percentage of BOLD signal provides an indirect marker of increased neuronal activity

Oxyhemoglobin (HbO) signal change

[56]

HbO signal change originates from a local rise in the HbO concentration, resulting from an influx of oxygenated blood that surpasses the metabolic demand for oxygen

Increased HbO signal provides an indirect marker of increased neuronal activity

β-value

[55]

Estimated coefficient that quantifies direction and magnitude of HbO signal changes in response to a task compared to baseline by applying a general linear model to raw HbO data

Positive β-value thereby represent higher oxyhemoglobin concentrations, which is an indirect measure of increased neural activity

Brain Analysis Results

Significant differences in cortical processing and functional connectivity between individuals with an ACL injury and healthy controls were observed in the majority (n = 21) of studies, regardless of the neuroimaging technique, with only four out of 25 studies not reporting statistically significant between-group differences [34, 41, 43, 51]. Differences in Regional Brain Activity

Frontal cortex. ACL patients consistently demonstrated distinct activation in the frontal brain regions compared to controls. PSD analysis revealed greater frontal theta (4–8 Hz) power during various motor tasks, including joint position sense, force reproduction, single-leg stance, and dynamic movements such as walking, running, landing, and ball kicking [32, 35–37, 44, 45]. Consistent with these results, one fMRI study showed increased BOLD responses in several frontal areas, such as the frontal gyri, anterior cingulate cortex, inferior frontal pole, and paracingulate cortex [49, 50]. Notably, two studies identified frontal activation differences during non-injured limb movements, including increased frontal beta-2 power and heightened BOLD signal changes in the middle frontal gyrus and frontal pole [33, 53] in the ACLR group.

Motor cortex. In line with frontal PSD differences, two single-leg stance paradigms observed elevated theta (4–8 Hz) power in the contralateral motor cortex, as well as increased bilateral motor cortex and ipsilateral premotor alpha-2 (9–13 Hz) power [36, 44] in ACLR patients. Dissimilarities of theta and alpha power in motor areas were also found during more dynamic conditions such as walking, running, and landing, alongside increased delta (0–4 Hz) and beta (13–30 Hz) power [35]. fMRI results were inconsistent, two studies demonstrated heightened activation in the contralateral primary motor cortex [48, 52]; whereas Kapreli et al. [47] reported decreased contralateral premotor cortex activation in chronic ACL-deficient patients, and Lepley et al. [49] found no significant difference. Lower activation in the ipsilateral motor cortex was evidenced in two studies, both in the acute and chronic postoperative stage [48, 54]. Accordingly, a decreased HbO change in the ipsilateral motor region corresponding to the premotor and supplementary motor cortex in comparison to controls was demonstrated by Cao et al. [55].

Parietal cortex. Findings regarding parietal PSD changes were variable. While Baumeister et al. [32] demonstrated lower alpha-2 power, other studies illustrated an increased alpha-2 power over the contralateral [36], central, and ipsilateral parietal cortex [44]. Furthermore, Miao et al. [35] found general increased in parietal power across all frequency bands during dynamic movements compared to controls. However, multiple EEG studies were not able to demonstrate significant differences in parietal PSD [34, 38, 43]. Similar discrepancies were found in the fMRI studies. Greater BOLD signal change in the contralateral primary somatosensory cortex [52], contra- and ipsilateral secondary somatosensory cortex [47, 48], the superior parietal lobule [50], and the precuneus [52] were revealed. In contrast, Culiver et al. [54] reported decreased BOLD signal change within the ipsilateral primary somatosensory cortex and precuneus in patients six weeks post-ACLR. Similarly, Kapreli et al. [47] observed less activation of the contralateral primary somatosensory cortex and posterior parietal cortex in ACLD patients. The two fNIRS studies did report consistent results, both demonstrating lower activation in the primary somatosensory cortex [55, 56].

Parieto-occipital cortex. Only five out of 15 EEG studies examined electrocortical activity in parieto-occipital regions approximating visual processing regions. While four studies were unable to demonstrate significant differences in neither the occipital [32, 37, 44], nor parieto-occipital spectral power [38], Piskin et al. [45] reported greater posterior alpha-2 synchronization in the parieto-occipital cortex. In contrast, fMRI studies consistently reported increased BOLD signal change in several visuospatial processing regions, including the posterior inferior temporal gyrus in ACLD patients [47]. Similarly, reconstructed patients had more activation of the lingual gyrus [48, 50], lateral occipital cortex [50, 52], and intracalcarine cortex [50].

Differences in Neural Dynamics of Motor Planning

Two EEG studies investigated event-related neural dynamics in the motor cortex preceding movement to gain insights into differences in motor planning [41, 42]. Of these, only Sherman et al. [42] were able to reach statistical significance, demonstrating a smaller stimulus- and response-locked LRP area during a knee extension Go/NoGo paradigm. Although not specifically focusing on motor planning, fMRI papers reported inconsistent activation patterns in brain regions associated with movement preparation, consisting of heightened BOLD responses in the pre-supplementary and premotor cortex, as well as decreased activation in the supplementary cortex in the ACLR group [47–49].

Differences in Connectivity Measures

Four studies employed specific analysis techniques to explore either brain–muscle or intra-cortical connectivity following ACLR compared to healthy controls [39, 40, 45, 46]. The brain–muscle connectivity was first examined by Sherman et al. [39] using CMC analysis, illustrating less gamma-band connectivity between the brain and quadriceps muscles. A follow-up study by Riehm et al. [40] performed a CMcRQA analysis on the same dataset but did not find significant between-group differences. Nevertheless, this analysis revealed asymmetrical connectivity between the injured and non-injured leg, a pattern absent in healthy controls [40]. The study by Lehmann et al. [46] was the sole one to investigate functional brain connectivity using wPLI analysis, revealing increased connectivity between multiple intra- and inter-hemispheric regions of the sensorimotor network in the patient group. On the contrary, the study of Piskin et al. [45] demonstrated less coarse-scale MSE in the right posterior and midfrontal cortex as an indirect indicator of reduced capacity for long-range network integration.

Discussion

The objective of this scoping review was to synthesize current evidence regarding movement-related cortical activity in ACL patients and its potential impact on motor performance compared to healthy individuals. Despite the methodological heterogeneity, the findings of this review collectively point to variations within the sensorimotor network in ACL-injured athletes. Specifically, heightened engagement of frontal cognitive brain regions was consistently retrieved across neuroimaging techniques, while multiple fMRI studies additionally demonstrated increased activity in visual processing areas. Although these patterns suggest that ACL patients may rely on a distinct neural basis of motor control, findings regarding motor and sensory cortical activity were inconsistent. Furthermore, the available evidence remains largely inconclusive regarding the impact of these cortical activity patterns on motor performance.

The increased reliance on cognitive control of movement has been suggested based on several EEG studies that reported higher frontal theta power in ACL patients, indicative of higher activity in this area [32, 35–37, 44, 45]. Although the lower spatial resolution of EEG precludes precise source localization, the increased theta power could hypothetically be generated by the anterior cingulate cortex and is commonly interpreted as a neural marker of focused attention, error detection, and goal-directed decision-making [32, 35–37, 44, 45, 47, 51]. Complementary fMRI results support these electrocortical findings by equally demonstrating increased anterior cingulate cortex activation, alongside greater engagement of regions within the frontal gyri involved in error detection and complex motor sequencing during simple knee flexion–extension [49, 53, 68, 69]. Notably, these frontal areas are also connected to parietal areas via the superior longitudinal fasciculus as part of a broader frontoparietal network, which supports initiation and flexible adaptation of cognitive control during goal-directed behavior [70, 71]. Together, these results may reflect that ACL patients increasingly rely on conscious, attention-dependent strategies to perform motor tasks, a neural strategy that appears to be sufficient in most of the experimental paradigms employed in the current literature review [37, 44, 45]. Nevertheless, upregulated cognitive control does not consistently preserve motor output, as illustrated by greater joint position error in ACL participants despite elevated frontal theta power [32]. In fact, increased activity in frontal cognitive regions has consistently been identified as a neural marker of task complexity, which could suggest that ACL patients perceive an identical motor task as more challenging [42, 72]. Interestingly, limited evidence has indicated a weak association between greater activity in cortical regions involved in cognitive control of movement and poorer proprioceptive and balance performance in both ACL-injured and healthy participants [36, 51]. Although still hypothetical, these findings may partly explain the link between increased neurocognitive reliance and more pronounced motor performance deficits [36, 51].

Compliant with the state of heightened neurocognitive control, EEG and fMRI studies reported increased recruitment of brain regions associated with motor planning (e.g., prefrontal cortex, pre-supplementary motor area) in ACL-injured athletes during both isolated knee movement and functional tasks [36, 44, 45, 47–49, 73]. Similar to frontal cognitive areas, activity within these regions has been shown to increase in response to more complex task demands. Although not providing direct evidence, these patterns may be consistent with greater challenges for ACL patients to maintain motor performance [72]. However, the observed difficulties in isolating appropriate motor plans, increased cortical inhibition, and more errant performance during rapid decision-making tasks such as Go/NoGo paradigms may reflect task dependency of neural strategies [42]. Collectively, these findings may suggest overreliance on motor planning during low-demand contexts, whereas high-demand conditions could trigger disproportionately dominant inhibitory mechanisms [42].

Patterns indicative of greater visual reliance during movement were identified within the ACL cohort, as reflected by increased BOLD signals in regions associated with visual processing (e.g., lingual gyrus) and cross-modal integration during isolated knee flexion and extension [47, 48, 50, 52]. Theoretically, greater activity within these specific regions could reflect increased need for visual processing to compensate for altered proprioceptive feedback following ACL injury. However, since none of these fMRI studies reported behavioral outcomes, it remains unclear whether and how these neural changes impact motor performance. Moreover, current EEG research reports inconsistent results regarding electrocortical activity in the parieto-occipital cortex and the corresponding motor performance. Specifically, two studies illustrated preserved force and postural control without significant differences in parieto-occipital alpha-2 power [37, 38]. Equally, preserved postural control was demonstrated by Lehmann et al. [46], albeit ACL patients displayed stronger functional connectivity between parieto-occipital and motor-occipital areas, suggesting increased involvement of a functional visual-motor network to sustain motor performance. In contrast, Piskin et al. [45] revealed less parieto-occipital activation in ACL patients during football kicking. The authors attributed this finding to the demandingness of the task, proposing that compensatory heightened cognitive-attentional activation may suppress engagement of visual areas [45]. Despite not reporting significant differences in kicking performance, the observed reduction in cortical complexity in both the frontal and parieto-occipital region alongside the increased trial-to-trial complexity may be indicative of the association between neural alterations following ACL injury and diminished athletic performance. Considered across neuroimaging techniques, findings tentatively suggest increased reliance on visual processing during motor control, although this pattern appears to vary with task demands.

The parietal cortex is a heterogeneous structure involved in somatosensory processing, sensorimotor integration, spatial attention, and executive functions through a frontoparietal network [70, 71]. This multifaceted role could contribute to the diverse findings in the parietal cortex retrieved across different studies included in this review. Firstly, reduced activity of the parietal cortex was illustrated by each neuroimaging technique during both isolated knee movement (e.g., knee flexion–extension) and multi-joint movements (e.g., squats, walking, or stair walking) [35, 36, 54–56]. Theoretically, reduced parietal activity in athletes with ACL injury may have several possible explanations. One potential mechanism is reduced proprioceptive processing following the loss of native ACL mechanoreceptors, which is supported by the results of Valeriani et al. [74] who reported decreased somatosensory-evoked potentials in ACL patients. Secondly, both the deafferentiation and altered sensory signaling following ACL injury (e.g., pain, swelling) reduce the reliability of somatosensory information originating from the knee joint, which might contribute to cortical down-weighting of somatosensory information and, consequently, diminished parietal activity [75]. Regardless of the exact cause, less parietal activity during various motor tasks may reflect reduced contribution of somatosensory information to motor control. Hypothetically, this may help to explain why ACL patients appear to rely more on cognitive control and visual processing to preserve motor output, as outlined above. Nevertheless, the moderate association between reduced activity of the ipsilateral parietal cortex and increased sway velocity indicate a potential clinical relevance of these results [36]. In contrast to these findings, both EEG and fMRI studies reported increased activity in parietal regions—including the somatosensory cortices, precuneus, and superior parietal lobule—in ACL participants during isolated knee movements [32, 48, 50, 52]. While the secondary somatosensory cortex is responsible for processing proprioceptive inputs, the precuneus is involved in multisensory integration, and the superior parietal lobule has been linked to heightened spatial processing demands [76–78]. Greater engagement of these cortical areas may therefore reflect increased reliance on higher-order neural resources during isolated knee flexion and extension. In this regard, the coinciding increase in parietal activity and more errant proprioceptive performance in ACL patients may be consistent with the hypothesis that greater activation of higher-order brain regions might be insufficient to attain effective movement control and could relate to motor performance deficits, although this interpretation remains tentative given the limited supporting evidence.

Previous transcranial magnetic stimulation studies have demonstrated decreased excitability of the primary motor cortex in ACL patients [79–81]. Consequently, in comparison to healthy controls, ACL patients require a stronger stimulation of the motor neurons to maintain cortical drive [81]. In this context, the heightened BOLD signal change in the primary motor cortex during repeated knee flexion/extension movements could be interpreted as a compensatory mechanism that originates within the corticomotor system to cope with the reduced excitability [48, 52]. Nevertheless, the lower motor cortex activity in ACL patients during both isometric knee extensions and single-leg stance protocols may hint that compensatory activation is not consistently generated in the corticomotor system [36, 38]. Supporting this idea, Sherman et al. [39] illustrated reduced gamma-band synchronization between the motor cortex and the vastus medialis and lateralis, which was interpreted as a sign of impaired cortical drive and multisensory integration into the motor plan. Moreover, the concurrent reduction in force steadiness might reflect a diminished ability to dynamically modulate the quadriceps motor neuron pool due to the impaired brain–muscle connectivity [39]. Interestingly, the altered sensorimotor integration following ACL injury was also retrieved during the follow-up non-linear coherence analysis (i.e., CMcRQA) on the same dataset [40]. Although there were no significant between-group differences, the unique between-leg asymmetries revealed in ACL patients suggested that the motor cortex is less responsive to sensory input that originates from the affected knee joint [40]. Taken together, it appears that diminished cortical excitability and altered brain–muscle connectivity may cause a state of motor inefficiency. Recruitment of larger-scale cross-modal neural networks could serve as a strategy aimed at maintaining motor function, although it should be acknowledged that these interpretations are limited to the available literature.

Mechanisms Underlying Cortical Alterations

The distinct patterns of cortical activity observed in ACL-injured athletes could be explained by the fundamentals of sensorimotor integration during motor control [21, 82]. While moving, the actual sensory input is continuously being evaluated with regard to the predicted sensory feedback of a motor command (forward modeling) [82, 83]. The resulting sensory prediction error (i.e., the difference between the real-time sensory input and the predicted sensory input) is thereby used to adjust the ongoing movement and refine the forward model for future movements [82–84]. A key component within this sensorimotor feedback loop is sensory reweighting, which is the ability of the CNS to dynamically adjusts the relative contribution of visual, vestibular, and somatosensory inputs based on task complexity, environmental characteristics, and accuracy of the sensory input [75, 82, 85]. After ACL injury, both the deafferentiation and pathophysiological consequences (e.g., pain, swelling) may alter sensory input, which increases the somatosensory noise entering the CNS [21, 86, 87]. Schnittjer et al. [21] described that the ensuing conflict between sensory predictions and actual sensory input could contribute to a state of sensorimotor uncertainty. This state is characterized by diminished movement accuracy, driven by increased variability in both sensory inputs and motor outputs [21]. The principal findings of this review appear to be consistent with the concept of sensory reweighting, as movement-related cortical activity patterns in ACL patients suggest greater cross-modal processing with increased reliance on neurocognitive control and visual input. Interestingly, the distinct cortical basis of motor control observed in patients with ligamentous injuries are not confined to the knee, as patients with chronic ankle instability exhibit comparable patterns of brain activity [88]. Specifically, increased frontal theta power and heightened BOLD signal changes in the superior and medial frontal gyri have been reported in individuals with chronic ankle instability, alongside increased reliance on visual information and recruitment of cortical regions surrounding the primary motor cortex [85, 88, 89]. Taken together, the comparable neuroimaging results following ACL injury and chronic ankle instability indicate that peripheral ligament injuries may transcend the musculoskeletal level, as proprioceptive alterations associated with ligament damage might contribute to a different cortical basis of motor behavior [86].

Implications for Athletic Performance

The second objective of this scoping review was to explore the impact of movement-related cortical activity on motor performance. As previously outlined, the distinct cortical patterns in ACL patients are associated with both preserved and reduced motor performance during analytical, postural, and functional motor tasks [32, 33, 36, 37, 39, 46]. Together, these results suggest that although dissimilar cortical patterns may influence motor output, their actual impact on motor performance appears to be task-dependent and insufficient to consistently preserve motor performance across different tasks.

Task complexity may be a decisive factor in the success of motor performance preservation, with both the type of attentional focus and the presence of additional cognitive, visual, or motor demands as potential key determinants [90, 91]. Traditionally, a distinction is made between internal focus of attention, during which a person pays attention to the movement itself, and external focus of attention, characterized by a focus on the effects of a movement [91]. Among the 14 studies that reported behavioral data, eight of the ten studies with an internal focus of attention did not report significant between-group differences [33, 34, 37, 38, 43, 44, 46, 51]. In contrast, three out of four studies with an external focus of attention reported worse motor or cognitive performance in ACL patients compared to uninjured participants [36, 41, 42, 45]. This decline in motor and/or cognitive performance when both systems are challenged simultaneously is referred to as cognitive–motor interference [90]. Although speculative, it could be presumed that this cognitive–motor interference might be related to the different cortical basis of motor control in ACL-injured athletes. Specifically, the increased neurocognitive reliance to maintain basic motor outputs may reduce the neuronal reserves available to handle additional cognitive, visual, motor, or combined loads [90, 92]. During simple motor tasks, the absence of supplementary loads may allow neurocognitive resources to be engaged in task execution, which could serve as a strategy to cope with sensorimotor uncertainty through increased cognitive control of movement [37, 38]. However, additional visuospatial or visual cognitive demands accompanying motor tasks with an external focus of attention also require competing executive functions (i.e., attention control, working memory, decision-making) and multisensory processing to achieve task objectives [42, 45]. Eventually, the overlapping recruitment of cortical regions (e.g., anterior cingulate cortex, lingual gyrus) for both task execution and managing sensorimotor uncertainty may exceed neurocognitive capacity [36, 42, 45]. Albeit the limited neuroimaging evidence precludes definitive conclusions, the decreased movement consistency during soccer kicking and decreased cognitive performance during a Go/NoGo knee extension task are consistent with the hypothesis that overlapping demands on neurocognitive capacity may increase the possibility of performance decrements [42, 45].

Despite providing valuable insights, current neuroimaging research does not represent the complexity, dynamism, and high loads inherent to sport-specific movements. Consequently, the potential impact of increased cognitive and visual cortical involvement on athletic performance and its contribution to reinjury risk cannot be inferred from the present review. Nevertheless, recent biomechanical research on side-step cutting and drop vertical jumps showed that ACL patients adopt more aberrant movement patterns under increased cognitive demands, such as lower approach speeds or jump height, reduced knee joint loading, stiffer landings [7, 90, 93–96]. Notably, these aberrant biomechanics were also accompanied by worse cognitive performance in both working memory and decision-making functioning [90, 95, 96]. Although not supported by the included neuroimaging data, these biomechanical results appear to corroborate our hypothesis that increased neurocognitive reliance in ACL-injured athletes could exacerbate the susceptibility for cognitive–motor interference. Ultimately, given the continuous need to allocate visual and attentional resources between movement execution and environmental cues in sports, it is plausible that the increased susceptibility for cognitive–motor interference may contribute to reduced performance, impaired movement quality, and an increased risk of reinjury, but this remains to be clearly established in future research [96, 97].

Implications for Rehabilitation

Current rehabilitation following ACL injury might insufficiently address the distinct cortical activation patterns of ACL patients. Therefore, rehabilitation might benefit from integrating training strategies targeting the different cortical processes into the standard rehabilitation protocol [11]. A potentially relevant approach is the use of exercises that require an external focus of attention. On a neural level, external focus of attention is thought to promote the utilization of unconscious and automated processes, which may increase the availability of cognitive resources during high-demand situations [91, 98, 99]. Secondly, it could be suggested to represent the complex sporting environment by concurrently imposing physical and visual cognitive challenges during rehabilitation exercises. According to the ‘control-to-chaos’ framework proposed by Chaput et al. [100], rehabilitation exercises could therefore progress following a continuum from controlled settings to high levels of chaos, starting from the early postoperative stages. Progression of exercises can be achieved by integrating visual cognitive dual-tasks (e.g., Stroop tasks) or by applying contextual interference techniques (e.g., mechanical perturbations or stroboscopic glasses) [100]. Given the plausible susceptibility for cognitive–motor interference following ACL injury, this approach might offer additional advantages beyond those achieved through standard rehabilitation. First, patients are engaged in exercises aimed at restoring the motor and cognitive abilities during more game-like situations, thereby increasing ecological validity and potentially enhancing readiness for return to sport [101]. Secondly, incorporating visual, cognitive, or combined visual cognitive challenges might reduce the reliance on neurocognitive resources. Although hypothetical, by limiting the reliance on compensatory cognitive and visual capacity during exercises, such interventions could promote the development of cortical activation patterns that are less dependent on cross-modal processing. In this context, a systematic review demonstrated enhanced motor and cognitive performance in healthy athletes after engaging in a dual-task training program challenging both systems [102]. However, it should be noted that current research has not yet investigated whether these visual cognitive rehabilitation interventions provide clinical benefits for either cortical activation patterns or motor performance in ACLR patients. Consequently, future research should prioritize establishing the potential added value of the above-listed suggestions to further optimize clinical practice.

Limitations and Recommendations for Future Research

General limitations across the existing neuroimaging studies include substantial methodological heterogeneity and low ecological validity. Although methodological consistency was observed across the fMRI studies, providing valuable insights into structure-specific brain activation during movement, the non-functional supine position, absence of behavioral data acquisition, and low temporal resolution limit the generalizability to functional settings [103, 104]. In this regard, recent advances in mobile EEG data acquisition and processing have improved spatial specificity while preserving high temporal resolution, positioning EEG as a promising tool for obtaining spatiotemporal neural insights in more ecologically valid settings [23–25]. Nevertheless, a critical evaluation of the included EEG studies illustrates considerable heterogeneity, alongside an underrepresentation of source-based analyses exploiting the spatiotemporal potential of mobile EEG. Consequently, interpretations about the neural origins of the reported electrocortical activity should be approached with caution. Moreover, given that the majority of experimental designs employ highly controlled, isolated movements without an external focus of attention, there remains a stark contrast with the dynamic and unpredictable sporting environments to which ACL athletes aim to return [85]. In addition, the absence of combined motor and cognitive performance assessments in a substantial portion of the included studies restricts insights into the impact of cortical changes on performance. Lastly, small primary sample sizes, considerable heterogeneity in patient characteristics, and potential sample overlap across the included literature warrant caution, as these factors limit statistical power, may complicate cross-study comparability, and reduce the generalizability of the conclusions. As a result of these shortcomings, the current understanding of movement-related cortical activity and the potential impact on motor and cognitive performance in ACL patients remains largely hypothetical. Therefore, future research should prioritize methodological standardization in data collection, processing, and analysis within more realistic environments to strengthen the ecological validity. Furthermore, considering the inherent combination of motor and cognitive demands in all sporting contexts, along with the apparent increase in cognitive–motor interference following ACL injury, this review puts forward some additional recommendations for future research. First, we encourage the use of motor tasks that incorporate an external focus of attention by introducing visual cognitive or visuospatial load within dual-task paradigms. Second, we recommend that these approaches be accompanied by systematic capturing and analysis of both motor and cognitive performance. Other limitations that warrant attention in future research is the lack of longitudinal study designs capturing the evolution of neural adaptations and the absence of evidence regarding the effects of dual-task based interventions on cortical activation patterns. To address these gaps, upcoming studies should incorporate longitudinal approaches and evaluate the impact of targeted intervention programs on both neural and functional outcomes. By doing so, a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between neuroplastic changes and clinical outcomes could be obtained, providing a potential foundation for optimized rehabilitation guidelines aimed at enhancing athletic performance and reducing the risk of recurrent injuries.

Conclusion

This scoping review explored differences in movement-related cortical activity in ACL patients compared to healthy controls and its potential impact on motor performance. Based on current neuroimaging evidence, ACL patients appear to rely on a distinct neural basis of motor control, characterized by an increased recruitment of higher-order cortical regions, enhanced cross-modal activation, and lower brain–muscle connectivity. Heightened recruitment of frontal regions associated with cognitive control was most consistently reported, alongside greater engagement of visual processing areas. Furthermore, cortical activity patterns within motor and somatosensory cortices were markedly heterogeneous. Although the different neural basis of motor control appears effective to preserve basic motor performance, limited evidence suggests that the dissimilar patterns of cortical activity seem to be insufficient to consistently sustain motor output. Moreover, it was hypothesized that these distinct patterns of cortical activity may even be associated with task-specific performance deficits, highlighting the potential relevance for athletic performance and rehabilitation. Nevertheless, the sparse reporting of motor performance metrics, conflicting evidence, and low ecological validity of the experimental paradigms precludes definitive conclusions on the potential influence on athletic performance. Future research should aim to elucidate robust differences in cortical activity between ACL patients and healthy controls while establishing a more clearly defined association between these patterns and motor performance. Additionally, studies may also incorporate visual cognitive dual-task-based exercises within a functional setting to explore their potential clinical implications. Lastly, longitudinal designs are warranted to examine how these neural and functional adaptations evolve over time.
