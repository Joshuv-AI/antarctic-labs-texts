Deficiency of Ugcg in LSECs alleviates high-fat diet-induced MASLD
Authors: Rui Han, Yanyan Li, Yuhui Liu, Manman Li, Liangliang Ren, Weiran Lin, Ying Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Hepatic glycosphingolipid biosynthesis is implicated in insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). While UDP-glucose ceramide glucosyltransferase (UGCG) serves as the rate-limiting enzyme in glycosphingolipid synthesis, its cell-specific roles in MASLD pathogenesis remain undefined. Our study investigates the mechanistic contribution of LSEC-expressed UGCG to high-fat diet (HFD)-induced insulin resistance and MASLD progression.Methods:Primary cell sorting was used to analyze LSEC-specific enrichment of UGCG in wild-type mice under normal chow (NC) diet and high-fat diet (HFD) conditions. LSEC-specific Ugcg knockout mice (Ugcg Cdh5cre+) and littermate controls (Ugcg Cdh5cre−) were subjected to 12 weeks of HFD or NC feeding. Hepatic steatosis was assessed via histopathology; glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity were evaluated functionally. Endothelial fenestration architecture was quantified using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Ganglioside GM3 levels were measured via LC–MS. LSEC–hepatocyte cocultures were employed to investigate VLDL secretion and lipid metabolism-related gene/protein expression, with nitric oxide (NO) and endothelin-1 (ET-1) signaling verified by ELISA.Results: Ugcg deficiency in LSECs attenuated hepatic steatosis, improved glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity, and restored endothelial fenestration architecture without compromising vascular integrity. It also reduced LSEC defenestration and CD31+ capillarization, promoting endothelial homeostasis. Mechanistically, insulin receptor-β (IRβ) was predominantly localized in LSECs; HFD-induced IRβ downregulation was reversed by UGCG inhibition (Genz-123346), correlating with reduced GM3 levels. GM3 was shown to suppress IRβ in a dose-dependent manner. In cocultures, Ugcg deficiency increased VLDL secretion and elevated the expression of hepatocyte lipid metabolism–related genes and proteins through NO/ET-1 signaling pathways.Conclusions:Our findings establish UGCG as a master regulator of LSEC metabolic functions through GM3–IRβ axis modulation. LSEC-targeted UGCG inhibition mitigates hepatic insulin resistance via NO/ET-1–mediated hepatocyte metabolic reprogramming, providing a novel therapeutic paradigm for MASLD.

INTRODUCTION

The term “metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD)” was introduced in 2023 as an update to both the traditional definition of NAFLD and metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease criteria introduced in 2020. 1 It emphasizes the fundamental role of metabolic dysfunction in liver fat accumulation, specifically highlighting key factors of metabolic syndrome such as obesity, insulin resistance, and diabetes in driving the onset and progression of the disease. 2 The prevalence of MASLD among adults exceeds one-third, and is even higher in certain high-risk groups. 3 Despite advances in understanding MASLD, the mechanisms behind it remain unclear, and the lack of specific biomarkers and effective treatments poses challenges to diagnosis and management. This study is designed to elucidate the key molecular pathways of MASLD and identify novel therapeutic targets, laying the groundwork for better diagnostic and treatment strategies.

Recent research on liver diseases underscores the significance of liver non-parenchymal cells, encompassing LSECs, KCs and HSCs. 4 LSECs, constituting ~15%–20% of the total hepatic cell population, are located between hepatocytes (HCs) and blood flow from the hepatic artery and portal vein. 5 LSEC possess unique fenestrae and lack a basement membrane, facilitating the entry of lipoproteins, chylomicron remnants, and other macromolecules from the bloodstream into the Disse space, where they can be utilized by HCs. 6 Capillarization of LSEC decreases the exchange of substances between sinusoids and blood, impedes the flow of HC-derived VLDL, and retains cholesterol and triglycerides within HCs. Moreover, it can also diminish the transfer of chylomicrons to HCs, promote de novo lipogenesis and lead to a compensatory upregulation of cholesterol and triglyceride synthesis in HCs. 7 The dysfunction of LSECs is primarily characterized by the inhibition of endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) activation and a reduction in the synthesis of nitric oxide (NO). 8 In conclusion, LSECs serve not only as the primary defense against liver damage but also play a critical role in MASLD.

Insulin resistance, serving as the central pathological mechanism underlying type 2 diabetes mellitus and metabolic syndrome, exhibits a strong correlation with obesity, highlighting the critical significance of early intervention and metabolic homeostasis maintenance. 9 Insulin signaling pathway is an important mechanism regulating glucose, lipid, and protein metabolism. The fundamental mechanisms involve insulin binding to membrane-bound receptors, subsequent activation of insulin receptor substrates, and the initiation of downstream metabolic processes including glucose uptake, glycogen synthesis, lipid storage, and protein synthesis through the PI3K–AKT signaling pathway. 10 However, the occurrence of insulin resistance impairs this signaling pathway, reducing the sensitivity of cells to insulin and manifesting as disorders in glucose and lipid metabolism. Hepatic insulin resistance enhances the liver’s uptake of free fatty acid (FFA) and the synthesis of triglycerides, while suppressing lipid oxidation and VLDL secretion, ultimately leading to hepatic lipid accumulation. 11 Furthermore, obesity-induced chronic low-grade inflammation exacerbates hepatic insulin resistance and accelerates fatty liver progression through the sustained release of pro-inflammatory mediators and adipokines. 12 Conversely, MASLD aggravates systemic insulin resistance and metabolic disorders by releasing pro-inflammatory factors and liver-derived secretions, creating a vicious cycle. In addition to the traditional insulin-sensitive cells 13 , LSECs have been shown to express insulin receptors that bind insulin with high affinity and play a role in the hepatic clearance of insulin. 14 Nevertheless, the regulatory role of insulin signaling in LSECs in hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism under MASLD conditions remains to be elucidated. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms of insulin resistance and its relationship with MASLD is essential for developing early intervention strategies, improving therapeutic outcomes and preventing associated metabolic complications.

Glycosphingolipids (GSLs) are a class of sphingolipid molecules ubiquitously distributed in animal cell membranes. GSLs play pivotal roles in diverse cellular functions, signal transduction, and metabolic regulation. 15 Dysregulated GSL metabolism has been strongly associated with the pathogenesis of various diseases, including cancer,16,17 metabolic disorders,18,19 and neurodegenerative diseases.20,21 Accumulating evidence demonstrates that hepatic glycogen and GSLs accumulation in patients with MASLD is significantly associated with insulin resistance and dysregulated lipid metabolism.22,23 UDP-glucose ceramide glucosyltransferase (UGCG), a pivotal enzyme in GSLs biosynthesis, functions as a glycosyltransferase that catalyzes the initial step of glucosylceramide synthesis. Two UGCG inhibitors, AMP-DNM 24 and Genz-123346 25 , have demonstrated dose-dependent efficacy in animal models of insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes, significantly attenuating hepatic lipid accumulation and inflammatory responses. However, Jennemann et al 26 demonstrated that HC-specific Ugcg knockout mice failed to ameliorate obesity-induced hepatic insulin resistance and steatosis. Our previous experiments revealed that genes involved in the GSLs synthesis pathway, which is led by Ugcg, were significantly enriched in LSECs. 4 These findings suggested that HCs may not be the main effector cell of GSLs-induced insulin resistance. In conclusion, investigating UGCG function in LSECs may offer novel therapeutic strategies and intervention targets for managing obesity-induced insulin resistance and MASLD.

METHODS

Animal models and diets

Ugcg LSEC-specific knockout mice (Ugcg Cdh5cre+) were generated by flanked lox (Ugcg Cdh5cre−) mice (floxed exon 3, constructed by Model Animal Research Center of Nanjing University) with Cdh5 cyclization recombination (Cdh5cre) mice, which obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (in C57BL/6 genetic background, have Cre recombinase driven by the promoter of the Cdh5 gene) (Supplemental Tables S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C76). All experiment mice were specific pathogen-free, clean grade, reared at a temperature of 20–22 °C, and humidity of 50%–55%. The environment featured a 12-hour day–night cycle. Eight-week-old mice were fed with normal chow (NC) or high-fat diet (HFD) (Research Diets Inc.) for 12 weeks. Based on calorie content, HFD consists of 60% fat, 20% carbohydrate, and 20% protein. All the experimental model mice used were male and 8 weeks old. Body weight was recorded weekly, and fasting blood glucose and fasting insulin were measured every 4 weeks. Record the dates of the experimental mice at various time points. After 12 weeks, the mice were fasted for ~12 hours without water restriction. Then the mice were weighed, and subsequent sampling and testing of relevant indicators were completed. All procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the guidelines approved by the Committee on Animal Research and Ethics of the National Center for Protein Sciences (Beijing), China (Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee ID-20220725-54MO). All experiments were conducted by researchers certified to oversee animal experiments. This study did not involve human participants or human samples. Therefore, there were no aspects of the research that required assessment for compliance with the Declarations of Helsinki or Istanbul.

Cell culture and in vitro studies

The LSEC line SK-Hep1 was purchased from Crisprbio Biotech (CE15916). SK-Hep1 were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were cultured at a temperature of 37 °C and under an atmosphere containing 5% CO2. In an in vitro MASLD model in SK-Hep1 cells, cells were incubated with FFA (palmitate:oleate, 1:2) at a final concentration of 1 mM for 12 hours. Genz-123346 (MCE, HY-12744) was administered at 20 µM for 18 hours to inhibit UGCG. S961 (Selleck, S6922) was applied at 50 nM for 18 hours to antagonize the insulin receptor. GM3 (MCE, HY-114456) was exogenously administered at 20 µM.

Fractionation and culture of primary cells

Primary cells were isolated from the livers of Ugcg Cdh5cre− and Ugcg Cdh5cre+. Two-step liver perfusion digestion in situ was performed with collagenase IV and DNase I according to the previously described protocol with some modifications. 4 Briefly, HCs were isolated by modified in situ perfusion followed by natural sedimentation after differential centrifugation to enrich the HCs. Liver non-parenchymal cells were excluded from HCs. Non-parenchymal cells from the OptiPrep density gradient (Axis Shield) working solution with densities of 8.4%, 11.2%, 17%, and 24% were carefully collected. The collected cells were primarily a mixture of KCs and LSECs. In addition, PE-conjugated F4/80 and APC-conjugated CD146 were used to label KCs and LSECs. The corresponding isotype antibodies were also used as negative controls to measure the nonspecific binding of the specific antibodies. Cell viability was determined by DAPI (BD Biosciences) stained. Forward and side scatter gates were set to exclude debris and to include all viable cells. All data were acquired with a BD FACS Aria II (BD Biosciences) and analyzed with Diva 6.1.2 (BD Biosciences). Primary LSECs were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 20% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco). Primary HCs need to be added to 2 mM GlutaMAX (Gibco), 5 μg/mL insulin (Beyotime) and 10 mM dexamethasone (Gibco). All sorted primary cells were cultured at 37°C and 5% CO2 in collagen-coated plates (LV BioTech). The state of cell culture growth was recorded in real time using inverted phase contrast microscopy. Sorted primary cells were utilized for western blot and real-time quantitative PCR.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism 8 software (GraphPad Software, Inc.). The statistical evaluation of 2-group comparisons was executed using the Student t test. For multiple group analyses involving 3 or more groups, the choice between ANOVA followed by the Tukey post hoc test and the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by the Dunn post hoc test was determined based on the normality of the data distribution.

Additional experimental methods are included in the Supplemental Materials, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C76. The lists of primers and antibodies used are provided in Supplemental Tables S2 and S3, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C76.

RESULTS

UGCG is mainly expressed in LSECs and upregulated in HFD-induced MASLD

The HFD-induced MASLD model has been widely employed to investigate the histopathological features and pathogenic mechanisms of human MASLD. To assess UGCG expression alterations in MASLD progression, we initially analyzed its expression profile in hepatic total protein extracts from MASLD mice. Over the course of 12 weeks of HFD feeding, hepatic UGCG protein expression levels remained stable without significant elevation (Figure 1A). Consistent with these findings, hepatic UGCG protein expression remained unchanged throughout various time points during HFD induction (Supplemental Figure S1A, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C76). Although HCs represent the predominant hepatic cell population, emerging evidence suggests that UGCG alterations in MASLD may not be primarily mediated by HCs. To investigate potential UGCG alterations in non-parenchymal cells, we examined its cellular localization and expression patterns. Integrated proteomic and transcriptomic analyses demonstrated predominant UGCG expression in LSECs (Supplemental Figure S1B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C76). 4 Western blot further validated the results (Supplemental Figure S1C, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C76). After isolating primary HCs, LSECs, and KCs from wild-type (WT) mice fed an NC and HFD, we observed that the expression of UGCG in HFD-fed mice was specifically elevated in LSECs, but not in HCs and KCs (Figure 1B). Consistent results were revealed in immunofluorescence (IF) (Figures 1C, D) and further confirmed using an in vitro MASLD model 27 in SK-Hep1 cells (Supplemental Figure S1D, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C76).

Ugcg deficiency in LSECs did not affect the liver vascular network

To elucidate the role of Ugcg in LSECs, we established Ugcg LSEC-specific knockout mice (Ugcg Cdh5cre+) using the Cre-loxP–mediated recombination (Figure 2A). Subsequently, we conducted primary cell isolation and sorting from hepatic tissues of WT mice, isolating the main non-parenchymal cells, including LSECs and KCs (Supplemental Figures S2A, B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C76). Real-time quantitative PCR (Figure 2B), western blot (Figure 2C), and IF (Figure 2D) were analyzed demonstrating the specific knockout effect of Ugcg in LSECs. We subsequently assessed endothelial marker expression profiles and characterized vascular network distribution patterns (Figures 2E, F). Collectively, our findings demonstrate that Ugcg exhibits predominant expression in LSECs, while its cell-specific knockout in mice showed no significant effects on hepatic vascular network integrity.

Ugcg deficiency LSECs of mice slows the progression of MASLD

Next, we established HFD-fed MASLD models in Ugcg Cdh5cre+ and Ugcg Cdh5cre− mice. After feeding HFD for 12 weeks, we observed a significant increase in body weight and liver weight in Ugcg Cdh5cre− mice, while those with Ugcg Cdh5cre+ exhibited a slight weight gain (Figures 3A–C). The same results were obtained for the ratio of liver weight to body weight (Figure 3D). HFD-fed Ugcg Cdh5cre− mice exhibited consistently higher fasting glucose levels than Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice for 12 weeks (Figure 3E). In addition, we documented representative hepatic morphological features through gross examination (Figure 3F), complemented by histological analyses using hematoxylin–eosin and Oil Red O staining (Figure 3G). Compared with controls, mice lacking Ugcg in LSECs demonstrated alleviated hepatic steatosis and a lower NAFLD activity score (Figure 3H). To ascertain that the weight loss observed in Ugcg knockout mice was not caused by the intrinsic effects of UGCG deficiency on the overall condition of the animals, we measured the daily average food intake and body weight gain in both groups (Figures 3I, J). Others include voluntary wheel running activity, oxygen consumption (VO2), carbon dioxide production (VCO2), and respiratory exchange ratio (RER) (Supplemental Figures S3A–G, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C76). However, under the HFD feeding, Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice exhibited higher body weight gain along with increased energy expenditure, suggesting that the metabolic improvements and reduced hepatic steatosis observed in these mice may be linked to elevated energy metabolism. The results indicated that the reduction in body weight is not caused by general toxicity or reduced activity. Furthermore, serum levels of ALT and AST, key hepatic function biomarkers, were significantly decreased in HFD-fed Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice (Figures 3K, L). Consistent with these findings, serum concentrations of triglycerides, total cholesterol, and VLDL were significantly reduced in HFD-fed Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice. (Figures 3M–O. Furthermore, the content of triglycerides in liver tissue was also significantly decreased Supplemental Figures S3H, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C76). Dysregulated lipid metabolism represents a crucial early-stage feature of MASLD pathogenesis. Subsequently, we examined the expression profiles of key proteins and mRNAs associated with lipid metabolic pathways. (Figure 3P, Supplemental Figure S3I, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C76). These results indicate that Ugcg deficiency in LSECs of mice slows the progression of MASLD.

Ugcg deficiency of LSECs attenuates the insulin resistance of MASLD mice induced by HFD

Insulin resistance, a fundamental pathological mechanism underlying MASLD development, significantly impairs hepatic glucose and lipid homeostasis. To investigate the mechanisms underlying LSEC-specific Ugcg deficiency-mediated attenuation of HFD-induced insulin resistance, we detected insulin resistance–related indices. After 12 weeks of sustained HFD feeding, Ugcg Cdh5cre− mice demonstrated significantly elevated fasting blood glucose levels, whereas Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice displayed only marginal increases. (Figure 4A). Ugcg Cdh5cre− mice maintained on HFD demonstrated persistently elevated fasting insulin levels and significantly higher Homeostatic Model Assessment for insulin Resistance scores compared with Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice (Figure 4B). Furthermore, Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice exhibited improved glucose homeostasis and enhanced insulin sensitivity relative to Ugcg Cdh5cre− mice, as demonstrated by glucose tolerance test and insulin tolerance test analyses (Figures 4C, D). To evaluate the role of Ugcg in LSECs in glucose and lipid homeostasis, we analyzed the expression levels of glucose metabolism-related proteins in the liver tissue of Ugcg Cdh5cre− and Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice (Figure 4E, Supplemental Figure S3I, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C76). These findings collectively demonstrate that LSEC-specific Ugcg deficiency ameliorates HFD-induced insulin resistance.

Ugcg deficiency in LSECs recovered fenestration and mitigated the capillarization of LSECs was associated with the activation of eNOS

The distinctive fenestrated architecture of LSECs plays a crucial role in regulating molecular exchange between the vascular compartment and hepatic parenchymal cells.28,29 The changes in porosity of LSECs’ fenestrae directly affect the homeostasis of liver metabolism. Therefore, we investigated whether LSEC-specific Ugcg deficiency influences fenestral structural alterations in LSECs. First, we employed scanning electron microscopy to quantitatively compare fenestral density in LSECs between HFD-fed Ugcg Cdh5cre− mice and Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice. Our results demonstrated a significant reduction in fenestra density in Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice compared with Ugcg Cdh5cre− mice (Figure 5A). IF staining for LYVE1 and laminin revealed attenuated LSECs capillarization and reduced basement membrane deposition associated with Ugcg deficiency (Figure 5B). Subsequently, we observed a significant decrease in the protein expression of LSECs' capillarization markers (Figure 5C). Fenestrae formation and maintenance are primarily regulated through VEGFR-mediated signaling and NO-dependent pathways. We further assessed eNOS activation status and quantified circulating NO levels in HFD-fed Ugcg Cdh5cre+ and Ugcg Cdh5cre− mice (Figures 5D, E). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that LSEC-specific Ugcg deficiency restores fenestrae architecture and attenuates capillarization through eNOS activation.

Ugcg deficiency in LSECs regulating the expression of IRβ is related to GM3

Insulin resistance represents a primary pathogenic factor in MASLD development, while MASLD progression can reciprocally exacerbate insulin resistance. Initially, we examined insulin receptor-β (IRβ) expression patterns across various hepatic cell populations in normal mice. Our analysis revealed predominant IRβ expression in LSECs (Figures 6A, B). Furthermore, after 12 weeks, HFD-fed mice exhibited lower expression levels of IRβ protein in LSECs compared with NC-fed mice (Figure 6C). Notably, LSEC-specific Ugcg deficiency reversed this downregulation (Figure 6D). Consistent with our hypothesis, Genz-123346 (a specific UGCG inhibitor) treatment effectively downregulated UGCG expression while upregulating IRβ protein levels in SK-Hep1 cells (Figure 6E). Previous investigations have demonstrated that endothelial insulin resistance is associated with LSECs dysfunction, where NO and endothelin-1 (ET-1) regulation becomes insulin signaling-dependent under insulin resistance conditions. 30 To further investigate the relationship between endothelial insulin resistance and the production of NO and ET-1, we conducted additional experiments using S961, a specific insulin receptor antagonist, to inhibit IRβ signaling in SK-Hep1 cells under FFA treatment and subsequently assessed eNOS activity and NO/ET-1 production of SK-Hep1 cells (Figures 6F, G). The results indicated that inhibition of insulin signaling disrupted endothelial signaling homeostasis. GSLs, the primary products of UGCG-mediated biosynthesis, serve as essential structural components of lipid rafts. GSLs composition alterations significantly influence insulin receptor localization and function. 31 Accumulating evidence demonstrates that GM3, a key ganglioside, plays a crucial role in modulating insulin receptor localization, membrane distribution, and functional activity within insulin signaling pathways.32–34 Hence, we examined GM3 metabolic profiles in LSECs from both control (Ctrl) and UGCG inhibitor-treated (Genz-123346) groups (Figure 6H). Subsequent analyses revealed that GM3 levels were also significantly reduced in the tissues of Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice (Figure 6I). Simultaneously, IF analysis demonstrated time-dependent IRβ downregulation in SK-Hep1 cells following GM3 stimulation, with significant reduction observed at 18 hours and pronounced decrease by 24 hours, suggesting a potential inverse correlation between IRβ expression and GM3 levels (Figure 6J). Collectively, these findings indicate that LSEC-specific Ugcg deficiency ameliorates hepatic steatosis and insulin resistance by upregulating IRβ expression in LSECs, a process mediated through GM3 metabolic regulation.

The deficiency of Ugcg in LSECs mitigated the steatosis of HCs

HCs constitute the primary functional units of hepatic lobules, and their bidirectional communication with LSECs significantly influences MASLD progression. Firstly, we isolated and cultured fresh primary HCs and LSECs in vitro (Supplemental Figure S4A, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C76). Results showed that primary HCs of Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice significantly decreased lipid droplet accumulation (Figure 7A). LSECs, specialized vascular endothelial cells within hepatic microvasculature, engage in complex cellular interactions with HCs, immune cells, and other vascular endothelial populations. LSEC-derived secretory factors modulate HC function through both direct and indirect mechanisms, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis and progression of insulin resistance. Among them, NO and ET-129,35 may be the potential secretion of LSECs that indirectly affect HC lipidosis. We detected the secretion levels of NO in conditioned medium (CM) of LSECs and ET-1 in liver tissue, respectively, from Ugcg Cdh5cre+ or control mice after feeding NC or HFD for 12 weeks. After continuously feeding HFD for 12 weeks, LSEC-specific Ugcg knockout increased the secretion of NO and decreased the secretion of ET-1 in LSECs compared with the control group (Figure 7B). Next, to examine whether LSECs with Ugcg deficiency secrete factors that act on HCs to alleviate MASLD, activated HCs were treated with CM from 2-day cultured, isolated LSECs from either Ugcg Cdh5cre+ or control mice, following 12 weeks of feeding with NC or HFD (see the procedure in Supplemental Figure S4B, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C76). We detected the major indicators of hepatic function and lipid metabolism in CM of HCs. We found that VLDL was greatly decreased in CM of Ugcg Cdh5cre+ MASLD mice HCs (Figure 7C, Supplemental Figure S4C, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C76). Then, we measured de novo lipogenesis and β-oxidation in HCs' lysates. FAS activity was significantly reduced in HFD-fed Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice compared with controls, while β-hydroxybutyrate levels remained unchanged (Figure 7D), indicating that the protective effect against liver steatosis is mainly due to suppressed de novo lipogenesis rather than enhanced β-oxidation. Consistently, the mRNA and protein expression of lipid synthesis and transport markers (including ACC, SREBP-1c, FAS, ACLY, FABP1) were significantly decreased in Ugcg Cdh5cre+ MASLD mice (Figures 7E, F). In summary, Ugcg deficiency in LSECs directly inhibited lipid accumulation and VLDL secretion in HCs, which slowed down the occurrence and development of MASLD. This phenomenon may be regulated by NO and ET-1 secretion of LSECs.

DISCUSSION

Higher prevalence, serious associated complications, and significant economic burden of MASLD worldwide urgently necessitate new diagnostic and treatment tools. 3 MASLD not only impacts liver health but is also strongly linked to cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and other conditions related to metabolic syndrome. 2 Management of MASLD currently relies primarily on lifestyle interventions, while specific drug therapies are still under investigation. 36 In-depth study of the pathological mechanism of MASLD and development of new prevention and treatment strategies are of great significance for improving the overall public health level and reducing the burden on the medical system. 37 The majority of MASLD pathogenesis studies concentrate on HCs.38,39 However, LSECs and HCs collaborate to ensure the normal functioning of the liver. Consistent with previous findings, 4 we discovered UGCG expression was predominantly present in non-parenchymal cells and highly elevated in LSECs after 12 weeks of HFD feeding MASLD mice. However, there was no change in the liver tissue. This result showed that LSECs are the main cell type for UGCG-induced hepatic insulin resistance and MASLD.

In this study, we constructed the LSEC-specific Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice by challenging them with an HFD diet, while not affecting vascular endothelial network markers, induced loss of fenestrae and capillarization in LSECs. Abnormal glucose and lipid metabolism, including insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and high blood glucose, are the main characteristics of MASLD, driving disease progression via hyperglycemia, inflammation, and oxidative stress.40,41 In our current study, the deletion of UGCG markedly inhibited hepatic steatosis, reduced gluconeogenesis and lipid synthesis/transport, and enhanced fatty acid oxidation, thereby alleviating glucose/lipid metabolic disorders and insulin resistance. Interestingly, we also found that LSEC-specific deletion of Ugcg significantly suppressed HFD-induced weight gain in mice. To explore the underlying mechanism, we evaluated energy metabolism and found that Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice exhibited higher oxygen consumption (VO2) and overall energy expenditure compared with Ugcg Cdh5cre− mice. This suggests that weight reduction may not only result from decreased fat accumulation, but also from elevated basal metabolic rate. Notably, this metabolic phenotype was not observed in mice under a normal diet, indicating that Ugcg deletion alone does not affect baseline metabolism but interacts with dietary stress to modulate energy homeostasis. Numerous studies have shown that diets high in lipids, carbohydrates, and gut microbiota products can induce LSECs abnormalities during early MASLD stages even before liver inflammation and fibrosis develop.42–44 By comparing Ugcg Cdh5cre− and Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice, UGCG was shown to maintain LSECs’ homeostasis by activating eNOS and elevating serum NO level.

GSLs are lipid molecules found in cell membranes that affect insulin resistance by altering the localization and signaling pathways of insulin receptors. Several studies have shown that the presence of gangliosides on cell membranes may affect insulin receptor distribution and function and related to insulin resistance and MASLD.23,45,46 GM3 as a ganglioside belong to the GSLs family and mainly involved in cell signaling and intercellular interactions. 34 A few studies showed that GM3 regulates insulin receptor activity by influencing its conformational state, membrane localization, and the lipid raft microdomains, all of which are essential for receptor clustering and activation.32,33 This interaction impacts insulin receptor signaling, potentially contributing to insulin resistance and disrupting glucose homeostasis by affecting downstream pathways and insulin sensitivity. Nonetheless, the majority of these studies have primarily concentrated on adipocytes and HCs. In this study, we found that in the HFD-induced MASLD mouse model, IRβ was mainly located in LSECs and the expression was regulated by UGCG-mediated GM3 synthesis. The insulin receptor in the insulin signaling pathway is the core and initiation; it is the “starting switch” of insulin signal transduction. By binding to insulin, insulin receptors initiate a series of intracellular signaling pathways that regulate a variety of metabolic processes. Its function directly impacts cellular response to insulin, thereby affecting the body’s metabolic balance. Our study provided evidence that the influence of instability of gangliosides on insulin signaling and improved understanding of the cellular integrity of the insulin receptor signaling pathway induced by ganglioside changes. Furthermore, we also isolated primary HCs from Ugcg Cdh5cre− and Ugcg Cdh5cre+ mice liver during 12 weeks of NC or HFD feeding. These HCs were treated with the corresponding primary cell CM. The results suggest that the absence of Ugcg in LSECs affects the metabolic homeostasis of HCs by affecting the secretion of NO and ET-1.

In conclusion, HFD-induced upregulation of UGCG in LSECs leads to capillarization, characterized by reduced NO levels and increased ET-1 secretion. This, in turn, leads to the accumulation of GM3 on the cell membranes, which facilitates the sequestration of the insulin receptor into lipid rafts, impairing signal transduction. These molecular alterations contribute to insulin resistance and metabolic dysregulation. Notably, Ugcg knockout reverses these effects by restoring endothelial function, eliminating GM3 deposition, and normalizing the insulin receptor membrane dynamics, thus mitigating the associated metabolic disturbances (Supplemental Figure S5, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C76. Our results combined GSLs with hepatic insulin resistance from the perspective of LSECs, providing more theoretical research basis for MASLD therapeutic targets.

However, we recognize that Cdh5-Cre–mediated Ugcg deletion occurs in endothelium beyond the liver, which may contribute to extrahepatic effects. Future work using emerging LSEC-specific Cre drivers will be essential to pinpoint the liver-restricted role of UGCG in metabolic regulation. 47 Furthermore, the specific mechanisms of insulin signaling in LSECs and their impact on the overall liver metabolism require further study and exploration. We acknowledge that definitively establishing GM3’s role in HC lipid accumulation will require targeted in vitro experiments; therefore, future studies will include GM3 supplementation and genetic modulation in primary HCs to directly assess its effects on lipid droplet formation and lipogenic gene expression.

In general, the relationship between LSECs and HCs in MASLD is complex, and their interaction is closely intertwined. Understanding the roles of these 2 types of cells in the development of MASLD will help to reveal the pathological mechanism of the disease and provide new ideas and targets for the prevention and treatment of MASLD. The expansion of diagnostic criteria for MASLD allows for more detailed classification of its subtypes, enabling more targeted treatments. Currently, no drugs have received FDA or EMA approval specifically for MASLD. Nevertheless, various marketed medications have demonstrated efficacy in treating MASLD in clinical trials. These include certain hypoglycemic drugs such as pioglitazone, GLP-1 receptor agonists, SGLT-2 inhibitors, and metformin, as well as other drugs like vitamin E and pentoxifylline.48,49 Future research focusing on the molecular mechanisms and signaling pathways of LSECs will help identify new targets for the treatment of MASLD. This is expected to contribute to the development of innovative therapeutic approaches and diagnostic tools, offering new strategies and directions for clinical treatment. 50
