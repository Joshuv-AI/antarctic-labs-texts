Conservative Safety Assessment of a Nitinol Microhook Coaptation Device on Intact Nerve
Authors: Jonathan Isaacs, Geetanjali S. Bendale, Ananya Gomatam, Hope T. Richard, Charles Reiter, Kush Savsani, Marc Mazur, Taylor Allen, Noah Clough
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsNerve Tape is a novel nerve coaptation device that eliminates the technical challenges of microsuturing. The device incorporates tissue‐engaging Nitinol microhooks within a flexible backing of decellularized porcine small intestine submucosa (SIS). Preclinical nerve injury models have demonstrated device safety and effective promotion of regeneration and recovery. Here, we further assess the impact of the device's components on nerve tissue and function, using a highly conservative model in which the device is applied to intact nerves.MethodsNerve Tape, sutures, or SIS were applied to rabbit tibial nerves in a manner simulating transection repairs but leaving the nerves intact. After three months, “repaired” nerves and target gastrocnemius muscles along with contralateral control nerves and muscles were evaluated for perineural scarring, inflammation, and function utilizing gross inspection, morphology measurements, electrophysiology, and histology.ResultsAnalysis of endpoint data, including muscle mass/girth, nerve compound action potential (NCAP) amplitude, conduction velocity, axon counts, and g‐ratio revealed no significant differences between experimental or control groups (distal axon count/g‐ratio: Nerve Tape 6546 ± 2300, 0.647 ± 0.04; Suture 7979 ± 666, 0.664 ± 0.033; SIS 7537 ± 2117, 0.689 ± 0.02; control 6158 ± 1958, 0.631 ± 0.02). Gross adhesion thickness at the treatment site was higher in the Suture group versus the Nerve Tape group. Granulomas and neovascularization were most pronounced in the Suture group. Macrophage/lymphocyte/plasma cell presence was associated with SIS material. No additional tissue response was observed in response to Nitinol components.DiscussionNerve Tape's SIS and Nitinol microhook design maintained the health of an intact nerve with sequelae comparable to sutures or SIS without microhooks.

Introduction

Nerve Tape is a novel microhook‐based repair tool for efficient and consistently precise coaptation of nerve ends. The device contains opposing arrays of Nitinol microhooks embedded in sheets of processed, decellularized porcine small intestine submucosa (SIS) extracellular matrix (Figure S1A). Application of the device has been previously described [1]. Briefly, each severed nerve end is positioned and secured to the microhooks so that nerve ends are aligned and in close apposition. The SIS backing is then wrapped around the coaptation, engaging more microhooks and entubulating the completed repair. Preclinical studies comparing the device to microsutures included cadaveric biomechanical testing, a cadaver usability study, and transected nerve repair models in rats and rabbits. This data demonstrated Nerve Tape's superior holding strength, markedly faster repair times, improved nerve end alignment, and equal or superior axonal regeneration and functional recovery [1, 2, 3].

Nerve Tape was engineered to provide reliable holding strength and prevent repair dehiscence with minimal impact to vital intraneural structures (i.e., fascicles). The SIS and Nitinol materials were each selected for their established track records of safety and bio tolerance around nerve tissue [4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. SIS is commonly used in devices for the repair of tissues including nerve, and Nitinol is typically used in devices such as vascular stents. Nerve Tape comes in a range of sizes designed for target nerve diameters ranging from approximately 1 mm‐8 mm, and microhook heights within each device size are also tailored for the target nerve (ranging from 360 to 550 μm). Unlike microsuture needles, which are sharpened to glide through tissue layers, microhooks are angled and shaped to “snag” superficial connective tissues and in practice do not necessarily enter to their full height.

In a transected rabbit tibial nerve repair model, histological analysis specifically assessed intraneural microhook positioning. Nerves repaired with suture frequently contained sutures within fascicles, but the same was not observed for Nerve Tape microhooks. Even on occasions where microhook tracks were observed near or possibly dimpling a fascicle, the outer perineurial sheath appeared intact, and immunohistologic imaging of neurofilaments confirmed robust axon populations adjacent to the tracks [2]. Lack of fascicle penetration may be due to both the tougher perineurium membrane surrounding fascicles and independent fascicular gliding within the inner epineurium, allowing fascicles to slide away when contacted by the fixed‐length microhooks [9]. Histomorphometry, electrophysiology, and muscle recovery revealed either a slight advantage or no statistical differences when comparing Nerve Tape repairs to connector‐assisted and microsuture‐only repairs [2]. This study model suggests negligible adverse impact of the device.

Here, we continue our assessment of Nerve Tape with an especially conservative model of safety, in which the device is applied to a non‐injured segment of nerve. While Nerve Tape is not designed or intended for use on intact nerves, this model allows for heightened sensitivity to any subtle effects on nerve tissue that might be masked in a nerve transection model by the sequelae of injury response and regeneration.

Methods

After obtaining Virginia Commonwealth University (VCU) institutional animal care and use committee (IACUC) approval, Nerve Tape, SIS, or microsutures alone were applied to intact rabbit tibial nerves, which were subsequently assessed for tissue response and function. Twenty‐four (24), 20‐ to 30‐week‐old, male and female albino New Zealand White rabbits ( Oryctolagus cuniculus ) weighing between 3.1 and 4 kg, were divided into three treatment groups. All procedures were performed under sterile conditions, and anesthesia was induced and maintained using isoflurane gas (2%–5%) continuously administered via endotracheal tube or nose cone throughout the procedure. The left lower extremity was shaved and prepped, and the sciatic nerve and its branches were exposed via a semi‐tendinosis biceps femoris splitting approach to allow tibial nerve isolation (same length of nerve exposed for all three groups). The tibial nerve received one of three experimental treatments, each designed to simulate device application in a transected nerve repair. For the Nerve Tape group (n = 8), the tibial nerve was lifted off the tissue bed, and a 13 mm Nerve Tape device sized for 2 mm diameter nerves was placed deep to the tibial nerve with the microhooks facing up. The intact tibial nerve was placed onto the opposing microhooks. Using techniques to simulate a nerve repair, slight downward pressure was applied, and the intact nerve was alternatively pulled proximally and distally to encourage and ensure microhook engagement. The short leaf of the Nerve Tape was then wrapped around the nerve trunk, and gentle pressure was again applied to encourage and ensure microhook engagement. Finally, the long leaf was circumferentially wrapped around the nerve trunk to entubulate the nerve and complete the construct (Figure S1B). For the Suture group, four 9–0 nylon microsutures (AROSurgical, Newport Beach, CA) were threaded through the epineurium using standard microsurgical techniques, positioned at 90° intervals around the circumference of the tibial nerve to simulate the microsuture placement of a direct nerve repair. For the SIS group, the intact tibial nerve was placed onto a 13 mm sheet of SIS (processed identically to the SIS that makes up the backing of Nerve Tape and sized to the same dimensions). The SIS was loosely wrapped around the intact nerve so that the edges overlapped and the nerve trunk was completely entubulated. A single stitch of 9–0 nylon (AROSurgical, Newport Beach, CA) was placed through the overlapping SIS edges at each end of the SIS construct (total of two microsutures) to secure the device without penetrating the nerve.

At the 12‐week endpoint non‐survival surgery, both the left (experimental) and right (control) limbs were prepped, and tibial nerves on each side isolated and tested. A five‐centimeter length of tibial nerve was exposed for assessment and scored on extent and quality of adhesions based on predetermined scales (Supplementary Table S1). Adhesion thickness was measured indirectly at the implant site using the diameter of the nerve plus surrounding scar as a surrogate for comparison between groups. The extent of adhesions was calculated as percentage of exposed nerve (5 cm) with adhesive connections. The distal tibial nerves were exposed at the ankle for electrophysiological testing of both experimental and control limbs. A compound nerve action potential was measured via a recording cuff electrode (Microprobes for Life Science, Gaithersburg, MD) placed proximal to the implant site, near the spine. A stimulating bipolar hook electrode (Natus Medical Inc., Middleton, WI) was placed on the tibial nerve near the ankle. The active ground electrode was placed in non‐active muscle tissue between the stimulating and recording electrodes. A headstage (A‐M Systems, Sequim, WA) was utilized to manage stimulation signals, and an in‐line amplifier (Model 1800, A‐M Systems, Sequim, WA) used to smooth output signals for recording. The amplifier included high‐pass and low‐pass filters to pass signals between 1 Hz and 5 kHz and an initial ×10k gain setting to optimize signal strength and signal‐to‐noise ratio. Single square pulses of 0.2 ms duration were applied at different intensities, and the resulting waveforms were recorded at a sampling rate of 40 k/s. The recording program (LabChart Pro 24/T, ADInstruments, Sydney, Australia) also allowed the application of a mains filter to reduce noise. The stimulus strength (up to 2 V) was increased until a maximum amplitude nerve action potential waveform was recorded. Each stimulus intensity was repeated 60 times at 1 Hz to obtain an average response. The distance between recording and stimulating electrodes was measured with a ruler, recorded, and maintained throughout the course of the experiment to accurately calculate conduction velocities.

The experimental tibial nerve at the level of the device or the contralateral tibial nerve at the analogous level in the control limb was harvested along with the gastrocnemius muscles from each limb. The muscles were measured at widest girth using digital calipers (Mitutoyo America Corp., Aurora, IL) and weighed. The nerve tissue was then prepared for histology. For Nerve Tape specimens, the outer layer of SIS material and the Nitinol hooks were peeled away before tissue processing to allow tissue sectioning and slide preparation. Nerve tissue proximal and distal to the coaptation site was excised and post‐fixed in Karnovsky's solution (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Hatfield, PA), dehydrated in alcohol, and embedded in resin. Thin, 1 μm transverse sections were obtained, mounted on charged slides, and stained with toluidine blue for axon morphometry analysis. High magnification (40×) brightfield images were sampled throughout the nerve section to obtain 6–8 images per sample (Nikon Eclipse E 600, Elements BR Image Acquisition, Nikon, Tokyo, Japan). Axon counts and g‐ratios were quantified using automated segmenting software (AxonDeepSeg, NeuroPoly, École Polytechnique, Université de Montréal). Separate nerve tissue blocks from the control nerve or proximal, distal, and midpoint of the implant site were post‐fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, then processed and embedded in paraffin. Thin (12 μm) sections were prepared with a microtome in sequential batches of 50–100 and mounted on glass slides. These sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) (Figure S2) and submitted in blinded fashion for analysis by a board‐certified pathologist using a pre‐determined scale (Table S2). The implant sites for each group were scored for multiple features of tissue reaction including chronic and acute inflammation, granuloma formation, giant cells, necrosis, and neovascularization. For each group, the highest score was taken in each sample and compared as an incidence within the entire population in that group. These scores were then compared between the three groups for distribution of the various scores ranging from 0 to 3. All histomorphometric analyses were performed blinded to the experimental group.

Statistical Analysis

Utilizing GraphPad Prism (GraphPad, Boston, MA) software, one‐way ANOVA tests were run for all adhesion, muscle morphology, nerve conduction, collagen deposition, and histomorphometry data. A Kruskal‐Wallis test was utilized when ANOVA assumptions (normality & variance homogeneity) were not valid. For the scoring of inflammatory reaction at implant site, highest scores from each group were compared using Dunn's test for incidence scores. For all tests, probability (p) values of less than 0.05 were reported as significant.

Results

On gross inspection, all Nerve Tape devices maintained complete nerve entubulation without unwrapping. Average adhesion thicknesses measured at the simulated repair sites were 3.9 ± 0.8 mm for Suture, 3 mm for SIS, and 2.75 ± 1 mm for the Nerve Tape groups. Adhesion thickness in the Suture group was significantly higher as compared to the Nerve Tape group (p = 0.01). The average extent of adhesion measured along the length of the exposed nerve was 31.2% ± 9.9% for Suture, 17.1% ± 13.8% for SIS, and 24.3% ± 23% for the Nerve Tape groups (Figure 1).

Adhesions around simulated nerve repairs. (A) gross images of example suture, SIS, and Nerve Tape “repairs” at 12‐week nonsurvival surgery. (B) Extent of adhesions and adhesion thickness (*p < 0.05) (error bars represent standard deviation) (Scale: 5 mm).

There were no statistically significant differences in muscle atrophy as evidenced by intra‐group comparison of average gross muscle mass or muscle girth (Figure 2). Effect on muscle mass was expressed as a ratio percentage of experimental to control, with the average being 100.5 ± 2% for Nerve Tape group, 97.1 ± 6% for Suture group, and 99 ± 8% for the SIS group. Muscle girth was 98.5 ± 4% for Nerve Tape group, 100.1 ± 6% for Suture group, and 101 ± 5% for the SIS group.

Mean normalized muscle mass and girth of gastrocnemius muscle at 12‐week nonsurvival surgery (error bars represent standard deviation).

There were no statistical differences in axon histomorphometry or nerve conduction parameters between experimental and control groups. Average conduction velocity and peak‐to‐peak amplitude for the Nerve Tape group were 124 ± 21 m/s and 37.4 ± 12.2 μV, for the Suture group were 133 ± 20 m/s and 35.9 ± 17 μV, for the SIS group were 115.5 ± 54 m/s and 38.1 ± 24 μV, and for the control nerves were 137.8 ± 13.5 m/s and 43.2 ± 25.2 μV (Figure 3).

Mean nerve conduction and amplitude recordings from Suture, SIS, Nerve Tape, and Control nerves at 12‐week nonsurvival surgery (error bars represent standard deviation).

Mean proximal axon count and average g‐ratio for the Nerve Tape group were 6336 ± 3519 and 0.638 ± 0.045, for the Suture group were 8795 ± 1690 and 0.638 ± 0.029, and for the SIS group were 7132 ± 1094 and 0.67 ± 0.032. Mean distal axon count and average g‐ratio for the Nerve Tape group were 6546 ± 2300 and 0.647 ± 0.04, for the Suture group were 7979 ± 666 and 0.664 ± 0.033, and for the SIS w/sutures group were 7537 ± 2117 and 0.689 ± 0.02. The mean axon count and average g‐ratio for the control group were 6158 ± 1958 and 0.631 ± 0.02 (Figure 4).

Axon counts and g‐ratio values proximal and distal to the virtual coaptation for Nerve Tape, SIS, Suture, and for control nerves (min‐to‐max values) (error bars represent standard deviation).

There were no statistical differences between groups for acute inflammation, granuloma formation, or necrosis. For giant cells and neovascularization, the Suture group had significantly higher scoring as compared to the Nerve Tape group. Lymphocyte/macrophage/plasma cell presence was scored significantly higher in both the Nerve Tape and SIS groups as compared to the Suture group (Figure 5).

Inflammation scores for chronic inflammation, acute inflammation, granuloma formation, giant cell identification, presence of necrosis, and presence of neovascularization for Nerve Tape, SIS, and Suture groups. (*p < 0.05).

Discussion

While Nerve Tape efficacy, as established in small animal models, serves as a surrogate of overall device safety, the novel incorporation of penetrating Nitinol microhooks warrants further scrutiny. When applied to intact healthy nerves, Nerve Tape was not associated with clinically relevant morbidity. Any damage from microhook penetration, constriction, or inflammation can be considered negligible and similar to suture related morbidity.

While some mild atrophy could be explained by iatrogenic damage during surgical manipulation or decreased volitional limb activity in response to post‐surgical discomfort, we saw no evidence of this, and normalized experimental limb gastrocnemius muscle mass and girth were close to 100% for all groups. Axon counts and g‐ratio values for experimental and control limbs alike, were also within the range of previously reported values for normal nerves [10, 11, 12] and indicate that any collateral damage related to any of the repair techniques were inconsequential. The average nerve conduction velocities were actually slightly elevated compared to previous reports in the literature [13]. This may in part be related to our measurements of latency at the first deflection point from baseline, favoring the largest, fastest conducting myelinated nerves so that the conduction velocity skewed high. Regardless, none of the studied nerves exhibited significant conduction deficits suggestive of nerve damage.

Acute inflammation, gauged by presence of neutrophils, was for the most part not present in any of the groups at the 12 week end point. Chronic inflammation (lymphocytes, macrophages, plasma cells), while present in all groups, was more pronounced in the SIS and the Nerve Tape groups. This pattern might be related to assessment timing as implant‐related neutrophil numbers typically peak early and rapidly decrease, while macrophages and lymphocytes accumulate later in the inflammatory response process [14]. Despite negative connotations, the cell types chosen to asses chronic inflammation can also be involved in well‐characterized remodeling processes. While Zhukausas et al. noted a similar increased number of macrophages and lymphocytes around SIS material in a rodent SIS conduit study, subanalysis confirmed a preponderance of pro‐regenerative M2 macrophages (versus pro‐inflammatory M1 macrophages). The authors concluded that the non‐crosslinked nature of the SIS encouraged cell invasion and accumulation and viewed this as a positive sign indicative of remodeling [15]. Badylak et al. confirmed that M2 activity was pro‐remodeling and not a sign of inflammation [16]. Multinucleated giant cells, more indicative of a foreign body reaction [17], were present to some extent in all groups but more extensively in the suture group. Consistent with our other observations, multinucleated giant cells form when several proinflammatory M1 cells fuse together [14]. Neovascularization or granuloma formation were not present in the Nerve Tape group but intermittently identified in both the suture groups and in the SIS groups (which also utilized closure‐sutures). Suture material has been shown to stimulate granuloma formation [18] and neovascularization in other tissues [19]. Neovascularization is a nonspecific marker of inflammation or hypoxia and may be stimulated by macrophages [20, 21]. The presence of multinucleated giant cells, granulomas, and neovascularization around sutures, whether implanted in isolation or with SIS, may explain increased perineurial fibrosis, but did not seem to adversely affect electrophysiology or axon health. Nitinol itself did not appear to elicit any specific reaction. Nitinol is considered a biocompatible material, generally associated with minimal cellular response [7, 8]. Notably, the SIS used in this study is the same thickness that is used to manufacture NT and is sourced and processed using the same methods. Because the primary difference between the Nerve Tape and SIS groups was the presence of microhooks, these findings suggest negligible adverse tissue impact of Nerve Tape's microhooks.

As one study limitation, some level of tissue response would be expected following any surgery and device implantation. The actions of exposing and manipulating the nerve alone have been shown to cause subtle nerve damage and scarring. Kokkalis et al. reported fibrosis around their sham (exposure only) surgery group [4], and multiple authors have demonstrated axonal damage following nerve disturbance [22, 23]. We did not perform sham surgeries on our control nerves, which were not exposed or manipulated until end point testing. This limits our ability to fully delineate the degree of reaction specifically related to the specific implants, though the varying degrees of manipulation necessary to mimic surgical repair could perhaps be considered a relevant variable. Nerve tissue mobilization and handling might differ between techniques if an actual repair was being performed. Additionally, when utilizing a SIS wrap or conduit for nerve repair, the end sutures are typically passed through both the SIS material and the outer epineurium to stabilize both the SIS material and the coaptation. Here we purposely only passed the sutures between SIS layers to permit a more conservative differential assessment of the effects of suture and SIS alone.

Tissue response to Nerve Tape was at least as low as the other treatment groups, including SIS not containing microhooks. In previous animal injury models in which Nerve Tape is used as intended, it has been demonstrated at least as safe and effective as comparable repair techniques [2, 24]. This present study thus adds to these previous findings, providing further confidence that Nerve Tape is safe for nerve repair.
