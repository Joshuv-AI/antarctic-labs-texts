Comparative analysis of drying methods and optimization of Teff Injera drying parameters using RSM and SLP approach
Authors: Dawit Andualem Asrate, Addisu Negash Ali, Fekerte Berhanu Tadle
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe drying process of Teff Injera, a staple and highly favored traditional Ethiopian food made from Teff, was investigated to optimize moisture removal and determine the ideal drying time under controlled conditions. The study focused on the effects of drying temperature and airflow speed on the drying rate. Experiments were conducted using both open sun drying and oven drying methods. The sun drying trials were carried out between January 26th and 29th, 2023, in Bahir Dar, Ethiopia, within the sunlight window of 10:30 AM to 3:30 PM, utilizing a K-type thermocouple connected to a data logger and computer equipped with SquirrelView software. Oven drying experiments explored the combined effects of drying parameters, considering both free and forced convection methods at temperatures ranging from 40 °C to 90 °C and airflow speeds of 0, 0.5, and 1 m/s. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to statistically analyze the data, while Response Surface Methodology (RSM) and Sequential Linear Programming (SLP) were employed to optimize the Injera drying process by minimizing drying time through two key process parameters, drying temperature and airflow speed. Results indicated that four days were required to achieve optimal drying using the sun drying method. In contrast, forced convection at 90 °C and 1 m/s airflow speed reduced the drying time to just 140 min. A third-order polynomial regression with a coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.993) was applied to the forced convection data to identify the optimal drying conditions. The optimum parameters, with a 99% confidence level, were determined to be a drying temperature of 84 °C, an airflow speed of 0.8 m/s, and a drying time of 146 min.Keywords: Drying methods, Optimum drying time, Optimum drying temperature, Optimum airflow speed, Injera drying, Response surface methodology, Sequential linear programmingSubject terms: Biophysics, Energy science and technology, Engineering

Introduction

Injera is the main dish and the most favorite traditional food in Ethiopia, basically made from cereal crops’ flour fermented with starter dough. Teff crop is the most suitable cereal crop for making Injera compared to other crops such as sorghum, rice, maize, barley, and wheat1,2. Teff accounts the largest volume of crops production in Ethiopia, and also the second cash crop next to coffee. Its production covers around 85.5% of area of cultivation. Teff is grown by 6.5 million smallholder farmers across Ethiopia which implies that 43% of Ethiopian farmers grow Teff3. Today, Injera made from Teff becomes a significant staple food for more than 50 million individuals in the Horn of Africa. Injera has a circular pancake shape with a soft-spongy structure with the average weight of 310 g, thickness of 2–4 mm, and a diameter of 520–580 mm. It is baked with an average temperature of around 200 °C4–6. Teff Injera is rich in important mineral contents such as fat, crude-protein, Ca, K, Na, P, Zn, Cu, and Fe7,8. Its nutritional profile and gluten-free nature makes it a very important substitute to wheat, and other crops. As global demand for gluten-free foods continues to rise, exporting Teff Injera presents a significant opportunity for Ethiopia. However, its limited shelf life remains a major challenge, highlighting the need for an effective drying method that preserves its nutritional value2. Injera has a shelf life of approximately 3 to 4 days at ambient temperature, after which fungal growth begins to occur, resulting in spoilage1.

There is an increasing trend in the global food market industries to improve the food products value chains through different techniques9. Drying is one of the most common food preservation techniques to improve the food value chain10. Drying can reduce the moisture content of Injera to a level where spoilage is prevented, while preserving its nutritional content. During the past few decades, considerable efforts were made to understand some of the physical, chemical, and biochemical changes that occur during dehydration to develop methods to prevent undesirable quality losses11,12. Generally, drying methods can be classified as open sun or oven drying. Open sun drying relies on direct solar energy and is highly inefficient due to its dependence on ambient weather conditions. It is unsuitable for large-scale applications due to inconsistent sunlight and risks of contamination and moisture reabsorption13. In contrast, oven drying technologies such as cabinet, tunnel, conveyor, fluidized bed, and vacuum dryers offer controlled conditions that enhance drying efficiency and product quality14. Drying operates through mass and heat transfer, reducing food water activity by surface moisture evaporation15. Given Injera’s 2–4 mm thickness, it qualifies as a thin-layer product, making thin-layer drying models appropriate16. Oven drying allows precise control of airflow, temperature, humidity, and time. Studies show Injera’s moisture content can be reduced from 65.5% to 12% within 4 h using solar drying methods5,6.

Injera has a moisture content between 62–65%1 which is sufficient enough for microorganisms growth. Oven drying technologies can reduce the moisture content to a level of no microbiological activity and biochemical reactions of nutritive values, formation of brown pigments, and off-flavors12,17. The moisture content removal efficiency and performance of oven drying technologies are significantly dependent on the drying rate. Based on the results of previous studies18,19, the drying rate is dependent on the drying air temperature, airflow speed, food type, food layer thickness, moisture content, food porosity, and humidity. The airflow speed, drying air temperature, and surface area of the food exposed to the drying have significant effects on the drying rate whereas the relative humidity and pressure are the least significant factors affecting the drying rate13,20–22. Therefore, it is important to optimize the drying time by considering major drying parameters11,23. However, the nutritional compositions of most foods require certain conditions of water activity, drying rate, drying temperature, and airflow speed24 to survive. Consequently, in order to have high quality food after drying, it is necessary to understand the drying sensitivity of nutrients found in foods25.

Existing research on drying injera remains limited and primarily focused on sun or solar drying, methods that are inefficient and dependent on ambient weather conditions. In contrast, studies on forced convection oven drying of injera are notably scarce, highlighting the need for method-specific investigations beyond solar-based approaches. Furthermore, mathematical modeling of injera drying under both free and forced-convection conditions remains underexplored, particularly models that dynamically account for airflow, temperature variations, and consistent moisture removal rates.

To address these gaps and support broader commercialization and standardization, this study aims to determine optimal, controlled oven-drying parameters for injera and compare them with conventional sun drying. Specifically, the research focuses on:

A polynomial regression model describing the drying kinetics was developed, and statistical analysis using analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to assess the significance of each parameter. Sequentially, validation of RSM results were carried out using sequential linear programming (SLP), taking into account predetermined airflow speed and drying temperature levels18,26,27 (Misha et al., 2013a).

Injera baking and sample preparation

A pure Teff Injera was prepared by mixing Teff flour and water at a ratio of 1:1.5 followed by fermentation using yeast as a starter at ambient temperature and baked on a baking pan at a temperature of around 120 °C4. From the local Injera baking experience, 60 h fermentation time is acceptable in terms of quality and sensory properties of Injera2. The baked dough was fermented for 3 days, and diluted with hot water and again stored for one more day. At the end of these steps, it was baked and sliced uniformly into small number of pieces before measuring the initial weight and starting the drying process. The average thickness of Injera was measured to be 4 mm. Each run of the drying experiment was conducted with a 100 g of such pieces of Injera. Drying methods

Sun drying method

The initial drying was conducted using open sun drying to establish baseline drying characteristics of Injera for comparison with oven drying methods. The experiment took place at Bahir Dar University, Ethiopia (11.5742°N, 37.3614°E) from January 26–29, 2023. Based on previous literature28, the effective solar exposure period was set between 10:30 AM. and 3:30 PM. The drying site experienced an average ambient temperature of 35.8 °C during the study period, consistent with the monthly average temperature reported by the Ethiopian National Meteorology Agency (https://www.ethiomet.gov.et/) ( 35.3 °C from 2019–2023).

A K-type thermocouple (± 0.2 °C accuracy) was used to measure the drying temperature, connected to a 2F8 model Squirrel Data Logger for continuous temperature monitoring. Squirrel View 2020 software was used to log and manage the recorded data at 1-min intervals, ensuring high-resolution temperature profiling. The number of drying days required to reach optimal moisture levels was also recorded. Figure 2 illustrates the layout of the open sun drying setup and monitoring equipment.

During the oven-drying experiments, fan speed was varied from free convection to forced convection in order to evaluate the influence of airflow on the drying process. Airflow velocities of 0, 0.5, and 1.0 m/s were selected as representative setpoints. These values span the practical operating range for thin-layer drying, provide distinct differential effects for modeling, and bracket the optimal operating window of small-scale forced-convection ovens16. Importantly, the selected speeds keep the Reynolds number within the laminar transitional regime, ensuring predictable boundary layer behavior and uniform drying while preventing mechanical disturbance of the fragile injera matrix, which was observed at airflow speeds above ~ 1.2–1.5 m/s during pilot tests. All drying experiments were conducted within a temperature range of 0–90 °C, carefully considering the thermal stability of Teff injera and the preservation of its desirable soft and dry texture4,28–30, as pilot tests showed that temperatures above ~ 100–120 °C resulted in fractured and over dry injera.

Before loading the samples into the drying chamber, the dryer was preheated to the desired temperature and operated for at least 30 min to ensure a stable temperature. The samples were then evenly distributed on a mesh tray, and their mass was recorded every 20 min throughout the drying period. To prevent moisture absorption from the environment, the samples were placed in a desiccator for 20 min before each weighing. The chosen drying parameters were based on both literature findings and the specifications of the drying equipment available in our laboratory.

In addition to moisture content and drying rate analysis, ANOVA and Response Surface Methodology (RSM) were employed to develop a regression model predicting drying time (t) as a function of drying temperature (T) and airflow speed (V). ANOVA, a statistical tool for decision-making, was used to determine the significance of experimental parameters16,31. The adequacy of the model was validated using R2 and P-values, where values of R2 closer to 1 indicate a better fit and P-values closer to 0 indicate stronger statistical significance22 (Sarkar et al., 2021). Furthermore, Sequential Linear Programming (SLP) was applied in MATLAB to solve constrained optimization problems within specified temperature (45–90 °C) and airflow speed (0–1 m/s) ranges. This approach, which uses successive first-order linearization of the objective function under constraints, was employed to validate the optimization results obtained from RSM18,26,27,36,37.

Results and discussion

Sun drying

The drying kinetics of Injera can be expressed by the amount of moisture content removed per drying time4. As shown in Table 4, open sun drying of Injera has conducted in four consecutive days with five sunshine hours per a day (10:30AM −03:30 Pm). On the first day, 57.63% of the initial moisture was removed. This increased slightly to 57.93% on the second day and 58.53% on the third day. By the fourth day, moisture removal reached 58.57%, showing minimal change compared to the previous day. These results indicate that the drying rate stabilizes after the third day; thus, effective sun drying of injera requires approximately three sunshine days (15 h). The moisture content removal obtained by other researchers indicated the same pattern to our experimental results1,37,38. As shown in Table 4, the open sun drying experiment took three consecutive sunshine days to attain a converging moisture content removal, approaching its final dried weight from an initial 100 g sample.

Oven drying

The consecutive moisture removal of Injera using oven drying method was converged within a short-period of time than the open sun drying method. The effects of temperature and airflow speed variations on the moisture removal at different drying time are shown in Fig. 5 (a, b, and c). These graphs indicated that, higher drying temperature and airflow speed leads to shorter drying time20. In contrast, drying at 40 °C exhibits a relatively sluggish rate, with distinct mass loss curves observed particularly under free convection and 0.5 m/s forced convection drying. This behavior arises because airflow alone cannot provide a sufficient driving force for internal moisture diffusion within the injera, unlike at higher temperatures or greater airflow rates. At 40 °C, the maximum drying times observed were 11 h under free convection, 5 h under forced convection at 0.5 m/s, and 3 h under forced convection at 1 m/s. Under free convection at 90 °C, the drying time decreased to 4 h, representing the minimum drying duration for injera in free convection. Comparatively, at the same temperature (90 °C) under forced convection, the drying time was reduced from 1 h 40 min to 1 h 20 min as the airflow speed increased from 0.5 m/s to 1 m/s, as shown in Fig. 5 (b and c). In contrast, open sun drying required about three sunshine days (15 h) to achieve a similar drying level, highlighting the superior efficiency of oven-based drying methods.

Mass loss of Injera during drying under (a) free convection drying (b) forced convection at 0.5 m/s and (c) forced convection at 1 m/s.

The mass losses versus drying time of other temperature curves remain relatively similar. In the forced convection drying, the reduction of mass loss has observed between 80 °C and 90 °C as the airflow speed is increasing. This indicates that there is a possibility of the optimum drying temperature to be between 80 °C and 90 °C. It has proved that, the texture of dry product processed by oven drying is much better than sun drying. In general, the airflow speed variation shows an significant effect at all temperatures with a drying time differences at high temperature and airflow speed levels1. Forced convection at 0.5 m/s and 1 m/s significantly enhanced the drying rate, while temperatures ~ 90 °C negatively affected the Injera texture.

Figure 6 further shows drying temperature had a significant effect on the drying time of Teff injera. At higher temperatures (70–90 °C), drying was completed much faster, while at 40 °C the process was considerably slower, requiring about 12–13 h under free convection compared to only 1–2 h at 90 °C. Drying rate

The drying rate (DR) in Fig. 7 (a, b, and c) at free convection, 0.5 m/s forced convection, and 1 m/s forced convection respectively show the airflow speed and drying temperature have direct relationship to the rate of moisture removal. When the airflow speed increases from 0.5 m/s to 1 m/s, it shows a corresponding significant increase on the drying rate. It was also observed that drying at 40 °C exhibited a relatively linear drying rate, whereas higher temperatures of 80 °C and 90 °C have showed a contrary behavior. When the drying temperature is higher the DR becomes nonlinear23, and reduced with shorter drying time than the remaining temperatures. When comparing drying methods, free convection drying exhibited lower drying rates (0.175–0.2 g/min). In contrast, forced convection significantly improved the drying rates, ranging from 0.49–1.75 g/min at 0.5 m/s and 0.75–2 g/min at 1 m/s airflow speeds. Since the internal resistance of the moisture flow in the drying process is limited to a certain level of airflow speed, the airflow speed above a certain level will not have significant effect on the drying rate23. These findings suggest that forced convection leads to consistent drying behavior, while also enhancing drying rates and overall drying efficiency.

The results obtained followed a similar trend with no significant differences compared to recent studies5,6,29,30. Besides, solar drying offers a sustainable and cost-effective alternative to conventional methods; however, it is limited by weather dependency, extended drying durations, and inconsistent drying, all of which can impact product quality. The study by5,6 on the drying kinetics of Injera (Dirkosh) using a mixed-mode solar dryer demonstrates a feasible approach for enhancing energy efficiency and maintaining acceptable product quality, particularly in rural settings. Optimization of drying process parameters

To obtain the minimum drying time, optimization of the drying process parameters using RSM was used as shown in Fig. 13. Therefore, the optimum values of drying temperature, airflow speed, and drying time has determined with a 99% desirability. The optimum drying temperature of Injera (84.444 °C) will create the thermal stability to maintain the nutrients stability. The airflow rate above a certain level has no significant effect on the drying rate. In the same pattern, the airflow speed above 0.7879 m/s can be considered as insignificant for Injera drying process. As shown in Fig. 13, the vertical red lines display the optimal points, while the blue dashed line indicates the minimum drying time achieved. The high desirability value (0.9903) confirms the effectiveness of these optimized parameters. The combination of those optimum parameters (airflow speed and drying temperature) provided the optimum drying time of 146.402 min.

Sequential Linear Programming (SLP) was employed to optimize the Injera drying process by minimizing drying time through the adjustment of drying temperature and airflow speed. As presented in Fig. 14, SLP identified optimal conditions at 84.531 °C and 0.79 m/s, achieving a drying time of 148.1205 min. The convergence behavior, stabilizing around the 100th iteration out of 500, highlights the efficiency of the method. These results closely align with those derived from Response Surface Methodology (RSM), indicating a strong consistency between the two optimization approaches.

The minor discrepancies observed between SLP and RSM results stem from the methodological differences SLP relies on linear approximations and iterative refinement, while RSM models complex variable interactions through second-order polynomials. Despite this, the convergence of outcomes supports the robustness of both methods. In this context, SLP served as a validation mechanism for RSM’s statistically-driven optimization, reinforcing its reliability in identifying effective drying parameters for the drying process.

Conclusion

This study conducted comparative drying experiments between sun drying and oven drying methods to evaluate differences in drying efficiency for Teff Injera. Significant variations in drying time and performance were observed between the two methods, underscoring the need for improved drying technologies based on optimized parameters. While sun drying remains a cost-effective and accessible option in rural areas, it is constrained by environmental variability, extended drying durations, and inconsistent drying rates, which can compromise product quality.

The sun drying process required three days, with approximately five sunshine hours per day, to remove ~ 58.57% of the moisture. The drying rate stabilized after the third day, indicating that effective sun drying of Injera requires about 15 h of sunlight. In contrast, forced convection oven drying under controlled conditions achieved a comparable moisture reduction in 140 min, demonstrating superior efficiency and consistency. These findings align with recent studies highlighting the trade-off between energy sustainability and drying precision.

RSM identified optimal conditions at 84.5 °C and 0.8 m/s, achieving a drying time of 146 min. SLP yielded closely aligned results, confirming the robustness and consistency of the optimization. The strong agreement between the two methods, despite their methodological differences, underscores the reliability of the identified conditions. These findings validate the effectiveness of combining statistical and mathematical optimization techniques and offer practical insights for enhancing energy efficiency and overall performance in traditional food drying systems.

Despite the valuable findings, this study has several limitations. Long-term storage stability and product quality were not assessed. Experiments were conducted at a laboratory or small-scale setup, so results may differ at larger or industrial scales. Additionally, other quality parameters of Injera, including texture, nutritional content, and sensory attributes, were not thoroughly evaluated. For future research, an expanded design of experiments (DOE) incorporating higher levels of temperature and airflow speed is recommended. Fitting the drying data to empirical kinetic models, such as the Page, Henderson-Pabis, or Logarithmic models, would provide further insight into the drying behavior of Teff Injera and enhance predictive process optimization.
