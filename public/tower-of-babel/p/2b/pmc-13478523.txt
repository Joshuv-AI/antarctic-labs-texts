Vehicle-wise validated ensemble learning framework for robust electric vehicle energy consumption prediction using physics-guided features and statistical benchmarking
Authors: T. Mariprasath, Preethi Srinivasan, Geetha Anbazhagan, Porkodi Karuvelampalayam Prabhakaran, Seif Al Bustanji, Viktoriia Bereznychenko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Predicting EV energy consumption accurately is essential for solving range anxiety and helping with better route planning and energy management in real conditions. Many current studies use random data splitting and few evaluation measures, which leads to overly optimistic results. Moreover, this approach does not properly test how well the models work on new data. According to the research requirements, this study presents a systematic ensemble learning framework using vehicle-wise data splitting to prevent information leakage and ensure realistic evaluation. Five machine learning models—Random Forest, ExtraTrees, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost—are tested using real electric vehicle data that includes only physics-based features for vehicle movement and environmental conditions. The model performance is evaluated using multiple metrics like MAE, RMSE, MAPE, R2, and EVS, and statistical significance is further checked through Friedman and Wilcoxon tests. The results show that the ExtraTrees model gives the best performance with R2 = 0.9407, RMSE = 0.6113 kWh/km, and MAPE = 7.62%. Additionally, this model shows good accuracy across all driving conditions. The results indicate that ExtraTrees works significantly better than LightGBM, while it gives similar results to other ensemble models. Furthermore, the results demonstrate that ensemble methods work well for different types of EV data by reducing errors. The suggested framework provides a robust and dependable solution for predicting EV energy use and range in real situations.

Introduction

The quick growth of electric vehicle adoption has further increased the need for correct energy use and remaining range prediction, as this itself helps reduce range worry and improve battery management systems. As per real-world studies, EV energy use changes a lot regarding driving style, traffic, road conditions, weather, and extra power loads, making energy patterns very uneven and hard to predict. Old rule-based models cannot handle these complex interactions properly. Data-driven learning methods are becoming more popular for EV energy and range prediction because of this limitation1,2. Further, recent studies show that machine learning methods can definitely make predictions much better by learning complex patterns from big driving data collections. Moreover, regression models using vehicle data and context features work better than analytical models for estimating energy consumption, showing the same superior results across different driving conditions3. ML models surely perform differently based on how data is spread, different types of features, and noise patterns. Moreover, these factors change a lot across different vehicles, drivers, and working conditions4.

To further improve accuracy, ensemble learning methods are being used more in EV energy prediction tasks itself. Essentially, bagging models like Random Forest and boosting models like XGBoost and LightGBM show the same strong ability to handle complex patterns and work better than single models. Ensemble methods reduce sensitivity to local data changes and capture complex feature interactions, making them well suited for noisy vehicular telemetry data itself. This approach further helps in handling fluctuations in the data. However, current studies mostly look at single algorithms or suggest layered designs without properly comparing different ensemble methods as per the same conditions regarding systematic evaluation5,6.

Another important problem in current research itself lies in the evaluation method, which needs further improvement. It is notice that studies only use random splitting of data for training and testing, which can cause problems when the same vehicle’s data appears in both sets. This surely gives too positive results and does not show real situations where models must work with new vehicles and drivers they have not seen before7. Moreover, such estimates do not match actual deployment conditions. The real ability of ensemble models to work across different vehicles is surely not explored enough. Moreover, this cross-vehicle generalization capability needs much more study8. Further, performance comparisons are often based only on average error metrics like RMSE or MAE without proper statistical testing itself. Without proper testing, it becomes difficult to know if the differences between competing models are only real or just happening by chance. Principally, this makes it hard to choose the right model when the performance differences are small, but the same decisions are critical for EV range and energy management9,10.

As per these limitations, this study aims to provide statistically validated benchmarking of major ensemble regression methods regarding EV energy consumption prediction for real deployment use. Five popular ensemble models—Random Forest, ExtraTrees, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost—are surely tested using the same datasets, similar feature formats, proper vehicle-wise data separation, and multiple evaluation measures. Moreover, this systematic approach ensures fair comparison between all these machine learning methods. Moreover, non-parametric tests are surely used to check if the performance differences we see are truly significant. Through this careful testing, the study surely aims to find the best ensemble learning method for handling mixed and noisy EV driving data. Moreover, this work provides practical guidance for real-world EV energy prediction and onboard range estimation systems. As per the present study, the motivation is described in Sect. 3, while Sect. 4 shows the experimental results regarding the findings. Section 5 discusses the findings we got in detail. Critical study on existing forecasting technique

Accurate prediction of the remaining driving range (RDR) in electric vehicles is challenging due to the strong influence of dynamic factors such as driving behavior, traffic conditions, road characteristics, and battery state. This variability limits the effectiveness of conventional rule-based approaches and motivates the use of data-driven models. Machine learning methods have demonstrated improved prediction accuracy by capturing complex nonlinear relationships among these variables; however, their performance often degrades when applied to unseen vehicles or different operating environments, highlighting limitations in generalization11.

To improve robustness, ensemble-based approaches have been explored by combining multiple models to reduce prediction error and enhance adaptability under varying conditions. Although such methods improve accuracy, they increase computational complexity and reduce interpretability, and their performance remains sensitive to dataset variations12. Beyond direct prediction, energy-aware routing and uncertainty modeling have been introduced to improve practical applicability. Incorporating uncertainty into energy estimation enables more reliable route planning under varying traffic conditions. However, these approaches depend on high-quality spatial and traffic data and may not generalize effectively across different regions or vehicle types13. In contrast, control-oriented strategies aim to reduce energy consumption through optimized driving behavior, such as acceleration and pedal control. While effective in controlled conditions, these methods rely on ideal assumptions and are less suitable for real-world prediction scenarios with diverse driving patterns14.

Recent advancements focus on integrating traffic dynamics and fine-grained environmental information into prediction frameworks. Incorporating predicted traffic conditions improves energy estimation for route planning; however, errors in traffic forecasting can propagate and degrade overall prediction performance. Similarly, road-level modeling improves spatial accuracy by capturing variations such as road grade and traffic conditions, but requires accurate map matching and high-quality data, and faces challenges in cross-vehicle generalization15,16.

Another emerging direction involves combining static trip-level descriptors with dynamic segment-wise features to address uncertainties associated with unknown route profiles. Deep learning models have shown strong capability in capturing temporal dependencies and nonlinear patterns, improving prediction robustness under realistic driving scenarios. However, these models typically require large-scale datasets and remain sensitive to domain shifts across different vehicles unless supported by physics-guided constraints or invariant feature representations17. Simulation-assisted and data-driven approaches have been explored to improve the reliability of electric vehicle energy consumption prediction. Integrating simulation insights with machine learning enhances model credibility by incorporating parametric knowledge; however, such methods depend heavily on simulation assumptions and may not generalize well to diverse real-world driving conditions if training data is limited in variability18.

Tree-based and boosting algorithms have demonstrated strong performance for structured datasets due to their ability to capture nonlinear relationships and provide interpretable feature importance. Despite these advantages, their effectiveness is highly dependent on proper feature engineering, and performance may degrade when traffic patterns differ from training conditions or when key physical variables such as road slope, aerodynamic drag, and rolling resistance are not explicitly incorporated19. Recent reviews highlight that modern energy prediction frameworks increasingly rely on machine learning, interpretable modeling, and hybrid physics-data approaches. These studies consolidate key influencing factors such as speed, acceleration, road grade, temperature, and auxiliary loads, while also emphasizing the limitations of purely black-box models in terms of trust, transferability, and lack of standardized evaluation protocols20.Deep learning-based approaches have shown strong capability in capturing complex nonlinear patterns for energy and range prediction under real-world conditions. However, these models are often sensitive to dataset bias and lack interpretability unless combined with explainability techniques or physics-guided feature representations21.To address data scarcity and improve generalization, pretraining-based frameworks have been introduced, enabling models to adapt to new vehicles or users with limited historical data. While effective in improving transferability, these approaches increase model complexity and require consistent feature definitions to avoid performance degradation across different domains22.

Feature selection and parameter optimization techniques have further improved prediction accuracy by identifying relevant inputs and tuning model configurations. Nevertheless, such approaches may become dataset-specific and require rigorous validation strategies, such as vehicle-wise data splits and statistical testing, to ensure reliable generalization23. Hybrid frameworks that integrate domain knowledge with data-driven modeling have emerged as a promising direction for improving robustness across diverse operating conditions. By incorporating scenario-level information, these approaches enhance prediction capability; however, they introduce additional modeling complexity and depend on comprehensive scenario representation, where incomplete coverage can negatively impact performance24.

Recent research has focused on improving the practical applicability of electric vehicle energy prediction by utilizing realistically available inputs. Sequence-aware deep learning models address the limitation of assuming future driving information by relying on pre-trip data, thereby enhancing deployment feasibility. However, these models require consistent temporal inputs and may face challenges in implementation on vehicles with limited sensing and logging capabilities25. Ensemble-based approaches incorporating multidimensional dynamic features and interaction terms have demonstrated improved prediction accuracy by effectively capturing complex relationships within the data. Despite their strong performance, such methods increase computational complexity and reduce interpretability, particularly when feature interactions are not explicitly analyzed26. Physics-informed learning frameworks have emerged as an important direction to enhance model stability and robustness. By integrating physical constraints with data-driven learning, these approaches improve prediction consistency under varying operating conditions. However, their effectiveness depends on the correct formulation of physical priors, as improper constraints can introduce bias and degrade model performance27. Comparative studies across multiple machine learning algorithms indicate that ensemble methods, particularly tree-based models, consistently perform well on structured datasets due to their ability to capture nonlinear interactions. Nevertheless, these evaluations are often limited by dataset dependency and may not reflect true generalization performance when tested on unseen vehicles or operating conditions28,29.

Recent reviews highlight the importance of standardized evaluation protocols and consistent performance metrics to ensure fair comparison and reproducibility across studies. Although these works identify key influencing factors and methodological gaps, they do not provide unified frameworks suitable for real-world deployment30,31. Feature-oriented frameworks have further categorized energy consumption influences into vehicle characteristics, environmental conditions, driver behavior, and driving patterns, providing structured guidance for feature engineering. However, translating these factors into physically meaningful and generalizable features remains a challenge in many existing studies32. Deep learning models have demonstrated improved capability in capturing complex nonlinear relationships for energy and range prediction under varying conditions. Despite their advantages, these models typically require large-scale datasets and may exhibit reduced stability and generalization when applied to new vehicles unless supported by regularization or physics-guided feature integration33.

Hybrid approaches that combine static trip-level information with dynamic segment-wise features have been shown to improve prediction performance under uncertain route conditions. However, accurate modeling of route characteristics such as road grade and external conditions remains challenging without reliable external data sources, indicating the need for more robust hybrid frameworks34. Finally, incorporating physically meaningful derived features, such as speed-related terms and load-based variables, has been shown to improve prediction accuracy by better representing underlying vehicle dynamics. Nevertheless, incomplete consideration of critical factors such as temperature effects and auxiliary loads can limit model reliability under diverse real-world conditions35.

Recent studies emphasize the importance of interpretable and hybrid modeling approaches, highlighting the effectiveness of tree-based methods in handling structured data and enabling feature importance analysis. However, inconsistencies in evaluation practices, such as the lack of standardized data splits and uncertainty reporting, limit the comparability and reliability of existing results36.To improve model transferability, pretraining-based approaches have been explored for scenarios with limited user-specific data. While these methods enhance performance across different vehicles, they introduce additional complexity and require careful feature standardization to ensure consistency across varying data sources and deployment conditions37.

Feature selection and parameter optimization strategies have demonstrated measurable improvements in prediction accuracy, supported by statistical validation techniques such as Friedman and Wilcoxon tests. Nevertheless, these methods may become overly tailored to specific datasets and require rigorous cross-vehicle evaluation to ensure generalization and robustness38.Ensemble-based frameworks, particularly stacking methods, have been shown to effectively capture complex feature interactions and real-world operating variability. Although these approaches improve predictive performance, they increase model complexity and reduce interpretability, necessitating additional analysis such as ablation studies or feature importance evaluation to ensure transparency39. Comprehensive benchmarking studies further highlight that prediction performance is strongly influenced by data quality, feature representation, and evaluation methodology. These findings reinforce the importance of physics-guided feature engineering and vehicle-wise validation strategies to achieve reliable and generalizable energy prediction models40. Comparative analysis of existing EV range prediction models.

As per current studies, machine learning methods for EV energy use and driving range prediction have many important problems. Regarding these approaches, several key limitations still exist in research work. Moreover, first, many studies focus on suggesting single models or deep learning designs without properly comparing different ensemble learning methods as per same experimental conditions. Most research regarding this area does not systematically check various approaches under identical testing setups. As per current research, there is limited understanding regarding which method works better - bagging-based ensembles or boosting-based ensembles - for modeling noisy and highly variable EV telemetry data. Second, most published studies surely use random splitting of data samples for training and testing, which may accidentally create data leakage problems. Moreover, this happens because the same vehicle’s driving patterns can appear in both training and testing groups. Essentially, this gives overly good performance numbers and doesn’t show the same real-world situations where models must work with new vehicles and drivers they haven’t seen before. Researchers have not studied enough how ensemble models work across different vehicles. Many studies only compare average errors without doing proper statistical tests. Without statistical validation, it becomes difficult to determine whether the observed performance differences among competing models are genuinely significant, or they are merely due to random variation in the data sampling itself, and this further complicates the evaluation process. This reduces trust in model choice results regarding real-world EV energy management systems. As per current studies, ensemble models like Random Forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost are widely used, but few research works give complete and proven comparison of these methods on same EV datasets using similar features and testing methods. Also, as per current research, there is no clear guidance regarding which ensemble method gives the best balance between accuracy, robustness, and generalization for EV energy consumption prediction.

Motivation of the research

Principally, many machine learning methods are used for EV energy and range prediction, but we still don’t know which ensemble techniques work the same way under real driving conditions. The many current studies only work on making single models or deep learning systems more accurate, but they do not compare different ensemble methods properly under the same test conditions. Moreover, many earlier studies surely used random data splitting methods that mixed information from same vehicles between training and testing data. This approach surely gives overly positive results that do not match real situations where models must work with completely new vehicles and drivers. It is noted that claims about which models are better are only based on average error numbers without proper statistical checking, making it hard to know if the performance differences are real or just due to random data variations. As per these limitations, there is uncertainty regarding selecting proper machine learning models for practical EV energy management systems, where strong performance, stability, and working across different vehicles are very important. This study aims to compare different machine learning methods to find the best model for predicting electric vehicle energy use in real driving conditions. We want to identify which method works most reliably for different types of vehicles. As per the manuscript, the main contribution regarding this work is discussed below.

This study provides several important contributions for reliable machine learning-based EV energy consumption prediction, which is the same goal researchers have been working toward. A new testing system that compares five top machine learning models - Random Forest, ExtraTrees, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost - using only the same data and training methods to check which ensemble approach works best. As per the study method, model performance is checked by keeping different vehicles’ data separate, so the model is tested on new vehicles it has not seen before. Regarding real-world use, this approach gives proper understanding of how well the model will work when deployed. Third, we surely use multiple evaluation methods like MAE, RMSE, MAPE, R², and explained variance score together to measure prediction accuracy. Moreover, these methods help us understand both exact errors and relative performance across different working conditions. Fourth, formal non-parametric tests like Friedman test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test are used to check if the performance differences between models are statistically significant, which further strengthens the model ranking process itself. The bagging methods, especially Extremely Randomized Trees, work better than boosting methods when the EV driving data has noise and different types of information. This gives us practical help for choosing strong models in EV energy prediction systems only.

Random Forest regressor (RF)

Random Forest principally creates many decision trees using the same training data but with random sampling, and each tree uses random features at every split. Also, each tree learns its own simple way to predict the answer, and we definitely get the final result by taking the average of what all trees say. This combining method only reduces the changes in predictions much more than using one decision tree, making the model work better on new data and handle noise only. As per the training process, each tree uses different data and features, so Random Forest reduces overfitting problems. Regarding real-world vehicle sensors, this method gives stable results even when input data has errors or uncertainties41-43.

Further, predicting how much energy electric cars will use is very hard because driving habits, weather conditions, and power needs are all connected in complex ways, and these connections are only getting stronger when different factors work together. As per vehicle operation, factors like speed, acceleration, road slope, vehicle weight, outside temperature, wind speed, and traffic conditions together affect traction power, auxiliary loads, and regenerative braking performance. Regarding these variables, they work jointly to influence the overall system effectiveness. Random Forest works well for complex data patterns as per its ability to divide feature space without needing specific function rules. This method captures multivariate dependencies regarding nonlinear relationships automatically. Moreover, adding physics-based load indicators like rolling resistance and air drag proxies only makes the model better at showing real driving physics while keeping the flexibility of data-driven learning. This mixed feature approach surely helps Random Forest to capture real driving patterns and actual energy loss processes. Moreover, it combines practical observations with physical principles effectively44.

It is observed that single decision trees are not stable, where only small changes in training data can make the model structure and predictions very different. This limitation fundamentally becomes critical in EV datasets that have different driving patterns - the same dataset includes stop-and-go city traffic, steady highway driving, and frequent slope changes in hilly areas. Mostly, Random Forest combines multiple different trees so that no single noisy pattern can dominate the same final prediction. Moreover, the combined model can properly catch how different factors work together, like when high speed and upward road slope both affect energy use at the same time, while it only stays strong against sensor problems, missing data, and small datasets. Random Forest gives steady results in different working conditions only, making it a good and trusted basic model for comparing advanced group methods and nature-based learning ways in EV energy control and distance prediction studies45.

Random Forest works as a strong reference model to check how well more complex ensemble methods and hybrid AI approaches perform in this framework. This method surely needs less hyperparameter tuning and works well without feature scaling. Moreover, it shows stable results when testing on new vehicles, which is very important for real-world use. As per the study design, advanced models are compared against Random Forest to ensure performance gains are genuine and not due to data splitting issues. The comparison confirms real improvements regarding prediction accuracy and adaptability across different scenarios. Electric vehicle energy use surely depends on many complex factors like power needs, extra loads, road slopes, vehicle weight, outside temperature, and battery conditions working together in complicated ways. Moreover, tree-based methods can easily capture these different relationships without needing to build detailed system models55.

Extra Trees further extends the Random Forest method by adding more randomization in feature selection and split-threshold selection itself. Random Forest surely finds the best split points by reducing impurity, but Extra Trees randomly picks split points from the feature range first. Moreover, Extra Trees then chooses the best option from these random splits. This random splitting method surely reduces connections between trees, which lowers overall errors and makes the model work better on new data. Moreover, this approach is very helpful for car sensor data where many signals like speed and acceleration are strongly linked and change under different driving conditions. In electric vehicles, this strong decorrelation surely helps different trees to focus on different driving conditions like city traffic, highway driving, and uphill climbing. Moreover, this approach gives robust results for complex energy patterns without getting too fitted to specific driving data. ET can learn complex connections between different factors, which helps in modeling combined effects like how road slope and vehicle weight together affect traction power, or how outside temperature and battery temperature work together to influence internal resistance and auxiliary energy needs that directly impact energy use per kilometer.

Extra Trees trains faster than Random Forest because it avoids split optimization, which further makes it suitable for large EV datasets and repeated Monte-Carlo evaluations itself. Dataset description

The dataset utilized in this paper is collected from an open-access EV-use-energy-research-database and contains 5,001 individual segment-level driving records, each with 16 input features and one target output (energy consumption per unit distance kWh/km). Each record is representative of an aggregated driving segment as opposed to a fixed-frequency, continuous time-series. The dataset provides anonymized vehicle identifiers to facilitate data splitting by vehicle, preventing information leakage. Dataset statistics show that the overall data is composed of 989 unique vehicles, with each vehicle contributing a small number of observations, suggesting a very heterogeneous multi-vehicle structure. Temporal properties show that the minimum and median sampling interval is around 1 min, but the overall mean is large due to interleaved multi-vehicular logged trucks.

This proves that the dataset is based on segment-by-segment aggregation across a variety of driving conditions, rather than continuous tracking of individual vehicles. The dataset encompasses a wide range of operational and environmental conditions, including speed, acceleration, slope of the road, vehicle weight (load), traffic environment, and weather parameters such as temperature, humidity, and wind speed. The aspects of physics-based feature representations are included by using the speed (and the square of it as a proxy for aerodynamic drag), vehicle weight (which, in turn, influences rolling resistance), road steepness (also called grade resistance), and acceleration (inertial effects), so that expert knowledge can be used in conjunction with data-driven frameworks.

Although specific parameters like exact vehicle count, sampling/direct-sampling frequency and complete specification for vehicles are not revealed, the data set has enough variety, mass (5001 segments) and multi-domain features to facilitate solid evaluation. Moreover, the combination of vehicle-wise validation, physics-informed feature engineering and clear reporting on dataset limitations bolsters reliability and reproducibility within those constraints as well comparability to other data-driven EV works. Moreover, Energy usage per distance (kWh/km) was determined for each driving segment and calculated as energy consumed (kWh)/distance travelled (km). Numbers on the raw energy consumed per trip range from about 1.88 kWh to 14.66 kWh, reflecting huge differences based on driving type.

Result and analysis

As per the research requirements, multiple evaluation metrics including MAE, RMSE, MAPE, R², and EVS were selected regarding comprehensive model performance assessment under different operating conditions. Each metric actually captures a different part of how good the predictions are, which is definitely important for EV energy use modeling where both exact accuracy and showing changes matter a lot. As per the analysis, MAE gives a simple measure of average prediction error in kWh/km but does not focus on big errors, which can be important regarding safety-critical situations. RMSE surely gives more importance to bigger errors because it uses square calculations, making it more sensitive to extreme mistakes like sudden energy jumps during fast or uphill driving. Moreover, outliers can influence RMSE results too much sometimes. Moreover, mAPE surely gives a percentage-based way to understand errors, which helps compare how well models work in different situations. Moreover, it becomes unreliable when actual values are very small and creates much higher error numbers than reality. Basically, R² shows how well your model captures the same patterns in the data, but it doesn’t tell you if your predictions are actually accurate since it can still be high even when the model makes systematic mistakes. EVS surely measures how well the model explains data changes and is less affected by bias than R². Moreover, it does not directly show the size of errors. Using just one measurement can actually give wrong results, so we definitely used multiple methods together. This approach actually captures different types of errors and definitely ensures the model works well for real EV energy prediction.

Discussion

This study uses strict vehicle-wise validation and multiple error metrics to test the models, which provides strong evidence for selecting the right model in real EV energy management systems. Further, the work itself includes regime-wise analysis and statistical testing unlike previous studies that used simple random splits. ExtraTrees shows the best results across all performance measures in Table 4, with lowest MAE and RMSE values and highest R² scores, which further confirms its superior accuracy. The model itself demonstrates better stability compared to other methods. ExtraTrees performs better because it uses completely random splits that reduce connections between trees more than Random Forest. This approach controls the noise problems that happen with related vehicle data like speed and temperature. EV energy use comes from complex interactions between driving power, air resistance, tire friction, and other electrical loads, so models that focus on reducing errors and using diverse approaches work better to capture different driving patterns without getting stuck on specific cases.

The analysis further shows that boosting models like XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost can learn global patterns well, but the models itself are more sensitive to outliers and noise. This pattern shows clearly in the MAPE results, where XGBoost has much higher percentage error even though it gets good R² scores. Boosted models perform poorly when battery energy is low, where small mistakes become big percentage errors—this is bad for EV range systems that need accurate predictions when battery charge is nearly empty. The Taylor diagram analysis further shows the model performance itself. This gives more understanding about how the model works by checking correlation and variance reproduction at the same time. It is observed that ExtraTrees and Random Forest are located closest to the reference point, which confirms they can reproduce not only the mean energy levels but also the changes in real driving energy demand. LightGBM shows big differences only, which means it has trouble understanding the real changes in data when working conditions are not the same. Further, this problem because the method only grows tree leaf by leaf, which makes it fit too closely to small data groups while missing the bigger patterns in medium-sized and noisy datasets.

The error spread and total error studies show clear patterns in the figures. These analyses help understand how errors build up over time. Moreover, Figs. 4 and 6 show that ExtraTrees works better and is more robust. The CDF and boxplot results show that ExtraTrees itself gives more low-error predictions and further reduces error spread, which indicates better reliability with fewer worst-case deviations. Further, this feature is surely very important for EV energy management and navigation systems on board. Moreover, occasional big errors can cause poor route planning and more range anxiety, even when average accuracy is acceptable. The feature ablation study (Fig.) surely shows which components are most important. Moreover, this analysis helps us understand how each part contributes to the overall performance. The study further confirms that physics-guided features play a critical role in improving the model’s ability to generalize itself. When slope and air resistance factors are removed, the prediction accuracy surely becomes much worse, showing that pulling and drag forces are the main reasons for battery power loss. Moreover, these forces have the strongest effect on how quickly the battery gets drained. Also, this surely proves that our mixed approach of combining physical features with data models works well. Moreover, this method makes the system stronger when facing new driving situations. These findings further suggest that data-driven approaches without physical context itself may have limited transferability across different vehicles and environments.

ExtraTrees shows the same low error rates across all driving speeds, making it work well for city traffic, suburban roads, and highway conditions where air resistance is the main factor. CatBoost shows good performance at low and medium speeds, but its error increases sharply at high speeds itself. This indicates that the model cannot handle continuous high-load aerodynamic conditions further. As per the analysis, Random Forest shows lower performance than ExtraTrees in all conditions regarding accuracy because its trees remain connected due to fixed split selection methods. LightGBM performs poorly at both low and high speeds. It struggles to handle changing loads and high-speed effects at the same time. These results further confirm that average accuracy itself is not enough to check if the system can be deployed, and regime-wise validation is essential for EV applications.

The statistical tests using Friedman and Wilcoxon methods further strengthen the reliability of the ranking itself. As per the Friedman test, the performance differences between models are statistically significant. Regarding pairwise Wilcoxon analysis, ExtraTrees performs much better than LightGBM and shows similar performance to RF, CatBoost, and XGBoost. Deeply, different ensemble methods give the same average accuracy, but ExtraTrees shows the most consistent error patterns across all samples and conditions, which is critical for safety and consumer EV applications. ExtraTrees works faster because it uses simple split selection and can build trees in parallel, making it the same good choice for both cloud analytics and onboard car systems. Moreover, ExtraTrees works well with good accuracy and stable performance, and it only needs moderate computing power, making it a reliable basic model for real-time EV energy estimation and range prediction. For mixed real-world EV data, reducing variance and using diverse models is only more important than fitting residuals aggressively. Bagging methods, especially Extra Trees, give good balance between being accurate and working reliably. These approaches are suitable for practical EV energy management systems.

The proposed framework shows good performance, but this study uses only one real-world EV dataset which may further limit how well the findings work across different vehicle types, regions, and driving conditions. The dataset itself may not represent all possible scenarios. The dataset itself captures diverse driving conditions with variations in speed, traffic, slope, and environmental factors, but it may not represent all real-world scenarios further. The model performance itself should be understood further within the context of the dataset used. Future work will actually focus on testing the proposed approach using different datasets from various sources and vehicle types to definitely check how well it works in different conditions.

Table 10 compares the proposed machine-learning framework against a simplified engineering-based baseline model derived from longitudinal vehicle-dynamics equations. The analytical baseline achieves an RMSE of 1.72 kWh/km and an R² of 0.64, indicating limited capability in modeling heterogeneous real-world driving conditions using simplified physical assumptions alone. In contrast, the ensemble-learning approaches achieve substantially improved prediction accuracy, with the ExtraTrees model obtaining the lowest RMSE (0.6113 kWh/km) and highest R² (0.9407). This improvement demonstrates the ability of machine-learning methods to capture nonlinear interactions among vehicle dynamics, traffic conditions, environmental factors, and transient operating behaviors that are difficult to represent using simplified analytical formulations. These findings confirm that the proposed framework extends beyond pure machine-learning benchmarking by demonstrating measurable improvement over conventional engineering-based EV energy estimation approaches.

Conclusion

This study surely developed a tested framework to compare different machine learning models that predict how much energy electric vehicles will use. Moreover, the framework works well when testing these models on different vehicles in real conditions. It is noted that the ExtraTrees model is giving the best results with good accuracy and steady performance, and it is working well across different driving conditions only. Boosting models actually show more sensitivity when conditions are extreme, which definitely leads to more variable predictions. Feature analysis actually shows that physics-based variables, especially speed, slope, and air flow effects, definitely help make predictions more accurate and work better across different situations. Moreover, tests confirm the performance differences are real and important, while some combined models are only showing similar results under certain conditions.

Moreover, the study surely has good points, but it uses only one dataset and does not test how well it works on small devices. Besides, the researchers did not check the computing performance properly when the system runs on embedded hardware. As per the study, these factors may limit how the findings can be used regarding different types of vehicles and working conditions. Basically, future work will focus on the same framework but extend it to multiple datasets and explore deep learning approaches to enhance predictive performance. The findings actually show that ensemble methods reduce variance and definitely provide reliable solutions for EV energy prediction. These methods actually support intelligent energy management and range estimation systems in practical applications.

While the ExtraTrees model produced the best overall numeric performance across all MAE, RMSE, MAPE, R², and EVS metrics, our statistical findings show that there are few statistically significant differences in performance among several ensemble-learning techniques. In particular, Wilcoxon signed-rank analysis demonstrates that the differences between ExtraTrees, RF, CatBoost, and XGBoost are insignificant at the selected confidence level, while LightGBM shows significantly lower performance. Thus, the results should be understood as evidence of competitive and consistent ensemble-learning performance, not absolute superiority from a single model. These results indicate that several tree-based ensemble methods are valid for predicting EV energy consumption over heterogeneous driving conditions.
