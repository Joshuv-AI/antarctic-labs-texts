Longitudinal Decline in Electrochemical Skin Conductance Reflects Disease Progression in Multiple System Atrophy
Authors: Paulo Bastos, Marc Kermorgant, Fabienne Ory‐Magne, Clémence Leung, Margherita Fabbri, Olivier Rascol, Alexandra Foubert‐Samier, David Bendetowicz, Wassilios G Meissner, Anne Pavy‐le‐Traon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMultiple system atrophy (MSA) is characterized by progressive autonomic/motor dysfunction, but robust biomarkers do not exist. Electrochemical skin conductance (ESC) provides a noninvasive measure of sudomotor function, but its longitudinal predictive value and prognostic relevance in MSA remain insufficiently studied. We aimed to comprehensively evaluate ESC in a large longitudinal cohort.MethodsWe analyzed 175 patients with MSA followed for a mean of 4.2 ± 2.1 years. Disease severity was assessed using UMSARS Parts 1 and 2, COMPASS31, SCOPA‐AUT, and orthostatic blood pressure (BP) measurements. Hand and foot ESC were measured using Sudoscan. Cross‐sectional and longitudinal associations were examined using linear mixed‐effects models, repeated‐measures correlations, and grouped 10‐fold cross‐validation. Mortality predictors were assessed with time‐varying Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsESC was strongly inversely associated with UMSARS 1 and 2 (β = −0.58 to −0.65; r = −0.67 to −0.75; all p < 0.0001), whereas associations with orthostatic BP, autonomic symptom scales, and disease duration were weaker or inconsistent. ESC declined significantly over time (feet −6.88 ± 0.60 μS/year; hands −5.41 ± 0.46 μS/year; both p < 0.001), paralleling UMSARS progression. ESC outperformed orthostatic BP drops in predicting UMSARS scores in mixed‐effects models and cross‐validation. Higher ESC independently predicted lower mortality, while higher UMSARS scores and greater orthostatic BP drops predicted increased risk. Median survival was 46.2 months.ConclusionsESC is a sensitive biomarker of disease severity, progression, and survival in MSA, substantially outperforming orthostatic BP measures. Its simplicity and prognostic value support incorporation into routine monitoring and clinical trials.Keywords: autonomic failure, biomarkers, electrochemical skin conductance, multiple system atrophy, sudomotor dysfunction

Introduction

Multiple system atrophy (MSA) is a rare and rapidly progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by cerebellar ataxia and/or poorly levodopa‐responsive parkinsonism together with autonomic failure. Autonomic impairment is a core diagnostic feature in current criteria [1, 2] and reflects degeneration of preganglionic autonomic neurons within the brainstem and spinal cord [3]. While orthostatic hypotension (OH), urinary dysfunction, and other cardiovascular or urogenital symptoms are well‐established manifestations of autonomic failure in MSA, sudomotor dysfunction is increasingly recognized as an almost universal feature of the disease [4]. Quantitative assessment of sweating, particularly when combined with cardiovascular autonomic testing, has been shown to improve diagnostic differentiation between MSA and other synucleinopathies such as Parkinson's disease with autonomic failure [5].

Electrochemical skin conductance (ESC) offers a rapid, noninvasive, and reproducible evaluation of sudomotor function based on the electrochemical reaction between sweat chloride and metal electrodes. Previous studies have reported significant cross‐sectional associations between ESC and motor, neuronal, or autonomic impairment in neurological disease including MSA and amyloidosis [6, 7], suggesting its potential as a clinically meaningful biomarker. However, despite these encouraging findings, ESC is not yet widely accepted or validated as a reliable marker of disease severity or progression. Key gaps remain: prior studies have typically involved modest sample sizes, limited longitudinal follow‐up, narrow statistical approaches, and minimal comparison with established autonomic markers such as orthostatic BP responses.

In this study, we sought to rigorously evaluate the performance of ESC in MSA by leveraging a large, well‐characterized cohort with repeated measurements over several years. Our objectives were fourfold: (1) to characterize cross‐sectional associations between ESC and clinical severity across motor, autonomic, and cardiovascular domains; (2) to quantify the longitudinal trajectory of ESC and compare it with the progression of established clinical scales; (3) to formally compare the predictive value of ESC versus orthostatic BP drops for estimating UMSARS severity using mixed‐effects modeling and cross‐validated prediction; and (4) to evaluate the prognostic value of ESC versus orthostatic BP and the UMSARS when it comes to patient survival. Leveraging a more comprehensive longitudinal analysis, we aimed to determine whether ESC provides robust, clinically meaningful information that could support its broader adoption as a biomarker in MSA.

Methods

Study Population

A total of 175 patients followed at the Toulouse French MSA reference center were included. Patients had to meet the criteria of probable/possible MSA [1] and to have at ≥ 1 ESC evaluation [1, 2]. All patients have provided written informed consent and the study was carried out in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. All patients are part of a longitudinal prospective study with continuous enrollment registered with the Commission Nationale Informatique et Liberté 1338780, CCTIRS 10.065. The follow‐up was carried out up to July 2025, at which point patients were censored if not dead. Evaluations took place from July 7, 2008 to March 28, 2022, those with SUDOSCAN data from July 31, 2012 to May 16, 2022, and 85% of patients have died at the end of the follow‐up window on July 10, 2025 (Table 1). Regarding anticholinergic drugs which may interfere with sudomotor control, 7 (4%), 17 (10%), or 23 (13%) of patients were treated with Trospium chloride, Fesoterodine, or Solifenacin, respectively.

Clinical, Cardiovascular, and Electrochemical Evaluation

Disease severity clinical assessment was performed using the Unified MSA Rating Scale (UMSARS), SCOPA‐AUT, and COMPASS 31 scales [8, 9, 10]. Blood pressure (BP) was measured in the supine position and with the patients standing every minute for 10 min to detect orthostatic hypotension (OH) [11]. The maximum percentage difference between the supine systolic/diastolic BP and standing systolic/diastolic BP (divided by the supine systolic/diastolic BP, % ΔBP) was calculated. Measurement of ESC involves an evaluation of sweat gland function based on the established principles of the electrochemical reaction between chloride (present in sweat) and nickel (which is a component of the stainless‐steel electrodes used in SUDOSCAN). A low DC voltage ≤ 4 V is applied, generating a current proportional to the flow of chloride (ions) supplied by the sweat glands and ducts. ESC, expressed in microSiemens (μS), was calculated for each hand and foot based on the generated current and the applied voltage. The device consists of two sets of electrodes for the feet and hands connected to a computer for recording and data management. No subject preparation is required for this test, which lasts 3 min. The detailed description of the method employed can be found in the following reference [12]. ESC was assessed as the mean ESC of the feet and hands separately using Sudoscan (Impeto medical, Paris, France). Abnormal hands and feet ESC thresholds were defined as < 60 μS for the hands or < 70 μS for the feet, as previously established using a cohort of healthy subjects [12].

Statistical Analysis

Clinical Associations With ESC

To evaluate the individual associations/correlations between ESC measurements and different clinical scores, both repeated‐measures correlations and linear mixed models using both raw scaled data and ranked scaled data were performed using the rmcorr and lme4 packages, respectively [13, 14]. Fixed effects significance was assessed using t‐tests with Satterthwaite's degrees of freedom approximation (to obtain p values) [15].

Clinical Progression Over Time

To assess whether different clinical scores progressed over time, we modeled each score as a function of elapsed time (in years, to obtain an annualized rate of change) using linear mixed‐effects models with a random intercept for each participant using the lme4 package [14]. Fixed effects significance was assessed using t‐tests with Satterthwaite's degrees of freedom approximation (to obtain p values) [15]. In addition, repeated‐measures correlation analyses were carried out to confirm any associations within individuals using the rmcorr package [13].

Comparison of ESC vs. Blood Pressure Drops in Predicting the UMSARS 1 and 2

To compare the ESC measurements versus BP drops when it comes to their power at capturing the UMSARS, linear mixed‐effects models were employed using the lme4 package [14]. Two different models were constructed for each UMSARS score (1 or 2) using two sets of predictors. The ESC model included the average ESC measurements of the feet and hands as fixed effects. The BP model included SBP and DBP values as fixed effects. Both models incorporated a random intercept for each participant to account for within‐subject correlations due to repeated measurements or clustering. A combined model containing all four predictors (ESC feet, ESC hands, SBP, and DBP) was also fitted to assess the incremental explanatory value of each predictor set. Models were fitted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Marginal and conditional R 2 values were calculated using the MuMIn package to quantify the proportion of variance explained by fixed effects alone (marginal R 2) and by the full model including random effects (conditional R 2) [16]. Fixed effects significance was assessed using t‐tests with Satterthwaite's degrees of freedom approximation (to obtain p values) [15]. Likelihood ratio tests (using ANOVA) compared nested models (BP model vs. combined, ESC model vs. combined) fitted with maximum likelihood (ML) estimation to determine whether adding predictors significantly improved model fit.

To further compare the predictive performance of ESC measurements versus orthostatic BP drops for estimating disease severity, we performed a 10‐fold grouped cross‐validation analysis. The outcome variables were UMSARS 1 and 2 scores, and three predictive models were tested for each outcome: ESC model (included ESC from the feet and hands as fixed effects), BP model (included the maximum percentage drops in SBP and DBP as fixed effects), and combined model (included both ESC and BP predictors). Linear mixed‐effects models were fitted with a random intercept for participant to account for within‐subject correlation due to repeated measurements. Cross‐validation folds were defined at the participant level to ensure that all measurements from a given individual were included exclusively in either the training or test set for each fold (grouped k‐fold CV). For each fold, models were trained on 90% of the participants and tested on the remaining 10% [17]. Predictive performance was assessed using the root mean square error (RMSE) and the coefficient of determination (R 2) between predicted and observed values. The average RMSE and R 2 across folds were calculated for each model.

Survival Analysis

We performed survival analysis with the time‐to‐event defined as the interval from the date of first consultation to death or last follow‐up. Kaplan–Meier (KM) curves were generated to estimate overall survival probabilities, and median survival times were reported with 95% confidence intervals. Survival curves were visualized using the survminer package [18].

Time‐Varying Cox Proportional Hazards Analysis

The data were structured in start‐stop format, where each row represented a time interval with updated covariate values. The covariates of interest included ESC (hands and feet), UMSARS scores (1 and 2), and percentage drops in SBP and DBP. For interpretability, all variables were scaled per 10‐point increment. Time‐varying Cox models were fit using the survival package, with clustering by patient identifier to account for repeated measures within individuals [19]. Robust standard errors were calculated to adjust for intra‐individual correlation. Hazard ratios (HRs) represent the instantaneous relative change in the risk of death associated with a one‐unit change in the covariate during the follow‐up period. Model assumptions, including proportional hazards, were checked using Schoenfeld residuals. Forest plots were generated to visualize the HRs and 95% confidence intervals for all covariates derived from the time‐varying Cox models.

All analyses were performed in R 4.3 [20].

Results

A total of 175 patients were included in this study (27% MSA‐C|73% MSA‐P, 82% MSA‐Probable|18% MSA‐Possible, 43% Male|57% Female), with an average of 2.8 (±2.11) evaluations. Patients had, on average, 4.2 (±2.1) years of disease duration at baseline.

Clinical Associations Between ESC and Clinical Measures

First, we examined the associations of Hands and Feet ESC with a range of clinical scores and autonomic parameters using linear mixed‐effects models (scaled raw and rank‐transformed data) and repeated measures correlation (Table 2). Strong associations were observed with global motor impairment. Namely, both the UMSARS 1 and UMSARS 2 total scores showed strong negative associations with ESC feet (β = −0.65 and −0.60, both p < 0.0001) and Hands (β = −0.64 and −0.58, both p < 0.0001). Repeated‐measures correlations confirmed large negative relationships (r feet = −0.72 and −0.75, r hands = −0.67 and −0.72; all p < 0.0001). Several UMSARS 1 item scores (urinary, sexual, gastrointestinal) were also significantly and negatively associated with ESC in all modeling approaches, with moderate correlation magnitudes (r = −0.28 to −0.38, all p < 0.0001). Note that the orthostatic item score showed no significant association (Table 2).

BP measures correlated modestly with ESC. Supine SBP and DBP were weakly but significantly negatively associated with ESC values (β = −0.17, p < 0.01, r = −0.23, p < 0.01). Likewise, orthostatic BP drops (minimum Δ % SBP and Δ % DBP) showed positive associations with both ESC feet and hands (β = 0.13–0.22, p < 0.01, r = 0.14–0.24, p < 0.05). Heart rate changes were not associated with ESC measures (Table 2). No consistent relationship was found between ESC and disease duration or autonomic symptom questionnaires (SCOPA‐AUT, COMPASS31), except for a small negative association with COMPASS31 secretomotor for ESC Feet (r = −0.20, p = 0.046), which did not remain consistent across models. Overall, ESC values most strongly associated with objective motor impairment (UMSARS scores) and to a lesser extent with BP measures, but not with self‐reported autonomic symptom burden or disease duration (Table 2).

The presence of anticholinergic drugs did not present statistically significant associations with the ESC scores themselves and failed to modify the strength of association between ESC scores and other clinically measurements to any appreciable extent (2%–10%).

Using the previously established cutoffs for “abnormal” ESC recordings, 70%/64% of patients had abnormal hand and feet ESC at the first visit, respectively, and 80%/82% of patients had abnormal hand and feet ESC at the last visit, respectively.

Clinical Progression Over Time

We evaluated the annualized progression of autonomic (Table 3, Figure 1). To begin with, changes in BP and heart rate parameters were not significant in mixed models. Specifically, the supine‐to‐standing percent drops in SBP (0.07% ± 0.34%, p = 0.834), DBP (−0.25% ± 0.36%, p = 0.479), and HR (0.36% ± 0.42%, p = 0.388) failed to show significant annual trends. Likewise, autonomic symptom scales SCOPA‐AUT and COMPASS31 did not show significant longitudinal change. In contrast, both ESC feet and ESC hands values significantly decreased over time, with annualized changes of −6.88 ± 0.60 μS/year (p < 0.0001) and −5.41 ± 0.46 μS/year (p < 0.0001), respectively. These decreases were supported by significant negative repeated measures correlations (r = −0.69 and −0.68, both p < 0.0001), indicating consistent within‐subject decline. Likewise, both the UMSARS 1 and 2 motor scores showed significant progression, increasing annually by 3.19 units/year (p < 0.0001) and 3.24 units/year (p < 0.0001), respectively. This progression was significantly higher the first year, closer to 8 units/year. Repeated measures correlations further confirmed strong positive associations (r = 0.85 and 0.83, both p < 0.0001), consistent with symptoms worsening over time. Overall, these results highlight significant decline in sudomotor function (ESC) and motor impairment (UMSARS), while BP and autonomic symptom scores remained largely stable throughout follow‐up (Table 3, Figure 1).

Comparison of ESC vs. BP Drops in Predicting the UMSARS 1 and 2

We compared the predictive ability of ESC from hands and feet versus the predictive ability of SBP and DBP percent drops in relation to the UMSARS 1 scores using linear mixed‐effects models with participant ID as a random intercept (Figure 1). The ESC model (ESC feet + ESC hands) explained substantially more variance in UMSARS 1 scores (marginal R 2 = 0.45, conditional R 2 = 0.77) than the BP model (SBP + DBP, marginal R 2 = 0.05, conditional R 2 = 0.32). In the ESC model, both ESC feet (β = −0.15 ± 0.02, p < 0.001) and ESC hands (β = −0.13 ± 0.02, p < 0.001) were significant predictors, with lower conductance associated with higher UMSARS 1 scores. In the BP model, neither the SBP (β = −0.07 ± 0.06, p = 0.200) nor the DBP 2 (β = −0.09 ± 0.06, p = 0.140) had significant associations with the observed UMSARS 1. Likelihood ratio testing showed that adding ESC to the BP model greatly improved model fit (χ 2 (2) = 199.3, p < 0.001), whereas adding BP to the ESC model did not (χ 2 (2) = 2.6, p = 0.280). These results indicate that ESC measures (hands and feet combined) are markedly more informative for predicting UMSARS 1 scores than orthostatic BP percent drops.

The same pattern was observed when comparing the predictive ability of ESC from hands and feet versus the predictive ability of SBP and DBP BP percent drops in relation to the UMSARS 2 scores using linear mixed‐effects models with participant id as a random intercept. The ESC model (ESC feet + ESC hands) explained substantially more variance in UMSARS 2 scores (marginal R 2 = 0.39, conditional R 2 = 0.72) than the BP model (SBP + DBP, marginal R 2 = 0.02, conditional R 2 = 0.34). In the ESC model, both ESC feet (β = −0.16 ± 0.02, p < 0.001) and ESC hands (β = −0.12 ± 0.03, p < 0.001) were significant predictors, with lower conductance associated with higher UMSARS 2 scores. In the BP model, SBP was not a significant predictor (β = 0.04 ± 0.06, p = 0.530), whereas DBP had a small negative association with UMSARS 2 (β = −0.13 ± 0.06, p = 0.041). Likelihood ratio testing showed that adding ESC to the BP model greatly improved model fit (χ2 (2) = 170.3, p < 0.001), whereas adding BP to the ESC model did not (χ 2 (2) = 2.5, p = 0.285). These results indicate that ESC measures (hands and feet combined) are markedly more informative for predicting UMSARS 2 scores than orthostatic BP percent drops (Figure 1).

In 10‐fold grouped cross‐validation for both the UMSARS 1 and 2, the ESC model achieved the highest predictive performance (mean R 2 = 0.394, mean RMSE = 6.51 for the UMSARS 1 and mean R 2 = 0.377, mean RMSE = 7.04 for the UMSARS 2), followed by the combined model (mean R 2 = 0.396, mean RMSE = 6.53 for the UMSARS 1 and mean R 2 = 0.370, mean RMSE = 7.08 for the UMSARS 2), and the BP model (mean R 2 = 0.106, mean RMSE = 7.97 for the UMSARS 1 and mean R 2 = 0.068, mean RMSE = 8.77 for the UMSARS 2). The combined model did not materially improve over the ESC model, while both models substantially outperformed the BP model in terms of explained variance and prediction error. These findings confirm that ESC measures provide markedly better out‐of‐sample predictions of UMSARS scores than orthostatic BP percent drops.

Survival Analysis and Its Predictors

Among the 175 patients included in the analysis, 149 deaths occurred during the extended follow‐up. The median overall survival from the moment of first evaluation was 46.2 months (95% CI 40.5–52.2 months, Figure 2). We next examined the association between time‐updated covariates and mortality using time‐varying Cox models. Forest plots summarizing the HRs and 95% confidence intervals for ESC (hands and feet), UMSARS 1 and 2, and percentage drops in SBP and DBP are shown in Figure 2. Both ESC feet and hands were inversely associated with the instantaneous risk of death. A 10‐point increase in ESC feet corresponded to a HR of 0.88 (95% CI 0.82–0.94, p < 0.001), while a 10‐point increase in ESC hands had an HR of 0.86 (95% CI 0.80–0.93, p = 0.00025), indicating that higher ESC values were protective. In turn, higher UMSARS scores were associated with increased mortality. For a 10‐point increase in UMSARS 1, the HR was 1.57 (95% CI 1.29–1.90, p < 0.001), and for UMSARS 2, the HR was 1.56 (95% CI 1.31–1.86, p < 0.001), reflecting a higher instantaneous risk of death with greater disease severity. To a smaller extent, greater time‐varying reductions in BP were also associated with lower survival. An attenuation of 10% in the DBP drop had an HR of 0.81 (95% CI 0.70–0.93, p = 0.003), while a 10% attenuation in SBP drop had an HR of 0.82 (95% CI 0.70–0.96, p = 0.012). Overall, the time‐varying analysis highlights that both disease severity (UMSARS) and peripheral autonomic dysfunction (most notably ESC, but also BP drops to a smaller extent) are dynamically associated with significant changes in the instantaneous risk of death over time.

Discussion

In this large longitudinal cohort of 175 MSA patients, we provide the most comprehensive evaluation to date of sudomotor dysfunction assessed by ESC and its relationship to clinical severity, disease progression, autonomic function, and survival. Even though many patients do not report symptoms of anhidrosis, objective sweat dysfunction is a highly prevalent and early feature of the disease. Our findings reinforce previous studies showing both pre and post‐ganglionic sudomotor impairment in MSA using QSART and thermoregulatory sweat testing [4], as well as structural small‐fiber loss demonstrated on skin biopsy in early MSA‐P [21].

From a purely methodological viewpoint, the potential integration of ESC into multicenter studies requires consideration of measurement standardization and reproducibility. ESC acquisition using Sudoscan is rapid, non‐invasive, operator‐independent, and reproducible, supporting its scalability across sites. Importantly, reproducibility has been formally evaluated in dedicated studies. An accuracy study conducted according to ISO 5725‐2 standards (Accuracy (trueness and precision) of measurement methods and results) demonstrated low coefficients of variation (~3%–4% in healthy subjects and ~6%–7% in patients with diabetes) and high intraclass correlation coefficients (0.80–0.95) across repeated measurements and multiple devices. In addition, real‐world clinical data have shown strong inter‐device correlations (r = ~0.85–0.93), and repeatability analyses report low intra‐sample variability (~2–3 μS), supporting the robustness of the technique. These values are comparable to or better than those reported for other small fiber neuropathy assessment methods [22, 23, 24]. Together, these data support the reproducibility and scalability of ESC measurements across centers.

The bottom line of this study is the strong association between ESC and global disease severity. Both feet and hand ESC values were robustly correlated with UMSARS 1 and 2, with effect sizes (β −0.6) and repeated‐measures correlations (r = −0.7 to −0.75) that surpass most alternative autonomic markers. Accordingly, these relationships were significantly stronger than those observed for BP measures, autonomic questionnaires, or disease duration. ESC showed moderate associations with selected autonomic item scores (urinary, sexual, bowel), but not with the orthostatic item score—consistent with sudomotor dysfunction reflecting a distinct autonomic domain. Overall, ESC captures a core aspect of MSA pathophysiology that closely tracks motor impairment and overall disease burden.

Longitudinal analyses have likewise underscored the prognostic value of ESC as a progression marker. Over an average follow‐up of more than 4 years, ESC values declined steadily at clinically meaningful rates (−6.9 μS/year for feet and −5.4 μS/year for hands), with strong within‐individual consistency. In contrast, neither orthostatic BP drops nor autonomic questionnaires (SCOPA‐AUT, COMPASS31) showed significant annual changes, despite their currently accepted role in MSA diagnosis and monitoring. These findings align with earlier QSART studies reporting progressive sudomotor impairment in MSA, but our larger sample and denser follow‐up provide clearer evidence that ESC declines closely in parallel with motor disease progression, as reflected by the substantial yearly increases in UMSARS scores. Importantly, the stability of BP and questionnaire‐based measures suggests that ESC may be more sensitive to early and continuous autonomic deterioration than traditional autonomic assessment tools.

This study directly compared the predictive ability of ESC with orthostatic BP drops, the most widely used autonomic marker in MSA. ESC outperformed BP drops in estimating both UMSARS 1 and 2 scores. ESC explained much more variance than SBP/DBP drops, and adding BP variables did not improve prediction beyond ESC alone. ESC models had markedly higher predictive accuracy and lower error than BP models. These findings provide strong evidence that ESC captures disease severity more reliably and consistently than orthostatic BP change, which has traditionally been considered a hallmark measure of autonomic failure in MSA.

We also demonstrate that ESC has significant prognostic value. Higher ESC values were independently associated with reduced mortality risk, with HR of 0.86–0.88 per 10 μS increase. These protective effects were comparable in magnitude to the adverse impact of increasing UMSARS scores, a well‐established predictor of survival. BP drops were also associated with mortality, although to a smaller degree, as previously demonstrated [25].

The strengths of this work include the large cohort size, the long follow‐up period, the use of repeated measures with longitudinal modeling, the direct comparison of ESC with BP metrics, and the integration of survival analyses with time‐updated physiological data. Together, these approaches allowed us to evaluate ESC from complementary angles—cross‐sectional, longitudinal, predictive, and prognostic—providing a rigorous assessment. However, not all patients had the same number of ESC evaluations, which may introduce trajectory heterogeneity. Also, pathological confirmation was unavailable. Furthermore, ESC does not localize the level of sudomotor dysfunction (preganglionic vs. postganglionic), and comparisons with gold‐standard sudomotor autonomic tests such as QSART or TST were not available.

Taken together, these findings support ESC as a promising candidate biomarker for use in clinical trials. Its strong association with disease severity, sensitivity to longitudinal change, and prognostic value are very useful attributes for a progression (and perhaps response) biomarker. In particular, the continuous decline in ESC and its within‐subject consistency suggest that it may detect subtle disease changes over shorter time intervals than currently used clinical scales. In this context, an important question is how ESC fits relative to established clinical endpoints such as UMSARS. While UMSARS remains the gold standard for assessing clinical severity, it is subject to inter‐rater variability and may be less sensitive to short‐term change. In contrast, ESC provides an objective, quantitative measure with minimal operator dependence. The strength of its association with UMSARS observed here suggests that it captures overlapping but not identical aspects of disease biology, raising the possibility that ESC could complement (but certainly not replace) clinical scales by providing a more sensitive physiological readout of disease progression. Nonetheless, we have herein observed stronger standardized responses at the level of the UMSARS relative to ESC, reinforcing that the current gold standard remains the most sensitive available “individual” marker. Furthermore, whether ESC is responsive to MSA‐centered therapeutic interventions remains to be studied. In addition, formal validation studies will be required to establish its analytical validity, clinical validity, and clinical utility in accordance with biomarker qualification frameworks. If validated as a trial endpoint, ESC could have important implications for study design. Biomarkers with lower variability and higher sensitivity to change can reduce required sample sizes and study duration. The relatively consistent longitudinal decline observed in ESC suggests that it may offer advantages in statistical power compared to more variable autonomic or questionnaire‐based measures, although this would need to be formally evaluated in trial simulations.

Conclusion

ESC is a sensitive, dynamic, and clinically meaningful biomarker in MSA. ESC correlates closely with disease severity, declines steadily over time, outperforms orthostatic BP drops in predicting motor impairment, and is independently associated with survival. Given its simplicity, noninvasiveness, and suitability for repeated assessments, ESC represents a valuable tool for clinical monitoring and a promising candidate biomarker for future therapeutic trials in MSA.
