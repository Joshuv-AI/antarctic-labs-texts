ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

The finding of a thrombus-in-transit (TIT) with impending paradoxical embolism (IPE) through a patent foramen ovale (PFO) is extremely rare. Even with modern echocardiographic techniques, most published articles comprise only a case report or small case series.1 Treatment of a TIT is controversial given the lack of prospective randomized trials, with therapeutic options ranging from emergent thoracotomy with thrombectomy and surgical PFO closure, anticoagulation alone, systemic fibrinolysis, or, more recently, percutaneous thrombectomy (Figure 1).2 Cardiac surgery is generally considered the preferred treatment in hemodynamically stable patients, as observational studies have demonstrated lower rates of post-treatment embolic events and mortality. Anticoagulation alone is primarily used in patients with important comorbidities and high surgical risk, while fibrinolysis is reserved for poor surgical candidates, hemodynamically unstable patients with high-risk pulmonary embolism, or for cases of acute ischemic stroke, as it may cause TIT fragmentation and IPE (in up to 23.5% of patients).2,3 More recently, some case reports have described the use of percutaneous thrombectomy with employment of embolic protection devices for the treatment of TIT.4, 5, 6, 7Figure 1Therapeutic Approach to Thrombus in Transit With Impending Paradoxical Embolism

The Current Case

Kam et al8 report the first successful case of simultaneous percutaneous thrombectomy of TIT with IPE through a PFO and high-risk pulmonary embolism using the AlphaVac F1885 system (AngioDynamics) under intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) guidance, in a 64-year-old woman recently diagnosed with metastatic pancreatic cancer. The patient was deemed to have prohibitive surgical risk and contraindications to systemic fibrinolysis.

This article is notable for describing the feasibility of simultaneous percutaneous treatment of TIT and high-risk pulmonary embolism, whereas other published articles have generally focused on percutaneous treatment of TIT alone.

Furthermore, the procedure is guided by ICE instead of transesophageal echocardiography (TEE). The choice of ICE was well considered, as it can be performed without general anesthesia or deep sedation, which is particularly advantageous in hemodynamically unstable patients. Furthermore, although TEE is associated with a low complication rate, esophageal intubation can induce vagal and sympathetic reflexes, with blood pressure and heart rate fluctuations that may cause the mobilization of any intracardiac material, with subsequent embolization.9 The main limitation of ICE is excessive catheter manipulation in the right atrium, but this risk can be minimized by direct visualization of the thrombus, avoiding direct contact.8

Conclusions

As demonstrated by the Kam et al8 paper, percutaneous techniques have emerged as a potential alternative treatment in selected cases of TIT with IPE (Figure 1).

The available data suggest an apparently lower morbidity compared with cardiac surgery and systemic fibrinolysis, particularly regarding bleeding risk, which represents a central aspect in the therapeutic decision-making process for these frequently unstable patients.2,10 The main risks associated with these techniques relate to the possibility of systemic embolization during intracardiac manipulation. However, this risk can be significantly mitigated using embolic protection devices, reinforcing the safety of the procedure when performed in experienced centers.6, 7, 8 Owing to the limited evidence and absence of comparative trials, their use is not yet widespread and remains confined to centers with specific experience. Nevertheless, these techniques are based on a solid pathophysiological rationale, allowing mechanical thrombus removal and immediate reduction of thrombotic burden, while simultaneously avoiding some of the risks associated with systemic therapies. Thus, with increasing clinical experience and ongoing device development, these approaches may move closer to first-line therapy in carefully selected patients.
