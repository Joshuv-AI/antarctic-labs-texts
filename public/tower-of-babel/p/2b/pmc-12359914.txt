Global, regional, and national epidemiology of type 1 diabetes in children from 1990 to 2021: trend and health inequality analyses based on the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021
Authors: Jing Xie, Weilan Li, Xiaomin Li, Xueli Zhang, Jing Liu, Zeye Liu, Shenqi Jing, Hua Shao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundType 1 diabetes (T1D) is a significant health challenge for children. Assessing the epidemiological patterns and health inequalities associated with T1D at the global, regional, and national levels, along with future projections, is essential for informing equitable public health policies.MethodsBased on the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 database, this study examined trends in the incidence, prevalence, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), and mortality of T1D in children aged 0–14 years. Attribution decomposition analysis was used to explore the main drivers of the changes in the T1D burden, including the effects of population growth, epidemiological changes, and fertility rates. Regression models and concentration curves were employed to evaluate health inequality among countries and regions with different sociodemographic index (SDI) levels, and predictive models were used to estimate future trends of T1D over the next 30 years.ResultsFrom 1990 to 2021, the global incidence and prevalence of T1D increased significantly, by 32.04% and 41.47%, respectively, whereas the global DALY and mortality rates decreased by 18.33% and 24.83%, respectively. Attribution decomposition analysis revealed that population growth and epidemiological improvements were the main drivers of the changes in the T1D burden. DALYs and mortality decreased significantly in high-SDI countries, whereas health inequality remained severe in low-SDI countries. The predictive models indicate that the incidence and prevalence of T1D among children worldwide will continue to rise in the coming decades, particularly among males. By 2050, DALYs and mortality are expected to decline further.ConclusionAlthough the global T1D burden has increased, advances in medical technology, especially in high-SDI countries, have alleviated its health impacts. To reduce global health inequality, low-SDI countries must strengthen international cooperation by improving T1D prevention and control through resource sharing and the introduction of medical technologies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01905-3.

Introduction

Type 1 diabetes (T1D) is an autoimmune disease characterized by pancreatic β-cell destruction that primarily affects children and adolescents [1]. Although the exact aetiology of T1D has not been fully elucidated, the interplay between genetic susceptibility and environmental factors is considered to be the main driving force behind its onset [2]. Recently, the incidence of T1D, particularly among children and adolescents, has increased worldwide because of lifestyle changes and environmental stress, which poses significant public health challenges and imposes long-term burdens on patients and families [3].

The epidemiological characteristics of T1D exhibit significant geographical and socioeconomic disparities worldwide. High-income countries have higher diagnosis rates and lower mortality rates than low-income countries do, reflecting advanced health care systems and awareness [4]. Conversely, low- and middle-income countries face challenges, including the scarcity of medical resources, inadequate diagnostic capabilities, and insufficient patient awareness of the disease, leading to increased mortality and reduced quality of life for children with T1D, highlighting the significant health inequities present globally [5]. Moreover, rapid global socioeconomic development and urbanization have significantly increased the incidence of T1D globally [6]. Factors such as urbanization, industrialization, and modern lifestyles contribute to this trend, with altered diets, reduced physical activity, and changing infection patterns potentially increasing risk by affecting immune function and genetic susceptibility [7]. Despite its global increasing trend, the incidence of T1D shows marked regional variation. For example, the incidence remains consistently high in high-income regions such as Nordic countries, North America, and Australia, whereas it is relatively low in low- and middle-income regions such as Sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Southeast Asia [5]. However, its incidence is now increasing rapidly in some low- and middle-income countries, a phenomenon that has attracted widespread attention [8]. Addressing global T1D challenges requires analysing health inequities, which reflect socioeconomic imbalances and the impact of health care resources, policies, and sociocultural factors on disease control [9]. By identifying these inequities, we can develop more targeted public health strategies, optimize medical resource allocation, and improve the diagnosis and treatment levels of T1D globally, ultimately reducing the disease burden and improving the quality of life for patients.

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study is one of the most comprehensive and systematic projects currently available [10]. Through the GBD study, we can obtain detailed information on the incidence, prevalence, and mortality of T1D among children and disparities across different regions and countries. Additionally, this study provides a powerful tool for analysing health inequities and investigating the impact of socioeconomic and environmental factors on T1D.

Therefore, we utilized the latest data from GBD 2021 to systematically analyse global, regional, and national trends in the incidence and health inequities of childhood T1D from 1990 to 2021, aiming to reveal global patterns and trends; explore the impact of socioeconomic, geographical, and environmental factors on incidence and mortality; and assess health inequities, ultimately providing scientific evidence for global public health decision-making and contributing to the development of more precise and effective disease prevention and control strategies.

Methods

Data sources

The GBD is a large-scale epidemiological study conducted by research teams worldwide that aims to systematically assess the impact of various diseases, injuries, and risk factors on human health at both global and regional levels [10]. The study utilizes multiple data sources, including data on incidence, prevalence, mortality, disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), as well as on risk factors, to quantify the burden of various health issues over specific periods globally and regionally. In GBD research, data can be accessed through the GBD Compare website, and all input data can be identified via the Global Health Data Exchange website (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool). In this study, data files from GBD 2021 that contained health-related data across countries and regions from 1990 to 2021 were downloaded using the GBD Results Tool. The study used approved GBD protocols (contact ID: 0034o00001nHH4NAAW) and followed the GATHER guidelines.

Study design

This study, based on GBD 2021, aims to analyze the global, regional, and national epidemiological characteristics of T1D in children aged 0–14 years from 1990 to 2021. The study adopts a retrospective observational design and analyzes data related to T1D from the GBD database to assess trends in prevalence, incidence, mortality, and DALYs in children with T1D across time periods, regions, and countries, while also evaluating health inequalities and projecting epidemiological trends for the next 30 years.

Data definitions

The following are definitions of key data indicators used in this paper to measure specific conditions of health status and disease burden: (1) Prevalence: The number of children with T1D per 100,000 population during a specific period. (2) Incidence: The number of newly diagnosed T1D cases among children per 100,000 population during a specific period. (3) Mortality: The number of deaths caused by T1D among children per 100,000 population during a specific period. (4) DALYs: The years of healthy life lost due to T1D, including years lost due to premature death and years lived with disability caused by the disease [11]. (5) Percentage change in epidemiological rates: The percentage change in specific health indicators (such as incidence, mortality, prevalence, and DALYs) over a particular period. (6) Sociodemographic index (SDI): A composite indicator used in GBD to measure the level of social and demographic development in countries or regions, consisting of three components: per capita income, educational attainment, and fertility rate [12]. The SDI score ranges from 0 to 1, indicating development levels from lowest to highest. Based on SDI, countries and regions are categorized into five groups, which includes: the high SDI group, which includes high-income countries such as North America and Western Europe; the high-middle SDI group, including rapidly developing countries like Eastern Europe and East Asia; the middle SDI group, referring to emerging economies at mid-levels of socioeconomic development, such as Latin America and parts of South Asia; the low-middle SDI group, involving regions with lower levels of development, such as Southeast Asia and parts of Africa; and the low SDI group, representing countries with the lowest development levels and the greatest health challenges, mainly concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa and parts of South Asia. (7) Attribution decomposition: A statistical method used to analyze the driving factors behind changes in disease burden, aiming to break down changes in indicators such as incidence, prevalence, mortality, or DALYs into contributions from different factors [13]. Major drivers include population growth, population aging, epidemiological changes (such as medical advancements and improved preventive measures), and fertility changes. Through attribution decomposition, researchers can identify the relative contributions of these factors to changes in disease burden, helping to better understand the most influential drivers and guiding the development of effective public health policies and interventions to reduce global disease burden. (8) Health inequality: The unjust disparities in health status, disease burden, and access to healthcare among different socioeconomic groups, geographic regions, genders, and age groups [13, 14]. Using indicators such as incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALYs, GBD studies employ regression analysis, concentration curves, and concentration indices to quantify and assess these differences, revealing that regions with lower SDI often face higher disease burdens and poorer health outcomes. Health inequality analyses help identify global and regional health disparities and guide the development of public health policies aimed at reducing these inequalities.

Data analysis

All data analyses in this study were conducted using R version 4.1.3, as detailed below: (1) Global trends: Descriptive statistical methods based on aggregated data were used to calculate the global incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and mortality of T1D in 2021. These values were compared with those from 1990, and the percentage change was calculated. The corresponding trends across the five SDI quintiles were compared by calculating the percentage change in incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and mortality rates, with 95% uncertainty intervals (UI) provided. (2) Geographical differences and temporal trends: Spatial data analysis was used to illustrate geographical differences and temporal trends. Through temporal trend analysis, the changes in T1D incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and mortality rates from 1990 to 2021 were evaluated across continents and regions, with particular attention to regions showing increasing or decreasing trends. (3) Age distribution trends: Stratified analysis was conducted by age group to assess the temporal trends in T1D incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and mortality for different age groups (< 1 year, 2–4 years, 5–9 years, 10–14 years). Linear regression analysis was used to explore age-specific trends globally and within different SDI quintiles, determining whether the trends changed significantly over time. (4) Correlation between epidemiological indicators and SDI: A regression model was used to examine the linear trend relationships of T1D incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and mortality with SDI. (5) Decomposition attribution analysis: Decomposition model-based attribution analysis was used to quantify the contribution of factors such as population growth, epidemiological changes, and fertility rates to changes in T1D incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and mortality. Decomposition methods were applied to quantitatively assess the contribution of different determinants. (6) Health inequality analysis: Regression analysis was used to examine the relationship between health inequality and SDI, exploring the trends in incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and mortality across SDI levels. Concentration curve methods were employed to measure health inequality, and concentration indices were calculated to assess the changes in global health inequality from 1990 to 2021. The slope index of inequality was calculated using a regression model to analyze the relationship between the social and demographic position of countries and their T1D prevalence, incidence, DALYs, and mortality rates. Health inequality concentration index was calculated through the fitting of Lorenz concentration curves and the cumulative relative distribution of T1D prevalence, incidence, DALYs, and mortality across countries, with the area under the curve obtained through numerical integration [14]. (7) Predictive trends: Bayesian age-period-cohort model was used to predict future trends, forecasting changes in T1D incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and mortality rates between 2022 and 2050 based on trends observed from 1990 to 2021. Gender-specific predictive models were also used to estimate the changes in incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and mortality rates for males and females in the coming decades, and gender differences were compared.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically analysed the global, regional, and national trends in childhood T1D disease burden from 1990 to 2021, as well as related health inequality issues. The findings revealed an increasing trend in the incidence and prevalence of T1D worldwide but also a decline in T1D-related DALYs and mortality owing to advancements in medical technology. Significant disparities persist across regions and SDI quintiles, underscoring the need for tailored prevention policies. However, the interpretation and generalization of these findings may be subject to certain limitations, which require further discussion.

First, the global burden of T1D presents a complex trend. From 1990–2021, the global incidence and prevalence of T1D increased by more than 30%, whereas DALYs and mortality declined by more than 18%. These findings suggest that although the global burden of T1D has continued to increase, especially with increasing prevalence among children, improvements in medical care and advancements in treatment have alleviated its overall health impact [15].

Further analysis revealed significant differences in these global trends across SDI countries. High-SDI countries experienced the greatest increases in incidence and prevalence but significant decreases in DALYs and mortality, indicating that these countries have not only excelled in T1D diagnosis and management but also made significant progress in reducing the disease burden [16, 17]. In contrast, low-SDI countries experienced a slight decline in T1D incidence, while the prevalence rose modestly, suggesting that these countries still face substantial challenges in T1D management and prevention. Furthermore, the burden of T1D varied geographically. In 2021, the highest incidence and prevalence of T1D were reported in North America, Northern Europe, and parts of the Middle East, whereas Africa, Central and South America, and parts of Asia reported relatively lower rates. Notably, Sub-Saharan Africa presented the highest DALY and mortality rates, whereas Europe, North America, and Australia presented relatively lower rates. These disparities underscore global health inequities linked to economic and health care resources [18–20]. Generally, a higher SDI is correlated with increasing incidence/prevalence but decreasing DALYs/mortality, demonstrating the benefits of medical advances and early intervention in wealthier nations. Nevertheless, global health inequalities persist and have worsened [8, 21–23]. Regression analysis revealed that the concentration index for DALYs and mortality increased from 1990 to 2021, whereas that for mortality increased from − 0.36–0.43, indicating that health inequality, particularly in low-SDI countries, has become more pronounced in the global T1D burden [8]. Therefore, although the global health outcomes for patients with T1D have improved, the unequal distribution of health resources remains a critical issue for future public health policies [24]. The successful experiences of high-SDI countries offer valuable lessons for others, but the challenges in low-SDI countries underscore the need for global efforts to ensure equitable health care and reduce health inequalities. Additionally, age and sex differences further exacerbate the uneven distribution of the T1D burden [25, 26]. The incidence and prevalence of T1D increased in all paediatric age groups, with the steepest increases in 5- to 14-year-olds. Global DALYs and deaths fell, but the decline varied by sex and age: boys experienced faster increases in incidence/prevalence yet larger reductions in DALYs/mortality than girls did, possibly reflecting biological and sociocultural differences. The study revealed that the incidence and prevalence of T1D increased across all age groups, particularly in the 5–9 and 10–14 age groups, with more rapid increases in males than in females, and that the reductions in DALYs and mortality were more pronounced in males. These sex differences may be related to biological variations and sociocultural factors [4, 26]. These sex disparities highlight the need for sex-specific approaches in T1D epidemiology and targeted public health interventions.

Additionally, this study used decomposition analysis to investigate the main drivers of changes in the incidence, prevalence, DALYs, and mortality of T1D in children globally and across different SDI regions from 1990 to 2021. The results indicate that the global increase in T1D incidence and prevalence was driven primarily by population growth and epidemiological changes, with these factors playing a more significant role in high-SDI countries. In contrast, although the overall incidence and prevalence of T1D were lower in low-SDI countries, epidemiological changes had a relatively greater impact in these regions. The decomposition analysis further identified key drivers of declining global DALYs and mortality. First, improvements in epidemiology have played a crucial role in reducing the health burden of T1D, especially in high- and middle-SDI countries. For example, advancements in disease management, medical interventions, and health policies in these countries have contributed significantly to decreases in T1D-related DALYs and mortality [23]. Additionally, lower fertility rates in high-SDI countries also contributed to a reduction in the health burden of T1D [27]. Conversely, population growth had a more noticeable impact on DALYs and mortality in low-SDI countries, indicating that these regions continue to face substantial challenges in controlling T1D [28, 29]. Particularly among children, population growth could further exacerbate the disease burden in these areas. The significance of this decomposition analysis lies in its ability to uncover the complexity of T1D burden changes and provide policy-makers with specific intervention directions. For example, global policies should tailor targeted responses to the specific drivers of T1D burden in different regions and countries. In high-SDI countries, where the T1D incidence is increasing, further optimizing disease management strategies could help mitigate the health impact. In low-SDI countries, controlling population growth and improving epidemiological conditions are key to reducing the T1D burden.

Moreover, according to the predictive model, the incidence and prevalence of T1D in children worldwide are expected to continue rising over the next few decades, particularly among males. In high-incidence countries like Finland and Sweden, the incidence rate is projected to continue rising through 2050. Several factors contribute to this trend, including genetic susceptibility [30], environmental factors [31, 32], and demographic changes, underscoring the need to develop and strengthen long-term prevention and control strategies in these regions. By 2050, these projected increases will be largely driven by population growth, accelerated urbanization, and lifestyle changes [3, 33]. However, despite increasing incidence and prevalence, the T1D-associated DALYs and mortality are expected to continue to decline significantly due to advances in medical technology worldwide. This decline is expected to be most pronounced in high-SDI and middle-high-SDI countries, which have superior health care infrastructure, advanced diagnostic technologies, and more comprehensive disease management systems. For example, high-SDI countries have made significant progress in early diagnosis, insulin therapy, and the prevention of complications related to T1D in children [16, 21, 34]. In contrast, the incidence and prevalence in low-SDI countries are also expected to increase, but the extent of the decline in DALYs and mortality may be smaller. This trend reflects the challenges these countries face in controlling T1D, including limited health care resources, weak infrastructure, and insufficient health education [16, 19]. Furthermore, as population growth accelerates in low-SDI countries, these regions may face even greater pressure to prevent and control T1D. To address this challenge, low-SDI countries need to strengthen international cooperation by introducing advanced medical technologies and knowledge to enhance diagnosis and management capabilities for T1D.

Although this study revealed global trends in childhood T1D and related health inequalities from 1990 to 2021, several limitations should be noted. First, the study relied on GBD data, which may suffer from insufficient or uneven reporting in certain regions. In some low-income countries, the lack of reliable epidemiological data may have led to an underestimation of the true burden. Additionally, the GBD methodology relies on various assumptions and modelling techniques, which may introduce some uncertainty in the estimates. Second, the SDI has inherent limitations as a health inequality measure. While it integrates education, income, and fertility to reflect development, it fails to capture key contextual determinants such as cultural norms, health policy efficacy, health care distribution, and T1D awareness. Consequently, sole reliance on SDI may obscure actual health inequality patterns. Third, the concentration index may fail to reflect dynamic health inequalities, particularly in low-SDI settings, where it underestimates persistent disparities stemming from inadequate health care resources and policy gaps. These limitations highlight the need for future research to adopt more sophisticated indicators to evaluate health inequality, including specific analyses of health care access, the impact of health policies, and sociocultural barriers.

Conclusions

This study, by analysing global trends in childhood T1D from 1990–2021, revealed the increasing burden of T1D worldwide, particularly in terms of incidence and prevalence. However, due to advances in medical technology, global DALYs and mortality have declined, especially in high-SDI countries. These findings provide important data to support the development of global public health policies. Furthermore, through decomposition analysis, the study revealed that population growth and epidemiological changes are the primary drivers of the increasing T1D burden, particularly in high-SDI countries. In these countries, epidemiological improvements significantly reduce DALYs and mortality, whereas health inequality issues remain prominent in low-SDI countries, where resource scarcity poses greater challenges to control of T1D. Future public health policies should continue to focus on these health inequalities and promote the equitable distribution of health care resources through enhanced global cooperation to address the challenges posed by the global spread of T1D. Moreover, predictive trends indicate that while the global burden of T1D is expected to continue increasing, particularly among children, strengthening early diagnosis, improving treatment methods, promoting health education, and increasing access to public health services could further reduce the health impact of T1D. Achieving this goal will require concerted efforts from countries worldwide, especially low-SDI countries. Strengthening international cooperation and sharing successful experiences will be key to addressing the global spread of T1D.
