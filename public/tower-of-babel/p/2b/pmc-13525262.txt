Admission lactate-to-hemoglobin ratio and mortality in critically ill patients with type 1 acute myocardial infarction: A mimic-iv database study
Authors: Thanh Luan Nguyen, Thi Bao Yen Nguyen, Thuy Oanh Hoang, Ngoc Nhu Y Nguyen, Vu Thanh Le, Cong Dang Tran
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe lactate-to-hemoglobin ratio (LHBR) integrates information on metabolic stress and oxygen-carrying capacity. This study aimed to evaluate the association between admission LHBR and mortality among critically ill patients with type 1 acute myocardial infarction (AMI).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using the MIMIC-IV database (version 3.1) and included adult patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) with type 1 AMI. LHBR was calculated from the first lactate and hemoglobin levels around ICU admission. The primary outcome was 30-day mortality; secondary outcomes were 60-, 90-, and 180-day mortality. Associations were evaluated using restricted cubic spline analyses, Cox proportional hazards regression models, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses.ResultsA total of 4,651 patients were included and categorized into LHBR tertiles using cutoffs of ≤0.127 (T1), >0.127–0.209 (T2), and >0.209 (T3). Restricted cubic spline analyses showed significant nonlinear associations between LHBR (as well as the natural logarithm-transformed LHBR) and all mortality outcomes (all P-nonlinear <0.05). In the adjusted purposeful selection model, both intermediate (T2) and high (T3) LHBR tertiles were associated with increased 30-day mortality compared with the lowest tertile (T1) (T2 vs T1: HR 1.32, 95% CI 1.07–1.64; T3 vs T1: HR 2.05, 95% CI 1.66–2.52). Comparable findings were observed for 60-day mortality (HRs 1.29 and 1.94), 90-day mortality (HRs 1.30 and 1.89), and 180-day mortality (HRs 1.26 and 1.75), respectively. Admission LHBR demonstrated slightly better discrimination for 30-day mortality than either lactate or hemoglobin alone.ConclusionsHigher admission LHBR was associated with increased short-term and long-term mortality in critically ill patients with type 1 AMI. Further studies are warranted to validate these findings and determine the clinical utility of LHBR in this population.Keywords: acute myocardial infarction, lactate-to-hemoglobin ratio, mortality, prognosis, critical illness

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) remains a major cause of mortality and disability worldwide despite substantial advances in reperfusion strategies, antithrombotic therapies, and intensive cardiovascular care.1,2 Type 1 AMI results from acute atherothrombotic coronary artery occlusion caused by plaque rupture or erosion, leading to abrupt myocardial ischemia and necrosis.3,4 Although outcomes have improved considerably over recent decades, mortality remains high among patients who develop cardiogenic shock, heart failure, or other forms of critical illness requiring intensive care.5–7 Early identification of patients at increased risk of adverse outcomes therefore remains an important component of contemporary AMI management.8,9

Lactate is a widely used biomarker of metabolic stress and circulatory dysfunction. Elevated lactate concentrations are frequently observed in patients with AMI and may arise from systemic hypoperfusion, impaired cardiac output, sympathetic activation, microcirculatory abnormalities, and concomitant critical illness.10,11 Previous studies have consistently demonstrated associations between elevated lactate levels and adverse outcomes in patients with acute coronary syndromes, cardiogenic shock, and critically ill cardiac populations.12–14 However, lactate concentrations are influenced by multiple physiological and pathological processes, and patients with similar lactate levels may differ substantially in their underlying physiological reserve and capacity to tolerate acute illness.15,16

Hemoglobin plays a central role in systemic oxygen transport.17,18 Reduced hemoglobin concentrations decrease arterial oxygen content and may aggravate the imbalance between myocardial oxygen supply and demand during acute ischemia.19,20 Anemia is common among patients with AMI and has been associated with heart failure, recurrent ischemic events, cardiogenic shock, and increased mortality.21–23 Because oxygen delivery depends not only on blood flow but also on oxygen-carrying capacity, hemoglobin may provide clinically relevant information that complements lactate measurements.

From a physiological perspective, lactate and hemoglobin reflect different aspects of the response to AMI. Lactate reflects the metabolic consequences of circulatory stress and impaired tissue oxygen utilization, whereas hemoglobin is directly related to oxygen transport capacity. Combining these parameters into a single index may therefore provide complementary prognostic information. Several recent studies have shown that an elevated lactate-to-hemoglobin ratio (LHBR) is associated with adverse outcomes in critically ill populations.24–26 Despite this biological rationale, evidence regarding the clinical significance of LHBR in patients with AMI remains limited, and its relationship with short-term and long-term mortality has not been adequately characterized. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the association between admission lactate-to-hemoglobin ratio and mortality among critically ill patients with type 1 AMI. We also examined the dose–response relationship between LHBR and mortality and evaluated the consistency of this association across clinically relevant patient subgroups.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV, version 3.1), a large critical care database containing detailed information on patients admitted to Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in Boston, Massachusetts, between 2008 and 2022. 27 The database includes demographic data, diagnostic information, laboratory measurements, physiological variables, treatments, and longitudinal survival outcomes. MIMIC-IV has been approved by the institutional review boards of Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center and the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. In accordance with institutional requirements and applicable national regulations, this retrospective study using fully de-identified patient data was exempt from additional institutional review board approval, and the requirement for informed consent was waived. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement. 28 Database access was granted to Thanh Luan Nguyen after completion of the Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative (CITI) training program (certification ID: 73990653).

Study population and cohort selection

Patients were identified from all ICU admissions recorded in MIMIC-IV. Acute myocardial infarction was defined using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth and Tenth Revision (ICD-9 and ICD-10) diagnosis codes. Eligible ICD-9 codes included 410. x, whereas eligible ICD-10 codes included I21. x and I22. x. To focus on type 1 AMI, admissions coded exclusively as type 2 myocardial infarction (ICD-10 code I21. A1) were excluded. When both type 1 and type 2 AMI codes were present during the same hospitalization, the admission was classified as type 1 AMI and retained in the cohort.

Patients without available baseline lactate or hemoglobin measurements were excluded because calculation of the primary exposure was not possible. To minimize potential distortion of hemoglobin values, patients who received packed red blood cell transfusion before baseline hemoglobin assessment were excluded. Admissions with insufficient information to determine the temporal sequence between transfusion and hemoglobin measurement were also excluded. Because repeated ICU admissions may introduce within-patient correlation, only the first ICU admission for each patient was included. The study was restricted to adults aged 18 years or older at the time of ICU admission.

Exposure definition

The primary exposure was LHBR, calculated as the ratio of serum lactate concentration (mmol/L) to hemoglobin concentration (g/dL). Lactate values were obtained from laboratory records (itemid 50813), and hemoglobin values were extracted from hematology measurements (itemid 51222). For each biomarker, the first available value recorded within the period extending from 12 hours before to 24 hours after ICU admission was selected as the baseline measurement. LHBR was then calculated using these baseline values.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was 30-day all-cause mortality after ICU admission. Secondary outcomes included all-cause mortality at 60, 90, and 180 days. Survival status and follow-up duration were obtained from the longitudinal mortality information available within MIMIC-IV. In-hospital mortality was also reported as a descriptive clinical outcome but was not considered a prespecified endpoint in the time-to-event analyses.

Data extraction

All data management and statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Data were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database through structured query language (SQL) scripts executed in Google BigQuery. Relevant tables were linked using unique patient, hospital admission, and ICU stay identifiers. Variables were derived from the index ICU admission only.

Covariates

Variables considered as potential confounders were selected a priori based on their established associations with mortality in patients with AMI and critical illness. Demographic characteristics included age, sex, and race. Comorbidity burden was assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index together with major chronic conditions, including diabetes mellitus, congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, renal disease, liver disease, and malignancy. Physiological variables were obtained from the first ICU day. Heart rate and respiratory rate were defined as the highest recorded values during this period, whereas systolic, diastolic, and mean arterial pressures were defined as the lowest recorded values. Neurological status was assessed using the lowest Glasgow Coma Scale score. Laboratory parameters included hematologic and biochemical measurements. White blood cell count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, platelet count, and lactate were obtained from the first available measurement recorded within the predefined baseline window extending from 12 hours before to 24 hours after ICU admission. Additional laboratory variables, including bicarbonate, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, glucose, sodium, potassium, and chloride, were extracted from the first ICU day.

Severity of illness was evaluated using the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score and the Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II). Sepsis was identified according to Sepsis-3 criteria, requiring suspected infection and organ dysfunction within the period extending from 24 hours before to 24 hours after ICU admission. Organ-support therapies included invasive mechanical ventilation and vasopressor administration during the first ICU day. Vasopressor burden was categorized according to the number of vasoactive agents administered (0, 1, or ≥2 agents), including norepinephrine, epinephrine, vasopressin, and dopamine. Because myocardial infarction subtype may influence prognosis, AMI was further classified as ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) or non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) according to diagnostic coding information.

Missing data

The extent of missing data was low, with no study variable having more than 1% missing values (Table S1). Extreme values were regarded as potential measurement or data-entry errors and were recoded as missing. Given the negligible proportion of missing observations, continuous variables with missing values were imputed using median values before analysis.

Statistical analysis

Patients were categorized into tertiles according to admission LHBR. Continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR) and were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables are presented as number (percentage) and were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. All analyses were two-sided, and a p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Restricted cubic spline analyses

Restricted cubic spline functions within Cox proportional hazards models were used to characterize the association between LHBR and mortality. LHBR was analyzed as a continuous variable using four knots placed at predefined percentiles of its distribution. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated across the observed range of LHBR, with the median LHBR serving as the reference value. Nonlinearity was assessed using Wald tests. These analyses were performed for all mortality outcomes. Additional restricted cubic spline analyses were performed using the natural logarithm-transformed LHBR [ln (LHBR)] to evaluate the robustness of the observed nonlinear exposure–response relationship.

Survival analyses

For the primary analyses, patients were classified into tertiles according to LHBR, with the lowest tertile used as the reference category. Cox proportional hazards regression models were applied to evaluate the associations between LHBR tertiles and 30-day, 60-day, 90-day, and 180-day mortality. To examine the consistency of the findings, a series of models with increasing levels of adjustment was constructed.

Discriminative performance analyses

To compare the discriminatory ability of admission LHBR, lactate, and hemoglobin for predicting the primary outcome, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were performed for 30-day mortality. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for each biomarker. Optimal cut-off values were determined using the Youden index, and the corresponding sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were reported. Pairwise comparisons of AUCs were performed using the DeLong test for correlated ROC curves, with LHBR used as the reference biomarker. Because anemia has been associated with higher mortality,21,22 hemoglobin values were directionally inverted during ROC and DeLong analyses to ensure consistent interpretation of discrimination across all biomarkers.

Subgroup analyses

Prespecified subgroup analyses were performed according to sex, AMI subtype (STEMI versus NSTEMI), anemia status (hemoglobin concentration <9 g/dL), diabetes, congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, renal disease, liver disease, and sepsis. Within each subgroup, the association between LHBR tertiles and 30-day mortality was evaluated using multivariable Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for age, Charlson Comorbidity Index, SAPS II, vasopressor burden, and invasive mechanical ventilation. Potential effect modification was assessed using interaction terms between LHBR tertiles and subgroup variables, with statistical significance evaluated by likelihood ratio tests.

Results

Study population and baseline characteristics

A total of 4,651 patients with type 1 AMI were included in the final analysis after application of the eligibility criteria (Figure S1). The median age was 70 years (IQR, 61–78), and 34.8% were women. The median lactate concentration was 1.7 mmol/L (IQR, 1.2–2.7), the median hemoglobin concentration was 11.1 g/dL (IQR, 9.3–13.0), and the median LHBR was 0.16 (IQR, 0.11–0.25). Overall, 42.4% of patients had concomitant sepsis, 47.8% required invasive mechanical ventilation, and 12.5% received two or more vasopressors. The 30-day, 60-day, 90-day, and 180-day mortality rates were 21.3%, 24.6%, 26.2%, and 29.1%, respectively (Table S1).

Patients were stratified into tertiles according to admission LHBR: T1 (≤0.127), T2 (>0.127–0.209), and T3 (>0.209), with increasing LHBR being associated with older age and a progressively greater burden of chronic comorbidities (Table 1). Across tertiles, the median Charlson Comorbidity Index increased from 5 in T1 to 6 in T2 and 7 in T3, while the prevalence of congestive heart failure, renal disease, liver disease, and malignancy increased steadily (all p <0.001).

A similar gradient was observed for physiological severity at ICU admission. Median heart rate increased from 96 to 100 and 104 beats/min across tertiles, whereas mean arterial pressure decreased from 59 to 58 and 56 mmHg. Illness severity scores also rose progressively, with median SAPS II increasing from 36 to 38 and 44, and median SOFA score increasing from 2 to 2 and 3 across tertiles (all p <0.001).

The individual components of LHBR demonstrated marked stepwise changes. Median lactate concentrations increased from 1.1 mmol/L in T1 to 1.7 mmol/L in T2 and 3.6 mmol/L in T3, whereas median hemoglobin concentrations decreased from 11.8 to 10.8 and 10.5 g/dL, respectively (both p <0.001). Higher tertiles were also associated with progressively higher white blood cell counts, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, glucose, and potassium concentrations, together with lower bicarbonate levels.

Markers of clinical instability became increasingly common with increasing LHBR. The prevalence of sepsis increased from 34.8% in T1 to 40.1% in T2 and 52.5% in T3, while the proportion of patients receiving two or more vasopressors increased from 6.5% to 9.7% and 21.4%, respectively. The proportion of STEMI also increased across tertiles, from 21.2% in T1 to 24.1% in T2 and 31.6% in T3 (all p <0.001).

Clinical outcomes worsened progressively across LHBR tertiles. In-hospital mortality increased from 6.3% in T1 to 13.0% in T2 and 35.2% in T3. Likewise, 30-day mortality increased from 8.3% to 16.4% and 39.3%, while 180-day mortality increased from 14.1% to 25.5% and 47.8%, respectively (all p <0.001). Hospital and ICU lengths of stay were significantly longer in higher LHBR tertiles.

Association between lactate-to-hemoglobin ratio and mortality

Restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated significant nonlinear associations between admission LHBR and all mortality outcomes (Figure 1). Mortality risk increased progressively with increasing LHBR, although the relationship was distinctly nonlinear across the entire range of observed values (all P-nonlinear <0.001). Similar nonlinear patterns were observed after natural logarithmic transformation of LHBR (Figure 2), with significant associations persisting for all mortality endpoints (all P-nonlinear <0.05).

Survival analyses

In unadjusted Cox regression analyses (Model 1), mortality risk increased progressively across LHBR tertiles (Table 2). Compared with patients in T1, those in T2 had approximately twofold higher risks of death across all follow-up periods, whereas patients in T3 had substantially higher risks, with hazard ratios ranging from 4.58 to 5.99 (all p <0.001).

After sequential adjustment for demographic characteristics, comorbidities, illness severity, laboratory variables, sepsis status, vasopressor burden, and invasive mechanical ventilation, the magnitude of the association was attenuated but remained statistically significant across all models (Table 2 and Table S2, Models 2–4). In the adjusted purposeful-selection models (Model 5), T2 remained associated with increased 30-day mortality (HR 1.32, 95% CI 1.07–1.64; p = 0.011), while patients in T3 had a twofold higher risk of death compared with those in T1 (HR 2.05, 95% CI 1.66–2.52; p <0.001) (Table 2 and Table S3). Similar associations were observed for the secondary outcomes. Compared with T1, T3 remained associated with increased risks of 60-day mortality (HR 1.94, 95% CI 1.61–2.34), 90-day mortality (HR 1.89, 95% CI 1.58–2.26), and 180-day mortality (HR 1.75, 95% CI 1.48–2.06) (all p <0.001). Patients in T2 also consistently exhibited modest but significant increases in mortality risk across all follow-up periods (Table 2 and Table S4–S7).

Discriminative performance of LHBR, lactate, and hemoglobin

Restricted cubic spline analyses of the individual LHBR components demonstrated that admission lactate exhibited a significant nonlinear association with 30-day mortality (P-nonlinear <0.001), whereas hemoglobin showed no evidence of a nonlinear relationship (P-nonlinear = 0.277) (Figures S2 and S3). Receiver operating characteristic analyses demonstrated that admission LHBR had the highest discriminative ability for 30-day mortality (AUC 0.740, 95% CI 0.722–0.758), followed by lactate (AUC 0.731, 95% CI 0.713–0.749) and hemoglobin (AUC 0.536, 95% CI 0.516–0.556) (Figure 4). Compared with LHBR, discrimination was modestly but significantly lower for lactate (DeLong test, p = 0.009), whereas hemoglobin alone demonstrated substantially poorer discrimination (p <0.001) (Table 3). The optimal cut-off value for LHBR determined by the Youden index was 0.196, with a sensitivity of 65.4% and specificity of 70.9% for predicting 30-day mortality.

Significant interactions were observed for anemia status, sex, congestive heart failure, chronic pulmonary disease, and sepsis (all P-interaction <0.05), indicating that the magnitude of the association between LHBR and mortality varied across these subgroups. In patients without anemia, mortality risk increased progressively across LHBR tertiles, with hazard ratios of 1.83 (95% CI 1.45–2.32) for T2 and 4.02 (95% CI 3.23–5.01) for T3 relative to T1. By contrast, the association was attenuated in patients with anemia, where only T3 remained significantly associated with increased mortality risk (HR 2.25, 95% CI 1.45–3.49), whereas T2 was not significantly different from T1 (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.49–1.36) (P-interaction = 0.029).

No significant interaction was observed according to AMI subtype (P-interaction = 0.491). Elevated LHBR remained associated with increased mortality in both NSTEMI and STEMI. In NSTEMI, mortality risk increased progressively across tertiles, with hazard ratios of 1.62 (95% CI 1.26–2.08) for T2 and 3.32 (95% CI 2.63–4.19) for T3 compared with T1. In STEMI, patients in T3 likewise had a more than threefold higher mortality risk (HR 3.37, 95% CI 2.31–4.91), whereas the association for T2 did not reach statistical significance (HR 1.41, 95% CI 0.93–2.14).

Discussion

In this large cohort of critically ill patients with type 1 AMI, admission LHBR was associated with both short-term and long-term mortality. Mortality risk increased progressively across LHBR tertiles, and both intermediate and high LHBR levels remained associated with adverse outcomes after multivariable adjustment. Restricted cubic spline analyses demonstrated significant nonlinear associations between LHBR and mortality across all follow-up periods. The association was observed in both NSTEMI and STEMI, whereas its magnitude appeared to vary according to anemia status.

The current study extends the growing evidence supporting the prognostic utility of the lactate-to-hemoglobin ratio across diverse critically ill populations. Previous studies have reported significant associations between elevated LHBR and adverse outcomes in critically ill patients with gastrointestinal bleeding, sepsis, and heterogeneous emergency department populations.24–26 In these settings, LHBR was independently associated with both short-term and long-term mortality and exhibited nonlinear or threshold relationships with risk in some studies. Among them, a recent MIMIC-IV analysis in patients with sepsis reported that logarithmically transformed LHBR was independently associated with 28-day mortality and demonstrated a threshold relationship with mortality. 26 The present study extends these observations to critically ill patients with type 1 AMI, a population in whom the prognostic significance of LHBR has not been well characterized.

The findings should also be interpreted in the context of previous studies evaluating other lactate-based composite biomarkers in critically ill patients with AMI. Wang et al. reported that the lactate-to-albumin ratio was associated with short-term mortality in critically ill patients with AMI and demonstrated moderate discriminative performance, with AUCs of approximately 0.73 for 14-day, 28-day, and 90-day mortality. 29 Although both studies combine lactate with a second biomarker, albumin and hemoglobin reflect different pathophysiological processes. Albumin is influenced by nutritional status, systemic inflammation, and capillary leakage, 30 whereas hemoglobin primarily reflects oxygen-carrying capacity. In addition to demonstrating independent associations with both short-term and long-term mortality, the present study directly compared the discriminatory performance of LHBR with those of lactate and hemoglobin individually.

To further characterize the discriminative performance of LHBR relative to its individual components, we performed head-to-head ROC analyses comparing admission LHBR, lactate, and hemoglobin. Admission LHBR demonstrated the highest discriminative ability for 30-day mortality, although the improvement over lactate was modest. These findings suggest that combining lactate and hemoglobin into a single index may improve discrimination compared with either biomarker alone. As a composite biomarker integrating information related to metabolic stress and oxygen-carrying capacity, LHBR may reflect multiple dimensions of physiological disturbance in critically ill patients. In the current study, higher LHBR levels were associated with progressively greater physiological derangement, higher illness severity scores, more frequent sepsis, greater vasopressor requirements, and substantially higher mortality rates, supporting its close relationship with overall disease severity. The nonlinear association observed in the restricted cubic spline analyses further indicates that mortality risk does not increase uniformly across the entire range of LHBR values. Instead, risk appeared to rise more steeply at higher LHBR levels, suggesting that the prognostic relevance of LHBR may be particularly important among patients with marked physiological disturbance.

The subgroup analyses provide additional context regarding the consistency of the observed associations. The association between LHBR and mortality was generally consistent across AMI subtypes, with no evidence of effect modification according to NSTEMI or STEMI status. This finding suggests that the relationship between LHBR and mortality is broadly preserved across major clinical presentations of type 1 AMI, despite important differences in pathophysiology, clinical management, and baseline risk between NSTEMI and STEMI. In contrast, the magnitude of the association varied according to anemia status. Although patients with anemia in the highest LHBR tertile continued to experience significantly increased mortality risk, the association was less pronounced than that observed among patients without anemia, and no significant increase in risk was observed in the intermediate tertile. One possible explanation is that pre-existing reductions in hemoglobin may narrow the effective range of LHBR and thereby diminish its ability to discriminate intermediate levels of risk. Nevertheless, the persistence of increased mortality risk among patients in the highest tertile suggests that markedly elevated LHBR retains clinical relevance even in the presence of anemia.

The clinical implications of these findings deserve consideration. Both lactate and hemoglobin are routinely measured in critically ill patients and are readily available shortly after ICU admission. Calculation of LHBR requires no additional testing, specialized equipment, or added cost. Because LHBR was associated with mortality and demonstrated slightly greater discriminatory ability than lactate alone, it may provide a simple composite biomarker for prognostic assessment in critically ill patients with type 1 AMI. However, external validation is required before LHBR can be considered for routine clinical implementation or incorporation into existing prognostic models.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. First, the retrospective observational design precludes conclusions regarding causality, and residual confounding cannot be excluded despite extensive adjustment. Second, all data were derived from a single-center critical care database, which may limit generalizability to other healthcare systems and patient populations. Third, LHBR was calculated using a single baseline measurement and did not account for temporal changes in lactate or hemoglobin during hospitalization. Dynamic changes in these biomarkers may provide additional prognostic information. Fourth, although the influence of recent blood transfusion was minimized through the exclusion criteria, unmeasured factors affecting lactate production or hemoglobin concentration may still have influenced the results.

Future studies should validate these findings in external cohorts and prospective populations. Evaluation of serial LHBR measurements may help determine whether changes in LHBR over time provide additional prognostic information beyond baseline assessment. Further research is also needed to compare LHBR with established risk stratification tools and to determine whether incorporation of LHBR into existing prognostic models improves clinical decision-making in patients with AMI.

Conclusion

In critically ill patients with type 1 AMI, higher admission LHBR was associated with increased short-term and long-term mortality. Admission LHBR also demonstrated modestly greater discriminative ability than lactate or hemoglobin alone for predicting 30-day mortality. External validation is warranted to confirm these findings and determine the potential clinical utility of LHBR in this population.
