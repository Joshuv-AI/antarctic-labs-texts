Exploring the barriers to Registered Nurses undertaking clinical teaching in clinical settings: A qualitative descriptive study
Authors: Riana Rolinda Hoebes, Daniel Opotamutale Ashipala
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThe study aimed to explore and describe the barriers affecting Registered Nurses in undertaking clinical teaching in clinical settings. The exploration was based on the experiences of Registered Nurses at a selected teaching hospital in Namibia. The researcher intended to identify strategies that could be used to mitigate the negative effects of barriers to effective clinical teaching.DesignA qualitative explorative, descriptive and contextual research design was used.MethodsPurposive sampling was used to select 16 participants. Data were collected through in‐depth, semi‐structured interviews, which were audio‐recorded and transcribed verbatim. Data were analysed using thematic analysis.ResultsClinical teaching is negatively affected by several barriers that hamper clinical teaching and nursing students' learning outcomes. Seven main themes were identified: (1) participants' understanding of clinical teaching; (2) organizational barriers; (3) patient barriers; (4) Registered Nurse barriers; (5) student barriers; (6) training institution barriers; and (7) strategies to mitigate barriers to clinical teaching.ConclusionThe findings revealed that several barriers negatively affect clinical nursing education and hamper the clinical teaching and learning of nursing students.Patient or Public ContributionThis study may assist in the development of targeted interventions that address the barriers that hinder clinical teaching at teaching hospitals. Additionally, this study could assist in improving the health care that is provided by nursing students to patients. The findings may assist with improved quality by reducing nursing students' theory‐practice gap and by promoting collaboration between Registered Nurses and nurse educators in clinical settings.Keywords: barriers, clinical setting, clinical teaching, Registered Nurses, student nurses

INTRODUCTION

The competence of nurses is based on the knowledge and skills they acquire through nursing training (Denat et al., 2016). Therefore, nursing training is comprised of a combination of theoretical and practical learning experiences that enable students to acquire the requisite knowledge, skills and attitudes for providing quality nursing care (Admi et al., 2018; Günay & Kılınç, 2018). In Namibia, and many other countries, clinical education forms more than half of the formal educational courses in nursing (Arkan et al., 2018). Since nursing is a performance‐based profession, clinical learning environments play an important role in the acquisition of professional nursing skills (Gemuhay et al., 2019). For this reason, clinical education is considered to be an essential part of any nursing education program (Arkan et al., 2018).

Clinical teaching begins when an educator positively affects the professional life of students by sharing their insights and knowledge with students to expand students' abilities. The success of this learning partnership depends, in part, on the characteristics of the Registered Nurse educator and the student nurses (Albloushi et al., 2019). Registered Nurses are role models, who students strive to emulate. Thus, the calibre of a Registered Nurse is an important factor in encouraging students to learn. If a nurse educator does not have the suitable attributes and qualities to excel in their role, it may hinder their students' clinical performance and their perception of professional practice.

The clinical teaching of nursing students is viewed internationally as the foundation of sound nursing practice. Nursing students need to be guided on how to become responsible, accountable and self‐reliant professionals who can operate within the scope of the working practice (Gemuhay et al., 2019). A key aspect of clinical teaching is the interpersonal relationship between a professional healthcare provider and students in the clinical setting during clinical education (LaRocco & Fanelli, 2021). In addition, clinical teaching provides nursing students with the opportunity to transform theoretical information into different intellectual and psychomotor skills, which are required for quality nursing care (Adhikari, 2018).

Well‐directed clinical teaching is a strong mechanism to enhance critical thinking, clinical judgement, decision‐making, clinical skills and positive attitudes among nursing students (Bifftu et al., 2018). Translating theory into practice in a structured manner requires professional support and trust in the workplace. It must also give students the opportunity to apply theory (which should be valid and up‐to‐date) in practical ways via direct experience. It thus bridges simulated situations with real‐life scenarios (Hashemiparast et al., 2019). Clinical teaching also directs students by systematizing the principles of nursing care as part of the framework of unit management, consequently empowering students through clinical accompaniment and skills development. It is through clinical teaching that students are guided towards constructive patient education and patient management, thereby enhancing their acceptance of responsibility (Emvula, 2016).

Unfortunately, despite clinical learning being a crucial component of nursing education, the effectiveness of clinical teaching can be hampered by various barriers (Asirifi et al., 2017). This concern is especially valid in the clinical practice environments in sub‐Saharan Africa where several challenges exist to student learning. Factors that have been reported as adversely affecting students during clinical learning include individual factors (connected to students and nurse educators), environmental factors (such as the hospital environment) and social‐economic factors (such as resources) (Papp et al., 2003). Of particular concern in sub‐Saharan and developing contexts are low‐resourced settings for which reliable healthcare evidence is needed in order to prioritize the use of their scarce resources for clinical teaching purposes (McMichael et al., 2005). Indeed, according to the World Bank, most developing countries have a Gross National Income per capita of under US$4036 (World Bank, 2014). The impact of resource‐constrained environments on learning has been evidenced by a study conducted in Botswana, which revealed that nursing students failed to apply theory to practice because they lacked adequate supervision in the clinical area. This resulted in low performance in their clinical practice (Rajeswaran, 2016). Limited opportunities for hands‐on practice in training hospitals, a shortage of nurse tutors and clinical instructors, and an excess of students in a program can also negatively affect the performance of students in clinical practice (Masenga, 2015; Pinehas et al., 2017).

These, as has been shown in the literature, are barriers experienced regularly in Sub‐Saharan contexts. Further possible barriers to learning include difficulties in accessing clinical settings due to financial challenges, staff workload, language barriers, a lack of faculty support and mentorship, difficulty with teaching and learning styles and a lack of students' ability to think critically and apply skills (Onovo, 2019). According to Bradley et al. (2022), these barriers are more likely to be experienced by nursing students in rural placements. Additionally, literature document these barriers are less likely to exist in better‐resourced settings (Burns & Rapee, 2022).

Fortunately, understanding these barriers and their effect on clinical learning can assist in better addressing and eliminating them (Shadadi et al., 2018). Hence, this study was undertaken. However, the total elimination of these barriers will require improving the cultural, educational and professional clinical settings that permit both theoretical and practical teaching and learning (Hashemiparast et al., 2019).

BACKGROUND

Training improves the effectiveness of Registered Nurses' clinical teaching skills, which consequently increases their confidence. This, in turn, may then lead to Registered Nurses having a more positive attitude towards their students and clinical teaching. Lethale et al. (2019) conducted research in Canada, which found that over half of the surveyed clinical teachers believed they required training for the role, including guidance while teaching. A lack of training and its negative influence on clinical teaching was also observed in a study conducted in Ireland by McSharry and Lathlean (2017) who found that the majority of their participants (final‐year student nurses) stated that clinical learning was not helpful to them because most of their clinical teachers lacked the requisite teaching skills due to a lack of training.

The large workload of Registered Nurses was another factor that was found to negatively influence clinical teaching (Gcawu et al., 2021). Indeed, Registered Nurses who are also clinical teachers require more time to cope with the demands of their teaching position because they are also expected to fulfil the responsibilities of a full patient load (Collier, 2018). Nurse midwives have likewise expressed concern about managing their own workloads while simultaneously monitoring and evaluating student nurses (Bifftu et al., 2018). Ashipala and Nghole (2022) research similarly revealed that heavy workloads interfere with teaching roles because, apart from clinical teaching, these nurses have patients to see and management responsibilities. Ashipala and Nghole (2022) study also revealed that Registered Nurses require support from their managers if they are to be effective clinical instructors.

When considering the students' role in clinical learning, Albsoul et al. (2019) found that student barriers, such as a lack of motivation to learn, have a negative impact on clinical teaching. Registered Nurses who are clinical facilitators have complained that unmotivated students show no interest in learning, which negatively impacts their development of key skills (Ratcliffe & George, 2022). Derya et al. (2022) further expressed that working with unmotivated students overwhelms Registered Nurses because, as clinical teachers, they feel as though they have to force some nursing students to engage with their own learning. Yet, clinical teachers are not necessarily trained in student engagement strategies and the pressure they are already under due to their patient workload is compounded by the large numbers of students they have to teach.

Several studies on clinical teaching have been conducted in Africa, including one by Asirifi et al. (2017), which explored the perceptions of Ghanaian nursing students, clinical nurses and nurse educators of their clinical teaching experience. Their findings indicated that clinical teaching using the preceptorship approach in Ghana was not well established. Many African studies, such as Asirifi et al., had similar findings to those conducted in Western countries (e.g. Natesan et al., 2020; Stander et al., 2020; Torous et al., 2020; Wisniewski et al., 2020). In other words, research, globally, has shown that when Registered Nurses, who are also clinical teachers, receive inadequate orientation to clinical teaching, they have insufficient time to carry out their teaching roles, have too many students in their classes and lack the support of management to motivate their students to learn. Additional findings from a study by Magobolo and Dube (2019) were that students lack motivation to learn, and clinical nurses focus too much on clinical needs rather than clinical teaching. Important to note here is that our research shows that no Namibian studies were conducted on the barriers facing Registered Nurses who are also clinical teachers have been completed, hence the relevance of this study.

STUDY AIM

Aim

The study's aim was to explore and describe the barriers affecting Registered Nurses in undertaking clinical teaching in a clinical setting, based on the experiences of Registered Nurses in a low‐resourced setting.

Design

The research is a cross‐sectional, qualitative, descriptive study. The data were analysed inductively and were collected via in‐depth, face‐to‐face interviews. Therefore, a descriptive, exploratory, and qualitative design was applied as it enabled the Registered Nurses to relay their experiences regarding the barriers to clinical teaching. The use of a qualitative research methodology enabled the researcher to gain an in‐depth understanding of the barriers to clinical teaching based on the experiences of Registered Nurses in a clinical setting (Levitt et al., 2018).

Context

The study was conducted at a teaching hospital that is located in, what is considered to be, a low‐resource setting. The hospital employs 37 Registered Nurses who work in different departments at the hospital, with a maximum of 40 patients per department. A minimum of one Registered Nurse and one Enrolled Nurse work at a department during the day and night shifts. Training institutions allocate their nursing students to the hospital for clinical practice, which is conducted by Registered Nurses. However, the student‐nurse‐to‐registered‐nurse ratio is high, hampering effective clinical teaching. The student‐nurse‐to‐registered‐nurse ratio at this hospital is 1:10. Consequently, the student population is large compared to the number of nurses who are available to support student learning.

Sample

The target population for this study comprised 37 Registered Nurses working at a teaching hospital in Namibia. Purposive sampling was used to select 16 participants. In other words, although 37 Registered Nurses were recruited, only 16 Registered Nurses were interviewed because data saturation was reached. Data saturation is the point at which no new idea emerges from the data (Brink et al., 2018). The inclusion criteria for the purposive sampling in this study were: Being a Registered Nurse who had carried out clinical teaching of nursing students and who had been working at the hospital for more than a year and 2. Agreeing to participate in the study by signing a consent form. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are presented in Table 1. A written invitation letter/flyer that explains the purpose of the study was shared through email with the hospital management to distribute to potential participants through various media such as emails and to be placed on the notice boards of the hospital, hospital tea rooms and board rooms, in order for information about the study, and recruitment of the potential participants, and if willing, to contact the researcher. Potential participants who responded to the researcher's invitation were contacted via email or telephonically for appointments. A record was kept of interested persons' contact details and the researchers invited them to an information session. Persons who meet the inclusion criteria were invited to participate, and informed consent was requested should they agree to participate.

Data collection

Data were collected by the first researcher through in‐depth, individual, face‐to‐face interviews that took place from September to October 2021. An interview guide was developed based on the literature review, the central research question and the research objectives. A pilot interview was conducted with three participants from the same sampling population to test the interview guide's ability to generate relevant data. Given the interview guide's suitability, no revisions were made to the interview guide and data from the pilot interview did not form part of the study. The interview guide asked the following questions:

What is your understanding of clinical teaching?

What is your understanding of clinical teaching?

What are the barriers affecting Registered Nurses in undertaking the clinical teaching of nursing students at this hospital?

What are the barriers affecting Registered Nurses in undertaking the clinical teaching of nursing students at this hospital?

What strategies should be implemented to support Registered Nurses' in undertaking clinical teaching at this hospital?

What strategies should be implemented to support Registered Nurses' in undertaking clinical teaching at this hospital?

Open‐ended questions were used to allow the participants to share their experiences in detail (Moser & Korstjens, 2018). The interviews, which lasted for 40–50 min, were conducted in English at a venue that was convenient to the participants. The audio recordings, interview transcripts, and field notes were stored in a password‐protected and encrypted computer, which will be deleted after 2 years if the study is published. If it is not published, the data will be deleted after 5 years.

Ethical considerations

The university's Ethical Research Review Committee approved this study (reference number: SoNREC 18/2021), with additional approval being granted by the Ministry of Health and Social Services Ethical Review Board (reference number: 17/3/3/RRH). The research participants had the right to withdraw from the study at any time, and their participation was voluntary. Assurance was given that the data would be kept confidential and used only for the purposes of this research. Anonymity was ensured by not mentioning the participants' names in the interviews and using codes instead. There were no risks or direct benefits related to participation in this study.

Data analysis

Data from the audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, and the field notes were integrated into the transcripts before they were analysed using qualitative thematic analysis (Braun & Clarke, 2019). The transcripts and narratives were thematically analysed following Braun and Clarke (2019) six‐step method of data analysis: (1) organizing and preparing the data; (2) developing a sense of all the data; (3) codifying the data following the nine steps of Tesch; (4) identifying and describing themes; (5) representing the findings; and (6) interpreting the data (Braun & Clarke, 2019).

The data were subsequently analysed to give them meaning (Grove et al., 2017) and were presented using the main research questions and objectives as the key themes.

Rigour

The four criteria used to ensure trustworthiness and increase rigour included credibility, transferability, confirmability and dependability, based on the model of Lincoln and Guba (1985).

Credibility was achieved through prolonged engagement, as the researcher remained in the field for 4 weeks until data saturation was reached. This enabled her to acquire an in‐depth knowledge of the phenomenon under study. Throughout the research process, the researcher worked with a reflective awareness of her perspectives on the barriers to clinical teaching based on the experiences of Registered Nurses working at the hospital. Transcripts of the data are available upon enquiry, and the findings of the study are supported by related literature on the phenomenon under investigation. The data collection methods are explained thoroughly in the study, and the data were transcribed verbatim as part of the analysis. All the steps followed in the research process were documented by the researcher to ensure that the data can be audited. Dependability was ensured through a code‐recode strategy and triangulation.

Seven categories emerged as the main barriers to clinical teaching in clinical settings: (1) participants' understanding of clinical teaching; (2) organizational barriers; (3) patient barriers; (4) Registered Nurses' barriers; (5) student barriers; (6) training institutional barriers; (7) strategies to mitigate barriers to clinical teaching (see Table 3). Emerging themes and sub‐themes.

Theme 1: Participants' understanding of clinical teaching

This theme describes the participants' understanding of the concept of clinical teaching. They described clinical teaching as a process of educating or teaching students within a hospital or clinical setting. The sub‐themes that emerged from the discussions with the participants included teaching within a hospital setting, and practical teaching. Additional sub‐themes included teaching as a process, acquiring experience and knowledge, and practical teaching.

Sub‐theme 1.1: Hospital‐based teaching

It emerged from the study that the participants understood clinical teaching to be teaching that takes place in a hospital. Teaching within the hospital context is important for student nurses to know what is expected of them during their practical placement.

“It's a way of passing on, on generating information related to medical or related to health from one person to another person within a hospital set up.” [P16, 27 years old, female]

Sub‐theme 1.2: Practical teaching

The participants described clinical teaching as teaching that requires registered nurses to demonstrate procedures to nursing students in real‐life situations, which students should then be able to replicate.

“Clinical teaching is a teaching which is based on the nursing practice on the ground. It includes the real teaching and practical knowledge of real‐life procedures.” [P6, 36 years old, female]

Theme 2: Organizational barriers

The participants highlighted various organizational barriers that may affect the clinical teaching of students. Sub‐themes include a lack of resources and staff workload.

Sub‐theme 2.1: Lack of resources

The participants mentioned that the hospital was faced with a shortage of working equipment and resources, which makes it difficult for them to teach nursing students.

“We don't have enough resources … so you cannot give proper teaching.” [P1, 43 years old, female]

Sub‐theme 2.2: Staff workload

The participants mentioned that their large workload gives them less time to teach and guide their students. They emphasized that they cannot adequately teach nursing students in the clinical environment due to the lack of registered nurses working in the ward.

“We are under staffed. We don't have enough nurses. Sometimes let me say we are only two on a shift. And then we don't make time to teach students because if we were many, we could delegate one nurse to attend to students.” [P5, 38 years old, female]

Theme 3: Patient barriers

The third theme reflects the participants' experiences of patients hindering clinical teaching. The sub‐theme that emerged links to the lack in variety of procedures available for student nurses to practice.

Sub‐theme 3.1: Lack in variety of procedures and patient's willingness

The participants mentioned that, at times, patients are not willing to allow student nurses to assist them or to practice a procedure on them. Participants also mentioned that there are also times when there are no patients, willing or unwilling, on whom student nurses can practice particular procedures.

“Sometimes, the patients are not there or you are willing to do the procedure with the student but it is not really in that format, whereby it will be applied on the patient themselves or they might not be in the state to get the procedure.” [P11, 29 years old, female]

Theme 4: Registered Nurses' barriers

The fourth theme relates to the individual barriers of the participants themselves that hinder them from conducting clinical teaching. The sub‐themes include poor insight into students' needs, a lack of interest in teaching students, and language barriers.

Sub‐theme 4.1: Poor insight into students' needs

Participants reported that registered nurses might not be aware of, or familiar with, the practical needs of nursing students.

“Sometimes it might also be that the staff members are also not aware of whatever the students need to be taught or they also are not familiar with some of the procedures.” [P2, 30 years old, female]

Sub‐theme 4.2: Lack of interest in teaching students

One participant expressed his thoughts that some registered nurses are lazy and are not interested in teaching nursing students.

“You might find some staff that is lazy or they don't pay much attention to the students or they ignore what the students require.” [P13, 40 years old, male]

Sub‐theme 4.3: Language barriers

One participant mentioned that some registered nurses do not speak the local spoken language.

“As a foreign nurse, I cannot pronounce or say some words in somebody's vernacular language, so explaining some things might also not be easy and also our understanding of certain words might differ… which makes our communication not… effective.” [P3, 30 years old, female]

Theme 5: Students' barriers

The fifth theme relates to the participants' experiences of students' barriers to clinical teaching. The sub‐themes include fear, disengagement, and professional ethics.

Sub‐theme 5.1: Fear

The participants highlighted that some nursing students manifest different types of fear, which could be of nursing staff, of making mistakes, or of the unknown. One participant further mentioned that some nursing students prefer to hide behind others while observing a procedure.

“Sometimes some like to be behind others while the others are observing a certain procedure. They don't want to come close to see or maybe they think, ‘If I will be asked to do, what am I supposed to do or be asked a question which I don't know, so I should be like far, far away from the procedure which is being done’.” [P1, 43 years old, female]

Sub‐theme 5.2: Disengagement

The participants reported that some nursing students show a lack of interest in the nursing profession.

“Some of them are dodging. Today you see they are coming and tomorrow they are not.” [P1, 43 years old, female]

“Sometimes I feel like there are some students that are not willing to, or that are not eager to, learn, because you can demonstrate something to a student and then you ask the student to come and demonstrate back. After two or three days, the student fails to demonstrate back.” [P9, 29 years old, female]

Sub‐theme 5.3: Non‐professional behaviour

One participant mentioned that students are on their cell phones while on duty, some arrive late, and others refuse to listen to the registered nurses.

“We have students that are working on their cell phones the whole day. Students are not coming on time. Some students feel like my supervisor or my lecturer told me this, I am not going to listen to a registered nurse in the ward.” [P4, 32 years old, female]

Theme 6: Training institutional barriers

The sixth theme describes the participants' experiences of barriers associated with the training institution that may impact clinical teaching. Sub‐themes include a lack of the theoretical preparation of students and a lack of communication.

Sub‐theme 6.1: Lack of theoretical preparation of students

The participants stated that lecturers often send students to the clinical environment without teaching them the appropriate theory first.

“I feel like the lecturers at the schools are not doing their part of putting more effort in to first give the theory to the students so they know what they are going to do.” [P10, 28 years old, female].

Sub‐theme 6.2: Lack of communication

One participant reported the tutors do not communicate with the registered nurses in the wards.

“The tutors … don't communicate, they don't even discuss, plus they don't even want to teach. It is just that there is no communication between the tutors and the registered nurses in the hospitals.” [P4, 32 years old, female]

Theme 7: Strategies to mitigate barriers to clinical teaching

Finally, theme 7 relates to the suggestions that participants made regarding strategies that could be put into place to enhance clinical teaching. Sub‐themes include the need for adequate resources, the need to increase the number of registered nurses, students having a thorough theoretical knowledge, supervisory visits, and good communication.

Sub‐theme 7.1: Organization to provide resources

According to the participants, there is a need for resources and equipment to facilitate teaching.

“We need resources so that they can help us to teach the students… For instance, like BP machines.” [P1, 43 years old, female]

Sub‐theme 7.2: Organization to increase the number of nursing staff and invest in professional development

Participants emphasized that the Ministry should employ more registered nurses to overcome the current staff shortages.

“By employing enough registered nurses so that we are at least four or five in a department so that someone can get time to teach the students while others attend … to the patients.” [P9, 29 years old, female]

One participant described the importance of registered nurses accessing updated policies and guidelines to facilitate teaching and learning. Another participant spoke about the importance of being trained in how to teach in the clinical environment.

“We have to acquire the updated policies and guidelines at all times so that people can read on their own and be able to at least pass on the knowledge to the students.” [P13, 40 years old, male]

“A bit of teaching for staff members, like in‐service training, to help them sharpen their skills and to keep their knowledge fresh and up‐to‐date.” [P2, 30 years old, female]

Sub‐theme 7. 3: Theoretical preparation of students

Participants highlighted the importance of lecturers teaching theory before sending students for clinical placements.

“The tutors should at least demonstrate first at school, then the students will come and give feedback to the registered nurses, then we correct them where they are not doing it right.” [P10, 28 years old, female]

Sub‐theme 7.4: Supervisory visits and planning placements

The participants reported that lecturers should make supervisory visits during nursing students' practical placements for observation and input.

“The tutors should keep visiting the clinical areas or the departments where their students are being placed, just to check how the students are doing.” [P10, 28 years old, female]

“They must minimise a lot of students they are placing at the hospital. Especially when they are from the same school; not a lot of students at once.” [P11, 29 years old, female]

Sub‐theme 7.5: Establishing good communication

Participants mentioned the importance of communication between the lecturers and registered nurses to promote quality clinical teaching.

“A collaboration of the clinical instructors from the schools, all the lecturers, and then the staff members here … would probably be helpful.” [P2, 30 years old, female]

DISCUSSION

The participants in this study understood clinical teaching to be practical teaching, as per Chinai et al. (2018). Chinai et al. (2018) describe clinical teaching as the way in which student nurses learn to practice the theory of nursing, enabling the incorporation of academic knowledge and practical knowledge in the clinical environment. In other words, clinical teaching is bringing together the art and science of nursing. Similarly, Bifftu et al. (2018) state that through clinical teaching, student nurses learn how to exercise the philosophical concepts of nursing theory in real situations. However, despite the importance of clinical teaching in bridging the practice/theory gap, this study has highlighted the existence of several barriers that may negatively impact Registered Nurses and nursing students in the clinical teaching environment.

The participants in this study, for example, mentioned that the hospital faces a shortage of working equipment and resources, which makes it difficult for them to teach. This supports the findings of Bvumbwe and Mtshali (2018), who found that the absence of material resources results in serious problems for nursing education in sub‐Saharan Africa. Nik‐Ahmad‐Zuky et al. (2020), Ashipala and Kampale (2022) and Muhora and Ashipala (2021) all noted that a lack of resources and materials hinders nursing students' learning.

Additionally, a large workload results in nurses having less time to teach, guide, and spend time with their students. Indeed, Motsaanaka et al. (2020) found that a large workload, due to staff shortages, leads to registered nurses having little time to supervise students. Similarly, studies carried out by Motsaanaka et al. (2020) and Anderson et al. (2020) found that burnt‐out registered nurses have little time to teach. This was supported by Gatewood et al. (2020), who found that staff shortages combined with the several operational duties performed by registered nurses are barriers to the clinical teaching of nursing students.

It also emerged from this study that despite the willingness of registered nurses to teach students, few patients are present in the wards, which may impede nursing students' practical application of theory. This finding links to a study by Mbakaya et al. (2020), who found that having few patients in a ward hinders the clinical learning of nursing students.

Registered nurses might also not be aware of, or familiar with, the practical needs of nursing students (Adibelli & Boyaci, 2018). A similar finding was reported by Wu et al. (2021), who found that registered nurses lack clinical teaching knowledge and skills. Additionally, Maditjani (2018) found that registered nurses lack knowledge of what is required of them when it comes to helping student nurses attain clinical competence. Participants in this study expressed the fact that some registered nurses are lazy and lack interest in teaching, which were similar concerns raised in the findings of Nguyen et al. (2018). A study by Kalyani et al. (2019) also found that some nurses have negative perceptions towards students. Similar findings were obtained by Kaphagawani and Useh (2018) and Sahan and Guven (2020), who reported that nursing staff are not willing to teach or assist nursing students.

The participants in this study also expressed the fact that some registered nurses lack confidence in their teaching skills, while one participant also mentioned the language barrier as being an issue too. In terms of the confidence of registered nurses in teaching nursing students, several studies have made similar findings. Makarem et al. (2019), for example, found that nurses do not have much professional confidence during their first 10 years of work, while Kalyani et al. (2019) state that nurses are not competent at teaching nursing students. In the same vein, Moghaddam et al. (2020) reported that nurses' lack of teaching skills is a barrier to student learning. In terms of language being a barrier to student learning, similar findings were reported in a study by Ashipala and Matundu (2023), who found that a poor command of the language of the clinical environment can result in unfavorable or unpleasant experiences and poor learning outcomes. This was also an issue highlighted by El Naggar and Almaeen (2020), who reported that language was an obstacle to the clinical teaching of nursing students.

Participants in this study also expressed their concern over the fact that some nursing students prefer to hide behind others while observing a procedure. This concurs with the findings of Nik‐Ahmad‐Zuky et al. (2020), who stated that nursing students fear making mistakes and harming patients; they may fear the early days of a practicum when they begin work in a new ward (Jafarian‐Amiri et al., 2020). Similarly, Farzi et al. (2018) and Hashemiparast et al. (2019) found that a lack of self‐confidence among nursing students disrupts the clinical education process.

In our study, the participants also reported that some nursing students show a lack of interest in the nursing profession in general or are disengaged during their clinical learning. Indeed, some registered nurses reported that students are on their cell phones while on duty, some arrive late, and others refuse to listen to the registered nurses. Several studies have had similar findings. For example, a study by Maditjani (2018), found that registered nurses find it difficult to teach nursing students due to their absenteeism from the clinical setting and lack of interest in learning. Similar findings were also reported in studies by Lapeña‐Moñux et al. (2016) and Adibelli and Boyaci (2018), who found that some nursing students do not have the desire to work or learn during their practical placements. Nguyen et al. (2018) and Ashipala and Shaluwawa (2021) also noted that nursing students use cell phones during their clinical practice and may have negative attitudes, are immature or fearful, which prevents learning from taking place and may result in unprofessional behaviour.

Added to this, is the nurse participants' concern with the fact that some lecturers send nursing students into the clinical environment without adequately teaching them theory first. This supports Farzi et al. (2018) conclusion that a student's lack of clinical knowledge is an obstacle to the clinical teaching of nursing students, and Omid et al. (2018) note that the soundness of students' theoretical and practical knowledge should be determined with the use of written tests and simulations before they enter the clinical environment. In addition, the participants reported that lecturers should make supervisory visits to nursing students during their practical placements. This is also as per Maditjani (2018), who found that lecturers need to supervise nursing students during their practical placements, so they are aware of any shortcomings. Indeed, Maditjani (2018) stress the importance of lecturers ensuring that nursing students are equipped for clinical allocation. In our study, too, participants expressed their concern over the fact that some nursing students lack clinical experience when entering the clinical environment. This, according to Chinai et al. (2018), means that they cannot carry out procedures in certain cases. Similarly, Amini et al. (2020) found that nursing students lack clinical knowledge.

Poor communication was yet another barrier identified by the participants – especially communication barriers that exist between lecturers and registered nurses. As with several of the findings of this study, this seems to be in keeping with the findings of similar research. Asirifi et al. (2017) and Motsaanaka et al. (2020), for example, found that poor communication can be a barrier to effective learning during clinical teaching. Motsaanaka et al. (2020) specifically found that a lack of communication between lecturers, student nurses, and registered nurses creates unfavorable clinical experiences, blocking opportunities for nursing students to learn and reach optimal learning outcomes.

With regard to resources, the participants expressed the need for additional resources and equipment to facilitate the teaching of nursing students. This is in keeping with the findings of Nguyen et al. (2018) in Vietnam who recommended that the Ministry of Health and Social Services fill the vacant posts of registered nurses. Linked to concerns over resources, it also emerged from this study that registered nurses need to acquire updated policies and guidelines in order to facilitate student teaching and learning. Robinson (2021) revealed that regulations, both new and updated, should be developed, and distributed to staff to ensure the provision of quality care. The importance of in‐service training in providing registered nurses with the most up‐to‐date health developments is yet another important finding of this study, which is as per Nguyen et al. (2018) recommendation that continuous professional development opportunities should be made available to registered nurses so that they may keep their knowledge relevant. Similarly, Maditjani (2018) stressed the importance of reskilling and up‐skilling registered nurses to improve their teaching skills.

Finally, the participants in this study emphasized the need for nursing schools to properly plan for the placement of nursing students and reduce the number of students placed in clinical practice at any one time to prevent overcrowding. On the same note, Dağ et al. (2019) recommended that clinical education units should cautiously plan the number of nursing students receiving clinical education in order to promote sound student teaching and learning. This ultimately links back to the participants' concern over poor communication, since with good communication between lecturers and registered nurses, quality clinical teaching can be promoted. Indeed, an effective learning environment needs good communication and teamwork between students, mentors, unit managers, and clinical facilitators (Kaliyangile & Ngoma, 2020).

Study limitations

The study only included Registered Nurses in a specific teaching hospital in Namibia. Future studies, including those in a broader context, might provide additional insights into the phenomenon, which would make the results more generalizable to a broader population.

CONCLUSION

The purpose of this study was to explore and describe barriers to clinical teaching based on the experiences of Registered Nurses. This study found that students are not always theoretically and practically prepared for clinical practice, as lecturers rarely supervise their students during clinical practice and send too many students at once. The findings of this study can aid Registered Nurses, student nurses, the clinical setting and training institutions in understanding the roles they play in clinical teaching and learning. It is recommended that further research be undertaken to explore the effects of the barriers to clinical teaching, which could aid in understanding the challenges faced by nursing students during their clinical practice. A greater understanding of the challenges could also help hospitals develop a conducive learning environment. This paper has made several recommendations that could be used to mitigate the negative impact of some barriers to clinical teaching experienced by Registered Nurses in a clinical setting.
