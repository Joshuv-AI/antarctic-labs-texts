Real‐world‐data for phenotypes and genotypes of rare monogenic genetic epilepsies and genes of uncertain significance for epilepsy
Authors: Haley Morris, Elizabeth Mathew, Shalini Bahl, Marta Villa‐Lopez, Saadet Mercimek‐Andrews
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe objectives of this study were to develop a real‐world‐data (RWD) database for patients with epilepsy to provide further real‐world‐evidence (RWE) for monogenic genetic epilepsies; to assess the usefulness of a diagnostic algorithm in epilepsy; and to examine protein 3D structures using in silico tools to predict variant pathogenicity.MethodsWe stratified patients into Group 1 (with genetic diagnoses) and Group 2 (with no genetic diagnoses). We performed protein 3D modeling of variants of uncertain significance (VUS) in genes.ResultsWe included 167 patients in our RWD database. We report the genotypes and phenotypes of 44 distinct monogenic genetic epilepsies from 66 patients. The diagnostic yield of clinical exome sequencing (ES) was 31%. Developmental delay, developmental brain malformation, movement disorder, and infantile‐onset epilepsy (seizure onset <2 years of age) were more common (p < 0.05) in Group 1. The diagnostic yield of biochemical investigations was 0%. Compared to wild type protein structures, there were predicted conformational changes to protein structure including changes in α‐helices and/or β‐sheets and losses in loops in the protein structures expressed by variants of uncertain significance (VUS) for ARHGAP35, BACH2, BAZ2B, DOP1B, EPHA5, FRYL, HMGXB4, MTDH, NHSL2, NLGN2, NRXN2, P2RX4, PRICKLE2, PRRC2A, RALGAPB, RNF157, SREBF2, ZNF532, and ZNF536, all of which are genes of uncertain significance (GUS). We calculated the estimated diagnostic yield of ES to be 48% by using a 3D protein structure prediction tool, and the functional characterization of a LARP7 VUS, as well as previous case series of GUS.SignificanceWe report 19 GUS that accounts 10% of the cohort with GUS. If those GUS would have been proven to cause genetic epilepsies, the diagnostic yield of ES would have been increased 17%. The GUS and VUS in monogenic epilepsy genes will require further functional characterization. International collaborations are needed to increase our understanding of these rare epilepsies.Plain Language SummaryWe wanted to create a database for people with epilepsy. INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is one of the most common neurological disorders and burdens 45.9 million people and their families worldwide. 1 Its prevalence is about 1% in the general population. 2 Epileptic encephalopathy (EE) is a term used in patients with epilepsy and developmental delay, where epileptic activity can worsen their motor and cognitive functions. 3 Developmental and epileptic encephalopathy (DEE) is characterized by refractory epilepsy and developmental regression that are associated with a wide range of neurodevelopmental disorders. DEE usually starts in infancy or childhood and is the most severe form of epilepsy. 4 , 5 , 6

A list of genes for monogenic genetic epilepsies was published by Oliver et al., 7 and approximately 90% of those genes are associated with DEE. 5 The authors developed a publicly available database that is updated twice yearly (https://github.com/bahlolab/genes4epilepsy) (updated September 2025). This database has more than 1000 genes as causes of monogenic genetic epilepsies (accessed October 2025). Exome sequencing (ES) and genome sequencing (GS) have led to the discovery of several monogenic genetic epilepsies over the last two decades. In a recent systematic review and meta‐analysis, a large number of studies and the diagnostic yield of genetic investigations in epilepsy were reported. 8

Epilepsy experts (on behalf of the ILAE Genetics Commission and the Task Force on Clinical Genetic Testing in the Epilepsies) recommend genetic investigations in patients with (1) severe childhood onset epilepsies, especially DEE; (2) epilepsy with neurodevelopmental disorders; (3) progressive myoclonus epilepsies and progressive phenotypes; and (4) non‐acquired focal epilepsies with familial syndromes. Additionally, genetic investigations can be considered if (1) epilepsy is found to be non‐acquired focal and pharmacoresistant during presurgical evaluation; (2) epilepsy is due to cortical malformations. 9

The National Society of Genetic Counselors reported an evidence‐based practice guideline for genetic investigations and counseling for the unexplained epilepsies. 10 Identification of underlying genetic diagnosis in epilepsy is crucial, as it can direct the optimal selection of anti‐seizure medications or precision therapies for the management of seizures in children and adults. 9 , 11 , 12 , 13 Implication of genetic diagnoses on the medical management of epilepsy was reported in up to 72% of patients with monogenic genetic epilepsies. 14 , 15

Inherited metabolic diseases are a subgroup of monogenic genetic epilepsies, and some of these diseases have specific treatments to improve patient outcomes. Epilepsy was reported as a prominent feature in 256 different inherited metabolic diseases. 16 We previously reported that the prevalence of inherited metabolic diseases was up to 25% in childhood epilepsy. 17 , 18 , 19

Real‐world‐data (RWD) is derived from electronic patient charts and serves as clinical evidence of natural history studies of rare diseases that contribute to real‐world‐evidence (RWE). 20 , 21 , 22 The objective of this study was to develop a RWD database for patients with epilepsy to provide further RWE for monogenic genetic epilepsies. In 2019, we developed a stepwise diagnostic algorithm in childhood epilepsy to identify underlying genetic causes. 18 We applied this algorithm to our patients to assess its usefulness in our clinical practice. Finally, we also applied extensive in silico tools to examine protein 3D modeling to predict variant pathogenicity for variants of uncertain significance (VUS).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethics

The Research Ethics Office, Health Research Ethics Board, University of Alberta (Study ID: Pro00117378) approved this retrospective cohort study. The Northern Alberta Clinical Trials and Research Centre (NACTRC) and Alberta Health Services (AHS) provided operational and administrative approval (PRJ38569).

Patients' inclusion and exclusion criteria

This study was performed in a single epilepsy genetic clinic at an Academic Health Sciences Centre between 1 January 2021 and 31 December 2024. This clinic accepts referrals for genetic diagnostics and genetic counseling for abnormal genetic investigations. Inclusion criteria were: (1) Patients with epilepsy, including children (<18 years of age) and adults (≥18 years of age); (2) patients who had been referred to the epilepsy genetic clinic; (3) patients who underwent genetic and genomic investigations. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients who experienced one provoked seizure; (2) patients who did not have genetic investigations performed.

Patients' clinical, biochemical and genetic analyses

We reviewed Electronic Patient Charts for the clinical features, biochemical investigations, electroencephalography (EEG), neuroimaging features, and molecular genetics and genomic investigations. Biochemical investigations included plasma amino acids, lactate, ammonia, transferrin isoelectric focusing, plasma acylcarnitine profile, total and free carnitine, homocysteine, urine organic acid analysis, and urine guanidinoacetate and creatine. We entered all information into an Excel database (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA).

Molecular genetics and genomic investigations using individual and/or parents' DNA samples were performed in clinical molecular genetics and genomic laboratories according to their methods. We applied the variant classification guidelines provided by The American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics and The Association for Molecular Pathology (ACMG/AMP) to interpret the variants. 23 , 24 All variants were assessed for their allele frequency in the general population through searches in the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD v4.1.0) (http://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/about). 25

A genetic diagnosis was confirmed in patients with pathogenic and/or likely pathogenic variants. We applied the recent dyadic nomenclature for naming of monogenic genetic epilepsies. 26 Patients were stratified into two groups to compare their phenotypes. Group 1: Patients with genetic diagnoses, and Group 2: Patients with no genetic diagnoses.

Protein three‐dimensional (3D) modeling

We applied protein 3D modeling to VUS on genes: (1) K‐known to be associated with monogenic genetic epilepsies: associated with the phenotype, de novo (or presumed de novo due to negative family history and/or unavailability of biological parents) autosomal dominant; X‐linked in males (may or may not be inherited from their mothers); autosomal recessive inheritance patterns; (2) genes of uncertain significance (GUS) de novo (or presumed de novo due to negative family history and/or unavailability of biological parents) autosomal dominant; X‐linked in males (may or may not be inherited from their mothers); autosomal recessive (both parents heterozygous) inheritance patterns.

Protein 3D structure prediction was performed on the wild type and variant protein structures. We retrieved protein FAST‐All (FASTA) sequences from the UniProt database and input them to the AlphafoldColab2 program for 2D structure visualization and processing. 27 The output data files from AlphafoldColab2 were then input into PyMol 2.5.5 Edu for 3D protein structure visualization. We assessed sequence coverage and confidence levels for reliability and validity of structures. We performed all imaging of structures in accordance with the sequence alignment of variant protein structures compared with wild type protein structures. We assessed variant protein models for predicted change in structure and amino acid interaction in relation to the wild type proteins. We labeled variant residues for each gene variant. To verify the significance of the protein shifts, predicted alignment error (PAE) plots were generated using PAE Viewer from data obtained from AlphafoldColab2. 28

Statistical methods

We performed statistical analysis using R statistical software (version 4.0.2). Results are given as mean ± SD (range). Non‐parametric Fisher's exact test was chosen to compare between groups, as indicated where appropriate. Results were considered statistically significant with a two‐tailed p‐value of <0.05.

RESULTS

Demographics, diagnoses, and clinical features of patients

There were 167 patients who met the inclusion criteria (children n = 123, and adults n = 44) and were included in our RWD database. Demographic data, clinical features, epilepsy phenotypes, neuroimaging findings, and EEG results of all patients in Group 1 (with genetic diagnoses) and Group 2 (without genetic diagnoses) are summarized in Table 1. The age of seizure onset was <12 years in 82% of the patients. A small number of patients (8%; n = 13) had their seizure onset in adulthood. The most common seizure type was generalized tonic–clonic in 65% of patients (children n = 76 and adults n = 32). Drug‐resistant epilepsy (poor seizure control after trying two anti‐seizure medications at therapeutic doses) was present in 36% of the patients (n = 60). Eight patients had an epilepsy surgery (vagal nerve stimulator n = 3; corpus callosotomy n = 1; MVA resection n = 1; left temporal lobectomy n = 1; right mesial frontal resection n = 1; left hemispherectomy n = 1). Six patients were treated with the ketogenic diet.

The DEE phenotype was present in 18 patients, and the EE phenotype was present in 105 patients. Forty‐four patients had epilepsy with no history of developmental delay or cognitive dysfunction. Movement disorder was reported in 22 patients (children n = 14 and adults n = 8), autism spectrum disorder (ASD) was reported in 50 patients (children n = 36 and adults n = 14), and attention‐deficit and hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) was reported in 40 patients (children n = 36 and adults n = 4).

The diagnosis of 44 different genetic diseases was confirmed in 66 patients (40%) (children n = 49; adults n = 17), including autosomal dominant (n = 51); autosomal recessive (n = 6); X‐linked (n = 3) and pathogenic copy number variants (CNV) (n = 6) by different molecular genetics and genomic investigations (Figure 1 and Table S1 in Data S1).

Genes and type of molecular genetic tests and number of patients for each gene are depicted. CMA, chromosomal microarray; ES, whole exome sequencing.

Chromosomal microarray (CMA) was performed on 120 patients (children n = 95 and adults n = 25), and 29 had abnormal CNV, including 11 pathogenic and 18 of uncertain significance. CMA confirmed a genetic diagnosis in six out of 11 patients with pathogenic CNV (children n = 4; adults n = 2). Five out of 11 pathogenic CNV were not associated with epilepsy phenotypes. As some of the patients were referred with their CMA results for genetic counseling, we did not calculate the diagnostic yield of CMA. However, CMA was requested by the senior author (S.M.‐A.) in 16 adult patients, and its diagnostic yield was 6.3% (1/16).

A next‐generation sequencing panel for epilepsy (TNGSPE) was performed on 58 patients (children n = 49 and adults n = 9), and it confirmed a genetic diagnosis in 17 patients (children n = 16; adults n = 1). As some of the patients were referred for genetic counseling with their TNGSPE results, we did not calculate the diagnostic yield of TNGSPE. However, TNGSPE was requested by the senior author (S.M.‐A.) in nine adults, and its diagnostic yield was 11% (1/9).

Clinical ES was performed for 95 patients (children n = 69; adults n = 26), and it confirmed a genetic diagnosis in 29 patients (children n = 22; adults n = 7). All clinical ES was performed by the senior author (S.M.‐A.), and its diagnostic yield was 31% (29/95).

Three of the 13 patients with adult‐onset seizure had a confirmed genetic diagnosis, including 16p11.2 deletion syndrome, KRIT1‐related cavernous malformations, and EIF2B3‐related vanishing white matter disease (using CMA n = 1; TNGSPE n = 1; clinical ES n = 1, respectively).

SCN1A‐related epilepsy was the most common monogenic genetic epilepsy (9% of all patients with genetic diagnoses; n = 6). Two patients had de novo variants, and three patients had variants inherited from one of their parents. We could not perform parental variant testing in one patient (parents declined familial variant testing). There were four different phenotypes in six patients, including intractable childhood epilepsy with generalized tonic–clonic seizures (n = 1), generalized epilepsy with febrile seizures plus (n = 3), generalized tonic–clonic seizures (n = 1), and epilepsy with focal seizures (n = 1). Interestingly, three patients from the same family had three different phenotypes, including generalized tonic–clonic seizures (n = 1), generalized epilepsy with febrile seizures plus (n = 1), and epilepsy with focal seizures (n = 1).

KCNQ2‐related epilepsy was the second most common monogenic genetic epilepsy (7.5% of all patients with genetic diagnoses, n = 5). Three patients had de novo pathogenic variants, and one patient (Epi143) had a pathogenic variant inherited from an affected parent (Epi007). We could not perform parental variant testing in the patient Epi007 (parents declined familial variant testing; both had a negative history of epilepsy). There were three different phenotypes in five patients, including self‐limited familial neonatal epilepsy (n = 3), neonatal‐onset DEE (n = 1), and non‐neonatal‐onset DEE (n = 1).

We identified 62 pathogenic or likely pathogenic (P/LP) variants in 40 genes. There were 90 VUS in 76 genes, including 59 known disease‐causing genes and 19 GUS. All variants and CNVs, and their ACMG variant classifications, are summarized in Tables S2 and S3 in Data S1. Twenty‐two out of 29 patients, who were diagnosed by clinical ES, could have been diagnosed by the current TNGSPE which includes BCL11B, CACNA1A, CDK8, DLG4, DYRK1A, EBF3, EIF2B3, EIF3F, GNA01, GRIN2B, KCNQ2, KCNQ5, KPTN, MBD5, NUS1, PACS1, PIGO, PIGT, PRRT2, RHOBTB2, SCN1A, SCN8A, SETD1B, SETD1B, SHANK3, and WDR26.

One patient had a VUS and a LP variant in PIGO (OMIM 614730). This patient presented with global developmental delay, epilepsy, strabismus, and dysmorphic features (hypertelorism, long palpebral fissures, short nose, broad nasal bridge, broad nasal tip, tented mouth, brachytelephalangy, broad halluces, and absent finger‐ and toenails on her 5th fingers and toes bilaterally), and marginally elevated alkaline phosphatase (478 U/L; reference range 130–430) level. Due to the typical clinical features of phosphatidylinositol glycan anchor biosynthesis class O protein (PIGO) congenital disorders of glycosylation (PIGO‐CDG) and segregation of variants in both parents, we interpreted this VUS as causative, confirming the diagnosis of PIGO‐CDG and included this patient in Group 1 (patients with genetic diagnoses).

None of the patients had abnormal biochemical investigations to suggest any of the inherited metabolic diseases. However, three patients (2%) were diagnosed with one of the inherited metabolic diseases by clinical ES, including PIGO‐CDG (n = 1), phosphatidylinositol glycan anchor biosynthesis class T protein (PIGT) CDG (PIGT‐CDG) (n = 1), and nuclear undecaprenyl pyrophosphate synthase 1 (NUS1) CDG (NUS1‐CDG) (n = 1). Additionally, one of the symptomatic siblings was diagnosed with PIGT‐CDG by familial variant testing. All these patients had normal transferrin isoelectric focusing tests.

There were treatment implications of genetic diagnoses in 27% (n = 18/66) of the patients with genetic diagnoses (SCN1A‐, SCN8A, KCNQ2‐, and CACNA1A‐related epilepsy or EE, and TSC2‐related tuberous sclerosis) for the choice of anti‐seizure medications. There were management implications of genetic diagnoses in 9% (n = 6/66) of the patients with monogenic genetic epilepsies (PIGO‐, PIGT‐, NUS1‐CDG, and KRIT1‐related cavernous malformations). All patients received genetic counseling for their recurrence risks for their family planning.

Comparison of group 1 (patients with genetic diagnoses) and group 2 (patients without genetic diagnoses)

The majority of phenotypic, neuroimaging, and EEG features were similar between Group 1 and 2. This is likely due to unevenly distributed numbers of patients in each feature in our study cohort (Table 1). Infantile‐onset epilepsy (seizure onset <2 years of age) was more common in Group 1 compared to Group 2.

Protein three‐dimensional (3D) structure prediction of monogenic genetic epilepsy genes

We found 11 VUS in nine genes, including AGF2A, CUX2, MCM3AP, NSDHL, PIGO, POLR1C, SCN1A, SYNGAP1, and TAF1 (Figure S1). We applied protein 3D structure prediction to six VUS in five genes. We excluded VUS in SYNGAP1, TAF1, CUX, and AFG2A. The SYNGAP1 VUS was predicted to be an out‐of‐frame donor site rescue. As protein 3D structure prediction requires a defined amino acid sequence as input, this splice site variant creates uncertainty about the protein product. Therefore, we did not apply protein 3D modeling for the SYNGAP1 VUS. The TAF1 variant protein's predicted Local Distance Difference Test (pLDDT) was <50. The AlphaFold2 does not have confidence in its prediction of where the residue is located in space as well as how it interacts with its neighboring residues. Therefore, this region is unreliable, and we did not apply protein 3D modeling for the TAF1 VUS. We generated PAE plots for CUX2 VUS. The mean PAE of the alpha‐helix in the wild type CUX2 protein structure, where the largest shift was observed, is about 6.46 angstroms. The alpha‐helix in the variant CUX2 protein structure had a mean PAE of 7.92 angstroms. The mean PAE for both wild type and variant protein structure is greater than 5 angstroms. Therefore, the shift seen cannot be considered significant and the variant CUX protein structure was not reliable. The mean PAE of the alpha‐helix structure in the wild type AFG2A protein structure, where the largest observable shift was observed, is 9.30 angstroms. The mean PAE for both wild type and variant protein structure is greater than 5 angstroms (5.80 angstroms for the c.830A>C (p.Asp277Ala) VUS and 6.69 angstroms for the c.2487C>G (p.Asp829Glu) VUS in AFG2A). Therefore, the shifts seen cannot be considered significant and the variant AFG2A protein structures were not reliable. For these reasons, we did not include protein 3D modeling for the CUX and AFG2A VUS.

Variant NSDHL, PIGO, and POLR1C protein structures showed predicted changes in protein orientation and differences in residue distances.

Variant MCM3AP and SCN1A protein 3D models generated high PAE values that suggest uncertainty for the predicted changes in protein orientation when comparing with wild type protein structures (Figure S2).

Protein three‐dimensional (3D) structure prediction of genes of uncertain significance (GUS)

The VUS in ARHGAP35, BACH2, BAZ2B, DOP1B, FRYL, HMGXB4, NHSL2, P2RX4, RALGAPB, ZNF532, and ZNF536 demonstrated losses in alpha helices and/or beta sheets compared to wild type protein structures in 10 patients (Figure S3) The VUS in PRRC2A resulted in changes to the protein conformation, including losses in loops compared to the wild type protein (Figure S3).

The VUS in EPHA5, MTDH, NLGN2, NRXN2, PRICKLE2, RNF157, and SREBF2 resulted in changes in the conformation of protein compared to wild type in seven patients (Figure S3). DISCUSSION

Reported outcomes of our RWD database

We report 66 patients with 44 different genetic diseases in children and adults with epilepsy using different genetic and genomic investigations from a single epilepsy genetics clinic at an academic health science center. The diagnostic yield of clinical ES was 31% (32% in children and 27% in adults). There were statistically significant differences between Group 1 (genetic diagnoses) and Group 2 (no genetic diagnoses) for patients with movement disorders (more common in Group 1), and seizure onset <2 years (more common in Group 1). Otherwise, we did not find any statistically significant differences between Group 1 and Group 2 for the seizure types, refractory epilepsy, developmental delay, and cognitive dysfunction. We also did not find statistically significant differences between epilepsy phenotypes of DEE, EE, and epilepsy between Group 1 and Group 2.

Biallelic loss‐of‐function variants in HMGXB4 (c.1193_1196del; p.Lys398Argfs*25) were previously reported in a family with three affected children who presented with intellectual disability, global developmental delay, and dysmorphic features. Reverse transcriptase qPCR showed decreased expression of HMGXB4. 29 One of our patients had the same homozygous VUS in HMGXB4. We think that this is the likely genetic diagnosis for this patient.

Previously, 10 patients with neurodevelopmental disorders were reported with heterozygous loss‐of‐function variants in BAZ2B. 30 Our patient with BAZ2B VUS presented with epilepsy (infantile‐onset seizure) and a neurodevelopmental disorder. This patient had a stop gain VUS predicted to result in a loss of 1198 residues and a deletion of exons 19 to 37 in our 3D protein structure analysis compared to the wild type protein. While no associations have been made between variants in BAZ2B and epilepsy, we think that this is a likely genetic diagnosis for this patient.

Fourteen patients with developmental delay, intellectual disability, and dysmorphic features were previously described with heterozygous variants in FRYL. 31 Our patient presented with a similar phenotype to those reported previously, including adult‐onset focal and febrile seizures, developmental delay, cognitive dysfunction, congenital heart disease, and progressive sensorineural hearing loss. FRYL VUS is a stop gain variant predicted to result in a loss of 1825 residues and deletion of exons 32–64 in the 3D protein structure compared to wild type. While more information is required about the effects of P/LP variants in FRYL in humans, we think that this is the likely genetic diagnosis for our patient.

Six patients with global developmental delay, with or without epilepsy, behavioral problems, autistic features, and ADHD had heterozygous variants (two missense, one nonsense, and one frameshift) in PRICKLE2. 32 One patient presented with epilepsy (at age 17 years) and ADHD had a missense variant in PRICKLE2, predicted to result in a change in protein conformation compared to wild type. We think that this is the likely genetic diagnosis for our patient. Additionally, we previously reported functional characterization of a VUS in LARP7 in the medical literature (Epi082), we think that this is the genetic diagnosis for our patient. 33

We entered all our VUS in GUS into matchmaker databases and received information for international collaborations for patient cohorts of BAZ2B, FRYL, NLGN2, NRXN2, and ZNF536. Using functional characterization of a LARP7 VUS (n = 1; 33% diagnostic yield of ES); previous case series of GUS reported in the medical literature for HMGXB4, BAZ2B, FRYL, PRICKLE2 (n = 4; 36% estimated diagnostic yield of ES); loss‐of‐function de novo or biallelic VUS in BACH2, DOP1B, EPHA5, MTDH, NLGN2, P2RX4, PRRC2A, SREBF2, RNF157, and ZNF536 (n = 10; 46% estimated diagnostic yield of ES) and loss‐of‐function VUS in RALGAPB and NHSL2 (not known if de novo) (n = 2; 48% estimated diagnostic yield of ES). Functional characterization of VUS in monogenic genetic epilepsy genes and VUS in GUS will likely confirm genetic diagnoses for monogenic genetic epilepsies to increase our knowledge further for phenotypes and genotypes.

Unfortunately, 60% of the patients with epilepsy in our study had no genetic diagnosis. We hope that matchmaker databases and international research collaborations will be helpful to report the genotypic and phenotypic spectrum of these GUS as causes of monogenic genetic epilepsies in the future.

After completion of clinical genetic investigations and providing genetic counseling, we discharged patients from our epilepsy genetic clinic with a recommendation of a re‐referral in 2 years for ES re‐analysis or VUS re‐classification. Unfortunately, we have received a small number of re‐referrals for ES re‐analysis and for VUS re‐classification over the years. The genetic diagnostic outcome did not change in any of those patients for their diagnostic journey. We cannot perform ES re‐analysis without a new referral and a consenting process.

Diagnostic yields of CMA, TNGSPE, and ES

In a recent systematic review and meta‐analysis, Sheidley et al. 8 examined the diagnostic yield of a variety of genetic tests. The diagnostic yield of CMA in epilepsy was 9% (95% CI = 7%–11%) based on the results of 43 studies that included 5654 patients. In this study, we diagnosed six patients (4%) with an abnormal copy number variant. Sheidley et al.'s diagnostic yield of TNGSPE was 19% (95% CI = 16%–24%) in 30 011 patients from 81 studies, whereas we diagnosed 26% of the patients using TNGSPE. Nine adult patients with normal cognitive functions underwent TNGSPE, and only one patient had a confirmed genetic diagnosis of NPRL3‐related epilepsy. Finally, Sheidley's diagnostic yield of ES was 24% (95% CI = 18%–30%) in 3079 patients from 40 studies. There were 29 patients diagnosed by clinical ES in our current study, and its diagnostic yield was 31%.

Biochemical investigations

Prior to the availability of TNGSPE and ES, extensive biochemical investigations were performed in blood, cerebrospinal fluid, urine, muscle, and skin samples, as the inherited metabolic diseases were highly suspected in the differential diagnosis of epilepsy. The prevalence of inherited metabolic diseases was between 0.58% and 26% in epilepsy in previous studies. 18 , 19 , 33 , 34 We applied our previously reported stepwise diagnostic algorithm to our study cohort and found 0% diagnostic yield for biochemical investigations for patients with epilepsy. For this reason, we discontinued applying our stepwise diagnostic algorithm in epilepsy as of January 2025. TNGSPE and clinical ES should be the first line of investigations for patients with epilepsy. Biochemical investigations should not be applied as first‐line investigations which will be helpful to decrease the burden of invasive blood and urine sample collections on patients and families as well as reduce healthcare system costs.

Biochemical investigations remain critical if a VUS is identified in genes related to inherited metabolic diseases. Appropriate biochemical investigations should be applied in acute or chronic hyperammonemia, lactic or metabolic acidosis, hypoglycemia, multisystem disease, progressive neurodegenerative disease course, or symmetrical progressive brain MRI changes in patients with epilepsy.

Contribution of genetic diagnoses of epilepsy to optimal management

Genetic diagnosis in epilepsy is crucial due to several treatment and management implications, including: (1) treatment of seizures; (2) disease‐specific precision therapies; (3) eligibility for clinical trials; (4) family planning and recurrence risks; (5) prognosis; (6) a end of diagnostic odyssey and pre‐empting further diagnostic investigations. In the Sheidly et al. 8 systematic review, genetic diagnoses resulted in treatment changes in 12%–80% of the patients. In our study, 36% of patients had management implications due to their genetic diagnoses. When treatment and management implications were not available, our clinic was able to at least provide genetic counseling for recurrence risks for themselves and for their family.

SPECIFIC ADDED VALUES OF THE CURRENT STUDY

We integrated protein 3D structure prediction for VUS in genes associated with monogenic genetic epilepsies and in GUS. To the best of our knowledge, protein 3D structure prediction is not applied in clinical studies, and this represents one of our study's distinctive features. Our approach addresses a gap identified in the literature that protein 3D structural analysis may provide mechanistic insights into pathogenicity. By incorporating protein 3D modeling predictions alongside functional characterization of the LARP7 VUS and matchmaker databases, we achieved an estimated diagnostic yield of 48% for clinical ES.

Prospective discontinuation of biochemical investigations based on RWE marks another distinguishing contribution. The diagnostic yield of biochemical investigations was 0% in our cohort. Those should be reserved for specific clinical scenarios. Our study finding challenges the traditional guidelines for stepwise algorithms for biochemical investigations.

Our study includes both pediatric and adult patient populations with epilepsy that differentiates our cohort from most published series. Our study reports ES diagnostic yields stratified by age (32% in children, 27% in adults) and identifies that nine adults with normal cognitive function underwent TNGSPE with only one had a confirmed diagnosis. This highlights the diagnostic challenges in cognitively normal adults with epilepsy, a population underrepresented in genetic epilepsy literature. Our RWD database provides comprehensive phenotyping and reflects actual clinical referral patterns, including patients with inappropriately ordered tests and absent pretest genetic counseling as well as appropriate genetic counseling when treatment implications are unavailable.

We expand epilepsy phenotypes for GUS in our study. We report BAZ2B, FRYL, PRICKLE2, and HMGXB4 variants as a likely genetic diagnosis for our patients and provide detailed phenotypic characterization. Our study differs from large‐scale gene discovery studies that focus on statistical associations rather than detailed clinical characterization of individual cases.

LIMITATIONS OF THE CURRENT STUDY

Our study had several limitations including: (1) The information regarding details of epilepsy was based on chart review and history taken from patients or parents; (2) the COVID‐19 pandemic limited us to relying mostly on virtual appointments, so detailed neurological examination were often not performed; (3) CMA and TNGSPE for epilepsy were requested by pediatricians or pediatric neurologists and only those patients with abnormal genetic and genomic investigations were referred for genetic counseling; (4) for some of our patients, genetic tests were not appropriately ordered, and pretest genetic counseling was not provided; (5) we could not calculate diagnostic yield of CMA and TNGSPE for the study cohort, as we received referrals for genetic counseling for some of those patients; (6) the patient population, and referral reasons were heterogenous that complicated comparison of diagnostic yields and introduced potential selection bias; (7) our study cohort did not include children with brain malformations as they were directed to a multidisciplinary clinic for pediatric brain malformations at our academic health center (8) in silico prediction tools and 3D protein modeling have limitations for the variant classifications, as they are valuable complementary tools. 3D protein modeling could contribute to the resolution of some VUS when integrated with clinical and genetic evidence for the variant interpretation in selected cases. The prediction tools cannot substitute functional characterization of VUS. Unfortunately, we did not perform functional characterization of VUS except the LARP7 VUS; (9) some of our statistical analyses were based on small subgroups. There were multiple comparisons without clear correction for multiple testing. These factors limited the robustness of the statistical conclusions.

CLINICAL RELEVANCE OR FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Through our study cohort, we collected RWD pertaining to 167 patients experiencing epilepsy and we reported 44 different monogenic genetic epilepsies for their phenotypes and genotypes. This work is a significant contribution to the overall RWE for monogenic genetic epilepsies, adding to the matchmaker databases, international collaborations, and potentially providing answers for clinicians working with these rare cases. Building the international database of RWE impacts the future capacity of artificial intelligence in identifying patients and new treatments for monogenic genetic epilepsies. 35

The GUS and VUS in monogenic genetic epilepsy genes will require further functional characterization and international collaborations using matchmaker databases. We believe protein 3D modeling is an important prediction tool to assess the pathogenicity of truncated VUS and may replace functional characterization of variants in the future. Functional characterization of missense variants is essential for monogenic genetic epilepsy discoveries.
