Lecanemab treatment modulates brain volume and cerebrospinal fluid pathways in early Alzheimer's disease: Insights from longitudinal magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Yuxin Li, Yang Zhao, Aocai Yang, Yan Wang, Jiaxiang Xin, Yufan Chen, Xuewen Xing, Fei Liu, Yao Zou, Wei Zhao, Lin Song, Tao Gong, Guangbin Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONLecanemab provides clinical benefits in early Alzheimer's disease (AD), but its longitudinal effects on brain structure, particularly cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) compartments, remain unclear. This study examined treatment‐associated structural changes using longitudinal MRI.METHODSThirty‐one patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or early AD underwent baseline and follow‐up (1–7 months) 3D T1‐weighted MRI during lecanemab treatment. Gray matter (GM), ventricular, choroid plexus (CP), and perivascular space (PVS) volumes were quantified. Longitudinal changes were analyzed with paired tests and linear mixed‐effects models (LMMs).RESULTSSignificant GM volume reductions were observed in AD‐vulnerable regions (hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, precuneus), with increased CSF volumes. PVS volume showed a modest, non‐significant decline. CP enlargement was associated with ventricular expansion.DISCUSSIONLecanemab treatment was associated with coordinated parenchymal and CSF‐related structural changes. CP and PVS dynamics may represent potential MRI‐based markers for monitoring early AD.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common cause of age‐related neurodegeneration and is characterized by progressive cognitive decline, synaptic dysfunction, and widespread neuronal loss that ultimately leads to global brain atrophy. 1 Central to its molecular pathology is the accumulation of β‐amyloid (Aβ), which triggers neuroinflammatory responses, tau‐related neurodegeneration, and large‐scale network breakdown. 2 In accordance with the National Institute on Aging/Alzheimer's Association (NIA/AA) biological framework, AD is defined by abnormal Aβ and tau biomarkers, highlighting the importance of therapies that directly target the underlying disease process.

Lecanemab is a humanized monoclonal antibody that selectively binds soluble Aβ protofibrils and promotes their removal through microglial clearance and peripheral sink mechanisms. 3 , 4 The CLARITY‐AD Phase 3 trial demonstrated that lecanemab reduces cerebral amyloid burden and slows cognitive and functional decline in early AD, with similar findings reproduced in Phase 2b and open‐label extension studies. 5 These results support lecanemab as a disease‐targeting therapy for early‐stage AD. 1

The structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies of Aβ immunotherapy have frequently reported reductions in total and regional brain volumes following treatment. This effect has been described as “pseudo‐atrophy”, reflecting the resolution of treatment‐related inflammation and interstitial fluid accumulation rather than ongoing neurodegeneration. 6 , 7 Thus, longitudinal MRI is essential for distinguishing therapeutic remodeling of brain tissue from true atrophic progression. 8

Beyond parenchymal injury, AD is increasingly recognized as a disorder involving impaired cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) dynamics and clearance. The choroid plexus (CP), the primary site of CSF production, contributes to neuroimmune regulation and protein homeostasis, whereas perivascular spaces (PVS) facilitate CSF–interstitial fluid exchange and glymphatic clearance. 9

Previous neuropathological and neuroimaging studies have demonstrated structural alterations in both CP and PVS across the AD continuum. In AD, the CP exhibits stromal fibrosis, calcification, basement membrane thickening, epithelial atrophy, and inflammatory changes, potentially compromising blood—CSF barrier integrity and CSF secretion. 10 Imaging studies further report CP enlargement in AD and mild cognitive impairment (MCI), with increased CP volume associated with amyloid burden and cognitive decline. 11

Similarly, enlarged PVS—particularly in the centrum semiovale and basal ganglia—are frequently observed in AD and have been linked to amyloid deposition, small‐vessel disease, and impaired glymphatic clearance. 12 Increased PVS burden is widely considered an imaging marker of disrupted interstitial fluid drainage and vascular dysfunction. 7 Together, these findings suggest that CP and PVS remodeling reflects alterations in CSF dynamics and neuroimmune processes that may interact with amyloid‐targeting therapies.

In this study, we examined longitudinal changes in brain parenchymal volumes and CSF‐associated structures, including the CP and PVS, in individuals with MCI or early AD receiving lecanemab. We aimed to determine whether MRI‐derived metrics capture treatment‐associated structural remodeling and alterations in CSF‐related compartments.

METHODS

Study design and ethics

A total of 35 individuals with MCI or early AD were consecutively recruited from the Memory Clinic of Shandong Provincial Hospital. Amyloid positivity was determined by amyloid PET imaging using independent visual assessments performed by two experienced nuclear medicine physicians according to established clinical criteria. Of these, 15 patients who received lecanemab constituted the treatment group, whereas 20 age‐, sex‐, and education‐matched individuals receiving standard clinical care without lecanemab served as the control group. In the control cohort, treatment allocation was based on clinical considerations, patient preference, and drug accessibility rather than neuroimaging‐based exclusion criteria. Importantly, no participant in the control group was excluded from lecanemab therapy due to baseline MRI findings such as extensive cerebral microhemorrhages, superficial siderosis, large‐vessel infarction, or severe white matter (WM) disease. Routine safety sequences, including fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) and susceptibility‐weighted imaging (SWI), did not reveal amyloid‐related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) or other exclusionary findings at baseline. All participants fulfilled established diagnostic criteria, including the 2004 Petersen criteria for MCI 13 and the 2011 NIA/AA criteria for probable AD. 14 This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant national and institutional regulations. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong First Medical University (Approval No. R202407050332). All participants or their legally authorized representatives provided written informed consent prior to participation. For participants with cognitive impairment, informed consent was obtained from both the participant (when capable) and a legal guardian or family member.

Eligible participants were required to be 50–85 years of age, have a Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) score of 0.5 or 1, Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) scores between 15 and 28, be able to undergo MRI scanning, and provide written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included a history of significant head trauma, major cerebrovascular disease, encephalitis, brain tumor, psychiatric disorders, a family history of Parkinson's disease, contraindications to MRI, structural abnormalities involving the brainstem or cervical cord, recent or chronic use of medications affecting neurotransmitter systems, or inadequate image quality due to motion artifacts.

Intervention and follow‐up

In the treatment group, lecanemab (Leqembi; Eisai Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan, and Biogen Inc., Cambridge, MA, USA) was administered intravenously at 10 mg/kg every 2 weeks for up to 14 infusions, without dose titration, consistent with the CLARITY‐AD regimen. 15 Baseline structural MRI (3D T1‐weighted) and neuropsychological assessments were obtained 2 weeks prior to the first infusion.

Although the lecanemab prescribing information recommends MRI monitoring prior to the 5th, 7th, and 14th infusions to screen for ARIA, this study was conducted in a real‐world clinical setting. Accordingly, posttreatment MRI examinations were scheduled based on individualized clinical needs, including safety considerations, patient tolerance, and feasibility of appointments, rather than strictly following the infusion‐based monitoring schedule used in pivotal trials.

As a result, MRI follow‐up occurred at non‐fixed timepoints, comprising a median of 3 scans per patient (range: 2–7), typically within 1–7 months after treatment initiation (commonly around 1,2, 4, 6, or 7 months). This flexible monitoring strategy reflects routine clinical practice and allowed adequate assessment of longitudinal structural changes. Importantly, all acquired scans met the quality requirements for volumetric analysis, and ARIA surveillance was completed without detection of ARIA‐E or ARIA‐H in any participant.

Participants in the control group received standard pharmacological therapy with sodium oligomannate (GV‐971; 150 mg orally three times daily; total daily dose 900 mg), 16 in accordance with its approved clinical use in China. MRI and cognitive assessments in the control group were performed at comparable intervals (approximately 5‐7 months of follow‐up). After rigorous quality control, 14 patients in the lecanemab group and 17 participants in the control group with complete longitudinal MRI datasets were included in the final analyses (Figure 1).

MRI acquisition

All MRI examinations were performed on a 3.0‐T clinical scanner (Siemens MAGNETOM Vida, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with an 18‐channel head coil in the Department of Radiology at Shandong Provincial Hospital. Baseline MRI scans were acquired 2 weeks prior to the first lecanemab infusion, consistent with the predefined imaging schedule. In the lecanemab group, subsequent follow‐up MRI examinations were conducted at individualized, clinically indicated time points within approximately 1–7 months after treatment initiation, most frequently around 1,2, 4, 6, or 7 months. Time‐matched MRI acquisitions were obtained for participants in the control group over a comparable 5‐ to 7‐month follow‐up period.

High‐resolution 3D T1‐weighted magnetization‐prepared rapid gradient‐echo (MPRAGE) images covering the whole brain were acquired on a 3.0 T clinical scanner (Siemens MAGNETOM Vida, Erlangen, Germany) using an 18‐channel head coil. Acquisition parameters were as follows: repetition time (TR) = 2200 ms, echo time (TE) = 2.46 ms, flip angle = 8°, field of view (FOV) = 230 × 230 mm2, matrix = 256 × 256, slice thickness = 0.9 mm, yielding 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.9 mm3 isotropic voxels. The receiver bandwidth was 240 Hz/pixel, and the total acquisition time was approximately 5 min 07 s. Parallel imaging (generalized autocalibrating partially parallel acquisitions [GRAPPA] factor = 2) was used to reduce scan time and minimize motion artifacts.

Additional T2‐weighted, FLAIR, and SWI sequences were acquired for structural screening and safety monitoring, particularly for the assessment of ARIA. 17 ARIA was evaluated according to the criteria established by the Alzheimer's Association Research Roundtable, including ARIA‐E (vasogenic edema or sulcal effusion) identified on FLAIR or T2‐weighted imaging, and ARIA‐H (microhemorrhages or superficial siderosis) assessed on SWI. 18 All scans were independently reviewed by two experienced neuroradiologists.

Image processing and segmentation

All high‐resolution T1‐weighted images were processed using the Free Surfer software suite (version 7.4) for automated cortical reconstruction and volumetric segmentation, generating vertex‐wise cortical thickness maps and regional gray and WM volumes. Additional segmentation of gray matter (GM), WM, and CSF compartments was performed using the FSL FAST and FIRST toolboxes to ensure cross‐method consistency. 19

For CP segmentation, all T1‐weighted images were first nonlinearly registered to the MNI‐ICBM152 standard template using Advanced Normalization Tools (ANTs). CP structures were then delineated in this normalized space using a validated deep learning‐based 3D U‐Net model trained on T1‐weighted MRI data. The resulting CP masks were subsequently inverse‐transformed back into each participant's native space for volumetric quantification.

CP volumes were available in both native and standard spaces; however, all statistical analyses were performed on native‐space volumes to avoid potential bias introduced by spatial normalization. For longitudinal analyses in particular, native‐space CP volumes were used to preserve true anatomical changes without deformation effects from template registration.

PVS volumes were estimated from preprocessed T1‐weighted images using a multiscale Frangi vesselness filter to enhance tubular structures, followed by exclusion of WM hyperintensity masks to minimize false positives. 20 All PVS and CP segmentations underwent visual quality control and were manually refined when necessary by two independent raters blinded to group allocation. Representative examples of the CP segmentation quality are shown in Figure S1.

To account for inter‐individual differences in head size and ensure comparability between the lecanemab and control groups, all regional volumetric measures—including PVS and CP—were normalized to each participant's intracranial volume (ICV) prior to statistical analysis. For PVS, proportion‐based indices were calculated by expressing PVS volume within each anatomical compartment (e.g., WM, subcortical regions, whole brain) as a percentage of ICV. This ICV‐adjusted framework enabled both within‐subject longitudinal analyses and between‐group comparisons to be performed using standardized volumetric metrics.

Structural images were processed through a unified pipeline combining Free‐Surfer and FSL for cortical and subcortical segmentation, including key regions of interest (ROIs) such as the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex and lateral ventricles. Manual quality control was performed for all scans. For longitudinal analyses, ROI volumes were derived in native space to assess within‐subject changes over time. For cross‐sectional comparisons, ROI measures were normalized for intracranial volume to account for head‐size variability. All ROI data were standardized, range‐scaled, and converted to long format for statistical modeling.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Data preprocessing and visualization were conducted with the tidy verse and ggplot2 packages. All statistical tests were two‐tailed, and a significance level of α = 0.05 was applied unless otherwise specified.

Demographic and baseline comparisons

Group differences in demographic and clinical characteristics were examined using appropriate parametric or nonparametric tests. Age was compared with independent‐sample t tests, and sex distribution with Fisher's exact test. Education level and neuropsychological scores, including the MMSE, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD), Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA), Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), and activities of daily living (ADL), were analyzed using t tests or Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests according to data normality.

Baseline MRI‐derived regional volumes were compared between the lecanemab and control groups using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with age and sex as covariates. All volumetric measures were normalized to ICV to account for interindividual variability in head size. To correct for multiple comparisons across brain regions, p values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Regions with FDR‐adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significant and were controlled for in subsequent longitudinal analyses.

Within‐group longitudinal analysis

Longitudinal structural changes within each group were assessed using paired t tests comparing ICV‐normalized regional volumes between baseline (the earliest available scan) and the final follow‐up. Quantitative indices included total brain, GM, WM, hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, fusiform gyrus, para‐hippocampal gyrus, precuneus, posterior and anterior cingulate cortex, amygdala, CP, and PVS volumes. Mean percentage change (Δ mean) and effect size (Cohen's d) were calculated for each region. Multiple comparisons were corrected within each group using the FDR method with an adjusted significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Longitudinal modeling and domain‐level aggregation

Between‐group differences in longitudinal trajectories were evaluated using linear mixed‐effects models (LMMs) with Group, Time, and their interaction (Group × Time) as fixed effects, and Subject as a random intercept to account for repeated measurements. Time was modeled as a continuous variable representing months from baseline, allowing estimation of individual‐specific slopes while accommodating variability in follow‐up intervals across participants. All volumetric measures were ICV‐normalized before modeling. The Group × Time term tested whether the rate of volumetric change differed between the lecanemab and control groups, reflecting treatment‐related effects over time.

Individual trajectories were visualized using locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) with 95% confidence intervals. Group‐level change scores (ΔVolume = follow‐up − baseline) were compared using independent‐sample t tests, and FDR correction was applied to control for multiple. For interpretability and reduction of multiple comparisons, regional volumes were averaged into six anatomical domains (frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital, limbic, and subcortical) based on the Desikan–Killiany and Harvard–Oxford atlases. Mean ICV‐normalized domain volumes were calculated for each participant, and between‐group comparisons were performed to identify broad regional patterns of change.

CSF–Parenchymal coupling analysis

To explore the relationship between CSF dynamics and parenchymal remodeling, longitudinal variations (Δ values) in ICV‐normalized CP and PVS volumes were correlated with changes in regional brain volumes using Pearson correlation analysis. Multiple comparisons were again corrected using the FDR procedure. The primary analysis evaluated the association between ΔCP and ΔLateral ventricle volumes as an index of CSF‐parenchymal coupling, representing coordinated remodeling of CSF‐producing and containing compartments. Secondary subgroup analyses were conducted within each treatment cohort to assess internal consistency and potential treatment‐specific effects.

Longitudinal volumetric changes

During the follow‐up period, patients receiving lecanemab demonstrated measurable structural alterations on magnetic resonance imaging. Paired t tests revealed a significant reduction in total brain volume (−12.22 ± 21.50%, p = 0.0002), accompanied by a corresponding increase in CSF volume (0.48 ± 0.99%, p = 0.0085).

Notable gray matter (GM) volume reductions were observed in limbic and association cortices, particularly within the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, fusiform gyrus, and precuneus (all PFDR < 0.05). PVS volume exhibited a mild declining trend over time (−0.01 ± 0.03%, PFDR = 0.09), which did not reach statistical significance (Figure 2).

By contrast, participants in the control group showed no significant longitudinal changes in total or regional brain volumes (all P FDR > 0.05). In addition, no imaging evidence of ARIA‐E or ARIA‐H was identified in either group during the entire follow‐up period.

MRI structural findings

MRI structural measures in this study refer to ICV‐normalized quantitative volumetric indices derived from high‐resolution 3D T1‐weighted imaging, encompassing both brain parenchymal structures (cortical GM, subcortical nuclei, hippocampal subfields, and WM) and CSF‐related compartments (total CSF, lateral ventricles, CP, and perivascular spaces). These metrics provide a comprehensive characterization of tissue‐ and fluid‐related structural alterations over time. Full ROI‐level results for all parcellated regions are provided in Supporting Information.

In the lecanemab‐treated group, both paired before–after analyses (Δ = Last − Baseline) and linear mixed‐effects (LME) models revealed significant bilateral longitudinal volume reductions across multiple GM regions (Figures 3, 4). For clarity and to avoid redundancy, the main text highlights the left‐hemisphere regions, which were representative of the bilateral patterns, including the hippocampus, precuneus, entorhinal cortex, fusiform gyrus, superior frontal gyrus, frontal gyrus, cuneus, and transverse temporal gyrus. Corresponding right‐hemisphere findings—showing similar directions and effect sizes—are reported in Figures S2–S3. These parenchymal reductions were accompanied by increases in total CSF and lateral ventricle volumes, indicating a bilateral shift in tissue‐fluid balance over the course of treatment.

Correlations between CSF‐associated and regional brain structures

To further examine the interactions between CSF‐related and regional brain structural changes, correlation analyses were conducted between longitudinal variations (Δ values) in CP and PVS volumes and those of regional brain structures. Changes in left CP volume were positively correlated with changes in right lateral ventricular volume (r = 0.66, P FDR = 0.0077). In contrast, Δ PVS in subcortical regions was negatively correlated with Δ entorhinal cortical volume (r = −0.66, P FDR = 0.0105). Similar but non‐significant trends were observed between global PVS changes and entorhinal cortical volume reduction (r = −0.54, P FDR = 0.25) (Figure S4).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we characterized longitudinal changes in regional brain structures and CSF‐associated compartments in patients with AD undergoing lecanemab therapy. Treatment was associated with significant reductions in local GM volume, most prominently in the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, anterior cingulate cortex, and fusiform gyrus. These GM decreases were accompanied by a gradual enlargement of the CP. Such concomitant changes suggest that lecanemab‐related amyloid clearance may influence not only parenchymal tissue integrity but also the morphology of periventricular structures involved in CSF regulation.

In the present study, patients receiving lecanemab exhibited regional GM volume decreases, most notably within the hippocampus, entorhinal cortex, and precuneus. This pattern is broadly consistent with observations from previous post‐immunotherapy MRI studies, 2 where similar cortical and limbic volume reductions were interpreted as apparent “atrophy” following Aβ removal. Rather than indicating progressive neurodegeneration, such changes may reflect the redistribution of interstitial fluid and the resolution of inflammatory edema accompanying Aβ clearance. 21 Lecanemab‐mediated reduction of soluble and insoluble Aβ aggregates could alleviate microvascular and interstitial disturbances, contributing to a partial restoration of local fluid equilibrium within vulnerable cortical and limbic regions. Collectively, these findings suggest that the observed GM decreases may represent a normalization of tissue hydration and extracellular fluid balance.

The downward trend in PVS volume, although not statistically significant, provides further mechanistic insight. PVS are CSF‐filled conduits surrounding penetrating vessels that facilitate interstitial solute clearance through the glymphatic system. 22 Their enlargement is a well‐established indicator of impaired perivascular drainage, vascular dysfunction, and neuroinflammation. The subtle contraction of PVS observed in the lecanemab group may thus suggest partial recovery of perivascular fluid circulation and improved solute clearance following Aβ removal. 23 Similar reversibility of PVS morphology has been demonstrated in obstructive sleep apnea and other conditions after restoration of CSF dynamics, 24 further supporting the role of PVS as a dynamic biomarker of glymphatic recovery. Given that PVS changes did not survive correction for multiple comparisons, these findings should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis‐generating, especially considering the relatively modest sample size.

In our study, patients receiving lecanemab demonstrated longitudinal increases in CP volume over the follow‐up period. This finding is in line with previous neuroimaging studies reporting CP hypertrophy in AD and other neuroinflammatory conditions, where such changes have been associated with altered CSF dynamics and immune activation. 25 The CP plays a dual role as the principal site of CSF production and as an immunoregulatory interface mediating Aβ transport and peripheral immune signaling. During lecanemab therapy, the observed CP expansion may reflect structural remodeling associated with shifts in CSF dynamics or neuroimmune activity. However, in the absence of direct measurements of CSF production or inflammatory biomarkers, these interpretations remain speculative. 26 Accordingly, CP enlargement should be interpreted cautiously and may represent part of a broader treatment‐related alteration in CSF‐related compartments rather than a definitive marker of functional change.

In this study, changes in left CP volume were positively correlated with changes in lateral ventricular volume, suggesting that enlargement of the CSF‐producing CP may be accompanied by expansion of the CSF‐containing ventricles, possibly due to altered CSF turnover during amyloid clearance. Together, these findings suggest that lecanemab‐induced amyloid removal may influence both CSF dynamics and local tissue hydration, leading to coordinated structural changes across fluid‐regulating and cortical regions.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this was a single‐center study with a relatively modest sample size, which may limit statistical power and the generalizability of the findings. Larger, multicenter cohorts will be necessary to validate these observations across more diverse populations and clinical settings. Second, clinical and biochemical indicators were not incorporated to validate the imaging findings; integrating longitudinal cognitive assessments and CSF biomarkers in future research would help clarify the clinical relevance of the observed structural changes. Third, variability in follow‐up intervals inherent to real‐world practice may have influenced the estimation of longitudinal trajectories, despite the use of linear mixed‐effects modeling to account for repeated measures. Nonetheless, the study reflects real‐world longitudinal clinical implementation of anti‐amyloid therapy and provides preliminary evidence that CSF‐related structures, including the CP and PVS, may exhibit treatment‐associated remodeling. These findings offer a foundation for future mechanistic and large‐scale investigations

Taken together, our findings suggest that lecanemab treatment was associated with coordinated changes across brain parenchymal and CSF‐related structures. The observed pattern—characterized by cortical and limbic volume reductions, PVS contraction trends, and CP enlargement—may represent the structural correlate of a restored CSF–glymphatic balance following amyloid removal. These volumetric markers, particularly PVS morphology and CP dynamics, could serve as sensitive imaging biomarkers for monitoring treatment response and neuroimmune adaptation in future anti‐Aβ interventions.
