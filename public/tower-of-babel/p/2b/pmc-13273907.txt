Hutch diverticulum revealed by intermittent inguinal mass and associated vesico-ureteral reflux in a child
Authors: Ahmed Chaabouni, Khouloud Ouertani, Wafa Elleuch, Mohamed Fourati, Nouri Rebai, Mourad Hadj Slimen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenital bladder diverticula are rare in children and result from a developmental defect of the detrusor muscle, allowing mucosal outpouching through the bladder wall. Hutch diverticulum is an uncommon congenital paraureteral diverticulum frequently associated with vesicoureteral reflux. We report the case of a 7-year-old boy born to second-degree consanguineous parents who presented with an intermittent right inguinal mass and decreased urine output. Imaging revealed a giant Hutch diverticulum with grade II reflux. Surgical treatment consisting of diverticulectomy and intravesical ureteral reimplantation using the Politano–Leadbetter technique led to a favorable outcome.

Introduction

Primary congenital bladder diverticulum (PCBD) occurs in the absence of obstructive factors such as posterior urethral valves (PUV) and neurogenic bladder. Hutch Diverticulum (HD) is defined as the protrusion of the mucosal and submucosal layer through the muscle bundles of the underlying detrusor muscle. HD is located at the vesicoureteral junction with a backward direction from the homolateral ureteral orifice.1 HD is usually unilateral and more common in male patients. It may be associated with the Ehlers-Danlos, Williams-Elfin and Menkes syndromes. HD usually occurs in childhood and rarely during adulthood. It is found in 0.2-13% of all children presenting with urinary tract infection.1

Its close anatomical relationship with the ureter explains the strong association with vesicoureteral reflux (VUR), as distortion of the ureterovesical junction compromises the antireflux mechanism. In pediatric populations, VUR is relatively common (≈1% of children) and is primarily related to a short intramural ureter, resulting in reflux of varying severity.1

VUR is considered nearly physiological during infancy and often resolves spontaneously within the first few years of life. With growth and maturation, progressive elongation of the intravesical ureter and improved detrusor function enhance the competence of the antireflux mechanism. As a result, a significant proportion of cases especially low-grade VUR (grades I–III) undergo spontaneous resolution within the first few years of life. The higher grade of VUR and the lower probability of spontaneous resolution of this condition during early childhood, involving surgical correction of VUR.1,2

Case presentation

A 7 year-old-boy born to second-degree consanguineous parents, with an unremarkable perinatal history, presented with a one month history of an intermittent right inguinal mass. The mass was associated with vomiting, generalized abdominal pain, irritability, and reduced urine output. On examination, the infant was alert, no dyspnea, and no jaundice. His vital signs were within normal limits. There was no edema. Abdominal examination revealed palpable mass at the right inguinal area, measuring 3 × 4 cm. The mass was ovoid in shape, had regular margins, was firm and tender on palpation, and was pulsatile.

His laboratory investigations revealed a leucocyte count within normal limits, no anemia. Renal function was preserved, with normal serum creatinine levels. Urinalysis revealed no sign of urinary tract infection (UTI).

Abdominal-pelvic ultrasound revealed a paraureteral bladder diverticulum measuring 4 × 4 cm suggestive of a Hutch diverticulum (Fig. 1). The postoperative course was uneventful, with complete resolution of the inguinal mass and favorable clinical outcome.

Discussion

Hutch diverticulum is a rare congenital paraureteral anomaly caused by focal detrusor deficiency at the ureterovesical junction, leading to distortion of the antireflux mechanism and frequent association with vesicoureteral reflux. Given their congenital origin and low likelihood of spontaneous resolution, diverticula are generally diagnosed in childhood and may manifest with urinary tract infections.

Literature review1,2 emphasizes that certain reflux may resolve spontaneously with no surgery needed.The presence of associated anatomical abnormalities significantly reduces this likelihood. Further, it may lead to complications, including recurrent infections and renal scarring.

Primary VUR is common in patients with a history of febrile UTI, with a prevalence of almost 40% in such cases, but also in nearly 20% of patients with antenatally diagnosed hydronephrosis.1 In the general population, VUR is probably present in 1% of children.1 The antireflux mechanism depends on an adequate length of the intramural ureter and sufficient detrusor backing at the ureterovesical junction.1

This present case is distinguished by two key features: the atypical presentation as an intermittent inguinal mass and the absence of any urinary tract symptom.

In Hutch diverticulum, the paraureteral defect may distort the ureteral hiatus or shorten the effective submucosal tunnel, facilitating retrograde urine flow from the bladder into the upper urinary tract. This explains the association between Hutch diverticulum and ipsilateral VUR.3, 4, 5, 6

Two clinical presentations of VUR have been classically described in the literature, based on gender segregation of the disease. The first group includes male infants, diagnosed before 2 years of age, often presenting with a history of antenatal hydronephrosis. The second group would be for female infants diagnosed at an older age, commonly after toilet-training, with lower grades of VUR.1

The originality of our case lies in its rare manifestation likely related to the dynamic filling of a giant diverticulum extending toward the inguinal region. Clinically, such presentation may mimic a reducible inguinal hernia, hydrocele, spermatic cord cyst, or the closure anomalies of the peritoneal-vaginal canal, potentially delaying diagnosis without recognition of the underlying urinary anomaly.

Therefore, bladder diverticulum should be considered as an important diagnosis, when the abdominal or inguinal mass changes with bladder filling, in children presenting with intermittent inguinal swelling even in the absence of urinary symptoms associated. As any anatomical abnormalities with associated particularities, bladder diverticulum in children comes with special investigations and management.

Imaging is a master key to diagnosis. For several decades, the standard approach to assess children for potential VUR was to directly assess VUR by voiding retrograde cystourethrography (VCUG) after renal ultrasound (US).1,7 In 2011, the Subcommittee on Urinary Tract Infections of the American Academic of Pediatrics recommended limiting VCUG investigation after at least two episodes of urinary-tract infections or after the first episode of febrile UTI in cases of abnormal renal US, such as pelvic or ureteral dilation, renal scars, or bladder wall abnormality.1,8 This approach, named the bottom-up approach after febrile UTI, offers the advantage of confirming the presence or absence of VUR of any grade but with the risk of discomfort due to urethral catheterization or induced pyelonephritis.1,2 In current practice, many pediatric nephrologists and urologist choose to limit radiation exposure or infectious risk of invasive explorations choosing renal ultrasound as a first-line examination to indentify urinary-tract abnormalities. However, ultrasound alone cannot reliably assess the grade of reflux or its relationship to the ureteral orifice and diverticulum. Voiding cystourethrography (VCUG) remains the gold standard for confirming VUR and determining its grade.1

The main diagnostic modalities include ultrasonography of the genitourinary system, antegrade cystourethrogram and dynamic tests including manometry and urometry. They allow an imaging study in order to determine the potential consequences of VUR and, rarely, of obstructive uropathy, and evaluate any renal or ureteral damage.5 The antegrade cystourethrogram, which is the major diagnostic modality, visualizes both the storage and the voiding phase of the bladder.5

In our case, the patient had an abdominal-pelvic ultrasound revealing a paraureteral bladder diverticulum associated to minor ureteral distension. To complete the investigations, he had Voiding cystourethrography (VUR) confirming Hutch diverticulum and staging the reflux to grade II.

Although grade II reflux may disappear spontaneously, treatment strategies must integrate the global anatomical context rather than reflux grade alone. In our patient, the diverticulum was symptomatic through intermittent inguinal mass, creating a persistent structural abnormality unlikely to be resolved spontaneously.

Besides, the coexistence of vesicoureteral reflux and the obstruction of the uterovesical junction which is the case of Hutch diverticulum, is a strong indication for surgery for our patient, despite preserved renal function.

Large diverticula, sizing more than 1/3 of the urinary bladder transverse diameter, may produce major complications such as a recurrent urinary tract infection, lithiasis, subcystic obstruction and rupture, are considered as indications for surgical management 1, 2, 3, 4, 5.

The classic surgical approach of removal of a HD is via cystectomy. Nowadays, the excision of a HD can be achieved transurethrally, laparoscopically and robotically assisted.6,7 In the context of the open method, the catheterization of the ipsilateral ureter is the first step and then, the mural part is prepared. Next, the diverticulum's mucosa is removed, and the underlying muscular wall is reinforced. If the ureter is part of the wall of diverticulum, resection of the diverticulum and reimplantation of the ureter are performed.1,2,9

Ureteral reimplantation is the gold standard treatment for VUR. It aims to create a new submucosal channel for the distal ureter, measuring at least 4 times the ureteral diameter (Paquin's law),1 in order to obtain a passive flap valve mechanism when intravesical pressure increases. Multiples techniques of reimplantation have been described over the years, with an excellent success rate of 95%–98% and low complication rate.10,11 These techniques are sorted between intravesical and extravesical approaches. The intravesical reimplantation developed by Cohen is the most frequently performed to treat VUR. The main alternative to this technique is the suprahiatal Politano Lead Better ureteroneocystostomy.1,10,11

Our patient underwent diverticulectomy associated with intravesical ureteral reimplantation using the Politano-Leadbetter technique. This remains a reliable and well-established reconstructive option in children.

Another notable aspect of the case is the history of second-degree parental consanguinity. There is no proven causality link between VUR or HD and consanguinity. Congenital anomalies of the kidney and urinary tract (CAKUT), including VUR, have demonstrated familial clustering and genetic heterogeneity.

This raises the possibility that congenital paraureteral diverticula may share developmental pathways with other anatomical anomalies. Reporting such associations is valuable and may stimulate future genetic or epidemiological studies.

Conclusion

From a practical standpoint, this case conveys several clinical messages. First, Hutch diverticulum should remain part of the differential diagnosis of pediatric bladder outpouchings and unexplained VUR. Second, unusual extra-pelvic presentations such as intermittent inguinal swelling are possible. Third, normal renal function does not exclude clinically significant lower urinary tract malformations. Finally, timely surgical management can provide definitive correction and prevent delayed complications such as recurrent infections or reflux nephropathy. It expands awareness of the variable presentation of Hutch diverticulum and emphasizes the need for genetic research.
