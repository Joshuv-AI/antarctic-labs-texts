Next-generation vaccine adjuvants: Integrating nanotechnology, systems immunology, and computational approaches for precision vaccinology
Authors: Tina Zarrinpanah, Maryam Mashhadi Abolghasem Shirazi, Seyed Mohammad Hasan Modarressi, Setareh Haghighat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVaccines based on purified antigens, recombinant proteins, and nucleic acid platforms increasingly depend on adjuvants to induce robust, durable, and appropriately polarized immune responses in humans. While classical adjuvants such as aluminum salts and oil-in-water emulsions have enabled the success of many licensed vaccines, their largely empirical design limits adaptability to emerging pathogens and population-specific needs. This review presents a translational framework for next-generation vaccine adjuvant development by integrating nanotechnology-based delivery systems, innate immune signaling mechanisms, and systems-level computational strategies relevant to human vaccination. We summarize the mechanisms and clinical relevance of licensed and advanced adjuvants, including alum, MF59, AS01/AS04, saponins, toll-like receptor agonists, and lipid nanoparticles, with emphasis on influenza, HPV, herpes zoster, and COVID-19 vaccines. By linking immunological mechanisms with delivery engineering and predictive modeling, this review highlights rational strategies to support safer and more effective human vaccines.KEYWORDS: Vaccine adjuvants, human vaccines, nanotechnology-based delivery systems, systems immunology, precision vaccinology, lipid nanoparticles, innate immune signaling

Introduction: from empirical adjuvant use to rational immunoengineering

Vaccination remains one of the most successful medical interventions in human history, preventing millions of deaths annually and reshaping global public health. Traditional vaccines based on live-attenuated or inactivated pathogens have effectively controlled diseases such as smallpox, polio, and measles. However, the rising complexity of infectious and chronic diseases, exemplified by emerging pathogens like SARS-CoV-2 and immune-evasive cancers, has exposed the limitations of conventional vaccine platforms that rely on empirical adjuvant formulation. Subunit and recombinant vaccines, while safer and more stable, frequently exhibit low immunogenicity and require strategically designed adjuvants to trigger potent and durable immune responses.1–3

Adjuvants derived from the Latin adjuvant, meaning “to help,” are no longer passive enhancers of immunity but precision immunomodulators that shape both the magnitude and the quality of vaccine-induced protection. Classical adjuvants such as alum, MF59, and CpG ODNs have demonstrated the power of innate immune activation, yet their mechanistic predictability, tissue-specific targeting, and personalization remain limited.4,5

While numerous reviews have summarized adjuvant types and mechanisms, few have systematically connected their immunological pathways with advances in computational modeling, nanotechnology, and systems immunology. This conceptual gap constrains the rational integration of immune signaling with materials science a convergence that is critical for the design of next-generation, safe, and adaptive vaccines.2,6

Recent advances in vaccinology have highlighted the critical importance of adjuvants in enhancing vaccine efficacy and modulating immune responses. Modern vaccine platforms, particularly subunit, recombinant, and mRNA vaccines, often exhibit limited intrinsic immunogenicity and therefore require effective adjuvants to induce robust and durable immune protection. Vaccine adjuvants not only enhance antigen presentation and activation of innate immunity but also shape the magnitude and quality of adaptive immune responses. In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward the development of next-generation adjuvants with improved safety profiles and targeted immunomodulatory properties. Therefore, understanding the mechanisms of action, clinical applications, and safety considerations of different adjuvant classes is essential for rational vaccine design and the development of more effective vaccines.

The present review aims to provide a comprehensive overview of licensed and emerging vaccine adjuvants, with particular emphasis on their mechanisms of action, immunological properties, advantages, limitations, and current clinical relevance.2,7,8

Evolution of adjuvant science: from crude enhancers to mechanistic precision tools

The history of adjuvant discovery closely mirrors the evolution of modern immunology. The first evidence of adjuvant activity was reported in 1925, when Gaston Ramón observed that certain substances, such as agar, lecithin, or saponin, enhanced the production of diphtheria and tetanus antitoxins in animals.9 This discovery marked the birth of adjuvant science, transforming vaccine formulation from a purely empirical process into a field guided by immunological principles. As vaccine antigens became increasingly purified, transitioning from whole pathogens to subunit and recombinant forms, their intrinsic immunogenicity declined, thereby intensifying the need for adjuvants to restore or enhance immune potency.2,3

Among the earliest and most extensively used adjuvants are aluminum-based compounds, collectively known as alum. Approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 1926, aluminum hydroxide, aluminum phosphate, and aluminum sulfate rapidly became standard components of many human vaccines.10 Alum exerts its effect primarily through depot formation at the injection site, allowing sustained antigen release and facilitating uptake by antigen-presenting cells (APCs). It predominantly induces a Th2-skewed response, characterized by strong antibody production but limited T-cell activation. Despite its remarkable safety record and widespread use in vaccines such as hepatitis B and diphtheria – tetanus – pertussis (DTP), alum’s inability to elicit robust Th1 or cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) responses has driven the pursuit of more versatile adjuvant systems.11

The late 20th century marked a pivotal shift from empirical formulations to rational adjuvant design, propelled by advances in molecular immunology and innate immune signaling. Oil-in-water emulsions such as MF59 and AS03 emerged as safe, potent alternatives that induce balanced Th1/Th2 responses and enhance both humoral and cellular immunity.6 Meanwhile, saponin-based adjuvants such as QS-21 and Matrix-M introduced a new paradigm of nanoparticulate immunostimulants capable of promoting strong CTL responses. Concurrently, the discovery of Toll-like receptor (TLR) agonists such as monophosphoryl lipid A (MPL; TLR4 agonist) and CpG oligodeoxynucleotides (TLR9 agonists) enabled targeted activation of innate immune pathways, mimicking natural infection while maintaining high safety profiles.1,12

In the 21st century, the integration of nanotechnology and immune engineering has fundamentally redefined adjuvant development. Novel systems such as liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, and lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) now serve dual functions: acting as antigen delivery vehicles while simultaneously stimulating innate immune sensors. The clinical success of LNP-based mRNA vaccines against COVID-19 exemplifies this paradigm, demonstrating that delivery vehicles themselves can act as effective adjuvants by promoting cytosolic delivery and immune activation.13,14

Over time, adjuvant classification has evolved from composition-based to mechanism-based frameworks. Historically, adjuvants were grouped by their physical or chemical nature: mineral salts, emulsions, or microbial derivatives. In modern vaccinology, they are more accurately categorized according to their immunological functions, which fall into two major groups:

Delivery systems, which facilitate antigen stabilization, controlled release, and efficient presentation to immune cells (e.g., liposomes, emulsions, polymeric or lipid nanoparticles).

Immunostimulatory adjuvants, which directly activate pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) and cytokine signaling networks (e.g., TLR agonists, saponins, and microbial components).

This functional classification reflects a conceptual transition from empirical enhancement to precision immunomodulation, where adjuvants are designed to elicit specific immune outcomes humoral, cellular, or mucosal-based on antigen properties and disease targets.1

Immunological architectures of adjuvant function: linking innate sensing to adaptive memory

Adjuvants are indispensable components of modern vaccines, functioning as immune potentiators that enhance both the magnitude and quality of vaccine-induced immune responses. While antigens alone can initiate immune recognition, their intrinsic immunogenicity, particularly in subunit, recombinant, or peptide-based vaccines, is often insufficient to generate durable or protective immunity. Adjuvants bridge this gap by activating innate immune sensors, optimizing antigen processing, and orchestrating adaptive immune responses to achieve long-term protection.1

Mechanistically, adjuvants act at multiple levels of the immune cascade. They stimulate antigen-presenting cells (APCs), particularly dendritic cells and macrophages, through the engagement of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and NOD-like receptors (NLRs). This activation enhances antigen uptake, processing, and presentation through major histocompatibility complex (MHC) molecules, thereby priming CD4+ helper and CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). The resulting interplay between innate and adaptive immunity promotes a balanced response that encompasses both antibody-mediated and cell-mediated arms. For example, aluminum-based adjuvants mainly promote Th2-biased immune responses and enhance antibody production through depot formation and inflammasome activation. In contrast, emulsion-based adjuvants such as MF59 and AS03 enhance immune cell recruitment and improve antigen delivery to draining lymph nodes. TLR agonist adjuvants, including CpG oligodeoxynucleotides and monophosphoryl lipid A (MPL), induce potent Th1-oriented cellular immune responses by activating innate immune signaling pathways. These mechanistic differences play a crucial role in determining the quality, durability, and specificity of vaccine-induced immunity.15

An ideal adjuvant should exhibit defined and reproducible biological activity, ensuring strong and consistent immune stimulation across diverse populations, including infants, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals, while minimizing local inflammation or systemic reactogenicity.16

Adjuvants enhance vaccine performance across multiple immunological dimensions:

Enhancement of Antigen Immunogenicity: Adjuvants increase antigen visibility and facilitate efficient immune recognition, accelerating the initiation of robust immune responses.

Durability of Immune Memory: By promoting the differentiation of long-lived memory B and T cells, adjuvants extend protection duration and reduce the frequency of booster immunizations.

Improved Quality of Immune Response: They enhance antibody affinity maturation, isotype switching, and neutralizing antibody generation, which are essential for high-quality protective immunity.

Induction of Mucosal Immunity: Certain adjuvants, particularly liposomes, chitosan-based polymers, and nanoparticles, stimulate local IgA production at mucosal surfaces, blocking pathogen entry through epithelial barriers.17

From a public health perspective, adjuvants play a crucial role in antigen dose sparing, allowing more vaccine doses to be produced from limited antigen stocks, an advantage particularly valuable during pandemics or in resource-limited settings.18 Furthermore, in multivalent vaccine formulations, adjuvants minimize antigenic interference and help achieve balanced immune responses against multiple targets.19

Despite their numerous benefits, adjuvants are not without limitations. They lack direct antimicrobial or therapeutic activity and may vary in efficacy depending on the antigen structure, route of administration, or host immune status. Consequently, designing optimal adjuvant antigen pairs requires a deep mechanistic understanding of immune signaling pathways, cytokine modulation, and host-pathogen interactions to ensure both safety and efficacy.20

Ultimately, adjuvants function as immunological architects, fine-tuning the immune landscape to achieve precise, potent, and durable protection. They represent not merely auxiliary components, but pivotal elements that define the transition from empirical immunization to rational, mechanism-based vaccine design a cornerstone of next-generation vaccinology.21

Precision pairing of adjuvants and antigens: toward rational vaccine engineering

The success of vaccination depends not only on the antigen’s molecular design but also on the strategic selection of an adjuvant that complements its immunological properties. Different antigen types, protein, peptide, polysaccharide, or nucleic acid, elicit distinct immune profiles and therefore require tailored adjuvant strategies to optimize efficacy, duration, and safety. Understanding these antigen – adjuvant relationships enables the rational design of precision vaccines capable of inducing balanced humoral, cellular, and mucosal immunity.1

Protein and subunit antigens

Protein-based and subunit vaccines are among the safest and most established vaccine platforms; however, they are inherently weak immunogens due to the absence of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). Consequently, they depend on potent adjuvants to activate innate pathways and enhance antigen presentation.22

Aluminum salts (alum) remain the most widely used adjuvants, inducing strong antibody responses through a Th2-biased mechanism. Yet, their inability to elicit robust Th1 or CTL responses has led to the development of more sophisticated systems.

Oil-in-water emulsions (MF59, AS03) promote balanced Th1/Th2 immunity and are used in influenza and hepatitis vaccines.

Saponin-based adjuvants (QS-21, Matrix-M) induce strong cellular immunity and enhance cross-presentation, exemplified by the herpes zoster vaccine Shingrix (AS01, MPL + QS-21).11

Nanoparticle carriers, such as PLGA and liposomes, improve antigen stability and targeting to APCs, prolonging immune activation.

Peptide-based vaccines

Peptide vaccines offer high specificity and safety but suffer from poor immunogenicity due to rapid degradation and inefficient MHC class I/II presentation.

TLR agonists such as CpG ODNs (TLR9), Poly I:C (TLR3), and MPL (TLR4) are key enhancers of innate immune activation, driving cytokine secretion and DC maturation.

Saponin-based adjuvants (e.g., QS-21) amplify CD8+ CTL responses, which are crucial in cancer and chronic viral infections.23

Nanoparticle formulations (PLGA, liposomes, or lipid nanocarriers) provide sustained antigen release and protection from proteolytic degradation.

Combination adjuvants, such as CpG + QS-21 or TLR agonist + emulsion systems, have demonstrated synergistic enhancement of both humoral and cellular immunity, particularly in therapeutic cancer vaccines.24

Polysaccharide and glycoconjugate vaccines

Polysaccharide-based vaccines derived from bacterial capsules induce T cell – independent responses that are short-lived and poorly immunogenic in infants and the elderly.

Conjugation with protein carriers (CRM197, tetanus toxoid) converts these into T cell – dependent antigens, enhancing memory formation.

Traditional adjuvants like alum and MF59 augment antibody titers and expand serotype coverage, while calcium phosphate offers a biocompatible alternative with minimal IgE induction, making it suitable for repeated immunizations.25

Recent advances explore nanoconjugate adjuvants where polysaccharides are coupled to nanoparticle scaffolds to induce balanced Th1/Th2 immunity against pathogens.26

DNA and mRNA vaccines

Nucleic acid – based vaccines represent a paradigm shift in vaccinology. Their efficacy depends on efficient intracellular delivery and innate immune activation.

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) have become the gold standard, as they encapsulate and stabilize nucleic acids while facilitating cytosolic delivery via endosomal escape. Importantly, ionizable lipids within LNPs can engage TLR7/8 and RIG-I pathways, providing intrinsic adjuvanticity.27

Polymeric carriers (PLGA, cationic polymers) and TLR agonists (CpG, Poly I:C) are also employed to augment immune activation and induce type I interferon responses, enhancing both CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell responses.

The clinical success of Pfizer-BioNTech (BNT162b2) and Moderna (mRNA-1273) vaccines validated LNPs as multifunctional adjuvant delivery systems that define next-generation immunization platforms.28

Live-attenuated and viral vector vaccines

Live-attenuated and viral vector vaccines naturally elicit strong immunity by mimicking infection and promoting in situ antigen expression. Although they often do not require exogenous adjuvants, certain formulations benefit from them to boost mucosal immunity, prolong protection, or enable dose sparing.

Chitosan-based adjuvants, due to their mucoadhesive and immunostimulatory properties, enhance local IgA and mucosal immune activation.29

Silica nanoparticles, liposomes, and bilosomes have been utilized to increase antigen retention and APC targeting, particularly in intranasal or oral vaccines against influenza, cholera, and poliovirus.30

Mucosal adjuvants play an increasingly important role in preventing infections at the epithelial entry barrier, a frontier of next-generation immunization strategies.31

Integration of AI-based predictive models may enable real-time optimization of adjuvant – antigen matching for precision vaccinology.

The immunogenic potential of a vaccine depends critically on the molecular compatibility between antigen structure and adjuvant mechanism.

The following sections summarize the principal adjuvant classes currently approved or under active investigation in modern vaccinology.

Mineral salt–based adjuvants

Aluminum-Based Adjuvants (Alum)

Aluminum salts, collectively referred to as alum, are the oldest and most widely used human vaccine adjuvants. Approved in 1926, alum remains integral to vaccines against hepatitis A/B, diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, and HPV.46

Alum enhances immune responses by forming a depot at the injection site, sustaining antigen release, and promoting antigen-presenting cell (APC) recruitment. It mainly induces a Th2-skewed humoral response, generating robust antibody titers but limited Th1 or cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) activity.

Although safe and cost-effective, alum’s limitations include minimal cellular immunity and occasional granuloma formation. Current research focuses on hybrid alum nanoparticle composites and TLR agonist co-formulations to overcome these constraints.47

Calcium Phosphate Adjuvants

Calcium phosphate offers a biocompatible and biodegradable alternative to aluminum salts. It induces both humoral and cellular immunity with a balanced Th1/Th2 profile and is particularly suited for intracellular pathogen targets. Its natural occurrence minimizes long-term toxicity risks, yet challenges such as formulation reproducibility and particle aggregation have limited large-scale deployment. Recent innovations in nano calcium phosphate adjuvants show enhanced immune potency and physicochemical stability.48

Emulsion-based adjuvants

Oil-in-Water (O/W) Emulsions

O/W emulsions consist of submicron oil droplets dispersed in water, promoting antigen uptake and controlled release.

MF59, a squalene-based emulsion, was the first licensed adjuvant of this type (influenza vaccine Fluad) and functions by recruiting monocytes and dendritic cells, enhancing cytokine expression, and promoting antigen transport.49

AS03, containing squalene and α-tocopherol, further augments innate activation and enables antigen dose sparing, especially beneficial during pandemics.50 Both MF59 and AS03 elicit balanced Th1/Th2 responses and are effective in the elderly and immunocompromised.

Water-in-Oil (W/O) Emulsions

W/O emulsions, such as Freund’s Adjuvants and Montanide ISA series, form long-lasting depots and induce strong Th1-biased cellular responses.51

Although classical Freund’s adjuvants are restricted to preclinical research due to reactogenicity, Montanide ISA 51 and 720 have become valuable in therapeutic cancer and veterinary vaccines, providing potent CTL activation while maintaining acceptable tolerability.

Nanoparticle and nano-structured adjuvants

Polymeric nanoparticles (PLGA)

Biodegradable poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles protect antigens from degradation and enable sustained release. They improve dendritic cell uptake and cross-presentation, inducing both humoral and cellular responses. PLGA is FDA-approved and applicable for protein, DNA, and mRNA vaccines.52

Chitosan nanoparticles

Derived from chitin, chitosan is a cationic polysaccharide with mucoadhesive and immunostimulatory properties, ideal for mucosal vaccines. It promotes epithelial adhesion and enhances IgA and systemic IgG responses, making it promising for intranasal and oral vaccines against respiratory and enteric infections.53

Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs)

LNPs revolutionized vaccination through the success of mRNA vaccines (Pfizer-BioNTech, Moderna). Composed of ionizable lipids, cholesterol, phospholipids, and PEG-lipids, they stabilize mRNA, facilitate cytosolic delivery, and activate TLR7/8 signaling.

LNPs act as both delivery vectors and innate immune activators, forming the foundation of next-generation nucleic acid vaccines and cancer immunotherapies.54

Inorganic and metallic nanoparticle adjuvants

Metal-based nanoparticles such as silver (AgNPs) and gold (AuNPs) exhibit strong antigen-loading and immunostimulatory capabilities. AuNPs provide a stable scaffold for antigen conjugation, improving humoral and CTL responses.55

Zinc oxide (ZnO) and cobalt oxide (Co3O4) nanoparticles modulate cytokine secretion and promote Th1/Th2 balance.

Silica nanoparticles (SiNPs) offer tunable porosity and biocompatibility.

Mechanistic pathways of adjuvant action: cellular programming through nanostructured systems

Adjuvants enhance vaccine efficacy by bridging the innate and adaptive immune systems, acting as immune catalysts that amplify, shape, and sustain protective responses. Unlike antigens, which provide specificity, adjuvants orchestrate immune activation through complementary biological and physicochemical mechanisms. Their actions encompass innate immune stimulation, antigen depot formation, APC activation, immune polarization, and mucosal enhancement, collectively determining vaccine potency and durability.56

Activation of innate immunity

The innate immune system provides the first line of defense against pathogens, recognizing conserved microbial motifs through pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including Toll-like receptors (TLRs), NOD-like receptors (NLRs), and RIG-I – like receptors (RLRs).

Adjuvants mimic these microbial signals to initiate NF-κB and IRF3/7 signaling cascades, leading to the release of IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and type I interferons. These cytokines recruit and activate antigen-presenting cells (APCs) such as dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages at the injection site.

Different adjuvants activate distinct innate immune signaling pathways. For example, MPL functions as a TLR4 agonist and CpG ODNs (TLR9 agonist) induce Th1-skewed responses by promoting IL-12 and IFN-γ secretion. In contrast, alum activates the NLRP3 inflammasome resulting in caspase-1–mediated IL-1β release and local inflammation. Similarly, lipid nanoparticles nanoparticles (LNPs) also exhibit intrinsic adjuvanticity by engaging TLR7/8 and RIG-I pathways during mRNA vaccine delivery. Beyond innate immune activation, many adjuvants also improve vaccine efficacy through sustained antigen retention and controlled release mechanisms.2

Antigen depot formation and sustained release

Many adjuvants create a “depot effect,” retaining antigen at the injection site for gradual release, thereby prolonging APC exposure and enhancing immune priming.76 Aluminum hydroxide and calcium phosphate bind antigens electrostatically, ensuring slow antigen diffusion. Oil-in-water emulsions (MF59, AS03) form dynamic depots that attract immune cells and maintain antigen persistence. This controlled release enhances immune memory formation and reduces the number of required booster doses. In addition to depot formation, efficient recruitment and activation of antigen-presenting cells are essential for the initiation of adaptive immunity.

Recruitment and activation of antigen-presenting cells (APCs)

Efficient activation of antigen-presenting cells (APCs) is a hallmark of adjuvant activity and plays a central role in initiating adaptive immune responses. Upon exposure to adjuvants, dendritic cells (DCs) upregulate co-stimulatory molecules such as CD80, CD86, and MHC-II, while simultaneously secreting cytokines that drive T-cell differentiation and immune polarization.44 Nanoparticle- and liposome-based adjuvants are particularly effective in this process due to their nanoscale size (20–200 nm), surface charge, and physicochemical characteristics, which optimize cellular uptake through endocytosis and phagocytosis.

Importantly, increasing evidence suggests that certain nanostructures can function not only as antigen delivery systems but also as intrinsic adjuvants with direct immunostimulatory activity. The physicochemical properties of nanomaterials, including particle size, hydrophobicity, surface composition, and charge, can independently regulate immune activation and cellular programming through stimulation of pattern-recognition receptors (PRRs), inflammasome signaling, and cytokine induction pathways. For example, lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) used in mRNA vaccine formulations exhibit inherent adjuvant properties by activating TLR7/8-mediated sensing pathways, promoting type I interferon production, and facilitating endosomal escape for cytosolic antigen expression. Similarly, cationic liposomes enhance dendritic cell maturation and antigen cross-presentation, thereby promoting cytotoxic T-cell responses. Polymeric nanoparticles such as PLGA and chitosan nanoparticles also improve APC activation, antigen persistence, mucosal uptake, and transcytosis through M cells, ultimately enhancing both systemic and mucosal immunity.45

In addition, inorganic nanomaterials including silica nanoparticles and gold nanoparticles have demonstrated the ability to modulate cytokine production, inflammasome activation, and APC recruitment depending on their structural and surface properties. Virus-like particles (VLPs) similarly possess self-adjuvanting characteristics due to their repetitive nanoscale architecture, which efficiently stimulates innate immune recognition and B-cell receptor clustering. Collectively, these findings support the emerging concept that nanostructures themselves may act as multifunctional next-generation adjuvant systems by integrating antigen delivery, immune activation, and immunomodulatory functions within a single platform, thereby improving vaccine efficacy, precision immune targeting, and long-term protective immunity. Following APC activation and antigen presentation, adjuvants critically influence the magnitude and polarization of adaptive immune responses.

Since most pathogens invade through mucosal surfaces, mucosal adjuvants play a vital role in eliciting local IgA and tissue-resident T-cell responses. Applied immunoengineering: translating adjuvant science into vaccine platforms

Infectious diseases

MF59 and AS03 expand antibody breadth in influenza vaccines.

MPL (AS04) and QS-21 (AS01) power Cervarix (HPV) and Shingrix (zoster) vaccines.

STING and TLR7/8 agonists are under evaluation for cancers and HIV.87,88

Cancer immunotherapy

Adjuvants are pivotal for breaking immune tolerance to tumor antigens. QS-21, CpG, and Poly I: C boost DC activation and CTL generation, while nanoparticle carriers enable co-delivery of antigens and immune modulators for precision tumor targeting.89

Autoimmune and Therapeutic Applications

Beyond prophylactic vaccination, adjuvants are being engineered for immune reprogramming to suppress overactive responses in autoimmunity, allergies, and chronic inflammation. Cytokine-based and TLR-modulating adjuvants hold promise for next-generation tolerogenic vaccines.90

Synergistic and Combination Adjuvant Systems

Modern vaccine design increasingly utilizes combination adjuvants to achieve synergistic immune activation.

AS01 (MPL + QS-21): dual activation of TLR4 and saponin pathways → robust Th1/CTL responses (Shingrix).

AS04 (Alum + MPL): depot effect with TLR signaling (Cervarix).

Such systems exemplify rational immunoengineering, integrating multiple adjuvant functions for optimized potency and safety.91

Summary

Adjuvants operate through an intricate interplay of innate sensing, antigen persistence, APC activation, and adaptive modulation. Their effectiveness depends on precise coordination between cellular signaling, cytokine dynamics, and delivery kinetics.

Integrating nanotechnology with molecular immunostimulation has transformed adjuvants from passive additives into programmable immune regulators, enabling vaccines that are safer, longer-lasting, and adaptable to emerging global health challenges.92

The rapidly evolving landscape of global health, marked by emerging and reemerging infectious diseases, antimicrobial resistance, and cancer, demands next-generation vaccines that are safe, potent, and capable of inducing broad, durable, and context-specific immunity.

Adjuvants, once regarded as inert additives, have now emerged as strategic immunomodulators driving the rational design of advanced vaccine platforms. Despite substantial progress, critical scientific, regulatory, and translational challenges must be addressed before adjuvant science can fully realize its transformative potential.2

The next era of vaccinology depends on the convergence of immunology, materials science, and computational design. Balancing efficacy and safety

Achieving the delicate equilibrium between immunogenic potency and safety remains the central challenge in adjuvant innovation. Excessive innate activation, as seen with early formulations like Complete Freund’s Adjuvant, can trigger strong reactogenicity or systemic toxicity, rendering them unsuitable for human use. Despite their immunological benefits, vaccine adjuvants may also be associated with safety concerns that require careful evaluation. Common adverse reactions include local inflammation, pain, redness, swelling, and transient systemic symptoms such as fever and fatigue. In some cases, excessive immune activation may contribute to reactogenicity or rare inflammatory complications. Therefore, the balance between immunogenic efficacy and safety remains a major challenge in adjuvant development.

Recent advances in adjuvant engineering have focused on improving biocompatibility, reducing toxicity, and achieving more targeted immune stimulation. Nanoparticle-based delivery systems and rationally designed adjuvants may provide improved safety profiles while maintaining strong immunostimulatory activity. Continuous preclinical and clinical safety assessments remain essential for the successful translation of novel adjuvants into licensed vaccines.93

Future approaches emphasize precision immunomodulation, where immune activation is localized, transient, and tunable.

Biodegradable nanoparticles (e.g., PLGA, LNPs) and hydrogel-based carriers can confine adjuvant activity to target tissues.

Controlled-release formulations and pH-responsive delivery systems minimize systemic inflammation while preserving efficacy.

Advancing this balance will require integrating in vivo biosensing and AI-based toxicity prediction to monitor and optimize immune stimulation in real time.

Mechanistic understanding and predictive modeling

Although the immunostimulatory potential of adjuvants is well recognized, their molecular mechanisms remain incompletely defined.

Emerging fields such as systems immunology, multi-omics (transcriptomics, proteomics, metabolomics), and computational network modeling are enabling deeper insights into adjuvant immune system interactions.7

Predictive in silico models and organ-on-chip microphysiological systems can simulate vaccine responses, helping identify molecular signatures of protection and reduce reliance on animal models.

Such integrative approaches will accelerate rational adjuvant design and reduce the empirical “trial-and-error” paradigm that has dominated vaccinology for decades.94

Personalized and population-specific vaccinology

Interindividual variability including age, sex, genetics, microbiome composition, and comorbidities strongly influences vaccine responsiveness.95

A single adjuvant formulation cannot achieve uniform efficacy across all demographic groups.

The next frontier lies in precision vaccinology, where adjuvants and delivery systems are personalized to distinct immunological phenotypes:

Neonates and elderly may require adjuvants enhancing weak innate signaling.

Immunocompromised patients may benefit from sustained-release or cytokine-co-delivery systems.

Advances in immune profiling and machine learning will allow adjuvant optimization for individual immune signatures, ushering in the era of personalized immunization.8

Expanding mucosal and cancer immunization

Because most infections originate at mucosal interfaces, designing adjuvants capable of inducing robust mucosal IgA and tissue-resident memory T cells (Trm) is a pressing priority.

Promising platforms include:

Chitosan-based polymers, liposomal bilosomes, and mucoadhesive nanoparticles for oral or intranasal vaccination.96

Bacterial-derived immunostimulants (e.g., detoxified CTB, flagellin) that enhance mucosal antigen presentation.

In oncology, adjuvants play a pivotal role in breaking tumor immune tolerance. Combining immune checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., PD-1/CTLA-4 blockers) with saponin- or TLR-based adjuvants can synergistically enhance tumor-specific CTL activity.97

Hybrid nano-immunostimulant platforms are now enabling targeted delivery of tumor antigens with simultaneous activation of innate sensors.

Regulatory and manufacturing barriers

Regulatory approval remains a major bottleneck in adjuvant development.

The limited historical safety database, combined with stringent toxicological requirements, slows clinical translation.22

Global collaboration among agencies such as EMA, FDA, and WHO is essential to establish standardized frameworks for evaluating adjuvant safety and immunogenicity.

Moreover, scalable and cost-effective manufacturing technologies including continuous nanoparticle synthesis and cold-chain – independent formulations are critical to ensure equitable global access, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.

Lessons from the rapid regulatory success of mRNA – LNP COVID-19 vaccines illustrate how adaptive frameworks can expedite approval while maintaining rigorous safety oversight.98

Artificial intelligence and machine learning

AI algorithms are increasingly applied to predict adjuvant – antigen interactions, optimize nanoparticle formulations, and forecast toxicity profiles. Deep learning tools such as DeepVacPred, VaxiDL, and ImmunoNet analyze molecular features to identify synergistic immune activators.

Systems immunology and multi-omics

High-throughput transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, and single-cell datasets enable mapping of immune signatures associated with protection versus inflammation. Integrative models derived from these datasets guide the rational pairing of adjuvants with specific immune pathways.

Synthetic biology and bioengineering

Synthetic biology allows modular design of “smart” adjuvants that sense and respond to local immune cues. Programmable microbial vesicles, cytokine-mimetic peptides, and biomaterial-based sensors exemplify next-generation platforms bridging biology and computation.1

AI-driven toxicity prediction and ethical integration of human data in adjuvant design

Artificial intelligence (AI) and computational modeling are emerging as transformative tools in the rational design of vaccine adjuvants. By integrating high-dimensional immunological datasets including transcriptomic, proteomic, and cytokine profiles, AI algorithms can predict adjuvant efficacy, toxicity, and immune polarization patterns before clinical testing. Machine learning frameworks such as DeepVacPred, AdjuvantGPT, and ImmuneNet enable in silico screening of molecular candidates, thereby reducing reliance on animal studies and accelerating translational development.

Beyond predictive modeling, AI-driven immunogenicity mapping facilitates personalized vaccine design by correlating host genetic or microbiome features with immune responsiveness. However, the use of large-scale human immunological data introduces new ethical and regulatory challenges, including data privacy, algorithmic bias, and cross-border data governance. Establishing transparent standards for AI validation, equitable data sharing, and informed consent will be essential to ensure that computational adjuvant design remains both scientifically rigorous and ethically responsible. Ultimately, the convergence of AI-based modeling, systems immunology, and ethical bioengineering will define the next frontier of precision vaccinology, where adjuvants are not empirically selected but intelligently engineered to match the complexity of human immunity.7

Clinical applications and trial evidence of vaccine adjuvants

Several established vaccine adjuvants have demonstrated significant clinical efficacy and acceptable safety profiles in human trials and licensed vaccine formulations. Aluminum salts remain the most widely used adjuvants in licensed vaccines and have shown strong antibody-inducing capacity in hepatitis and diphtheria-tetanus vaccines.99 MF59, an oil-in-water emulsion adjuvant used in influenza vaccines, has demonstrated enhanced immunogenicity, particularly in elderly populations.100 Similarly, AS03-containing influenza vaccines showed robust immune responses during pandemic influenza outbreaks.101

In addition, AS01, a liposome-based adjuvant system containing monophosphoryl lipid A (MPL) and QS-21, has shown high efficacy in the recombinant herpes zoster vaccine and malaria vaccine formulations.102 CpG-containing adjuvants have also demonstrated promising clinical outcomes by promoting Th1-biased cellular immune responses and improving vaccine-induced protection.103 Collectively, these clinical findings highlight the important translational role of adjuvants in improving vaccine efficacy, durability, and protective immunity.

Conclusion: from empirical enhancement to intelligent immune design

Adjuvants have progressed far beyond their original role as passive immune boosters, emerging instead as precision immunomodulators that actively shape the magnitude, quality, and persistence of vaccine-induced immunity.

With advances in molecular immunology, systems biology, and materials science, adjuvants are now recognized as core determinants of vaccine efficacy, not merely auxiliary components. Their capacity to direct immune polarization, promote durable memory formation, and synergize with antigen design has positioned them at the heart of next-generation vaccine innovation.

Looking forward, adjuvants will underpin the development of multi-functional vaccines that are both preventive and therapeutic, capable of combating infectious diseases, cancers, and immune-mediated disorders alike. The convergence of nanotechnology, computational vaccinology, and synthetic biology promises to yield programmable adjuvant systems that can precisely tune immune responses to individual and population needs.

Future advances in vaccine adjuvant research are expected to focus on the development of highly targeted, personalized, and safer immunomodulatory systems. Emerging technologies such as nanotechnology, artificial intelligence-assisted adjuvant discovery, and mucosal vaccine platforms may significantly improve vaccine efficacy against infectious diseases and cancer. In addition, combination adjuvant systems capable of inducing balanced humoral and cellular immunity represent a promising strategy for next-generation vaccines.

A deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms underlying adjuvant-mediated immune modulation will facilitate the rational design of vaccines with enhanced efficacy, durability, and safety. Continued research in this field will play a pivotal role in addressing current and future global health challenges. Sustained collaboration between academia, biotechnology innovators, and regulatory bodies will be crucial to transform these scientific advances into safe, effective, and globally equitable vaccines that redefine preventive and therapeutic immunization in the 21st century.
