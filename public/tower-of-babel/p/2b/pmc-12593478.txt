Prioritizing food systems interventions to reduce adolescents’ nutrition insecurity and malnutrition in low-income settings: protocol for the Dishi Fiti Ishi Fiti (Eat Well Live Well) mixed-methods study
Authors: Gwenyth O Lee, Benjamin JJ McCormick, Wiktoria Staromiejska, Vincent Mutuku, Elizabeth L Fox, Simon Kimenju, Patrice Akusa Mawa, G Asiki, Shauna Downs
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionAdolescents in informal urban communities, defined as settlements that fall outside of formal governmental planning and regulatory frameworks, are at increasing risk of poor-quality diets and malnutrition in all its forms. The food environment is the interface of adolescent food choice and the broader food system, and food environment interventions have the potential to improve adolescent diets and nutritional outcomes.Methods and analysisWe will conduct a mixed-methods study, integrating methods from participatory systems science and nutritional epidemiology to characterise linkages among adolescents’ neighbourhood and home food environments, and their food choices, diets and nutritional outcomes. We will recruit adolescents, caregivers, school staff and food system actors from five communities along a gradient of urban informality in Nairobi, Kenya, to participate in cognitive mapping, group-based modelling and a cohort study over one academic year to evaluate dietary choices and nutritional outcomes.Ethics and disseminationThe study has been approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Rutgers University (Pro2024001981) and Amref Health Africa (P1831-2025). Adult participants will provide written informed consent, and adolescents will provide written informed assent to participate in the study. Findings will be disseminated through peer-reviewed journals, conference presentations and to participants through planned participatory interaction throughout the study.Keywords: Adolescents, NUTRITION & DIETETICS, PUBLIC HEALTH, Research Design

Introduction

Nearly a third of the world’s population lacks access to adequate food, and over three billion people cannot afford a healthy diet. Nutrition insecurity, defined as a lack of consistent and equitable access, availability and affordability to safe foods and beverages,1 2 leads to malnutrition through two main pathways: (1) insufficient intake of energy and nutrients, leading to undernutrition; and (2) overconsumption of inexpensive, energy-dense foods, leading to obesity and diet-related non-communicable diseases (figure 1).3 These burdens of malnutrition are a leading cause of poor health, disease, disability and death.4 87% of all countries globally now experience multiple burdens of malnutrition,5 and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are the most affected.6 7

Pathways to malnutrition, adapted from Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations.3

Informal urban settlements, defined as settlements that fall outside of formal governmental planning and regulatory frameworks,8 pose an especially high risk of both undernutrition and overweight/obesity. The food environments of informal settlements fail to support healthy food choices, as the foods sold in these communities are often less safe, less nutritious and more expensive.9,11 Residents of informal settlements are also socially and legally vulnerable, which reduces their ability to seek and demand better options. Multiple studies suggest that residents of informal urban settlements are at high risk of multiple burdens of malnutrition, often experiencing undernutrition and obesity within the same household.12,17 Given that approximately 24% of the world’s urban population now resides in such settlements,18 there is an urgent need to identify strategies to improve food environments and nutritional outcomes in this context.

Adolescence (10–19 years)19 is a key period during which dietary behaviours, such as food choice, eating behaviour and dietary intake,20 develop.21,25 Moreover, the physical, social and developmental changes that adolescents experience put them at heightened risk for poor nutrition outcomes.26,28 Adolescence is also when the risk of childhood undernutrition transitions into increased susceptibility to cardiometabolic diseases in adulthood.29 Despite this, data on the diets of adolescents in LMICs are limited, and data from informal settlements are even more so.27 28 30 The little data that do exist suggest that adolescents’ diets often lack nutritious foods such as fruits and vegetables and include high amounts of energy-dense sugar-sweetened beverages, salty and sweet snacks.27 28 30 While evidence-based interventions to support healthy adolescent food choices have been developed and implemented in HICs, less is known about how to support healthy adolescent food choices in LMICs.30,32 Prior approaches have often focused on school food environments,33 34 which have led to improvements in diets35,37 and attenuation of Body Mass Index (BMI) increases over the intervention periods.38 39 Leveraging the school platform is one of the most effective strategies for delivering nutrition interventions to adolescents in LMICs,39 however, there has been less consideration of other spaces outside of school where adolescents spend time.

We have designed the ‘Dishi Fiti Ishi Fiti’ (Eat Well Live Well) study to inform approaches to address nutrition insecurity and malnutrition among adolescents in LMICs. We use a systems science approach to examine multilevel influences on adolescents’ food choices, dietary patterns and malnutrition to identify the root causes of nutrition insecurity and prioritise interventions to address them both in and outside of school settings. The objectives of ‘Dishi Fiti Ishi Fiti’ are to: (objective 1a) characterise the food system and (objective 1b) identify the individual, household and community level factors that influence adolescents’ food choices in informal and formal urban settlements in Nairobi, Kenya; (objective 2) quantify the influence of household and community nutrition insecurity on (objective 2a) dietary patterns, multiple forms of malnutrition and (objective 2b) food preferences; and (objective 3) codesign food systems interventions to improve adolescents’ food choices, nutrition security and malnutrition outcomes.

Methods and analysis

Study timeline

Data collection for this 4-year project began in July 2025. We anticipate completing the project in 2028.

Conceptual underpinning

Our study design builds on the Innocenti framework,40 which conceptually links adolescent diets and food systems. The framework comprises food system drivers, determinants (ie, food supply chains, food environments, behaviours of caregivers and adolescents), influencers and interactions, which together determine adolescent diets.40 The dynamic linkages among the elements of this framework enable us to identify entry points for the design and implementation of food systems interventions with the greatest potential to improve adolescents’ diets. 40The Innocenti framework onto our research questions is mapped in figure 2, guided by the steps for identifying a package of actions to cocreate child-centred food systems described by Hawkes et al.41 A key feature of this framework is that it is centred around adolescents and their decisions, before moving outwards to address nutrition insecurity in the form of household-, community- and systems-level drivers.

The adolescent-centred food systems approach and study. Our study approach is designed to address the assessment questions outlined by Hawkes et al41 to identify the package of actions to create a child-centred food system.

Study setting

Sub-Saharan Africa has the largest number of countries experiencing multiple burdens of malnutrition42 43 and the greatest proportion of people living in informal settlements. Kenya, one of the most populous countries in the region, exemplifies this challenge.44 60% of the population lives in informal settlements, and the country experiences high burdens of stunting (26% of children <5 years), anaemia (29% of women of reproductive age) and overweight/obesity (37% of adult women).5 Between 2010 and 2019, adolescent overweight increased by 47% in girls and 70% in boys, and underweight declined by <10% in both.5 In Nairobi, adolescents from rural areas often first reside in informal settlements when they migrate to the city.45 As a result, adolescents are a disproportionately large share of the informal settlement population, leading to calls for health programming tailored to ‘the special circumstances of adolescents in informal settlements’.45

We will work in five communities (or wards) within Embakasi South, which is among the poorest of the 17 constituencies in Nairobi County.46 The five wards represent a gradient of informality (figure 3). According to the typology proposed by Mwau and colleagues,47 two of these communities are described as ‘informal shack settlements’ (Mukuru Kwa Njenga and Mukuru Kwa Reuben), two are defined as ‘tenements’ (Kware and Pipeline) and one is a mixed low- and middle-income formal estate (Imara Daima) (figure 4). This gradient supports our understanding of how informal food environments may discourage or promote healthy food choices, and how household-level socioeconomic status intersects with community-level food environments.

Impact of community formality on the food environment. A gradient of informal to formal settlements provides data on how variation in the food environment affects the food choices and nutritional outcomes of low-income adolescents.

Location of study communities within Nairobi. Location of the five study communities within Nairobi (blue box insert).

Within each of the five study communities, we will examine schools and their surrounding neighbourhoods. We will select at least two low-fee community schools—the primary schools in informal settlements, which serve approximately three-quarters of students living in these settings.48 49 As adolescents spend much of their time in and around their schools, school and school-adjacent food environments are important spaces in which their independent food choices are initially developed and become established.

Recruitment

We will purposively select schools based on our community-based partners’ prior relationships, size (larger schools are prioritised), location and inclusion of different adolescent ages. We will then recruit adolescents, adolescent authority figures (caregivers, teachers and principals), food system actors and community stakeholders within each of the study sites.

Adolescents: after making school staff fully aware of the project and gaining their permission to conduct the study, we will hold meetings to introduce both adolescents and their parents/caregivers to the study and to answer their questions. We will include adolescents aged 10–18 years old (expected to be 11–19 by the end of the study) who attended one of the study schools and resided in Embakasi South, Nairobi. We will exclude pregnant adolescents from our study sample. We will note the age and gender distribution of the student population to evaluate potential selection bias and enable participant data to be weighted based on their representation in the school population. Adolescents will be stratified by age group (ie, 10–14 years old and 15–19 years old) and gender, with an approximately equal distribution among both to be expected.

Objective 1a

Characterise the food system. We will map the food environment surrounding each study school. We define the school-adjacent food environment as all vendors selling food and/or beverages (eg, stores, mobile carts, restaurants, etc) located within 250 m of the school. We will use a combination of food environment assessments (community food environment mapping, in-depth vendor assessment and cost of a healthy diet) from the Food Environment Toolbox51 to measure: food availability (type and diversity of foods on offer), food affordability (food prices), food properties (storage, quality and labelling), vendor properties (location, type and vendor hygiene) and food messaging (promotion, advertising and information about food). Food environment mapping will be conducted three times to account for seasonal variability in these attributes. The timing of the data collection will coincide with the adolescent surveys conducted in objective 2a.

Objective 1b

Identify the individual, household and community-level factors that influence adolescents’ food choices in informal and formal urban settlements in Nairobi, Kenya. Cognitive mapping and group-based modelling are two participatory systems mapping methods that are often used in combination.52 We will use these methods to first identify factors that influence adolescent food choices (cognitive mapping) and then to map the causal relationships between these factors, including their drivers and feedback (group-based modelling). This approach is intended to support shared understanding and communication about decision-making in complex social systems,53 as well as the development of robust responses to key problems. Activities will be conducted with participants representing each of the following groups: (1) adolescents from each study community; (2) adults whose decision-making capacity impacts the adolescent’s food environment including (a) ‘adolescent authority figures’: parents, teachers and principals; and (b) ‘food system actors’: food vendors, wholesalers or other participants in the supply chain. All interviews and focus group discussions will be recorded verbatim, translated and transcribed.

Objective 2

Quantify the influence of household and community nutrition insecurity on objective (2a) dietary patterns, multiple forms of malnutrition and objective (2b) food preferences. We will conduct an open cohort study of adolescents enrolled in each study school, collecting measures of nutrition insecurity and nutritional status (anthropometry and blood collection for micronutrient analysis) at three time points at the beginning, middle and end of a single academic year. We will also interview caregivers at enrolment and measure their heights and weights. Finally, we will conduct limited surveys with school staff to collect descriptive information about each school related to meal programmes, school gardens and other school programmes or infrastructure. Details of the planned cohort data collection are provided in online supplemental table 1. All caregiver-reported measures will be collected once, at enrolment, while adolescent-reported measures will be conducted at each cohort study visit (up to three times per child, except for dietary recalls, which will be collected up to six times per child). To evaluate food preference, we will also conduct discrete choice experiments (DCEs), a quantitative technique from behavioural economics that asks individuals to state their preference between different scenarios and examine the extent to which an individual is willing to trade one attribute (eg, quality) for another (eg, price).54 55

Objective 3

Codesign food systems interventions to improve adolescents’ food choices, nutrition security and malnutrition outcomes. Stakeholders will be invited to participate in a series of consultative discussions over the course of model development and application. The goal of discussions in the first study years will be to compare models developed by adolescents and other actors, identify points of alignment and misalignment between stakeholder perceptions and adolescent perceptions, and to develop a robust systems map that integrates the perspectives of multiple actors and integrates multiple data sources to identify contextually appropriate interventions.

Sample size determination

Objective 1a: given that we do not know the number of vendors that will be present in our study catchments during the food environment assessments, it is difficult to provide an accurate estimate; however, we anticipate that the number of vendors will not exceed 5000. Objective 1b: during the free listing exercises, we will enrol 10–20 adolescents per school, ∼10–20 adolescent authority figures and ∼10–20 food systems actors, as recommended to reach theoretical saturation and to enable subgroup comparisons.56,58 The same participants will be included during the group-based modelling exercises, although broken up into groups of 4–10 individuals, which is aligned with the recommended size for conducting group-based modelling.56 Objective 2a: up to 1000 adolescents and their primary caregivers may be enrolled in the cohort, although only ∼700 will be present at any given time. Our sample size calculation ensures the detection of associations between nutrition insecurity and dietary/nutritional outcomes within each community and age group (10–14 and 15–19 years). Using probability proportional sampling in larger schools, we estimate ∼90% participation based on prior Mukuru studies.59 The projected sample size supports detectable differences of 0.22 or greater BMI-for-age Z (BAZ) and 3.2 g/L or greater haemoglobin (Hb), with smaller effect sizes detectable over time due to the reduction of error variance due to repeated measures. Objective 2b: the same adolescents will be included in the DCEs—this will allow us to have sufficient power to analyse differences among different age groups and transects (informal, semiformal and formal),60 and is aligned with the sample size required to gain sufficient precision in choice experiments.61 Objective 3: finally, while there is no fixed number of participants that should be included to inform intervention ranking, we will aim for 10–15 stakeholders (maximum of 25) to balance the inclusivity of different viewpoints with the practicality of consensus building.61 62

Data management and analysis

Qualitative data will be audio-recorded, and limited participant demographic data will be noted. Survey forms will include built-in checks to reduce missing responses and flag logical inconsistencies. Quality control checks will be run to check for missing or implausible values, duplicated records or inconsistencies. Discrepancies will be resolved through communication between the data management team and field supervisors.

Objective 1a

We will calculate descriptive spatial statistics, summarising vendor types and the attributes of foods sold and promoted in each neighbourhood. Based on the foods sold, we will calculate the Market Food Diversity Index and an adapted version of the modified Retail Food Environment Index,5163,65 which assesses the diversity and overall healthiness of foods sold. Using food price data, we will calculate the Cost of Healthy Diet,66 which assesses the lowest cost to purchase the required amounts of food to consume a healthy diet using the healthy diet basket as the reference diet,67 given that Kenya’s national dietary guidelines68 lack detailed quantitative recommendations for each food group. We will also compare food prices across food groups using the relative caloric price of foods.69 These data will be used to triangulate adolescent and caregivers’ perceptions of their food environments with objective food environment data.

Objective 1b

Key themes from the interview transcripts will be identified through content analysis,70 71 and transcripts coded by two members of the research team.72 All transcripts will be open, axial and selectively coded as per accepted approaches to analyse qualitative data.73 A codebook will be created and maintained as part of an iterative process—the coders will meet regularly to review the codebook, identify new codes and discuss coding discrepancies. The food choice influencers identified through the free listing exercise will be ordered by salience74 using Smith’s S index,75 to identify factors influencing food choices that are more familiar, important or relevant within a particular community.74 Similarities and differences between the most salient influencers across schools, neighbourhoods and age groups will be compared using correlation matrices.76 77 The pile sorting of foods and food outlet types will be visualised using non-metric multidimensional scaling maps and hierarchical cluster analysis.74 We will use the ratings of the food by the different influencers (property-fitting analysis) and transcripts of the interviews to interpret how the different food choice influencers relate to the different foods or food outlet types.74 We will conduct partition analysis to determine whether the ways adolescents characterised and sorted foods differed across neighbourhoods.78 System models from each group-based modelling exercise will be compared across communities, and models from different key informant groups will be conceptually integrated to determine how adolescent food environments and higher-level elements of the food system intersect.

Objective 2a

The primary exposures for aim 2 are measures of nutrition insecurity. As there is no validated and accepted measure of nutrition insecurity, we have combined several new and established tools to capture the theoretical domains of nutrition insecurity, namely, food and water insecurity in the home environment, and food access in the community environment. These tools are specified and summarised in online supplemental table 1) and include the Food Insecurity Experience Scale,79 the Household Water Insecurity Experience Scale,80 the Child Food Insecurity Experience Scale81 and the Food Environment Perceptions Survey (FEPS).82 The FEPS, while not yet fully validated, is a novel tool recently developed and piloted by our team in two LMICs to assess perceptions of food availability, affordability, convenience, promotion and quality, as well as how these dimensions influence food choices and food sources.82 These instruments collectively provide a holistic view of nutrition insecurity. Our outcomes measures are: (1) adherence to dietary guidelines, (2) micronutrient status (measured by altitude- and inflammation-adjusted Hb, inflammation-adjusted transferrin receptor, and inflammation-adjusted retinol binding protein and thyroglobin) and (3) BAZ scores.83 Dietary patterns will include the probability of meeting the dietary reference intakes for macro- and micronutrients84 based on the Kenyan national dietary guidelines.

We will first assess the relationship between nutrition insecurity and dietary and nutritional outcomes through generalised linear mixed models.85 86 We will adjust for potential confounding variables, including household socioeconomic status, based on our conceptual causal model of these factors in relation to exposure and outcome. We will increase statistical precision by adjusting for additional predictors of child diets and nutritional status, such as age and sex. Following these preliminary regression models, we will then assess the causal pathways between nutrition insecurity and child nutritional status through adolescent food choices and diet as mediators. Using a conceptual framework based on hypothesised relationships between these variables, a Bayesian network87 88 will be developed. The Bayesian network will be constructed as conditionally independent generalised linear regressions. We hypothesise a mediating role of nutrient intake such that micronutrient status will be determined (at least partially) by intake. Each node (ie, the micronutrient biomarker outcomes and micronutrient intake variables) will include additional dummy variables to adjust for age and sex. To fit the model, the diet variables will be standardised. Mean effects (for continuous biomarkers) and odds ratios (for binary micronutrient status biomarkers) per 1 SD difference and 95% credibility interval will be calculated. The model will be used to investigate how conditions from different transects might affect biomarkers. We will compare reported nutrition insecurity, adolescent behaviours, dietary intake data and biomarkers across the transect and refit our overall model for each community or community type. To evaluate potential bias related to differential loss to follow-up (LTFU) in the cohort, we will compare the characteristics of adolescents represented in multiple cohort timepoints to those present at only some timepoints, conduct sensitivity analyses to determine the extent to which model results are impacted by different missing data assumptions, and consider the use of multiple imputation to adjust for LTFU.

Objective 2b

We will analyse the DCE data using mixed logit models. We will estimate trade-offs between, and substitutions among, different food attributes.61 Preference coefficients for each attribute will be expressed as relative importance scores by dividing the difference in utility between the highest and lowest level for a single attribute by the sum of the differences of all attributes.89 Differences in preferences between adolescents living in different neighbourhood transects and age groups will be examined. We will also assess the effects of nutrition security, adherence to dietary guidelines and nutritional status on preference structures by their inclusion as interaction terms in expanded models.90

Objective 3

We will ask stakeholders to define desirable targets for intervention efficacy (eg, a reduction of anaemia of 5%; an increase in adolescent dietary diversity of 10%) and use empirical data from objectives 1 and 2 to determine whether a particular intervention might meet this target. We will then conduct a multicriteria decision analysis (MCDA) to rank interventions based on stakeholder-derived criteria related to the feasibility, acceptability, equity, sustainability and likely impact of the interventions identified.91 These overarching criteria have been previously developed and applied to the prioritisation of interventions to improve diets in LMICs.62 92 Prior to beginning the MCDA, stakeholders will refine the criteria to be included in the MCDA, based on their experience, to ensure there is buy-in for the ranking process.93 Once the criteria have been finalised, stakeholders will be asked to weight the criteria (by distributing 100% across all criteria) based on their relative importance.91 Each criterion will then be scored for each intervention, ranging from a score of 1 (difficult, unacceptable) to 4 (easy, acceptable).62 Likert scales will be used to score the likely impact.62 The scores will then be summed, using the weights decided on by stakeholders, to generate a ranking of all codesigned interventions. This aim will also lead to the development of a MCDA scoring system that can be applied to prioritise future interventions and policies.

Patient and public involvement statement

Our mixed-methods study uses a participatory approach to actively engage with adolescents, parents, teachers/principals, food system actors and community stakeholders throughout the project. Representatives from G-Thamini, a non-governmental organisation that supports youth empowerment and education in the study communities, were engaged in study design, and adolescents, representatives and study schools, and other key stakeholders will be engaged throughout the study through the completion of our objectives. We will convene a community advisory board to gain feedback on different project components, ensure the project is responsive to the communities’ needs and provide opportunities to discuss any concerns that may arise, as well as the most effective ways to disseminate study findings to the community.

Ethics and dissemination

This project will generate qualitative and epidemiological data, which will be shared according to best practices. All de-identified quantitative data will be available on reasonable request and following any relevant institutional agreements. Depending on the nature of the request, the study team may make specific suggestions to the requester; for example, they may suggest that individuals who were originally involved in the data collection, especially members of the study team based in Kenya, be invited to participate in the proposed analyses. This restriction is intended to ensure that data are analysed with a sufficient understanding of the original study and to promote equity in global health research. Qualitative data may include sensitive information shared by subjects. We will therefore share qualitative data according to the recommendations provided by Tsai et al,94 which includes additional steps to ensure that transcripts and field notes are fully deidentified, that consent forms provide options to decline future data sharing, and an option to share coding queries as an alternative to full transcripts.

The study has been approved by the Rutgers University Institutional Review Board (Pro2024001981) and the Amref Ethical and Scientific Review Committee (P1831-2025). Written informed assent (from adolescents below 18 years) and consent (from adults) will be obtained from all participants. Study results will be published and presented at international conferences and to community stakeholders. Our dissemination plan within the community will be informed by our community advisory board.

Discussion

Through this project, we shall understand the drivers of undernutrition and overweight/obesity among adolescents across a gradient of low-income neighbourhoods in Nairobi. Our participatory approach begins from the perspective of the adolescent and works outward, using qualitative and quantitative data to address this question through a systems science lens. This approach is aligned with calls to better integrate the full picture of people’s realities when identifying, designing, implementing and evaluating policies and interventions to improve diets and reduce the burden of malnutrition in all its forms.95

Key strengths of our study include: (1) our multilevel approach that will provide a deep understanding of adolescent food choices, their drivers, and how the home, school and community environments that adolescents engage with influence them, and (2) the ability to evaluate a range of ages to evaluate how food choices and diets evolve through adolescence. Using novel modelling approaches to evaluate hypothesised linkages among diet, inflammation and micronutrient status in a multicountry study of early child health,85 we will examine the impact of the food environment on adolescent nutrition across a gradient of informal, semiformal and formal conditions. This approach allows us to bridge epidemiological data on food systems and within-person biological systems (relating dietary intake to micronutrient status) to understand how macrolevel changes in the food system impact adolescent health.

While our participatory approach, systems science thinking and methodologies are innovative in the context of LMIC food systems research,41 96 there are some potential limitations. The cohort will be followed for only one academic school year, which may be insufficient to evaluate long-term associations among food environments, adolescent diets and nutritional outcomes. Another limitation of our study is that while we will codesign and prioritise interventions at the school and neighbourhood level, they will not be implemented as part of this project. Strategies that have local support and are cocreated through this work face unforeseen barriers in terms of implementation.
