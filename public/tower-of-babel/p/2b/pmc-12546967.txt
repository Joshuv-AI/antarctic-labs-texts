Acute and chronic nasal inflammation induces lymphangiogenesis in the olfactory mucosa in mice
Authors: Suzuho Komaki, Ryuichi Imai, Yuzuki Sugimoto, Rei Settsu, Aki Obara, Atsuyoshi Shimada, Robert Dantzer, Geoffroy Laumet, Fumiaki Imamura, Sanae Hasegawa-Ishii
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLymphangiogenesis, the formation of new lymphatic vessels, is primarily driven by VEGF-C-mediated activation of VEGFR-3 and plays a critical role in immune regulation and tissue repair. Although lymphangiogenesis has been well documented in various inflamed tissues, its occurrence and spatiotemporal characteristics in the olfactory mucosa during nasal inflammation remain poorly understood. To address this, we investigated the localization and development of lymphatic vessels in a mouse model of both acute and chronic nasal inflammation.MethodsAcute inflammation was induced by intranasal administration of lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 10 μg per nostril) in 8-week-old male mice, with saline-treated mice as controls. Behavioral tests were conducted at 24 and 48 h post-administration. Nasal tissues were collected at multiple time points up to four weeks. Cytokine expression was analyzed by quantitative RT-PCR, and VEGF levels were quantified using ELISA. Immune cell infiltration and lymphatic vessel localization were assessed histologically using markers such as Lyve-1, VEGFR-3, and Prox-1. For the chronic inflammation model, mice received unilateral intranasal LPS or saline administration three times per week for 10 weeks, followed by histological analysis of lymphatic remodeling.ResultsMice showed transient reduction in food and water intake and body weight, some aspects of sickness behavior within 24 h, but did not display depression-like phenotypes at 24 and 48 h post-LPS, as measured by duration of immobility in the tail suspension test and forced swim test, and percent sucrose volume consumed in the sucrose preference test. LPS treatment induced a sustained inflammatory response, with elevated pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine expression persisting for up to two weeks. Immune cell infiltration and lymphangiogenesis were localized to specific areas of the olfactory mucosa, particularly the inner regions of the first and second turbinates. These inflammatory hotspots exhibited increased expression of lymphatic markers, primarily due to proliferation of lymphatic endothelial cells. VEGF-C and VEGF-A levels were significantly upregulated following LPS treatment. In the chronic model, lymphangiogenesis became more widespread throughout the olfactory mucosa, and was accompanied by dense infiltration of CD11b + immune cells.ConclusionsNasal inflammation induces region-specific lymphangiogenesis during the acute phase, likely driven by local proliferation of lymphatic endothelial cells. Under chronic inflammatory conditions, both inflammation and lymphangiogenesis expand across the olfactory mucosa. Further studies are needed to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms and to determine the functional relevance of olfactory lymphangiogenesis.

Introduction

Lymphatic vessels are distributed throughout the body and form an extensive network that is essential for maintaining tissue fluid homeostasis, facilitating immune cell trafficking, and enabling the absorption of dietary fats. At sites of inflammation, lymphatic vessels play a pivotal role in transporting antigen-presenting cells to regional lymph nodes, activating lymphatic endothelial cells, and initiating the adaptive immune responses.

Over the past few decades, the identification of lymphatic-specific markers such as Lyve-1, VEGFR-3, Prox-1, and podoplanin, along with the discovery of key lymphangiogenic regulators including VEGF-C and VEGF-D, has substantially advanced our understanding of lymphatic biology (Kaipainen et al., 1995; Banerji et al., 1999; Wigle et al., 2002; Tammela and Alitalo, 2010). It is now well established that new lymphatic vessels can form in inflamed tissues a process known as lymphangiogenesis, largely mediated by VEGF-C/VEGFR-3 signaling (Tammela and Alitalo, 2010; Kim et al., 2014; Liao and von der Weid, 2014). In inflamed tissues, immune cells such as CD11b + macrophages and stromal fibroblasts can release VEGF-C in response to inflammatory cytokines including TNF-α and IL-1β, and the secreted VEGF-C binds to VEGR-3 on lymphatic endothelial cells, thereby promoting lymphangiogenesis (Ristimaki et al., 1998; Baluk et al., 2013). In models of acute and chronic skin inflammation, for example, infiltrating macrophages secrete VEGF-C and VEGF-D, which bind to VEGFR-3 on lymphatic endothelial cells, thereby initiating lymphangiogenesis (Kataru et al., 2009; Huggenberger et al., 2010). Similarly, in hepatic ischemia-reperfusion injury, macrophage-derived VEGFs promote lymphangiogenesis in the portal tract region (Nakamoto et al., 2020). In a mouse model of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced peritonitis, CD11b + macrophages were shown to release VEGF-C and VEGF-D, driving lymphangiogenesis and remodeling of the diaphragmatic lymphatics (Kim et al., 2009). In addition, inflammation-induced lymphangiogenesis has been reported in cornea injury (Cursiefen et al., 2004; Maruyama et al., 2005), myocardial infarction (Klotz et al., 2015; Vieira et al., 2018), inflammatory bowel disease (D'Alessio et al., 2014), and acute and chronic kidney injury (Zarjou et al., 2019; Balasubbramanian et al., 2020).

Nasal inflammation is a prevalent condition worldwide. When prolonged, it can progress to chronic rhinosinusitis and has been linked to various neuropsychiatric disorders, including depression and anxiety (Croy and Hummel, 2017; Smith and Alt, 2020; Fan et al., 2024). However, the presence and functional significance of lymphangiogenesis in the olfactory mucosa during nasal inflammation remain poorly understood. Clinical studies have reported mixed findings: some observed no significant changes in the number, size, or length density of olfactory lymphatic vessels in edematous ethmoid sinus mucosa compared to healthy tissues (Kim et al., 2007), while others reported reduced lymphatic density in chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, especially within inflammatory hotspots (Luukkainen et al., 2017; Pesold et al., 2024). Conversely, increased lymphatic vessel number and endothelial length density have been reported in mild and severe persistent allergic rhinitis relative to healthy controls (Kim et al., 2010). These discrepancies may reflect variations in disease stage, etiology, limited sample size, systemic health conditions, or genetic background among patients.

To address these limitations, we employed a murine model in which nasal inflammation is reproducibly induced by intranasal administration of LPS. In our previous work, we demonstrated that this model elicits robust immune cell infiltration, increased expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and loss of olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs) in the olfactory mucosa of adult male mice (Hasegawa-Ishii et al., 2017). Building upon this model, the present study investigates whether lymphangiogenesis occurs in the olfactory mucosa during both acute and chronic phases of nasal inflammation. Our findings demonstrate that lymphangiogenesis is induced in specific regions of the olfactory mucosa and evolves over time from the acute to the chronic phase. These findings provide a reproducible experimental framework for studying lymphatic remodeling during nasal inflammation.

Materials and methods

Animals and experimental design

For acute nasal inflammation study, 8-week-old male C57BL/6JmsSlc mice (Sankyo lab, Tokyo, Japan) received a single administration of 10 μg LPS from Escherichia coli O55:B5 (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) dissolved in physiological saline (1 mg/mL) into bilateral nostrils after deeply anesthetizing the mice with isoflurane. LPS from E. coli O127:B8 (Sigma-Aldrich) was intraperitoneally injected at a dose of 0.83 mg/kg to induce transient depression-like behavior. Behavioral assessments were performed in mice at 24 and 48 h post-LPS. They included body weight measurement, food and water intake, the tail suspension test (TST), the forced swim test (FST), and sucrose preference test. All behavioral experiments were conducted in Kyorin University, except for the TST and FST performed 24 h after intranasal administration and intraperitoneal injections, which were conducted at Michigan State University.

Olfactory tissues, including the olfactory mucosa and olfactory bones, were freshly collected en block at 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, as well as 1, 2, and 4 weeks post-administration to examine cytokine expression. Nasal tissues, including olfactory and respiratory mucosa and nasal bones, were freshly collected en block at 24, 48, and 72 h post-administration to analyze VEGF-C and VEGF-A proteins expression using an ELISA assay. To model chronic nasal inflammation, mice received repeated intranasal administration of 10 μg LPS into unilateral nostril three times per week for 10 weeks. Behavioral assessment

Body weight was measured at 12, 24, and 48 h after intranasal administration. Daily food and water intake were recorded. The TST and FST were conducted as previously described (Laumet et al., 2018). Briefly, in the TST, mice were suspended by the tail for 10 min and video-recorded. For analysis, the first 1 min was discarded, and the remaining 9 min were divided into three successive 3-min-periods (TST-1, TST-2, and TST-3). Immobility time was quantified for each period by observers blinded to treatment. In the FST, mice were placed in water (24–26 °C) and observed for 6 min. Immobility time during the last 5 min was measured by observers blinded to treatment. For the sucrose preference test, mice were provided with two bottles, one containing water and the other containing 1 % sucrose solution, for one week prior to testing to allow familiarization. Sucrose preference was calculated as the ratio of 1 % sucrose intake to total fluid intake (water plus 1 % sucrose intake).

RNA extraction and relative quantitative RT-PCR assay

Olfactory tissue and olfactory bulb were collected on ice under a stereomicroscope and stored at −80 °C until use.

Total RNA was isolated from frozen olfactory tissue using a NucleoSpin RNA kit (Marcherey-Nagel GmbH & C. KG, Valencienner, Duren, Germany) following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA quantity was assessed by measuring absorbance at 260 nm. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 200 ng of total RNA using the ReverTra Ace reverse transcription kit (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Real-Time PCR was performed using TaqMan Gene Expression Assays (Applied Biosystems-Thermo Fisher Scientific, Table 1) in a total volume of 20 μL for 40 cycles (95 °C for 3 s, followed by 60 °C for 30 s) with the 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems-Thermo Fisher Scientific). Transcript levels were normalized to those of Gapdh gene. Data are expressed as relative fold changes calculated using the 2ˆ-(ΔΔCT) method. Histological preparation

Mice were anesthetized with ketamine (100 mg/kg body weight) and xylazine (10 mg/kg) and transcardially perfused with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by 4 % paraformaldehyde in PBS as a fixation solution. Their heads were decapitated and placed in the same fixation solution overnight. After removing the lower jaw and upper teeth, the heads, including the nasal tissue, skull and brain, was immersed en block in 2x K-CX (Falma, Tokyo, Japan) for 6 h at room temperature for decalcification, then rinsed with running water for over 6 h. The heads were cryoprotected in 20 % sucrose at room temperature overnight, embedded in OCT compound (Sakura Finetek, Torrance, CA, USA), and frozen in ethanol cooled with dry ice. Frozen blocks were stored at −80 °C until use.

Hematoxylin and eosin staining and immunostaining

Olfactory tissues were coronally sectioned into 16 μm slices using a cryostat at the level of anterior border of the eyes (Kagoya et al., 2021). Then the sections were mounted on glass slides, dried, and stored at −30 °C until use.

For hematoxylin and eosin staining, frozen sections were dehydrated in graded ethanol and defatted in xylene, then stained in Carazzi's hematoxylin (Muto Kagaku, Tokyo, Japan) for 3 min. After rinsing in water and differentiation, sections were washed in running water to develop the nuclear stain. They were subsequently counterstained with pure eosin (Muto Kagaku) for 2 min, rinsed in water, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with HSR (Sysmex, Kobe, Japan).

Sections were rehydrated with TBST buffer [10 mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.4) and 100 mM NaCl with 0.1 % Tween 20]. For antigen retrieval, sections were incubated in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) or in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) at 70 °C for 40 min and cooled to room temperature. The sections were blocked with blocking buffer (5 % normal donkey serum in TBST) for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer. The primary antibodies used in the present study are listed in Table 2. The sections were then incubated with host-matched secondary antibodies (ImmPress anti-rabbit, anti-rat, anti-goat IgG, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA, USA) for 1 h at room temperature and stained with ImmPACT DAB (Vector Laboratories). Nuclei were stained with Carrazzi's hematoxylin for 10 s. Finally, the slices were cleared and coverslipped with HSR. Image analysis and morphometry

Positive rate

After staining with anti-CD11b, Ly6G, Lyve-1, and VEGFR-3 antibodies, photographs of the first and second turbinates and the septum of the olfactory mucosa were taken. The Inner region was defined as the lamina propria located on the inner side of the turbinate structures: for the first turbinate, the area extending from the dorsal tip, down along the medial side, and back up to the same dorsal level (Fig. 2a, red outline); and for the second turbinate, the ventral half of the lamina propria starting from its base (Fig. 2b, red outline). The Outer region comprised the lamina propria on the opposite side: for the first turbinate, from the dorsal tip descending laterally to the ventral end; and for the second turbinate, the lower half of the medial aspect (Fig. 2a and b, blue outline). The immunopositive area in each region (Inner, Outer, or Septum) and the total area were measured by calculating the number of pixels by using Photoshop software. The positive rate was determined by dividing the immunopositive area by the total area.

Cell density

After staining with the anti-Prox-1 antibody, photographs of the first and second turbinates and the septum of the olfactory mucosa were taken. Using Image J software, the length of Inner, Outer, or Septum regions at the baseline of the lamina propria along the direction of the nasal bone was measured. The cell density was calculated by dividing the number of Prox-1 immunopositive cells by the length of each region, expressed as cells per millimeter (cells/mm). Due to the edematous nature of the lamina propria caused by nasal inflammation following intranasal LPS administration, the length, rather than the area of the lamina propria, was used for cell density calculation.

Protein extraction and ELISA assay

Nasal tissue was freshly collected and stored at −80 °C until use. Each nasal tissue sample was homogenized with 500 μL of CelLytic™ MT Cell Lysis Reagent (Sigma-Aldrich) supplemented with 5 μL of protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma-Aldrich) and 120 U/mL of benzonase endonuclease (Sigma-Aldrich). The homogenate was centrifuged at 10000×g for 5 min, and the supernatant was collected. The protein concentration of each supernatant was measured at an absorbance of 562 nm using the TaKaRa BCA Protein Assay Kit (TaKaRa Bio, Shiga, Japan).

For VEGF-C analysis, all samples were adjusted to a total protein concentration of 8 mg/mL using PBS and then diluted 4-fold with Reference Standard & Sample Diluent, resulting in a final concentration of 2 mg/mL total protein. For VEGF-A analysis, all samples were adjusted to a total protein concentration of 2 mg/mL by diluting with distilled water. The amounts of VEGF-C or VEGF-A proteins were measured using the Mouse VEGF-C ELISA kit (Elabscience, Houston, TX, USA) and the Mouse VEGF ELISA kit (R&D), respectively, following the manufacturers’ protocols.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistica software (Dell Software, Round Rock, TX, USA).

Behavioral analysis

Differences in body weight, food and water intake, and sucrose preference were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with treatment (saline or LPS) and time (12, 24, and 48 h post-treatment) as factors. The TST and FST were analyzed using unpaired t-test (saline vs LPS). A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Quantitative RT-PCR assay

Gene expression data from quantitative RT-PCR were reported as relative fold changes calculated using the 2ˆ-(ΔΔCT) method. ΔCT values were statistically analyzed using one-way ANOVA to assess the main effect of treatment (saline, LPS 24, 48, 72, and 96 h, and 1, 2, and 4 weeks). Tukey's HSD post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Histological analysis

In an acute nasal inflammation study, the positive rate of CD11b, Ly6G, Lyve-1, and VEGFR-3 as well as the cell density of Prox-1-immunopositive cells, were analyzed using one-way ANOVA to evaluate the main effect of treatment (saline, LPS 24, 48 and 72 h and 2 weeks). Tukey's HSD post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. In a chronic nasal inflammation study, the positive rate of CD11b and Lyve-1 was analyzed using two-way ANOVA to evaluate the main effects of treatment and sides (treatment: saline or LPS x sides: contralateral and ipsilateral). Tukey's HSD post hoc test was applied for multiple comparisons. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

ELISA assay

The concentrations of VEGF-C and VEGF-A protein were analyzed using a one-way ANOVA to assess the main effect of treatment (saline, LPS 24, 48, and 72 h). Tukey's HSD post hoc test was performed for multiple comparisons. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To determine whether nasal inflammation induces acute depression-like behavior, we performed the TST and FST at 24 and 48 h post-treatment and measured sucrose preference during the first and second 24-h periods. As a positive control, we examined the TST and FST at 24 h after intraperitoneal injection of LPS, which is known to induce depression-like behavior (Laumet et al., 2018). Following intraperitoneal LPS injection, there was a trend toward increased immobility in the TST-3, and a significant increase in immobility time was observed in the FST at 24 h post-injection. In contrast, no significant differences in immobility time were observed in either the TST or FST between intranasal LPS and saline administration (Fig. 3d–f). Similarly, sucrose preference did not differ significantly between the intranasal LPS and control groups at either 24 or 48 h (Fig. 3g).

Time course of LPS-induced nasal inflammation

We next investigated cytokine expression in the olfactory tissue and the olfactory bulb following a single intranasal LPS administration using quantitative RT-PCR. In the olfactory tissues, the expression of Il1b was upregulated after LPS treatment, peaking at 73.6-fold at 24 h and remaining elevated until 4 weeks. The expression decreased at 72 h and later compared to the peak level at 24 h post-LPS (Fig. 4a). Tnf and Il-6 expressions also increased following LPS treatment, remaining elevated for up to 2 weeks and returning to baseline by 4 weeks (Fig. 4b and c). Notably, Tnf and Il6 peaked at 24.1- and 936-fold, respectively, at 24 h post-LPS. Discussion

We demonstrated for the first time that lymphangiogenesis occurs in the olfactory mucosa of mice with nasal inflammation. In the acute phase, it was localized to inflammatory hotspots and associated with increased VEGF-C and VEGF-A levels, mainly through the proliferation of lymphatic endothelial cells. Chronic nasal inflammation further led to sustained and widespread lymphangiogenesis, paralleling the expansion of inflammation (Fig. 14). Previous reports of lymphangiogenesis in the nasal mucosa of patient samples showed inconsistent results due to methodological and clinical heterogeneity (Kim et al., 2007, 2010; Luukkainen et al., 2017; Pesold et al., 2024). By using a mouse model, we obtained reproducible data that clearly demonstrate the dynamics of lymphangiogenesis under nasal inflammation.Fig. 14Summary of this study.Schematic diagram shows half of the olfactory mucosa in acute and chronic nasal inflammation. Effects of single LPS administration on mouse behavior and tissue injury

In this study, mice received intranasal LPS as a model of nasal inflammation. A single administration of LPS induced a transient reduction in body weight during the first 24 h, likely reflecting a combination of temporary decreases in food and water intake and increased energy expenditure associated with the inflammatory response (Wang and Ye, 2015). Since systemic inflammation induced by intraperitoneal LPS injection has been reported to acutely lead to depression-like behavior following sickness behavior (Frenois et al., 2007; O'Connor et al., 2009), and because there is a well-established association between chronic rhinosinusitis and depression in humans (Croy and Hummel, 2017; Smith and Alt, 2020; Fan et al., 2024), we assessed depression-like behavior in these mice. There was no evidence of depression-like phenotypes as assessed by the TST, FST and sucrose preference test, at least within 48 h post-treatment. These findings suggest that localized nasal inflammation, unlike systemic inflammation, may not be sufficient to trigger acute depression-like behavior. Alternatively, depression-related phenotypes may require more prolonged or repeated nasal inflammatory stimulation to emerge.

Nevertheless, even a single LPS administration caused mild, sustained elevation of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines in the olfactory mucosa for up to 2 weeks. CD11b+ and Ly6G + cells occasionally accumulated in hotspots in the olfactory mucosa at 2 weeks, which may continue to ongoing cytokine secretion, although their positive rates were not significantly higher than in saline-treated control. In contrast, cytokine levels in the olfactory bulb returned to the baseline within 72 h except for IL-1β. Together with our previous finding of early disappearance of immune cells from the olfactory bulb (Asano et al., 2022), these results suggest that inflammation in the brain may be downregulated earlier than in the olfactory mucosa.

Spatial expansion of inflammatory sites during chronic nasal inflammation

Our previous work showed that LPS-induced nasal inflammation and subsequent OSN loss were more severe in the lateral side of the olfactory mucosa, including turbinates, compared to the medial side, including the septum (Hasegawa-Ishii et al., 2017). In the present study, we refined the localization of inflammatory regions, identifying the Inner regions of the turbinates as primary sites of acute inflammation. The nasal cavity surrounded by the Inner regions likely acts as a niche where the LPS solution accumulates, facilitating early activation of macrophages and recruitment of immune cells in the Inner region. This leads to the localized release of inflammatory mediators, creating inflammatory hotspots. Resident fibroblasts and infiltrating immune cells, including macrophages, neutrophils, and dendritic cells, are known to secrete VEGF-C and VEGF-A, thereby promoting lymphangiogenesis (Tammela and Alitalo, 2010). Accordingly, we observed lymphangiogenesis closely associated with these inflammatory hotspots during the acute phase.

During the chronic phase, inflammation progressively expanded to include the Outer regions of the turbinates and the Septum, consistent with our previous findings (Hasegawa-Ishii et al., 2019, 2020). The spatial pattern of inflammation in the olfactory mucosa corresponded with damage within the olfactory bulb, reflecting the axonal connection of OSNs. In the acute phase, immune cell infiltration and glial activation were predominantly confined to the lateral olfactory bulb (Asano et al., 2022). Following three weeks of repeated LPS exposure, degeneration of tyrosine hydroxylase-expressing periglomerular cells and synaptic loss were mainly observed laterally (Hasegawa-Ishii et al., 2017). After 10 weeks of chronic nasal inflammation, the olfactory bulb atrophied not only in the lateral side but also in the medial side, although the atrophy was still more severe in the lateral side (Hasegawa-Ishii et al., 2019, 2020).

Importantly, the sites of lymphangiogenesis expanded in parallel with the spread of inflammation during chronic nasal inflammation. These findings confirm that lymphangiogenesis occurs at inflammatory sites in the olfactory mucosa during both acute and chronic nasal inflammation.

Potential function of olfactory lymphangiogenesis

Inflammation-induced lymphangiogenesis is generally thought to support the clearance of inflammatory debris and the initiation of adaptive immune responses, thereby facilitating tissue repair (Kataru et al., 2009; Huggenberger et al., 2010; Nakamoto et al., 2020; Klotz et al., 2015; Vieira et al., 2018; D'Alessio et al., 2014; Balasubbramanian et al., 2020). Previous studies have reported that blockade of VEGF-C/D – VEGFR-3 signaling by soluble VEGFR-3, VEGFR-3 kinase inhibitors, anti-VEGFR-3 antibodies, or soluble VEGF-C delays antigen clearance and resolution of inflammation, concomitant with impaired lymphangiogenesis (Kataru et al., 2009; Huggenberger et al., 2010; Nakamoto et al., 2020; D'Alessio et al., 2014). Similarly, deletion of macrophages by clodronate liposomes comparable effects (Kataru et al., 2009). Conversely, recombinant VEGF-C administration or VEGF-C overexpression in transgenic mice accelerates antigen clearance and resolution in association with enhanced lymphangiogenesis (Kataru et al., 2009; Huggenberger et al., 2010; Nakamoto et al., 2020; Vieira et al., 2018). In this context, it is plausible that olfactory lymphangiogenesis observed in our model may contribute to the resolution of inflammation and preservation of homeostasis in the olfactory mucosa.

The olfactory mucosa constitutes the initial site of the olfactory neurocircuit, where OSNs detect odorants and relay information to the brain. Nasal inflammation can damage the olfactory mucosa and lead to the loss of OSNs, resulting in olfactory dysfunction. Impaired olfactory input not only reduces quality of life but has also been associated with altered brain function and the onset of neuropsychiatric conditions such as depression (Leon and Woo, 2022; Sabiniewicz et al., 2022). These observations highlight a potential link between inflammation-induced olfactory dysfunction and neuropsychiatric outcomes, providing a coherent framework for understanding the broader consequences of nasal inflammation.

Thus, preservation of its integrity is of critical importance. Given the emerging evidence that lymphatic pathways regulate inflammatory resolution, targeting these pathways may provide a novel therapeutic approach. Indeed, age-related lymphatic dysfunction can be restored by activation, such as pharmacological or mechanical manipulation (Yoon et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2025), suggesting that stimulating lymphatic pathways may represent a potential strategy for mitigating chronic rhinosinusitis and related disorders.

In addition, the olfactory mucosa has been proposed as a potential exit route for brain-derived waste contained in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), primarily through lymphatic vessels near the cribriform plate ((Hsu et al., 2019, Norwood et al., 2019, Spera et al., 2023)). During neuroinflammatory conditions such as experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis (EAE), these lymphatic vessels undergo VEGF-C – VEGFR-3-dependent lymphangiogenesis and may facilitate drainage of CSF and brain-derived antigen presenting cells (Hsu et al., 2019, 2022). These lymphatics are thought to connect with olfactory lymphatic vessels and ultimately drain to submandibular, accessory submandibular, or deep cervical lymph nodes via the nasopharyngeal lymphatic plexus (Jin et al., 2025). Taken together, these anatomical and functional considerations suggest that olfactory lymphatic vessels could participate in brain waste clearance. In particular, given the transient but robust neuroinflammation observed in the olfactory bulb after nasal inflammation in our mouse model, olfactory lymphangiogenesis may help facilitate the resolution of neuroinflammation and thereby contribute to the maintenance of brain homeostasis.

Limitations of this study

First, although nasal inflammation induced lymphangiogenesis in the olfactory mucosa, the underlying molecular mechanisms were not investigated. Future studies could examine whether inhibiting VEGF-C or VEGF-A signaling, for example via local administration of neutralizing antibodies, suppresses lymphangiogenesis. Such experiments would help clarify the molecular drivers of lymphatic remodeling in this context. Second, the functional relevance of olfactory lymphangiogenesis was not directly assessed. While our findings and previous reports suggest a possible role in early resolution of inflammation, direct functional evidence is needed. Third, only 8-week-old male mice were used in this study; inclusion of females and mice of different ages would help determine the generalizability of these findings. Fourth, our sample size was relatively small, so subtle differences may not have been detected.
