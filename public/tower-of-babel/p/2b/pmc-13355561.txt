The PAFIS score for risk stratification of atrial fibrillation detected after stroke: a multicenter cohort study
Authors: Jung-Chi Hsu, Ting-Chuan Wang, Yen-Yun Yang, Shu-Lin Chuang, Chih-Chieh Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtrial fibrillation detected after stroke (AFDAS) is clinically important, but AFDAS-specific risk tools for patients without known atrial fibrillation (AF) remain limited. We developed and temporally validated the Prediction of AF in Ischemic Stroke (PAFIS) score.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed ischemic-stroke patients from the National Taiwan University Hospital Integrative Medical Data Center. The development cohort included patients hospitalized in 2010–2020 (n = 3406), and the temporal validation cohort included those hospitalized in 2021–2023 (n = 1366). Known AF (KAF) was defined as AF documented before stroke or within 14 days after stroke; AFDAS was defined as newly documented AF beyond 14 days among patients without KAF. Multivariable logistic regression restricted to KAF-free patients was used for score derivation. Discrimination, calibration, and time-to-AFDAS risk stratification were assessed.ResultsAmong KAF-free patients, AFDAS was detected during routine clinical follow-up in 176 of 2175 (8.1%) in the development cohort and 148 of 1366 (10.8%) in the validation cohort. The final PAFIS score included age ≥ 75 years, female sex, valvular heart disease, left atrial diameter ≥ 40 mm, and tricuspid regurgitation peak gradient ≥30 mmHg. AUCs were 0.72 (95% CI, 0.68–0.76) in development and 0.65 (95% CI, 0.60–0.70) in validation. PAFIS outperformed CHA₂DS₂-VASc, HAVOC, and AF-ESUS, but not Brown ESUS-AF. Observed AFDAS detection rates increased across risk groups in both cohorts.ConclusionsPAFIS provides a simple AFDAS-specific tool for selective post-stroke rhythm monitoring. Because AF ascertainment was based on routine clinical care without standardized prolonged monitoring, PAFIS predicts AF detection under routine practice rather than true AFDAS incidence. Prospective multicenter validation with standardized monitoring is warranted.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) contributes to up to one-third of all ischemic strokes worldwide, and AF-related ischemic strokes are more frequently disabling than strokes occurring in sinus rhythm [1]. Based on the timing of AF detection relative to ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack (IS/TIA), patients can be categorized into three groups: those without AF, those with clinically recognized AF prior to stroke (known AF, KAF), and those whose AF is first detected only after the index event, termed atrial fibrillation detected after stroke (AFDAS) [2]. AFDAS has been reported in approximately 6–7% of patients within the first three to five days of acute ischemic stroke; its prevalence rises to 25–30% with prolonged cardiac monitoring [2], [3].

The mechanisms underlying AFDAS are heterogeneous, involving both cardiogenic and neurogenic pathways [4]. Compared with KAF, AFDAS is generally characterized by shorter paroxysmal episodes, fewer cardiovascular comorbidities, and milder structural cardiac abnormalities. Early-onset AF following stroke, often described as part of the Stroke-Heart Syndrome, is believed to be driven primarily by autonomic dysregulation and inflammatory responses to acute brain injury, particularly with insular infarction [5], [6]. This neurogenic substrate may explain why AFDAS has been associated with a lower risk of recurrent stroke than KAF [7], [8].

Nevertheless, some patients with AFDAS have substantial atrial cardiopathy and systemic vascular disease, suggesting that undetected paroxysmal AF episodes pre-dated the stroke and only became apparent with extended monitoring. In such patients, AFDAS may carry an AF burden similar to KAF, and recent registry data show that AFDAS is associated with a 73% higher risk of dementia, challenging the assumption that AFDAS is uniformly benign [9]. At the same time, the ARCADIA trial did not demonstrate benefit of empiric anticoagulation over antiplatelet therapy in cryptogenic stroke with atrial cardiopathy, reinforcing that the actionable target is confirmed AF rather than atrial cardiopathy alone [10].

Several risk scores and validation studies have been proposed to support AF screening after stroke (Brown ESUS-AF, HAVOC, AF-ESUS, CHASE-LESS, AS5F, and subsequent national cohort and registry-claims-electronic medical record validation studies), but most were derived or validated in specific stroke populations or used different prediction horizons, limiting direct generalizability to the broader ischemic stroke population [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]]. Furthermore, prior models have varied in their target outcome (any AF, prevalent KAF, or AFDAS), in the time horizon of prediction, and in the inclusion of biomarker and echocardiographic variables. A score that explicitly targets AFDAS in patients without known AF, integrates clinical and echocardiographic information available in routine practice, and is rigorously evaluated for discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility, may help allocate prolonged rhythm monitoring resources more rationally. The present study was designed to develop and temporally validate such a tool – the Prediction of AF in Ischemic Stroke (PAFIS) score – using an AFDAS-specific derivation framework with AFDAS as the outcome in patients without KAF.

Methods

Study population, data source, and cohort construction

We conducted a longitudinal retrospective cohort study using the National Taiwan University Hospital Integrative Medical Data Center (NTUH-iMD), a validated electronic health record repository established in 2006 that includes demographic data, ICD-9-CM and ICD-10-CM diagnostic codes, laboratory results, prescriptions, procedures, and imaging records from NTUH and its affiliated branches [18], [19]. The study was approved by the NTUH Institutional Review Board and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients hospitalized for ischemic stroke were eligible if cerebral infarction was confirmed by brain computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging within one month of hospitalization. The index date was defined as the date of stroke hospitalization. Patients aged <20 years, those with incomplete electronic health record data, suspected infective endocarditis, hemorrhagic stroke, or transient ischemic attack without radiologically confirmed infarction were excluded.

Known atrial fibrillation (KAF) was defined as AF documented before ischemic stroke or within 14 days after stroke. Atrial fibrillation detected after stroke (AFDAS) was defined as newly documented AF occurring >14 days after stroke among patients without KAF. AF was identified using ICD codes and standard 12‑lead electrocardiography during routine inpatient or outpatient care. Patients without documented AF during follow-up were classified as having no documented AFDAS during routine clinical follow-up. Follow-up was censored at the last clinical encounter or death. Because systematic prolonged rhythm monitoring was not universally performed, AF ascertainment reflected routine clinical practice and may have underestimated the true underlying incidence of post-stroke AF.

Patients hospitalized between January 1, 2010 and December 31, 2020 formed the development cohort. Among 3406 patients with ischemic stroke, 1231 with KAF were excluded, leaving 2175 KAF-free patients for AFDAS prediction-model derivation. Temporal validation was performed in a nonoverlapping cohort of 1366 KAF-free patients hospitalized between January 1, 2021 and December 31, 2023, with follow-up through December 31, 2024. There was no patient overlap between cohorts.

Predictors and score development

Candidate predictors were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, routine availability, and prior AFDAS literature, and were used to construct the PAFIS score. Missing candidate predictors were imputed separately within each cohort using missForest, a non-parametric random forest-based imputation algorithm, to avoid information leakage between the development and validation cohorts. The extent of missingness for candidate predictors is reported in Supplementary Table S1.

All candidate predictors were entered simultaneously into multivariable logistic regression without univariable screening or stepwise selection. LASSO logistic regression with 10-fold cross-validation was performed as a sensitivity analysis. Final coefficients were estimated using unpenalized logistic regression, and integer points were assigned by rescaling the β coefficients to construct a clinically interpretable risk score according to an established point-based risk-score derivation approach [20]. After score construction, patients were stratified into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk groups based on the development cohort, and the same risk strata were applied unchanged to the validation cohort.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student's t-test. Categorical variables were summarized as number and percentage and compared using the chi-square or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

Discrimination was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), with 95% confidence intervals estimated by the DeLong method [21]. AUCs were compared with CHA₂DS₂-VASc, Brown ESUS-AF, HAVOC, and AF-ESUS using the DeLong test for correlated ROC curves. Calibration was assessed using calibration plots, calibration intercept and slope, Brier score, and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. Time-to-AFDAS analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier curves, log-rank tests, and Cox proportional hazards models according to PAFIS risk group. Decision-curve analysis was used to evaluate clinical net benefit. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and accuracy were calculated at each PAFIS cut-point. All analyses were performed using R version 4.3.3. A two-sided p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

In this study, we identified clinical and echocardiographic predictors of atrial fibrillation detected after stroke (AFDAS) among patients with ischemic stroke and developed the PAFIS score to stratify AFDAS risk. In the AFDAS-specific derivation restricted to patients without known atrial fibrillation (KAF), the final score included age ≥ 75 years, female sex, valvular heart disease, left atrial diameter ≥ 40 mm, and TRPG ≥30 mmHg. This AFDAS-specific derivation is important because predictors associated with prevalent or early post-stroke AF do not necessarily retain independent associations with delayed AF detection among patients without KAF. These findings underscore the close cardiovascular substrate underlying AFDAS, particularly atrial and cardiopulmonary remodeling, and support a risk-stratified approach to post-stroke rhythm surveillance.

Consistent with prior literature, KAF accounted for approximately one-third of ischemic strokes in this cohort [1]. Among patients without KAF, AFDAS was detected in about 8 % during routine follow-up. Reported AF detection rates after stroke vary across clinical settings and depend on the timing and intensity of rhythm surveillance, including short-term versus prolonged monitoring protocols [22], [23], [24]. Because systematic prolonged monitoring was not universally performed in the present study, the observed AFDAS detection rate should be interpreted as AF detected during routine clinical care rather than the full burden of AF that might be identified with standardized prolonged monitoring.

Existing scores and validation studies for AF risk after stroke have demonstrated heterogeneous performance and were often developed or validated in specific stroke populations with different time horizons [11], [12], [13], [14], [15], [16], [17]. Although some models incorporate ECG parameters, biomarkers such as NT-proBNP or troponin, and echocardiographic indices, comprehensive integration remains uncommon [25], [26], [27], [28]. In our analysis, the PAFIS score showed moderate discrimination and only modest advantage over several existing scores. In cross-study terms, its discrimination is within the range of existing broader-population post-stroke AF scores rather than markedly superior, and direct comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because cohort case-mix, AF ascertainment intensity, monitoring duration, and follow-up vary substantially. The contribution of PAFIS therefore lies less in superior raw discrimination than in its AFDAS-specific derivation among patients without KAF, broader ischemic-stroke population, and incorporation of formal calibration, decision-curve analysis, and transparent variable selection. Accordingly, the PAFIS score should be viewed as a supplementary tool that can assist clinicians in selective rather than universal monitoring, particularly when resources are limited or when advanced monitoring is not routinely reimbursed.

The clinical role of PAFIS is to guide the intensity of rhythm surveillance after ischemic stroke. Patients with low scores may continue with routine follow-up, whereas those with intermediate to high scores may merit prolonged external monitoring. Patients in the high-risk group may be considered for implantable loop recorders where feasible, considering local reimbursement policies, patient values, and overall stroke mechanism. This tiered approach aims to optimize resource allocation and to improve the yield of AF detection in populations most likely to benefit.

Therapeutic implications should remain conservative. The ARCADIA trial did not demonstrate a benefit of empiric anticoagulation over antiplatelet therapy for secondary prevention in cryptogenic stroke with atrial cardiopathy, and the benefit of stroke prevention in AF requires sustained anticoagulation once AF is confirmed [10], [29]. These observations suggest that atrial cardiopathy or high predicted AFDAS risk without documented AF should not uniformly warrant anticoagulation. Therefore, targeting intensified monitoring to patients at higher risk of AFDAS is a pragmatic strategy. Prolonged surveillance modalities, including ambulatory Holter monitoring, mobile cardiac outpatient telemetry, external loop recorders, and implantable loop recorders, can increase AF detection, but cost and access considerations necessitate judicious patient selection [22], [23], [30]. In Taiwan, implantable loop recorders are not reimbursed, further highlighting the need for careful risk stratification and selective deployment of monitoring resources.

The reduction in AFDAS discriminative performance from derivation to validation warrants consideration. Several factors may contribute. First, the validation cohort was derived from the same hospital network during a later period, which limits geographic heterogeneity and may capture temporal shifts in patient mix or practice patterns. Second, differences in monitoring intensity and diagnostic pathways across hospital levels and time could influence AF ascertainment. Third, the validation cohort had a shorter follow-up duration than the derivation cohort, which may underestimate the true underlying AFDAS incidence and attenuate performance. In addition, calibration was weaker in temporal validation, supporting the interpretation of PAFIS as a rank-based stratification tool rather than a precise absolute-risk calculator until recalibration is performed in larger independent cohorts. Together, these factors argue for future validation across independent, geographically distinct centers using standardized surveillance protocols.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was retrospective and based on EHR data from a single integrated hospital network; therefore, generalizability may be limited, and the validation cohort should be regarded as temporal within-network validation rather than geographically external validation. Second, AF ascertainment relied on ICD codes and routine 12‑lead ECG without systematic prolonged monitoring, which likely underestimated the true underlying AFDAS incidence and introduced surveillance bias; therefore, observed rates should be regarded as routine-care AF detection rates rather than true incidence estimates. Third, stroke severity, lesion topography, AF subtype, AF burden, left atrial strain, and detailed ECG markers were not uniformly available. Fourth, validation calibration was moderate, so PAFIS should currently be interpreted primarily as a rank-based stratification tool rather than a precise absolute-risk calculator. Finally, full head-to-head comparison with all existing scores in the validation cohort was limited by variable availability, and cross-study AUC comparisons should be interpreted cautiously because case-mix, monitoring intensity, and follow-up differ across studies. Prospective multicenter validation with standardized rhythm monitoring is required before routine clinical adoption.

Conclusion

The PAFIS score, derived from AFDAS-specific multivariable regression in patients without known AF and assessed for discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility, supports risk-stratified selective rhythm monitoring after ischemic stroke. Because AF ascertainment in this study reflected routine clinical care, PAFIS should be interpreted as predicting AF detection under routine practice rather than true AFDAS incidence. It is intended to identify patients who may benefit from intensified rhythm surveillance, not to guide anticoagulation in the absence of documented AF. Prospective multicenter validation with standardized rhythm-monitoring protocols is warranted before routine clinical implementation.
