Erythrocyte-inspired biomaterials for cancer immunotherapy: Integration within the cancer-immunity cycle
Authors: Parisa Javadi, Amir Azadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Erythrocyte-based drug delivery systems serve as an attractive strategy for cancer immunotherapy with improved biocompatibility, increased circulation time, and less immunogenicity. Due to their distinct physiological characteristics, erythrocytes can be developed as biomimetic carriers for targeted drug delivery, antigen presentation, and immune modulation. This review discusses the different strategies utilized to leverage erythrocytes in cancer immunotherapy, such as erythrocyte membrane-coated nanoparticles, antigen-hitchhiking systems, and immune-stimulatory erythrocyte-derived vesicles. Notably, we uniquely contextualizes erythrocyte-inspired platforms across each stage of the cancer-immunity cycle, highlighting their dual role as both drug delivery vehicles and active immunomodulators. We have explained how engineered erythrocyte-based systems enhance the cycle of cancer-immunity by increasing tumor antigen presentation and reshaping the tumor microenvironment for greater immune cell infiltration and cytotoxicity. In addition, recent advances in erythrocyte-based immunotherapeutic platforms, their advantages over conventional delivery platforms, and challenges in their clinical application are discussed. Overall, the integration of erythrocyte biomimetic technology into immunotherapy provides a novel approach to enhance cancer treatment efficacy and reduce systemic toxicity, paving the way for more targeted and effective therapies.

Introduction

Cancer stands as a leading global contributor to disease and death. Although conventional treatments have shown some success and benefits, they also present challenges. Due to the fact that therapeutic results vary among individuals, significant side effects and drug resistance arise from the adaptation of cells because of new growth pathways and changes in target receptors [1].

Cancer immunotherapy represents a group of advanced treatments designed to strengthen an individual's immune system to detect and target tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) responsible for cancer progression. Additionally, it aims to combat factors that hinder the immune system, with the goal of eliminating or managing tumors while also extending the period of disease stability and overall survival rates [2]. A primary benefit of cancer immunotherapy is its capability to generate immunological memory and reduce the risk of disease recurrence [3,4]. Moreover, by activating systemic immune responses and training the host's immune system, it can target both primary and metastatic tumors [5]. Cancer immunotherapy employs various techniques, including cancer vaccines, immune checkpoint inhibitors, cytokines, adoptive cell therapy, and therapeutic antibodies [6].

In recent years, due to the successes achieved in the clinic through immunotherapy, there has been significant progress in treatment of various diseases. However, attributable to the low rate of response and safety-related adverse effects, few patients have benefited from such treatments [7]. To improve the effectiveness and minimize potential side effects, researchers continue to investigate new molecular targets and innovative combination strategies. Precision medicine now focuses on targeted delivery methods, recognized for their accuracy and capacity to efficiently deliver therapeutic medicines to the exact location of the tumor. This strategy is a key component in effectively treating cancer [8].

Cell-based delivery systems, which mimic biological and physiological functions, offer numerous benefits. Since the systems are recognized as “self” by the immune system, they exhibit high biocompatibility and reduced immunogenicity. Furthermore, considering their high functionalization capability, they can target tumor cells, inhibit tumor growth, and serve as biomimetic carriers in immunotherapy [9]. Among these, erythrocytes (red blood cells, RBCs) are the most numerous blood cells and make up 80% of the body's cell population. Besides their role in oxygen transport [10], erythrocytes significantly contribute to many immunological functions [11]. Their lack of organelles and increased surface area compared to volume make them ideal candidates for drug loading and functionalization, and the most widely used cells in biomimetic drug delivery systems (DDSs) [12].

These distinctive features make them important carriers in immunotherapy. Since they lack genetic material, their use in immunotherapy lowers the risks associated with gene- or cell-based treatments and enhances their safety [13,14]. Moreover, the reticuloendothelial system (RES), which includes the lymph nodes, spleen, and liver, significantly affects erythrocyte metabolism. Aged and damaged erythrocytes are cleared by the spleen, whereas the liver recovers and recycles their iron content. Consequently, the organs of the RES, along with the immune system, offer significant advantages for drug delivery over other tissues [15].

This review highlights erythrocyte-derived delivery platforms used in cancer immunotherapy, their preparation methods, and their applications. The next section examines erythrocyte components that influence the immune system, followed by an investigation of the cancer-immunity cycle and the effective factors in each stage, as well as a discussion of erythrocyte-based systems for such applications.

Delivery vehicles in immunotherapy

Barriers and resistance mechanisms in cancer immunotherapy

Tumor resistance to immunotherapy is a major challenge in immunotherapy and has prompted the search for innovative delivery systems. Due to their intricate genetic makeup, tumors can become resistant to therapies designed to inhibit their growth. Several immunosuppressive mechanisms contribute to tumor resistance, including regulatory T cells (Tregs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), suppressive immune checkpoints, immunosuppressive cytokines, metabolic suppression of immunity, and physical barriers in the tumor microenvironment (TME) [16,17].

For instance, cancer cells overexpress the programmed cell death protein ligand (PD-L1) to interact with their receptor, the PD-1, on the surface of T cells, and as a result, they suppress cytotoxic T cells (CTLs) [18]. Another immunosuppressive mechanism involves indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO-1), an enzyme overexpressed in cancer cells that regulates immune surveillance via the kynurenine metabolic pathway, where it converts tryptophan into kynurenine within the TME [19]. This process disrupts the function of CTLs while promoting the expansion of Tregs [20].

Other mechanisms of resistance include mutation in gene expression, epigenetic changes, and drug efflux, which result in reduced intracellular drug accumulation [21]. Additionally, tumors maintain a complex and heterogeneous metabolic regulatory network, with the production of inappropriate metabolites and the recruitment of different cells to sustain an immunosuppressive microenvironment [22]. As a result, immune system cells and monoclonal antibodies face problems with these barriers to penetrate cancer cells or overcome immunosuppressive signals within the TME within the TME. These factors make cancer cells resistant to common immunotherapy. As a result, advancing immunotherapy depends on new methods to overcome this resistance. Addressing it increasingly requires the use of targeted delivery systems, including the transfer of antigens to lymph nodes and the delivery of immune checkpoint inhibitors to tumor tissue [23,24].

Conventional delivery platforms in cancer immunotherapy

Numerous delivery vehicles have been developed to improve the effectiveness of cancer immunotherapy via delivery of tumor antigens, immunomodulators, nucleic acids, and immune checkpoint inhibitors. Conventional systems include organic and inorganic delivery systems, each offering distinct therapeutic advantages while facing important biological and translational limitations.

In comparison with other organic systems, liposomes represent the most clinically well-developed type of nanocarrier due to their biocompatibility and capacity for carrying both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs. They have been extensively investigated for the delivery of cytokines, tumor antigens, and immune checkpoint inhibitors [25,26]. Other lipid-based systems, which include solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) and nanoemulsions, have also shown promise regarding their application in the stabilization and bioavailability enhancement of drugs [27]. Polymeric nanoparticles such as PLGA, PEI, and chitosan provide additional flexibility for controlled drug release and nucleic acid delivery [28], whereas dendrimers and hydrogels [29] have been investigated for targeted and localized immunotherapy applications. Despite these advantages, many organic systems still face limitations related to premature drug leakage, poor structural stability, limited tumor penetration, inflammatory toxicity, and inconsistent long-term biosafety [26,30,31].

Inorganic nanoparticles provide multifunctional properties beyond conventional drug delivery. Gold nanoparticles, magnetic nanoparticles, mesoporous silica nanoparticles, quantum dots, and carbon-based nanomaterials have attracted considerable attention because of their imaging capability, photothermal responsiveness, and theranostic potential [32]. Several of these systems can additionally induce immunogenic cell death and enhance dendritic-cell activation and T-cell infiltration. However, concerns regarding long-term accumulation, biodegradability, oxidative stress, and inflammatory toxicity continue to hinder their broader clinical translation [32,33].

Despite the substantial progress achieved with conventional delivery systems, many platforms continue to face critical barriers, including rapid immune clearance, nonspecific biodistribution, inadequate tumor penetration, systemic toxicity, and limited long-term safety. In addition, several synthetic materials may themselves trigger immune responses. For example, polyethylene glycol (PEG), commonly used to improve nanoparticle stability and circulation time, has been associated with the generation of anti-PEG antibodies [7]. These limitations have stimulated growing interest in biomimetic and cell-derived delivery systems designed to improve immune compatibility, prolong circulation, and enhance therapeutic precision. Cell-based and biomimetic delivery systems

In response to the limitations associated with conventional exogenous delivery platforms, increasing attention has been directed toward endogenous and biomimetic delivery systems. These systems have rapidly developed in this field because of their prolonged circulation time, reduced immunogenicity, and an inherent preference for specific tissues [49]. These systems, along with specific functional changes that can be applied, have a high potential against cancer recurrence through long-term immune system surveillance. Among the options, cell-based delivery systems offer considerable advantages. These systems have been used to improve tumor targeting in chemotherapy [50].

Despite these advances, cancer immunotherapy still faces challenges related to carrier cell cytotoxicity and instability during systemic circulation. To address these challenges, the progression of cell-based delivery systems has prioritized cell surface engineering due to its well-established manufacturing methods and extensive customization options. The components of cell membranes, including lipids, carbohydrates, and proteins, are highly diverse, which exposes functional groups, like thiol and amine groups, in cysteine and lysine residues. These functional groups can be chemically conjugated with immunotherapeutic agents through simple and efficient chemical reactions, making cell-based carriers highly adaptable for cancer immunotherapy [51,52].

Immunotherapy using erythrocyte biomimicry

Advantages of erythrocyte-based delivery systems

Among cell-based DDSs, erythrocytes are plentiful, easily obtainable, and simple to isolate cells [53]. They offer several advantages, in particular a high surface-to-volume ratio for functionalization, the absence of genetic materials, which eliminates risks of proliferation and unwanted mutations in other cells, and their role in innate immunity. With a relatively uncomplicated structure once matured [54] (lacking organelles and containing mostly hemoglobin), the purification of membranes containing self-identifying markers such as CD47 and complement regulatory proteins is moderately straightforward [55]. Additionally, erythrocyte-based drug carriers are cost-effective, well-tolerated by the body, and exhibit prolonged circulation times compared to other immune cells, allowing for sustained drug delivery to cancer cells. When appropriately engineered, they are able to circulate in the body even longer [14,31,56]. Moreover, erythrocytes can be functionalized to target particular tissues and immune cells, underscoring their value in drug delivery, biomedical applications [15,53], and cancer immunotherapy. Compared with other cell-based systems, erythrocyte-based systems offer prolonged circulation, immune evasion, and reduced macrophage engulfment. On the contrary, erythrocyte membranes lack any disease-specific targeting ligands, thus limiting their homotypic or inflammatory site targeting ability compared to other bioinspired carriers [69]. On the other hand, cancer cell membrane NPs display strong homotypic recognition via tumor-associated adhesion molecules, leading to enhanced accumulation and immune-specific antigen activation, though they pose safety issues and require special purification procedures [62,63]. Likewise, white blood cell (WBC) membranes confer active targeting to inflamed or tumor microenvironments and can modulate immune responses more directly but are technically complex to reproduce [60,62]. Platelet membrane systems exemplify vascular damage and cancer site targeting ability, especially at metastatic microenvironment sites with manufacturing challenges [59,62]. In general, erythrocyte systems focus on evading the immune system and stability, while other biomimetic systems focus on active targeting and stimulating the immune system, each presenting distinct translational advantages and limitations. The use of hybrid membranes attempts to combine both of these qualities into one system [57,59,63].

Immune functions and immunotherapeutic mechanisms of erythrocytes

Depending on their method of use and stage in their life cycle, erythrocytes exhibit different immune-related properties and can be utilized for both immune activation and immune tolerance [9]. Throughout their lifetime and circulation, erythrocytes naturally alter their physiological properties with aging or as a result of external damage and cell death [9], which affect their interactions with the immune system. In this review, the focus is on their role in immune activation.

Normal erythrocytes, as components of the innate immune system, contribute to the clearance of circulating bacteria by absorbing and transporting them to antigen-presenting cells (APCs), thereby facilitating immune activation. Consequently, normal erythrocytes can be used as a suitable carrier to activate the immune system in immunotherapy. In contrast, damaged erythrocytes with “necrotic-like” properties (necrotic, erythrocyte membrane and erythrosome) can directly induce and activate the immune response. As a result, these features should be considered in their design [9].

Once injured, erythrocytes undergo several changes, such as oxidative stress, which alters CD47 composition, and phosphatidylserine (PS) externalization, resulting in a change in the signal from "don't eat me" to "eat me" [[70], [71], [72]]. These erythrocytes are used as carriers in immunotherapy to induce and activate the immune responses and target the spleen, a site that houses B, T, and NK cells [9].

Unintentional damage to erythrocytes during drug loading can impact their biocompatibility and drug loading efficiency, except in cases where drugs are intentionally targeted to macrophages. Such damage involves conformational changes and abnormal clustering of membrane glycoproteins, particularly glycophorin A and band 3, caused by cross-linkers. Additionally, osmotic stress and cytoskeletal dysfunction can decrease erythrocyte plasticity, weaken mechanical stability, and increase oxidative sensitivity. Modifications and crosslinking drugs to the erythrocyte surface also alter its surface antigens and membrane plasticity. These modifications act as adjuvants that stimulate non-specific immune responses and facilitate antigen delivery. Carrier erythrocytes not only effectively deliver antigens but also improve immune responses [12,73] by displaying multiple copies of antigens on their surface.

Immunological and translational challenges

One of the most important unsolved questions when considering the clinical application of erythrocyte-based systems is the risk of an immune response, based on either autologous or allogeneic source of the erythrocyte membrane. The preclinical data indicate that both can demonstrate similar short-term biocompatibility; for instance, investigations by Dai et al. [74] revealed no difference between the use of autologous and allogeneic erythrocytes in terms of tumor growth inhibition, effectiveness, body weight, and hematological indicators. Nevertheless, this conclusion was derived from animal experiments, which have a different blood group system and immune response than humans [74].

Autologous erythrocytes largely eliminate alloimmune reactions and the risk of infectious diseases, although they present logistical challenges related to individualized preparation and large-scale production [75]. In contrast, allogeneic erythrocytes provide practical benefits, including the avoidance of patient phlebotomy. Asparaginase-loaded red blood cells have already been evaluated in clinical trials [76]; nonetheless, these approaches remain far from widespread clinical implementation, and questions regarding the relative merits of allogeneic versus autologous sources persist. Allogeneic erythrocytes may, upon repeated administration, induce recipient immunization. Exposure of the immune system to non-self structures on the surface of donor-derived red blood cells has been associated with reported immunization rates of approximately 15–30% in patients receiving multiple injections [77].

Nevertheless, during the preparation of erythrocyte-based delivery systems, including drug loading or surface conjugation, the multistep processing procedures such as separation, repeated incubations, washing, and resealing can efficiently remove extracellular proteins and free hemoglobin, thereby reducing certain risks associated with allogeneic erythrocytes [77]. Additional strategies to improve safety may include extended antigen matching, the use of universal donor–type erythrocytes, removal or masking of immunogenic epitopes, optimization of membrane purification methods, and long-term monitoring of anti-erythrocyte antibodies in clinical studies. Ultimately, comprehensive evaluation in human trials will be necessary to fully determine the immunological safety of these systems.

Biomimetic nano-delivery systems are designed using intact cells or cell-derived components, specifically cell membranes, proteins [66], etc. In the next section, both models and their preparation methods are examined in detail.

Erythrocyte membrane-Camouflaged nanoparticles

In recent decades, the swift advancement of nanotechnology has significantly contributed to drug delivery in cancer immunotherapy, hence enhancing the potential for clinical translation of cell-based DDSs [78,79]. Nanoparticles (NPs) are designed to target different organs of the body to prevent the systemic and rapid elimination of the drug as well as systemic toxicity. However, the NPs themselves are subject to clearance by the RES, primarily in the liver and spleen. Additionally, upon interaction with biomolecules in biological environments such as blood circulation, interstitial fluid, and the extracellular matrix (ECM), a protein corona forms around NPs, triggering recognition by the MPS and leading to their elimination [80]. To enhance nanoparticle retention and optimize their delivery, they must be engineered for proper orientation and targeted transport to their intended destination.

One approach to address these challenges is the use of biomaterial-based NPs that mimic biological molecules, thereby enhancing their structural and functional resemblance to natural cells [81]. A notable method in this area is cell membrane-coated NPs (CNPs), which integrate synthetic nanoparticle cores with cell membranes to improve immune evasion and drug delivery efficiency. These biomimetic NPs can circulate in the bloodstream, prevent identification and clearance by RES and MPS, and effectively overcome biological and physical barriers for tumor-targeted drug delivery and immunotherapy [82,83]. Additionally, extracellular vesicles (EVs) can also be used as particle coatings and contain functional cell membrane markers [84,85]. These NPs can function properly even after repeated administrations [86].

CNPs are multifunctional particles that combine the biointerfacing capabilities of cell membranes with the characteristics of synthetic nanoparticle cores. They have made significant progress since 2011, when erythrocyte membrane-coated PLGA NPs were designed to extend circulation time in vivo [87]. These NPs innovatively and effectively bridge the gap between synthetic NPs and biological systems. They have broad applications in disease diagnosis and treatment, including drug delivery, immunotherapy, and vaccine development [[88], [89], [90], [91], [92]]. When erythrocyte membrane is used as a nanoparticle coating, it can indirectly improve immunotherapy by loading immunotherapeutic agents with evasion from the immune system, increasing circulation time, and homotypic targeting, along with self-recognition molecules from cells such as white blood cells and cancer cells [[93], [94], [95]]. Cell membranes can include cell-surface proteins, giving NPs immune system-evasion properties, unlike synthetic PEG coatings [96]. CNPs frequently exhibit similar tropisms to the cells from which their membranes originate [97,98], enabling precise antigen delivery or the presentation of immunostimulatory signals for immunotherapy applications [99,100].

Erythrocytes, primary immune cells and platelets are also commercially available from blood banks, which facilitates the clinical translation of CNP formulations. Furthermore, they can be lyophilized for prolonged storage [84,98]. The use of blood products nearing expiration, which are not suitable for direct clinical use, may also reduce the waste of these precious resources. The particles should be evaluated for their performance through methods like TEM, Western blot, stability assays, mass spectrometry, and protein analysis [101,102]. Preparation methods

To prepare erythrocyte membrane or erythrocyte membrane-coated NPs, the first step is to obtain erythrocytes from a suitable source [84]. Once collected, the next step is to extract their membrane, which is achieved by using a hypotonic solution to lyse the cells and remove intracellular contents, followed by centrifugation to isolate the membranes [7,114]. The membrane coating process is then performed using methods such as ultrasonication [115], extrusion [116], and microfluidics [117].

During this process, mechanical pressure temporarily compromises membrane integrity, facilitating its wrapping around the nanoparticle and forming a core-shell structure. Studies show that the membrane on NPs acquires a right-side-out orientation. This process is enabled by the nanoparticle at the core and its interaction with the asymmetric charge distribution of the membrane bilayer [96]. Membrane asymmetry is important because it is responsible for cell-like properties and maintaining membrane function. However, certain preparation methods may affect membrane integrity. For instance, sonication generates intense local energy that can compromise the structural and functional integrity of the membrane, making careful optimization essential [115]. As a result, physical extrusion is often the preferred membrane coating method for materials that cannot sustain extreme disturbance. In contrast, microfluidics offers greater control over the process because more parameters can be adjusted, including voltage, mixing channel geometry, and electric field pulse rate. It also provides more predictable scalability [117]. Personalized cancer nanomedicine is considered a promising strategy that may ensure patients receive the most appropriate treatment [118]. The development of such customized nanomedicine has been advanced by cell membrane coating technologies, which increase circulation time, promote immune evasion, and improve the specificity and efficacy of nanomedicines, thereby contributing to effective tumor suppression [7]. If a patient's own blood is used for membrane extraction, the process becomes safer and personalized. In some commercial applications, devices are being developed that can load drugs into erythrocytes at the patient's bedside. This concept could be further expanded to produce NPs or coat NPs with erythrocyte membranes in real time for personalized treatments.

Membrane surface modification for enhanced functionality

Erythrocyte membranes naturally express numerous surface markers that may not be necessary for the intended purpose or may not be present in sufficient quantities or with the desired functionality for a specific application [84]. Several methods have been created to overcome such restrictions and enhance the performance of CNPs by modifying the cell membrane coating. Because the chemical methods commonly used for functionalization damage cell proteins and their function, other methods are used for this purpose. These include (Fig. 1b):

2.4.4.2.1. Ligand anchoring using lipids through hydrophobic interactions with the cell membrane. This method can be applied to both small and large molecules. Folate and the AS1411 aptamer has been attached to erythrocyte membrane-coated NPs through a lipid anchor and a PEG-based linker, representing an early strategy to improve CNP binding to cancer cells via membrane engineering [119]. Similar methods have been applied to functionalize CNPs with targeting ligands such as the angiopep-2 peptide or the tripeptide Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD), thereby enhancing payload delivery to tumor tissues [120,121].

2.4.4.2.2. Membrane hybridization: a strategy involving the combining two or more cell membranes to coat the NPs, makes it possible to simultaneously maintain the unique properties of each cell and the development of multifunctional NPs [122]. When erythrocyte membranes are used in combination with other cells such as dendritic cells, cancer cells and NK cells, they can directly affect the immune system. Cancer cell membranes through improving and increasing APC presentation [53], dendritic cell membranes by targeting and activating T cells, and NK cell membranes contribute to macrophage polarization [123].

2.4.4.2.3. Genetic engineering: an effective and powerful method for giving cells additional targeting and therapeutic capabilities [124]. Engineered cells can express molecules that are more stable than those of natural cells, allowing for reproducible, high-quality, and highly customizable functionalities [7]. For example, genetic modification of source cells to express very late antigen 4 (VLA4), which binds VCAM1 on inflammatory endothelial cells, enables the production of CNPs directed against VCAM [125]. In another approach, expression of the influenza envelope protein hemagglutinin in source cells significantly improved endosomal escape of the resulting CNPs following uptake, thereby enabling more efficient cytoplasmic delivery of their mRNA payload [126]. Additional examples include CNPs engineered to express high-affinity variants of signal regulatory protein-α (SIRPα), which lack downstream signaling activity but competitively inhibit CD47 [127], or engineered to express PD-1 to neutralize the immunosuppressive effects of PD-L1 signaling [128,129].

2.4.4.2.4. Metabolic engineering: another strategy in which source cells are exposed to modified sugars, leading to alterations in membrane composition and function. The modified membrane can then be chemically manipulated to introduce ligands [130]. These membrane engineering techniques have enabled the generation of highly customized functional platforms with improved performance over native cells. For instance, the same approach has been used to generate azide groups on their surface, therefore facilitating the easy attachment of ligands modified with dibenzocyclooctyne via copper-free click chemistry [130].

Mannose-modified erythrocyte membranes have also been utilized to improve the targeted delivery of cancer antigen-functionalized NPs to dendritic cells through their mannose receptors. In one study, a nanovaccine was developed using antigenic peptide (hgp1002533), the adjuvant MPLA, and mannose-inserted erythrocyte membrane-coated PLGA-NPs. This erythrocyte membrane-coated NP delivery platform combined the antigen entrapment and stimuli-responsive properties of polymeric NPs with the antigen presentation, adjuvant characteristics, and membrane protein integrity of erythrocytes. This discovery may offer a new way to create a safe, effective, low-cost, biomimetic vaccine [107].

In another study, erythrocyte membranes were modified to express N glycolylneuraminic acid, a tumor-associated carbohydrate antigen not endogenously synthesized in humans but obtainable from dietary sources, which accumulates on cancer cells. Altered glycosylation patterns are associated with cancer metastasis and can generate tumor-associated carbohydrate antigen (TACA) neoantigens. As a non-human immunogenic carbohydrate, Neu5GC accumulates on human cancer cells and functions as a neoantigen. This study produced glyconanoparticle biomimetic NPs to provide an active cancer vaccination for Neu5GC-positive tumors. αGal knockout pig erythrocytes were used to make NPs with or without Neu5gc-glycoconjugation [113]. Erythrocyte-assisted nanoparticle hitchhiking

Erythrocytes have a natural ability to trap and attach pathogens for detoxification, a feature that has been adapted into a hitchhiking drug delivery strategy [131]. By anchoring NPs to the erythrocyte surface, this method improves drug loading and selective delivery to target sites, thereby broadening the therapeutic window without compromising erythrocyte function [132,133]. As these erythrocyte-based carriers circulate with the blood flow, drug release is influenced by the varying shear forces encountered in different tissues. Ultimately, cargo detachment from erythrocytes occurs based on the interplay between these forces and the binding strength of NPs [[134], [135], [136]].

Understanding shear forces within different types of tissues and organs allows the design of a therapeutic system of antigens, immunomodulators, and chemotherapy drugs for precise targeting as lymph node targeting and tumor site delivery require distinct biodistribution behaviors [137]. In the case of lymph node targeting, particles need to be designed in a way that allows them to separate effectively from the erythrocytes after their passage through the capillaries or peripheral tissues so that they can enter the lymphatic vessels for subsequent migration to the draining lymph nodes. This can be accomplished by optimizing erythrocyte binding affinity, decreasing particle size, and modifying their surfaces to facilitate uptake by the lymph system or APCs. Conversely, in tumor targeting, delivery will benefit from enhanced erythrocyte binding during circulation, allowing prolonged vascular transport and accumulation in tumor microvasculature before release. Shear-dependent detachment, ligand-directed tumor cell adhesion, and regulated release rates could increase the accumulation at cancerous lesions [137]. Notably, the adjustment of erythrocyte affinity is a critical parameter in design. Excessive binding might hinder drug release, while insufficient binding might trigger early detachment and elimination from the body. Therefore, rational optimization of erythrocyte-hitchhiking systems should integrate particle physicochemical properties, vascular dynamics, and target tissue biology to achieve site-specific delivery.

However, the hitchhiking method is complicated due to multiple factors, such as the magnitude of shear forces, the strength of nanoparticle–erythrocyte binding, and the interaction between erythrocytes and APCs [134,138]. The strength of the immune response is also influenced by the quantity of antigen loading on the erythrocytes and any membrane modifications, such as antibody coating or heat treatment. Ukidve et al. introduced an EDIT (erythrocyte-driven immune targeting) approach, in which antigen-loaded NPs (OVA-NPs) were attached to the surface of erythrocytes to increase spleen targeting and induce a T lymphocyte response. Using polystyrene carboxylate NPs (200 nm) loaded with ovalbumin (OVA) as a model antigen, they found that the optimal erythrocyte-to-nanoparticle ratio (300:1) resulted in predominant spleen accumulation (lung-to-spleen ratio ≈ 0.8). Phagocyte depletion studies further confirmed that the NPs were primarily internalized by APCs located in the spleen. In a prophylactic EG-7 OVA tumor model, the hitchhiking system effectively enhanced immune responses and inhibited tumor growth.

NPs can be attached to erythrocyte surfaces using several methods [139].

Physical adsorption

This method relies on non-covalent interactions, either with or without erythrocyte modifications. Though it has low selectivity, it is usually performed ex vivo by incubating NPs with erythrocytes to reduce non-specific binding and facilitate manipulation. To extend intravascular circulation, Chambers et al. [140] physically associated NPs (ranging from 200 to 450 nm) to the surface of erythrocytes; the results revealed that approximately 5% of erythrocytes loaded with NPs stayed in the circulation for 12 h. Among the tested NPs, 220 and 450 nm ones exhibited the most prolonged circulation times.

Using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), the morphology of various-sized NPs bound to erythrocytes was investigated, and no appreciable shape changes in the NPs or host erythrocytes were found. The authors proposed that the observed adhesion was likely driven by a combination of van der Waals interactions, electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic effects between polystyrene NPs and the erythrocyte membrane.

Covalent binding

This approach provides stronger and more stable attachment by forming covalent bonds with functional groups such as carboxyl, amine [139], biotin-avidin [141], thiol [142], and lipid [143] groups. Due to the need for controlled reaction conditions, covalent binding is primarily performed ex vivo.

Biological anchoring

This method is carried out in two ways: coupling to cell surface receptors or molecules, and antibody or antibody fragment-mediated affinity binding. Binding of NPs by antibodies results in specific and selective attachment of NPs to antigens [143]. Unlike covalent binding, which requires ex vivo loading, biological anchoring allows in vivo nanoparticle attachment, reducing erythrocyte damage, simplifying clinical processes, and enhancing the feasibility of clinical applications (Fig. 1c) [139].

For effective immune activation by erythrocytes, it is essential that the cargo be attached through reversible interactions rather than stable covalent bonds. Commonly used non-covalent interactions include electrostatic, hydrophobic, and hydrogen bonding [9]. Antigenic NPs that detach from erythrocytes trigger the immune system's specific response prior to apoptosis of the carrier erythrocytes [135,136]. On the other hand, irreversible binding supports prolonged circulation of erythrocyte-based delivery systems, lasting until the erythrocyte undergoes programmed cell death and is subsequently taken up by antigen-presenting cells together with its antigenic cargo [9].

Limitations and translational considerations

It is crucial to take into account the following considerations when utilizing erythrocytes as delivery mechanisms in the hitchhiking model. Excessive attachment of drug-loaded carriers on the surface of erythrocytes can diminish their elasticity and ability to deform, which may result in faster removal of these cells by the RES and a reduction in their circulation duration [144]. In addition, ensuring the integrity of erythrocytes throughout the cell engineering process is essential for maintaining their functionality in vivo. Recent findings have highlighted the possibility of kidney failure resulting from hemoglobin released from damaged erythrocytes [145]. Furthermore, erythrocytes have limited ability to overcome biological barriers [146].

Consequently, the use of erythrocytes for drug delivery necessitates thorough evaluation of multiple aspects, including the method of drug loading (such as surface binding or coating), formulation quality control, and the range of potential applications. Currently, numerous pharmaceutical companies have advanced technology platforms for erythrocyte-based delivery systems, including EryDel's hypotonic loading method and Erytech's enzyme and antigen coating technology [147].

Components of erythrocytes effective in immune activation.

Damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs)

DAMPs, which are produced by tissues after injury, contribute to the modification of the body's immune response. Erythrocytes contribute to this process by releasing hemoglobin upon damage, leading to the formation of oxidized hemoglobin and heme. These molecules serve as erythrocyte-associated DAMPs and influence immune and non-immune cells alike. They perform as a chemoattractant and activate the complement system and host defense systems by stimulating innate immune receptors, heme oxygenase 1/ferritin, and innate immunologic memory [148]. Although their specific roles in oxidized hemoglobin states have not been widely studied, current evidence suggests involvement in immune mechanisms, particularly through the generation of ROS that trigger inflammation, as illustrated in Fig. 2a [149].

Hemoglobin

Hemoglobin is a redox-sensitive molecule with the potential to produce ROS, which leads to oxidative stress and cellular damage [150,151]. It regulates innate immunity through interactions with pathogen‐associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and Toll-like receptors (TLRs), modulating the function of T cells and macrophages while inducing the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and tumor necrosis factor α (TNFα) [151,152]. Furthermore, hemoglobin's high affinity for nitric oxide (NO) contributes to cell damage and endothelial dysfunction [153]. Additionally, hemoglobin acts as a potent DAMP by activating TLR4, further amplifying inflammatory responses [152].

Erythrocyte membrane

The distinctive characteristics of erythrocytes are closely linked to their membrane structure, consisting of a lipid bilayer containing numerous transmembrane proteins [154]. These proteins are essential for a wide range of biological processes, including adhesion, transport, and the maintenance of cell integrity. The erythrocyte membrane also contains integrins, peripheral membrane proteins, and carbohydrates embedded within the membrane structure.

However, the process of preparing erythrocytes as carriers causes structural changes in the membrane, including changes in the inner and outer components and differences in surface composition, resulting in a change in the type of immune responses [155,156]. Despite these changes, erythrocyte membranes preserve the physiological characteristics of the mother cell, including flexibility, biocompatibility, biodegradability, and low immunogenicity. Additionally, the preservation of various erythrocyte membrane components, including glycans and proteins, during isolation supports the biocompatibility and prolonged circulation of the delivered cargo [157,158].

The following sections discuss specific erythrocyte membrane components and their implications for cancer immunotherapy and vaccine design.

Lipids

Lipids account for approximately 40% of the erythrocyte membrane weight, which includes 24% phospholipids, 12% non-esterified cholesterol, and 4% glycerides and free fatty acids [15]. For the erythrocyte membrane that needs to change shape and move, cholesterol is a major structural component that helps maintain the structural integrity and fluidity of the membrane [15].

Among phospholipids, phosphatidylserine (PS) is particularly important for immune recognition. Unlike other phospholipids that freely transition between membrane leaflets, PS is normally restricted to the inner membrane. However, during erythrocyte aging or membrane damage, PS translocates to the outer membrane, accompanied by increased intracellular calcium ion (Ca2+) levels, triggering immune recognition and the "eat me" signal as represented in Fig. 2b [159].

Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are functional parts of the erythrocyte membrane. Another important aspect of the erythrocyte membrane in immune responses is the presence of carbohydrate residues, which are found in a range of forms on membrane proteins and lipids. Glycoproteins and glycosphingolipids play a role in determining blood groups. The carbohydrate residues are rich in sialic acid and mainly consist of galactose and mannose as structural components, while their branches are composed of sialic acid and fucose.

Sialic acid is the predominant carbohydrate residue found in the erythrocyte membrane, carries a negative charge. It is primarily found in sialoglycoproteins, including glycophorin A, B, and C [15]. PAS-1 and PAS-2 represent two interchangeable forms of glycophorin A, and their ratios change depending on the age of erythrocytes. In addition, sialic acid N-acetylneuramic acid plays a significant role in the circulation of erythrocytes and helps to protect erythrocytes from destruction by the immune system and maintain the membrane flexibility [160].

Sialic acid structure

Sialic acid is a nine-carbon monosaccharide prominently displayed on the outer surface of erythrocytes. The most prevalent derivatives found in the cell membrane include N-acetylneuraminic acid (Neu5Ac), N-glycolylneuraminic acid (Neu5Gc), 2-keto-3-deoxy-D-glycero-D-galacto-nononic acid (KDN), and N-acetyl-9-O-acetylneuraminic acid (Neu5AcAc) [15,161].

Distribution of sialic acid on the erythrocyte membrane

Glycans and glycosphingolipids on the erythrocyte membrane often carry sialic acids. These residues are also widely incorporated into secretory glycoproteins [162]. The composition of sialic acids in erythrocytes differs among species; for instance, Neu5Gc is abundant in many mammals, but absent in humans. On the outermost surface of the membrane, sialic acid combines with proteins to form glycoproteins known as glycophorins [15].

Sialic acid serves multiple functions, including the regulation of cell and molecular interactions, and is critically involved in antioxidant activity, inflammation, immune regulation, and homeostasis [163,164]. The strong negative charge of sialic acid contributes significantly to the erythrocyte surface zeta potential, enhancing water interaction and promoting adhesion to both other erythrocytes and vessel walls [165]. The presence of sialic acid also contributes to the structural integrity and flexibility of erythrocytes. Its levels serve as an indicator of cell aging, as a reduction in sialic acid content shortens the erythrocyte lifespan [166]. Oxidative stress causes desialylation, altering the rheological properties of these cells (Fig. 2c) [167,168].

Proteins

Based on various investigations in this area, it has been observed that SDS–PAGE analysis of erythrocyte membrane-coated particles relative to the mother erythrocyte membrane indicates appropriate conservation of membrane protein content [[169], [170], [171]]. In addition, key proteins such as CD47 have been specifically evaluated using Western blotting, with results confirming their retention on the particle surface [169,172]. The right-side-out orientation of the erythrocyte membrane, which is critical for maintaining biological function, has also been investigated using immunoblotting with anti-CD47 antibodies, demonstrating preservation of membrane sidedness [170]. Available in vivo and clinical findings further support the functional activity of these proteins after membrane isolation and coating [[169], [170], [171], [172], [173]].

CD47

CD47 is a transmembrane glycoprotein belonging to the immunoglobulin superfamily [174]. It is widely expressed on the cell surface that is glycosylated at the N-terminal domain of IgV with five transmembrane helices and a short cytoplasmic tail on the surface of cells, including erythrocytes and cancer cells [174]. The signaling function of CD47 depends on several factors, the most important being the type of ligand that binds to it. CD47 has three main ligands: SIRPα, thrombospondin-1 (TSP-1), and integrin [175].

When CD47 interacts with SIRPα on macrophages, it triggers cytoplasmic phosphorylation of immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibitory motifs (ITIMs) and recruits SHP-1 and SHP-2 phosphatases. These phosphatases prevent the accumulation of myosin IIA, which plays a role in self-identification, thereby inhibiting phagocytosis and generating the "don't eat me" signal [176,177]. This feature is desirable for drug carriers, increases the duration of the presence of the carrier containing the drug, prevents its premature removal by the immune system in the blood, and increases its therapeutic effect. The expression level of CD47 on the cell membrane is proportional to the age of the cell [178]. As erythrocytes age, CD47 levels decline, and the protein undergoes structural changes. Oxidative stress can further alter CD47's conformation, disrupting the "don't eat me" signal and leading to recognition and removal by the immune system [9,179,180].

As a result, CD47 is recently used as a target and an innate checkpoint receptor for cancer immunotherapy [175]. Additionally, physical modifications to CD47, such as mechanical extrusion or sonication to generate nanoerythrosomes, can promote phagocytosis, the secretion of specific cytokines, and immune activation [12]. Consequently, aged, apoptotic, and damaged erythrocytes may exhibit an amazing potential for immunotherapy applications (Fig. 2d).

CD58

CD58 is a glycosylated membrane protein expressed in various human cells, including erythrocytes [181]. It fortifies the immune system by promoting immune cell interactions with antigens [182]. CD58 interacts with CD2, a ligand found on B cells, T cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and bone marrow cells [183], exhibiting structural similarity [184]. T cells and APCs form an immunological synapse (IS) during cell-to-cell interaction and intracellular signaling, to which CD58 and CD2 contribute, modulating and amplifying immune responses [183].

Natural killer-enhancing factor

Studies have shown that erythrocytes can augment the cytotoxicity of NK cells through natural killer-enhancing factors (NKEFs), which belong to the antioxidant family [[185], [186], [187]]. Lymphokine-activated NK cells exhibit enhanced killing activity in the presence of NKEF-A and NKEF-B, two key members of this family [188].

Erythrocyte-EVs

EVs are cell-derived particles, including exosomes, ectosomes, microvesicles, microparticles, and apoptotic bodies that mediate intercellular communication [189]. Erythrocyte-derived EVs exhibit pro-inflammatory properties by polarizing macrophages toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype through TLR4-MyD88-NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways [190]. These particles are capable of crossing the blood-brain barrier and activating central nervous system microglia [191] and also have the ability to hyperactivate circulating monocytes [192]. However, more studies are still needed to better understand the biological and immunological roles of these particles.

Understanding the crucial functions of cell-based DDSs in the cancer-immunity cycle enables the improved design of cell-based cancer immunotherapy platforms, which are gaining increasing importance due to their rapid development and active engagement in specific stages of the cancer-immunity cycle. This approach elicits antitumor immunity with fewer off-target toxicities. Erythrocytes can facilitate and enhance immunotherapy both directly and indirectly: directly by boosting the antitumor immune response, and indirectly by improving targeted delivery of therapeutic agents and extending circulation time.

Cancer-immunity cycle

The immune system combats cancer through a process known as the cancer-immunity cycle, which involves a sequence of coordinated events that must be recurrently activated to generate an effective antitumor response [193]. These steps determine the final immune response by balancing regulatory T cells and effector T cells (increased effector T cells and decreased Tregs) [79]. Understanding this cycle provides insight into designing immunotherapy strategies that effectively target each stage of the anticancer immune response [193].

Dendritic cells, acting as APCs, first capture the antigens produced by tumors and subsequently present them to T lymphocytes. CTLs eliminate tumor cells, while APCs stimulate and activate effector T cells to migrate toward and infiltrate tumor tissues. Effective immunotherapy depends on the presence of CTLs and their ability to penetrate metastatic sites, leading to the recognition and elimination of tumor cells expressing TAAs [7,79,193]. Although the immune system can recognize and destroy tumor cells through the cycle, each step requires reinforcement to ensure a robust response.

In cancer patients, the cycle does not function optimally, as suppressive factors at each stage prevent the generation of a suitable response [194]. However, strengthening the entire cycle may also pose risks, since excessive activation could damage normal cells and tissues. Therefore, the optimal strategy involves targeted enhancement of specific steps rather than a broad nonspecific approach. Given that monotherapy may result in incomplete tumor ablation, combination therapy has become the preferred option in recent clinical settings. This strategy augments tumor eradication and strengthens antitumor immune responses [79]. Several immunotherapeutic approaches are commonly combined with other treatment modalities.

Strategies for improving the cancer-immunity cycle [7] include the following.

Facilitating the release of tumor antigens with factors that cause immunogenic cell death (ICD)

Two principal types of cancer immune antigens are recognized: TAAs, which are highly expressed in cancer cells, and neoantigens, which emerge due to genetic mutations and impaired regulatory control [195]. Neoantigens lead to the expression of MHC I-bound peptides, distinguishing them from their normal counterparts and allowing recognition by CD8+ T cells [196]. However, this recognition alone is insufficient to elicit a protective therapeutic response due to inhibitory factors such as immune checkpoints in lymphoid organs and immunosuppressive elements within the TME (e.g., tumor bed, immunostat function) [193].

Cells nearing death can expose or release DAMPs, including histones, calreticulin (CRT), adenosine triphosphate (ATP), heat shock proteins (HSPs), and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1). This process disrupts immune tolerance, transforms non-inflamed (cold) tumors into inflamed (hot) tumors, and initiates antitumor immune activation [6,197,198]. In addition, when tumor cells are destroyed, they release their own antigens, contributing to the formation of a favorable TME that stimulates the innate immune system and increases antigen presentation to APCs, thereby improving immunotherapy [199]. Investigating these mechanisms is essential for developing novel therapeutic agents and drug delivery strategies. Nanomedicine, as a targeted therapeutic approach, has the potential to integrate immunotherapy with additional modalities, effectively converting a cold tumor, characterized by immune depletion, into a hot tumor [200].

Therefore, conventional chemotherapy, along with tumor ablation methods, specifically radiation therapy (RT), photothermal therapy (PTT), and photodynamic therapy (PDT), as well as targeted approaches like nanoparticle-based delivery systems, can significantly enhance cancer immunotherapy. These highly efficient methods facilitate in situ tumor neoantigen presentation, boosting the immune response against cancer. Chemotherapy

For decades, chemotherapy and immunotherapy have been regarded as opposing treatment strategies. Due to its immunosuppressive effects and non-selective cytotoxicity, chemotherapy has traditionally been considered to induce a non-immunogenic form of cell death, promoting immune tolerance rather than eliciting a specific CTL response [201]. However, growing evidence now suggests that chemotherapy can enhance tumor immune responses by increasing antigen presentation and inducing ICD [202]. Chemotherapy contributes to antitumor T cell responses through mechanisms particularly promoting tumor-specific CD8+ T cell activity, inducing IFN-γ secretion, and selective reduction of MDSCs in the TME [203,204].

Certain chemotherapeutic agents have been identified to produce ICD in cancer cells, triggering antitumor immune responses through TAAs released in cancer cell debris following chemotherapy [205,206]. Agents such as bleomycin, cyclophosphamide, paclitaxel (PTX), anthracyclines (e.g., doxorubicin (Dox) and mitoxantrone) [207,208], DNA-damaging agents (e.g., oxaliplatin) [209], and proteasome inhibitors (e.g., bortezomib) [210] have been used to stimulate ICD. This process induces the release of “eat me” signals, promotes tumor antigen, and improves the response of T cells [211]. These agents can be combined with cancer immunotherapy [212,213]. Additionally, studies suggest that low-dose Dox can modulate dendritic cell function, further contributing to immune activation.

The integration of chemotherapy with immunotherapy can be approached in three distinct ways: 1) chemotherapy with a standard treatment cycle, 2) low-dose chemotherapy to regulate immunity, and 3) immune system reactivation following high-dose chemotherapy occurs through mechanisms involving cytokine release and homeostatic proliferation [201,214]. For instance, Bao et al. utilized targeted polymer micelles combined with chemotherapeutic agents and an erythrocyte membrane-enveloped polymer nano-vaccine to evaluate antitumor efficacy and immune mechanisms in transplanted and metastatic melanoma models. They administered a standard dose of chemotherapy via a single tail vein injection, followed by multiple intradermal nano-vaccine injections the next day [214].

Building on the previous work, Su et al. proposed a novel strategy by encapsulating Dox and chlorin e60 (Ce6) within mesoporous silica NPs (MSNs) and coating them with an erythrocyte membrane (RMSNs-Dox/Ce6). To further address systemic toxicity, other study has developed localized chemoimmunotherapy “cocktails” by embedding ICD-inducing chemotherapeutics and immunological adjuvants within an alginate (ALG) matrix. These formulations may also incorporate immune checkpoint inhibitor antibodies for local injection or standard intravenous administration. Once injected into solid tumors, ALG undergoes in situ gelation, leading to localized retention and prolonged drug release, thereby reducing systemic toxicity [221].

RT

Radiation therapy is another method of eradicating cancer cells [222]. The oxygen level is a critical factor in determining the antitumor effect; therefore, TME hypoxia should be considered when applying this treatment.

Phototherapy

Phototherapy has emerged as a promising clinical treatment for solid tumors through two primary mechanisms: PTT, which uses thermal energy to kill tumor cells, and PDT, which induces oxidative stress–mediated apoptosis and necrosis via producing reactive oxygen species (ROS). The heat generated during PTT enhances blood flow and oxygen levels in tumor tissue, improving the penetration of phototherapeutic agents and boosting the effectiveness of PDT [223]. Likewise, ROS produced during PDT can suppress heat shock protein expression, further enhancing the effectiveness of PTT [224].

Phototherapy significantly contributes to tumor destruction and modulating vascular permeability, improving the efficiency of synergistic therapies. For instance, PTT-induced alterations in tumor permeability enhance RT sensitivity, allowing for the effective elimination of residual cancer cells and metastases.

Leveraging the precise spatiotemporal control of phototherapy enables the localized release of chemotherapeutic drugs directly at the tumor site. Additionally, phototherapy induces ICD by upregulating calreticulin on dying tumor cells and promoting the release of DAMPs. These signals prompt immune cells to recognize and engulf cancer cells, leading to tumor-specific antigen and cytokine release, ultimately activating immune responses and facilitating synergistic immunotherapy [[225], [226], [227]].

Phototherapy has several advantages, including being minimally invasive, highly efficient, and low in toxicity. However, it also has limitations, like poor tumor targeting of phototherapy agents, particularly in large tumors (∼100 mm3), as it lacks deep tumor penetration and target specificity [228]. Other challenges include elimination by the immune system and low biocompatibility. To address these limitations, biomimetic agents have been explored [223]. Erythrocytes have been investigated as potential candidates for cancer therapy for their capacity to relieve tumor hypoxia and induce oxidative damage. However, their application remains limited due to constraints such as oxygen supply capacity and the ROS production rate [229].

Cancer cells require a substantial amount of oxygen to sustain rapid proliferation, often resulting in a hypoxic tumor microenvironment, which can further compromise PDT effectiveness. As seen in Fig. 5b, a novel approach known as erythrocyte-facilitated photodynamic therapy (RBC-PDT) has been introduced by Tang et al. RBC-PDT leverages erythrocytes, which act as natural oxygen transporters, to deliver photosensitizers. By positioning photosensitizers close to an oxygen source, RBC-PDT can efficiently generate singlet oxygen even under low-oxygen conditions. Additionally, the long circulation time of erythrocytes ensures a sustained intravascular concentration of photosensitizers during PDT, maximizing tumor blood vessel damage and enhancing therapeutic efficacy [216].

Other biomimetic strategies include erythrocyte membranes-coated iron oxide (Fig. 5c) [217], copper selenide NPs (Fig. 5d) [218], and gold nanocages (Fig. 5e) [219]. These constructs absorb light in the NIR-II window (≥1000 nm) [218], facilitating deeper tissue penetration during PTT in mouse models.

PTT is a highly effective method for in situ tumor neoantigen presentation, holding significant potential for cancer immunotherapy. The objective of in situ vaccination is to stimulate immunity against endogenous antigens released during treatment, rather than introducing exogenous antigenic material [230]. A striking example is the BPQD-RMNV platform, comprising black phosphorus quantum dots (BPQDs) cloaked with erythrocyte membranes. These nanovesicles circulate longer and preferentially accumulate in tumors. When irradiated, they induce tumor cell apoptosis and necrosis in basal-like 4T1 breast tumors, recruit dendritic cells for antigen capture, and when combined with anti–PD-1 therapy, significantly delays residual and metastatic tumor growth. Importantly, this combined therapy facilitates CD8+ T cell infiltration and activation, underscoring the synergy between PTT and cancer immunotherapy (Fig. 5f) [220].

Another study investigated a theranostic nanocarrier designed to induce ICD. The erythrocyte membrane coated nanocarrier (RBCm-IR-Mn), containing IR780-loaded Mn-ferrite NPs, was engineered to generate a cytotoxic thermal dose for PTT while enhancing immune system activation. In vitro, this approach effectively induced apoptosis and significantly increased ICD markers, including calreticulin and HMGB1, following PTT treatment. Furthermore, in vivo experiments in a mouse sarcoma model showed an 85% increase in survival rate, indicating the promising potential of RBCm-IR-Mn NPs for PTT-driven cancer immunotherapy [231].

A related study introduced NPs loaded onto erythrocyte membranes (M@AP) for PTT-immunotherapy of tumors. The formulation consists of self-assembling positively charged aggregation-induced emission luminogen (AIEgen) P2-PPh3, serving as a photothermal agent for PTT, and negatively charged immune-stimulant polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (Poly (I:C)), which activates the immune system. M@AP NPs accumulate in tumors via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect and erythrocyte membrane binding. Upon NIR laser irradiation, AIEgen generates heat, inducing localized tumor cell apoptosis and promoting the release of tumor antigens. This heat also modulates the tumor microenvironment, facilitating immune cell recruitment. Furthermore, combining PTT with Poly (I:C) boosts the immune response by stimulating dendritic cells and promoting the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. The erythrocyte membrane coating improves long circulation time and tumor targeting, reducing immune system clearance. This biomimetic approach maximizes PTT's therapeutic potential by inducing ICD while stimulating the immune system, thereby enhancing cancer immunotherapy [109].

Enhancing cancer antigen presentation and DC maturation

APCs, particularly DCs, are crucial for T cell activation. DCs acquire cancer cell antigens and migrate to lymph nodes (LNs) to deliver them to T lymphocytes, releasing proinflammatory cytokines that further activate T cells. Therefore, DC-targeted vaccinations may strengthen anticancer immunity [232,233].

Cancer vaccines utilize specific antigens or DNA/RNA encoding TAAs to generate an immune response aimed at preventing or treating cancer [234]. Tumor vaccines work by presenting TAAs, thereby inducing specific antitumor immunity. Despite significant progress in tumor vaccine research, major challenges remain [235]. One of the primary challenges is identifying appropriate tumor antigens. Even when a viable antigen is found, determining the most effective delivery method, whether through TAAs or neoantigens remains an ongoing area of investigation. To date, only a few cancer vaccines have received FDA approval [236,237]. The key obstacles to their success are low antigenicity of target antigens, tumor heterogeneity [237], and low mutational load with only a few peptide neoantigens [238] in some cancers. Due to such drawbacks, the search for viable cancer vaccine antigens remains an active area of investigation.

A cancer vaccine typically consists of a carrier, an antigen, and an adjuvant to enhance the activity of the antigen. These vaccines stimulate the immune system to recognize and eliminate tumors while also preventing their formation [239]. Compared with adoptive T cell therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors, cancer vaccines are simpler, more cost-effective, and associated with fewer side effects, as they primarily target tumor-specific antigens [240]. Cancer vaccines may utilize antigens derived from whole tumor lysates that are overexpressed in cancerous tissues or mutant neoantigens unique to tumor cells [241].

Cancer vaccines contain TAAs and adjuvants to initiate and boost the anticancer immune response. In the anti-tumor immune response, adjuvants increase the antigenicity of weak antigens, amplify the level and type of circulating antibodies, strengthen cell-mediated hypersensitivity, and protect antigens from enzymatic decomposition in vivo. Common adjuvants in cancer vaccines include 5′-C-phosphate-G-3′ (CpG), polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (poly (I:C)), STING, TLR agonists, cytokines, and saponins, among others [242].

To boost vaccine efficacy, researchers have explored various antigen delivery strategies, including polymeric NPs, liposomes, and emulsions. Notably, the use of erythrocytes as carriers for vaccine delivery has shown great potential, offering greater immunogenicity and therapeutic potential [57].

Advancements in biomaterials engineering have provided novel opportunities to improve cancer vaccine design. A range of biomaterials, from micro- and nanodelivery systems to depot platforms, have been utilized for vaccine administration [243,244]. These technologies augment existing cancer vaccine strategies by prolonging the delivery of multiple immunomodulatory agents, precisely targeting specific sites, and extending in vivo bioactivity, thereby generating more potent and enduring immune responses compared with conventional vaccination [245].

At the end of their lifespan, erythrocytes are transported to the spleen [246,247]. Due to their natural spleen-targeting ability, erythrocytes have been explored for cancer vaccine applications. Erythrocyte-based antigen delivery systems can protect antigens from rapid clearance, enable in situ targeting of splenic APCs, facilitate erythrophagocytosis-mediated antigen delivery to APCs, and promote antigen processing and presentation to T cells.

Extensive research has focused on gel-based delivery platforms capable of transporting diverse therapeutics, including agents that modulate immune responses and vaccines targeting cancer [248,249]. Compared with synthetic polymer hydrogels, which face challenges related to complexity, regulatory barriers, and commercialization costs, blood clots offer significant translational potential [249]. Blood clots possess inherent biocompatibility, do not produce non-self-toxic by-products, and exhibit favorable safety characteristics. The cross-linked erythrocytes within blood clots have reduced CD47 levels, which activate the complement system and become targets for phagocytosis [250]. Additionally, blood clots attract immune cells, including macrophages, DCs, B cells, T cells, and natural killer cells, thereby fostering an immune response. According to previous studies, it has been proven that aged and damaged erythrocytes induce M1-polarized macrophages, thereby stimulating pro-inflammatory immunity. Therefore, they can be used for immunotherapy and personalized vaccines, with TAAs or adjuvants incorporated to elicit specific immune responses.

Inspired by the natural clotting process, Fan et al. developed a tumor vaccine based on autologous blood clots that can be implanted with suitable biocompatibility. The internal network of the blood clot gel contains numerous damaged erythrocytes, which cause the accumulation of immune cells and stimulates antitumor immunity. Similarly, Fei et al. created an injectable erythrocyte gel for photothermal immunotherapy, where subcutaneous injection allowed erythrocytes to form a gel with platelets and thrombin [73]. Upon NIR laser exposure, the gel mediated tumor photoablation, released TAAs, and activated APCs in tumor-draining lymph nodes, eliciting long-lasting systemic immunity, as shown in Fig. 6b. This combined strategy has demonstrated significant potential in enhancing immune memory to prevent cancer recurrence or metastasis. In general, erythrocytes offer notable advantages over previously used photothermal agents, addressing challenges related to manufacturing, safety, and commercialization [147].

Additionally, cancer cell membranes contain numerous TAAs and neoantigens, making them promising for cancer vaccine development [252]. Han et al. developed a unique method to direct damaged erythrocytes to spleen antigen-presenting cells, which are abundant in B, T, and NK cells due to their susceptibility to entrapment in the filtration system. Sonication and membrane extrusion were used to create tumor antigen-loaded nanoerythrosomes (nano-Ag@erythrosome). Exploiting necrotic-like alterations in damaged erythrocyte membranes, including increased cell volume, plasma membrane rupture, and cytoplasmic loss, this nanoplatform effectively targeted splenic APCs to activate immune responses. Erythrocyte membranes capture APCs, while cancer cell membranes create TAAs (Fig. 6c) [53].

Induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)-based vaccines offer broad-spectrum antitumor immunity due to shared epitopes with cancer cells [239,253]. However, using intact iPSCs poses risks, including tumorigenicity, necessitating pre-treatment with mitomycin C or γ-radiation. Their large size also increases the likelihood of accumulation in the lungs and other tissues. Zhai et al. combined iPSC total protein with erythrocyte-liposome-mannose carriers to enhance spleen-targeted antigen delivery, achieving effective tumor-specific immune responses and preventing tumor growth and metastasis, while addressing risks associated with iPSC tumorigenicity through pre-treatment, as illustrated in Fig. 6d [240].

Chemotherapy-induced ROS in the TME diminish and eliminate ICD-induced immune responses [241]. Treating chemotherapeutics to cancer cells in vitro maintains the immune-stimulating effects of cell death with minimal toxicity. Tumor heterogeneity limits the effectiveness of antigen delivery against cancer cells [254]. The use of a diverse set of chemotherapy-induced tumor antigens (cAgs) bound to erythrocytes may promote anticancer immune responses. Su et al. demonstrated that erythrocytes covalently modified with cAgs were efficiently phagocytosed by DCs, enhancing T cell infiltration and promoting durable antitumor immunity with lower toxicity than conventional therapies (Fig. 6e) [251].

Collectively, these studies highlight the potential of erythrocyte-based and biomaterial-enhanced delivery systems to improve antigen presentation, stimulate adaptive immunity, and enable personalized cancer immunotherapy. Future strategies may involve ex vivo modification of patient-derived erythrocytes with antigens and adjuvants, followed by reintroduction to achieve tailored photoimmunotherapy or systemic immune activation [73].

Improving initiation and activation of T cells

To improve the immune response against cancer cells, adoptive cell transfer therapy involves extracting, modifying, and reintroducing the patient's immune cells [255]. One of the most advanced forms of ACT is chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) therapy, which includes CAR T cells and CAR NK cells [256]. These engineered immune cells are designed to recognize and attack specific tumor antigens, offering a highly targeted approach to cancer treatment. However, challenges, specifically immune exhaustion, antigen escape, and potential toxicities remain significant barriers to the widespread application of CAR-based therapies.

APCs are key players in innate immunity, serving as a bridge between the innate and adaptive immune responses. After capturing tumor antigens, DCs subsequently present them to the T cells in the bone marrow, a crucial step for T cell activation. Consequently, numerous investigations have concentrated on the advancement of biomimetic APCs [[257], [258], [259], [260], [261]], which mimic the function of natural APCs to stimulate and activate T cells while increasing the release of inflammatory cytokines, thereby promoting an immune response. This approach holds great promise for enhancing adoptive T cell transfer and directly activating T cells for cancer immunotherapy [258].

Erythrocytes are well suited for constructing artificial APCs (aAPCs) due to their biocompatibility, high surface-to-volume ratio, and membrane flexibility [262]. Their optimal size (5–8 μm) enables them to effectively encompass the microscale immunological synapse and facilitate the reorganization of membrane-anchored proteins via membrane fluidity [263]. Their long lifespan also increases contact time with T cells when delivered intravenously [247]. For example, erythrocytes were engineered to display antigen peptides, major histocompatibility complex-I (MHC-I) molecules, and CD28-activating antibodies, along with IL-2 to provide signals for T cell proliferation and differentiation. Sun et al. proposed a novel approach to artificial APCs with surface-anchored T cell activating ligands that showed significant promise in adoptive immunotherapy. Nonetheless, precisely controlling ligand placement on those platforms using standard bioconjugation chemistry remains problematic. They employed DNA-assisted bottom-up self-assembly to accurately regulate the lateral and vertical distribution of T cell activation ligands on erythrocytes. The clustered lateral arrangement of peptide-major histocompatibility complex (pMHC) on erythrocytes, positioned at a minimal vertical distance from the cell membrane, enhances T cell activation, presumably due to its superior resemblance to natural APCs. Optimized erythrocyte-based artificial APCs can promote T cell proliferation in vivo and effectively suppress tumor growth by adoptive immunotherapy. DNA technology is a distinctive instrument for accurately modifying the cell membrane interface and adjusting cell-cell interactions, providing potential applications in immunotherapy (Fig. 7b) [259].

Another approach involves engineering erythrocyte-based aAPCs with peptides linked to MHC I, 4-1BBL, and IL-12. In in vivo tumor models, this causes robust, antigen-specific Tcell proliferation, memory formation, immune activation, tumor control, and antigen dissemination. Due to vascular and spleen limitation, 4-1BBL and IL-12 exhibit low toxicity. In vitro, the allogeneic aAPC RTX-321 activates HPV-specific T cells and increases effector function. Its surface contains the HPV16 peptide E711-19, 4-1BBL, and IL-12. Thus, RTX-321 may be an ‘off-the-shelf’ in vivo cellular immunotherapy for HPV-positive cervical and head/neck malignancies (Fig. 7c) [264].

Regulation of the TME (promoting immune cell infiltration)

The TME is defined by oxygen deprivation, acidic conditions, and increased interstitial fluid pressure, all of which reduce the effectiveness of treatments [265]. Tumors undergo rapid growth, leading to the development of abnormal metabolic microenvironments. These microenvironments involve increased glycolipid metabolism and the accumulation of lactate, a byproduct of glycolysis, resulting in an acidic environment with a pH of 6.5–6.8. Additionally, tumors experience internal hypoxia, elevated levels of ROS, and overexpression of proteases, which collectively contribute to the formation of an acidic and oxygen-deprived TME [262]. Such a microenvironment not only fosters tumor progression but also severely impairs the performance of chemotherapeutic agents and photosensitizers, as these treatments may become ineffective due to structural changes induced by the acidic conditions [266]. Enhancing TME conditions to facilitate immune cell penetration is therefore a key strategy in cancer therapy.

Adenosine (ADO), which accumulates improperly in the TME [267], serves as a metabolic checkpoint that enables tumors to evade immune surveillance [268]. Overexpressed CD39/CD73 ectonucleotidases hydrolyze extracellular ATP, leading to increased ADO levels in hypoxic TME [268]. Thus, eliminating the immunosuppressive effects of adenosine is crucial for improving cancer immunotherapy.

Sun et al. conducted a study in which bioactive nanovesicles (ENV@FeS) were developed to overcome the immunosuppressive activity of adenosine in the TME. The nanovesicles are composed of erythrocytes rich in adenosine deaminase (ADA), which metabolizes the suppressive adenosine into inosine, enhancing immune system activity. The inner core of the erythrocyte vesicles contains FeS, which utilizes its photothermal effect to improve tumor targeting and induce immunogenic cell death, thereby boosting antitumor immunity. In vivo studies showed increased infiltration of cytotoxic T cells (CD8+) and M1 macrophages, along with a decrease in T regulatory cells, M2 macrophages, and MDSCs. These effects collectively contributed to significant tumor growth suppression in a triple-negative breast cancer model. Furthermore, adenosine levels increase during PTT due to ATP breakdown mediated by CD73, negatively affecting immune system stimulation and promoting immunosuppression. To counteract this, a nanoparticle system called AptEM@CBA was developed. In this platform, chitosan-based nanogels are used to co-deliver black phosphorus quantum dots (BPQDs) as photothermal agents and the CD73 inhibitor α, β-methyleneadenosine 5′-diphosphate (AMPCP). To enhance tumor targeting and evade immune clearance, the nanogels are cloaked with erythrocyte membranes modified with the AS1411 aptamer, creating a biomimetic, stealth-like delivery system. The system works by blocking CD73, thereby mitigating adenosine-induced damage to dendritic cells and regulatory T cells. Under near-infrared light, the photothermal properties of BPQDs facilitated dendritic cell maturation and triggered the release of AMPCP, effectively blocking CD73-mediated adenosine production and suppressing regulatory T cell activity. The dual action led to a synergistic boost of T cell–mediated immune responses. However, a notable challenge arises from immunogenic cell death, which generates substantial levels of extracellular ATP. Tumor cells frequently overexpress the immunosuppressive enzyme CD73 (ecto-5′-nucleotidase), enabling rapid ATP hydrolysis into adenosine, thereby dampening antitumor immunity [272,273]. Elicited adenosine inhibits dendritic cell maturation and creates an immunosuppressive TME with many Tregs that suppress the immune response. CD73 blockade inhibits T cell inactivation and boosts antitumor response by blocking adenosine production, making it a promising immune modulation target (Fig. 8b) [225].

Another approach combined glucose oxidase (GOx) and thrombin, with cyclic Arg-Gly-Asp (cRGD) peptide-modified erythrocyte vesicles (EV@RGT). The system accumulates at the tumor site and releases its enzymes in the tumor's acidic microenvironment. The combination of GOx and thrombin depletes tumor glucose, leading to nutrient starvation, while increasing ROS production. This, in turn, induces NIR-II photothermal therapy and activates the immune system. Tumor vessels, clogged with blood, absorb NIR-II due to the presence of hemoglobin, aiding in tumor eradication. ROS generated by DNA damage through the STING/NF-κB pathway promotes the repolarization of macrophages to the M1 phenotype, thereby further helps tumor eradication. The vesicle system exerts a strong anti-tumor effect by inducing nutrient deprivation, enhancing photothermal therapy, and activating the immune system, thus facilitating immune cell infiltration and tumor destruction (Fig. 8c) [270].

To reprogram the TME, Zhang's group designed a cascade nanocatalytic system by depleting lactic acid within and outside tumor cells, thereby enhancing anti-PD-L1 therapeutic efficacy [108]. The PML nanostructures were created by loading glycolysis inhibitor, 3-(3-pyridinyl)-1-(4-pyridinyl)-2-propen-1-one (3PO), and lactate oxidase (LOX) in hollow MnO2 (HMnO2) NPs, then coated with erythrocyte membranes. In tumors, LOX catalyzed lactate oxidation while 3PO inhibited glycolysis, reversing immunosuppression and enhancing anti-PD-L1 therapy. PMLR displayed prolonged circulation, high tumor accumulation, effective lactate depletion, and improved CTL activity. Combination therapy with PMLR and anti-PD-L1 yielded superior tumor control compared with monotherapy (Fig. 8d).

The combined clinical evidence suggests that tumor suppressor gene deletion or mutation may be linked to the TME and ICB therapy resistance. Reduced T cell infiltration and immunosuppressive cell proliferation have been linked to PTEN gene deletion, which negatively affects the PI3K-AKT pathway. While introducing PTEN via plasmid DNA into tumor cells can help restore its function and counteract resistance to immunotherapy, systemic instability has limited its clinical use. Liu et al. [271] developed an erythrocyte membrane–coated, pH-sensitive nanoparticle system (ABNPs@mRNA) functionalized with ApoE to enable non-invasive delivery of PTEN mRNA across the blood-brain barrier for glioblastoma therapy (Fig. 8e).

The goal of many immunotherapeutic strategies is the efficient induction of CTLs.

Improving recognition and elimination of cancer cells by T cells

Immune responses are controlled by checkpoint proteins. Cancer cells often exploit these proteins to evade immune surveillance. ICIs block the proteins, thereby enabling the immune system to recognize and eliminate cancer cells [274,275]. Consequently, ICIs amplify T cell–mediated immune responses. T and B lymphocytes express PD-1, while cancer cells express PD-L1. The immune response of T and B cells is often affected by PD-1_PD-L1 interactions [276]. PD-1 on immune cells binding to PD-L1 on tumor cells suppresses immunity, facilitating unrestrained tumor growth. ICIs obstruct this connection, augmenting T cell activation and facilitating tumor eradication [79].

Likewise, regulatory T cells display CTLA-4 on their surface. This antigen binds to CD86 and CD80 on APCs, thereby inhibiting CD28 interaction to terminate T cell and antigen-presenting cell-mediated immune responses [277]. Such an interaction results in immunological suppression. By inhibiting CTLA-4, ICIs regenerate T cell responses against cancers. This approach has the potential to achieve long-lasting responses and complete regression of tumors in various cancers [278,279], but their response rate is less than optimal, a clinical response rate of less than 30% [280]. It induces a non-specific immune response, and as a result, side effects such as cytokine storm, which can even be fatal. Therefore, the improvement of ICI therapy and its combined use with other treatments have been investigated both in clinical trials and in basic research (Fig. 3) [281,282].

Tumors also exploit immune checkpoints, like PD-L1, which binds PD-1 on T cells to evade immunity [283]. The techniques to inhibit these immunological checkpoints have gained significant attention. While checkpoint inhibitors are effective [284], systemic delivery can lead to nonspecific distribution, drug wastage, and severe adverse effects stemming from excessive immune activation [79]. To ensure safe and effective treatments, spatiotemporal drug delivery is essential.

Zhao et al. developed ImmunoBait NPs, CXCL10-loaded NPs that attach to erythrocytes in a system called erythrocyte-anchored systemic immunotherapy (EASI). These NPs adhere without forming a chemical bond and remain attached under low shear stress conditions. However, when erythrocytes pass through the high shear stress environment of lung capillaries, the NPs detach and specifically accumulate in metastatic nodules. This strategy facilitated nanoparticle accumulation in metastatic lung nodules, significantly increasing CXCL10 concentration in the lungs and promoting the recruitment of CD8+ T cells, NK cells, and TH1 CD4+ cells. As a result, metastasis progression was effectively slowed in mice [285]. Additionally, strengthened tumor-specific and memory CD8+ T cell responses further inhibit distant tumor growth upon rechallenge. The erythrocyte-mediated systemic immunotherapy demonstrated both local (in situ immunization) and systemic tumor suppression, prolonged survival, and immune activation, highlighting its potential as a versatile cancer vaccine approach [135]. Zheng et al. demonstrated a bionic skeleton composed of Au(I)–peptide oligomeric complex NPs, which self-assemble into spherical clusters covered by an erythrocyte membrane, forming the final particle named Nery-PMIV. The system enhances macrophage absorption, colloidal stability, prolonged blood circulation, improved tumor targeting, and GSH release. The study demonstrated that Nery-PMIV restored p53 and p73 in vivo and in vitro by destroying MDM2/MDMX. It effectively boosted antitumor immunity induced by anti-PD-1 treatment in a mouse model. Furthermore, nanoparticle-mediated p53 and p73 restoration increased CD8+ T cell activation, making them more responsive to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy. Since all the components used are biocompatible, Nery-PMIV presents a safe and relatively effective therapeutic agent and immunotherapy sensitizer (Fig. 9b) [286].

Tim-3, a newly identified immune checkpoint protein, regulates immunological responses with expression by cytotoxic T cells, IFN-γ-producing helper T cells, dendritic cells, and other lymphocyte subsets [287]. Tim-3 can negatively impact the immune response by binding to the main ligand of galectin-9, causing effector T cell exhaustion or apoptosis, or indirectly affecting DCs and regulatory T cells, reducing CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity [288]. Several anti-Tim-3 blocking antibodies are currently in clinical testing to reduce tumor immunosuppression [287]. Thus, combining Tim-3 inhibition with radical-based treatment strategies may boost antitumor immunity by promoting more efficient antigen presentation, stimulating T cell responses, and suppressing immunoregulatory cell populations [229].

Erythrocytes have the ability to participate in the immunological activities of the body due to the ability to produce ROS through hemoglobins. In addition to bacterial infections [134,289], this ability can be leveraged for other diseases, including cancer, for radical therapy. Until now, they have not been used owing to the limited amount of ROS produced by these cells as well as the antioxidant defense present in cancer cells. Moreover, erythrocyte-based delivery systems face additional challenges, including their large size, which hinders their passage through blood vessels and tumor tissue, the risk of contamination, and issues related to storage conditions and blood group compatibility [290]. Thus, making artificial erythrocytes without these limitations seems very desirable as a carrier to create a carrier with the ability to carry oxygen and produce a radical storm. Radical therapy is divided into PDT, sonodynamic therapy (SDT), and chemodynamic therapy (CDT), which use ROS to kill cancer cells and are known as promising strategies, especially in combined treatments [291,292]. Although several challenges hinder radical therapy, including tumor hypoxia, weak and shallow light penetration into target tissues, the stringent acidic conditions required for Fenton reactions, the need for H2O2, and the presence of antioxidant defense mechanisms such as thiols [293], these limitations remain significant.

Treatments by radicals can stimulate ICD by producing and secreting antigens associated with tumors, which improves and applies the presentation of antigens for use as anti-tumor immunity [294,295]. In this context, Li et al. created FTP@RBCM, consisting of a Fe-porphyrin-based metal-organic framework (MOF) core doped with platinum NPs and cloaked in erythrocyte membranes (RBCMs), which can enhance Tim-3 blockade immunotherapy with photodynamic and chemodynamic therapy (PDT/CDT) capabilities along with catalase- and glutathione peroxidase–like enzymatic functions. Unlike natural erythrocytes, which face limitations related to large size, fragility, and handling challenges, the artificial FTP@RBCM maintains prolonged circulation without such drawbacks. FTP@RBCM also increases O2 self-supply and ROS creation to boost radical storms and generate intense local inflammation to kill the primary tumor. Combining radical therapy with Tim-3 blockage can induce strong antitumor immunity to stop distant tumor growth (Fig. 9c) [229].

Challenges, clinical translation, and future perspectives

Safety and blood compatibility

Erythrocyte-based drug delivery in the context of immunotherapy is still an emerging field, and these approaches have yet to be clinically implemented. However, several safety concerns must still be addressed prior to clinical translation. Potential risks include immunogenicity, complement activation, hemolysis, antibody-mediated clearance, and unintended off-target biological effects, particularly when allogeneic erythrocyte membranes are used. In addition, fabrication procedures and prolonged storage may alter membrane structure and protein composition, potentially affecting hemocompatibility, circulation stability, and therapeutic performance. Storage-associated reductions in membrane proteins involved in immune evasion, such as CD47, may further increase susceptibility to phagocytic clearance [296]. Therefore, comprehensive evaluation of hemocompatibility, immunotoxicity, biodistribution, and long-term biosafety remains essential for the development of safe erythrocyte-based immunotherapies [297].

Manufacturing, scalability, and regulatory challenges

Manufacturing and scalability remain major obstacles for the clinical implementation of erythrocyte-based therapeutic platforms. Current fabrication approaches often rely on laboratory-scale isolation and membrane-processing techniques that may be difficult to standardize for industrial production. Achieving reproducible large-scale manufacturing while minimizing batch-to-batch variability is particularly important, as even small differences in membrane composition or nanoparticle loading efficiency may influence therapeutic efficacy and pharmacokinetic behavior. Standardized protocols for erythrocyte isolation, membrane coating, cargo loading, sterilization, and quality control are therefore required alongside compliance with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards. However, erythrocyte-based therapies hold strong translational potential, as they are readily available through blood banks, facilitating their transition into clinical applications [84].

The selection of erythrocyte source material also presents practical challenges. Although autologous erythrocytes offer superior immune compatibility, individualized manufacturing may limit scalability and increase production costs. Conversely, stored donor-derived erythrocytes may improve accessibility through existing blood-bank infrastructure but require careful assessment of storage-related biochemical and mechanical alterations. Additional challenges include optimization of cryopreservation methods, maintenance of membrane stability during storage, and development of sterilization procedures that preserve membrane functionality while ensuring product safety. Furthermore, regulatory approval pathways for erythrocyte-based therapeutics remain complex and will require rigorous characterization, biosafety validation, and manufacturing reproducibility before widespread clinical adoption can be achieved [297]. Clinical translation considerations

Analysis of discontinued or terminated clinical investigations involving erythrocyte-based therapeutic platforms provides important insights for future trial design. Variability in erythrocyte source material and insufficient characterization of pharmacokinetics, biodistribution, immune activation, and clearance kinetics may contribute to inconsistent therapeutic outcomes. Incorporation of pharmacodynamic biomarkers and real-time tracking strategies during early-phase studies could therefore improve understanding of in vivo behavior and treatment response. In addition, patient-specific factors such as inflammatory status, hematologic disorders, and prior transfusion history may influence therapeutic performance and should be considered in patient stratification and eligibility criteria.

Future perspectives

Despite their promising potential for clinical translation [298], erythrocyte-based immunotherapies require further in-depth studies to fully characterize their mechanisms and optimize their impact on the immune system. One prospective approach is the integration of molecular imaging techniques, including PET/SPECT, MRI, and fluorescence-based cell tracking, which can facilitate real-time monitoring of immune responses and therapeutic efficacy. In parallel, immune and inflammatory biomarkers such as cytokine signatures (IFN-γ, IL-6, TNF-α), T-cell activation markers (CD69, CD25), macrophage polarization profiles, and systemic inflammatory markers could provide complementary information regarding immune activation and treatment response [299]. This combination of imaging-based tracking with biomarkers will not only enhance our understanding of the underlying mechanisms but also aid in the development of more effective and safer erythrocyte-based immunotherapies and accelerate the clinical translation of erythrocyte-based approaches [116,300,301].

Another emerging direction is the potential integration of erythrocyte-based delivery systems with gene-editing technologies such as CRISPR/Cas platforms for in vivo immune-cell engineering. Owing to their biocompatibility, prolonged circulation time, and low immunogenicity, erythrocyte-derived carriers and extracellular vesicles may serve as promising vehicles for the delivery of CRISPR-associated ribonucleoproteins, guide RNAs, or other gene-editing payloads to immune cells. This strategy could enable targeted modulation of immune-cell responses and enhance the precision of erythrocyte-based immunotherapies. Nevertheless, several challenges remain to be addressed, including efficient cargo encapsulation, selective targeting of immune-cell subsets, maintenance of editing efficiency in vivo, and reduction of off-target effects and unintended immunogenicity [302,303]. Further studies are therefore required to evaluate the safety, scalability, and translational feasibility of combining erythrocyte-based platforms with genome-editing technologies.

Conclusion

Delivery systems involving erythrocytes have emerged as a promising tool in cancer immunotherapy with advantages such as prolonged circulation, biocompatibility, immune evasion, and improved targeting capacity. Through the exploitation of the intrinsic characteristics of erythrocytes, biomimetic carriers can optimize the delivery of tumor antigens, immune checkpoint blockers, and other therapeutic agents to strengthen the cancer-immunity cycle. In addition, erythrocyte-based platforms have shown promise in antigen presentation, TME regulation, and immunostimulation through immune cell infiltration, making them highly valuable tools in localized as well as systemic antitumor immune responses.

Despite these advantages, some issues still need to be addressed. Issues like mass production, immune clearance, cross-species heterogeneity, and specific targeting must be overcome in order to achieve their maximal clinical utility. Moreover, the integration of erythrocyte-based systems with state-of-the-art nanotechnology, gene editing, and personalized medicine approaches could further augment their therapeutic potential.

Future research should focus on improving erythrocyte engineering techniques, optimizing stability and targeting efficiency, and conducting more rigorous preclinical and clinical trials to validate their efficacy and safety. With continued advancement, erythrocyte-based immunotherapy holds extremely high potential for transforming cancer treatment, offering a safer and more effective alternative to current standards.
