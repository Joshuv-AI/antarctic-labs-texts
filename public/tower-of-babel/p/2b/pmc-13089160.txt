Significance of SLC1A5 expression in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma: A bioinformatics and in-vitro analysis
Authors: S Kaarunya, Palati Sinduja, Monal Yuwanati, Senthilmurugan Mullainathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHead and Neck Squamous Cell Carcinoma (HNSCC) is associated with high mortality due to tumor proliferation and metastasis. Altered function of metabolic transporters such as glutamine transporter SLC1A5 in tumor cells and their microenvironment plays a crucial role in proliferation and metastasis. The aim of this study was to assess the expression of SLC1A5 in HNSCC and evaluate its prognostic significance using computational analysis.Materials and methodsSLC1A5 expression in tumor and normal tissue was analyzed followed by computational prognosis analysis in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-HNSCC cohort. Survival analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier analysis. Differences between the groups were statistically evaluated using the log-rank test, and hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated. SLC1A5 expression was validated on separate HNSCC tumor and normal tissue samples using qPCR.ResultsDifferential expression studies showed that SLC1A5 was significantly overexpressed in HNSCC tumors as compared to normal tissues in TCGA cohort, which was confirmed with qPCR analysis in separate HNSCC cohort (p < 0.05). High SLC1A5 was associated with low overall survival in TCGA cohort. While STRING analysis does not confer great significance to prognosis, it accentuates SLC1A5 as a hub in amino acid metabolism and tumor progression pathways in HNSC.ConclusionThe overexpression of SLC1A5 is linked with unfavorable survival in HNSCC, highlighting its value to the metabolic reprogramming in HNSCC.Keywords: SLC1A5, Head and neck cancer, Glutamine metabolism, Prognostic marker, Epigenetics, Neoplasm

Introduction

Head and Neck Squamous Cell Carcinomas (HNSCC) have significant mortality and part of sustainable developmental goal by World Health Organization. This is mostly attributed to local and regional recurrence and metastasis.1,2 One of the major factors contributing to HSNC recurrence is the harboring of genomic instability or mutations in adjacent, clinically normal-appearing mucosa.3 On the other hand, distant metastasis is due to the spread of cancer cells through the hematogenous.4 Although the prognosis of HNSCC patients can be limited by local disease control with the help of extensive chemo-radiotherapy and radical surgery,5 identifying or predicting distant metastasis is challenging.6

HNSCC mainly arises from the mucosal surfaces of the oral mucosa. Oral mucosa is exposed to several carcinogenic agents in the form of tobacco, areca-nut, and betel leaves.7,8 The long-term exposure incorporates genetic aberration, making the oral mucosa prone to HNSCC.9 Further, few OSCC patients shows rapid proliferation and metastasis in short period of time.10 Understanding these genetic aberrations is crucial to predicting distant metastasis or recurrence.10,11 However, recent genomic studies have greatly improved the understanding of molecular mechanism involved in development of HNSCC.12 These molecular mechanisms involve somatic mutations, changes in copy number, and epigenetic modifications implicated in proliferation and metastasis.13

Cell metabolism is key event in rapid proliferation of cancer cells.14, 15, 16 It is usually controlled by cell wall transporters. One of cell transporters member is solute carrier (SLC) which has gained importance for its role in cell metabolism by facilitating the update of glucose and other nutrients essential for proliferation and sustained cell growth.17 Further, this enables cancer cells to maintain metabolic balance by ensuring nutrient influx and regulating intracellular signaling pathways that drive cell proliferation and survival.16,18 Dysregulation of SLC transporters leads to increased cancer cell proliferation, survival, and invasion.19 In addition, it can lead to resistance to standard chemoradiotherapy regimens. These traits make SLC transporters appealing targets for specific therapy.19 Interestingly, several members of the SLC superfamily are often expressed at the cell surface. However, SLCs have expressed abnormally in HNSCC and are closely correlated with enhanced invasive capacity, apoptotic escape, organotropic metastatic colonization, and cellular metabolic remodeling.20 There is a growing interest in the role of SLC transporters in HSNCC. However, the expression profiles and functions of specific SLC transporters in HNSCC studied limited. Unlike other solid tumors, such as breast, colorectal, and pancreatic cancers, the involvement of SLC transporters in the prognosis, therapeutic response, and progression of HNSCC is still largely elusive.21

Among the members of the SLC family, SLC1A5, or Alanine-Serine-Cysteine Transporter 2 (ASCT2), is a transporter protein specifically aiding the absorption of glutamine by cancer cells.22 Glutamine supports DNA production and preserves antioxidant homeostasis, and aids cancer cell adaptation to low-nutrient and low-oxygen conditions.23 SLC1A5 overexpression was reported in lung, breast, and colorectal cancers and correlates with aggressive tumor behavior, resistance to therapy, and mortality.24,25 Researchers have discovered a new form of metabolism that is associated with the SLC1A5 protein. The newer variant of the protein does not function similarly to the older one that sits on the cell membrane.26 It is rather exported into the mitochondria in mitosis with a specific amino acid sequence at the N-terminal of the target protein. One unique feature of this SLC1A5 mitochondrial isoform is that it is activated more in conditions with low oxygen, which are common in extremely aggressive head and neck cancer.27 This activation is at least partially mediated through a protein known as HIF-2α, which helps the cell to sense low oxygen. Therefore, the presence of this SLC1A5 mutation likely helps cancer cells survive and adjust to hypoxic conditions, which are standard in advanced stages of the disease, by optimizing glutamine metabolism in the mitochondria.27,28

Further, the concurrent expression of SLC1A5 with other amino acid transporters, including SLC7A5 (LAT1) and its chaperone subunit SLC3A2, also enhances amino acid exchange and mTORC1 activation in cancer cells.24 These three are some of the most differentially expressed genes in different cancers and activated immune cells, indicating a common metabolic axis that can be targeted by tumors for proliferative and survival benefit. Collectively, current evidence strongly supports that SLC1A5 plays a central role in mediating metabolic plasticity and oncogenesis in many types of cancer.29 However, their specific functions, expression patterns, and prognostic significance in HNSCC have yet to be established. A detailed analysis of the functional significance of SLC1A5 in HNSCC may give new insight into the SLC1A5 mechanism in carcinogenesis and introduce a new target for precision oncology strategies. The present study investigated the expression profile and functional significance of SLC1A5 in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA)-HNSCC cohort, and validated with OSCC patients.

Materials and methods

Clinical sample collection and ethics approval

Fresh tumor specimens (n = 15) and adjacent non-tumor mucosal tissues (n = 10) were collected from patients undergoing surgical resection for primary HNSCC after obtaining the patient's consent. Inclusion criteria comprised treatment-naive patients with histologically confirmed HNSCC. Patients with recurrent disease, prior therapy, systemic disorders, co-morbidities, or non-squamous malignancies were excluded. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Tissue samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C for subsequent molecular analyses. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Institutional Human Ethical Committee. (IHEC/SDC/UG-2373/25/GPATH/118).

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from both tumor and adjacent normal tissues using TRIzol® reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer's protocol. RNA quantity and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and integrity was confirmed by gel electrophoresis. One microgram of high-quality RNA was reverse transcribed to complementary DNA (cDNA) using the Takara First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Takara Bio Inc., Japan). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using the Bio-Rad CFX Opus 96 real-time system and SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara, Japan). GAPDH served as the endogenous reference gene. Each reaction was run in triplicate under the following thermal cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^-ΔΔCt method.

Gene expression profiling from TCGA data

To evaluate SLC1A5 expression across cancer types, transcriptomic data were obtained from TCGA using the TIMER 2.0 platform (http://timer.cistrome.org/). The mRNA expression levels of SLC1A5 in HNSCC were further analyzed using the UALCAN database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/), which stratifies samples based on clinical and pathological parameters (age, gender, Tumor grade, Nodal status, stage).

Survival analysis

Association of SLC1A5 expression and survival in patients with HNSCC was analyzed using the Kaplan–Meier Plotter platform (https://kmplot.com/analysis/). HNSCC patients’ data were divided into high and low SLC1A5 expression groups based on an optimal threshold established. Overall survival (OS), along Hazard ratios (HR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) with using the log-rank test were estimate the relative risk associated with SLC1A5 expression.

Tumor vs. normal and metastatic comparisons

Differences in SLC1A5 mRNA expression levels among tumor, normal, and metastatic tissues was performed using the TNMplot database (https://tnmplot.com/analysis/). This enabled direct assessment of expression differences across both paired and unpaired datasets integrated from TCGA and the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) project.

Immune and functional enrichment analysis

To investigate the immunological context of SLC1A5 expression, immune regulatory correlations were analyzed through TIMER 2.0 (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/). The analysis utilized the TCGA-HNSCC dataset to examine relationships between SLC1A5 expression and tumor-infiltrating immune cell populations, immunomodulatory factors, and immune subtypes, to determine the potential roles in tumor immune evasion and microenvironment modulation. Also, multivariate Cox proportional hazards survival model analysis for immune infiltrates, clinical factors (age, gender, ethnicity, tumor stages) and SLC1A5 was carried out.

Protein–protein interaction network and pathway analysis

The protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of SLC1A5 was generated through the STRING database (https://string-db.org/) identifying interacting and functionally related proteins. To further understand the biological significance of these interactions, enrichment analyses were conducted using Metascape (https://metascape.org/), integrating Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway data.

Protein expression mapping

To validate SLC1A5 expression at the protein level, immunohistochemistry (IHC) data were obtained from The Human Protein Atlas (https://www.proteinatlas.org/). These enabled visualization of tissue-specific expression patterns and protein localization within HNSCC tissue specimens.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (https://www.graphpad.com/). For comparing SLC1A5 expression between tumor and adjacent normal tissues, paired or unpaired Student's t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were applied as appropriate. Correlations between SLC1A5 expression and immune parameters/clinicopathological features were evaluated using Pearson's/Spearman's correlation coefficients. Survival curves were analyzed using the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test. A two-sided p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

Results

SLC1A5 is significantly upregulated in HNSCC tumor tissues

To confirm the transcriptional overexpression of SLC1A5 seen in TCGA datasets, the study performed quantitative RT-PCR to measure levels of SLC1A5 mRNA in clinical samples of HNSC. SLC1A5 was found to be much more abundant in tumor tissues (n = 15) than in normal tissues (n = 10) (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1(A)). The mean relative RNA level in SLC1A5-positive tumors is approximately twice that of the corresponding nontumor counterparts, confirming its high transcriptional activity in malignant tissue.

A) Significant upregulation of SLC1A5 mRNA expression is observed in OSCC tissues compared to normal tissues. (p < 0.01, t-test). B) Comparison of SLC1A5 mRNA expression levels between primary OSCCs and matched normal tissues. A significantly higher SLC1A5 expression is detected in OSCCs than in matched normal tissues (p < 0.01, t-test).

Paired analysis confirms tumor-specific upregulation of SLC1A5

To further control for inter-individual variability, we conducted a paired analysis using matched tumor and adjacent normal tissue samples from the same patients (n = 10). The analysis showed that SLC1A5 mRNA levels were consistently higher in tumor samples compared to their normal tissue counterparts across all matched pairs (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1(B)). This uniform pattern of upregulation further supports that SLC1A5 overexpression is a consistent molecular feature of HNSCC tumors rather than a result of patient-specific background variation.

Correlation of immune infiltrate and SLC1A5

Multivariate Cox-regression analysis of 389 HNSCC patients for immune infiltrate and SLC1A5 adjusted for covariate showed that age and stage IV disease were the most significant predictors of poor survival, with older age modestly increasing risk and stage IV conferring nearly a three-fold higher hazard. In contrast, SLC1A5 expression demonstrated a non-significant association with increased hazard. Immune infiltration by B cells, CD8+ and CD4+ T cells showed protective trends, however, SLC1A5 expression did not influence overall survival (log-rank p = 0.926) (Fig. 2). Differential expression of SLC1A5 in HNSCC

The gene expression data 520 HNSCC and 44 normal tissues from the TCGA database were included in the study. To investigate the transcriptional signature of HNSCC, differential expression analysis of tumor and corresponding adjacent normal tissues was performed. As illustrated by the heatmap (Fig. 3(A)), a few keratin family genes (e.g., KRT6B, KRT16, KRT6C, KRT14, and IVL) and S100 family genes (e.g., S100A7, S100A8, S100A9) exhibited robust upregulation in the tumor tissue relative to normal tissues, indicative of increased epithelial proliferation and stress reactions characteristic of squamous differentiation. Differential expression analysis of the TCGA-HNSC dataset revealed heterogeneous expression patterns of SLC1A5 across tumor samples when compared with normal tissues. While certain analyses suggested relatively lower expression in the overall TCGA cohort, stratified analysis focusing on oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) demonstrated increased expression levels. Importantly, qRT-PCR validation performed on independent OSCC clinical samples confirmed significant upregulation of SLC1A5 (p < 0.05). These findings highlight the biological heterogeneity of HNSCC across anatomical sites. The global expression patterns also supported a distinct transcriptional transition from benign to cancer tissues, suggesting a coordinated dysregulation of epithelial integrity, immune signaling, and metabolic control genes during the development of HNSCC.

A) Gene expression pattern of selected genes (including SLC1A5) in normal vs. tumor HNSCC tissues. B) Correlation between SLC1A5 and KRT6B gene expression in HNSCC. C) Kaplan–Meier plot- Overall survival curve based on SLC1A5 expression levels.

To investigate the interplay among the differentially expressed genes, we examined the association between SLC1A5 and KRT6B, a keratin family member known to be upregulated in tumor tissues. The correlation analysis revealed a negative association between their expression levels in HNSCC (r −0.46, Fig. 3 (B)). This suggests a possible antagonistic interaction between SLC1A5-mediated metabolic reprogramming and keratinocyte differentiation pathways in HNSCC. In turn, the inverse relationship shows that the suppression of glutamine transport (due to low SLC1A5 expression possibly) may be linked with the induction of pathways regulating epithelial differentiation markers, such as KRT6B, in the tumor microenvironment.

Prognostic relevance of SLC1A5 expression in HNSC

Kaplan-Meier survival analysis (Fig. 3(C)) was performed after stratifying HNSCC patients into high and low expression of SLC1A5 to evaluate the clinical significance of SLC1A5 expression. While patients with high SLC1A5 had lower overall survival, the difference did not achieve statistical significance (Hazard Ratio: 1.18 [95% CI: 0.87-1.61], log-rank p = 0.28). These findings suggest that while downregulation of SLC1A5 stands out in the tumor biology of HNSCC malignancies, it may not serve as a strong independent prognostic marker in this cohort. Other integration studies based on multi-omic data or functional validation may disclose its contextual relevance in tumor progression and therapy response.

Pan-cancer expression profiling of SLC1A5 within TCGA tumors

To assess the correlation of SLC1A5 expression in various cancers, RNA-seq data of TCGA cohort, including a number of tumor types, was considered. SLC1A5 showed a heterogeneous pattern of expression among Pan-cancer cohort (Fig. 4 (A)). Among these, HNSCC displayed a relatively modest expression of SLC1A5, which signifies its possible metabolic role in this type of tumor. Epithelial cancers had augmented expression of SLC1A5: kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP); kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC); lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD); and prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD). SLC1A5 is highly variable across cancers, reflecting the tissue-dependent metabolic requirements and the wide-ranging functions of glutamine transport during tumorigenesis. The fact that these types of tumors-for instance, renal and prostate-are metabolically active and uniformly over-express SLC1A5 suggests a pro-oncogenic role in the emission of glutamine-dependent biosynthesis.

A) Comparison between all TCGA tumor types (paired and unpaired samples). B) Expression of SLC1A5 in different TCGA cancers (unpaired samples).

Differential expression of SLC1A5 in tumor and normal tissues

Differential expression of SLC1A5 expression was compared using profiles of matched tumor and normal tissues (unpaired) across cancers (Fig. 4 (B)). There was a differential upregulation of SLC1A5 in several tumors in comparison with their normal counterparts, including the KIRP, KIRC, LIHC, LUAD, and PRAD. In contrast, SLC1A5 was notably downregulated by tumor samples in HNSC, reaffirming previous observations and suggesting a different regulatory mechanism in squamous tumors. These differential trends imply that SLC1A5 may have tissue-specific roles, potentially oncogenic in some cancers where it is upregulated, and possibly repressed or functionally redundant in others, like HNSCC. The observed downregulation in HNSCC could also indicate a shift away from glutamine dependency, possibly compensated by alternative metabolic pathways.

SLC1A5 expression is driven higher in HNSCC tumors as compared to normal tissue

To explore transcriptional regulation of SLC1A5 in HNSCC, we compared expression in primary tumor tissues (n = 520) with that in adjacent normal tissues (n = 44). SLC1A5 expression was found to be significantly elevated in tumor samples (p < 0.05), median transcript levels, nearly doubling those observed for normal tissue, suggest a putative tumor-promoting role for SLC1A5 in HNSCC (Fig. 5 (A)).

A) Expression level of SLC1A5 in tumor vs. normal tissue samples (TCGA data). B) Promoter methylation level of SLC1A5 in HNSCC (TCGA dataset).

Promoter hypomethylation marks upregulation of SLC1A5 in HNSCC

The methylation status of the SLC1A5 promoter was assessed demonstrating in HNSCC tumors relative to normal tissues. The reduction in promoter methylation in tumor tissues compared with normal tissues (528) (p < 0.05) was observed (Fig. 5 (B)). These observations point toward epigenetic deregulation, such as promoter hypomethylation contributing to the aberrant overexpression of SLC1A5 in HNSCC. This might help in increased glutamine uptake and metabolic reprogramming of tumors.

Protein- protein interaction analysis

Protein–protein interaction analysis using the STRING database revealed a distinct interaction network centered around SLC1A5, highlighting its strong functional associations with several key transporters and enzymes involved in amino acid metabolism and cellular nutrient sensing (Fig. 6). These activities of SLC1A5 with SLC7A5, SLC3A2, GLS, and GLS2 appear very coordinated within glutamine transport and glutaminolysis pathways. Additional interacting partners, SLC6A14, SLC38A2, and SLC6A5, provide evidence for further positioning SLC1A5 within the realm of general amino acid transport. Connections with SLC2A1 (the glucose transporter) and some endogenous retroviral envelope proteins such as ERVW-1 and ERVFRD-1 give hints of possible cross-talk between metabolic regulation and immunomodulation.30 SLC1A5 may represent an important metabolic hub in HNSCC and could potentially influence interactions between tumor metabolism and the immune microenvironment.

Discussion

SLC1A5 is a sodium-dependent, high-affinity glutamine transporter that plays a key role in the metabolic changes of cancer cells. In present study, we found that it is consistently overexpressed in tissues from head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) compared to nearby normal epithelium. Our findings, along with the in silico analysis of TCGA datasets and validated in clinical OSCC samples via RT-qPCR, suggest that SLC1A5 expression is a molecular feature of HNSC and may contribute to tumor progression through metabolic, epigenetic, and immune regulatory mechanisms.

This result is in agreement with earlier findings in breast, renal, and other solid cancers.31 Overexpression of SLC1A5 has, in these cancers, been correlated with increased glutamine uptake, increased rate of tumor growth, and worse clinical progression.32 Our observations in HNSCC also contribute evidence to the concept that this transport is essential to meet the hijacked metabolic needs of aggressive tumors through mechanisms based on glutamine. Elevated levels of SLC1A5 are associated with increased cellular uptake of nutrients, supporting essential cancer processes such as rapid proliferation, tissue infiltration, and enhanced migratory potential.25 Studies have shown that suppress SLC1A5 function can trigger cell death across multiple cancer types.33,34 Similar results observed in HNSC models showing that this transporter is required for maintaining tumor cell viability. SLC1A5 changes the tumor immune microenvironment. Previous studies show that higher SLC1A5 expression is linked to decreased cytotoxic T-cell activity and a rise in immunosuppressive cell populations, suggesting that it contributes to immune evasion, that facilitate tumor endurance and continued growth.21

Evidence from different tumor models reveal that promoter hypomethylation can decrease the transcriptional repression, leading to SLC1A5 overexpression.35 In HNSCC samples, we observed a mild yet consistent decreased in SLC1A5 methylation levels, thus the epigenetic dysregulation contributes to its upregulation. This observation aligns with earlier studies showing the significance of molecular changes in head and neck cancers. The NEAT1/miR-125b-5p/SLC1A5 pathway, a regulatory pathway that has been involved in epithelial malignancies, and has a similar role in HNSCC, influencing both immune resistance and therapeutic response.

Multi-omics profiling provided further support for these findings, revealing SLC1A5 co-expression with SLC7A5, LAMC2, and MMP9, which are involved in amino acid transport, extracellular matrix remodeling, and invasiveness.24,36 Heatmap and expression plot analyses showed the variability and distribution of SLC1A5 among patient cohorts, supporting its role in HNSCC growth and progression. Although SLC1A5 expression was elevated in tumor tissues, it did not demonstrate independent prognostic significance in the present analysis. Therefore, while SLC1A5 may contribute to metabolic reprogramming in HNSC, its utility as a standalone prognostic biomarker remains limited.

To our knowledge, limited studies examined relation to SLC1A5 regulation in HNSCC. Both SLC1A5 and KRT6B were found to be higher in tumors compared to normal tissues. But its expression was inversely correlated in tumor samples. KRT6B is known to be associated with epithelial stress responses and hyperproliferative epithelial states rather than classical epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) signaling pathways.37 KRT6B is known to be associated with epithelial stress responses and hyperproliferative epithelial states rather than classical epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) signaling pathways. The inverse relationship observed in this study may therefore reflect differences between metabolic activity and epithelial differentiation states within the tumor microenvironment.24,25 Despite the strength of combining large-scale bioinformatics with experimental validation, our work also has certain constraints. In the first place, the clinical validation set was relatively small and restricted to OSCC patients. Second, protein level validation by immunohistochemistry or Western blotting was not performed, which would provide further support for translational relevance.

The correlation between SLC1A5 expression and immune cell infiltration observed suggests a potential interaction between tumor metabolic pathways and the immune microenvironment. As SLC1A5 functions as a key glutamine transporter, increased expression may influence metabolic competition for glutamine within the tumor microenvironment, which has effect on both tumor cells and infiltrating immune cells. However, the present study did not directly evaluate metabolic competition mechanisms or functional immune modulation. Furthermore, HPV status, an important determinant of immune response and metabolic behavior in head and neck cancers was not stratified in the current analysis. Therefore, additional studies incorporating HPV-stratified cohorts and functional metabolic assays are necessary to clarify the precise role of SLC1A5 in tumor–immune interactions in HNSC.

This study has several limitations. First, the bioinformatics analysis relied primarily on publicly available datasets such as TCGA, which may introduce inherent dataset bias. The discrepancy between the TCGA-HNSC dataset and the OSCC clinical samples may reflect tumor heterogeneity across different anatomical sites of head and neck cancers. Variations in HPV status, metabolic dependence on glutamine transporters, and tumor microenvironmental factors may contribute to differences in SLC1A5 expression across HNSC subtypes. Second, the clinical validation cohort was relatively limited in size. Third, functional experiments such as gene knockdown or overexpression assays were not performed to confirm the mechanistic role of SLC1A5 in tumor progression. Additionally, HPV-stratified analysis was not performed due to dataset limitations. Finally, survival analysis did not demonstrate significant prognostic value for SLC1A5, which should be considered when interpreting the clinical relevance of the findings. The present study integrates multi-database bioinformatics analysis with experimental validation using qRT-PCR in clinical OSCC samples. Additionally, immune infiltration analysis and protein–protein interaction network analysis were incorporated to provide a broader understanding of the potential biological role of SLC1A5 in HNSCC. This integrative approach improves the reliability of the findings and provides a comprehensive evaluation of SLC1A5 expression patterns. Due to SLC1A5 localization on the cell surface and essential role in glutamine transport, SLC1A5 has garnered increasing attention as both a diagnostic biomarker and a potential therapeutic target. SLC1A5 can be used as a promising candidate for targeted therapy, particularly when used in combination with immunotherapy or radiotherapy.

Conclusion

The present study shows that SLC1A5 is upregulated in HNSCC and is linked with altered tumor metabolism and immune modulation. The upregulation of SLC1A5 expression may be influenced from epigenetic alterations, including variations in DNA methylation patterns. Its association with pathways related to immune escape and extracellular matrix remodeling suggests that SLC1A5 facilitates the aggressive behavior of HNSCC. Although a direct relationship with patient survival was not observed, the evidence suggests that SLC1A5 plays an important role in tumor development and may be used as a promising biomarker and therapeutic target in future therapeutics.
