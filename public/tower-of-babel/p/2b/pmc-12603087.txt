DAF-16/FOXO and HLH-30/TFEB comprise a cooperative regulatory axis controlling tubular lysosome induction in C. elegans
Authors: Cristian Ricaurte-Perez, Joshua P. Gill, P. Kerr Wall, Olga Dubuisson, Kathryn R. DeLeo, K. Adam Bohnert, Alyssa E. Johnson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Transcription factors DAF-16/FOXO and HLH-30/TFEB have been linked to aging regulation, but how they synergize to promote longevity is not fully understood. Here, we reveal a functional interaction between these two transcription factors that supports healthier aging in Caenorhabditis elegans. Namely, DAF-16 and HLH-30 cooperate to trigger robust lysosomal tubulation under various contexts, which contributes to systemic health benefits in late age. Remarkably, lysosome tubulation can be artificially induced via overexpression of a small lysosomal gene, dSVIP, in the absence of one transcription factor, but not both. Mechanistically, intestinal overexpression of dSVIP leads to nuclear accumulation of DAF-16 and HLH-30 in gut and non-gut tissues and triggers global gene expression changes, including induction of vps-34 and related lipid-metabolism genes, that promote tubular-lysosome activity. Collectively, our work reveals a cellular process under control of DAF-16 and HLH-30 that elicits pro-health effects in aging.

Introduction

The increase in life expectancy during the last century is a remarkable achievement of modern civilization. Indeed, during an interval of 73 years, from 1950 to 2023, the life expectancy at birth in the United States increased from 68.14 to 79.11 years1. This has led to a growing elderly population, with the number of individuals over age 64 now exceeding the number of children under the age of five2. However, despite extended lifespans, the incidence of age-related degenerative diseases persists and may even be on the rise, indicating that individuals are not experiencing improved health in later years3. In response to the growing disparity between lifespan and healthspan, pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions aimed at improving late-age health have been tested. Among the non-pharmacological interventions, dietary restriction (DR) has been extensively studied during the last few decades, as it extends lifespan, attenuates functional decline, and delays chronic diseases across a broad variety of species4.

Although there is ample evidence supporting the beneficial impacts of DR, little is known about the cellular mechanisms underlying DR-dependent lifespan extension and healthy aging. Despite these shortcomings, the autophagy/lysosome system has been recognized as one pivotal mechanism required for the beneficial effects induced by DR; inhibiting autophagy negates the anti-aging effects of DR and abolishes lifespan extension in multiple species5. In addition to DR, several other longevity pathways converge onto autophagy6. Thus, autophagy functions as a unifying mechanism for cellular homeostasis maintenance and can facilitate cell autonomous7,8 and cell nonautonomous effects7,9 to promote longevity. Similarly, proper functionality of the lysosome, the major digestive organelle that disposes and recycles autophagic cargo, is necessary to extend the lifespan of dietary-restricted animals10. Therefore, mechanisms that boost autophagy and/or lysosome function could lead to treatments that slow or reverse age-related diseases.

Lysosomes, usually depicted as spherical-shaped structures, are sophisticated organelles that play critical roles in maintaining cellular homeostasis. Although they were originally thought to function solely in the breakdown of cellular waste, it is now clear that they also play a crucial role in regulating cell metabolism and signaling11. Moreover, lysosomes exhibit a high degree of morphological plasticity; vesicular lysosomes can undergo transformation into a tubular network that facilitates processes like antigen presentation12, cuticle remodeling13, and autophagosome-lysosome fusion14–16. In previous work, our lab found that tubular lysosome (TL) formation in the gut is necessary to extend the lifespan of C. elegans under DR conditions17. Moreover, experimentally stimulating TLs constitutively in the gut of well-fed wild-type animals is sufficient to mimic some effects of DR and promotes healthier aging17,18. Significantly, ectopic induction of TLs can suppress partial defects in earlier steps of autophagy17, suggesting that expansion of the lysosomal compartment into a tubular network can boost clearance of molecular waste when earlier steps of autophagy are sub-optimal. Taken together, these data suggest that TLs could represent a potential entry point for devising starvation mimetics.

Although we have gained a significant appreciation for the importance of TLs in regulating various aspects of animal physiology19, less is known about the molecular factors that regulate TL formation. Here, we find that the transcription factors DAF-16 and HLH-30, the C. elegans orthologs of mammalian FOXO (Forkhead box protein O) and TFEB (Transcription Factor EB), respectively, work in concert to drive robust lysosome tubulation under DR and natural aging conditions. Moreover, we report that TLs can be constitutively stimulated in the gut of well-fed daf-16 or hlh-30 mutants by overexpressing Drosophila small VCP interacting protein (dSVIP). Our evidence suggests that dSVIP induces TL formation in part by promoting nuclear localization of both DAF-16 and HLH-30, and, if one of these two transcription factors is absent, the other is still sufficient to support dSVIP-dependent TL induction. Remarkably, precocious TL induction in the gut reduces cellular hallmarks of aging and promotes late-age health in short-lived daf-16 and hlh-30 mutants, underscoring the anti-aging properties of TLs. Mechanistically, dSVIP overexpression in the gut stimulates pro-longevity transcription factor activity across multiple tissues, triggering global gene expression changes that facilitate systemic health improvements. mRNA-seq analysis revealed an enrichment in genes related to lysosome biogenesis, metabolic pathways, phosphatidylinositol signaling, and protein processing in the ER that were upregulated upon dSVIP overexpression and likely contribute to various aspects of TL biology. To verify the involvement of candidate upregulated genes, we performed a large-scale RNAi-based screen of over 500 candidate genes, which revealed a novel role for VPS-34, a class III phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI3K), and a cognate co-factor BEC-1, in TL induction. Inhibition of either vps-34 or bec-1 blocked DR-, aging- and SVIP-dependent TL induction and VPS-34 localizes to tubular structures in the intestine upon starvation. Collectively, our results suggest that a DAF-16 and HLH-30 regulatory axis controls TL formation under different conditions and further underscore SVIP as a potential interventional target for anti-aging therapies.

Results

DAF-16 and HLH-30 are critical for TL induction in the C. elegans gut

Although lysosomes have been canonically described as spherical-shaped organelles20, we and others have shown that vesicular lysosomes can morph into degradative tubular networks under certain stimuli, including DR12,15,17,21–24. Notably, we found that autophagic TLs are robustly stimulated in the gut of dietary-restricted C. elegans and are required to elicit the full beneficial effects of DR17. Because TLs are triggered under conditions of food limitation, we examined a potential role for DAF-16/FOXO and HLH-30/TFEB, two transcription factors known to contribute to the health benefits of DR25,26, in triggering TLs. To visualize lysosomes in their most natural context, we imaged a previously characterized fluorescent marker that has an mCherry tag incorporated at the endogenous C-terminus of the lysosomal membrane protein Spinster/SPIN-1 (SPIN-1::mCherry)17. Remarkably, mutation of daf-16 completely blocked TL induction in starved L1 larval animals (Fig. 1a, b). Although mutation of hlh-30 did not completely block TL formation in starved animals, TLs were significantly reduced compared to starved wild-type animals, suggesting a minor role for hlh-30 in starvation-dependent TL induction (Fig. 1a, b). We further examined TLs in a double daf-16; hlh-30 mutant, and, not surprisingly, TLs were completely blocked from forming like in the daf-16 single mutant (Fig. 1a, b). We then examined TL induction in dietary-restricted worms using eat-2 mutants, which provide a genetic model for dietary restriction (DR)27. Like our results with starvation-dependent TLs, loss of daf-16 had the most profound negative effect on DR-dependent TL induction but eat-2; hlh-30 mutants also demonstrated reduced TL induction compared to eat-2 mutants retaining hlh-30 function (Fig. 1c, d). Again, mutation of both daf-16 and hlh-30 completely blocked TL induction in eat-2 worms (Fig. 1c, d). Previously, we and others found that TLs are also naturally stimulated as adult C. elegans age17,28; thus, we imaged lysosomes in well-fed wild-type, daf-16, hlh-30, and daf-16; hlh-30 mutants throughout adulthood. As previously reported, we found that TLs were robustly triggered beginning at day 5 of adulthood (Fig. 1e, f). In contrast, daf-16 mutation blocked TL formation at all ages (Fig. 1e, f). In hlh-30 mutants, TLs were completely blocked at day 5 of adulthood, and severely impaired at day 10 of adulthood (Fig. 1e, f). Simultaneous mutation of both daf-16 and hlh-30 also blocked TL induction at all ages (Fig. 1e, f). Collectively, these data indicate that DAF-16/FOXO and HLH-30/TFEB are required to induce robust TL formation during DR and aging.

Overexpression of daf-16 or hlh-30 triggers TLs constitutively

Given that overexpression of either daf-16 or hlh-30 promotes longevity26,29, we next explored whether overexpression of either transcription factor would be sufficient to drive the morphological transformation of lysosomes from vesicles to tubules. We overexpressed daf-16 in the gut and visualized lysosomes using endogenous SPIN-1::mCherry. Indeed, intestinal overexpression of daf-16 from the strong gut-specific promoter, Pges-130, was sufficient to induce gut TLs in young (day 1) well-fed adults (Fig. 2a, b), which normally do not show TLs (Fig. 2a, b). Significantly, in wild-type C. elegans, daf-16 expression increases with age and peaks around day 6 of adulthood31, coincident with TL induction in mid-age (Fig. 1e, f)17. Thus, increased daf-16 expression during aging might trigger age-dependent TL induction. Perhaps counterintuitively, we and others have found that TLs are not stimulated in young adulthood by mutation of daf-2 (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b, ref. 28), which likewise elevates daf-16 activity by a different mechanism. We note, however, that TLs can be stimulated in aged daf-2 mutants (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b and ref. 28), and, importantly, further increasing daf-16 expression by transgenic means stimulated TLs constitutively in young daf-2 mutants (Supplementary Fig. 1c, d). Thus, daf-2 mutants are permissive to TL induction, but the level of daf-16 activity in daf-2 mutants may simply not be enough to constitutively induce TLs, as upon daf-16 gut overexpression. Another reason why daf-2 mutants may show dampened TL activation under baseline conditions is that they exhibit reduced proteotoxic stress (Supplementary Fig. 1e, f, Supplementary Fig. 1g, h, and ref. 32), a known trigger for TL induction17. Similar to daf-16 overexpression, intestinal hlh-30 overexpression also triggered TL formation in young well-fed adults (Fig. 2a, b). These data are consistent with our previous findings that inhibition of mTOR signaling, a known trigger for HLH-30 activation33, also stimulates TL formation in young well-fed animals17. Collectively, these results indicate that experimental overexpression of either hlh-30 or daf-16 is sufficient to stimulate TL induction in the C. elegans gut.

DAF-16 and HLH-30 have cooperative and sometimes compensatory functions34. Therefore, we were curious whether increased expression of either transcription factor could trigger TL induction in the absence of the other. Remarkably, increased expression of hlh-30 triggered robust lysosome tubulation in young well-fed daf-16 mutants, which are typically devoid of lysosome tubules (Fig. 2a, b). Similarly, increased expression of daf-16 in hlh-30 mutants also triggered robust lysosome tubulation in young well-fed animals (Fig. 2a, b). These data indicate that increased expression of either transcription factor can trigger robust lysosome tubulation in the absence of the other and establishes that DAF-16 and HLH-30 are each strong TL stimulators.

TLs are cellular determinants of daf-16-dependent longevity

Mutations in daf-16 shorten the lifespan of C. elegans35, and intestinal expression of daf-16 seems to be most critical for its lifespan effects36. Given that daf-16 overexpression is sufficient to trigger TL induction in wild-type animals and that ectopic TL induction in the gut alone can promote healthier aging17, we next explored whether TLs are important for the gut-specific actions of DAF-16 in longevity. First, we verified that gut-specific daf-16 overexpression was indeed sufficient to drive TL formation constitutively in daf-16 mutants (Fig. 2c, d). We also verified that gut-specific daf-16 overexpression restored daf-16 mutant lifespan back to near wild-type levels (Fig. 2e; Supplementary Dataset 2; Supplementary Fig. 2a). Next, we assessed if TL induction was causal in restoring normal lifespan of daf-16 mutants when daf-16 is overexpressed specifically in gut tissues. In past work, we showed that TLs can be genetically blocked by simultaneous mutation of C. elegans spin/Spinster paralogs17. Similarly, we found that combinatorial spin mutation also prevented TL formation in daf-16 mutant animals overexpressing daf-16 exclusively in the gut (Fig. 2c, d). While re-expression of daf-16 in the gut of daf-16 mutants increased lifespan to near wild-type levels, inhibition of TL induction in this genetic background reduced lifespan back to near daf-16 levels (Fig. 2e; Supplementary Dataset 2; Supplementary Fig. 2a).

We next examined if preventing TLs would also abrogate aspects of late-age health improvements seen upon daf-16 re-expression in the gut of daf-16 mutants36. While daf-16 null mutants with daf-16 re-expression in the gut demonstrated improved late-age mobility compared to daf-16 mutants alone, we found no significant improvement to late-age mobility when TL formation was impeded in this context (Fig. 2f; Supplementary Fig. 2b). These data suggest that TL formation is a necessary step for the gut-specific actions of DAF-16 in promoting organismal health and longevity and highlight that TLs contribute to longevity paradigms beyond DR.

dSVIP expression forces TL induction in daf-16 and hlh-30 mutants

In a prior study, we reported that experimental overexpression of Drosophila SVIP (dSVIP), a previously characterized TL stimulator16, induces TLs constitutively when expressed in the C. elegans gut, even under well-fed conditions17. Therefore, we were curious if the TL-inducing properties of dSVIP require daf-16 and/or hlh-30. We examined lysosome morphology in daf-16, hlh-30, and daf-16; hlh-30 mutants under starved and fed conditions, and in the presence or absence of dSVIP overexpression. Remarkably, in both daf-16 and hlh-30 mutants, gut dSVIP overexpression was sufficient to stimulate TLs under both starved and fed conditions (Fig. 3a, b and Fig. 3c, d). Notably, although TLs typically form to an extent in starved hlh-30 mutants (Fig. 1a, b), we found that dSVIP overexpression triggered more robust lysosome tubulation compared to starved hlh-30 mutants without dSVIP overexpression (Fig. 3a, b and Fig. 3c, d). Significantly, dSVIP overexpression did not trigger TL induction in double daf-16; hlh-30 mutants under either starved or fed conditions (Fig. 3a, b and Fig. 3c, d). Previously, we also reported that dSVIP-dependent TL induction requires the activity of VCP, a AAA+ ATPase that is recruited to lysosomes upon dSVIP overexpression and promotes lysosomal membrane fusion17. Thus, we examined whether dSVIP-dependent TL induction in daf-16 mutants was also VCP-dependent. Indeed, feeding worms a chemical inhibitor of VCP activity (CB5083) precluded TL induction in daf-16 mutants overexpressing dSVIP (Fig. 3e, f). Unexpectedly, TLs triggered by natural aging or dietary restriction were not impeded by VCP inhibition (Fig. 3g, h and Fig. 3i, j). These data indicate that VCP is required for dSVIP-specific TL induction but is not required for all modes of TL stimulation. Thus, TLs can be stimulated via multiple mechanisms.

Forced TL induction extends healthspan of daf-16 and hlh-30 mutants

We next examined the physiological ramifications of forced TL induction in daf-16, hlh-30, and daf-16; hlh-30 mutants. We first tested whether artificial TL induction via dSVIP gut overexpression could extend the lifespan of short-lived daf-16 mutants. Despite having strong TL induction, daf-16 mutants overexpressing dSVIP in the gut did not show an improved lifespan relative to daf-16 controls (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Dataset 2; Supplementary Fig. 3a). Similarly, we did not observe lifespan extension when dSVIP was overexpressed in hlh-30 or daf-16; hlh-30 mutants (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Dataset 2; Supplementary Fig. 3a). These data are consistent with our prior observations that dSVIP gut overexpression in wild-type C. elegans likewise does not extend lifespan17 (Fig. 4a; Supplementary Dataset 2; Supplementary Fig. 3a). However, in our previous work, we found that while there was no effect on lifespan, late-age mobility was significantly improved in wild-type animals overexpressing dSVIP in the gut17 (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Fig. 3b). Thus, we wondered whether, despite having no effect on lifespan, dSVIP gut overexpression could extend the healthspan of daf-16, hlh-30, or daf-16; hlh-30 mutants. To evaluate this, we assayed mobility decline throughout life, a strong correlate of healthspan37, in mutant strains with and without dSVIP gut overexpression. Remarkably, daf-16 mutant animals overexpressing dSVIP in the gut demonstrated improved late-age mobility compared to daf-16 mutants without dSVIP overexpression (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Fig. 3b). Likewise, dSVIP gut overexpression also improved the late-age mobility of hlh-30 mutants (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Fig. 3b). However, no improvement to late-age mobility was observed in a double daf-16; hlh-30 mutant overexpressing dSVIP (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Fig. 3b). To further assess the pro-health effects of dSVIP gut overexpression in strains lacking daf-16 and hlh-30 function, we examined autophagic cargo turnover, since proteostasis collapse is a major hallmark of aging. To quantitatively measure autophagic cargo turnover, we imaged a fluorescent autophagy reporter, SQST-1::mCherry::GFP, which we characterized previously17. SQST-1/p62 is an autophagy receptor that marks cargo destined for autophagic degradation38. Fusion of the tandem mCherry-GFP tag to the C-terminus of SQST-1 permits tracking of autophagic cargo fusion with lysosomes as the GFP fluorescence is quenched in the acidic lysosomal lumen while the mCherry fluorescence is unaffected. Consistent with our previous reports, we observed significantly reduced GFP fluorescence relative to mCherry when dSVIP was overexpressed in the gut of young fed wild-type worms (Fig. 4c, d), signifying enhanced autophagy. Similarly, we observed significant reduction in GFP fluorescence upon dSVIP overexpression in daf-16 and hlh-30 single mutants (Fig. 4c, d). In contrast, no significant reduction to GFP fluorescence was observed in daf-16; hlh-30 double mutants (Fig. 4c, d). Thus, like improvements to late-age mobility, the enhanced autophagy seen upon dSVIP overexpression requires either daf-16 or hlh-30 function.

The proficiency of lysosome-mediated degradation can be enhanced via several mechanisms; namely, increasing biogenesis, acidity, cathepsin activity or, as our data suggest, also through expanding the lysosomal compartment through tubulation. Thus, we were curious if expansion of the lysosomal compartment also increased other parameters of lysosome activity, such as cathepsin activity. We examined CPL-1 processing to the mature form39, a proxy of cathepsin activity, in wild-type, daf-16, hlh-30, and daf-16; hlh-30 animals with and without dSVIP overexpression. In all conditions, we did not observe any significant differences in CPL-1 cleavage compared to wild-type controls (Supplementary Fig. 4a, b). Because cargo turnover was nonetheless increased (Fig. 4c, d), these data fit with the model that dSVIP promotes increased lysosomal activity by expanding the lysosomal compartment rather than by directly increasing Cathepsin activity.

To further probe lysosomal degradation efficiency and proteostasis, we expressed self-aggregating fluorescent polyglutamine proteins (polyQ) in the gut, which accelerate cellular and organismal aging phenotypes40,41. Strikingly, dSVIP overexpression reduced the number of Q64 aggregates in daf-16 and hlh-30 single mutants at days 1 and 5 of adulthood (Fig. 4e, f). Notably, we again did not observe any significant suppression of protein aggregation by dSVIP overexpression in daf-16; hlh-30 double mutants (Fig. 4e, f). Collectively, our data suggest that gut dSVIP overexpression promotes healthier aging in short-lived daf-16 and hlh-30 single mutants, as in wild-type animals17, but is insufficient to do so when both transcription factors are missing and TLs fail to form. These data also lend further support to our model that dSVIP-dependent TL induction specifically improves healthspan without affecting lifespan.

dSVIP overexpression induces DAF-16 and HLH-30 nuclear localization

DAF-16 and HLH-30 are predominantly regulated by their subcellular localization; under basal conditions, each resides in the cytoplasm, but, when stimulated, each translocates into the nucleus to activate their target genes33. Because dSVIP requires either DAF-16 or HLH-30 to stimulate TL induction, we next explored whether dSVIP overexpression promotes nuclear localization of DAF-16 and/or HLH-30. Consistent with the idea that dSVIP activates both transcription factors, we observed strong DAF-16 and HLH-30 accumulation in the nuclei of gut cells when dSVIP was overexpressed in the gut of otherwise wild-type animals (Fig. 5a, b and Fig. 5c, d). We then tested if dSVIP-stimulated HLH-30 nuclear accumulation depended on daf-16 and vice versa. Even when daf-16 was mutated and non-functional, dSVIP overexpression still promoted HLH-30 nuclear accumulation (Fig. 5e, f), albeit to a lesser extent than when DAF-16 was present (Fig. 5c, d). Similarly, dSVIP overexpression still promoted DAF-16 nuclear localization to some extent even in the absence of HLH-30 (Fig. 5g, h). Under basal conditions, HLH-30/TFEB localizes at the lysosomal membrane, and signaling events at the lysosome are required to trigger HLH-30/TFEB nuclear localization42. We considered the possibility that the robust morphological shift of lysosomes into tubules could be the triggering event that prompts HLH-30 nuclear translocation, rather than dSVIP overexpression itself. To provide clarity on the order of events, we examined HLH-30 nuclear accumulation upon dSVIP overexpression when TLs were genetically blocked via combinatorial spin mutations. We reasoned that if HLH-30 activation is prompted by lysosome tubulation, we should not observe HLH-30 nuclear accumulation in spin-1,2,3 mutants with dSVIP overexpression. Remarkably, we still observed HLH-30 nuclear accumulation upon dSVIP overexpression even when TL formation was precluded by spin mutations (Fig. 5i, j). Thus, TL induction is not a prerequisite for HLH-30 activation, and HLH-30 activation likely precedes lysosome tubulation. Collectively, our results support a model in which dSVIP overexpression triggers nuclear localization of HLH-30 and/or DAF-16, which subsequently activate expression of specific factors required to induce lysosome tubulation.

We also uncovered evidence that dSVIP overexpression in the gut may stimulate transcription factor activation cross-tissue. In our analyses, HLH-30::GFP was expressed from an hlh-30 promoter (Phlh-30), which is active in multiple tissues even outside of the gut. Intriguingly, even though we overexpressed dSVIP exclusively in gut tissues, we noticed potential HLH-30 nuclear localization in non-gut tissues, including muscle tissues. We investigated this possibility further by co-imaging a muscle-specific nuclear marker (Pmyo-3::NLS::mCherry) with HLH-30::GFP. Remarkably, we observed strong co-localization of GFP and mCherry signals only in strains overexpressing gut dSVIP (Fig. 5k, l). Notably, we observed that in muscle cells with co-overexpression of a muscle-specific nuclear marker, HLH-30::GFP localization in the nucleus appeared in punctate structures; however, the HLH-30 nuclear signal appeared to accumulate within areas inside the nucleus in which the NLS::mCherry marker was not present. Thus, we surmise that this phenotype is an effect of the co-expression of HLH-30::GFP with the NLS::mCherry marker, as we did not observe a similar pattern when HLH-30 was expressed in gut tissues on its own (Fig. 5a, c, e, g, i). Regardless, these data indicate that dSVIP overexpression in the gut might elicit systemic benefits via cross-tissue HLH-30 activation.

Overexpression of dSVIP triggers robust transcriptional changes

To obtain a more holistic view of how dSVIP triggers pro-health changes at the organismal level, we examined global gene expression changes caused by overexpressing dSVIP. Though dSVIP is not a transcription factor, we found many differentially expressed genes upon its overexpression in the gut, including 1,274 upregulated genes (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Dataset 3), suggesting that SVIP overexpression triggers robust metabolic shifts. Because we also observed TL induction with dSVIP overexpression in daf-16 and hlh-30 mutants, we additionally performed mRNA-seq analysis in these two mutant backgrounds. Remarkably, in the daf-16 and hlh-30 mutant backgrounds, we observed even more differentially expressed genes upon dSVIP gut overexpression relative to controls (Fig. 6b, c). In contrast, we observed minimal transcriptional changes when dSVIP was overexpressed in the gut of daf-16; hlh-30 double mutants (Fig. 6d, e), a condition in which TLs fail to form (Fig. 3a, b and Fig. 3c, d). To explore the genetic pathways involved in these responses, we surveyed genes upregulated by dSVIP gut overexpression in each of the three genetic backgrounds supportive of TL induction. We observed enrichment for genes related to (among others) lysosomes, metabolic pathways, phosphatidylinositol signaling, and protein processing in the ER (Fig. 6f–h). In total, we identified 648 genes that were upregulated in at least two of the three datasets (Fig. 6i and Supplementary Dataset 4). Of these genes, we observed strong enrichment for lysosome-related genes as well as genes related to calcium signaling and axon regeneration (Fig. 6j). Thus, consistent with our cellular analyses, lysosome-related genes are strongly influenced by dSVIP overexpression, as are many other signaling pathways that might support organismal health.

Therefore, we reduced the stringency in our selection criteria and examined genes that were upregulated by dSVIP overexpression in a wild-type background and at least one of the other two mutant backgrounds (daf-16 or hlh-30 mutants). We screened 277 genes, which revealed two genes, grl-16 and sem-4, that had a modest but significant effect in blocking dSVIP-dependent SQST-1::mCherry::GFP turnover (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Dataset 4). We next took a candidate-based approach and selected genes that were upregulated in at least one of the datasets that were also associated with molecular pathways of interest based on GO terms and/or predicted or known biological functions (Supplementary Dataset 4). In this cohort, we screened 133 genes and identified nine genes (acs-20, ant-1.1, ant-1.3, ant-1.4, bbs-9, ced-3, ced-4, pdi-3, and vps-34) that had a significant effect in blocking SQST-1 turnover (Fig. 7c and Supplementary Dataset 4). Notably, ant-1.3 and ant-1.4 share 98% sequence identity, and the RNAi for each is expected to knockdown the other; thus, we cannot distinguish whether the effect is due to ant-1.3 and/or ant-1.4 knockdown. Intriguingly, we also found that the two C. elegans VCP homologs, cdc-48.1 and cdc-48.2, were upregulated in the hlh-30 gut dSVIP-overexpression strain; although we did not screen these two genes by RNAi, chemical inhibition of VCP/CDC-48 blocked dSVIP-dependent TL induction (Fig. 3e–f).

We also screened 50 genes that were upregulated in at least one dataset and/or were also predicted to be direct HLH-30 and DAF-16 targets based on data from the ChIP Atlas43; however, we did not observe a significant effect in blocking SQST-1 turnover when any of these genes were knocked down (Supplementary Dataset 4). Finally, we screened 55 genes that were not differentially expressed in any of the datasets but are related to other positive hits (Supplementary Dataset 4). Because vps-34 knockdown had one of the strongest effects in blocking SQST-1 turnover, we screened several other vps genes and genes known to interact with vps-34. In this cohort, knockdown of bec-1, vps-41, and vps-39 blocked SQST-1 turnover. In total, we screened over 500 genes and identified four general classes of genes having the most significant effects when genetically knocked down: membrane trafficking and lipid metabolism genes (vps-34, bec-1, vps-39, vps-41, acs-20), ant (adenine nucleotide translocator) genes (ant-1.1 and ant-1.4), apoptosis genes (ced-3 and ced-4), and genes affecting protein folding at the ER (pdi-3, cdc-48.1, cdc-48.2) (Fig. 7c).

Given the expansive membrane remodeling that would likely need to occur for lysosome tubulation, we were particularly interested in studying whether vesicle trafficking and lipid modifications influence TL membrane remodeling. Of the vps genes tested, vps-34 knockdown demonstrated the strongest effect on blocking autophagic cargo turnover in dSVIP overexpression animals (Fig. 7b, c). VPS-34 is a class III phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase (PI3K) that phosphorylates phosphatidylinositol (PI) to phosphatidylinositol 3-phosphate (PI3P) and associates with BEC-1 to regulate vesicle sorting processes that converge at lysosomes44. Notably, individual knockdown of either vps-34 or bec-1 blocked dSVIP-dependent (Fig. 7d, e), DR-dependent (Fig. 7f, g) and age-dependent (Fig. 7h, i) TL induction. We further examined whether vps-34 expression increases during starvation, a cue that triggers TL induction. We imaged vps-34::mCherry expressed from the endogenous vps-34 promoter45 in fed and starved L1 larval worms. Remarkably, total VPS-34::mCherry fluorescence signal was increased in starved worms compared to fed worms (Fig. 7j, k). At higher magnification, we observed VPS-34::mCherry localization at tubular structures reminiscent of lysosome tubules (Fig. 7l). While our data suggest that vps-34 is under transcriptional control by HLH-30, it is unknown whether it is a direct HLH-30 target. vps-34 does not possess a canonical TFEB-binding CLEAR consensus sequence; however, C. elegans HLH-30 might bind another, unidentified sequence, and thus it remains possible that HLH-30 could bind to and activate the vps-34 gene locus. Regardless, our data signify a critical role for the VPS-34-BEC-1 complex in regulating lysosome tubulation either upon dSVIP overexpression, during food limitation, or during aging.

Discussion

DR is an effective strategy to delay aging but its implementation has many practical limitations. Thus, identifying strategies that can mimic the effects of DR is a major goal. In previous work, we found that constitutive induction of an atypical form of tubular lysosomes can mimic the beneficial effects of DR and does so by amplifying cross-tissue proteostasis. Thus, understanding the control mechanisms behind TL induction could inform new strategies to harness the beneficial effects of DR. In this study, we found that the concerted action of two major pro-longevity transcription factors, DAF-16/FOXO and HLH-30/TFEB, play a pivotal role in the formation of TLs (Fig. 8). Moreover, we demonstrated that the conventional requirements to stimulate TLs can be artificially bypassed through intestinal overexpression of Drosophila SVIP, a previously characterized TL stimulator (Fig. 8). Mechanistically, artificial induction of TLs via dSVIP overexpression in the intestine caused nuclear translocation of DAF-16 and HLH-30. Remarkably, we even observed HLH-30 nuclear translocation across multiple tissues when dSVIP was overexpressed exclusively in the intestine. We surmise that cross-tissue amplification of HLH-30 activity leads to systemic effects that boost organismal health of aged C. elegans. Altogether, this work reveals that TL regulation might be applicable to healthy aging interventions.Fig. 8Schematic model for DAF-16- and HLH-30-dependent regulation of tubular lysosome induction.Left panel: Under contexts of high autophagic demand, such as starvation, calorie restriction, and natural aging, DAF-16 and HLH-30 cooperate to induce lysosomal tubulation in the C. elegans gut by triggering lysosomal gene programs. Right panel: dSVIP overexpression activates both DAF-16 and HLH-30 to trigger TLs constitutively.

Left panel: Under contexts of high autophagic demand, such as starvation, calorie restriction, and natural aging, DAF-16 and HLH-30 cooperate to induce lysosomal tubulation in the C. elegans gut by triggering lysosomal gene programs. Right panel: dSVIP overexpression activates both DAF-16 and HLH-30 to trigger TLs constitutively.

Although DAF-16 and HLH-30 have distinct functions, in some cases, these two transcription factors work in concert to trigger similar pro-health mechanisms, perhaps as a safeguard if one transcription factor is compromised. Their cooperative functions seem to be most pertinent under stressful conditions; for example, previous data indicate that DAF-16 and HLH-30 form a physical complex to co-regulate specific target genes to mediate resistance to oxidative stress34. However, in other contexts, such as dauer formation, the two TFs have counteracting functions34. Thus, DAF-16 and HLH-30 have a complex relationship that varies depending on the cellular needs. In the context of TL induction, our results suggest that DAF-16 is the predominant transcription factor required to trigger lysosome tubulation during food limitation; however, during aging, TL induction appears more equally dependent on DAF-16 and HLH-30. Despite these distinctions, we found that heightened activity of either transcription factor can compensate for the loss of the other. The partially redundant actions of both transcription factors are likely a result of co-regulated transcriptional targets as DAF-16/FOXO and HLH-30/TFEB co-occupy up to 41% of target promoters34. Potentially, in the absence of DAF-16, the transcriptional targets of HLH-30 could shift to more DAF-16-specific targets to compensate for DAF-16 loss, or vice versa. Our results add to the growing evidence that redundancy between DAF-16/FOXO and HLH-30/TFEB can reinforce critical signals for health-promoting support mechanisms.

Significantly, either transcription factor can be utilized for experimental induction of TLs, indicating that both transcription factors could be tapped as an interventional target to stimulate TLs on demand. We also found that dSVIP overexpression can trigger TL formation in daf-16 and hlh-30 mutants; thus, when one transcription factor is absent, dSVIP can trigger TL formation by activating the other transcription factor. Consistent with this conclusion, simultaneous loss of both hlh-30 and daf-16 precluded dSVIP-dependent TL induction, indicating that at least one transcription factor must be present for dSVIP to stimulate TLs (Fig. 3a, b and Fig. 3c, d). Molecularly, how dSVIP improves systemic health remains an open question. However, a major finding in our study is that constitutive induction of TLs via dSVIP gut overexpression results in the nuclear translocation of HLH-30 not only in the intestine but also in muscles (Fig. 5k, l). Potentially, this could explain the improved proteostasis observed across multiple tissues when TLs are deployed exclusively in the gut17. We propose a model in which HLH-30/TFEB signals specifically emanating from the intestine are important for integrating signaling events in multiple organs. This is supported by previous work demonstrating that intestinal signals broadcasting to muscle tissues are required to mediate stress resistance, improve systemic proteostasis, and increase longevity46,47. Similarly, trans-tissue signals originating in the gut and received by the nervous system increase oxidative stress resistance and extend lifespan48. Notably, overexpression of human SVIP can also stimulate TLs in the gut of well-fed C. elegans18, which provides support that the mechanisms we uncover in C. elegans may translate to mammalian systems.

Our studies also revealed several classes of genes related to vesicle trafficking, energy metabolism, and lipid signaling that link DAF-16 and HLH-30 activity to lysosome tubulation. Specifically, we identified a role for PI3K lipid signaling in lysosome tubulation; vps-34 knockdown blocked autophagic cargo turnover in dSVIP overexpression worms, and starvation caused an increase in vps-34 expression and VPS-34 localization to tubular structures. In previous studies, VPS-34 was shown to produce PI(3)P on autophagosomal membranes to promote phagophore maturation and closure49, and it has also been reported to influence lysosomal membrane dynamics during autophagic lysosome reformation50. Thus, we hypothesize that VPS-34 plays a similar role in catalyzing lipid signaling events at lysosome membranes to promote membrane expansion and/or fusion events in response to dietary and age-related cues. VPS-34 works in concert with several other proteins; for example, VPS-34 requires VPS-15 for recruitment to membranes51, and VPS-34 and VPS-15 can further assemble with BEC-1 and either EPG-8/ATG-14 or T23G11.7/UVRAG to form two distinct tetrameric complexes that aid in co-regulating various VPS-34 functions52,53. Notably, in addition to vps-34, our screen revealed that knockdown of bec-1 likewise blocked autophagic turnover, and, in previous work, we found that mutation of epg-8, the C. elegans ortholog of atg-14, also strongly blocked dSVIP-dependent autophagic turnover17. Altogether, these data further bolster a role for the VPS-34-BEC-1-EPG-8 complex in TL regulation.

In addition to identifying key genes required for TL induction, our studies have also raised some broader questions related to the role of TLs in organismal aging. Namely, if TLs are naturally stimulated during aging, why does constitutive TL stimulation by gut-specific dSVIP overexpression further boost health in aged animals? Although we do not fully know the answer to this yet, we hypothesize that the highly digestive nature of TLs, and the more efficient turnover of autophagic cargo when TLs are present permanently from youth, provides a more robust proteostasis system to prevent cumulative tissue damage. Early-life induction of TLs might help to attenuate the autophagic load at older ages by providing robust autophagic turnover throughout life. There are in fact examples from various species where continuous autophagy stimulation by different approaches extends lifespan and healthspan54–56. Remarkably, short-term rapamycin administration in young individuals results in prolonged autophagy activation that suppresses age-related pathologies in the gut57. As a corollary, brief TL induction in young animals might be sufficient to provide life-long health benefits. Notably, preventing TL induction under DR abolishes lifespan and health benefits in C. elegans17. We envision that with increased autophagic loads, lysosomes must undergo a compensatory change in morphology to accommodate heightened turnover demands. Otherwise, lysosomes become the restrictive factor in achieving full autophagic potential. Our study provides evidence of a support system that can be employed by cells to mitigate autophagic burden throughout lifespan and thereby enhance healthspan.

In summary, our study reveals molecular machinery and signaling pathways important for the induction of robust TL networks. These mechanisms can be tapped to promote healthy aging in C. elegans and, in theory, other animals. Our observations also indicate that early induction of TLs in the gut might further propagate pro-health signals to the whole organism to prevent age-dependent tissue deterioration. Finally, we suggest that the natural presence of TLs makes them ideal candidates to develop anti-aging interventions over other approaches, as we anticipate that their ectopic induction would have limited adverse consequences. Further studies to fine-tune their induction will be required to better exploit their activity and devise practical therapeutic strategies.

Methods

Strain generation

Supplementary Dataset 1 provides a complete list of strains used in this study. All strains generated in this study were generated using standard genetic crosses and/or microinjection58. For genetic crosses, transgenes expressing fluorescent proteins were tracked by stereomicroscopy, and gene deletions and mutations were verified by PCR and/or sequencing. For microinjection, constructs were injected individually or in combination into the gonad of adult hermaphrodites, each at a concentration of 25 ng/µl. Integration of transgenes was achieved using UV irradiation, followed by >6 generations of outcrossing.

To generate Pges-1::gfp::hlh-30::unc-54 3' UTR, hlh-30 (isoform afg) was PCR-amplified from C. elegans genomic DNA with a KpnI site upstream of the start codon using the following primers: forward, 5′-GGGGACAAGTTGTACAAAAAAGCAGGCTGGTACCATGGCCGATGACGACGACCGCG-3′; and reverse, 5′-GGGGACCACTTTGTACAAGAAAGCTGGGTTACGAAAAGTCCATGTGATAATGACCC -3′. This hlh-30 sequence was then cloned into the pDONR221 Gateway entry vector using BP clonase (ThermoFisher), and the insert was verified by DNA sequencing. A gfp coding sequence was PCR-amplified (from a lab stock plasmid15) with KpnI overhangs using the following primers: forward, 5′-GGCGGTACCATGAGTAAAGGAGAAGAACTTTCAC -3′; reverse, 5′-GGCGGTACCCTATTTGTATAGTTCATCCATGCCATG-3′. The pDONR221 hlh-30 plasmid was linearized by digesting with KpnI and the gfp PCR product was inserted at the N-terminus of hlh-30 using restriction enzyme cloning. pDONR221 GFP::hlh-30 was sequence verified and then combined with lab-stock plasmids pDONR P4-P1r Pges-1 and pDONR P2R-P3 unc-54 3′ UTR into the pDEST R4-R3 Gateway destination vector using LR clonase (ThermoFisher).

The plasmid Pges-1::daf-16::unc-54 3’UTR was obtained from Addgene catalog #175283 and Pmyo-3::NLS::mCherry was a gift from Dr. Joshua Kaplan (Harvard Medical School, Boston, MA).

Animal maintenance

Worms were raised at 20°C on NGM agar (51.3 mM NaCl, 0.25% peptone, 1.7% agar, 1 mM CaCl2, 1 mM MgSO4, 25 mM KPO4, 12.9 µM cholesterol, pH 6.0). Fed worms were maintained on NGM agar plates previously seeded with E. coli OP50 bacteria. Synchronous populations of worms were obtained by bleaching gravid hermaphrodites. Briefly, gravid worms were vortexed in 1 mL bleaching solution (0.5 M NaOH, 20% bleach) for 5 min to isolate eggs, and eggs were then washed three times in M9 buffer (22 mM KH2PO4, 42 mM Na2HPO4, 85.5 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgSO4) before plating. To obtain starved L1 animals, bleached eggs were spotted on NGM agar that lacked OP50 bacteria, and plates were maintained at 20°C for 24–48 h before imaging. For aging experiments, synchronous populations of animals were established by bleaching gravid worms. In all aging experiments, adult worms were picked onto fresh OP50-seeded NGM plates every day to separate adults from their progeny.

RNAi experiments

The hlh-30, vps34, bec-1, vps-39, vps-41, ant-1.1, ant-1.3/1.4, ced-3, ced-4, pdi-3, grl-16, sem-4, acs-20, and bbs-9 RNAi clones were obtained from the Julie Ahringer RNAi collection59 and verified by DNA sequencing. For RNAi experiments, synchronous populations of animals were grown on OP50-seeded NGM plates until late L4 stage, at which time they were transferred to RNAi plates (NGM plus 100 ng/µl carbenicillin and 1 mM IPTG) that had been seeded with bacteria expressing the RNAi clone. An empty L4440 vector was used as a negative control.

VCP inhibitor treatment

A 10 μM stock solution of the VCP inhibitor CB5083 (MedChem Express, Cat. # HY-12861/CS-5405) was prepared in DMSO and diluted to a final working concentration of 1 μM in M9 buffer. 300 μl of the working stock was directly spotted onto NGM plates that were previously seeded with OP50 bacteria. For control plates, DMSO was diluted 1:10 in M9 buffer, and 300 μl was directly spotted onto NGM plates that were previously seeded with OP50 bacteria. Late L4s were transferred to control (DMSO) or CB5083 plates.

Lifespan analysis

Synchronous populations of worms were transferred as late L4s to NGM plates seeded with OP50 bacteria. All lifespans had a starting sample size of at least 100 worms per genotype, which based on previous analyses should be sufficient to provide >80% power to detect differences with a 5% significance level. Animals that exploded, bagged, or crawled off plates were censored during analysis. Lifespans were analyzed using OASIS 2 software60, and statistical significance was assessed using a log-rank test. Each lifespan plot represents a single independent biological replicate.

Thrashing assay

Synchronous populations of animals were transferred as late L4s to NGM plates seeded with OP50 bacteria. Worms were transferred to fresh plates every day to separate adults from their progeny. To score thrashing rates, individual worms were transferred into a drop of M9 buffer on an NGM plate, and the number of body thrashes were counted in a 1 min period. Each worm represents an independent biological replicate.

Microscopy

For C. elegans whole animal imaging, 4% agarose (Fisher Bioreagents) pads were dried on a Kimwipe (Kimtech) and then placed on top of a Gold SealTM glass microscope slide (ThermoFisher Scientific). A small volume of 10 mM levamisole (Acros Organics) was spotted on the agarose pad. Worms were transferred to the levamisole spot, and a glass cover slip (Fisher Scientific) was placed on top to complete the mounting. To determine HLH-30::GFP localization, worms were analyzed within 3 min once mounting was completed.

Image analysis

For all imaging experiments, whole individual worms were imaged, and each worm represents an independent biological replicate. Images were processed using LAS X software (Leica) and FIJI/ImageJ (NIH). Lysosome networks were analyzed using “Skeleton” analysis plugins in FIJI. Briefly, images were converted to binary 8-bit images and then to skeleton images using the “Skeletonize” plugin. Skeleton images were then quantified using the “Analyze Skeleton” plugin. Number of objects, number of junctions, and object lengths were scored. An “object” is defined by the Analyze Skeleton plugin as a branch connecting two endpoints, an endpoint and junction, or two junctions. Junctions/object was used as a parameter to quantify network integrity.

To analyze fluorescence intensity, the gut tissue was outlined using the free-draw tool in FIJI/ImageJ, and average fluorescence intensity of the outlined area was measured. For all intensity experiments, 50% laser intensity, 300 ms exposure time, and 100% Fluorescence Intensity Manager settings were used.

For co-localization analysis, whole worm images were analyzed using Fiji’s colocalization plugin Coloc2 to determine the colocalization of green and red fluorescence, which was reported as the intensity correlation quotient (ICQ) score61. ICQ values toward to 0 indicate random fluorescence patterns, segregated localization values will tend toward -0.5 and colocalization of the fluorescent signals will be towards 0.538,62.

Western blotting

For each genotype, day 1 adult worms were washed off a 60 mm plate in M9 buffer and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 1 min briefly to pellet worms. Worms from a single plate were pooled and each plate represents an independent biological replicate, and three independent biological replicates were collected. Worms were lysed using a motorized grinder in RIPA buffer (sc-24948, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) to extract total protein, and the tissue was centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 30 min to pellet the solid debris. 5X sample buffer (1 M Tris-HCl (pH 6.8), 1 mM DTT, 20% SDS, 60% glycerol, Bromophenol Blue) was added to the soluble lysate, and samples were boiled for 10 min. Proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE on a 4−12% Bis-Tris gel (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific), subsequently transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, and blocked in 10% horse serum/PBS. For immunoblotting, membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with anti-Cathepsin L antibody (1:1000, ab58991, Abcam) or actin monoclonal antibody (ACTN05 (C4); 1:2000, #MA5-11869, Invitrogen). Subsequently, membranes were washed three times in PBS/0.1% Tween 20 and incubated with goat anti-rabbit IgG or goat anti-mouse IgG horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher). Proteins were detected using an ECL chemiluminescent reagent (Bio-Rad) and imaged using a Bio-Rad GelDoc imager.

RNA sequencing

Gravid adult worms were bleached, and eggs were plated onto NGM plates to produce synchronized populations of worms. For each genotype, day 1 adult worms were collected in M9 in three independent biological replicates. RNA extraction was done using standard a TRIzol TM reagent protocol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat# 15596018). Subsequently, genomic DNA removal was performed using a GeneJet RNA-purification kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, cat# K0702). The concentration of purified RNA was measured using a nanodrop, and RNA quality was assessed using a Bioanalyzer. At least 400 ng/μl of Purified RNA for each replicate was sent to Novogene for cDNA library preparation and Illumina sequencing (Illumina NovaSeq 6000).

Sequencing reads were mapped to the C. elegans reference genome (WBcel235) using HISAT263. We used featureCounts v1.5.0-p364 to count the reads mapped to each gene and calculate FPKM. We also used Salmon65 to quantify gene expression in alignment-based mode. Differential expression analyses was performed using the DESeq2 R package (1.20.0)66. DESeq2 provides statistical routines for determining differential expression in digital gene expression data using a model based on the negative binomial distribution. The resulting p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini and Hochberg’s approach for controlling the false discovery rate (FDR). We used adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05 and fold-change ≥ 2 as a cut-off for differentially expressed genes. Differentially expressed genes were analyzed with ShinyGO67 to look for enriched gene sets (adjusted p-value ≤ 0.05) with respect to KEGG68 database.

Statistical analyses

Data were statistically analyzed using GraphPad Prism. For two sample comparisons, an unpaired t-test was used to determine significance (α = 0.05). For three or more samples, a one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s, Tukey’s, or Šídák’s multiple comparisons was used to determine significance (α = 0.05). For grouped comparisons, a two-way ANOVA with Šídák’s multiple comparisons was used to determine significance (α = 0.05). Statistical significance of lifespan data were determined using a log-rank test.
