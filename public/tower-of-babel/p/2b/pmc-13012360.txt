Addition of immersive virtual reality-mediated programs with conventional physiotherapy on quality of life in knee osteoarthritis – A protocol of a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Kamalakannan Rathinavel, Ajay Kumar, Rajasekar Sannasi, Prem Venkatesan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Knee osteoarthritis (OA) is a debilitating condition causing persistent pain that significantly impacts a patient’s physical and psychological wellbeing, ultimately reducing their quality of life. Existing research predominantly compares immersive virtual reality (IVR) with passive treatments of electrotherapy, neglecting comparisons with active exercise-based conventional physiotherapy interventions. This trial investigates the effectiveness of IVR-mediated programs in addition to conventional physiotherapy on quality of life, fear of movement, and pain self-efficacy in knee OA.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This study has been planned to be conducted in a musculoskeletal outpatient department. A total of 96 patients will be screened for OA according to ACR criteria and randomly allocated to two groups: Group-A (IVR-based exercise program + conventional physiotherapy) and Group-B (conventional physiotherapy). The primary outcome would be Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS), and the secondary outcomes are psycho-cognitive domains such as Tampa scale of kinesiophobia and pain self-efficacy and muscle strength and functional performance test. The patients will undergo 6 weeks of supervised exercise programs, and outcomes measures will be recorded before the first treatment session and post treatment at 6 weeks and follow-up at the 12th week and 24th week. Normality of the data will be analyzed using Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Descriptive statistics will be expressed in either mean and standard deviation or median and 95% confidence interval based on normality. Effect Size Index (ESI) will be calculated for all the groups in postintervention after 6 weeks. Comparison of outcome measures will be done for both within and between group analyses at the different points of time with respective statistics using SPSS-20.0 program. A P value < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant.CONCLUSION:By identifying the most effective intervention in both physical and psychosocial impairments in managing OA knee, this study may be helpful for clinicians to opt the therapeutic intervention that treats OA knee patients suffering from pain, kinesiophobia, and compromised quality of life.

Introduction

Osteoarthritis (OA) is a chronic degenerative joint disease that involves progressive wear and tear of articular cartilage, subchondral bone erosion, ligaments, joint capsule weakening, and synovium thickening.[1] In global burden of diseases, injuries and risk factors study (GBI) of the past 3 decades, the prevalent symptomatic OA cases (hip, knee, and hand) in India increased 2.66-fold from 23.46 million in 1990 to 62.35 million in 2019 and OA was the 20th most common cause of years lived with disability in India in 2019, in which OA knee is the most common form and leading burden.[2] OA patients are associated with not only physical impairments but also negative psychological outcomes such as depressive symptoms and anxiety.[3] OA knee patients are less physically active with reduction in muscle strength and flexibility and impaired physical function; as a result of these changes, there is a reduction in quality of life (QoL). This low QoL may lead to depressive symptoms and anxiety. OA is a condition of long-term chronic pain; the increase in pain will make an individual to respond with avoidance or confrontation in coping with pain, the avoidance of activity increases the fear, and this vicious cycle forms the basis of fear of movement (kinesiophobia),[4] and the catastrophizing.[5] So, in OA patients, the occurrence of neuronal plasticity with long-lasting pain expressed as hypersensitivity continues to build fear development against pain.[67] Benyon et al.[8] have demonstrated that self-efficacy and active coping are predictive factors for improved mood and reduced disability in OA knee patients.

Exercise therapy is used as a first-line treatment for patients with knee OA, and it was recommended by current international guidelines [ACR, OARSI, EULAR].[91011] Well-structured conventional exercises, which consist of muscle strengthening, stretching, and a range of motion exercises, have proven to be effective in OA knee.[121314] A systematic review that investigated the effect of patient education in the pain and function of OA of hip and knee has concluded that patient education can be used as a stand-alone treatment and can also be combined with other conservative treatments in reducing pain and improved function.[15] Patient education has also been recommended as a first line of treatment as an exercise for OA knee.[91011]

Consideration of psychosocial factors such as kinesiophobia and pain self-efficacy suggests that the inclusion of immersive virtual reality (IVR)-based exercise in the management of painful conditions may offer a multitude of advantages. These include improvements in affective responses, coping strategies, gradual exposure, and embodiment.[16] A scoping review indicates that VR-based exercises, when combined with conventional treatments, can effectively reduce pain, improve the range of motion, decrease disability, and enhance patient satisfaction in chronic musculoskeletal conditions. However, the review also highlights significant challenges, such as a limited number of high-quality clinical studies, small sample sizes, and suboptimal search strategies.[17]

Similarly, a single-blind randomized controlled prospective trial demonstrated that immersive virtual reality exercises can improve pain, functionality, and balance in knee OA. Nevertheless, the effectiveness of such interventions on psychosocial measures like kinesiophobia and pain self-efficacy has yet to be thoroughly investigated. The literature indicates that these psychosocial impairments are prevalent in chronic knee OA, underscoring the need for further research.[48] Therefore, this study aims to compare the effects of integrated immersive VR exercises combined with conventional physiotherapy and patient education versus conventional physiotherapy and patient education alone. The outcomes of interest include quality of life, pain pressure threshold, kinesiophobia, pain self-efficacy, muscle strength, and functional performance in individuals with knee OA.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This is a randomized controlled assessor-blinded trial, with a study protocol written in accordance with the “Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials” (SPIRIT),[18] and results of the trial will be reported following the “Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials” (CONSORT) statement guidelines.[19]

Patients will be recruited from the institution-based musculoskeletal physiotherapy department and medical college hospital in India, and the interventions will be executed from both these locations.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria[20]

The patients who fulfil the following criteria are included in the study; based on the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) clinical classification criteria, at least three of the following should be positive: Age between 50 years and 75 years,[212223] morning stiffness <30 minutes, crepitus on knee motion, bony tenderness, bony enlargement, no palpable warmth,[24] having unilateral or bilateral grade 2 or 3 of knee OA according to the Kellgren and Lawrence radiological gradings, and a TSK-KA-11 score 23 or higher.[2526]

Exclusion criteria[20]

The patients with cognitive impairment, who have underwent a history of knee surgery, with joint infection, suffering with rheumatoid arthritis of knee, any form of Inflammatory arthritis, any past 6-month history of hip or knee replacement surgeries, intraarticular steroidal injections, arthroscopic surgeries, persons who are all in need of external support for walking, and any lower extremity-related, neuro-related, musculoskeletal diseases that affect the strength and balance are not included for the study.

Interventions

The exercise intervention will be conducted in an institutional-based musculoskeletal physiotherapy department and as well as in the physiotherapy division of a medical college hospital.

Group-A (Immersive virtual reality-mediated exercise program[27] plus conventional physiotherapy)

This group will be provided with supervised intervention conducted with an immersive type of VR-mediated exercises with headset [Occulus quest 3] and games from REWIN HEALTH SOFTWARE with CE and RoHS certification added with conventional physiotherapy (this treatment will be discussed in the interventional part of conventional physiotherapy). The exercises will be done under clinical supervision 3 days per week, for 6 weeks; the group will have a total duration of 50 min, in which conventional exercises will be 35 min and immersive VR-mediated exercises would be of 15 min. The exercises are described in Table 1. The patient will be advised that the application would end immediately in the event that he suffers from acute exhaustion, nausea, sudden and severe shortness of breath, chest discomfort, fullness, heaviness, discomfort, or pain, or balance issues.

Outcomes

Primary[34]

Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS)[35]

Knee-related QoL will be measured using Knee Injury and Osteoarthritis Outcome Score (KOOS), which is a self-reported limited time-consuming questionnaire administered to determine the patient-related changes after knee ailments. It consists of five domains for measurement pain, symptoms, activities of daily living, sport and recreation function, and knee-related QoL. Interpretation of scores can be done as considering its 5 domains where pain consists of nine items and symptoms bears seven items, ADL function has 17 items, sports and recreation function has five items, and QoL four items; the score is provided on the basis of Likert scale with five possible answers, with 0 as no problem and 4 as extreme problems, and each of five scores is added for an individual domain total.

Secondary[36373839]

Kinesiophobia by Tampa scale of kinesiophobia [Tampa scale of kinesiophobia Kannada version TSK-KA-11][2526]

Tampa scale of kinesiophobia-11 is a modified version of Tampa scale of kinesiophobia-17 used to measure the fear of movement and reinjury (kinesiophobia) associated with musculoskeletal pain; the interpretation is done as the minimal score is ≤22, the low score is 23–28, moderate 29–35, and high ≥36.[2526]

Pain self-efficacy questionnaire[374041]

The pain self-efficacy questionnaire will be tested to check the confidence of the people undergoing pain and how it affects their functional performance. It has 10 item questionnaires; 0 denotes the person is not at all confident and 6 denotes the person is completely confident. The raw score for 10 questions ranges from 0 to 60, in which it denotes low PSEQ (0–11), medium PSEQ (12–32), and high PSEQ (33–60). The higher the score, the person is higher with self-efficacy with pain.

Pain pressure threshold by algometry[3642]

An algometer (Wagner, FPN 200) will be used to assess the patient’s pain pressure threshold in the tender spot around the knee. Once the most tender spot is identified, apply the pressure gauge to the point of maximal sensitivity at 90-degree angle and increase the pressure continuously at an even rate about 1 kg/sec till the patient tells “yes” on the start of pain; note the reading and then stop pressure, and this indicates pain pressure threshold of the patient.

Knee extensors muscle strength by handheld dynamometry[38]

Patients will be seated at the edge of the couch with the foot and hands fixed with knee flexed at 60°. The assessor keeps the dynamometer (Fit must dynamometer) on the shin of leg 5 cm above the ankle. Then, the patient will be asked to exert a maximum knee muscle force to extend the leg for five seconds and the assessing therapist will adjust the resistance according to the patient’s force.

Functional performance test by 40Meter fast-paced walk test[4344]

In a 10-meter pathway, the patients will be made to walk in a maximum pace he could maintain along with stability. Participants complete four lapses to cover a distance of 40 meters, and the speed of walking is measured in meter/second (m/s). With this, the functional performance of the participants would be measured.[43]

Global rate of change (GROC)[39]

A seven-point scale intends to measure the rate of change of a person’s treatment prognosis on his conditions; the points in the scale denote the magnitude of the change for the treatment, which will be noted. The points [−1 to −7] denote a negative impact of treatment, and [+1 to +7] denotes a positive impact of treatment. GROC will be taken after a 3rd week of treatment and then by end of 6th week.

Premeasurement will be done for all outcome measures except GROC and the post-treatment measurement will be done after 6 weeks and by 12th week and last by 24th week (a total of 6 months follow-up will be done). GROC will be taken at the 3rd week of treatment and at 6th week. The 12th and 24th week follow-up is crucial to assess the persistence of IVR-mediated therapeutic benefits beyond the immediate treatment phase. This extended evaluation is vital for establishing long-term clinical efficacy in knee OA, particularly regarding sustained improvements in QoL and psycho-cognitive outcomes.

Adverse events, dropouts, adherence, and compliance monitoring

Adverse events will be registered by supervising physiotherapists, and patients will be asked to document any adverse effects during follow-up according to CONSORT group recommendations. Exercise adherence, dropouts, and loss to follow-up will be monitored by supervising physiotherapists using attendance registers and treatment schedule diaries. Since this study involves an IVR-mediated program combined with supervised conventional exercises, treatment adherence is expected to be strong. Adherence will be tracked through attendance records, while strategies such as SMS reminders or phone calls will encourage participation.

Demographic data

Gender, age, height, weight, cohabiting status, educational level, employment status, number of comorbidities measured with the Charlson Comorbidity Index, analgesic use type, and frequency.

Sample size

Sample size was determined using the standard deviation of KOOS score from the study conducted by Ashish John Prabhakar et al.,[45] The formula used was N = 2*([Z (1− ∝/2) + Z (1− β)] ^2*σ^2)/d^2, with power = 90%, Z (1− β) = 0.84, level of significance = 5%, Z (1− ∝/2) =1.96, SD of Koos (pain) in experimental group = 11.4, SD of KOOS (pain) in control = 19.9, pooled standard = 15.65, and expected mean difference = 9.9. The estimated sample size is 43 in each group, further assuming 10% attrition. The sample size required in each group is 48.

Randomization

The randomization process will be done by generating the random numbers from a computational method by a person who is involved in neither assessment nor treatment; the allocation process will be done by placing the consecutively numbered, sealed, opaque envelopes and opened individually immediately after baseline testing for each participant.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of the data will be performed using SPSS20.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp). Normality of the data will be analyzed using Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Descriptive statistics will be expressed in either mean and standard deviation or median and 95% confidence interval based on normality. Comparison of KOOS, Tampa scale score, knee muscle strength, and fast pace walking score at different time points within the group will be analyzed using repeated measures of ANOVA/Friedman’s test followed by post hoc if ANOVA is significant. Noncompliance and missing dropout data will be analyzed using intention to treat analysis. Comparison of improvement at different time points between the groups will be analyzed using unpaired t-test/Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables will be analyzed using Chi-square test. A P value < 0.05 will be considered statistically significant. Effect Size Index (ESI) will be calculated for all the group in post intervention after 6 weeks.

Blinding

As outcome assessors and data collectors, participants will be blinded. But the supervising physiotherapist while allocation and treatment and also for statistical analysis and interpretation will not be blinded.

Ethical consideration

This trial was registered to Clinical Trial Registry-India [CTRI] hosted at the ICMR’s national institute of medical statistics. In case of modification, it will be reported on time to CTRI. Informed consent will be written in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki-II and will be obtained from all patients who have been included; collected data and information will be kept confidentially by researchers and other stake holders. No data monitoring was established, authorship eligibility will be defined based on recommendations from international committee medical journal editors, and all authors declare no conflicts of interests.

Discussion

A comprehensive treatment of OA includes patient education and physical and psychosocial interventions. However, a patient-centric approach is needed according to their specific needs. Systematic review on psychosocial aspects and OA knee reveals participants may also experience some additional symptoms apart from pain and functional limitations arising from OA and/or comorbidities such as depression, anxiety, altered sleep chronic wide spread pain, impaired coping skills, and fear about movement.[37846] The most effective intervention in reducing physical and psychosocial impairments in managing OA knee requires an approach which addresses both physical and psychosocial domain. Including VR in comparison with sensory motor training in post-traumatic and chronic OA has shown beneficial change in pain, functional disability, and inflammatory biomarkers (CRP, TNF-alpha, IL-2, IL-4, and IL-6);[47] the metabolic brain activity with pain has been specifically reduced with nonpharmacologic, cognitive modulation of pain via attentional distraction;[48] studies also suggest the heightened immersion by fully immersive VR can have positive effects on patient engagement, motivation, and the feeling of being present in therapeutic exercises.[49] According to guidelines provided by American College of Rheumatology/Arthritis Foundation as a management of OA knee, a multimodal approach was exercise; patient education was strongly recommended.[9] However, studies on VR associated to musculoskeletal pain are of low quality, with a small sample, short-term follow-up, and do not include control groups.[16] Therefore, this study may be helpful for clinicians to decide the therapeutic intervention that treats OA knee patients comprehensively by having meaningful comparator groups and adequate follow-up; this study may provide the high-quality evidence in the field of OA knee rehabilitation with immersive VR programs. This trial may also have a few limitations, such as blinding, has not done for allocation of patients, and supervising physiotherapists. On the other hand, it has the strength of having outcome measures for self-efficacy, kinesiophobia, and measures for QoL along with objective measures of muscle strength and pain pressure threshold and functional performance.

Conclusion

OA of knee is caused by multiple factors which are under the umbrella of biopsychosocial frame. If this clinical trial is proven effective, it may provide new insightful ideas to physiotherapists treating OA knee with psychosocial issues such as kinesiophobia and low pain self-efficacy. Exercise therapy has been proven effective in OA knee patients on physical impairments such as pain, joint stiffness, knee function, and QoL. However, this study explores the effectiveness of exercise therapy on kinesiophobia and pain self-efficacy in addition to other physical impairments. Moreover, as immersive VR exercises are known for its effectiveness on psychosocial measures in other musculoskeletal conditions, this study will provide concrete evidence to the researchers and clinicians about immersive VR-mediated exercise and conventional exercise on various psychosocial measures and QoL in OA knee patients.
