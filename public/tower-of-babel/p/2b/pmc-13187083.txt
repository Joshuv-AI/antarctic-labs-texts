Preclinical in vivo dosimetry of healthy tissues in radiopharmaceutical therapy: implementation of a physiologically realistic mouse eye model in GATE
Authors: Elodie Jouberton, Nicolas Sas, Tommy Billoux, Philippe Auzeloux, Sébastien Schmitt, Leslie Mazuel, Jean-Michel Chezal, Elisabeth Miot-Noirault, Isabelle Ranchon-Cole, Florent Cachin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTargeted radionuclide therapy is a rapidly growing treatment modality that has shown great promise in managing metastatic cancers. However, the affinity of certain radiopharmaceuticals for off-target healthy tissues necessitates accurate dosimetric evaluations. This is especially the case for [131I]ICF01012, an experimental radiopharmaceutical used for advanced melanoma targeted radionuclide therapy, which targets melanin in pigmented tumors and metastases but also in melanized healthy tissues, including sensitive structures such as the retinal pigment epithelium. This study aimed to create the first anatomically precise, voxel-based mouse eye model to facilitate microdosimetric evaluations in the context of targeted radionuclide therapy. A voxelized model of the mouse eye was constructed using high-resolution MRI and SIMS imaging data from C57Bl/6J mice. The biodistribution of radioactivity in the eyes was assessed ex vivo by gamma counting. GATE Monte Carlo simulations were employed to calculate the S-values of the intraocular structures. Particular attention was given to the absorbed dose by the retina considering its proximity to the retinal pigment epithelium, which contains melanin.ResultsSIMS imaging revealed an increased iodine-127 signal in melanin-rich regions, particularly the retinal pigment epithelium and choroid. S values were obtained for all relevant source and target regions of the eye. Time-dependent biodistribution analysis of radioactivity revealed persistent retention of the radiotracer up to 21 days after injection. The retinal pigment epithelium received the highest dose, 24.14 Gy.MBq⁻¹, corresponding to an estimated absorbed dose by the retina of 6.91 Gy.MBq⁻¹.ConclusionThis work presents a novel dosimetry model for the mouse eye, providing essential insights into the exposure of melanin-rich tissues to iodine-131 radiation. Background

In recent years, the field of nuclear medicine has continued to expand, offering new therapeutic perspectives using targeted radionuclide therapy (TRT) and improved patient management through the theranostic principle [1, 2]. Although overall survival, progression-free survival, and treatment-related toxicities are standard clinical endpoints, the assessment of long-term toxicities and the identification of reliable imaging-derived biomarkers—capable of acting as predictive and prognostic indicators of therapeutic efficacy—remain challenging [3].

These challenges highlight the need to optimize the balance between treatment efficacy and toxicity. In current clinical practice, the administered activity in TRT typically follows a one-size-fits-all approach, with a fixed amount of activity prescribed independently of patient-specific factors such as weight or body surface area. However, the variability in patient response to treatment is one of the major challenges in radiopharmaceutical therapy [4]. Patient-specific dosimetry represents a promising approach by enabling the calculation of absorbed doses in both target tissues and healthy organs. This information can be used to optimize treatment planning and dose delivery in order to maximize efficacy and minimize adverse effects. Personalized dosimetry calculations for each treatment have been widely developed in recent years, to the point of being recommended, as outlined in the European Council Directive 2013/59/Euratom in the European Union [5]. Significant progress has also been made in imaging quantification, driven by technological developments in hybrid imaging modalities such as single photon emission computed tomography computed tomography (SPECT/CT) and positron emission tomography computed tomography (PET/CT), as well as by improvements in mathematical modelling [6]. These advancements have substantially reduced uncertainties associated with absorbed dose estimations [7]. Moreover, the integration of advanced computational methods — particularly Monte Carlo simulations — has further enhanced the accuracy and reliability of dosimetry calculations [8].

Our group has developed the melanin-targeting ligand N-(2-diethylaminoethyl)-6-iodoquinoxaline-2-carboxamide, dihydrochloride salt [131I]ICF01012, a small molecule derived from benzamides, with a high affinity and specificity for intra- and extracellular melanin. This makes it an ideal candidate for TRT in pigmented metastatic melanoma [9]. Several preclinical studies have demonstrated strong anti-tumor activity by inhibiting both primary tumor growth and metastatic spread, prolonged retention in a variety of melanoma cancer cell lines and low toxicity to healthy tissues in different murine and human melanoma models [10–15]. This radiotracer is currently in phase I clinical trial for the treatment of metastatic melanoma [16]. Due to the biological properties of ICF01012 melanin-targeting ligand and the physical properties of iodine-131, the eyes are organs at unconventional risk in [131I]ICF01012 TRT. Indeed, melanin is physiologically present in key ocular tissues, including the iris, the choroid, and is particularly abundant in the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) [17]. The RPE is a melanized cell monolayer which plays a crucial role in retinal function. Impairment of RPE cell functions can lead to photoreceptor cell death and is associated with an increased risk of vision loss [18].

The mathematical formalism adopted for the dosimetry for TRT is the medical internal radiation dose (MIRD) formalism, which requires two parameters to be estimated: (i) the time-integrated activity within the source regions and, (ii) the energy transferred and deposited from the source to the target region, as represented by the S-factors [19]. There are numerous Monte Carlo codes for calculating these S-factors, such as MCNPX (Monte Carlo N-Particle), PENELOPE (PENetration and Energy LOss of Positrons and Electrons) and GEANT4 Application for Emission Tomography (GATE) [20]. These tools are essential for accurately estimating absorbed doses using detailed anatomical models. Despite their robustness, such methods are computationally intensive and can be challenging to implement into routine clinician practice. In the preclinical setting, numerous mouse models have been reported to generate S-factors for various combinations of sources, targets and radionuclides and the best-known being the MOBY phantom [21–24]. However, these models do not always accurately represent specific organs or small structures. Given the small size and complex anatomy of the eye, existing S-factors derived from standard mouse models may therefore lack the spatial resolution necessary for accurate absorbed dose estimation. Consequently, the development of a dedicated and anatomically detailed eye model is essential, as has been done for other sensitive organs [25].

In this work, we present the development of a dedicated dosimetry model for the mouse eye in order to optimize personalized dose delivery in [131I]ICF01012 TRT. The model was based on experimental data obtained on C57Bl/6J mice using high-resolution MRI imaging, nanoscale secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) and iodine-131 biodistribution studies after administration of non-radioactive or 131I-labeled ICF01012, respectively. Monte Carlo simulations were performed using the GATE simulation toolkit, enabling rigorous and robust dosimetry. To our knowledge, this is the first dosimetry model of the mouse eye to be proposed for TRT. This model is essential for analyzing the link between the deposited dose to the retina and the associated radiobiological effects, in order to complete the data available for the clinical transfer of this radiopharmaceutical.

Methods

Animal model

Six-week-old female C57Bl/6J mice (Charles River Labs®, L’Arbresle, France) were used in this study. Mice were housed under standard conditions (4 to 6 animals per cage on ventilated racks, temperature 21–24 °C, 60% humidity, 12/12 light/dark cycle with free access to standard food and water). Mice were observed and weighed weekly or twice weekly to assess general clinical condition and to determine endpoints.

All experiments were conducted in accordance with the European Directive 2010/63/EU after approval by the governmental committee (authorization #32528–2021072217542784V2).

Biodistribution of [131I]ICF01012 in eyes

The radiosynthesis of [131I]ICF01012 was achieved via an electrophilic radioiodo-destannylation of its tributylstannane precursor, as previously reported [26–28].

Mice were injected intravenously (iv) while awake with 4.8 ± 1.2 MBq of [131I]ICF01012 in a volume of 0.1 mL into the lateral tail vein (n = 4/time point). Mice were sacrificed at one hour, 24 h, and on days 3, 7, 13, and 21 after injection. For all groups, mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane/oxygen (1 L/min) and then sacrificed by cervical dislocation. The eyes were removed under binocular lens, the whole left eye was kept, and the right eye was dissected as follows: sclera/choroid/RPE, retina, iris/ciliary body, crystalline lens, and cornea. Samples were weighed and counted using a calibrated γ-counter (Wallac 1480 Wizard® 3″, Perkin Elmer, Waltham, MA). Samples were counted in a 360-keV energy window (260–430 keV) for 60 s per sample. After correction for radioactive decay, results were expressed as percentage of injected activity (%IA) per tissue.

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)

After being sacrificed, eyes were removed under a binocular lens and stored in a solution of fixative (ExcaliburR) with 1:40 v/v gadoteric acid 0.5 mmol/mL (Clariscan™Gé) at 4 °C for 3 days. Then they were transferred to a solution of 1X Phosphate buffered saline (PBS), 1:40 v/v gadoteric acid 0.5 mmol/mL (Clariscan™Gé), and 0.01% sodium azide for 2 days at 4 °C before MRI scan. MRI acquisitions were performed on a vertical wide bore 9.4-T Bruker magnet (Bruker, Germany). The MRI system was equipped with a 5-mm microimaging radiofrequency (RF) coil leading to a maximum gradient strength of 1.5 T m− 1 along the three axes. The fixed eye was placed inside a 5-mm nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) tube (Hilgenberg, Germany), and Fomblin® (Sigma-Aldrich, France). A perfluorinated polyether was added to reduce magnetic susceptibility artifacts at material interfaces, which can distort NMR/MRI images, while remaining invisible in the NMR signal. Eye positioning inside the magnet was recorded in all three directions using a 2D fast low-angle shot (FLASH) acquisition. Then, the whole-eye 3D image was acquired based on a FLASH protocol. The echo and repetition times were set to 3.62 and 40 ms, respectively. The flip angle was 30° with a bandwidth of 3 kHz. A 6 × 6 × 6 mm³ field of view with a 176 × 176 × 64 matrix yielded a 34 × 34 × 94 μm resolution, and ten averages were acquired over ~ 54 min. Experimental part of this study was performed on In Vivo Imaging Auvergne (IVIA) platform, Clermont-Ferrand, France.

Nanoscale secondary ion mass spectrometry (SIMS) imaging

ICF01012 was obtained in accordance with the methodology previously described in the literature [26]. Three animals at a time were injected intravenously with iodine-127 ICF01012 (0.5 µmol in a volume of 0.1 mL of 0.9% NaCl). One hour or 72 h after administration, mice were sacrificed, and the cornea and lens were discarded to retain the posterior eye cup. The superior pole was identified. Samples were fixed overnight at + 4 °C in 0.2 M Na cacodylate buffer pH 7.4 containing 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 4.0% paraformaldehyde. The specimens were washed three times in 0.2 M sodium cacodylate buffer solution (pH 7.4) and post-fixed for one hour in the same solution containing 1% osmium tetroxide. Graded dehydration (ethanol then acetone) and resin infiltration were done in an Automate Micro-Wave (Leica Microsystem). Specimens were embedded in resin overnight at room temperature and then cured for 2 days in an oven at 60 °C. Thin Sect. (150 nm) were cut using a UC7 ultramicrotome (Leica Microsystem) and collected on silica wafer. Electron microscopy preparations were all performed by the Centre d’Imagerie Cellulaire Santé (CICS, Clermont-Ferrand, France). All chemical products were sourced from Electron Microscopy Science and distributed in France by Delta Microscopies.

Dynamic SIMS imaging was performed at Institut Curie (Orsay, France) using a NanoSIMS-50 ion microprobe (CAMECA-Ametek, Gennevilliers, France) operated in scanning mode [29]. Briefly, a Cs+ primary ion beam was used to generate negative secondary ion emissions from the sample surface. The tightly focused primary beam, with a spot size around 120 nm, scans over a field of view of about 50 μm x 50 μm with an image definition of 512 × 512 pixels. For the current study, 5 ion species (12C2−, 12C14N−, 32S−, 31P16O− and 127I−) were collected in parallel from the same sputtered volume. Although the absolute amount of iodine cannot be measured due to lack of reference standards, the relative signal height allows a direct comparison of iodine content in the same image field. Moreover, to allow reliable comparison of 127I− signal from one acquisition to another, or from one section to the other, the average intensity of 12C2− was taken as reference for correction between measurements, since 12C2− is the major constituent of the resin-embedded samples and a constant signal can be expected. Indeed, one expects a relatively constant 12C2− signal as this species was generated from the carbon content, the major constituent of the resin embedded samples. In addition, to improve counting statistics for 127I−, multiframe mode was used during the acquisitions, with a dwell time of 0.5 ms per pixel per frame and up to 80 sequential frames were acquired for accumulation purposes. The typical total acquisition time was 3 h per image set. Images were then processed using ImageJ software (1.54f, Wayne Rasband and contributors National Institutes of Health, USA) [30]. Raw sequences of ion images were extracted using the OpenMIMS plug-in (contribution of the National Institutes of Health and the National Institute of Biomedical Imaging and Bioengineering as a National Resource) [31]. Frame-to-frame alignment was carried out to compensate for slight shifts during acquisition, using the 12C14N− image as reference. Aligned sequences of images were subsequently summed to improve counting statistics.

The thickness of the radioactive source was measured on these images using the ImageJ software. To do this, 5 measurements were made on each image. The data were represented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Monte carlo simulation set-up

In this work, the simulations were performed with the GATE Monte Carlo simulation toolkit version 9.0 [32]. In order to achieve a realistic three-dimensional representation of the mouse eye in our simulations, we employed an STL (STereo-Lithography) file format derived from the MRI images to generate tessellated volumes within GATE. To this end, high-resolution MRI scans of the entire eye were first used to extract data, including overall eye volume, shape, diameter, and lens dimensions. For structures that are not easily visualized due to their thinness or complex morphology, such as the retina and RPE, tissue dimensions were derived with bibliographic survey of the real dimensions of the anatomical structures [33]. For instance, the crystalline lens was assumed to be analogous to a biconvex lens, with dimensions derived from MRI images. To facilitate the implementation of the RPE and retina, it was assumed that the thickness was constant at any point within the considered volume. Boolean operations were used to create the various layers and cavities of the mouse eye with a particular emphasis on the prevention of overlap and facilitating the construction of anatomically accurate tissue volumes. To illustrate this, the retina is represented by two distinct layers: the retina itself and the RPE. The following tissues densities and elemental compositions of the eye structure were considered in the simulations: 1.00 g/cm³ was assigned to the RPE, retina, and ciliary body, whereas the crystalline lens was modeled with a higher density of 1.07 g/cm³. The world volume and all remaining volumes were defined as water. A detailed list of the different structures’ used is provided in the supplementary data.

Source implementation

The activity maps were generated using a process analogous to the one used for defining the geometry. It was assumed that iodine-131 was uniformly distributed within the RPE. This hypothesis was validated through NanoSims imaging of iodine-127 as described above. Consequently, the RPE volume was considered as the main iodine radioactive source tissue. All simulations account for both β⁻ and γ emissions. The complete beta spectrum (ranging from 10 to 766 keV), as well as the discrete emissions characteristic of iodine-131—including internal conversion and Auger electrons—are included, in accordance with Medical Internal Radiation Dose (MIRD) nuclear data [19].

S value calculation

The simulations were conducted to calculate the S-values for a single eye, specifically S(Retina←RPE), S(RPE←RPE), and S(crystalline lens←RPE), by evaluating the energy deposited in the retina, RPE, and crystalline lens. After processing, GATE provided a voxelized map of the energy deposited over the entire organ of interest. These energy deposition maps were converted into absorbed dose by normalizing the deposited energy to the mass of the corresponding target tissue. The resulting absorbed dose was then normalized to the number of simulated nuclear disintegrations, allowing the derivation of S-values expressed in Gy per disintegration, which is equivalent to Gy/(Bq·s) in the MIRD formalism. A total of 5 × 106 particles for each eye structure were simulated to ensure a statistical uncertainty of less than 1%. Statistical uncertainties were estimated from the variance of the scored energy deposition provided by GATE and are reported as relative uncertainties.

Statistical analysis

All values are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed statistically using GraphPad Prism (version 10.3.1, GraphPad Software, Inc., San Diego, CA). For biodistribution studies, a non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test was performed, followed by a Dunn’s post hoc test. For tissue weights, statistical analysis was performed using the Mann–Whitney test and the p-values were considered statistically significant when < 0.05 (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

Results

The study combined experimental and computational approaches to quantify iodine-131 distribution and its dosimetric impact in ocular tissues (Fig. 1). SIMS imaging revealed iodine localization within melanin-rich structures, supporting source definition. Biodistribution studies with iodine-131 provided time-dependent activity data in ocular compartments. Monte Carlo simulations performed with GATE were used to calculate S-values, which were combined with biodistribution-derived cumulated activities using the MIRD formalism to estimate absorbed doses, thereby linking microscale distribution with macroscopic dosimetry.

Figure 2 shows SIMS imaging analysis of 12C14N−, providing a view of tissue’s fine structure allowing to identify the choroid, the retinal pigment epithelium layer (RPE) and the outer segment of the photoreceptors (Fig. 2a). The image of 31P16O− reflecting the phosphorus content of condensed chromatin near the nuclear envelope reveals the presence of cell nuclei (Fig. 2b). Intense 32S− spots correlate with melanin grains and potentially indicate the types of melanin (eumelanin, with weak sulfur content, or pheomelanin rich in sulfur) (Fig. 2c). In parallel, the image of 127I− provides the direct distribution of iodinated species across the section (Fig. 2d). Overlaying the 12C14N−, S⁻ and I⁻ signals revealed clear colocalization of the iodide signal with melanin polymers in the region of the RPE and the adjacent part of the choroid. Iodinated species were not detected in the retinal photoreceptor region. These results suggest that ICF01012 is mainly uptake by the RPE and fixed to melanin in the RPE.

Thereafter, in order to assess [¹²⁷I]-ICF01012’s biodistribution over time along the retinal pigment epithelium (Fig. 3), MRI imaging of the whole eye (Fig. 3a) was used to segment the different structures, i.e. the crystalline lens, cornea, iris + ciliary bodies, sclera + choroid + RPE and retina. SIMS images were taken in areas adjacent to or remote from the optic nerve at 1- and 72-hours post-injection (see Fig. 3b and i). The iodide signal fades when moving away from the optic nerve, similarly at 1 and 72-hours post-injection (Fig. 3c-e and g-i), however the technique does not allow quantification.

S value calculations for iodine-131 source

The sequence for implementing the mouse eye and determining the geometric factors is presented in Fig. 1. Ocular histological sections together with literature data define the relative organization of ocular tissues (Fig. 1a). MRI provided accurate geometrical dimensions of the whole eye, showing a major axis of 3.3 mm and a minor axis of 3.1 mm (Fig. 1b). These anatomical features were integrated into a three-dimensional computer-aided design software, FreeCAD. (Fig. 1c), resulting in a geometrically consistent eye model. GATE simulations based on this geometry enabled radiation transport and dose deposition in ocular tissues (Fig. 1d). The final output provided energy deposition maps showing the absorbed energy distribution in MeV in the retina (Fig. 1e).

The computation time to run a simulation was approximately 3 h per target. The calculated S-values and corresponding anatomical volumes are summarized in Table 1. As expected, the highest S-value was observed for the RPE itself (1.42 × 10− 2 Gy.MBq− 1.s− 1), consistent with the assumption of a uniform iodine-131 distribution within this structure. The neural retina, with a larger volume of 1.23 mm³ compared with the RPE, exhibited a lower S-value of 4.03 × 10− 3 Gy.MBq− 1.s− 1, reflecting the combined effects of energy deposition and spatial separation from the primary source. In contrast, the crystalline lens, located further from the RPE and with the largest volume (4.60 mm³), received the lowest dose per unit activity, with an S-value of 9.00 × 10− 5 Gy.MBq− 1.s− 1.

Dosimetry calculation

The RPE received the highest self-irradiation absorbed dose, with a value of 24.1 Gy.MBq⁻¹ (Table 4). The absorbed dose to the retina was 6.9 Gy.MBq⁻¹, calculated from contributions of both the RPE and the ciliary body. The crystalline lens showed a lower absorbed dose of 0.8 Gy.MBq⁻¹, resulting from cross-irradiation from the RPE and the ciliary body.

Discussion

This study provides a rigorous dosimetric evaluation of pigmented ocular components following the administration of [¹³¹I]ICF01012 in healthy mice. Importantly, the integration of multimodal imaging data and micrometric anatomical features into the modelling process enables a reliable reproduction of tissue volumes and absorbed dose distribution patterns within the eye.

A key strength of this work lies in the use of Monte Carlo simulations, which constitute an essential tool for accurately modelling energy deposition in small ocular tissues such as the retina and the RPE. While analytical approaches based on dose-point kernels (DPK) or voxel-dose kernels (VDK) using convolution are commonly implemented in dosimetry software, their applicability is limited in this context [34]. These methods typically assume spatial homogeneity and are therefore unable to account for the fine anatomical boundaries of the murine eye. In clinical practice, the limitation does not primarily arise from the kernel-based dose calculation itself, but rather from the spatial resolution of the activity maps used as input. When absorbed dose estimates are derived from activity maps obtained with imaging devices characterised by millimetric voxel sizes, pronounced partial volume effects arise at the submillimetric scale, particularly given the micrometric thickness of the retina. Using millimetric imaging voxels to estimate absorbed doses in micrometric structures such as the retina results in partial volume effects dominating and substantially compromising the validity of the dose estimates.

To overcome these limitations, we implemented a GATE Monte Carlo simulation framework that enabled anatomically faithful modelling of layered ocular tissue structures at the micrometric scale and ensured high-precision electron transport, which is particularly relevant given the medium-energy beta emissions of iodine-131.

Thus, in the present study, radioactivity quantification was performed using ex vivo organ counting, which - despite also subject to uncertainties -provides a more reliable estimation of activity in micro-structures. These challenges stem from the limited spatial resolution of preclinical SPECT systems and the pronounced partial volume effects, which are further exacerbated by the high-energy emissions of iodine-131. While ex vivo gamma counting is also subject to measurement uncertainties, it provides a more reliable estimation of activity in such small structures compared with imaging-based quantification [35].

To minimize underestimation of localized variations in dose deposition, high-resolution morphological data-combining MRI, NanoSIMS, autoradiography, and histology were incorporated into the initial implementation of the model. A single eye was used to define the geometric model in Monte Carlo simulations. Consequently, no cross-irradiation between the two eyes was considered. Although the limited variability in ocular mass observed across different time points supports the robustness of this approach, potential variations in regional shapes and tissue volumes may limit the broader generalizability of the model. It should be emphasized that the reported uncertainties primarily reflect the statistical precision of the Monte Carlo simulations, whereas residual uncertainties related to experimental measurements and biological variability may still affect the absolute absorbed dose estimates. To our knowledge, there is currently no reference model for this specific anatomical and dosimetric configuration. Previous studies performed on rabbit models have already highlighted differences in ocular dosimetry related to species [36], underlining the need for dosimetric models that are specific to the species, and finally to the patient.

In earlier work, we demonstrated in mice that the radiopharmaceutical [131I]ICF01012 exhibits a strong affinity for melanin, resulting in stronger retention in pigmented tissues compared with non-pigmented tissues [10, 28]. This binding behaviour suggests that the radiopharmaceutical uptake is closely related to melanin concentration. This further emphasises the need for species-specific, and ultimately, individualised dosimetric modelling approaches. In humans, melanin content can be evaluated using polarization-sensitive optical coherence tomography (PS-OCT) [37]. In addition, in our mouse model, approximately 76% of the ocular radioactivity was localized in the choroid/retina and 24% in the iris/ciliary body, which is consistent with the distribution of melanin in the human eye, where approximately 65% is located in the choroide/RPE and 35% in the iris/ciliary body [37, 38].

Furthermore, ocular morphology has a significant impact on the calculation of S-factors, which are central to internal dosimetry [19]. In humans, individualized anatomical parameters such as retinal thickness can be obtained from high-resolution ocular optical coherence tomography (OCT). In future clinical applications involving [¹³¹I]ICF01012 in humans, the integration of such morphological data would enable the construction of anatomically refined and patient-specific dosimetric models.

Overall, the present work demonstrates the feasibility and relevance of high-resolution Monte Carlo–based modelling for ocular dosimetry. In small-animal studies, such models represent an essential tool to refine therapeutic indices, such as tumor-to-retina absorbed dose ratios, which may be underestimated using conventional imaging-based approaches. By leveraging recent advances in computational power, this framework could, in future studies, be adapted to generate patient-specific predictive models. Importantly, while the present study establishes the methodological and computational foundations required for individualized dosimetry, it does not aim to demonstrate clinical individualization per se. Clinical translation would require the integration of patient-specific activity distributions, individualized ocular anatomy derived from clinical imaging, and validation against clinical toxicity or outcome data, which are beyond the scope of this preclinical work.

In the context of the ongoing clinical research involving [¹³¹I]ICF01012 in humans, the development of such anatomically and biologically informed dosimetric models appears essential for accurately comparing absorbed doses to the retina and to tumors, and for supporting individualized therapeutic strategies.

Conclusions

This study presents the first voxel-based dosimetry model of the mouse eye for [¹³¹I]ICF01012, enabling accurate dose estimates for sensitive structures such as the RPE and retina. Despite the limitations of our model, this work paves the way for improving therapeutic indices and ensuring the safe and effective translation of melanin-targeted radiotherapies.
