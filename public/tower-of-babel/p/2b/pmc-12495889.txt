What's in Your Soil? A Citywide Investigation of the Importance of Soil Lead for Predicting Elevated Blood Lead Levels in Chicago
Authors: Rome Thorstenson, James Montgomery, Christie Klimas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLead exposure remains a persistent environmental health threat. Soil contamination is recognized as an overlooked yet critical reservoir of childhood lead exposure due to a legacy of historical lead use in gasoline, paint, and industry. However, it is unclear whether measuring soil lead is an effective way to screen for risk at the community or neighborhood level, nor if soil lead is a significant predictor of elevated blood lead levels (EBLLs) beyond other socioeconomic and physical environment covariates. Building on prior soil sampling and conducting extensive citywide sampling and analysis, we assemble the largest data set of soil lead to date (n = 1,750) in Chicago. Combined with BLL data reported by the Chicago Department of Public Health (CDPH), municipal data, and census data, we investigated the association between soil lead concentrations, predicted BLLs from the EPA's Integrated Exposure Uptake Biokinetic (IEUBK) model, and EBLL from CDPH blood testing among children in Chicago at the community area scale. We present city‐scale soil lead and IEUBK risk maps for Chicago. Furthermore, while median household income remains the strongest single predictor of EBLL prevalence in our models, we provide evidence that soil lead independently contributes significant predictive power. Our findings position systematic soil monitoring as a practical tool for primary prevention, complementing existing prevention and intervention strategies and accelerating progress toward safer cities.

Introduction

Lead is a potent and irreversible neurotoxin, damaging the cardiovascular, nervous, renal, immune, reproductive, and cognitive systems (Leech et al., 2016; Sanders et al., 2009). Modeling by Lanphear et al. (2018) attributes roughly 412,000 annual deaths in the United States—and over 1 million worldwide (GBD 2017 Risk Factor Collaborators, 2018)—to lead exposure—comparable to tobacco smoke (O'Connor et al., 2018). Consequently, in 2024 the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) listed “protecting children from lead exposure” among its top priorities (EPA, 2024b), and the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) lowered its blood lead reference value to 3.5 μg/dL in October 2023 from 5 μg/dL (CDC, 2024).

Although bans on leaded paint (from 1978) and gasoline (phased out by 1986) reduced national exposure, soil—where gasoline‐ and paint‐derived lead accumulates and persists for centuries (Jacobs et al., 2002; Mielke, Laidlaw, & Gonzales, 2011)—acts as a pathway for exposure, particularly for children who ingest or inhale contaminated dust during outdoor play (Mielke, Gonzales, & Mielke, 2011; Needleman, 1998). Nationally, one in four U.S. homes is estimated to exceed recommended soil lead guidelines (Filippelli et al., 2024).

Soil lead mapping has been shown to be correlated with elevated blood lead levels (EBLLs) for many decades (M. A. Laidlaw, Mohmmad, et al., 2017; Mielke et al., 1989; Zahran, Mielke, et al., 2013), yet to the best of our knowledge no U.S. city has integrated systematic soil monitoring into routine childhood‐lead‐poisoning prevention programs. Public‐health practice remains largely reactive: most jurisdictions screen blood after exposure. This intervention often excludes identification and remediation of sources and misses asymptomatic children (Ettinger et al., 2019). There are some exceptions. For example, the municipal government of Rochester, NY mandates periodic lead hazard inspections of residential properties (Korfmacher et al., 2012). However, such preventative interventions remain uncommon and don't screen for soil lead. A proactive alternative is to monitor environmental lead reservoirs and predict where children are likely to experience EBLLs. To this end, spatial mapping is a critical tool as it facilitates the identification of exposure hotspots and informs targeted interventions.

Because Chicago combines extensive pre‐1978 housing stock with a history of heavy industry, soil lead levels vary dramatically across its 77 community areas. Investigations of lead contamination and exposure in Chicago have been conducted through the lens of the natural, social, and health sciences. Previous studies germane to our work have focused on one of two approaches: (a) measuring and/or mapping spatial patterns of EBLLs in children and assessing the relationship between EBLLs and health disparities (Evens et al., 2015; Lee & Hu, 2024; Oyana & Margai, 2010; Sachs, 1974; Sampson & Winter 2016; Tang & Carrel, 2022; White & Gala, 2022), or (b) measuring and/or mapping spatial patterns of total lead concentrations in Chicago soils (Finster et al., 2004; Hernandez Gonzalez et al., 2019; Khan et al., 1973; LaBelle et al., 1987; Lippert et al., 2020; Montgomery et al., 2016; Shinn et al., 2000; Watson et al., 2022; Witzling et al., 2010). We have found no studies centered on Chicago that combined both approaches.

This study addresses that gap by quantitatively translating Chicago's soil lead landscape into modeled child blood lead risk. We build on two lines of work that, to date, have remained separate. First, the EPA's Integrated Exposure Uptake Biokinetic (IEUBK) model converts environmental lead inputs—including soil—into predicted blood lead distributions for children under 7 years of age (EPA, 2021). While originally developed for high‐risk “Superfund” sites, it has been applied to urban areas (EPA, 2024a). For example, M. A. Laidlaw, Mohmmad, et al. (2017) analyzed 341 samples across Sydney, Australia and demonstrated that the predicted BLLs across the city were consistent with previous BLL testing.

Second, statistical risk‐screening tools combine sociodemographic proxies (e.g., housing age, poverty) with other lead sources and blood‐screening data to flag high‐risk areas. For example, in Chicago Potash et al. (2020) developed a random forest machine learning model using past BLLs, building characteristics, sociodemographic data, etc. to forecast risk, which was then used for rank‐order proactive intervention. However, their model does not include soil lead despite the compelling evidence that soil predicts BLL (M. A. Laidlaw et al., 2005; M. A. Laidlaw, Mohmmad, et al., 2017; Zahran, Mielke, et al., 2013, Zahran, Laidlaw, et al., 2013), and the work by Mielke et al. (2019). More evidence comes from the natural experiment of hurricane Katrina: it caused an approximately 44% decrease in soil lead which was associated with a 64% decrease in EBLLs. Soil lead is broadly underappreciated (Mielke et al., 2019, 2022). Furthermore, no U.S. city has deployed soil measurements with the IEUBK model at metropolitan scale and linked its predictions to routine EBLL monitoring and intervention.

We tested whether the inclusion of soil lead levels or the IEUBK‐predicted risk by community area (CA) improved the prediction of EBLLs in Chicago relative to models that did not include either of these lead‐related predictor variables. Based on our findings, we provide a city‐scale risk map and developed the case for integrating soil‐based monitoring and intervention into routine public health practice. Specifically, we:expanded the Watson et al. (2022) data set with the What's in Your Soil (WIYS) geocoded soil samples for the largest soil lead study of Chicago to date (n = 1,750),ran IEUBK on all soil data to obtain sample‐level expected BLL and probability of exceeding the Chicago and national EBLL risk thresholds, andregressed observed EBLL incidence from the Chicago Department of Public Health (CDPH) on these IEUBK outputs while controlling for median household income, housing age, industrial corridor proximity, and other relevant covariates.

This study brings together environmental modeling and public health surveillance to bridge a critical gap in lead exposure prevention. By quantifying the impact of soil lead using the EPA's IEUBK model and real‐world blood lead data, we offer a framework for proactively identifying communities at greatest risk. In doing so, we demonstrate how an overlooked source of lead poisoning risk can inform targeted, preventive interventions before children are exposed. More broadly, this work contributes to the growing effort to shift lead poisoning prevention from a reactive paradigm to a proactive one.

Materials and Methods

Soil Processing

The What's In Your Soil? Project (WIYS) was launched in 2011, with its sole focus on measuring and mapping soil lead concentrations in a portion of the Lincoln Park community area, one of 77 Chicago Community Areas (CAs). Since its inauguration, the objectives and scope of WIYS have expanded to provide free soil lead testing for homeowners in other CAs. In 2019, we conducted community‐based lead education health fairs at six Chicago Metropolitan YMCAs (Lippert et al., 2020). The health fairs used the SoilSHOP (Soil Screening, Health, Outreach, and Partnership, ATSDR) protocol (ATSDR, 2021). SoilSHOP allows people an opportunity to have their soil screened for heavy metals, particularly lead. Participants also receive information on lead contamination and ways to mitigate their exposure. We invited community members to bring soil samples from their yard, garden, and/or parkway for on‐site lead measurement using a handheld Niton XL2‐Plus portable X‐ray fluorescence (pXRF) analyzer (Thermo Scientific. Waltham, MA). We sent them instructions on how to collect and store their samples. They collected soil samples to 0–15 cm with a garden trowel. Each sample was a composite of 3–4 subsamples collected within the proximity of a central point at the sample location. The composite sample was sealed in a Ziploc bag and labeled with the street address or latitude/longitude coordinates. After scanning, community members received a scorecard with their lead results and literature on how to mitigate their exposure to lead dust. These soil samples were transported to the Environmental Soil Science Laboratory at DePaul University where roots, vegetation, and anthropogenic debris were removed. The samples were dried at 105°C in a convection oven for 24 hr, ground using a rolling pin, and passed through a #10 sieve to collect the fine‐earth (<2.00 mm) fraction. A subset of these samples was thoroughly mixed and then pulverized for 3 min in a Spex CertiPrep (Afton, OK) 8,000 m Mixer/Mill. The pulverized samples were passed through a #325 sieve (270 μm) and then packed into plastic cups and covered with a Prolene thinfilm for sample containment. The samples were scanned for 60 s with the pXRF. In the years since we conducted the lead health fairs, undergraduate students, Chicago Public School middle‐grade and high school students, and members of a community organization (Garcia, 2022), have collected soil samples from yards, gardens, and parkways following the lead health fair collection protocol. To date we have measured and mapped soil lead concentrations in 1,147 soil samples collected from 47 of the 77 CAs.

A National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) standard reference soil (SRM) (2711a), and two internal soil standards collected from yards in CAs were analyzed in each batch run for quality control purposes. The standards were sent to Stat Analysis Testing Lab (SATL), an EPA‐certified laboratory in Chicago, where they were digested in reagent‐grade nitric acid and analyzed using inductively‐coupled plasma‐mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS). The certified range of mass fraction values for the NIST 2711 soil is 1,100–1,400 mg/kg, with a median of 1,300 mg/kg. The lead concentration of the SRM reported by SATL was 1,300 mg/kg. We performed 285 pXRF scans of NIST 2711a. Concentrations ranged from 1,125 to 1,408 mg/kg., with an average of 1,314 mg/kg and a median of 1,316 mg/kg. These concentrations were within 100% ± 8% of the NIST certified median concentration. The pXRF concentrations of the two internal soil standards were within 100% ± 10% of concentrations reported by SATL.

Data Sources

Various factors have been found in previous research to correspond with high BLLs, including soil (M. A. Laidlaw et al., 2005; M. A. Laidlaw, Mohmmad, et al., 2017; Mielke & Egendorf, 2023; Zahran, Laidlaw, et al., 2013). We combine data from our own sampling, the work of other researchers, Chicago government databases, and census data for our models.

We were given permission to use the Watson et al. (2022) soil lead data set to augment our spatial mapping and IEUBK modeling. They collected and analyzed soil lead concentrations at 995 parkway locations and 156 residential locations, including lawns and gardens, in Chicago and suburban Cook County. We included the subset of these within city boundaries.

Numerous epidemiologic studies demonstrate that children living near lead‐emitting industries carry substantially higher blood‐lead levels than those farther away (Diawara et al., 2018; EPA, 2024a). Chicago's designated industrial corridors concentrate current and legacy metal‐emitting activities: smelters, foundries, rail yards, and scrap‐metal recyclers, etc. Urban parks, meanwhile, are high‐contact environments: children spend disproportionate outdoor time there, often on uncovered soil. Surveys across multiple U.S. cities, including South Philadelphia, document park soil lead frequently exceeding screening levels and show positive covariance between EBLL hotspots and lead‐rich park soils (Gandolff, 2023). To better understand whether these variables were significant when modeling EBLL risk, we obtained Chicago's CA boundaries from the City of Chicago Data Portal (CDP, 2010, 2025) and overlaid them with industrial corridor and park regions from the same source to identify the proportion of total such land use (CDP, 2024a, 2024b). Due to the importance of these types of land use, we include variables indicating the percent bare soil, tree canopy, industrial corridors, and parks for each CA in our analysis.

Children living with families below the poverty line carry significantly higher blood lead burdens than affluent peers even after race/ethnicity adjustment (EPA, 2024a, 2024b, 2024c, 2024d, 2024e), and neuro‐imaging evidence indicates that the cognitive penalties of residence in high‐lead‐ risk tracts are concentrated among low‐income children (Marshall et al., 2020). Renter occupancy further signals risk: rental units are far more likely to contain deteriorated lead‐based paint, lead‐laden dust, and contaminated yard soils because they tend to be older and receive less maintenance (Jacobs et al., 2002). To capture socioeconomic variation, we aggregated census tract‐level median household income (Med. Inc. 2000) and renter occupancy from the 2000 Decennial Census (Renters 2000) and the 2011–2015 American Community Survey (Med. Inc. 2015; Renters 2015) to the 77 CAs (U.S. Census Bureau, 2000a, 2000b, 2015a, 2015b).

We characterized environmental factors by calculating the percent of each CA designed as covered by tree canopy (Tree Canopy) and bare soil (Bare Soil), which we retrieved from the EPA's EnviroAtlas (EPA, 2019). Extensive bare soil heightens risk by increasing direct ingestion and wind‐driven resuspension of lead‐laden dust, especially under dry, windy conditions (CDC, 2022; M. A. Laidlaw & Filippelli, 2008). On the other hand—though there is little direct evidence in the literature—we hypothesize tree cover may be a predictor because it may interfere with the resuspension of soil lead and has been shown to decrease particulate matter from traffic sources (Maher et al., 2013). Urban canopy is sufficiently inert for 2019 conditions to approximate those prevailing throughout our 1995–2015 study window: a U.S. Forest Service inventory of 20 cities found a net loss of just 1% of canopy over 5 years (Nowak & Greenfield, 2018).

Finally, we acquired counts of children tested and children with BLL exceeding the threshold of 6 μg/dL for every census tract and year from 1995 to 2015 by request from the CDPH Office of Epidemiology (2025). We assume that soil lead concentrations (in ppm) remained constant from 1995 to 2015, which is reasonable given work on the persistence of lead in soils (Datko‐Williams et al., 2014; M. A. Laidlaw et al., 2005). CDPH‐ reported EBLLs were averaged over six‐year windows, from 1995 to 2000 and from 2010 to 2015, then aggregated from census tract to CA scale. CAs—visualized in Figure 1—are official Chicago divisions that rarely, and only minimally, split census tracts. Soil samples were aggregated to CAs. Nearly all CAs were represented: 68 had five or more soil samples, while 74 had three or more samples.

These maps illustrate the proportion of land area within each Chicago community area (CA) that is (a) covered by bare soil, (b) designated as industrial corridors (Ind. Corr.), (c) allocated to public parks, or (d) covered by tree canopy. In addition, household median income (Med. Inc.) (e, g) and the proportion of CA residents renting (f, h) are presented for two periods: 2000 and 2015.

Some variables, such as building age, we would have liked to include but could not due to data limitations and sampling methodology. We elaborate in the limitations section.

IEUBK Model

The IEUBK model was introduced by the EPA in 1994. It predicts BLLs from various sources and the probability of exceeding a risk threshold for children under the age of seven. Data sources include: soil, indoor dust, air, water, and food; however, the model can be run, as we do, with only soil measurements and use soil‐derivative (e.g., indoor dust) or default values for other fields. Numerous studies establish a clear correlation between direct soil lead measurements and EBLL (M. A. Laidlaw et al., 2012; M. A. Laidlaw, Mohmmad, et al., 2017; Mielke et al., 1989, 2019; Zahran, Mielke, et al., 2013). We use version 2.0.

Data Analysis

The data was analyzed using R (version 4.3.2) and RStudio (R Core Team, 2021; RStudio Team, 2024). We first used the packages fitdistrplus and nortest to determine the appropriate distributions for modeling elevated blood lead levels in the 1995 to 2000 and 2010 to 2015 time periods (Delignette‐Muller & Dutang, 2015; Gross & Ligges, 2015). While EBLL from 1995 to 2000 followed a normal distribution, EBLL from 2010 to 2015 were first log transformed. We used generalized linear models (GLMs) (McCullagh & Nelder, 1989), with gaussian distribution to model elevated blood lead levels as a function of multiple predictor variables. Since soil lead (ppm) and the IEUBK‐predicted probability of having BLL above the CDPH threshold of concern were highly correlated (Pearson R = 0.96), two sets of GLMs were run: the first using soil lead, and the second using IEUBK‐predicted probability of EBLL risk. Additional predictor variables included: (a) median income in 2000 and 2015; (b) the proportion of CA residents renting in 2000 and 2015; the proportion of (c) bare soil; (d) tree canopy; (e) parks; and (f) industrial corridors as a percent of total land in the CA. Additional R packages used in this analysis include: sf, tidyverse, patchwork, and stargazer (Hlavac, 2022; Pebesma & Bivand, 2023; Pedersen, 2024; Wickham et al., 2019).

We hypothesized two relationships: first, that soil lead concentrations would be significant in predicting areas of concern for high BLLs as quantified by CDPH‐reported EBLL (H 1); and second, that the IEUBK‐predicted EBLL would show a positive and significant relationship with CDPH‐reported EBLL (H 2), even controlling for socioeconomic and physical environment factors, such that it could be used for the same purpose.

While the IEUBK model used default values aside from soil lead data, we expected the transform to be informative because it is non‐linear and converts the soil lead (ppm) into expected BLL (μg/dL).

Artificial intelligence tools in the form of large language models were used to assist with this work, including for code writing, finding relevant papers, transferring citations between formats, and proofing (Google, 2025; OpenAI, 2023).

Results and Discussion

By combining the Watson et al. and WIYS data sets, we present the most extensive lead soil sampling project in Chicago to date and reaffirm that lead concentrations in soil remain high throughout the city. Across 1,750 soil samples, the city‐wide median lead concentration (Soil Lead) was 217 ppm with an interquartile range of 124–395 ppm; only 1.26% of observations fell below Illinois geogenic background of 25 ppm (EPA, 2024c). Under the EPA's 2024 recommended screening level for residential soil (200 ppm, or 100 ppm when multiple sources of lead are present), 54.00% of samples warrant remediation and 24.51% still exceed the retired 400 ppm standard (EPA, 2024). Aggregating by CA, the median rises to 327 ppm and the IQR is 234–443 ppm. Watson et al. (2022) reported similar findings (mean 282 ppm for parkways and 352 ppm for residential samples) on their subset of this data. This is typical of large urban environments; Mielke, Gonzales, and Mielke (2011) reported median soil lead from 500 to 1,000 ppm in some neighborhoods of New Orleans and M. A. Laidlaw, Mohmmad, et al. (2017) report a 133 ppm mean and 61 ppm median in Sydney, Australia—but the range spans orders of magnitude across the United States and world (Datko‐Williams et al., 2014; Filippelli et al., 2018; M. A. Laidlaw, Mohmmad, et al., 2017). Meanwhile, the CDPH data describes a decrease in observed BLLs over time (CDPH year EBLL), where the percent of children above the threshold of 6 μg/dL—nearly double the current CDC threshold of 3.5 μg/dL—falls from a median of 65.22% in 1995 to 15.95% in 2005 to 1.75% by 2015. This aligns closely with Mielke et al. (2019); they reported an average of 3.6 μg/dL in 2000 and 1.2 μg/dL by 2016. See Table 1 for more details.

Table 1 shows that model‐predicted BLLs (IEUBK Pred. BLL) are both elevated and highly skewed. At the individual‐sample scale (n = 1,750), the median prediction is 2.62 μg/dL (IQR = 1.96–3.82 μg/dL), beneath the current CDC reference value of 3.5 μg/dL, but a long upper tail extends to a maximum of 26.08 μg/dL. The median probability that a child exceeds that threshold (IEUBK P(BLL > 3.5 μg/dL)) is 26.9%, and the upper quartile surpasses 57%. When we aggregate to CAs, the median IEUBK‐predicted BLL is 3.28 μg/dL and median P(BLL > 3.5 μg/dL) = 38.7%. As we are working under the justified assumption that soil lead concentration is static over time, these numbers are relevant and applicable today. That is to say on average 38% of current children by CA are expected to have EBLLs based on soil alone, demonstrating soil lead is relevant threat to childhood health across the city.

Taken together, the IEUBK outputs quantify substantial lead‐exposure risk, comparable to that of M. A. Laidlaw, Mohmmad, et al. (2017) in Sydney. These predictions underestimate the CDPH data (reported as CDPH year EBLL elsewhere in Table 1) in the 1995–2000 period and overestimate them in 2010–2015. For the underestimation, in the earlier period there were other lead exposure pathways (e.g., peeling paint) in addition to soil, which have since decreased due to renovations, construction, and intervention, specifically enhanced screening of children for lead poisoning and more extensive lead education outreach efforts. On the other hand, for the overestimation in the 2010–2015 period, there may be limited child play in the soil areas where the sampling took place. This data set has much variability in soil sampling location (e.g., parkway, yard, raised bed, etc.), which has been demonstrated to be relevant to soil lead concentration (Zahran, Mielke, et al., 2013). Note also that nearly all soil sampling was conducted after 2015 and therefore possibly underestimating soil lead exposure—as soil lead, if not constant as we assume, more likely falls than rises over time—for both the 1995–2000 and 2010–2015 periods.

It has been observed numerous times that soil lead levels decrease from the city center to suburban and exurban regions, including in Chicago (M. A. Laidlaw, Mohmmad, et al., 2017; Watson et al., 2022). We observe this to a limited extent in our sampling (Figure 2a), and less so in the CDPH‐reported EBLL (Figures 3a and 3b). Rather, we identify lead hot spots on the west and south sides of the city. This roughly aligns with industrial corridors and marginalized communities (Figure 1) (Chicago Health Atlas, 2025), affirming the work by Sampson and Winter (2016, 2018) also conducted in Chicago, as well as numerous other studies which position lead as a persistent and critical environmental justice issue (Egendorf et al., 2021; M. Laidlaw et al., 2023; Leech et al., 2016; Tang & Carrel, 2022; Whitehead & Buchanan, 2019; White & Gala, 2022).

How Soil and IEUBK Predictions Relate to CDPH‐Reported EBLL

Aside from accounting for the complex relationship between soil and BLL, the advantage of using the IEUBK model is to easily convert measured soil lead to modeled BLL. The EPA provides a reference for sites being considered for remediation under the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act (CERCLA, also called Superfund), which is 200 ppm or 100 ppm when multiple sources of lead are present. We use this as a reference broadly, as have others (Filippelli et al., 2024). Mean‐ while the CDC sets a reference value for BLL separately (currently 3.5 μg/dL). Therefore, it is not obvious how a municipal health department should set their own tolerances, especially given empirical factors (i.e., EBLL rates) and historical legacies of lead.

To understand how closely the EBLL observed by the CDPH aligned with soil lead and IEUBK predictions based exclusively on soil, we used a linear regression to compare them across two periods in Figure 4. During 1995–2000, we found that soil lead explained 11.7% of the variance in CDPH‐reported EBLL (slope = 0.038 μg/dL‐ppm, p = 0.0043); IEUBK predictions explained 12.3% of the variance in CDPH‐reported EBLL (slope = 0.659 (unitless), p = 0.0033). Predictive power declined in 2010–2015 because a greater proportion of the CDPH samples fall below 6 μg/dL—again, far greater than the current CDC threshold of 3.5 μg/dL—yet the correlation remains significant (slope = 0.004 μg/dL‐ppm, p = 0.03 for soil and slope = 0.068, p = 0.035 for IEUBK). For reference, Zahran, Mielke, et al. (2013) achieved 76% variation at the census tract level in New Orleans; Bradham et al. (2017) account for 25% in Philadelphia at the household level; and Hill et al. (2025) accounted for 9.6% of the variance in Syracuse, NY.

We used linear regressions to model Chicago Department of Public Health (CDPH)‐reported elevated blood lead levels (EBLL) using two predictors separately: soil lead (a) and the soil‐based Integrated Exposure Uptake Biokinetic (IEUBK) model (b). During the 1995–2000 period, both soil lead (slope = 0.038 μg/dL‐ppm) and the IEUBK model (slope = 0.659) were highly significant predictors (p < 0.01), but the predictive power diminished in the 2010–2015 period (slope = 0.004 μg/dL‐ppm for soil and slope = 0.068 for IEUBK), still significant but at a lower level of significance for both (p < 0.05). Although the data from the second period was lognormally distributed, we used linear regression in both analyses for visual consistency.

Modeling Elevated Blood Lead Levels

For 1995–2000, while both soil lead (Table 2) and IEUBK predictions based exclusively on soil (Table 3) were significant in predicting CDPH‐reported EBLL rates in some of the generalized linear models (GLMs) (models 1 and 5 for soil lead; and model 1 for IEUBK predictions), the AIC was comparable for GLMs using median income without either lead‐informed variable (Tables 2 and 3, models 2 and 4). Indeed, the best‐fit model included only median income as a significant predictor of CDPH‐reported EBLL rates for the 1995–2000 period (Tables 2 and 3, model 2). Median income has been demonstrated to be a powerful predictor of EBLL as it is a proxy for multiple lead exposure paths, including soil, paint, and water (via pipes) (Egan et al., 2021; Hauptman et al., 2023).

Soil lead. In 1995–2000 a 100‐ppm increase in median soil lead at the CA level predicted a 3.8% rise in EBLL prevalence (p < 0.01; Table 2, model 1). After adjusting for income, renting, and land cover, the marginal effect shrank to 2.4% and was borderline significant (p < 0.1; Table 2, model 5). In 2010–2015, the marginal effect fell to 0.10%, yet both soil lead and the IEUBK forecast remained moderately significant (p < 0.05; Tables 4 and 5, model 5), indicating soil continues to explain risk of EBLL.

Our dependent variable is the EBLL rate as opposed to BLL in μg/dL, which other work has not addressed. In terms of soil predicting BLL, M. A. Laidlaw, Mohmmad, et al. (2017) conduct a survey of literature which generally report an increase of 1 μg/dL for roughly every 50–100 ppm increase in soil lead. Hill et al. (2025) reports a 0.25 μg/dL increase in BLL for every 100 ppm in soil lead.

Income and renting. Median household income was a highly significant predictor of EBLL in both periods (p < 0.01). Lower median income made EBLL more likely, consistent with the entrenched socioeconomic inequity of lead exposure (Sampson & Winter, 2016, 2018; Tang & Carrel, 2022; White & Gala, 2022). In Chicago, these disparities are amplified by the city's history of redlining and residential segregation, and by the persistent correlation between lower income and higher proportions of minority residents—conditions that have placed Black communities at particularly elevated risk (Egendorf et al., 2021; Intrator et al., 2016; Whitehead & Buchanan, 2019). Meanwhile renter occupancy was never significant, likely due to its high collinearity with income (Pearson correlation of −0.63 in 2000 and −0.57 in 2015).

Tree canopy and parks. The portion of CA land classified as under tree canopy was not significant in the earlier period, but it was highly significant (p < 0.01) in the later period. We hypothesized trees may play a role in atmospheric lead due to work demonstrating the canopy can intercept particulate matter, including lead dust, to decrease risk (Yuniati et al., 2024). However, the coefficient is positive: more trees are associated with greater risk. It does not follow that high tree cover is associated with mature trees and older buildings, and the legacy effects thereof, as there is near‐zero Pearson correlation of 0.02 between building age and tree cover. Instead, trees may act as a repository of soil lead. Atmospheric concentrations of lead has been linked to EBLLs (M. A. Laidlaw et al., 2012; Zahran, Laidlaw, et al., 2013); trees may act as windbreaks, collecting dust on leaves and increasing exposure risk.

Parks are also significant in CAs, but only in 2010–2015 (Tables 4 and 5, p < 0.05 for soil and IEUBK in model 4 and p < 0.1 for both in model 5). They are negatively correlated with EBLL. We speculate that when parks are readily available, children are less inclined to play in bare soil or parkways, decreasing risk. Previous work has linked elevated soil lead in parks to EBLLs (Hung et al., 2021), though Elnajdi et al. (2023) found limited relationships. Due to the high spatial variability in soil lead, further work is needed to situate park lead exposure in conversation with residential lead exposure.

Model fit. Models that use soil lead alone provide a slightly better fit than models substituting the IEUBK estimate, but the difference is almost negligible (compare model 5 across Tables 2–5). Prior work validating the IEUBK model has not investigated the CA scale, but found similar results at the census tract and child level (Birch et al., 2023; Brown et al., 2023). Altogether, the conversion remains valuable for translating soil lead units (ppm) into a health‐centric metric (μg/dL).

In the earlier period, the best models (by AIC) used median income and renters without soil or other independent variables (Tables 2 and 3, model 2), which has consistently been a dominant predictor of EBLL and negative health outcomes (Marshall et al., 2020). However, the lead exposure landscape has changed drastically since then (Wheeler et al., 2022). In the later period, the best models are those with all regressors, where adding soil or IEUBK‐predicted EBLL rate both improve AIC. This aligns with recent modeling efforts in the literature which include independent variables pertaining to sociodemographics and living conditions (Hill et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2020). An exception is Obeng‐Gyasi et al. (2021), which found soil lead and median income to be correlated but not significant in their census tract‐level models.

These findings support both hypotheses (H 1 and H 2) at the CA scale. Soil lead concentration was a significant predictor of EBLL even controlling for various socioeconomic and physical environment features, as was the IEUBK model's predictions.

Limitations and Future Work

This study has several limitations. First, while the most recent CDPH‐reported EBLL rates in Table 1 (CDPH 2015 EBLL) look promising—at least one quarter of CAs have no children above the risk threshold, and the median is 1.75% of children above the threshold—we emphasize the threshold used in this study (due to available data) is 6 μg/dL, almost double the current threshold of 3.5 μg/dL. Second, we do not explicitly account for variability in bio‐accessibility of lead in soil samples, which can significantly influence the relationship between soil lead and BLL (Yan et al., 2017). Also, the IEUBK model has upper limits of applicability in terms of soil lead concentrations, which a small number of samples surpass. Finally, our approach does not differentiate between distinct sources of lead in soil, such as distinguishing between contamination primarily from paint versus gasoline emissions. Due to heterogeneous data collection methods, we cannot address this by, say, controlling for sampling location (e.g., residential, parkway, etc.).

Sampling was constrained geospatially by practical considerations and available data, which may introduce bias and limit how well these findings generalize. While we considered investigating our hypotheses at the census tract level, our precision of sampling lacked sufficient density and breadth. Also, selection bias was relevant for a large number of samples (e.g., health fairs, outreach). Individuals may be concerned that learning about high soil lead may necessitate costly remediation or that they may be obliged to report soil lead when reselling their properties, decreasing property value. A further limitation of utilizing a community‐based sampling approach, such as was used for the lead health fairs, is that some community members may not have carefully followed the soil collection protocol, thus resulting in “uncontrolled inconsistency” (cf. Filippelli and Taylor (2018)) in the sample pool and some variability in lead levels. See Fawkes et al. (2024) for another example of citizen‐based science for heavy metal soil sampling.

Building age is often strongly correlated with soil lead because homes built or renovated before 1978 commonly used lead‐based paint (Schwarz et al., 2012). It can also act as a proxy for other lead pathways, such as lead pipes. To include building ages, we geocoded soil samples lacking building address using the reported latitude‐longitude pairs with the U.S. Census Bureau Geocoder (U.S. Census Bureau, 2025), retrieved building ages from the Cook County Government (2022) Assessor's Office and used the building nearest the sample within at most 25 m (selected due to average lot size and sample radius (Chicago Department of Transportation, 2007)). In some cases we were able to associate building ages with nearest buildings, but due to the nature of community‐ based research and random sampling from parkways, parks, etc. too many samples were not associated with a residential lot to warrant inclusion in the GLMs. Therefore, we plot a linear regression in Figure 5 between age and CDPH‐reported EBLL. We observe that the slope decreases significantly from the first to the second period (p < 0.05 for difference in slope, each individually p < 0.01), indicating building age has become less relevant over time. The correlation of soil lead and building age is 0.42.

Chicago Department of Public Health‐reported elevated blood lead levels as a function of building age changes from 1995 to 2000 (0.25 μg/dL‐year, p < 0.001) to 2010–2015 (0.04 μg/dL‐year, p < 0.001), suggesting lead intervention for household sources of lead have had a meaningful impact relative at the 6 μg/dL threshold.

Other researchers have developed machine learning models involving soil lead. Obeng‐Gyasi et al. (2021) predict the likelihood of soil lead above a threshold based on related geospatial variables, providing a way to scale spatial understanding of soil lead with less sampling. Hill et al. (2025) also describes a method for interpolating soil lead to a resolution of 50 m intervals. Increased sampling or modeling to increase data resolution may be critical due to the high variability of soil lead at small spatial scales due to extremely local sources (e.g., a peeling windowsill) that vary across or even within individual properties (Schwarz et al., 2016).

Future work incorporating building ages into ours models would be a promising next step. Similarly, researchers could add soil to the Potash et al. (2015, 2020) random forest model and evaluating whether it improves performance. In general, incorporating soil data into current primary prevent and predictive modeling workflows and testing the significance of their contribution would further develop the conversation around using soil for primary prevention.

Conclusion

This work emphasizes the power of systematic soil lead measurement for combating childhood lead poisoning, moving from reactive, after‐the‐fact interventions to proactive, preventative measures. Our analysis revealed that using an estimate of soil lead (either soil lead or IEUBK‐predicted probability of having BLL above the CDPH threshold of concern) was significant in identifying community areas with EBLLs in children, even when accounting for socioeconomic predictors such as median household income.

While ongoing efforts have largely reduced lead exposure from other sources, soil has emerged as a persistent threat, demanding its inclusion in municipal public health strategies (Filippelli et al., 2018; M. A. Laidlaw, Filippelli et al., 2017; Mielke & Egendorf, 2023; Wheeler et al., 2022).

Across 1,750 surface‐soil samples, more than half exceeded the EPA's 200 ppm recommended screening level, and community‐area medians routinely surpassed 300 ppm. Translating soil to BLL risk via the IEUBK revealed 27% of children were predicted to exceed the current CDC reference value of 3.5 μg/dL, with hotspots above 57%. Although our study acknowledges some limitations, the findings demonstrate that soil lead and IEUBK‐derived EBLL risk calculated from soil lead are powerful predictors of EBLL even controlling for various socioeconomic and physical environment factors.

Our findings therefore reframe soil as a critical residual pathway sustaining Chicago's lead burden and delineate the limits of relying on reactive, child‐blood surveillance alone. While median household income captures socioeconomic vulnerability, incorporating soil lead raises explanatory power and, more importantly, identifies a tractable environmental lever. Because soil testing is inexpensive, non‐invasive, and amenable to high‐resolution GIS analysis, it offers municipalities a primary‐prevention tool that can be deployed at scale without using children as “canaries in a coal mine” (M. A. Laidlaw, Mohmmad, et al., 2017).

As we continue to decrease blood lead in children—for which there is no safe level—we will need to address all sources of lead exposure. This work advances the case for giving soil lead its due by quantifying it with the IEUBK model and integrating soil‐based monitoring and intervention into routine public health practice.
