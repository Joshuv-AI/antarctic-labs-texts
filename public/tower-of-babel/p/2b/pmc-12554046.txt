Cumulative weight–based rabbit antithymocyte globulin dosing for induction in heart transplantation: Impact on outcomes
Authors: Emmanuel Bebawi, Pierre-Emmanuel Noly, Marie-Claude Parent, Annik Fortier, Simon de Denus, Maxime Tremblay-Gravel, Jacinthe Boulet, Winnie Tran, Geneviève Giraldeau, Yoan Lamarche, Anique Ducharme
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOptimal dose of rabbit antithymocyte globulin (rATG) for heart transplantation (HTx) induction remains uncertain. We conducted a retrospective analysis to assess the effect of cumulative rATG weight–based dosing in HTx induction.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed a total of 340 primary HTx patients (1983-2018) receiving a fixed rATG dose (125 mg daily for 3 days). Patients were retrospectively categorized based on cumulative rATG exposition: group A (<4.5 mg/kg), group B (4.5-7.5 mg/kg), and group C (>7.5 mg/kg). The primary composite outcome (death, acute rejection, and infection) and its components were evaluated at 24 months using absolute incidence, Kaplan-Meier survival curves, and Cox modeling.ResultsGroup A had a non–significant lower incidence of the primary composite end-point (A: 76.8%, B: 83.7%, C: 96.2%; p = 0.075), primarily driven by lower infections, with group B having significantly better survival for the primary composite end-point than group C on Kaplan-Meier curves (B vs C log-rank p = 0.044). Group B had lower mortality than group C (p = 0.012). Lower incidence of infection in group A compared to group B (p = 0.035) was also noted. No significant difference was observed in acute rejection episodes.ConclusionsAt 24 months post-HTx, our findings suggest that a cumulative rATG dosing of 4.5 to 7.5 mg/kg offers the best balance of AR, mortality, and infection, mainly by reducing the risk of infections and to a lesser degree mortality, without increasing AR. A cumulative dose of <4.5 mg/kg offers the lowest risk of infection, with 4.5 mg/kg likely representing the intersection dose.KEYWORDS: heart transplant, immunosuppression, induction therapy, rabbit antithymocyte globulins, heart failure

Background

Heart transplantation (HTx) remains the preferred treatment for advanced heart failure,1 with 1-year survival now approaching 95%.2, 3 Allograft rejection remains a challenge, often requiring induction and maintenance immunosuppression for prevention; however, half of the programs worldwide do not use induction therapy,4 as true survival impact remains controversial.4 Acute rejection (AR) after HTx typically occurs between 3 and 6 months, but can develop as early as 24 hours post-HTx with risk gradually subsiding over time.5 Supporters argue that induction therapy reduces early AR and delays nephrotoxic immunosuppressive drug introduction early post-HTx.4 Opponents raise concerns about adverse effects such as infections and malignancies, possibly outweighing potential long-term survival benefits.4, 6 Supported by limited data, the most recent International Society for Heart and Lung Transplantation (ISHLT) guidelines indicate that rabbit antithymocyte globulin (rATG) induction may be beneficial in patients at high risk for AR.7

Despite ongoing debate, there are limited data on optimal rATG dosage and regimen, resulting in various protocols worldwide.7, 8 Our center has used a fixed rATG induction dose regardless of weight. We aimed to re-evaluate this practice by retrospectively evaluating the cumulative weight–based rATG dosing impact in HTx induction, hypothesizing that lower cumulative dosing would balance mortality, infections, and AR.

Materials and methods

Study design

We retrospectively included all patients (≥16 years) who underwent primary isolated HTx with rATG at our center between January 1983 and June 2018, excluding patients with perioperative death.

Since the start of our HTx program, patients have received a fixed rATG dose of 125 mg daily for 3 days, with rare adjustments based on clinical judgment and lab results, alongside a triple immunosuppressive regimen of corticosteroids, an antiproliferative agent, and a calcineurin inhibitor. Sirolimus is used for selected patients at risk of or with established cardiac allograft vasculopathy (CAV). Since 1996, statins and aspirin were initiated early post-transplantation, irrespective of CAV status. From March 1, 2010, corticosteroids were systematically tapered within 6 to 12 months post-transplantation.

Patients were grouped by cumulative rATG dosing: A (<4.5 mg/kg), B (4.5-7.5 mg/kg), and C (>7.5 mg/kg).6 Data were retrospectively obtained from the Montreal Hospital Institute’s transplant database. The study was approved by our local institutional review board (2019-2447) with waving of consent.

Collected data included patient demographics (age, gender, and race), heart failure etiology, immunologic factors (calculated panel-reactive antibody level, cross-match status), pre-HTx emergency status (prioritization per the Canadian Cardiac Transplant Network [CCTN]), inotropes use and/or temporary mechanical support, durable left ventricular assist device (LVAD), era of transplantation (January 1, 1983-December 31, 1998; January 1, 1999-February 28, 2010; and March 1, 2010-June 30, 2018), comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, previous sternotomy, estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR], body mass index [BMI], history of smoking), cytomegalovirus (CMV) mismatch, donor factors (cause of death and age), duration of ischemia, and maintenance immunosuppression (agent and dose). Clinical outcomes data were extracted from the same database: incidence of AR (acute cellular rejection [ACR] and antibody-mediated rejection) based on the ISHLT 2004 and 2013 revised grading system, mortality and cause of deaths, occurrence of infection needing medical treatment, malignancy, and CAV.9

Outcomes

The primary composite outcome included death, AR (ACR [ISHLT ≥2R] and antibody-mediated rejection), and infections at 24 months, the period most likely to be influenced by rATG.10 Secondary outcomes included individual components of the primary composite end-point assessed at 24 months, along with 24-month etiology of death. Tertiary outcomes included the development of malignancy and CAV over the entire follow-up period, as these conditions typically develop in the longer term.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation, or median (Q1-Q3), for continuous variables, based on distribution, and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. One-way analysis of variance or chi-square tests were used for variable comparison as appropriate, with p-values <0.05 considered statistically significant. Median follow-up was estimated via the Kaplan-Meier method.

Kaplan-Meier curves for the primary composite end-point at 24 months were plotted by cumulative rATG–dosing group and compared using the log-rank test (significance if p < 0.05). The components of the composite end-point were analyzed similarly. Potential predictors of the primary composite end-point were studied using univariable and multivariable Cox regression models, considering rATG cumulative weight–based dosing, sex, CCTN listing status, HTx era, panel-reactive antibodies (≥10%), immunosuppressive agents at follow-up (tacrolimus vs cyclosporine and mycophenolate vs azathioprine and sirolimus). All potential predictors were first explored using univariable model, and all of them were then included in a multivariate Cox regression model, employing a backward selection process.

To evaluate whether the impact of cumulative weight–based rATG dosing on outcomes changed over time, an interaction analysis by transplant era was conducted. Interaction terms between rATG groups and era were included in multivariable models for each outcome. A significant interaction (p < 0.05) indicated era-specific effects, warranting stratified analyses.

To account for different follow-up durations, the etiology of death and malignancy per 1,000 person-years were computed. A receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve with estimation of the Youden’s J index was used to assess the diagnostic performance of rATG cumulative dosing for the primary composite end-point. The optimal cutoff dose value allowing discrimination of subjects with and without primary end-point was determined by the point on the ROC curve with the highest Youden’s J index value. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc, Cary, NC).

Results

Study population

A total of 340 primary HTx recipients received rATG induction: 69 in group A, 245 in group B, and 26 in group C. Most patients (325/340; 96%) received the fixed rATG dose of 375 mg (125 mg intravenous daily for 3 days) (Table S1).

Patients' baseline characteristics (Table 1) showed that most were male (78%), White (97%), with ischemic etiology (45%); they were evenly distributed across transplantation eras. The 3 groups were generally similar except: group C had more women (A: 87% male, B: 79% male, C: 46% male; p < 0.001) and a lower mean BMI (A: 30 ± 3 kg/m², B: 24 ± 3 kg/m², C: 20 ± 2 kg/m²; p < 0.001). There were differences in race (p = 0.042), diabetes (A: 29%, B: 11.1%, C: 7.7%; p < 0.001), dyslipidemia (A: 59.4%, B: 42%, C: 30.8%; p = 0.014), mean eGFR (A: 85 ± 28, B: 67 ± 22, C: 50 ± 19; p < 0.001), smoking history (A: 72.7%, B: 55.8%, C: 47.8%; p = 0.026), and male donors (A: 83.8%, B: 64.5%, C: 57.7%; p = 0.005). There were few patients supported with durable LVAD. Patients in group B had a higher usage of corticosteroids at discharge compared to the other groups (A: 85.5%, B: 94.7% vs C: 80.8%; p = 0.005), and had a higher proportion of patients receiving cyclosporine at discharge (A: 52.2%, B: 68.6%, C: 38.5%; p = 0.001).

Primary composite end-point

At 24 months, group A had a non–significant lower incidence of the primary composite end-point (A: 76.8%, B: 83.7%, C: 96.2%; p = 0.075), primarily driven by lower infections (Table 2). However, group B had significantly better outcomes than group C on Kaplan-Meier curves (overall log-rank p = 0.137, B vs C log-rank p = 0.044; Figure 1A).

Secondary end-points

Deaths

Group C exhibited higher mortality rates than group B (A: 14.5%, B: 12.7%, C: 30.8%, overall p = 0.044, B vs C p = 0.012; Kaplan-Meier log-rank p = 0.024, B vs C log-rank p = 0.005) (Table 2, Figure 1B). Mortality rates per 1,000 patient-years also remained higher for group C compared to groups A and B (A: 58.0, B: 63.3, C: 153.8) (Table S2).

Acute rejection

The absolute incidence of AR was similar across groups (A: 37.7%, B: 45.7%, C: 46.2%; p = 0.713) (Table 2). Similarly, there was no difference in AR survival on Kaplan-Meier curves (log-rank p = 0.466; Figure 1C).

Infections

Absolute incidence of infections was significantly lower in group A compared to B (A: 56.5%, B: 71.8%, C: 76.9%; p = 0.035) (Table 2). Although group C visually and numerically exhibited higher infectious events, Kaplan-Meier curves showed no significant difference (log-rank p = 0.217; Figure 1D).

Tertiary end-points

The incidence of post-transplant lymphoproliferative disorders (PTLD), skin/solid cancers, and other malignancies at full duration follow-up did not differ significantly across dosing groups (p = 0.535, 0.850, and 0.091; Table 2). There was no significant difference in CAV incidence (A: 39.1%, B: 37.6%, C: 15.4%; p = 0.070; Table 2).

Variables associated with the primary composite outcomes

Univariable Cox regression analyses revealed that transplantation era, presence/absence of tacrolimus/cyclosporine, of mycophenolate mofetil (MMF)/azathioprine and sirolimus were related to the risk of primary composite end-point (each with p < 0.001). Generally, rATG dosing was not related to the risk of the primary composite end-point (p = 0.144), but with a trend toward lower risk of the primary composite end-point with rATG <4.5 mg/kg and rATG 4.5 to 7.5 mg/kg (Table 3). By multivariable Cox regression analysis, transplantation era, presence/absence of tacrolimus/cyclosporine and sirolimus remained associated with the risk of developing the primary composite end-point (Table 3). Transplantation in the 2 earliest era, absence of tacrolimus and cyclosporine (compared to the presence of tacrolimus without cyclosporine), and sirolimus usage increased the risk of the primary composite end-point. When forced into the multivariable model, rATG dosing was not a significant predictor (p = 0.058), but there was a trend toward an increased risk of events for group C compared to group B (Table S3, p = 0.017). The interaction analysis by transplant era showed no significant differences, indicating similar event risk patterns across the 3 groups over time (p > 0.05, Table S4).

Receiver operating characteristic curve and area under the curve analysis

The ROC curve showed a nonsignificant trend for cumulative weight–based rATG dosing to predict the primary composite end-point at 24 months with an area under the curve of 0.576 (95% confidence interval [CI] 0.497-0.655; p = 0.058) (Figure 2). The cutoff was 4.95 mg/kg, yielding 49.1% sensitivity and 68.1% specificity based on Youden’s J index.

Discussion

We retrospectively evaluated the impact of cumulative weight–based rATG dosing on HTx outcomes. Key findings: (1) lower cumulative dosing was associated with a trend toward lower incidence of the primary composite end-point (death, AR, and infections), with <4.5 mg/kg group showing a nonsignificant trend toward lower incidence of events, and 4.5 to 7.5 mg/kg group demonstrating significantly better survival on Kaplan-Meier analysis compared to the 7.5 mg/kg group; (2) <4.5 mg/kg had significantly fewer infections; (3) 4.5 to 7.5 mg/kg had the lowest 24-month mortality; (4) lower risk was linked to recent transplant era, calcineurin inhibitors use, and absence of sirolimus; (5) no differences in AR, CAV, or malignancy across dosing groups. Our study aims to contribute to the scarce HTx literature on rATG dosing.

We found that lower rATG dosing was associated with a trend toward a lower incidence of the primary composite end-point, driven by fewer infectious events at 24 months. The 4.5 to 7.5 mg/kg group showed better 24-month survival compared to the >7.5 mg/kg group. These findings differ slightly from Aliabadi et al’s retrospective single–center study on rATG dosing and outcomes (mortality, treated AR episodes, and severe infections).8 Freedom from their combined end-point was highest in the 4.5 to 7.5 mg/kg group (1 year: 75%, 10 years: 56%) vs <4.5 mg/kg (61%, 33%), and >7.5 mg/kg (57%, 35%) (both p ≤ 0.001), mainly due to fewer 10-year severe infections.8 In our cohort, infection incidence also differed significantly, with our <4.5 mg/kg group having the lowest incidence. This discrepancy may stem from differing definitions: Aliabadi et al included only severe infections, while we included all treated infections, leading to a higher event rate driving our composite end-point. rATG dosing and duration also varied: Aliabadi et al used 0.5 to 2.5 mg/kg daily (mean cumulative dose 8.64 ± 4.43 mg/kg) over 1 to 10 days (mean 4.69 ± 1.72 days), with adjustments based on clinical and lab parameters. Their dose modulation might have been influenced by the evolution of prevalent clinical credence in any given HTx era. Indeed, the proportion of their patients in the different rATG groups changed over the observation period. In contrast, our fixed rATG dose of 375 mg (96% of patients) across all HTx eras allows consistent retrospective calculation of cumulative weight–based dosing and minimizes bias from practice changes over time, which was confirmed by the absence of interaction with eras.

Furthermore, a recent survey found that nearly half of HTx centers now use low rATG dosing (<4.5 mg/kg).6 Some authors recommend lower cumulative doses (≤3-3.5 mg/kg) for patients with pretransplant infections or low immunological risk.6 Others recommend tailoring rATG induction using sequential T-cell counts to reduce cost and total dose, but data in HTx are limited, and this approach is challenging to implement in small centers like ours.6

The absolute incidence of infection was significantly lower with rATG <4.5 mg/kg vs 4.5 to 7.5 mg/kg at 24 months, aligning with prior studies showing a dose-dependent increase in infections. Aliabadi et al reported lower severe infections with 4.5 to 7.5 mg/kg, >7.5 mg/kg linked to more severe infections, and <4.5 mg/kg showing higher risk than 4.5 to 7.5 mg/kg (hazard ratio [HR] 1.86, 95% CI 1.15-2.99; p = 0.011).8 Possible explanations include higher rejection rates in the low-dose group, leading to intensified immunosuppression and increased infection risk, and/or early infections prompting rATG discontinuation.8 By contrast, Lee et al’s retrospective single–center study of 201 HTx (2016-2018) found similar 3-month infection rates between patients receiving <4.5 mg/kg (29.5%) and 4.5 mg/kg (20.0%) rATG (p = 0.140).11 Mattei et al conducted a randomized trial comparing rATG (up to 12.5 mg/kg) with basiliximab and found higher rates of fatal infection in the rATG group (14.3% vs 0%, p = 0.027).12 However, this must be weighed against Carrier et al's findings of lower freedom from AR at 6 months with basiliximab (65%) compared to rATG (83%),13 and a recent meta-analysis indicating rATG may reduce mortality vs basiliximab (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.76-0.93).14 In kidney transplantation, each incremental increase in rATG dose was associated with higher CMV risk (p = 0.0073).15 In liver transplantation, a higher rATG dose (25 mg/kg) is linked to more fatal infections than the standard 7.5 mg/kg dose (34.6% vs 15.5%, p = 0.01).16

We observed significantly lower 24-month mortality in the 4.5 to 7.5 mg/kg group vs >7.5 mg/kg group. In contrast, Aliabadi et al reported no significant mortality difference, but noted a trend toward higher mortality in the <4.5 mg/kg vs the 4.5 to 7.5 mg/kg group (HR 1.56, 95% CI: 0.95-2.56).8 Lee et al reported lower 2-year survival in the <4.5 mg/kg rATG group compared to the 4.5 mg/kg group (86.9% vs 97.8%; p = 0.001).11 However, a prespecified analysis conditioned on 1-month survival (addressing potential treatment bias from sicker patients receiving lower doses) showed no significant difference between groups (p = 0.239).11 Our mortality findings by rATG cumulative dosing differ from prior studies, potentially reflecting true dose effects, as our fixed dosing protocol was seldom adjusted for adverse effects. Impact of rATG on 1-year mortality relative to no induction therapy remains uncertain in randomized controlled trials (OR 2.68, 95% CI 0.51-14.14) and observational studies (30-day [odd ratio (OR) 0.96, 95% CI 0.92-1.01], 1-year [OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.89-1.05], or 5-year mortality [OR 1.13, 95% CI 0.68-1.88]), though there may be a potential benefit in overall mortality (HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.80-0.98),14 but the impact of the dose used were not reported.14

AR rates did not differ between groups, possibly because higher cumulative rATG doses led to more infections, prompting immunosuppression reduction and a resulting rise in AR episodes. Aliabadi et al observed a nonsignificant trend toward more treated rejections in the <4.5 mg/kg group.8 Nevertheless, our findings align with Lee et al, who observed no difference in 2-year cumulative ACR grade ≥2,11 suggesting that lower cumulative rATG doses may be safe with modern immunosuppression in HTx.

Long-term assessment of rATG’s impact on AR is limited by scarce data beyond 36 months, with peak AR incidence occurring in the first 3 to 6 months post-HTx.5, 8, 11, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23 In our 24-month analysis, AR incidence aligned with previous reports,17, 18 but generally trended lower, possibly due to differences in demographics, prospective cross-matching, and rATG dosing.8, 11, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23 Additionally, small sample sizes in rATG studies may also exaggerate AR rate variations.8, 11, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23

While prior studies suggest rATG reduces and delays CAV, we found no significant difference among dosing groups—similar to Aliabadi et al.8 However, our analysis lacked a noninduction control for comparison. Low malignancy rates limited dose impact assessment, with no significant differences observed. The >7.5 mg/kg group’s small size and high early mortality may have reduced chances of PTLD development. Nevertheless, our findings align with a systematic review indicating that the PTLD risk is not affected by rATG dose, regardless of organ type, including heart.24 Widespread antiviral use, inversely linked to PTLD, may reduce the impact of intensive rATG on PTLD risk.6

Lastly, the predictive rATG cumulative dose cutoff for discriminating subjects developing the primary composite end-point was 4.95 mg/kg, though not statistically significant and likely underpowered. Our findings suggest the rATG cumulative dose balancing efficacy and safety lies between 4.5 and 7.5 mg/kg, likely closer to 4.5 mg/kg, aligning with previous studies.8, 11

The retrospective design limits causal inference, and unmeasured confounders may persist despite multivariate adjustments and bias-reduction efforts. Our data derived from a relatively small sample size, a single center, and collected over decades may lead to unintended biases. While absolute event rates at 24 months were significant, Kaplan-Meier analysis was not likely due to high event frequency, unequal group sizes, and small numbers in group C. This also explains why comparisons involving group C often lacked significance. Generalizability is limited by a predominantly White, male cohort, albeit representative of the Canadian HTx population. Without a control group, we could not assess induction therapy's benefit or compare rATG to other agents. To account for differences across transplant eras, we performed an interaction analysis, which showed no variation in event risk between groups over time. Patient distribution across eras was balanced, and era was included in the multivariable Cox regression which showed no interaction.

Our findings suggest that a cumulative rATG dosing of 4.5 to 7.5 mg/kg offers the best balance of AR, mortality, and infection—mainly by reducing infections and to a lesser degree mortality, within 24 months post-HTx without increasing AR. A cumulative dose of <4.5 mg/kg offers the lowest risk of infection, with 4.5 mg/kg likely representing the intersection dose. Absence of benefit and potential for increased infections with higher cumulative dosing is clinically significant. Prospective studies are greatly needed.
