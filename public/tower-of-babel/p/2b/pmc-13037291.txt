The optimal combinations of frequency and volume for improving physical performance, cognitive function and mental health in old individuals with possible sarcopenia: study protocol of a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xiao Li, Zbigniew Ossowski, Marcin Pasek, Peiyin Jin, Yue Chen, Zhengyi Li, Bingzhan Jin, Kunpeng Li, Yuzhen Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSarcopenia is an age-related disease characterized primarily by reduced muscle mass, decreased muscle strength, and/or impaired physical performance, which is closely associated with increased risks of falls, fractures, cognitive decline, and mortality. Although resistance training has been proven effective for sarcopenia, the optimal dosage regimen (training volume × frequency) for possible sarcopenia remains unclear.MethodsThis is the study protocol of a randomized controlled trial. 111 community-dwelling older adults (aged ≥ 65 years) with possible sarcopenia will be recruited and randomly allocated into three groups (n = 37 per group): high-frequency low-volume (5 sessions/week, 6 sets/session), low-frequency high-volume (3 sessions/week, 10 sets/session), and control group (no intervention). Supervised by a single coach, the 12-week resistance training program incorporates make-up sessions to ensure adherence. Assessments will be conducted at baseline, 6, 12, and 24 weeks. Primary outcome is handgrip strength. Secondary outcomes encompass the Timed Up and Go Test, 10-meter walking speed, the Montreal cognitive assessments, the Geriatric Depression Scale, the Geriatric Anxiety Inventory and the SF-36 Health Survey.DiscussionThis pioneering randomized controlled trial is the first to investigate optimal frequency-volume combinations (at identical total dosage) for improving muscle strength, physical function, and cognitive performance in community-dwelling older adults with possible sarcopenia. The study addresses a critical gap in establishing evidence-based exercise parameters for early intervention of possible sarcopenia.Trial registrationThis trial was prospectively registered at Chinese Clinical Trial Registry on February 8, 2025 (registration number: ChiCTR2500096917).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07225-z.

Background

Sarcopenia, defined as the loss of muscle mass, decreased muscle strength and/or reduced physical performance with age-related, was classified as a disease under ICD-10-CM in 2016 [1, 2]. It is associated with the increased risks in falls, fractures, cognitive impairment, loss of independence and mortality [3]. Recently, European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Old People (EWGSOP) and Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) introduced the diagnostic category of “possible sarcopenia”, the existence of low muscle strength with or without reduced physical performance, as the early and reversible stage of sarcopenia [1, 4]. Compared to sarcopenia, the possible sarcopenia has a relatively higher prevalence in the old population. A recent cohort study demonstrated higher rates of possible sarcopenia in rural (41.0%) and urban (31.1%) regions [5]. Possible sarcopenia was significantly associated with adverse outcomes, including physical dysfunction, cognitive impairment, mental health problems and poor quality of life [6, 7]. As possible sarcopenia has been shown to be reversible, the intervention of possible sarcopenia can prevent the occurrence of many serious risks and reduce social medical expenses [8, 9].

Resistance training can significantly enhance muscle performance in elderly patients with sarcopenia. One systematic review analyzed the existing randomized controlled trials and indicated that resistance training would benefit in knee extension strength, timed-up-and-go test and gait speed [10]. One Korean study found that the isometric muscle strength, 30-s chair stand and gait speed of sarcopenic women were improved after 16-week resistance training [11]. For possible sarcopenia, few studies had investigated the influence of resistance training on muscle performance. Only one Swedish study found that the 10-week resistance training could significantly decreased chair sit-stand time in old individuals with possible sarcopenia [12]. Though no direct evidence supporting the positive effects of resistance training on the cognitive function, mental health and quality of life among old individuals with possible sarcopenia, a bunch of studies confirmed resistance training could improve the cognitive function, mental health and quality of life among the old adults [13–15].

Although the resistance training was proved an effective intervention, the parameters of implementation, including dosage, volume and frequency, were still diverse. Exercise dosage typically expressed the quantification of exercise exposure [16]. Volume and frequency were considered as the main determinants for dosage. With the same volume and intensity applied, higher training frequency has been found to result in better muscle performance. One study compared the effects of 8-week resistance training with different frequencies (2 times, 3 times and 4 times per week) on muscle performance and pointed that the 4-time-per-week protocol was the most effective compared to the other two groups [17]. Similarly, with the same frequency and intensity, the larger volume is tended to have greater impact, which was confirmed by a recent systematic review with meta-analysis [18]. However, higher frequency/volume would induce the larger dosage, which might be the underlying reason for the conclusion that higher frequency/volume was more effective than the lower ones. When exercise dosage is standardized, medium-duration training proves superior to both shorter and longer sessions for mitigating sarcopenia-related muscle deficits [19]. Nevertheless, the optimal combination of volume and frequency is still unclear.

Based on the limited evidence, critical knowledge gaps persist regarding optimal combinations with identified dosage (volume × frequency) for managing possible sarcopenia. This study will identify optimal frequency-volume combinations of resistance training on physical performance, cognitive function, mental health and quality of life among old individuals with possible sarcopenia, which could provide the evidence-based support for implementation of the effective and efficient resistance training protocol among this underserved population.

Methods

Study design

A single-blinded, randomized controlled trial will be conducted in Anyang City, Henan Province, China. This study will employ a 12-week resistance training with 3 measures and 1 follow up. The primary outcome is handgrip strength. The secondary outcomes include the timed-up-and-go test, the 10 m walking speed test, the Montreal cognitive assessments, the Geriatric Depression Scale, the Geriatric Anxiety Inventory and the SF-36 Health Survey. The recruitment notice will be posted on the community bulletin board, as well as the waiting area and examination bulletin board of the community health clinic. The exclusion criteria will be as follows: (1) diagnosed cognitive impairment and could not cooperate and complete the study; (2) uncontrolled cardiovascular diseases, neuromuscular diseases, musculoskeletal deformity, symptomatic rheumatoid arthritis or deep venous thrombosis; (3) regular moderate or intense physical activities (no more than three times per week and no longer than 30 min per exercise); (4) surgery within 1 month; (5) participated in other studies of exercise interventions concurrently; and (6) unsuitable to participate in the research (assessed by Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire [PARQ], ‘Yes’ to at least one of the seven questions of PAR-Q). In addition, individuals will be considered as having dropped out of this study if they (7) quit the study spontaneously; and (8) show poor physical conditions that are not suitable for participating in exercises and could not meet the required exercise intensity for more than 3 weeks during the training.

Participant demographics and individual medical conditions, including data on sex, age, body mass index (BMI), living situations, exercise situations, smoking status, drinking status, fall history, and the number of comorbidities will be examined. The living situations will be categorized as alone or not alone. Fall history will be the number of falls in the past year. Exercise situation will be defined as how many times weekly more than 30 min of moderate-intensity physical activity will be performed and classified as 0/week, 1–2/week or ≥ 3/week. Smoking and drinking status will be classified as “never,” “former,” or “current”. The research investigator will supervise and manage the recruitment process, ensuring that participants are informed and consent is obtained.

Sample size calculation

To date, no study has examined the effects of different combinations of training frequency and volume with identified dosage on physical and cognitive function in the older people with possible sarcopenia. This study adopted an effect size of 0.29 based on the effects of exercise on handgrip strength among individuals with sarcopenia to estimate the sample size [20]. Since this study involved two factors (three groups and four times of assessments), the sample size was calculated to be 29 for each group with a power of 0.8 and α value of 0.05 using the software (G*Power 3.1). In consideration of 20% dropout rate, the total sample size was 111.

Randomization and blinding

All participants will be randomly assigned into 3 groups: the high frequency with low volume group (HFLV), the low frequency with high volume group (LFHV) and control group (CG). Each participant will be given an identification number by the main investigator, who will perform the randomization using a computer program (Research Randomizer Form www.randomizer.org/). The intervention will be conducted by a physical therapist, who will be blinded to randomization and assessment. The assessments and data analysis will be performed by a researcher, who will be blinded to randomization and intervention.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or members of the public will not be involved in this study. The research design, enrolment, allocation, interventions and assessments will be conducted by the trained researchers.

Exercise intervention

All training sessions will be conducted under the supervision of the same professional coach. Extra sessions catering for missing appointments will be arranged to confirm the participants will complete the same number of training sessions.

The participants in LFHV will conduct three training sessions per week with high volume (10 sets per training session); those in HFLV will conduct five training sessions per week with low volume (6 sets per training session). In total, 360 sets (30 sets per week * 12 weeks) will be conducted for both LFHV and HFLV groups. The control group will have no additional training.

All participants will be advised to keep their lifestyle as usual during the study.

Each training session contains a 5-min warm-up, a chair-based training with elastic-band and a 5-min cool-down. The chair-based training, targeting on upper (chest, back, biceps, triceps) and lower limbs (quadriceps, hamstrings, triceps surae, gluteus medius.

and minimus) muscles [19], will have 10 or 6 sets with 1 min interval after completing one set. Each set will include several movements including elbow extension/flextion, tricep extension, chest press, scapular squeeze, knee extension/flexion, heel rise, side leg lifts. Each movement will contain one “fatigue repetition maximum”, which will determined by the number of repetitions as the participants claim fatigue. If the number of repetitions is more than 10, the strength of elastic band will be increased. The different elastic band will be selected according the individual’s fatigue repetition maximum.

Participants will be provided with Thera-Bands and an exercise leaflet. Training workshops will be conducted for participants to ensure that correct postures are maintained in self-administered training sessions.

Outcome measures

All outcomes will be measured before the intervention (baseline), during the intervention (6 weeks), one day after the intervention (12 weeks), and 12 weeks after completing the intervention (at 24 weeks, which is the total study duration). The assessors will be blinded to the allocation and intervention.

Primary outcomes

Handgrip strength will be measured by a dynamometer (0-90KG, SENSSUN, PRC) on dominant hand. The elbow will be flexed 90°, and the forearm will be placed horizontally on the tabletop. The maximum value from three full-strength attempts will be recorded for data analysis.

Secondary outcomes

The timed-up and go test (TUG) was recommended as a suitable assessment for balance and physical function in the older people with low muscle function [19]. Time will be recorded for participants with their normal pace to rise from a chair, walk 3 m, turn around, walk back to the chair and sit down. The average time of two trials will be used in the data analysis.

The 10 m walking speed test (10MWT) will be assessed at self-preferred and maximum walking speed. It is used as a golden tool to evaluate the mobility in the older people with sarcopenia [19]. The time will be measured only for the middle 6 m. The average walking speed of three trials will be in the data analysis.

The Montreal cognitive assessments (MoCA) will be used to evaluate the cognitive function. It is a brief screening tool designed to test mild cognitive impairment in various cognitive domains. It can be completed in approximately 10 min and has been validated in multiple countries and regions [21].

The Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) and the Geriatric Anxiety Inventory (GAI) will be used to examine the depressive and anxiety levels. GDS and GAI were widely used in the previous studies [22].

The SF-36 Health Survey, a 36-item generic measure of health status, and widely applied in the international community will be used to examine the quality of life [23].

Monitoring

Session logs will be tracked by research staff for calculating attendance rate as the indicator of adherence. Adverse events will be documented through weekly self-reports and trainer observations. The participants who reported adverse event will automatically stop the training. If unexpected severe adverse events happen, this study will be terminated. Also, interim analysis will be performed by main investigator (XL) to determine whether the study should be terminated.

Statistical analysis

Kolgomorov-Smirnov test will be used to examine the normal distribution. Descriptive statistics will present frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables, depending on distribution normality. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (for data with normal distribution) or Kruskal-Wallis test (for data with non-normal distribution) will be conducted to compare the baseline characteristics of the three groups. Repeated measured ANOVA (time × group) or Friedman test will be used to explore the effect of different combinations of frequency and volume on physical and cognitive performance in the older people with possible sarcopenia. The last observation carried forward of an intention-to-treat analysis will be used for imputation of missing values. For statistical analysis, SPSS 29.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, Illinois, USA) will be utilized. Unless otherwise indicated, the significance threshold will be set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this will be the first randomized controlled trial to explore the optimal combination of frequency and volume with identical dosage for improving physical performance, cognitive function and mental health in the older individuals with possible sarcopenia in a community-based primary care setting.

According to the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019 guidelines, the introduction of the concept of “possible sarcopenia”, defined by the presence of low muscle strength (with or without preliminary evidence of reduced muscle mass) without meeting the full diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia, serves as a critical and valuable tool for population-based screening and early risk identification of sarcopenia [1]. By prioritizing early identification of individuals at risk, “possible sarcopenia” might enable healthcare providers to initiate timely lifestyle modifications before the progression to severe dysfunction [1, 3, 4].

Several previous studies have demonstrated that integrated exercise interventions yield significant improvement in muscle strength and physical performance [23, 24]. Bao et al. conducting a meta-analysis found that resistance training can increase handgrip strength, gait speed and TUG test [20]. However, despite the existing evidence supporting exercise as an effective strategy, the optimal parameters of resistance training remain poorly established and inconsistently reported in the literature. Critical exercise variables, including training frequency, volume, intensity, duration, and intervention period, are often heterogeneous across studies, making it challenging to derive evidence-based recommendations.

Few studies have investigated the optimal parameters, such as frequency and volume [17, 18]. Zaroni et al. [17] and Carvalho et al. [18] largely ignored the quantification and standardization of exercise dosage. Due to the oversight of differences in the effects of distinct exercise dosages, it remains unclear whether higher exercise frequency or greater exercise volume produces superior outcomes for sarcopenia-related population. Addressing this gap is essential to conduct this study to solve this question and give evidence-based recommendations for improving the efficiency of resistance training in older people with possible sarcopenia.

There are some strengths in this study. First, the dosage was fixed for training group. Two distinct training regimens (high-frequency/low-volume versus low-frequency/high-volume) were compared to answer the research question of this study: What is the optimal combination of frequency and volume as the dosage fixed. Second, the targeted population of this study was possible sarcopenia. The findings of this study could potentially provide some evidence to improve the effectiveness and efficiency of prevention in sarcopenia.

Meanwhile, this study has several limitations that warrant careful consideration. First, muscle mass will not be measured in this study. The absence of comparison in muscle mass before and after the intervention indicated that the optimal combination might not be applied to increase the muscle mass. However, due to the practical considerations, especially limited funding, it is impossible to conduct the muscle mass assessments (e.g., DXA/BIA) in this study. Second, this study will only examine the combinations of frequency and volume in two levels (High versus low). Thus, the results of this study will be cautious explanation and conclusion.t, the participants of this study will be from one single region, which would affect the generalizability of findings of this study.
