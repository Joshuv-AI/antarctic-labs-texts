Assessing weight stigma in school-aged children (grades 1 to 3): a cross-sectional study from Palestine
Authors: Lina Bustami, Manal Badrasawi, Ola Anabtawi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundObesity is a complex global epidemic. There are multiple causes and complications of obesity, both physical and psychological ones. One challenge that individuals with obesity encounter is the experience of weight stigma. Weight stigma is associated with negative outcomes, including psychological disturbances, eating disorders, weight gain, and avoiding physical activity. Weight stigma is prevalent among different age groups. The main purpose of the study is to explore weight stigma among school-aged children through the development of an image-based questionnaire. This study is conducted as a first step for further educational intervention studies to reduce weight stigma among children.MethodsThe study aimed to explore weight stigma in 150 children aged 6 to 9 years through direct interviews conducted by the researcher. Data collection started after the development and validation of a specific image-based questionnaire. Questionnaire development followed an intense literature review. Content validity was ensured by revision from experts in related fields. Reliability was checked by a pilot study, followed by data analysis to calculate Cronbach’s alpha. Weight stigma explored through a) stereotypes of others with obesity. b) social preference and rejection of individuals with obesity. c) children’s beliefs about weight controllability and judgment towards others with obesity. Based on that, the developed questionnaire was composed of three tasks: the Adjective Attribution Task, the Friendship Selection Task, and the Controllability Task.ResultsIn the Adjective Attribution Task, most of the participants attributed negative adjectives to the figure with overweight. In the Friendship Selection Task, figures with overweight were almost rejected as partners and friends, and only one child chose the figure with overweight as a best friend. The Controllability Task revealed a high percentage of judgment beliefs towards figures with overweight.ConclusionOur findings indicate that weight stigma is prevalent among school-aged children. This study is the first to provide a tool to explore weight stigma and to offer statistical data that will support future educational interventions. This work is important to reduce weight stigma among children, protect children’s psychological status, reduce obesity prevalence, and improve its management.Keywords: Weight Prejudice, Pediatric obesity, Child, Questionnaires, Psychometrics

Background

In 2016, obesity affected more than 340 million children and adolescents aged 5–19 years, and over 650 million adults [1]. WHO estimated that by 2025, 167 million people will be at risk of health problems related to being overweight or obesity (including both adults and children) [2]. Obesity significantly contributes to the global burden of non-communicable diseases [3]. In addition, obesity has psychological consequences, including low self-esteem, depression, and eating disorders [4]. Furthermore, stigma targets individuals with obesity, which is also associated with chronic stress and higher BMI [5].

Weight stigma consists of stereotyping, labeling, and discrimination against individuals based on their weight [6]. Studies have demonstrated that weight stigma contributes to higher calorie intake [7], following unhealthy diets [5], binge eating [8], and avoiding physical activities [9], which in turn will lead to weight gain. Furthermore, stigmatized individuals are at risk of experiencing psychological disturbances, including difficulties in regulating emotions, low self-esteem, and social isolation [6].

While weight stigma has serious consequences, it’s important to highlight its high prevalence in major life domains. For example, in employment domains, individuals with obesity are less desirable for employers than others with a lower body weight [10, 11]. In addition, weight stigma is common in medical, educational, social, and work environments [12].

In addition to external stigma, individuals with obesity may internalize negative thoughts and stereotypes about themselves, which is referred to as 'internalized' weight stigma. Thus, external stigma plays a major role in influencing internalized stigma [13, 14]. Weight stigma experienced from external environments interferes with individuals’ own beliefs about themselves and could affect their self-esteem. This pattern was clearly identified in a study assessing weight stigma by asking participants to rate sets of target figures on multiple dimensions (e.g., attractiveness, intelligence, happiness). In the same study, even individuals with obesity rated the targets with obesity negatively [15].

Although adults are facing multiple weight stigma forms in employment, health, and different social situations, children are also stigmatized [16]. However, children were found to show a tendency toward stigmatizing others with obesity as early as three years of age [17]. It was found that, when compared to other children with different disabilities (e.g., wheelchair-bound, amputee), peers’ greatest dislike was toward children who have overweight [18].

Weight stigma has significant negative effects on children’s academic achievement, overall well-being, and, consequently, their personal choices and future career prospects [17]. Weight stigma experienced by children and adolescents adversely reflected on their psychological and emotional health [9, 17]. Additionally, weight stigma in children leads to less motivation toward exercise and engagement in unhealthy eating patterns [16, 19–22] thus, it may contribute to the development [23] and persistence of obesity. Importantly, evidence suggests that negative effects on the mental as well as physical health of children can persist over a long period, which means they may extend into adulthood [19].

To measure weight stigma among children, studies have used various tools featuring figures representing different weight categories: underweight, normal weight, and overweight [17, 23–26]. Previous research showed children preferring targets with normal and underweight as friends compared to those with obesity [17, 23–26]. This procedure was one of the classical methods used to explore the presence of weight stigma attitudes. In Jenull & Mayer's study, children were shown three figures and asked to pick one to befriend, one to avoid, and one to invite to their birthday. Two thirds of them chose not to be friends with or invite the overweight figure to their birthday party [17]. Similarly, Sagone & De Carolie used Friendship and Rejection Tasks where children were asked to choose from three figures (underweight, normal weight, and overweight) the one they would like to have as a best friend, one that they would choose as classmate, and the one they wouldn’t like to playing and walking around with. Most children rejected the figure representing overweight as a playmate or walking companion [23].

On the other hand, children’s thoughts towards others with obesity were studied to explore weight stigma. In the study of Kraig & Keel, they used positive and negative adjectives and asked children to rate each figure (underweight, normal weight, or overweight) on each adjective on a subscale from 1 to 5. Results indicated that children hold more negative thoughts about figures with overweight [25]. This method was mostly used in previous studies to assess how children attribute different traits to figures with different weights [17, 23–27].

Many studies have addressed the attribution theory to explain why negative attributes are directed toward individuals with obesity [28, 29]. People base their judgments on beliefs about individuals' responsibility for obesity and the assumption that they have full control over their weight [30, 31]. Thus, some studies shed light on studying the beliefs that children hold towards obesity and individuals’ ability to control their weight [24, 27]. In 2004, Musher-Eizenman & Holub used a scale of five questions to evaluate the beliefs about obesity among children ages 4–6. Average scores were calculated for all the questions, and higher scores indicated more control over weight attribution [27]. Margulies & Floyd used a modified version of the same task. In their study, they have not only focused on overweight but have included eight items to assess children’s general belief about weight responsibility (these were related to both figures with underweight and overweight). Also, they assess their beliefs about body weight controllability with two additional questions. Researchers sum the scores of the first eight questions to obtain an average score [24]. However, Cronbach’s α for the total score in their study was 0.56. The result was similar to the first study of Musher-Eizenman & Holub (α = 0.56) [27], and to other previous studies that assessed the same aspect with similar tasks among children [32] and among adults [33].

Since no studies have addressed weight stigma among children in Palestine, this study will be the first to provide a tool for assessing it and to provide baseline data on its prevalence among children. This study is important because it’s conducted as a backbone for future educational interventions to reduce weight stigma among children. This will help minimize the consequences of weight stigma, which contribute to the increasing prevalence of obesity, poor obesity management, and psychological disorders.

Methods

Study design and population

A non-probability sampling technique was employed. One Public and two private schools were conveniently selected, and within each school, students from grades 1, 2 and 3 were randomly selected. The classes were chosen from these grades depending on availability and school schedules. A total of 150 children aged 6–9 years from three selected schools in Nablus City (Pioneers Baccalaureate, UNRWA, and Ethar School) participated in the study, including 47% males and 53% females. However, a pilot study indicated that children older than 9 years tended to overthink their answers, potentially affecting the validity of their responses. Procedure

Data collection took place from December 2023 to January 2024. Data were collected via 10—15-min face-to-face interviews conducted at the children’s schools. For the purpose of data collection, a structured questionnaire was developed by the research team. The current study was conducted in three phases: the development of the questionnaire, its validation, and the exploration of weight stigma among children.

The questionnaire was developed based on a literature review of previous studies assessing weight stigma in young children [17, 23, 24, 27, 34]. Since the questionnaire was designed for children, it utilized three-character figures. The researcher designed three colorful figures for our study, each representing underweight, normal weight, and overweight. Separate versions were created for male and female participants. The questionnaire comprised four sections: participant demographic data, Adjective Attribution Task, Friendship Selection Task, and Controllability Attribution Task.

The validation of the questionnaire was conducted by testing content validity and reliability. Content validity was assessed by researchers and experts in nutrition, psychology, and assessment. Reliability was tested by a pilot study conducted on children aged 6–10 years. The pilot study included 18 randomly selected children, with both males and females represented. Data collection from participants started after obtaining parents’ consent. In addition, children’s verbal consent was obtained. Based on the researcher’s observations during the pilot, some modifications were made to the questionnaire. However, the sample size was representative of at least 9% of the large study [35].

Despite modifications, the Controllability Attribution Task achieved a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.634 in the main study, which was the highest attainable value. Notably, low reliability in similar tasks has also been reported in previous studies among children and adults [32]. *Note: Participants in the pilot study were excluded from the main study.

Exploring weight stigmatization among children: before data collection, letters were sent to the parents of all 6- to 9-year-old children at the three participating schools in Nablus City. The letters explained the study’s nature, its design, and its objective to the parents. The letters also assured parents that the researcher would politely confirm the children’s willingness to participate. Parental consent was obtained by returning the signed form, with both parent and child names clearly written.

To minimize potential distress, interviews were conducted in a familiar school environment in a friendly, informal manner, framed as a game with colorful cartoon characters. The researcher spent a few minutes building rapport with each child, emphasizing that there were no right or wrong answers and that their responses would remain confidential. Children were introduced to three figures, representing underweight, normal weight, and overweight, and were asked to answer questions related to these characters as part of the game.

To reduce social desirability bias, interviews were conducted in very small groups, no more than five children; responses were anonymized with unique codes instead of names; and the researcher emphasized that their answers would remain private and would not affect their standing at school. These strategies align with established approaches in stigma research, such as emphasizing confidentiality to mitigate response bias, employing age-appropriate and neutral prompts, and using visual, playful materials to reduce participant discomfort [36].

Study instruments

Target figures

The study employed three tasks, each using colored, printed designed images representing characters with underweight, normal weight, and overweight. Before starting, children were asked to identify the differences among the three figures; all participants correctly recognized the weight categories.

Adjective attribution task

To assess anti-fat prejudice and stereotyping, a modified Adjective Attribution Task was used, based on prior studies [17, 23, 24, 34]. Children were presented with a list of 20 adjectives, half positive (e.g., beautiful, loved, happy) and half negative (e.g., sad, lazy, shy), randomized in order. The three figures were displayed at the top of the list (coded as 1 = underweight, 2 = normal weight, 3 = overweight). Children were asked to tick the figure(s) they believed matched each adjective. To improve response accuracy, the options ‘none of them’ (coded 4) and ‘all of them’ (coded 5) were also included. For each adjective, answers were summed and averaged to obtain a total score of 20 for each figure.

Friendship selection task

This task, adapted from previous studies [17, 23, 24] assessed children’s social preferences. During the interview, children were asked to circle the figure(s) they selected in response to eight questions, such as: ‘Who would you like as a friend?’, ‘Who would you invite to your birthday?’, and ‘Who would you share your food with?’ Children could also choose ‘all’ or two figures.The three figures were placed beside each question. Figures were coded as in the Adjective Attribution Task (1 = underweight, 2 = normal weight, 3 = overweight, 4 = no one, 5 = all). Responses were summed to generate a total score of 8, with percentages calculated for each figure per question.

Controllability attribution task

This task assessed whether children believed a person’s weight was controllable and whether they held judgments toward individuals with overweight. Previous studies in children [27] and adults [33, 37] informed the task design. Specifically, the Anti-fat Attitudes Questionnaire Willpower subscale [36, 37] and the Perceived Etiology of Obesity scale [33] were used in earlier research to assess beliefs regarding weight controllability. Ideas in the current study were derived from items of these scales. Six items were adapted from these sources and rephrased as sentences related to the overweight figure. Four items assessed beliefs about causes of obesity (e.g., ‘I think the child in the image eats too much,’ ‘I think the child in the image sleeps too much’), and two items assessed beliefs about personal control (e.g., ‘I think the child in the image can become thin if he/she wants’). Children indicated agreement or disagreement with each statement. For items reflecting judgmental attitudes, agreement was coded as 1 and disagreement as 0. For items reflecting environmental or external causes, agreement was coded as 0 and disagreement as 1. Scores were summed for each participant to quantify judgmental behaviors and attribution to environmental causes.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis for this study was conducted according to its various phases. For the pilot study, reliability was assessed using SPSS software to compute Cronbach's alpha. Factor analysis was employed to evaluate construct validity. Additionally, structural equation modeling was used to further assess the construct validity of the weight stigma measure, with guidance from experts proficient in this type of analysis. In the final phase of the study, the prevalence of weight stigma and its associated factors were analyzed using SPSS. Descriptive statistics were used to determine prevalence, while relationships between weight stigma and socio-demographic variables were examined using independent sample t-tests and one-way ANOVA. The level of significance was set at 5%, and the study power at 80%.

Discussion

The main objective of this study was to explore weight stigmatization behaviors among children and to develop a novel image-based questionnaire for the same purpose. The results revealed a high percentage of weight stigma reflected in children’s tendency to assign negative adjectives to characters with overweight, a preference for characters with normal or underweight in social choices, and the endorsement of judgmental beliefs toward peers with overweight.

As presented in the results, the Adjective Attribution Task showed that children frequently associated overweight figures with negative traits such as ugliness, laziness, and carelessness, while perceiving figures with underweight and normal weight as clean, loved, and good. This pattern of stereotyping aligns with findings from previous studies, which have consistently documented weight-based stereotypes and biases among children [17, 24, 27]. Particularly, although children could select multiple figures, negative adjectives were mostly attributed to the overweight figure, whereas positive traits were linked to the normal- and underweight figures. Such early-age discriminatory behaviors suggest that weight stigma may not originate from natural cognitive development but rather stem from social and cultural influences. This is supported by Cramer and Steinwert [34] who concluded that children’s weight stigma attitudes are primarily shaped by their social environment rather than innate cognitive processes.

Consistent with previous studies [17, 24, 27] our Friendship Selection Task showed a strong preference among children for figures with underweight and normal weight, while figures with overweight were rarely chosen as friends. This preference likely reflects children’s underlying beliefs that individuals with overweight possess negative traits, as suggested by the Adjective Attribution Task findings. Additionally, children’s friendship choices may be influenced by courtesy stigma, which is a form of stigma extended to those associated with stigmatized individuals, that could lead children to avoid associating with peers who have overweight or obesity due to fear of negative social perception [38]. While our study did not directly measure courtesy stigma, this concept provides a plausible framework to interpret the observed avoidance behavior. Understanding these social dynamics is important for addressing weight stigmatization in childhood, which aligns with the overall aim of identifying behavioral patterns that contribute to social exclusion based on weight. Supporting this interpretation, prior research has shown that individuals with normal weight sitting beside those with overweight are perceived more negatively than when seated next to peers of normal weight [39] a pattern observed in both adult and child populations [40].

Our results indicated a significant association between school type and name, which would highly reflect variations in socioeconomic status, and weight stigmatization tendency, as shown in both the Adjective Attribution and Friendship Selection Tasks. This aligns with previous research that highlighted the effect of socioeconomic and social class factors on attitudes toward weight and obesity stigma. For example, studies have shown that children and adolescents from higher socioeconomic backgrounds may exhibit stronger weight bias, potentially due to greater exposure to cultural ideals emphasizing thinness and health [41]. In contrast, lower socioeconomic status has been associated with different social norms and perceptions around body weight, which can either reduce or increase stigma expression [42]. Additionally, school environments serve as social contexts where biases may be either reinforced or contested, mirroring wider social and economic inequalities [43].

Female participants in our study exhibited higher weight bias in their friendship selections compared to males. Findings in the literature are mixed regarding sex effects on children’s friendship selection, some studies report stronger rejection of overweight figures by girls and higher preference for underweight peers [26, 34] while others found no sex-related differences [24, 44]. Girls may be more susceptible to weight-based teasing, and girls with overweight are more likely to experience stigmatization, whereas boys generally need to meet a higher BMI threshold to be stigmatized [45]. Gender differences in weight stigma are thought to reflect cultural beauty ideals “thinness” for females and “muscularity” for males, transmitted from adults to children [46]. Recognizing these patterns is important for tailoring early interventions to address both gender and social context influences on weight stigma.

In regards to the Controllability Attribution Task, children in our study believed that figures with overweight are responsible for their weights. The majority of children (81.9%) agreed that figures with overweight eat more than required, 77.2% disagreed that they exercise enough, and 63.8% agreed that they can become thin if they tried. These results align with Margulies’ study, which found that preschool-age children have a high tendency to judge others with obesity. The study suggested that controllability beliefs may mediate weight stigmatization among children, which was also expressed through stereotyping at this young age [24]. Similarly, Musher-Eizenman found that children’s prejudice and anti-fat attitudes were related to their belief that being overweight is controllable [27]. It is important to note that oversimplified beliefs about the origin of obesity greatly contribute to weight stigma [27]. As previously discussed, children are exposed early to social and cultural norms that influence these beliefs. Without adequate programs and educational materials to raise awareness, such misconceptions may persist, potentially reinforcing weight stigmatization and its consequences. In light of this, children’s judgmental attitudes toward figures with overweight may also reflect the stereotyping observed in the current study, explaining why most children (91.3%, 83.9%) attributed “hungry” and “lazy” to the figure with overweight, while attributing “healthy” to the figures with normal and underweight.

While weight stigmatization is prevalent across all age groups, it is particularly harmful and long-lasting when it affects children. It negatively affects academic performance, social interactions, physical health, psychological well-being, self-esteem, and overall quality of life [16, 47]. Internalized weight stigma may lead children to prefer isolation due to fear of bullying and teasing [48] and weight-based teasing is associated with unhealthy weight control practices and increased risk of eating disorders among children and adolescents [21]. These findings underscore the importance of addressing weight stigma at an early age to prevent long-term consequences.

It's concerning that there have been no previous studies addressing this issue in Palestine. This lack of research reflects a gap in educational efforts that raise awareness and correct children’s beliefs about obesity and its multifactorial causes. Consequently, weight stigmatization and judgment of individuals with obesity may persist unchecked. Our findings highlight the need for programs that increase children’s understanding of the multiple factors contributing to obesity, which could be implemented through regular school-based lessons. These interventions would help prevent the oversimplification of obesity as merely a matter of personal control, while our newly developed image-based tool provides a means to evaluate the effectiveness of such educational programs. Additionally, understanding the dynamics of socioeconomic status is also essential for designing these interventions that reduce weight stigma in diverse settings.

Finally, promoting equitable treatment of children with overweight or obesity in all settings, including schools, is crucial. Educational messages should emphasize positive messaging on lifestyle rather than weight itself, and media content should be carefully framed to avoid reinforcing weight bias. Taken together, these strategies can help reduce the prevalence and consequences of weight stigmatization among children while providing a foundation for evidence-based interventions in Palestine.

This study is the first to explore weight stigma among school-age children in Palestine, as well as the first to develop a tool for this purpose. We hope it will serve as a foundation for subsequent research, delivering both statistical data on weight stigmatization and a basis for designing effective intervention programs. However, the study has two main limitations. However, the study has two main limitations. First, some children tended to select figures based on familiarity or similarity to themselves, which could bias responses; this could be mitigated in future studies by assigning names to each character. Second, the sample was limited to two private and one UNRWA school with no governmental schools reflecting a specific range of socioeconomic and educational contexts. This limitation introduces potential sampling bias and restricts the generalizability of the findings to other regions, school types, or socioeconomic groups. Therefore, results should be interpreted with caution, and future studies should include a larger, more diverse and more representative sample to validate the tool and better understand weight stigmatization patterns across different contexts.

Conclusion

The present study indicated a high level of weight stigmatization among school aged children in three schools in Nablus. Children exhibited higher attribution of negative traits, and fewer attribution of positive traits to the character with overweight. Also, children showed preference of characters with under and normal weight, while a strong rejection of the character with overweight as a friend, either at school or at different social activity. In addition, the study revealed that children hold a strong belief in the ability of the figures with overweight to control their weight and a strong judgment towards them. Findings highlighted that efforts must be paid towards educational intervention, and the importance of raising children's awareness on the multiple factors contributing to obesity. This study recommended taking action towards reducing weight stigmatization among children to help protect children’s psychological status, reducing obesity prevalence, and improving its management.
