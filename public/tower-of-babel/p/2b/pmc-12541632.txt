Transforming mental health in Europe: from crisis to opportunity
Authors: The Lancet Regional Health – Europe
Published: 2025

Every day, millions of people grapple with mental ill health, often in silence, and too often without support. Approximately 17% of individuals in the WHO European Region have a mental disorder, yet one in three do not receive the treatment they need. Armed conflicts, economic uncertainty, and the climate crisis have intensified demand for mental health services, building on the increased pressures created by the COVID-19 pandemic and exposing deep and longstanding weaknesses in Europe's mental health systems. As we mark World Mental Health Day on Oct 10, this year's theme—“access to services–mental health in catastrophes and emergencies”—underscores the urgency of addressing these weaknesses.

With this in mind, The Lancet Regional Health—Europe has launched a Series on transforming mental health in Europe, chaired by Professor Sir Graham Thornicroft and led by experts in the field, covering four key themes: mental health within the health-care workforce, the mental health of children and young people, the state of mental health systems in central and eastern Europe, and gaps in mental health service coverage across Europe. These topics represent crucial and underexplored dimensions of mental health across the region, providing a robust synthesis of current evidence and emerging challenges, and together reveal both the scale of the crisis and the opportunity for transformation.

Mental health is a crucial part of Europe's health workforce crisis. The EU faces a shortfall of 1.2 million health workers, with many citing psychological distress and burnout as reasons for leaving. Most WHO member states report just one psychiatrist to 200,000 people. These chronic staff shortages, along with high workloads, poor leadership, discrimination, and workplace bullying and violence have contributed to unacceptable levels of psychological distress in health-care workers. As many as 28–32% of doctors and nurses report symptoms consistent with major depressive disorder, and 24% report symptoms of an anxiety disorder. Importantly, although these challenges predated COVID-19, the pandemic intensified pressures on health-care workers and further compromised their mental health.

Meanwhile, children and young people face a rapid decline in mental health. In the UK, suicide rates have consistently increased each year between 1990 and 2020, by up to 2.5% in boys and 8.5% in girls. The ramifications of the pandemic, including social isolation, disrupted education, and increased family stress, have collided with new threats: unregulated digital environments, climate anxiety, and the trauma of war. While children and adolescents try to adapt to the changing world, it is estimated that 95% of those aged 4–18 years are worried about the climate crisis. Escalating military conflicts in the region mean that millions of children have been displaced, with children accounting for around 40% of forcibly displaced people worldwide. Yet, in too many places, mental health services for youth are scarce, fragmented, or inaccessible—especially for the most vulnerable.

Nowhere are the challenges and opportunities for reform starker than in central and eastern Europe, where the legacy of the Soviet era is evident in the continued reliance on institutional psychiatric care, a predominantly biomedical approach, and a misapprehension of the psychosocial aspects of mental health. Systemic challenges, such as weak governance and chronic underfunding are compounded by the new realities of mass displacement and war. Mental health spending across these countries averages at just 3.2% of total health budgets, and most funding is channelled to psychiatric hospitals rather than community-based, preventive, or recovery-focused care. Mental health resources are likely to be further stretched by the ongoing war in Ukraine, leaving neighbouring countries accepting refugees in central and eastern Europe unprepared for the increased mental health burden.

Europe's mental health crisis is marked by profound gaps in service coverage and treatment. Marginalised groups, including refugees, face the largest disparities compared with need. In Türkiye, for example, only 10% of Syrian refugees who felt they needed help actually accessed mental health services. Obstacles to access include unprepared health systems, discrimination, and language barriers. Fragmented data systems and inconsistent coverage measures make it difficult to track progress or hold systems accountable. Across the region, treatment coverage for common mental disorders such as depression and anxiety remains low, and WHO's target—a 50% increase in coverage for severe mental disorders by 2030—will remain out of reach without harmonised data, robust monitoring, and a relentless focus on equity.

Europe must confront the systemic weaknesses that have led to the mental health crisis. Transformation will require more than incremental change. The Series calls on governments, policy makers, and communities to invest boldly and sustainably in mental health, aligning resources with the true burden of disease and prioritising prevention and early intervention through community-based, person-centred approaches. Central to this effort is the creation of harmonised data systems and placing people with lived experience at the heart of policy, service design, and evaluation to guide action. Only through proactive, systemic reforms can we safeguard mental health and uphold the right to care during times of crisis.
