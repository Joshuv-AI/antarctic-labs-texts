CRYOVATE: A Pilot Study of Lung Cancer Cryoactivation in Combination With Immunotherapy in Advanced NSCLC
Authors: Antoine Desilets, Gabryella Pinheiro, Wiam Belkaid, Olivier Salko, Julie Malo, Eleyine Zarour, Adeline Jouquan, Anne-Julie Thibaudeau, Marc-Antoine Nolin, John Stagg, Marie Florescu, Mustapha Tehfe, Normand Blais, Samer Tabchi, Jean Chalaoui, Philippe Stephenson, Arielle Elkrief, Vincent Quoc-Huy Trinh, Bertrand Routy, Moishe Liberman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNSCLC is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality. Although immune-checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have improved survival in patients with advanced NSCLC, treatment resistance remains a challenge. Cryoactivation, a technique inducing cell death by cycles of freezing and thawing, has the potential to augment tumor responses when combined with ICIs.MethodsThis single-arm phase 1 clinical trial enrolled patients with previously untreated advanced NSCLC and 50% or higher programmed cell death ligand-1 (PD-L1). Patients underwent cryoactivation followed by ICI monotherapy initiated 5 days later. The primary end point was the objective response rate. Co-secondary end points included the safety and feasibility of the procedure and overall survival. Immune cell infiltration by immunohistochemistry was performed on paired pre- and post-treatment samples, with patients dichotomized according to clinical benefit (CB) rate (CB versus no CB [NCB]).ResultsEight patients were enrolled. Two patients achieved a partial response, yielding an objective response rate of 25%. Median progression-free survival and overall survival were 3.8 and 13.0 months, respectively. The cryoactivation procedure was well tolerated, without grade 3 to 4 adverse events. Post-hoc analysis reported a CB rate of 50%. Immunohistochemistry analysis reported a numerical difference in the cluster of differentiation 8–positive (CD8+) T cell infiltration in CB versus NCB in the pre- and post-treatment biopsies (p = 0.09) and an increase in CD8+ T cells in the post-treatment biopsies of CB versus NCB (p = 0.03).ConclusionsAlthough cryoactivation combined with pembrolizumab was safe and well tolerated in patients with NSCLC, therapeutic benefits were not evident compared with historical cohorts of ICI monotherapy. Correlative analyses validated CD8+ T cell recruitment in patients deriving CB.Keywords: Cryoactivation, Immunotherapy, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, Non–small cell lung cancer

Introduction

Lung cancer is the primary cause of cancer-related death in North America.1 Immune-checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) represent the treatment backbone for patients with unresectable or advanced NSCLC.2 In the first-line setting, ICIs are standard-of-care either alone (programmed cell death ligand-1 [PD-L1] tumor proportion score [TPS] ≥ 50%)3 or in combination with platinum-doublet chemotherapy (PD-L1 TPS < 50%).4 Nevertheless, primary resistance still occurs in nearly half of treated patients and secondary resistance remains inevitable.5 Defining approaches to overcome treatment resistance represents a critical challenge in the immune-oncology arena and requires elucidation of several factors, including PD-L1 expression, tumor microenvironment contexture, and microbiome composition, to better characterize mechanisms involved in the cancer immune response.6, 7, 8, 9, 10

Many factors are associated with improved responses to ICIs, such as increased PD-L1 expression and high tumor mutational burden.8 In addition, a growing body of evidence suggests that patients who are most likely to derive long-lasting benefits from anti–PD-1 (anti–programmed cell death protein-1) treatment are likely to possess an ‘inflamed’ tumor phenotype, enriched with cluster of differentiation 8–positive (CD8+) tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in combination with high expression of PD-L1 and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) production.11,12 We previously expanded on these findings to determine immune infiltration by RNA sequencing and immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining for CD8+ T cells and PD-L1 in two cohorts comprising a total of 87 patients with advanced NSCLC previously treated with platinum-based chemotherapy. In this study, patients with lymphocytes co-expressing CD8+ T cells and PD-L1 benefited the most from ICIs.9 Therefore, identifying new strategies to transform “non-inflamed low TIL” tumors into “inflamed high TIL” tumors represents an actionable opportunity to decrease primary resistance to ICIs in NSCLC.

Immunogenic cell death is a phenomenon involving the killing of cancer cells which subsequently leads to immunomodulatory signal emission. Such immunogenic signals are conveyed by tumor-associated antigens and by damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) which are translocated outside of the intracellular space and later bind to pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) or toll-like receptors (TLRs) expressed on immune cells, thus activating the immune cascade.13 Typically described damage-associated molecular patterns include cytosolic adenosine triphosphate (ATP), endoplasmic reticulum chaperone calreticulin (CALR), and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1).14, 15, 16 Multiple inducers of immunogenic cell death have been described, such as cytotoxic agents or radiotherapy (RT), and are thought to act through dendritic cell signaling and downstream T cell activation.17 More recently, cryotherapy has also emerged as a possible immune-modulating strategy, eliciting tumor responses both in mice models and in the clinical setting.18, 19, 20, 21

Cryoactivation, a procedure by which tumor cell death is induced through cycles of freezing and thawing, represents an innovative activation technique aimed at triggering immunogenic cell death. The procedure involves the insertion of a cryoprobe in the tumor bed with subsequent application of very low temperatures leading to cell necrosis and tumor antigens release. In the absence of heat-related denaturation seen with ablation therapies (e.g., microwave, radiofrequency, high-intensity focused ultrasound [US]) and as intracellular content remains in circulation after cryoactivation, we hypothesized that such technique could induce immune-specific reactions influencing cancer cells outside of the ablated region.22, 23, 24 This phenomenon would be reminiscent of the abscopal effect, a reaction mediated by locoregional RT exposure with the potential to trigger a systemic immune response prompting metastatic disease regression.17 Although such immune activation would in itself be insufficient to eradicate tumor cells at distant sites, cotreatment with ICIs in the advanced setting could represent a synergistic therapeutic avenue to potentiate treatment responses.22 In this pilot study, we aimed to assess the safety and feasibility of combined cryoactivation in patients with advanced NSCLC before ICI initiation to transform ‘cold’ tumors into ‘warm’ tumors, with the intent to improve ICI efficacy.

Materials and Methods

Patient Selection

This study was a single-arm investigator-initiated phase 1 clinical trial conducted at the Centre Hospitalier de l’Université de Montréal (CHUM) in Montreal, Canada. Patients with pathologically or cytologically confirmed NSCLC (squamous or adenocarcinoma) were eligible if they met the following inclusion criteria: age 18 years or older, previously untreated metastatic or unresectable disease not amenable to curative treatment, 50% or higher PD-L1 TPS, presence of measurable disease as determined by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1, amenable to receive first-line pembrolizumab monotherapy. Patients had to have disease amenable to biopsy and be willing and able to undergo tumor biopsies at baseline and at 4 weeks after anti–PD-1 initiation. Lung lesions or proven metastatic lymph nodes had to be safely accessible to percutaneous treatment, flexible bronchoscopy, endobronchial US, or endoscopic US. Key exclusion criteria included the presence of targetable oncogenic drivers on tumor sample (i.e., EGFR, ALK, ROS1, or BRAF alterations), prior history of autoimmune disorders, and systemic anticoagulation and systemic glucocorticoids at a dose exceeding 10 mg/d of prednisone (or dosing equivalents). A complete list of eligibility criteria is available in the Supplementary Materials. This study was approved by the institutional review board at CHUM (Institutional Review Board number CE20.365), registered with the U.S. National Cancer Institute National Clinical Trials Network (NCT04793815), and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice Guidelines. Informed written consent was obtained for all patients.

Interventions

Target lesions for cryoactivation were selected by the thoracic surgery team after a thorough radiological evaluation of patients’ pretreatment imaging to ensure patient safety and minimize the invasiveness of the procedure. When present, superficial soft tissue or lymph node metastases were selected and cryoprobe placement was guided using peripheral US. For lesions not accessible percutaneously, lung lesions safely approached through endoscopic or computed tomography (CT) guidance were chosen to minimize the risk of damage to adjacent tissues. The cryoactivation procedure was performed by Moishe Liberman in the hybrid operating room at the CHUM 5 days before the first treatment with pembrolizumab. All procedures were carried out under real-time cone beam CT guidance (Artis Zeego, Siemens), real-time US (peripheral US, endobronchial US or endoscopic US), or direct tumor visualization by means of bronchoscopy to ensure accurate cryoprobe placement and to prevent displacement during the cryoactivation procedure. All health care workers involved in the procedure wore radioprotective gear during CT scanning. Re-biopsy of the lesion was conducted before cryoactivation and assessed using rapid on-site cytology with the cytopathologist present in the operating room. Cryoprobes were positioned as close to the center of the lesion or lymph node as possible and were activated for 30 seconds, repeated three times. Gas-powered cryoprobes using carbon dioxide (CO2) were activated after ensuring accurate placement. The tissue was frozen between −75°C and −80°C at the tip of the probe. After activation, the cryoprobe remained in place for a total of 60 seconds to allow for thawing of the probe tip and safe removal from the tissue without causing tissue devitalization.

Participants received pembrolizumab 2 mg/kg intravenously every 3 weeks, with the first dose administered 5 days after the cryoactivation procedure. Pembrolizumab was continued for up to 2 years or until evidence of disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. Radiographic tumor imaging was performed at baseline and scheduled every 8 weeks thereafter. Re-biopsy of the primary or metastatic mediastinal/pulmonary parenchymal sites was conducted in accordance with a predefined re-biopsy schedule, which included a mandatory re-biopsy at 4 weeks and optional re-biopsies at 3 months and in the event of disease progression. For biopsies of primary pulmonary parenchymal tumors, secondary parenchymal tumors, or metastatic lymph nodes, core biopsy techniques were employed, using either a 19- or 21-gauge needle, depending on the accessibility of the tumor or lymph node. To ensure the adequacy of the samples obtained, rapid on-site cytology was consistently employed. The biopsy samples were preserved as formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded specimens for correlative studies. The selection of biopsy sites was determined on the basis of considerations of lesion accessibility and size.

Outcomes

The primary end point of this study was the objective response rate (ORR). ORR was defined as the percentage of patients with either a complete response (CR) or a partial response (PR) according to RECIST version 1.1. Secondary end points included safety, progression-free survival (PFS), and overall survival (OS). PFS was defined as the time from the cryoactivation procedure until progressive disease (PD) or death from any cause, whereas OS was defined as the time from the cryoactivation procedure until death from any cause. Clinical benefit (CB) was assessed as a post-hoc analysis and was defined as the percentage of patients with either CR, PR, or stable disease as the best response. In the safety analysis, adverse events (AEs) were monitored continuously throughout the treatment period and graded according to Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 4.0. For most grade 3 immune-related AEs (irAEs), including pneumonitis, colitis, and hepatitis, pembrolizumab was permanently discontinued and corticosteroid treatment was initiated. Possible exceptions included grade 3 endocrinopathies or cutaneous toxicities: the potential for anti–PD-1 rechallenge was evaluated on an individual basis after consultation with the trial investigators. Patients who completed the cryoactivation procedure were included in the safety analysis, whereas patients who completed both the cryoactivation procedure and received the first dose of pembrolizumab were included in the efficacy analysis. All AEs were reviewed by an independent institutional Data Safety Monitoring Committee.

Immunohistochemistry

Research biopsy samples were subjected to IHC to evaluate immune cell infiltration. After hematoxylin and eosin staining, an immunohistochemical study of CD4, CD8, and CD163 was performed on 4 μm-thick unstained slides by Vincent Quoc-Huy Trinh. Cluster of differentiation 4 (CD4) staining was conducted using CD4 Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated antibody (Bio-Techne, #FAB8165G). CD8 staining for cytotoxic T cells was performed using CD8 monoclonal antibody (#53-0008-82; ThermoFisher). CD163 macrophage staining was performed using recombinant monoclonal CD163 antibody (#ab281746, Abcam). Staining was done with a Bond RX autostainer (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) with appropriate positive and negative controls and reviewed by a board-certified pathologist (Vincent Quoc-Huy Trinh). Slides were scanned with a Hamamatsu Nanozoomer HT2.0 at 20X, and whole-slide images were loaded in QuPath 0.5.0 with the H-DAB profile. Annotations of the whole tissue and the peritumoral (approximately 100 μm radius) area were performed by the pathologist. Anthracosis and tissue artifacts were removed from the annotated areas. An automated script was then used to evaluate the percentage of positive cells within the entire transected specimen (tissue area) and the percentage of positive cells within the peritumoral area collectively referred to as the tumor area (Supplementary Fig. 1). The QuPath automated cell detection script can be found in Supplementary Materials. As part of exploratory analyses, immune cell infiltration was compared in paired pre-cryoactivation and post-cryoactivation samples from each patient. Additional evaluations were based on CB rate, with patients dichotomized as deriving CB or no CB (NCB), and OS—grouped as above or below the median.

Statistical Design

A two-stage Simon design with interim futility analysis was used for sample size estimation.25 On the basis of the results of KEYNOTE-042,26 we adopted a null hypothesis of an objective response rate (ORR) of 35% and a desired ORR of 60%. To reveal that the ORR in the studied population was higher than 35% with a one-sided type I error of 10% and power of 80%, at least 10 responses needed to be observed among 19 patients enrolled. In the first stage, eight patients would be accrued. If two or fewer responses were observed, the trial would be stopped because of futility. Otherwise, 11 additional patients would be enrolled for a total of 19 patients. PFS and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method. Survival outcomes according to baseline clinicopathologic characteristics were compared using the log-rank test in univariable analyses to determine hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals. Paired t-tests were used to compare the percentage of immune cells in pretreatment versus post-treatment samples. Two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) tests were used to evaluate the interaction between the percentage of immune cells and efficacy outcomes (CB and OS). All tests were two-sided and statistical significance was set at a p value of less than 0.05. Statistical analyses were conducted using the R Studio (Posit, PBC) and GraphPad Prism (GraphPad software). Data are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

From June 2021 to January 2022, nine patients were screened and a total of eight patients were enrolled in the study. One screen failure was attributable to clinical deterioration and consent withdrawal before the cryoactivation date. Baseline characteristics are reported in Table 1. Median age was 66 years (range: 57 to 79 y) and 50% of the patients were female individuals. All patients were Caucasian. Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status was 1 in 75% of patients (n = 6). All patients had a positive smoking history and 25% (n = 2) were current smokers. The median smoking history was 42.5 pack years. The most prevalent lung tumor histological subtype was adenocarcinoma in 88% of patients (n = 7). As prespecified in the inclusion criteria, all patients (n = 8) had a PD-L1 TPS expression of 50% or higher, without concurrent EGFR, ALK, ROS1, or BRAF alterations. Three patients had KRAS mutations, including one patient with a KRASG12C mutation. Most patients (88%, n = 7) had stage IV disease at trial enrolment, and one patient had stage II NSCLC not amenable to curative treatment. Technical and safety data on individual cryoactivation procedures are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Efficacy

Two patients had confirmed PR, with an ORR of 25%, thus not meeting the primary end point of the study which was 35% (Table 2). Two patients had stable disease as their best response, with a CB of 50%. Four patients (50%) had primary PD, including one patient with early clinical progression who required a chemo-immunotherapy combination from cycle 2, with the addition of carboplatin-pemetrexed to pembrolizumab after clinical decision and consent withdrawal. With a median follow-up of 30.9 months, the median PFS was 3.8 months and the median OS was 13.0 months (Supplementary Fig. 2A and B). Three patients were still alive as of the last follow-up, with OS ranging from 26.8 to 30.9 months. In the overall population, the median time on treatment was 3 months (range: 1 to 22 mo) (Fig. 1). The patient with stage II NSCLC had a PFS of 5.2 months and an OS of 10.3 months. All patients ultimately progressed on pembrolizumab (Supplementary Fig. 3). In the second-line setting, four patients were treated with the best supportive care: three patients were not eligible for additional systemic therapies owing to declining performance status and one patient declined further treatment and opted for hospice care. Four patients were treated with the carboplatin-pemetrexed chemotherapy regimen after progression on pembrolizumab, all of whom had prolonged responses ranging from 8 to 12 months in the second-line setting. Five deaths were reported on trial, all of which were attributable to cancer progression. In univariable analysis, the presence of a KRAS mutation was associated with a trend for improved OS (hazard ratio for mortality = 0.2, 95% confidence interval: 0.1–1.4, p = 0.15). No significant differences in PFS or OS were observed when considering age, sex, ECOG, recent antibiotic use, and occurrence of irAEs.

Safety

Treatment-related grade 1 or 2 AEs were reported in six patients (75%). No grade 3 or higher AEs were reported on this trial. Grade 1 to 2 complications following the cryoactivation procedure were reported in four patients, including neck edema (grade 1), axillary lymph node pain (grade 1), pneumoperitoneum (grade 1), and pneumothorax (grade 1) (Table 3). Both pneumoperitoneum and pneumothorax cases were asymptomatic and diagnosed on routine follow-up imaging after the cryoactivation and biopsy procedures, without hemodynamic instability or need for intervention. In addition, no complications were reported after the 4-week research biopsy. When evaluating irAEs, two cases of thyroiditis were reported (grade 1 and grade 2) and one case of skin rash (grade 2) was reported (Table 4). None of the patients required pembrolizumab interruption or systemic corticosteroid treatment for irAEs.

Immune Cell Infiltration

All patients underwent biopsy at baseline of the tumor site subjected to cryoactivation. A total of five patients (63%) had a 4-week research biopsy performed and were amenable to the differential analysis of immune cell infiltration in IHC (Supplementary Table 1). All five biopsies had viable tumor cells on tissue analysis (Fig. 2A). In terms of the biopsy site, three samples originated from lymph nodes, whereas two samples were obtained from lung tumors. First, we observed a numerical increase in CD8+ T cell infiltration in pre- versus post-biopsy in CB compared with NCB (p = 0.09) (Fig. 2B). Second, when assessing CD8+ T cell infiltration pre- and post-cryoactivation in CB versus NCB, we found a significant up-regulation in CD8+ T cells in CB (p = 0.02). A similar signal was observed when dichotomizing patients according to OS (Fig. 2C). Of note, the increase in CD8+ T cell infiltration was mostly driven by patients with LN metastases subjected to cryoactivation, with the caveat of a limited number of patients in each anatomical subgroup (Supplementary Fig. 4). No significant difference in CD4+ or CD163+ cells was observed between paired samples (Supplementary Figs. 5A and B and 6A and B).

Discussion

In this phase 1 study, we enrolled eight patients with unresectable or advanced NSCLC characterized by a PD-L1 expression level of 50% or higher. These patients were eligible for pembrolizumab monotherapy and underwent a cryoactivation procedure, followed by anti–PD-1 treatment 5 days after cryoactivation. The cryoactivation procedure was well tolerated in all patients, and there were no unexpected AEs. Local complications were limited to grade 1 events, primarily localized pain, edema, and non-clinically relevant pneumothorax or pneumoperitoneum, all of which were managed conservatively and did not result in any delays in ICI initiation. Furthermore, the addition of cryoactivation to anti–PD-1 treatment did not raise concerns regarding irAEs. Notably, irAEs were limited to grade 1 to 2 immune-related thyroiditis or skin rash, which did not necessitate treatment discontinuation or the initiation of corticosteroid therapy.

With two patients achieving a confirmed PR according to RECIST version 1.1, this study failed to meet its primary end point and was stopped prematurely after the enrolment of eight patients. In contrast, CB was 50%, with three patients remaining on pembrolizumab monotherapy for more than 12 months (Fig. 1). These results compare somewhat unfavorably with historical reports from ICI monotherapy demonstrating an ORR of 38% to 39% in patients with 50% or higher PD-L1 tumors. In these studies, the median OS ranged between 20 and 26 months, versus 13.0 months in this study cohort.26, 27, 28 Several factors may have contributed to these discrepancies, including the inclusion of patients with unfavorable ECOG performance status in our study, reflective of a real-world population with multiple comorbidities. In addition, patients included in our study were older with 63% of patients aged over 65 years, when compared with 44% in KEYNOTE-048, and many refused second-line therapy options and opted for best supportive care. Nonetheless, three patients presented an OS of 24 months or longer and derived prolonged benefits from second-line regimens, which we posit could reflect the impact of initial immunomodulatory therapy on their overall disease trajectory.

Correlative analyses of matched pre- and post-treatment samples in our study found a numerical increase in CD8+ T cell levels after cryoactivation and pembrolizumab, with significant enrichment of CD8+ T cells observed in post-treatment samples of patients deriving CB from the combination. Such findings are reflective of preclinical studies reporting increased levels of cytotoxic T cell populations and IFN response in NSCLC allograft murine models subjected to cryoablation.29,30 Whereas cryoablation alone resulted in growth inhibition in non-ablated tumors in one of those studies, combination with anti–PD-1 exerted a synergistic effect with further antiproliferative effect than either treatment alone.29 Additional models in histologies other than NSCLC suggested CD8+ T cell infiltration could be facilitated by C-X-C motif chemokine ligand-9 (CXCL9) secretion from dendritic cells after cryoactivation, and ultimately translated into up-regulation of IFN-γ and granzyme B.31,32

Few clinical studies have previously investigated the use of cryotherapy as an adjuvant technique to improve cancer immunogenicity. A retrospective study published by Feng et al.33 evaluated the impact of argon-helium cryoablation combined with nivolumab versus nivolumab alone in 64 patients with advanced NSCLC. Findings included a numerically increased ORR from 6.3% in the nivolumab alone group to 15.6% in the cryoablation and nivolumab group, with a significant decrease in primary resistance rate, from 37.5% with anti–PD-1 alone to 12.5% when used in combination with cryoablation. A retrospective study of chemotherapy with or without endoscopic cryotherapy in both small-cell and NSCLC found an ORR of 72.1% with the cryotherapy combination when compared with 56.9% in the control group. In contrast, the median OS was 17.5 months in the cryotherapy group versus 14 months with chemotherapy alone.34 Response to anti–PD-1 therapy and transbronchial cryoablation has also been documented with pulmonary sarcomatoid carcinoma.35 Additional studies investigating the efficacy of cryoactivation in the context of NSCLC are currently ongoing (NCT04339218 and NCT04049474).

Despite representing the first study evaluating the impact of cryoactivation and ICI on immune cell infiltration in patients with NSCLC, this study had several limitations. First, the results of univariable analyses should be interpreted cautiously, given the limited number of patients, which may have hindered the detection of intergroup differences. Second, one patient withdrew consent prematurely after receiving a single dose of pembrolizumab because of increased respiratory symptoms without radiographic evidence of disease progression. This patient was conservatively classified as having PD, further limiting the interpretation of ORR in this study. Third, our study results do not allow us to delineate the differential impact of cryoactivation-associated necrosis and ICI monotherapy on the CD8+ T cell infiltration pattern observed in post-treatment samples. Finally, argon-helium cryoprobes are associated with faster cooling rates in the context of cryoablation procedures, when compared with CO2-based cryoprobes used in the present study.36, 37, 38 Although cell necrosis from cryogenic cell death has been documented when bringing cancer cells below the −20°C thermal zone,37,39,40 it remains difficult to ascertain the effective temperature reached at the tip of our CO2-based cryoprobes following freezing cycles of 30 seconds, with few in vivo studies providing a rationale for such approach.38 Whereas the goal of this pilot study was to induce immunogenic cell death in a limited number of cells while minimizing the morbidity of the cryoactivation procedure, additional studies should nonetheless evaluate the impact of argon-helium-based cryoprobes or extended freezing cycles on the immunogenicity of cryotherapy. Similarly, the optimal timing of ICI administration remains elusive, with cryogenic injury resulting in both immediate (within minutes) and delayed (within hours to days) cell necrosis and tumor antigen release.40, 41, 42, 43, 44 Future studies should consider exploring earlier ICI administration schedules to optimize immunogenic cell death.

Despite the potential for tumor antigen release in circulation achieved through cryotherapy without heat-associated denaturation, our study findings align with prior analyses which have failed to reveal consistent predictive patterns of immune activation when combining ICI with additional local ablative therapies.45 To that level, the abscopal effect, whereby RT at one metastatic site may trigger regression of cancer at distant non-irradiated sites, has historically been the subject of substantial debate. The rarity of this phenomenon could be attributed to the presence of preexisting immune tolerance mechanisms at the time of treatment, which could impede the emergence of sufficiently robust abscopal responses.45,46 Notably, published case reports of the abscopal effect have predominantly been reported in renal cell carcinoma, melanoma, and lymphoma, which are disease sites traditionally considered immunogenic, and where many modern immunotherapeutic strategies were pioneered.47 The scarcity of documented cases of abscopal effects emphasizes the substantial threshold of immune activation which may be required for translation into clinically meaningful responses, even with the addition of ICIs. Alternative ablation strategies have been suggested, including concurrent treatment of multiple metastatic sites through stereotactic beam RT to overcome the heterogeneity of tumor-associated antigens between metastatic sites.46

In conclusion, this phase 1 study underscored the feasibility and safety of lung cancer cryoactivation in patients with NSCLC treated with pembrolizumab monotherapy, both in terms of local and immune-related complications. Nevertheless, meaningful responses and survival benefits were not observed when compared with historical cohorts of patients with 50% or higher advanced PD-L1 NSCLC treated with ICI monotherapy. Additional studies are required to elucidate mechanisms underpinning the abscopal effect using modern ablation therapy modalities and to conduct additional correlative studies aimed at documenting their immunologic effects on the tumor microenvironment.
