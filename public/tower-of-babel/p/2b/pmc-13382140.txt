TRF2 enriched cytoplasmic chromatin drives cGAS-STAT1-mediated inflammation in senescence
Authors: Renuka Kandhaya-Pillai, Hee Yeon Kim, Jakub Belhadj, Julia M Sidorova, Tamar Tchkonia, James L Kirkland, Junko Oshima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTelomere dysfunction, persistent DNA damage, and chronic inflammation are interconnected hallmarks of cellular senescence, but their molecular links remains poorly defined. Here, we find that senescent cells accumulate telomeric protein TRF2-enriched cytoplasmic chromatin fragments (CCFs), which co-localize with γH2AX and H3K27me3 but lack DNA repair proteins, indicating persistent, unrepaired damage. Inflammatory stress, nuclear lamina dysfunction, and progeroid mutations compromise nuclear integrity, promoting nuclear rupture and extrusion of TRF2-bound nuclear DNA fragments into the cytoplasm. TRF2-enriched CCFs engage the cGAS-STAT1 pathway, sustaining SASP production and reinforcing senescence. Pharmacological inhibition of JAK-STAT or metformin treatment suppressed TRF2-enriched CCFs, reduced cGAS-STAT1 activation, DNA damage markers, and SASP. Our study uncovers TRF2-enriched CCFs as a previously unrecognized source of nuclear-derived DNA in senescent cells. We propose that damaged telomeric DNA extruded into the cytoplasm drives chronic immune activation and inflammation, linking nuclear instability to accelerated aging.Keywords: chronic inflammation, senescence, cytoplasmic chromatin fragements, TRF2, DNA damage

Introduction

Aging is one of the greatest risk factors for most chronic diseases.1,2 Telomeric damage, chronic sterile inflammation, and cellular senescence are interconnected biological processes of aging that are known to play significant roles in development of age-related conditions.3,4,5,6 Senescent cells accumulating during aging appear to be a source of chronic inflammation due to secretion of senescence associated secretory phenotype (SASP) factors. A viscous feedback cycle between persistent DNA damage response (DDR) and the SASP contributes to induction, maintenance, and spread of senescence. We and others have shown that the genetic or pharmacological clearance of senescent cells or suppressing inflammatory SASP factors delays, prevents, or alleviates multiple age-related pathologies.7,8,9,10,11 However, the molecular basis of connections among these aging hallmarks is not well defined and addressing this is critical for developing therapeutic strategies to promote healthy aging and enhance healthspan.

During aging, chronic low-grade inflammation, or “inflamm-aging”, alters immune function, causes telomeric damage, and induces cellular senescence through the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS).12,13 Inflammatory mediators and ROS produced during chronic sterile inflammation can directly cause DNA damage by inducing oxidative stress, double-strand DNA breaks, and genomic instability.14,15,16 Our previous study demonstrated that endothelial cells made senescent by the proinflammatory cytokine TNF-α (an important SASP factor) had profoundly increased ROS production and oxidative DNA damage.11 Similarly, multiple studies link chronic inflammation and telomeric dysfunction to progeroid aging.13,17,18 Telomeric dysfunction triggers a persistent DDR and contributes to senescence.15 Several studies have demonstrated that accumulation of damaged DNA foci localized within telomeres promotes and sustains inflammation and accelerates senescence.5,15,19,20 Synergistic interaction between persistent DNA damage responses and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) contributes to both stable cell-cycle arrest and the spread of senescence. However, the molecular signals that link these cellular events are not well defined.

DNA damage, whether caused by intrinsic or extrinsic stress, can lead to leakage of DNA from the nucleus or mitochondria into the cytosol.21,22,23 Senescent cells accumulate extranuclear DNA, including micronuclei, cytoplasmic chromatin fragments (CCF), retrotransposon cDNA, and DNA expelled from mitochondria.21,22,24 The appearance of cytosolic DNA, regardless of its origin, is detected by the cGAS-STING pathway, triggering an immune and inflammatory response. Several groups have identified a consistent pattern of aberrant cGAS-STING activity in different types of senescence, including cells undergoing replicative senescence with telomere shortening.25,26 Although our understanding of how DNA damage contributes to the emergence of extranuclear DNA is growing, molecular mechanisms linking cytoplasmic DNA-driven inflammation to aging remain to be explored.

While studying the role of cGAS in senescence, we found that senescent cells have increased telomeric TRF2 foci enriched in cytoplasmic DNA that colocalizes with the DNA damage marker γH2AX and cGAS protein. We observed that multiple types of senescent cells, including in cytokine-induced, replicative, and accelerated premature aging models, have cytoplasmic TRF2 colocalized with DNA. We found that defects or mutations in nuclear lamins result in nuclear rupture, leading to leakage of damaged telomere associated TRF2 from the nucleus to the cytosol. Treatment with a JAK inhibitor or metformin prevented the generation of cytosolic TRF2, suppressed cGAS-STAT1-dependent SASP production, and decreased DNA damage. Our findings demonstrate that the formation of cytoplasmic TRF2-bound chromatin fragments is a hallmark feature of senescent cells, linking DNA damage to aberrant immune activation and sustained SASP signature.

Results

Chronic exogenous TNFα activates the cGAS-mediated SASP

In previous work we found that chronic exposure to the proinflammatory cytokine TNFα, alone or in combination with other SASP related cytokines including IFNγ and IL-6, accelerates senescence in endothelial cells.11,27 Specifically, TNFα-mediated senescence involved sustained expression of SASP factors and interferon-stimulated genes. At the time of these findings, the underlying cause of the persistent interferon response was unclear. It is now more evident that formation of cytoplasmic DNA and activation of the cGAS-STING pathway are central drivers of interferon responses and inflammation in senescence.22,28 To examine whether pro-inflammatory stimuli promote cytoplasmic DNA accumulation and activate cGAS signals, we treated primary endothelial cells with TNFα. TNFα exposure induced a time-dependent increase in cGAS protein expression, which was accompanied by elevated p16 levels and increased SA-β-gal positive cells (Figures 1A and 1B). Western blot analysis confirmed that stimulation with various cytokines led to increased cGAS protein expression (Figure S1A), with TNFα alone eliciting a strong response. Notably, co-stimulation of TNFα with IFNγ amplified STING levels, which correlated with increased STAT1 and senescence markers (Figure S1B). Prolonged TNFα exposure also led to the appearance of DAPI-positive DNA fragments in the cytoplasm. Further dual immunostaining revealed that these cytoplasmic DNA fragments were positive for cGAS and colocalized with γH2AX, indicating double stranded DNA breaks in the cytosol (Figures 1C and 1D). A similar pattern was observed in replication-induced senescent endothelial cells (Figure S1C). To verify this phenomenon in a pathological aging model, we examined fibroblast cell lines derived from Werner syndrome (WRN)-mutant patients. WS cells had increased accumulation of cytoplasmic DNA bound to cGAS and γH2AX compared to normal proliferating fibroblasts (Figure 1E). Quantification revealed 38% of WS cells were positive for cytoplasmic DAPI, and among these cells, 89% exhibited positive co-localization of cGAS and γH2AX, indicating DNA damage signaling (Figure 1F). Moreover, several prominent SASP components were elevated in both TNFα-treated and WS cells (Figure 1F). These data suggest that chronic inflammatory stress can trigger release of nuclear DNA into the cytoplasm and aberrant cGAS-STING activation, supporting a link between inflammation, DNA damage, and accelerated senescence.

Cytoplasmic TRF2 fragments increase in senescent cells

The formation of CCF is a hallmark of senescence and age-associated inflammation,21 yet their genomic origin and molecular identity remain to be fully explored. While investigating endogenous sources of cytoplasmic DNA in senescent cells, we unexpectedly discovered that primary endothelial cells, upon entering replicative senescence, had accumulation of DNA fragments in the cytoplasm that stained positively for the telomeric protein TRF2 and colocalized with nuclear DAPI (Figure 2A). In proliferating endothelial cells, TRF2 was confined to the nucleus, whereas TNFα-induced senescent cells displayed TRF2 in the cytoplasm colocalizing with DAPI-positive DNA fragments (Figure 2B). Quantification revealed that both TNFα-induced and replicatively senesced cells had a higher proportion of cytoplasmic DAPI positive and TRF2- cytoplasmic DAPI enriched cells compared to proliferating cells (Figures 2C, 2D, and S2A). We next verified whether accumulation of TRF2 bound to nuclear DNA in cytoplasm is a general feature of senescence. Confocal imaging revealed increased TRF2-bound cytoplasmic DNA fragments colocalized with γH2AX in primary old fibroblasts, but not in young fibroblasts (Figure 2E). Importantly, across multiple senescence models including TNFα-mediated, replicative, and WS senescent cells, we observed increased TRF2 bound to cytoplasmic DNA fragments that stained positively for γH2AX, indicating double-stranded DNA breaks specifically at telomeres (Figure S2B). Quantification across models showed that the percentage of cells with cytoplasmic TRF2 varied with the senescence-inducing stress and correlated with SA-β-gal activity (Figures 2G, S2B, and S2C). To further characterize the identity of these TRF2 bound cytoplasmic DNA, we examined several markers of CCFs. In WS fibroblasts, TRF2-positive cytoplasmic DNA fragments were enriched for the heterochromatin marker H3K27me3 (Figure 2F). Cytoplasmic TRF2-positive fragments were enriched for the heterochromatin marker H3K27me3, the innate immune sensor cGAS, and nuclear Lamin B1, while they lacked the proliferation marker Ki67 and the DNA repair protein 53BP1 (Figures 2F–2H, S2B–S2D, S3A, and S4A). These findings establish TRF2-bound cytoplasmic DNA as a conserved feature of senescent cells and form the basis for the hypothesis that nuclear-derived CCFs are enriched for telomere-associated components.

Reduction or mutation in lamins increases cytoplasmicTRF2 and SASP production

Lamins are central components of the nuclear envelope, essential for maintaining nuclear integrity and chromatin organization. Disruption or loss of nuclear envelope integrity has been associated with aging processes and with several pathologies associated with aging.29,30 To assess whether proinflammatory stimuli perturb nuclear lamin architecture, we treated endothelial cells with TNFα and evaluated lamin expression. Western analysis revealed cells exposed to TNFα had decreased lamin B1 protein in a dose- and time-dependent manner, while levels of lamin A/C were modestly decreased (Figure 3A). Notably, short-term TNFα exposure (3 or 6 h) did not affect lamin B1 levels, but more prolonged exposure led to a trend to a decrease at 24 h, with a significant reduction observed at 72 h. This suggests that chronic inflammatory stress is required to alter nuclear lamin integrity. Compromised nuclear integrity is known to cause nuclear rupture, chromatin herniations, DNA damage, and impaired nuclear repair.31 To determine whether nuclear rupture occurs in our senescence model, we stained proliferating and senescent endothelial cells with lamin A/C or lamin B1 antibodies in combination with TRF2 and nuclear DAPI. Strikingly, in cells exposed to TNFα we observed events of nuclear rupture, accompanied by the leakage of nuclear DNA into the cytoplasm, where it overlapped with nuclear lamins in the cytoplasm, while this was absent in untreated control cells (Figure 3B). Quantitative analysis revealed a significant increase in TRF2 colocalization with both lamin A/C and lamin B1 in the cytoplasm upon TNFα stimulation compared to proliferating cells (Figure 3C). Furthermore, nearly 50%–60% of DAPI-positive cytoplasmic TRF2 puncta overlapped with lamin B1 or lamin A/C (Figure 3D), indicating that lamins encapsulated telomere-derived DNA fragments extruded from nucleus into the cytoplasm. Similarly, confocal microscopy of WS fibroblast cells revealed reduction in lamin B1 in the nucleus and increased leakage of nuclear DNA into the cytoplasm (Figure S3B). Lamin mutations in humans causes premature aging syndromes, including Hutchinson-Gilford progeria syndrome (HGPS), atypical Werner syndrome, and a group of other disorders collectively termed laminopathies.32 We used primary cells from HGPS and LMNA mutant models to study the link between lamins and cytoplasmic telomeric fragments. Primary fibroblasts isolated from HGPS patients showed accumulation of progerin and increased expression of senescence markers (Figure 3E). Nuclear DAPI staining of HGPS cells revealed altered, misshapen, irregular nuclei compared to control cells. Notably, confocal imaging indicated nuclear envelope ruptures and leakage of nuclear DNA into the cytoplasm that colocalized with lamin A/C and TRF2 in WS, HGPS, and LMNA mutant senescent cells (Figure 3F). LMNA-mutant cells had comparable phenotypes, confirming lamin dysfunction facilitates nuclear rupture and extrusion of nuclear DNA along with telomeric protein TRF2 from the nucleus into the cytoplasmic region (Figures 3F and S3C). Furthermore, both HGPS and LMNA mutant cells showed increased expression of multiple SASP-related inflammatory genes, indicating those associated with lamin in proinflammatory responses (Figure 3G). These data indicate both chronic pro-inflammatory stimuli and defects in lamin structure compromise nuclear integrity, leading to leakage of nuclear DNA associated with telomeric protein TRF2 into the cytoplasm, and causing SASP-related inflammation.

Damage in cytoplasmic TRF2 is persistent and lacks DNA repair

Damage in telomeric DNA is often irreparable, typically leading to a prolonged DDR, continuous inflammation, and genomic instability.11,15,33 We therefore suspected presence TRF2 bound cytoplasmic DNA could be associated with persistent, unrepaired DNA damage. We first assessed the distribution of DNA damage markers in both the nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments of senescent cells using immunostaining. Senescent cells displayed a significant increase in nuclear γH2AX and 53BP1 foci compared to non-senescent controls, with over 80% of nuclear 53BP1 foci colocalizing with γH2AX, indicative of a sustained DDR (Figure 4A). Strikingly, we also observed elevated levels of γH2AX in the cytoplasm, where it colocalized with the telomere-binding protein TRF2 and DAPI-stained DNA. In contrast, only a minor fraction of 53BP1 foci were seen in the cytoplasm. These foci were largely negative for TRF2 and lacked localization of γH2AX, further confirming the absence of repair protein 53BP1 recruitment to the damaged DNA regions in the cytoplasmic compartment (Figures S4A and S4B). This finding prompted us to monitor levels of DNA repair proteins. We recently reported that multiple genes associated with DNA repair, replication, and chromatin organization are decreased in both normal and stress mediated senescence.34 Consistent with this, compared to proliferating cells, senescent cells showed decreased expression of DNA repair components, as confirmed by western blotting (Figure S4C). Confocal images showed a reduction or lack of recruitment of both RAD51 and MRE11 to cytoplasmic TRF2, while a small fraction of MRE11 puncta was seen around the cytoplasmic region (Figure 4B). This result is consistent with the lack of a repair process at cytoplasmic telomeric DNA lesions, despite the presence of DNA damage responses.

Next, to validate the previous findings, we used the nucleoside analog 6-thio-2′-deoxyguanosine (6-thio-dG) to induce dysfunctional telomeres in telomerase-positive hTERT fibroblasts. Consistent with previous reports, 6-thio-dG exposure induced cell-cycle arrest, as evidenced by reduced proliferation and increased levels of p16 and SA-β-gal activity (Figures 4C–4E). We evaluated the persistence of damage following a single 10-day exposure to 6-thio-dG, or after a 5-day recovery period or post-treatment. We found sustained expression of SASP components included TNF and IL-6 after drug withdrawal Figure 4G. In addition, western analysis showed elevated levels of STAT1, cGAS-STNG, and DNA damage markers even five days after drug withdrawal (post) (Figure 4H), indicating chronic damage-associated inflammation. This was verified in endothelial cells recovering from cytokine treatment (Figure S4D). Together, these data indicate TRF2 bound cytoplasmic DNA accumulates persistent damage with compromised DNA repair and contributes to prolonged inflammation and genomic instability.

Crosstalk between cytoplasmic TRF2 and the cGAS-STAT1 axis

Multiple singling pathways are involved in senescence-associated inflammation and DNA damage. We previously demonstrated cytokine-induced senescence establishes positive feedback loop through hyper-activation of the JAK/STAT pathway, leading to prolonged SASP production and an interferon signature. To uncover the molecular mechanism for induction of cytoplasmic DNA-related TRF2, we focused on the relationship between cGAS and STAT1. Western blot and mRNA analyses revealed increased STAT1 phosphorylation across multiple models of senescence including replicative, WS progeroid cells, and LMNA mutant (Figures S5A and S5B). Because transcriptional activation of JAK/STAT and phosphorylation of STAT1 are downstream effectors of interferon response genes, we hypothesized that the cytosolic localization of damaged TRF2 could be essential for activating the cGAS-STAT1 pathway. Subcellular fractions isolated from endothelial cells stimulated with TNFα had increased cytosolic expression of pSTAT1 and cGAS-STING compared to proliferating control cells, which was concordant with increased levels of the senescence markers p16 and IL-6 (Figure 5A). Furthermore, proximity ligation assay (PLA) for TRF2 and cGAS antibodies indicated an association between with these proteins, with positive PLA puncta colocalizing with cytoplasmic DAPI signals in TNF-α-induced senescent cells compared to normal control cells (Figure 5B). Both PLA and dual immunostaining experiments showed positive overlap between phosphorylated STAT1 and TRF2 in the cytoplasmic region overlapping with cytoplasmic DNA fragments in both cytokine-induced and WS senescence models (Figures 5C and S6A). Our results showed that both the JAK-STAT and cGAS pathways play roles in the persistence of cytoplasmic DNA fragments and amplifying the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). To further test the role of the cytoplasmic STAT1-cGAS-TRF2 axis in senescence, we evaluated the effects of pharmacological inhibitors that target these pathways. We found treatment with the JAK inhibitor ruxolitinib (JAKi) suppressed STAT1 phosphorylation and reduced cGAS-STING activation in TNFα-exposed cells compared to untreated proliferating control cells (Figures 5D and S6B). Immunostaining experiments revealed that inhibition of JAK signals diminished cytosolic cGAS and TRF2 enrichment, with decreased cytosolic DNA fragments and suppressed TNFα mediated senescence (Figures 5E and S6C). Of the TGFβ inhibitor, JAK inhibitor, and metformin, metformin was most effective in reducing cytoplasmic cGAS levels and suppressing STAT1 phosphorylation in Werner cells (Figure 5F). This was accompanied by a decrease in DNA damage markers, including γH2AX, and reduced expression of SASP-associated cytokines such as TNFα (Figure 5F). In contrast, metformin had only a partial effect in TNFα-exposed endothelial cells, indicating a more pronounced response to metformin in the progeroid aging models. Confocal images further indicated that treatment with metformin and JAKi decreased 53BP1, TRF2 positive foci, as well as cytoplasmic γH2AX-cGAS-DAPI foci in WS cells (Figures 5G and S6D). These findings highlight the interplay between the cytoplasmic TRF2 and cGAS-STAT1 pathway and that pharmacological approaches, which disrupt this link, suppress inflammation and DNA damage.

Metformin restores autophagy and enhances lysosomal clearance

The formation of CCF has been linked with autophagic degradation of nuclear lamin B1 in senescent cells.35 To investigate whether alterations in lysosomal function contribute to the accumulation of nuclear DNA associated -TRF2 in cytosol, we examined autophagy-lysosomal activity in senescent models. Previous studies have shown that lysosomal processing of damaged nuclear DNA translocated into the cytoplasm activates cGAS-STING-mediated inflammation in senescence.21,35 Cells exposed to cytokines had accumulation of p62 and LAMP1. Confocal images revealed increased p62/(SQSTM1), which was bound to TRF2 at cytoplasmic DNA (Figure 6A). Western blot analysis showed that inflammatory or replicative senescence led to a reduction in Lamin B1, which was inversely correlated with levels of p62 (SQSTM1) and p16 (Figure 6B). Furthermore, LMNA mutant HGPS cells had positive overlap of LC3B puncta with cytoplasmic DAPI and lamin A/C (Figure 6C). To assess whether restoring autophagic flux could alleviate lysosomal dysfunction, we treated WS senescent cells with bafilomycin, a V-ATPase inhibitor that impairs autophagy flux by blocking lysosomal acidification, either alone or in combination with metformin. Pretreatment with metformin decreased bafilomycin-induced accumulation of autophagy markers, including LAMP1, p62, and LC3B (Figure 6D). Supporting this, treatment with metformin or pharmacological inhibition of the JAK/TGFβ pathway reduced expression of LAMP1 and p62 (SQSTM1) and prevented senescence as evidenced by reduced expression of p16 (Figure 6E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that defective autophagy-lysosomal processing promotes the accumulation of damaged telomeric protein TRF2 in the cytoplasm of senescent cells, and that pharmacological restoration of autophagy limits the buildup of TRF2 associated DNA fragments in the cytoplasm.

Discussion

Over the past decade, numerous discoveries have highlighted the interrelationship between DNA damage and inflammation, demonstrating that these processes act in concert to accelerate progression of aging processes.12,13,36 However, the molecular mechanisms connecting these two complex processes remain to be understood. In this study, we discovered that senescent cells accumulate cytoplasmic nuclear DNA fragments were enriched with telomeric protein TRF2. This phenomenon was observed across multiple cell types and senescence-inducing stress conditions, including inflammatory stress, replicative exhaustion, DNA damage, and pathologically accelerated aging models, further supporting the central role of cytosolic TRF2 in senescence.

Aging in cells is associated with increased accumulation of damaged nuclear DNA in the cytosol, which contributes to chronic sterile inflammation and a prolonged DDR.37 Several research groups have independently confirmed that senescent cells accumulate cytoplasmic DNA or CCF that positively correlate with heterochromatin-associated proteins, dsDNA damage, cGAS activation, loss of nuclear membrane integrity (Lamin A/C), and autophagy function.22 For the following reasons, we hypothesize that CCF primarily comprised of broken pieces of telomeric DNA: (1) human telomeric regions are highly heterochromatic and positive for chromatin markers, including H3K27me3 (a marker of CCF), (2) the telomeric shelterin protein TRF2 is known to interact with nuclear lamin A/C, and it is possible that loss of nuclear architecture, coupled with DNA damage, leads to disruption, fragmentation, and cytosolic accumulation of telomeric proteins, and (3) TRF2 is essential for telomere protection; its depletion or degradation induces telomere uncapping, activation of the ATM-dependent DDR, and upregulation of p53/p21, leading to senescence.38,39,40 Characterizing the precise telomeric DNA content within CCF represent a key to clarify how telomeric dysfunction contributes to chronic inflammation and aging. While our data show a direct association between TRF2 and CCF, whether these fragments harbor persistently damaged telomeric DNA remains unclear. Nonetheless, we have previously found telomere-PNA-positive TRF2 foci and telomere-PNA-positive γH2AX foci in nuclei as well as perinuclear regions of the cytosol in both wild type and LMNA-mutant primary fibroblasts.30 This is consistent with the earlier observation that DNA damage foci are localized to telomeres in LMNA mutant fibroblasts in association with accelerated replicative senescence.41 During cellular aging shelterin components are broadly reduced, contributing to telomeric instability30,42; however, cytoplasmic enrichment appears to be more prominent for TRF2, further studies are needed to determine whether this localization is unique to TRF2 or extends to other shelterin components. Furthermore, several recent evidences indicate that telomeric DNA may be released into the cytoplasm due to transcriptional stress, DNA damage, or telomeric dysfunction from shortening or structural instability.14,20,43 The molecular interactions and functional interplay between associated with cytoplasmic telomeric fragments and cGAS warrants further investigation.

Telomeric dysfunction is recognized as a central cause of cellular senescence and age-related changes.4,42 Damage to telomeres is known to: (1) trigger persistent DNA damage and sustained inflammation, (2) cause deficiencies or resistance in DNA repair, (3) provoke proliferative arrest, (4) generate CCF and activate cGAS-STING pathway, and (5) engage autophagy.14,19,43,44 Consistent with this, our data revealed that cytoplasmic TRF2 colocalizes with γH2AX, lacks 53BP1, and is linked with reduced or defective RAD51 recruitment, indicating compromised DNA repair and permanent damage-induced arrest. In addition, the association between cytoplasmic TRF2 and cGAS reinforces the notion that TRF2 in the cytoplasm acts as a chronic trigger, promoting a prolonged DDR and senescence-associated inflammation. Telomere-specific damage, induced by treatment with 6-thio-2′-deoxyguanosine (6-thio-dG) in hTERT positive cells, further indicates that telomeric damage drives the prolonged activation of inflammatory responses in senescent cells.

Loss of lamin B1 is a hallmark of feature of physiological and pathological aging.45,46 Reduction or mutation in nuclear lamins frequently causes spontaneous nuclear rupture and is often accompanied by chromatin herniation and formation of nuclear blebs. There is increasing evidence showing that loss of nuclear integrity, DNA damage, inflammation, and sustained genomic instability are central causes of laminopathies, including accelerated aging-like syndromes such as HGPS.41,47,48,49 We found that prolonged exposure to TNFα or inflammatory stimuli reduced expression of lamin B1 and facilitated release of damaged TRF2 from the nucleus into the cytosol in senescent cells. This is consistent with our previous report showing that atypical Werner primary cells carrying an LMNA mutation accumulate DNA damage and promote degradation of the telomeric protein TRF2.30 Our data builds on previous findings and indicate that prolonged inflammatory stress and dysregulation of lamin facilitates extrusion of TRF2 bound nuclear DNA into the cytoplasm. Our findings reveal mechanistic interplay between cytoplasmic DNA associated TRF2, cGAS, and the STAT1 axis links the persistence of DNA damage and chronic SASP in senescent cells.

Our analysis focused on the JAK/STAT-cGAS axis based on our previous finding that persistent STAT activation drives SASP and interferon-responsive gene expression during senescence.11 Other downstream branches of cGAS/STING signaling, including TBK1-IRF3 and NF-κB, may also contribute to sustained inflammation and merit future investigation.

We found persistent activation of the JAK/STAT pathway in senescent cells induced by inflammatory stressors. Specifically, we found TNFα synergizes with interferon signals and initiates an autocrine loop to maintain a sustained SASP and interferon-stimulated gene expression. Interestingly, we found that phosphorylated STAT1 levels were elevated and sustained in all types of senescence examined in this study, suggesting persistent STAT1 activation is a feature of senescence and aging. STAT1 is a critical transcription factor involved in immune signaling, particularly in response to interferons. It is likely that the cooperative relationship between the sustained activation of STAT1 and cytoplasmic TRF2 contributes to a vicious cycle of prolonged inflammation and DNA damage, reinforcing the permanence of senescence. In this study, we found that cytoplasmic TRF2 binds to cGAS in distinct senescence models, including inflammatory, replicative, and Werner progeroid cells. Notably, compared to TNFα-induced senescence, replicative senescence exhibited reduced cGAS protein levels, whereas Werner senescent cells maintained a basal level of cGAS expression. In contrast, Werner cells exposed to exogenous inflammatory stimuli had an increase in cGAS levels and amplified induction of proinflammatory genes (Figure S5C). This raises an important question about how cGAS expression is regulated. Specifically, how does the expression of cGAS arising from intrinsic genomic instability or endogenous inflammation differ from that triggered by external stressors? Studies have suggested that activation of cGAS and downstream signaling of interferon response genes differ depending on the source of cytosolic DNA.22 It is possible that self-DNA arising from endogenous or exogenous stress might be recognized by different DNA sensors, which could trigger JAK/STAT associated inflammation; a hypothesis that requires further investigation. Emerging evidence indicates lack of cGAS-STING activation in accelerated aging and DNA damage, diverging from the previously described role of cGAS.50 Thus, the exact role and mechanism of cGAS association with cytoplasmic DNA remains an open question. cGAS-STING activation engages TBK1-IRF3 to induce type I interferons and can also activate NF-κB signaling to promote pro-inflammatory cytokine production, moreover other downstream pathways may converge to drive age-associated inflammation, warranting further investigation. We also identified a positive association of autophagic proteins p62, LAMP1, and LC3B with the telomeric TRF2-CCF axis, highlighting that autophagy-lysosomal dysfunction allows nuclear-derived chromatin, including telomeric DNA, to accumulate as CCF (CCFs), supporting previous observations that defective autophagy sustains cytoplasmic DNA persistence.21,35 Future investigations are needed to determine how impaired autophagy contributes to the accumulation of telomeric fragments in the cytosol.

In conclusion, our study uncovers that telomeric protein TRF2 enriched CCF accumulate in senescent cells. We find that prolonged inflammatory stress, nuclear lamin dysfunction, progeroid mutation, or normally aging cells compromise nuclear lamina integrity, leading to the release of TRF2-associated nuclear DNA into the cytoplasm. Our data provide novel insights into the mechanisms underlying persistent DNA damage and reveal the key role of cytoplasmic TRF2 and cGAS-STAT1 signaling in the regulation of senescence and prolonged SASP. These findings highlight that pharmacological interventions targeting this pathway may offer a promising therapeutic strategy to reduce inflammation, prevent the progression of age-related pathologies, and improve healthspan.

Limitations of the study

Our findings identify the accumulation of cytoplasmic-TRF2 as a feature of senescent cells. Although our study demonstrates that cytoplasmic chromatin-enriched TRF2 promotes cGAS-STAT1-mediated inflammatory signaling during senescence, the precise origin and identity of the cytoplasmic telomeric DNA remains to be established. Furthermore, while persistent DNA damage and inflammatory stress are associated with the accumulation of telomeric fragments in the cytoplasm, the molecular mechanisms underlying their release and trafficking remain undefined. Addressing these questions will provide important insight into how telomere dysfunction engages innate immune signaling during aging.

Experimental model and study participant details

Cell lines and cultures

HUVECs were cultured in EGM-2 medium supplemented with growth factors. Control fibroblast cells include 82–6 normal primary cells derived from a newborn foreskin and 88–1 cells derived from the abdominal skin of a 62-year-old female.30 82-6 hTERT is an immortalized human diploid fibroblast line established as described previously.51 AG03141 and AG05229 are primary skin fibroblast lines derived from WS patients and obtained from Coriell Cell Research (https://catalog.coriell.org). MCI7885 primary skin fibroblasts are derived from a Werner syndrome patient and TW1010 from an LMNA mutant patient as previously described.49 HGADFN122, HGADFN127, and HGADFN143 are primary skin fibroblast lines derived from young patients with Hutchinson-Gilford Progeria Syndrome carrying the heterozygous LMNA mutation c.1824C>T (p.Gly608Gly) in Exon 11. These cells were purchased from the Progeria Research Foundation and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) with supplements. In this study, fibroblasts derived from Werner syndrome and Hutchinson-Gilford Progeria Syndrome patients are referred as follows: AG03141 (WS1), MCI7885 (WS2), AG05229 (WS3), HGADFN122 (HGPS1), HGADFN127 (HGPS2), and HGADFN143 (HGPS3). All primary fibroblast cell lines were grown in DMEM supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS), except for the HGPS cells, which were supplemented with 15% FBS, 1% GlutaMAX and 1x penicillin/streptomycin (Pen Strep). Cells were sub-cultured and maintained as 1 × 106 cells in T75 tissue cultures flasks under standard culture conditions at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 and 5% O2. All information of cell line source used are provided in supplemental information. The identity of all cell lines used in this study was consistent with the supplier’s characterization, and cultures were routinely monitored for morphology and growth characteristics. Mycoplasma contamination was periodically assessed and all cultures used in this study tested negative.

Method details

Senescence induction, cytokine stimulation, and inhibitor experiments

For TNFα-induced senescence, Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial Cells (HUVECs) were cultured in the presence or absence of 20 ng/mL TNF-α for either 3 or 5 days, as described previously.11 To achieve replicative senescence and WS senescence, cells were serially passaged until they reached replication exhaustion. Subcultures were performed at 4-to-5-day intervals or after achieving 80% confluence. For 6-thio-dG drug induced senescence, briefly, 82-6 hTERT cells were treated with 6-thio-dG (5–20 μM) for 10 days and cultured with media alone for 5 days without the drug. For cytokine stimulation, cells were treated with IL-6 (20 ng/mL), TNF-α (20 ng/mL), and IFN-γ (20 ng/mL) alone, in combination (TNF-α and IFN-γ at 10 ng/mL of each), or with all three cytokines (“cocktail” of IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ at 10 ng/mL of each) for 3 or 5 days, unless indicated otherwise. For experiments with inhibitors, cells were pre-treated with vehicle, Ruxolitinib (JAK inhibitor, 1-3 μM), Metformin HCl (5-20 mM), or SB-431542 (TGFβ inhibitor, 2 μm) prior to cytokine stimulation. All information on source of recombinant proteins and inhibitors used is provided in supplemental information.

Nuclear and cytoplasmic protein extraction

Cells grown in T150 flasks (1–3x106 cells) were washed three times with PBS. 200-600 μL of cytoplasmic extraction buffer (0.5% NP40, 10 mM Tris [pH 7.6], 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2 in RNAse-free water) were added to the flask to lyse the cells, which were then collected in 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes. The cell suspension was vortexed gently for 10 seconds and incubated on ice for 10 min. After incubation, cells were centrifuged at 1,200rpm for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant containing the cytoplasmic fraction was transferred to a new 1.5 mL centrifuge tube. The pellet was resuspended in 50-150 μL of nuclear extraction buffer (20 mM Tris pH 8.0, 1% SDS, fresh protease inhibitor), vortexed, and boiled at 95°C for 15 min to extract the nuclear protein.

Western Blot analysis

For protein expression analysis, total protein was extracted from cells using 2x Laemmli Sample Buffer, followed by dissociation using a syringe and boiling the sample for 5 min. Equal amounts of protein were loaded onto 4–12% gradient Bis-Tris gel and separated by SDS-PAGE. Proteins were then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. After blocking with 5% non-fat dry milk, the membranes were immunoblotted with primary antibody overnight at 4°C, followed with secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase. The membrane was then incubated with Signal Fire Elite ECL reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Signals were detected using the iBright CL1500 Imaging System. All information regarding the antibodies used is provided in the supplemental information.

RNA extraction and reverse transcription qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from sub-confluent cultures using 1 mL TRIzol Reagent and stored at −80°C until further processing. For RNA isolation, 200 μL chloroform were added to each sample, which were then mixed vigorously and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. Samples were centrifuged at 14,000 g for 15 min at 4°C. The upper aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube, and RNA was precipitated with isopropanol and washed with ethanol. The RNA pellet was air-dried for 15 min and resuspended in 20 μL of nuclease-free water. RNA concentration and purity were measured using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer. For reverse transcription, 2 μg of total RNA were converted into complementary DNA (cDNA) using the High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (supplemental information) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. qPCR was carried out using TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix with TaqMan probes. Reactions were performed in a 96-well format using the Applied Biosystem 7500 Real-Time PCR System. Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) or actin was used as the housekeeping control across all experiments. Data were analyzed using the delta delta CT method. All information regarding the primers used is provided in the supplemental information.

Senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) staining

To identify senescent cells, SA-β-gal activity was measured using a Senescence β-galactosidase staining kit (supplemental information). Briefly, cells were cultured in 6-well plates and treated as specified. After treatment, cells were washed twice with PBS and fixed with 1 mL of 1× fixative solution per well for 10 min at room temperature. After removing fixation solution, cells were washed twice with PBS and 1 mL of freshly prepared SA-β-gal staining solution were added per well. The 6-well plates were covered with aluminum foil and incubated overnight at 37°C without CO2. Following incubation, SA-β-gal staining solution was removed and cells were overlayed with 70% glycerol for long-term storage at 4°C. The percent of SA-β-gal positive cells was determined by counting 200 cells per sample in triplicate.

Immunofluorescence

For immunofluorescence, cells were cultured on coverslips, washed with cold PBS, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Free aldehyde groups were quenched with 10 mM glycine for 10 min. The cells were permeabilized with 0.2% BSA and 0.05% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, washed, and blocked in 10% FBS in PBS for 30 min. Cells were labeled with primary antibody for 1 h, followed by three washes with 0.2% BSA, 0.05% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min each. Cells were then incubated with secondary antibody conjugated with Alexa Fluor 488 or Alexa Fluor 594 for 45 min. After three washes with 0.2% BSA, 0.05% Triton X-100 in PBS, followed by a brief PBS wash, the coverslips were mounted on glass slides using Mounting buffer with DAPI. Images were captured using a Leica SP8X confocal microscope using the 40x or 63x objective. All information regarding the antibodies used is provided in the supplemental information.

Proximity ligation assay

Co-localization of proteins was visualized using a Proximity Ligation Assay (PLA). Cells were cultured on coverslips, washed with cold PBS, and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 10 min at room temperature. Free aldehyde groups were quenched with 10 mM glycine for 10 min. The cells were permeabilized with 0.2%, 0.05% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min, washed, and blocked in 10% FBS in PBS for 30 min. The following experimental procedures were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the Duolink in Situ Red Starter Kit Briefly, samples were incubated with the primary antibody for 1 h, followed by incubation with diluted PLA PLUS anti-rabbit and PLA MINUS anti-mouse probes for 1 h at 37°C to bind to the primary antibodies. Ligation was performed by incubating samples with ligase for 30 min at 37°C, followed by amplification with polymerase for 100 min at 37°C. After drying, coverslips were mounted with Duolink in Situ Mounting Medium with DAPI. PLA signals were visualized as red fluorescent dots, and images were captured using a Leica SP8X confocal microscope with a 63x objective.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data are presented either as representative examples or means +SEM of ≥3 experiments. p values were obtained using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test or two-way ANOVA. ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.00001. Data were plotted and analyzed using GraphPad Prism version 9.
