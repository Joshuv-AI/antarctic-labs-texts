Symptomatic posterior fossa arachnoid cyst treated with endoscopic cystoventriculostomy in an adult
Authors: Tadahiro Tsubota, Satoshi Kawajiri, Hidetaka Arishima, Kenichiro Kikuta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe present a case of symptomatic posterior fossa arachnoid cyst (PFAC) that was successfully treated with endoscopic surgery. A woman in her 60s presented with progressive lightheadedness. Brain MRI showed a non-enhancing cystic lesion in the cerebellum that exerted pressure on the fourth ventricle, causing hydrocephalus, which further exacerbated 6 months later. Considering that PFAC was the cause of the patient’s symptoms, an endoscopic cystoventriculostomy was performed. Postoperatively, the patient’s symptoms improved, and MRI showed reduction of the cyst and improvement of hydrocephalus. Although symptomatic arachnoid cysts are uncommon in adults, endoscopic cystoventriculostomy is a minimally invasive and effective approach, with applicability across a broad spectrum of cases.Keywords: Brain stem / cerebellum, Hydrocephalus

Introduction

An arachnoid cyst (AC) is a cystic lesion encased by an arachnoid membrane that contains fluid identical to the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). ACs in adults are predominantly distributed in the middle cranial fossa (34%), posterior cranial fossa (33%) and convexity (14%).1 Posterior fossa ACs (PFACs) are often asymptomatic, but patients can present with headache, dizziness, ataxia, tinnitus, hearing loss and seizures.2 It is extremely rare for ACs to enlarge and become symptomatic in adulthood, particularly in the elderly, and only a few such cases have been reported.

Surgical treatment is generally recommended for symptomatic PFAC, but owing to its rarity, consensus on the optimal surgical procedures is lacking. Treatment modalities involve craniotomy for capsule removal, craniotomy or endoscopic fenestration to facilitate cyst drainage into the CSF and cystoperitoneal shunting.3,8 Recently, PFAC cases with good outcomes after minimally invasive endoscopic surgery have been described, although such reports are rare.56 9,15

We present a case of symptomatic enlarged PFAC in an adult who had a favourable outcome following endoscopic cystoventriculostomy and review previous similar reports.

Case report

A woman in her 60s presented with a gradually progressing floating sensation and lightheadedness that had persisted for 18 months. MRI revealed a PFAC in the cerebellum and slight enlargement of the ventricles (figure 1a–c). The patient had no history of head trauma, intracranial haemorrhage or infection. At admission, the symptoms were mild, so the patient declined surgical treatment, and therefore was followed-up. Over the subsequent 6 months, the patient’s instability during walking progressed. Neurologically, the patient presented with tandem gait difficulty and demonstrated positive finger-to-nose test results, predominantly on the left side. Brain MRI revealed a 36×33×33 mm cystic lesion devoid of substantial components located on the left side of the median cerebellum. No contrast effect was observed on the cyst wall, and the content generated a signal equivalent to that of the CSF. The cyst compressed the fourth ventricle anteriorly to the right side and enlarged the lateral and third ventricles. Periventricular T2 hyperintensities were also observed (figure 1d–f). The patient’s symptoms were attributed to cerebellar compression and hydrocephalus caused by PFAC. Because of the worsening trend in both symptoms and MRI findings, surgical treatment was advised. Based on the preoperative MRI findings, the cyst and fourth ventricle were in close proximity. An endoscopic approach from the left posterior side, allowing cystoventriculostomy with a single burr hole via a straight route without damaging the vessels or cranial nerves, was chosen.

Differential diagnosis

In this region, the differential diagnosis includes epidermoid cysts, Dandy-Walker syndrome, mega cisterna magna, cystic tumours such as haemangioblastoma and pilocytic astrocytoma, as well as neuroepithelial cysts. Epidermoid cysts typically demonstrate diffusion restriction on diffusion-weighted imaging. Dandy-Walker syndrome is characterised by cerebellar vermian dysgenesis, and mega cisterna magna commonly communicates with the fourth ventricle. Cystic tumours usually exhibit a solid and/or contrast-enhancing component. Both neuroepithelial cysts and ACs are characterised by their neuroectodermal origin. The shared developmental origin potentially contributes to the frequent histopathological confusion between these entities and may similarly correlate with their pathophysiological and radiological resemblances. In the present case, definitive histopathological findings could not be obtained, precluding comprehensive pathological diagnostic evaluation. Based on the intraoperative findings of translucent CSF, cyst wall characteristics and epidemiological considerations regarding prevalence of this type of lesion, an AC was deemed the most probable diagnosis.

Treatment

For the surgical treatment, the patient was placed in the prone position under general anaesthesia. A linear left paramedian suboccipital skin incision was made, and the position of the burr hole was determined using a navigation system (2.5 cm lateral and 2.5 cm caudal to the inion). After dural opening, a sheath (NeuroSheath; Medikit, Tokyo, Japan) was transcortically inserted, puncturing the cyst, and the endoscope was introduced through the sheath. The cyst wall was translucent and thin. The bilateral cerebellum and cerebellar tonsils, fourth ventricle and colloid plexus, vermian branches of posterior inferior cerebellar arteries were identified through the arachnoid membrane. Using an endoscopic monopolar device (V.E.S.A. Flex; TOKIBO, Tokyo, Japan) and scissors-type forceps, the AC membrane at the bottom of the fourth ventricle was opened, and the window was enlarged sufficiently to prevent occlusion. The fenestration measured approximately 5×5 mm, and CSF flow was confirmed by the pulsation of the cyst wall. By creating openings at two distinct sites—the fourth ventricle side and the sheath insertion point—CSF flow was facilitated, which reduced the risk of relapse (figure 2, online supplemental video 1).

Outcome and follow-up

Following surgery, the patient’s unsteadiness was alleviated without complications, and brain MRI performed 6 months postoperatively revealed reduced cyst size and amelioration of hydrocephalus (figure 3a–c).

Discussion

Adult ACs rarely grow in size or become symptomatic.16,18 In a long-term follow-up study comprising 203 adult patients with ACs, cyst enlargement was observed in 2.5% of the patients, whereas 1% experienced new symptoms or deterioration of existing symptoms.1 Potential mechanisms contributing to the development of ACs include: (1) the presence of an osmotic gradient between the cyst lumen and subarachnoid space, (2) a one-way valve mechanism within the foramen traversing the cyst and subarachnoid space and (3) secretion of contents by the cyst wall.19 Even without a history of overt head trauma, minor injuries can promote cyst enlargement and increase the risk of adverse symptoms.16

Surgical treatments for symptomatic PFAC include membranectomy, cystventriculostomy, cystocisternostomy and cystoperitoneal shunting. Srinivasan et al classified PFAC into four types according to localisation and reported appropriate surgical approaches.8 Based on their report, our case corresponds to the lateral retro/intra-cerebellar type, and surgical excision of the cyst may be the preferred treatment. Previously, open surgery was probably the most common method, but with the advent of neuroendoscopes, favourable outcomes in PFAC cases treated with endoscopic fenestration have been reported, with success rates comparable to those of craniotomy and shunting.3,57 8 However, not many of these reports described adult cases. The keywords used for searches in PubMed and Google Scholar databases were ‘arachnoid cysts, endoscope, endoscopic’. Patients were 36 years old on average and typically presented with headache and gait disturbances. Cystoventriculostomy and cystocisternostomy were performed for cystic lesions located in various regions, including the posterior cerebellar region, internal cerebellum and cerebellopontine angle. Median or paramedian suboccipital burr holes were used for cysts located in the posterior cerebellar region, whereas the retromastoid burr hole approach was used for cysts in the cerebellopontine angle. None of the patients had complications, and all but one demonstrated either cyst reduction or symptom improvement. No instances of postoperative cyst regrowth were noted, and additional surgical interventions, such as shunting, were not necessary.

Cinalli et al20 reported that windows should be opened by at least 10–15 mm to prevent occlusions. In our case, the maximum fenestration diameter was approximately 5 mm. Given that the recommended window size for endoscopic third ventriculostomy is generally 5 mm or greater, we concluded that an adequate fenestration had been achieved. Based on the above, it is expected that the endoscopic approach might be sufficient even in cases of midline extra fourth ventricular type and lateral retro/intra cerebellar type, for which Srinivasan et al recommended surgical excision of the cyst.

Endoscopic surgery is minimally invasive but may not be appropriate when the space within the cerebral cistern and ventricles surrounding the cyst is insufficient, or when the approach is constrained by its proximity to neurovascular structures. In cystoventriculostomy, the initial step involves determining the location for fenestration, followed by planning a route that ensures anatomical safety and minimises the disruption of normal anatomical structures. In PFAC, it is important to ensure sufficient endoscopic range of motion for intraoperative manipulation, taking into consideration the depth of the operative field due to the inclination of the skull below the superior nuchal line, which is unique to the posterior cranial fossa, and the thickness of the posterior neck musculature and subcutaneous tissue.

There is no consensus on the optimal approach for patients with concomitant hydrocephalus. Lockard et al reported that 82% of patients with PFAC and hydrocephalus experienced improvement in hydrocephalus following PFAC treatment alone.21 Considering the invasiveness and potential complications of surgery, particularly in elderly patients, it is more appropriate to begin with PFAC treatment alone and closely monitor the symptoms and progression of hydrocephalus, rather than initiating treatment with shunting or endoscopic third ventriculostomy.

In the present case, the AC was located in the posterior cerebellar region, and because critical structures were absent in the posterior paramedian approach, endoscopic surgery through the paramedian suboccipital burr hole was selected. The cyst was close to the outlet of the fourth ventricle; therefore, it was selected as the site of fenestration. The midline route was avoided to prevent damage to the occipital sinus, vermian branch of the posterior inferior cerebellar artery and cerebellar vermis. Owing to the pressure of the cyst on the fourth ventricle from the left side, a linear route for fenestration was delineated from the left side, orthogonal to the cyst membrane. Moreover, the patient presented with left cerebellar ataxia, so the transcortical pathway through the left cerebellum was considered a less invasive option than an approach through the right cerebellum.

Shunt-related complications are possible, and shunting was considered if the symptoms and hydrocephalus failed to improve after endoscopic treatment. We achieved a good outcome with endoscopic cystoventriculostomy alone; however, the long-term outcome at 2 years postoperatively is unknown.

Conclusion

We reported a rare case of symptomatic PFAC in an elderly patient that was successfully treated with endoscopic cystoventriculostomy alone. The incidence of symptomatic ACs in elderly patients, such as in the present case, is expected to increase owing to global ageing. Endoscopic fenestration with a single burr hole may be recommended as a minimally invasive and effective treatment option, offering a suitable and accessible approach for elderly patients.
