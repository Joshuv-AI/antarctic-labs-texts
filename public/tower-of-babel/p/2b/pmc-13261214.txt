The polypill after acute coronary syndrome: A feasible pill to swallow?
Authors: Safi U Khan, Kershaw V Patel
Published: 2026

Acute coronary syndromes (ACS) remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with the highest risk of recurrent ischemic events concentrated in the months following the index event [1]. Early initiation and sustained adherence to guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT), including dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) and high-intensity statins, are essential to reduce mortality and adverse cardiovascular events. However, real-world persistence with GDMT declines rapidly after hospital discharge, particularly during the transition to outpatient care, due to regimen complexity, cost barriers, and gaps in care coordination [2,3].

Approximately 1 in 7 patients discontinue P2Y12 inhibitors within 30 days of drug-eluting stent (DES) implantation, exposing them to a two-fold higher risk of death or recurrent myocardial infarctions (MIs), with nearly 60 % of events occurring within the first 90 days [2,3]. Adherence to statin therapy is similarly suboptimal, with only 64 % of post-MI patients maintaining high adherence at one year [4]. These adherence gaps disproportionately affect racial and ethnic minorities, socioeconomically disadvantaged populations, and individuals with limited health literacy or fragmented healthcare access [5]. Strategies that streamline treatment regimens and improve therapeutic continuity during this vulnerable period, therefore, represent a major unmet need in contemporary cardiovascular care.

Polypill approach, which combine multiple evidence-based therapies into a single daily formulation, have emerged as a promising strategy to enhance adherence and improve outcomes in secondary prevention. The SECURE trial demonstrated a 3.2 % absolute risk reduction in major adverse cardiovascular events, corresponding to one event prevented for every 31 patients treated with polypill (aspirin, ramipril, and atorvastatin) over a median follow up of three years among older patients with recent MI compared with usual care [6]. However, prior polypill formulations have notably excluded a P2Y12 inhibitor, representing a critical limitation, as uninterrupted P2Y12 inhibition is central for mitigation thrombotic risk during the early post-ACS period. Accordingly, whether a comprehensive polypill strategy incorporating DAPT and high-intensity statin can be safely implemented and improve adherence in the early post-ACS phase remains an unanswered question.

In this issue of the American Journal of Preventive Cardiology, Coellar and Keshvani et al., present the POLY-ACS trial, a two-center, open-label, randomized pilot study enrolling 140 adults within 30 days of ACS treated with DES implantation [7]. Participants were randomized to receive either a once-daily over-encapsulated polypill containing aspirin 81 mg, rosuvastatin 40 mg, and either clopidogrel 75 mg or prasugrel 10 mg, or usual care. The median age was 58 years, 29 % were women, 63 % identified as Hispanic, 14 % Black and with many participants uninsured or receiving care in safety-net settings. Follow-up data were missing in 12 participants (8 in the usual care group and 4 in the polypill group).

The investigators assessed low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and platelet reactivity using impedance aggregometry, along with medication adherence measured by the Morisky Medication Adherence Scale. At 30 days, there were no statistically significant between-group differences in LDL-C (least-squares mean difference, −4.58 mg/dL; 95 % confidence interval [CI], −12.68 to 3.52), platelet reactivity (−0.08 Ω; 95 % CI, −0.96 to 1.11), or adherence scores. Clinical event rates were low and comparable between groups, with no stent thrombosis or excess adverse events observed.

The limitations of the trial are appropriately acknowledged. The modest sample size, missing data, and brief follow-up period limit statistical power to detect differences in adherence trajectories, biomarker changes, or clinical outcomes. Medication discontinuation typically evolves over months rather than weeks, such as when a refill is needed, and short-term follow-up may underestimate the benefits of regimen simplification. Randomization occurred approximately two weeks after discharge, missing the early post-discharge interval when medication errors and access barriers are most pronounced. Baseline therapeutic optimization was substantial, with median LDL-C levels of 57 mg/dL and near-maximal platelet inhibition, limiting the opportunity to detect incremental improvements.

Several features enhance the clinical relevance and translational significance of this study. The enrollment of a racially and ethnically diverse, socioeconomically vulnerable population, groups historically underrepresented in cardiovascular trials and disproportionately affected by medication discontinuation, addresses a critical gap in secondary prevention [5]. By demonstrating feasibility in this setting, POLY-ACS targets a population in whom adherence-focused interventions may yield greatest clinical benefit. The inclusion of a P2Y12 inhibitor represents an important conceptual advance, aligning the polypill with the early ACS period, to counter thrombotic risk. The use of objective biologic endpoints, including LDL-C and platelet function testing, strengthens the assessment of pharmacodynamic effectiveness beyond self-reported adherence. The pragmatic over-encapsulation strategy enhances feasibility by preserving therapeutic flexibility while avoiding many regulatory and manufacturing barriers associated with fixed-dose combination products.

Participants assigned to the polypill demonstrated significant reductions in LDL-C and maintained adequate platelet inhibition, whereas those receiving usual care showed no meaningful LDL-C improvement, and a modest increase in platelet reactivity. Notably, LDL-C reductions were numerically greater among individuals with poor baseline adherence, suggesting that regimen simplification may provide the greatest benefit in those at highest risk for treatment discontinuation. Although exploratory, these findings provide biologic support for the premise that simplified regimens preserve pharmacologic effectiveness by improving medication continuity.

Future studies should incorporate earlier initiation, ideally during the index hospitalization, and extend follow-up to 6–12 months to better capture adherence patterns and clinical outcomes. Scalable implementation will require flexible polypill configurations that accommodate individualized antiplatelet selection and integration with guideline-directed care. Pragmatic multicenter trials embedded within health systems will be essential to evaluate effectiveness and cost-effectiveness. More broadly, simplified therapeutic strategies such as polypills offer a promising avenue to improve health equity by reducing structural barriers to medication access and adherence.

In summary, the POLY-ACS pilot trial represents an important advance in the evolution of simplified therapeutic strategies for secondary prevention after ACS. By demonstrating the feasibility and safety of a once-daily regimen incorporating aspirin, a P2Y12 inhibitor, and a high-intensity statin in a diverse, underserved population, the investigators provide critical proof of concept. Larger, adequately powered trials with earlier initiation and longer follow-up are now needed to determine whether this approach can translate into improved adherence, better clinical outcomes, and reduced disparities in cardiovascular care.
