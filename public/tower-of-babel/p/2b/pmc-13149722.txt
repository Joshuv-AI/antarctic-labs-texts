Dietary polyphenols and hepatocellular carcinoma: protective mechanisms, challenges, and future directions
Authors: Tonghua Yang, Wanyu Xu, Beihai Yuan, Fangxing Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, with pathogenesis driven by metabolic reprogramming, oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and dysregulation of multiple signaling pathways. Dietary polyphenols-a structurally diverse group of phytochemicals abundant in fruits, vegetables, tea, and medicinal plants-have emerged as promising chemopreventive and therapeutic agents due to their pleiotropic bioactivities. This comprehensive review systematically searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar for relevant literature published from 2014 to 2025. It outlines the molecular mechanisms through which dietary polyphenols (including phenolic acids, flavonoids, lignans, stilbenes, and others) suppress HCC development by modulating lipid metabolism, glucose homeostasis, mitochondrial function, oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and detoxification pathways. Special emphasis is placed on their ability to reshape the tumor microenvironment (TME) and their synergistic potential when combined with conventional treatments. Nevertheless, the clinical translation of polyphenols is hampered by limitations such as poor bioavailability, chemical instability, and insufficient clinical validation. Future efforts should prioritize the development of advanced delivery systems (e.g., nanoformulations), investigation of polyphenol-drug synergies, and validation through rigorously designed clinical trials. This review underscores the promise of dietary polyphenols as complementary agents for HCC prevention and treatment, while highlighting the imperative for further mechanistic and translational research.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) poses a major global health burden, accounting for approximately 75–85% of all primary liver cancers and ranking as the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1–3]. It is responsible for an estimated 906,000 new cases and 830,000 deaths annually, with rising incidence largely attributable to chronic infections (hepatitis B virus [HBV] and hepatitis C virus [HCV]), metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) [4], alcohol consumption, dietary aflatoxin exposure, and underlying cirrhosis [1, 5, 6]. In particular, metabolic dysfunction-related fatty liver disease-especially non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)-has emerged as a predominant risk factor in developed countries, frequently progressing from simple steatosis (non-alcoholic fatty liver, NAFL) to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), fibrosis, cirrhosis, and eventually HCC [7, 8]. Pathogenetically, HCC is driven by metabolic reprogramming (e.g., enhanced aerobic glycolysis and de novo lipogenesis), oxidative stress (e.g., reactive oxygen species [ROS] accumulation), chronic inflammation (involving nuclear factor-kappa B [NF-κB], tumor necrosis factor-alpha [TNF-α], and interleukin-6 [IL-6]), and dysregulated signaling pathways such as phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT), mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), and Wnt/β-catenin. These alterations collectively promote proliferation, apoptosis evasion, invasion, and metastasis [9, 10].

The progression of HCC and its resistance to therapy are strongly influenced by the dynamic tumor microenvironment (TME). The HCC TME comprises diverse cellular components-such as cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), regulatory T cells (Tregs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and immune cells-as well as acellular factors like extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, cytokines, and growth factors. These elements foster immune evasion, stromal activation, and angiogenesis, thereby promoting invasion and treatment resistance [11]. Specifically, M2-polarized TAMs and immunosuppressive cytokines (e.g., IL-10, TGF-β) create an immunotolerant niche, while CAFs remodel the ECM and secrete pro-tumorigenic factors.

Current HCC treatments include surgical resection, liver transplantation, transarterial chemoembolization (TACE), targeted agents (e.g., sorafenib, lenvatinib), and immune checkpoint inhibitors. However, the majority of patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, which limits the efficacy of curative interventions. Furthermore, targeted therapies frequently encounter intrinsic or acquired resistance in over 50% of patients and are associated with significant toxicity, alongside a post-resection recurrence rate exceeding 70% within five years [12–15].

In this context, dietary polyphenols-plant-derived compounds characterized by phenolic hydroxyl groups-have garnered increasing interest for their potential in HCC prevention and adjunct therapy. These are classified into flavonoids (e.g., catechins, quercetin, dihydromyricetin), phenolic acids (e.g., chlorogenic acid, caffeic acid), stilbenes (e.g., resveratrol), and curcuminoids (e.g., curcumin) [16–18]. Unlike synthetic drugs, polyphenols exert pleiotropic effects-including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anti-tumor activities-with minimal toxicity [19, 20]. Epidemiological studies suggest that regular intake of polyphenol-rich foods (e.g., green tea, cocoa, berries, red wine, olives) correlates with reduced HCC risk, largely due to their ability to suppress malignant transformation in the liver [21–23].

Growing preclinical evidence indicates that dietary polyphenols inhibit HCC development by targeting key pathogenic pathways, modulating the TME, regulating epigenetic mechanisms, and enhancing chemosensitivity [20, 24–28]. Their multi-target action and favorable safety profile make them promising candidates for integration into HCC treatment strategies [29, 30].

This review provides a comprehensive analysis of the molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-HCC effects of dietary polyphenols, emphasizing their multi-target nature, current challenges in clinical translation, and future research directions. By synthesizing preclinical and clinical evidence, we aim to illustrate how these natural compounds disrupt oncogenic signaling and remodel the TME, offering innovative approaches for HCC management and therapy.

Literature search strategy

This review employed a systematic literature search to evaluate the anti-HCC properties of dietary polyphenols. Relevant studies were retrieved from PubMed/MEDLINE, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar using keywords and MeSH terms such as “dietary polyphenols,” “hepatocellular carcinoma,” “HCC,” specific compounds (e.g., curcumin, resveratrol, EGCG), and key mechanisms (e.g., apoptosis, metabolic reprogramming). Boolean operators (AND, OR) were applied to optimize the search strategy. The inclusion criteria encompassed original research on polyphenol effects in HCC models, mechanistic studies, and clinical trials, while the exclusion criteria eliminated non-English publications, non-HCC studies, reviews without original data, and combination therapies where polyphenol effects could not be isolated. Data extraction focused on experimental models, polyphenol characteristics, biological outcomes (e.g., proliferation, apoptosis, signaling pathways), and key findings. The screening process followed PRISMA guidelines (Fig. 1).

The molecular pathogenesis of liver cancer

Hepatocarcinogenesis is initiated by genetic alterations triggered by various etiological factors. Hepatitis B virus (HBV) and hepatitis C virus (HCV) promote viral genome integration and disrupt mitotic regulatory proteins, resulting in persistent inflammation and genomic instability [31]. Aflatoxin B1 induces DNA adduct formation, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses, with a characteristic mutation at codon 249 of the tumor protein p53 (TP53) gene [32]. Acetaldehyde, a metabolite of alcohol, facilitates DNA adduct and reactive oxygen species (ROS) formation while impairing immune surveillance [33]. Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) contributes to hepatocarcinogenesis through oxidative stress, lipotoxicity, insulin resistance, and the establishment of a pro-tumorigenic microenvironment, ultimately resulting in genetic damage [34].

The molecular pathogenesis follows a sequential pattern: aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is often an early event; followed by Telomerase Reverse Transcriptase (TERT) promoter mutations (60%), which confer limitless replicative potential through telomere elongation [35]; TP53 mutations (35–50%) lead to chromosomal instability [19]; and subsequent mutations in catenin beta 1 (CTNNB1) (20–40%), axis inhibition protein 1 (AXIN1) (9–13%), and AT-rich interaction domain 1 A (ARID1A) (12%) further drive malignant transformation [36–38]. These molecular alterations exhibit certain etiology-specific patterns, such as the association between HBV infection and TP53 mutations, while CTNNB1 mutations are frequently observed in HCV- and alcohol-related HCC [39].

Accumulation of these genetic alterations contributes to considerable intertumoral and intratumoral heterogeneity. Based on molecular profiles, HCC is broadly classified into two subtypes: proliferative and non-proliferative. The proliferative subclass, often associated with HBV infection, is characterized by high chromosomal instability, elevated alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) levels, frequent TP53 mutations, and poor prognosis. In contrast, the non-proliferative subclass, linked to HCV infection and alcohol consumption, exhibits CTNNB1 and TERT promoter mutations, relative chromosomal stability, lower AFP levels, moderate histological differentiation, and less aggressive clinical behavior [40, 41]. This heterogeneity poses major challenges for personalized treatment, prognostic assessment, and contributes to therapy resistance and recurrence [42, 43].

During tumor progression, multiple signaling pathways become dysregulated. These include the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase/protein kinase B/mammalian target of rapamycin (PI3K/AKT/mTOR) pathway, which promotes cell proliferation and survival [44]; the vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) pathway, a cornerstone of angiogenesis [45]; the Ras/mitogen-activated protein kinase (Ras/MAPK) pathway, which is hyperactivated in nearly all advanced HCCs and approximately half of early-stage cases [46]; the insulin-like growth factor (IGF) receptor signaling pathway, active in about 20% of HCCs [47]; the insulin-like growth factor (IGF) receptor signaling pathway, active in about 20% of HCCs [48]. Notably, these pathways are not isolated but form an interconnected network; for instance, PI3K/AKT can cross-talk with Wnt/β-catenin and Ras/MAPK signaling, creating a robust signaling web that promotes HCC progression and resistance. HCC remains a major cause of cancer-related death worldwide. Although therapeutic modalities-including chemotherapy, targeted agents, and immunotherapy-have advanced considerably, drug resistance continues to pose a central barrier to durable treatment response [49–51]. As schematically summarized in Fig. 3, this resistance arises from a complex interplay of biological mechanisms encompassing five key hallmarks: (1) Tumor heterogeneity-genomic alterations in HCC generate substantial genetic diversity, enabling the emergence of subpopulations capable of evading therapy; (2) Tumor growth kinetics-slow-proliferating or quiescent tumor cells often persist after treatment, as many therapies preferentially target rapidly dividing cells; (3) “Undruggable” genomic drivers-key mutations in genes such as MYC and TP53 currently lack effective targeted therapies, narrowing therapeutic options; (4) Selective therapeutic pressure-treatments such as radiotherapy exert selective forces that enrich for resistant HCC subclones; and (5) Influence of the immune system and tumor microenvironment (TME)-the TME promotes immunosuppression, impairing T-cell-mediated tumor clearance, while fostering a pro-tumorigenic niche through recruitment of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), M2-polarized macrophages (M2-Mφ), and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs).

In light of these challenges, polyphenols-naturally occurring phytochemicals with pleiotropic bioactivities-have attracted increasing interest for their potential to inhibit HCC progression and overcome drug resistance by simultaneously targeting multiple nodes within these dysregulated pathways and resistance mechanisms.

Polyphenols in the diet: sources, structures, and biological activities

Dietary polyphenols, recognized as the “seventh class of nutrients,” are a large family of plant-derived compounds characterized by phenolic structures. They are ubiquitous in plant-based foods such as fruits, vegetables, nuts, tea, coffee, and red wine [52–56]. Their broad health benefits, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antitumor activities, are well-documented in preclinical studies [20, 57]. However, a critical factor often overlooked is that their efficacy is not solely determined by dietary intake, but by their bioavailability and biotransformation. Most dietary polyphenols are consumed as glycosides or esters and must undergo extensive metabolism-by host enzymes and, crucially, by the gut microbiota-before absorption, which significantly influences their final bioactive forms and potencies.

Structurally, polyphenols are primarily categorized into flavonoids, phenolic acids, lignans, and stilbenes [58–60]. Based on their chemical structures, polyphenols are primarily classified into four major categories: (1) Flavonoids, such as flavonols, isoflavones, chalcones, anthocyanins, and catechins; (2) Phenolic acids, including hydroxybenzoic acids, hydroxyphenylacetic acids, and hydroxycinnamic acids; (3) Lignans, which comprise dibenzylbutyrolactones (dibenzylbutyrolactone lignans), furofurans (furanofuran lignans), arylnaphthalenes (arylnaphthalene lignans), and neolignans; (4) Stilbenes, exemplified by trans-stilbene and trans-resveratrol [58–61]. The anti-HCC potential of dietary polyphenols is underpinned by their diverse chemical structures, which dictate both their biological activities and dietary availability. The anti-HCC mechanisms of polyphenols are pleiotropic, encompassing the suppression of proliferation, induction of apoptosis, inhibition of metastasis, and modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment [61]. A critical synthesis of the evidence reveals that their multi-target nature often arises from the ability of different compounds (and their metabolites) to converge on key hub pathways dysregulated in HCC, such as PI3K/AKT, AMPK, and Nrf2 signaling. This mechanistic redundancy is a strength but also complicates the identification of a primary driver of efficacy for any single compound. While several polyphenol-rich traditional medicines are used clinically for other indications [62, 63], their direct application in HCC requires rigorous validation, as the complex matrices of herbs present additional challenges for standardizing active components and interpreting outcomes.

Dietary polyphenols with anti-HCC potential: A summary of classification, dietary sources, and absorption/metabolic site

Phenolic acids, including chlorogenic acid (CGA) and caffeic acid (CA), demonstrate hepatoprotective and anti-HCC effects. CGA regulates lipid/glucose metabolism and attenuates liver injury induced by CCl₄, APAP, and D-galactose via Nrf2 activation and NLRP3 inhibition. A recent in vivo study showed that CGA (50 mg/kg/d, p.o., 8 weeks) significantly inhibited DEN-induced HCC progression in rats, associated with modulation of gut microbiota and host metabolism [75, 76]. CA protects against APAP hepatotoxicity by activating Nrf2/HO-1 and improving mitochondrial function [77, 78]. Furthermore, CA ameliorates hepatic microcirculatory dysfunction and oxidative damage, counteracts the downregulation of sirtuin 3 (SIRT3), and enhances mitochondrial respiratory chain (MRC) activity [79].

Flavonoids represent a major subclass with diverse anti-HCC activities, capable of modulating cellular glucose uptake and metabolism in HepG2 cells [80]. Among them, dihydromyricetin (DMY) protects against various forms of liver injury [81, 82], and promotes liver health by regulating lipid metabolism, enhancing ethanol metabolism, and attenuating inflammation [83]. Quercetin (QUE) exerts concentration-dependent dual effects: at low concentrations (≤ 10 µM), it acts as an antioxidant and cytoprotector via Nrf2 activation; at high concentrations (≥ 30–50 µM), it shifts to a pro-oxidant role, inducing selective cytotoxicity in HCC cells through mitochondrial apoptosis and inhibition of survival pathways [84]. Its anti-HCC mechanism involves inhibition of Yin Yang 1 (YY1) and upregulation of p53, disrupting YY1-p53 interaction and promoting apoptosis [85]. Green tea catechins, particularly epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG), are widely studied. In a DEN-induced rat HCC model, EGCG (50 mg/kg/d, p.o., 20 weeks) upregulated p21 and downregulated CDC25A, suppressing hepatocarcinogenesis [86–89]. The EGCG-siRNA platform has validated its gene silencing capability in preclinical models through effective knockdown of PHD2, which stabilizes hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) and dampens inflammatory signal transduction. This targeted delivery system holds substantial promise for HCC intervention, with potential application against functionally diverse oncogenic drivers including anti-apoptotic proteins (Bcl-2, Survivin), transcription factors promoting proliferation (STAT3), and mediators of angiogenesis (VEGF) [90–92]. Epicatechin (EC) reduces hepatic oxidative damage via Nrf2 activation and suppresses inflammatory injury by inhibiting the HSP60-NF-κB axis [89, 93].

Lignans are metabolized by gut microbiota into bioactive enterolignans. Arctigenin, sesamin, lariciresinol, and schisantherin A exhibit notable anti-HCC activity through distinct mechanisms. Arctigenin demonstrates potent in vitro cytotoxicity (IC₅₀=4.74–59.27 nM) and suppresses tumor growth in a HepG2 xenograft model [94]. Sesamin induces cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in HepG2 cells via inhibiting the STAT3 pathway and modulating p53, p21, cyclins, and Bcl-2 family proteins [95]. Lariciresinol triggers mitochondrial-mediated apoptosis, evidenced by caspase-3/PARP-1 activation, loss of ΔΨm, cytochrome c release, and a reduced Bcl-2/Bax ratio [96]. Schisantherin A suppresses proliferation and migration in Hep3B and HCCLM3 cells by inhibiting glycolytic metabolism, and its intraperitoneal injection inhibits Hep3B xenograft tumor growth in vivo [97].

Dietary stilbenes, primarily resveratrol (RES) and its dimethylated derivative pterostilbene (PTS), demonstrate multi-faceted anti-HCC activities. RES exerts broad hepatoprotective and anticancer effects, attenuating alcohol- and drug-induced liver injury, improving lipid metabolism, reducing steatosis, and restoring mitochondrial function [98–100]. Mechanistically, RES induces G₁ cell cycle arrest in Huh7-HBx cells via Akt inhibition and cyclin D1 downregulation. In a Huh7-HBx xenograft model, RES treatment (50–100 mg/kg/d, i.p., 3 weeks) inhibited tumor growth, potentially through survivin downregulation [101]. PTS downregulates metastasis-associated protein 1 (MTA1) and histone deacetylases 1/2 (HDAC1/2), increases p53 acetylation, and induces cell cycle arrest and death in SMMC-7721 cells [102]. Furthermore, trans-resveratrol and tanshinone IIA exhibit synergistic cytotoxicity against HepG2 cells, suggesting potential for combination therapy [103].

Beyond the major subclasses, numerous other plant-derived polyphenols exhibit promising anti-HCC activity in preclinical models through diverse mechanisms. Secoiridoids such as oleuropein suppress proliferation and induce mitochondrial apoptosis in HepG2 cells via PI3K/AKT pathway inhibition [104], while hydroxytyrosol inhibits proliferation by activating store-operated calcium channels and increasing intracellular Ca²⁺ [105]. Notably, olive oil polyphenols including oleocanthal demonstrate multi-targeted effects against HCC by simultaneously inhibiting STAT3 signaling, modulating PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathways, and remodeling the tumor immune microenvironment [106]. Among phenolic terpenoids, parthenolide alleviates diethylnitrosamine-induced hepatocarcinogenesis in rats through potent antioxidant activity that significantly reduces hepatic MDA levels and inhibits ROS generation [107]. Thymol exhibits selective cytotoxicity in HepG2 cells (IC₅₀=11 µM) through mitochondrial-independent cell death mechanisms [108], Carnosic acid suppresses NF-κB activation and activates caspase-3 [109], and carnosol inhibits proliferation and migration via the AMPK-p53 pathway [110]. Furocoumarins demonstrate varied mechanisms: psoralen reverses P-glycoprotein-mediated multidrug resistance [111], bergapten inhibits tumor growth via PI3K/Akt regulation [112]; and xanthotoxin induces G2/M arrest while promoting autophagy [113]. Hydroxypropenylphenols include trans-anethole, which induces mitochondrial apoptosis [114], eugenol-loaded nanoemulsions that suppress proliferation via ROS elevation [115], and 6-gingerol that synergizes with doxorubicin to enhance G2/M arrest while reducing toxicity [116]. Eugenol, a phenolic terpenoid from cloves, inhibits HepG2 cell proliferation (6.25–25 µg/mL) by reducing TNF-α and ROS levels, and enhancing GPx and CYP2E1 gene expression. Additional compounds with anti-HCC activity encompass hydroxycoumarins such as esculetin, which inhibits tumor growth via ferritinophagy [117]. Urolithin A, which suppresses proliferation through antioxidant activity [107]; and the naphthoquinone juglone, which induces concurrent apoptosis and autophagy via the ROS-p53 pathway [118]. In the curcuminoid family, curcumin inhibits tumor growth by suppressing TLR4/NF-κB signaling [119], while bisdemethoxycurcumin combined with all-trans retinoic acid enhances S-phase arrest and apoptosis [120]. Finally, miscellaneous non-flavonoid polyphenols include arbutin, which ameliorates pathological alterations through anti-inflammatory and antioxidant mechanisms [121]; catechol, which inhibits epithelial-mesenchymal transition [122]; and salvianolic acid B, which regulates the Hippo/YAP signaling pathway by promoting YAP phosphorylation and cytoplasmic retention [123].

Preclinical studies on action mechanisms of dietary polyphenols

Targeting core metabolic hallmarks of HCC

Lipid metabolism reprogramming

Abnormal lipid metabolism is a hallmark of HCC. Dietary polyphenols restore lipid homeostasis by modulating multiple signaling effectors [142, 143, 145]. RES activates SIRT1, enhancing AMPK phosphorylation and downregulating ACC, SREBP-1c, and Lipin1 to suppress lipogenesis [143, 144, 146, 149]. In a choline-deficient diet-induced rat model, pterostilbene (PTS, 40 mg/kg/d for 12 weeks) more effectively attenuated HCC progression than RES via AMPK-dependent epigenetic remodeling of H3K27me3 on metabolic genes [150]. DMY activates AMPK, inhibiting the SREBP-1c/mTOR/LXR axis and stimulating the AMPK-PPARα/PGC-1α pathway to enhance β-oxidation [151, 152]. QUE activates the FXR/TGR5 pathway, reducing hepatic lipid accumulation [153]. CGA promotes free fatty acid release via PPARα and facilitates the hydrolysis of exogenous triglycerides [154, 155]. Tea polyphenols such as EGCG and ECG upregulate LDL receptor and SREBP-2 expression, thereby inhibiting intestinal cholesterol absorption [153, 156, 158]. Additionally, polyphenols post-transcriptionally modulate gene expression-e.g., ATP-binding cassette transporter A1 (ABCA1) and FAS-via interactions with microRNAs such as miR-33a and miR-122 [158].

Glucose metabolic homeostasis

Polyphenols regulate glucose homeostasis through multi-target mechanisms. EC counteracts high glucose-induced insulin signaling impairment, restoring glucose transporter 2 (GLUT2) expression and insulin receptor (IR) function [159]. RES improves insulin sensitivity and promotes skeletal muscle glucose uptake via SIRT1 activation [160]. CA stimulates protein kinase B (AKT) and AMPK signaling, enhances glucose uptake and glycogen synthesis in hepatocytes, and inhibits gluconeogenic enzymes including phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) and glucose-6-phosphatase (G6P) [158, 161, 163]. Rutin enhances insulin receptor kinase (IRK) phosphorylation and promotes GLUT4 translocation, improving peripheral glucose uptake [164]. EGCG facilitates GLUT4 translocation in skeletal muscle, restores insulin sensitivity in HepG2 cells, and suppresses hepatic glucose output via antagonism of the glucagon-protein kinase A (PKA) pathway [163, 165, 166]. QUE reduces hexokinase 2 (HK2) expression and inhibits the AKT-mTOR axis, thereby attenuating the glycolytic dependency of HCC cells [167]. As depicted in Fig. 5, the pleiotropic actions of polyphenols, detailed above, orchestrate a concerted rewiring of glycolipid metabolism, which constitutes a core mechanism for their anti-HCC properties.

Modulation of mitochondrial function and oxidative stress

Polyphenols critically regulate cellular energy metabolism and redox homeostasis through multiple mechanisms. RES enhances mitochondrial biogenesis and upregulates key lipid catabolic enzymes, including carnitine palmitoyltransferase I (CPT-1), thereby promoting fatty acid oxidation [168, 169]. DMY modulates the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway via Bcl-2/Bak-caspase-9/3 signaling and concurrently activates the AMPK-PGC1α/ERRα-SIRT3 axis to improve mitochondrial integrity and function [167, 170, 172]. QUE promotes PTEN-induced kinase 1 (PINK1)/Parkin-mediated mitophagy, a process essential for clearing damaged mitochondria and ameliorating high-fat diet-induced mitochondrial dysfunction [173]. Notably, EC directly inhibits lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) activity, disrupting the NAD⁺/NADH redox balance and inducing metabolic stress that suppresses HCC growth both in vitro and in vivo [174]. ellagic acid upregulates estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1), facilitating its nuclear translocation to transcriptionally suppress glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), disrupt the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), and ultimately induce oxidative stress and pyroptosis, thereby inhibiting HCC progression [175].

The potent antioxidant activity of polyphenols, conferred by their ortho-phenolic hydroxyl groups, enables direct reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging and metal ion chelation [176, 177]. CUR enhances peroxisomal fatty acid β-oxidation, inhibits NF-κB activation, and acts as a transient receptor potential melastatin 2 (TRPM2) channel inhibitor to alleviate oxidative mitochondrial damage [175, 178, 180]. EGCG modulates glutathione metabolism and cytochrome P450 2E1 (CYP2E1) activity, effectively reducing lipid peroxidation [181]. CGA attenuates fatty acid synthase (FAS) and acyl-CoA: cholesterol acyltransferase (ACAT) activity while simultaneously enhancing fatty acid β-oxidation [182]. Polydatin suppresses Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) via the microRNA-200a/Nrf2 pathway, thereby inhibiting thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP)/NLRP3 inflammasome activation and mitigating inflammatory oxidative damage [183].

Detoxification of hepatotoxicants and metabolic products

Beyond metabolism, polyphenols directly target crucial oncogenic cascades and epigenetic modifiers. In the PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, a central driver of HCC, curcumin and EGCG are potent inhibitors, with EGCG (50 mg/kg/d in DEN-induced rat models) upregulating p21 and downregulating CDC25A to suppress hepatocarcinogenesis [89]. Oleuropein (0–100 µM) induces mitochondrial apoptosis in HepG2 cells via this pathway [104]. Regarding the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, frequently dysregulated in HCC, curcumin inhibits hypoxia-induced Epithelial-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT) in HepG2 cells, a process intertwined with β-catenin signaling [184]. For the JAK/STAT pathway, sesamin induces cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in HepG2 cells by inhibiting STAT3, while curcumin blocks the IL-6/JAK/STAT3 axis, impeding EMT and angiogenesis [169]. The TGF-β pathway is suppressed by chlorogenic acid (CGA) via the TGF-β1/SMAD7 axis, reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine production and fibrosis [182, 185, 187]. Pterostilbene downregulates metastasis-associated protein 1 (MTA1) and HDAC1/2, increasing p53 acetylation in SMMC-7721 cells [150].

TME via anti-inflammatory and Immunomodulatory actions

The efficacy of many polyphenols begins with gut microbiota-dependent activation and immunomodulation through two interconnected pathways. They are initially metabolized by microbial enzymes (e.g., β-glucosidases, esterases) into bioactive aglycones, and are further transformed into metabolites such as phenyl-γ-valerolactones and phenolic acids [188, 189]. These compounds can enter systemic circulation via the portal vein-directly impacting the liver-or exert local effects, including enhanced intestinal barrier integrity. A meta-analysis on acute lung injury supports this, showing that polyphenol-rich supplementation reduces pro-inflammatory cytokine levels [190]. Furthermore, specific polyphenols like Baicalin and Kaempferol directly modulate the gut’s Th17/Treg balance, underscoring a key mechanism for their intestinal and systemic anti-inflammatory actions [191].

This gut-level immunomodulation underpins many systemic effects. For instance, chlorogenic acid (CGA) modulates the gut microbiota-TLR4-NF-κB axis. It helps maintain gut barrier integrity and enriches beneficial gut microbiota, thereby reducing the translocation of bacterial lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a TLR4 ligand, into the portal circulation. This decrease in hepatic LPS exposure attenuates TLR4-mediated activation of NF-κB signaling, ultimately mitigating liver inflammation [75, 192]. Similarly, pterostilbene downregulates the TXNIP/NLRP3 inflammasome, enhances PPARα signaling, and modulates the gut microbiota-TLR4-NF-κB axis to ameliorate inflammation along the gut-liver interface [193, 194].

Beyond these gut-mediated mechanisms, polyphenols directly suppress multiple pro-oncogenic signaling pathways. Curcumin suppresses pathways including PI3K/AKT, IL-6/JAK/STAT3, and NF-κB, thereby impeding epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), angiogenesis, and glycolysis [184, 195]. It also inhibits TLR4 signaling and the TGF-β1/SMAD7 pathway, reducing the production of key pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, IL-13, and IL-1β [182, 185, 187].

RES not only inhibits SREBP-1c and FAS expression but also improves mitochondrial function in CD8⁺ T cells, potentially revitalizing anti-tumor immunity [168, 196, 197]. DMY and EGCG aalleviate hepatic steatosis and inflammatory responses, partly through suppression of TNF-α [198]. EGCG further exerts anti-HCC effects largely via modulation of the PI3K/AKT pathway, concurrently inhibiting proliferation, inducing apoptosis, and suppressing metastasis [199]. Additionally, proanthocyanidin exerts multi-target anti-HCC activity by inhibiting key inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β), regulating Bcl-2/Bax-mediated apoptosis, and suppressing cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and clonogenicity [200].

Detoxification of hepatotoxicants

Polyphenols mitigate chemical-induced liver injury through multiple and often complementary mechanisms. For instance, chlorogenic acid (CGA) activates the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway and inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation, protecting against liver damage induced by carbon tetrachloride (CCl₄), acetaminophen (APAP), and D-galactose [75, 76]. CUR not only reduces hepatic injury and fibrosis caused by carcinogens like diethylnitrosamine (DEN) and aflatoxin but also chelates iron ions to mitigate iron-dependent oxidative stress [180, 198, 201, 203]. QUE confers protection by inhibiting CYP2E1, thereby reducing the formation of the toxic APAP metabolite N-acetyl-p-benzoquinone imine (NAPQI), and by inducing the cytoprotective enzyme heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) [204, 205]. DMY ameliorates hepatotoxicity through a multi-pronged approach involving modulation of APAP metabolism, inhibition of cell death, and promotion of liver regeneration [206]. Furthermore, EGCG alleviates non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)-associated liver injury by concurrently inhibiting the TGF/SMAD, PI3K/AKT/FoxO1, and NF-κB pathways [207]. Collectively, as illustrated in Fig. 6, these specific mechanisms are integrated into a broader protective network wherein polyphenols and their metabolites, shaped by hepatic and microbial metabolism, act through multi-organ pathways to counter hepatotoxic insults and disrupt the progression of liver disease.

Bioavailability, pharmacokinetics, safety, and delivery strategies

The clinical translation of dietary polyphenols is significantly hampered by their generally low oral bioavailability, a consequence of complex pharmacokinetics, extensive pre-systemic metabolism, and considerable interindividual variation. Following ingestion, most polyphenols undergo substantial phase II metabolism in enterocytes and hepatocytes, forming methylated, sulfated, or glucuronidated derivatives that often exhibit altered bioactivity and accelerated elimination [208]. Pharmacokinetic profiles vary markedly; for instance, cocoa flavanols reach peak plasma concentration (Cmax) at 2.8 ± 0.6 h, with an elimination half-life of 4.8 ± 1.3 h [209], whereas soy isoflavones display biphasic absorption due to enterohepatic recirculation [210]. The food matrix further modulates bioavailability, as demonstrated by a 1.7-fold higher urinary excretion of naringenin from orange juice compared to whole oranges [211]. Age may further affect naringenin pharmacokinetics, though clinical evidence remains limited [212]. Advanced formulations, such as encapsulating hydrophobic compounds like eugenol into porous silica, can prolong half-life and improve release kinetics [210, 213, 215]. Tea catechins, including EGCG, are metabolized by COMT, UGTs, and SULTs before absorption primarily via passive diffusion [216, 217].

Anti-HCC efficacy and delivery-dependent dosing

The anti-HCC efficacy of polyphenols is critically dependent on achieving effective local concentrations, necessitating optimized dosing and delivery strategies. Naringenin induces ferroptosis in HepG2 and Hep3B cells at non-toxic doses (< 0.2 mM) and suppresses tumor growth in vivo at 50 mg/kg by activating AMPK and promoting lipid peroxidation [218, 219]. Clinical evidence from a trial in NAFLD patients showed that 100 mg naringenin twice daily for 4 weeks significantly reduced oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory markers via NF-κB inhibition [220]. Synergistic effects have been achieved through nanoparticulate co-formulations, such as naringenin with ibuprofen or other botanicals, which enhance anti-angiogenic and pro-apoptotic effects in HepG2 cells [221, 222].

Similarly, EGCG inhibits HCC progression through PI3K/AKT suppression and cell cycle modulation, downregulating CDC25A and elevating p21 expression [89]. EGCG-loaded PLGA-siRNA nanoparticles further enhance apoptosis via caspase-3/7 activation [223], and its derivative Y6 impedes tumor angiogenesis by targeting MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT/HIF-1α/VEGF pathways [224]. Eugenol also exerts anti-inflammatory and pro-apoptotic effects in HepG2 cells at 6.25–25 µg/mL, with efficacy improved by nanoemulsions [115].

Promising candidates and advanced delivery systems

Beyond the well-studied polyphenols, several emerging candidates show high potency. Pterostilbene, a resveratrol analog, exhibits ~ 4-fold higher oral bioavailability and enhanced efficacy in NAFLD-HCC models via AMPK-mediated epigenetic mechanisms [225]. Arctigenin demonstrates remarkable nanomolar-range cytotoxicity (IC₅₀=4.74–59.27 nM) against HCC cell lines [94]. The development of advanced nano-formulations-including lipid nanoparticles, polymeric carriers, and metal-phenolic networks-has achieved 5- to 20-fold improvements in bioavailability for various polyphenols [226, 227]. These systems enable multi-targeted therapies; for example, a piceatannol-Fe³⁺ metal-phenolic network overcomes sorafenib resistance by synergizing photothermal therapy with ferroptosis [228]. A smart nanosystem co-assembled from ursolic acid and EGCG not only enhances stability and delivery but also activates innate and adaptive immunity by stimulating IL-12/IFN-γ secretion and promoting T cell infiltration, thereby reversing immunosuppression and inhibiting HCC progression [229]. Concurrently, gut microbiota-mediated biotransformation (e.g., of ellagic acid to urolithins) provides additional avenues for enhancing efficacy [131].

Safety, standardization, and drug interaction risks

While polyphenols from whole foods are generally safe at dietary levels, high-dose supplements require careful safety assessment. Epidemiological studies associate polyphenol-rich diets with reduced HCC risk [230, 231]. However, toxicity thresholds for purified compounds vary: the NOAEL for EGCG is ~ 500 mg/kg/day in animals, with human hepatotoxicity reported at > 800 mg/day [232, 233]. Curcumin (4–8 g/day) is generally well-tolerated, while resveratrol shows a favorable profile up to 1 g/day [234, 235]. A major translational challenge is the lack of unified dosing and the potential for significant drug interactions. Grapefruit flavonoids inhibit CYP3A4, elevating plasma levels of statins and calcium channel blockers [236, 237]. Concomitant use of garlic or Ginkgo biloba with warfarin may augment anticoagulant effects, increasing bleeding risk [238].

Furthermore, many flavonoids inhibit CYP enzymes and affect P-glycoprotein, potentially altering the pharmacokinetics of co-administered drugs like sorafenib and regorafenib [239, 240]. Standardization remains another critical barrier, with studies reporting 20–30% lower active content in commercial products than claimed, and variations in cultivation and processing leading to tenfold differences in polyphenol content [241, 242]. Reliable clinical application thus mandates improved delivery strategies, systematic dose-response studies, and thorough evaluation of herb-drug interactions.

Challenges and future perspectives

Among the plethora of polyphenols investigated, curcumin and resveratrol demonstrate the most extensive preclinical evidence but are plagued by exceptionally low bioavailability. In contrast, EGCG has more human trial data supporting its consumption, albeit with a narrower safety margin at high supplemental doses. Pterostilbene, with its superior metabolic stability, represents a promising next-generation analog worthy of accelerated clinical translation. The clinical translation of dietary polyphenols faces several interconnected challenges that must be systematically addressed. The characteristically low oral bioavailability of many prominent polyphenols, such as EGCG and curcumin, severely limits their systemic exposure and therapeutic potential. This is compounded by chemical instability under physiological conditions, rapid metabolism and elimination, and insufficient tumor-targeting specificity. Furthermore, the scarcity of large-scale, robust clinical trials hinders the validation of their efficacy and safety in human populations, particularly in combination with standard therapeutics where pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic interactions remain incompletely characterized. Future progress will depend on integrated strategies encompassing advanced nanoformulations-such as stimulus-responsive and tumor-targeted delivery systems-to improve bioavailability and specificity. Concurrently, rigorous long-term toxicological studies, scalable manufacturing processes, and well-designed clinical trials are essential to establish conclusive efficacy and safety. Elucidating the intricate interactions between polyphenols, the gut microbiome, and host metabolism through multi-omics approaches will further enable the development of personalized, microbiota-directed polyphenol interventions. Ultimately, overcoming these translational barriers requires a collaborative, multidisciplinary effort to bridge the gap between promising preclinical data and meaningful clinical application in HCC prevention and therapy.

Conclusion

HCC remains a major global health challenge with limited treatment efficacy, driving the search for complementary approaches such as dietary polyphenols-flavonoids, phenolic acids, stilbenes, and lignans-which exhibit multi-target anti-HCC activity through synergistic regulation of metabolism, oxidative stress, and tumor microenvironment. However, their clinical translation is hindered by poor bioavailability and insufficient clinical validation. Future progress depends on developing advanced nanoformulations for targeted delivery, validating polyphenol-drug synergies in well-designed trials, and leveraging multi-omics to decipher polyphenol-microbiome-host interactions. Integrating these strategies will unlock the potential of polyphenols as part of comprehensive, personalized HCC management.
