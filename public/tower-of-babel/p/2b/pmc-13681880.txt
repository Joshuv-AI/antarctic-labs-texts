Background

Microsporidia are a group of spore-forming, obligate intracellular parasites capable of infecting a wide range of hosts [1]. In humans, they are recognized as significant opportunistic pathogens, with Enterocytozoon bieneusi being the most common species associated with gastrointestinal disease, particularly in immunocompromised populations such as those with HIV/AIDS or solid organ transplant recipients [2]. Enterocytozoon bieneusi, is a primary cause of microsporidiosis, presenting symptoms like diarrhea, abdominal pain, and nausea, especially in individuals with weakened immune systems [3]. Diagnosis remains a challenge, as standard microbiological culture techniques are ineffective, often necessitating specialized staining or molecular methods.

We present a case of a renal transplant patient with a disseminated polymicrobial infection involving multiple drug-resistant bacteria and E. bieneusi. This case illustrates the critical role of metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) in uncovering elusive pathogens and guiding successful therapy in complex clinical scenarios.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old cachectic man (height 170 cm, weight 50 kg; BMI 17.3 kg/m²) was admitted to the Department of Infectious Diseases on December 25, 2025, with a 20-day history of recurrent high fever (peak temperature 40 °C), chills, fatigue, chest tightness, and diarrhea with melena. His past medical history was significant for end-stage renal disease, for which he had undergone renal transplantation 16 years prior. His maintenance immunosuppressive regimen consisted of methylprednisolone, mycophenolate mofetil, and cyclosporine. He also had a history of pulmonary embolism treated with rivaroxaban, which was discontinued six months earlier due to gastrointestinal bleeding.

On admission, physical examination revealed a cachectic man with stable vital signs. Bilateral lower extremity pitting edema was noted. Laboratory investigations demonstrated leukocytosis (WBC 16.01 × 10⁹/L), severe anemia (hemoglobin 49.0 g/L), thrombocytopenia (platelets 17 × 10⁹/L), elevated C-reactive protein (97.94 mg/L), and acute kidney injury (creatinine 429 µmol/L). The patient maintained a balanced fluid intake and output. Urinalysis showed no hematuria or proteinuria. Subsequent blood cultures yielded extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Escherichia coli and Klebsiella pneumoniae. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing using the VITEK 2 system (bioMérieux) with ESBL phenotype detection cards confirmed both isolates as extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing. Contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen and chest revealed bilateral pulmonary infiltrates, pleural effusions, intrahepatic and common bile duct dilation with pneumobilia, and thickening of the duodenal and colonic walls, radiologically suggestive of a duodenobiliary fistula (Fig. 1).

Hospital course and management

Days 1–3

The patient was placed under continuous cardiac and hemodynamic monitoring. Empiric antimicrobial therapy with meropenem (1 g every 12 h) was initiated. Supportive care included hemostatic agents, fluid resuscitation, blood transfusions, and antipyretics. Mycophenolate mofetil was withheld. While oxygenation improved with supplemental oxygen and cardiac biomarkers (BNP) showed a declining trend, the patient continued to experience hematochezia, with worsening anemia and coagulopathy (APTT 55.30 s, PT-INR 3.77). Transfusions of packed red blood cells, platelets, and coagulation factors were administered.

Days 4–7

Despite growth factor support and transfusions, the patient remained febrile with temperatures exceeding 38 °C daily. Hematochezia persisted, leading to a further drop in hemoglobin to 19 g/L. Renal function remained impaired (creatinine 245 µmol/L). Given the CT findings of pneumobilia and the progressive decline in hemoglobin, a gastroenterology consultation was obtained. Endoscopic evaluation was deemed high-risk due to profound anemia. An interventional radiology consultation also recommended conservative management over invasive procedures due to high procedural risks. Suspecting an occult opportunistic infection, peripheral blood was sent for metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS).

Day 8

The mNGS results returned, revealing a complex microbial landscape: Herpes simplex virus-1(1112 reads), Cytomegalovirus (290 reads), Klebsiella pneumoniae (509 reads), Enterococcus faecium (805 reads), Escherichia coli (308 reads), and Enterocytozoon bieneusi (185 reads). This report lists a positive reference range (≥ 8 sequence reads for bacteria and fungi) for qualitative calls. Following the mNGS report, quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) assays for CMV and HSV-1 DNA were performed on the patient’s plasma. Both assays returned negative results. Based on the clinical context and sequence reads, E. faecium and E. bieneusi were considered significant pathogens. The antimicrobial regimen was adjusted to include meropenem (1 g q12h), daptomycin (0.25 g every other day), and albendazole (0.4 g orally twice daily).

Days 9–15

A marked clinical improvement was noted by day 10, with defervescence and a significant reduction in diarrhea and hematochezia. However, on day 11, the patient developed a fever of 38.2 °C and tested positive for SARS-CoV-2. Molnupiravir (0.8 g orally every 12 h) was initiated. Notably, renal function showed improvement (creatinine 198 µmol/L, eGFR 36.11 mL/min/1.73 m²), allowing for an increase in the daptomycin dose to 0.25 g daily. Immunosuppression was further modified by discontinuing cyclosporine and escalating methylprednisolone (40 mg intravenous daily). Comprehensive supportive care was intensified, including albumin infusions, blood product transfusions, and correction of coagulopathy with vitamin K1. Octreotide and omeprazole were added for symptomatic management.

Days 16–24

By day 16, the patient was alert and hemodynamically stable. Vital signs were within normal ranges, with body temperature remaining steady between 36.0 °C and 37.2 °C (Fig. 2). Hematochezia had resolved, and laboratory parameters showed significant improvement: procalcitonin 0.019 ng/mL, BNP 889 ng/L, and normalized coagulation (PT-INR 1.23). The patient completed the antibiotic course and was discharged on day 24 in a stable condition.

Discussion and conclusions

The patient’s history of pulmonary embolism, for which he was previously treated with rivaroxaban, is notable. In the context of his chronic renal dysfunction, immunosuppressed state, and possible chronic inflammation, this may reflect an underlying pro-thrombotic tendency, though a full thrombophilia workup was not pursued during this acute admission. The need to balance anticoagulation for prior thromboembolism against the active, severe gastrointestinal bleeding (which had prompted rivaroxaban cessation 6 months prior) was a significant ongoing management challenge.

This case report highlights several critical aspects of managing severe infections in immunocompromised hosts. First, it demonstrates the diagnostic power of mNGS in identifying fastidious or non-cultivable pathogens like E. bieneusi. Standard blood cultures failed to detect this pathogen, yet its identification was crucial for tailoring effective therapy with albendazole. Critically, in our clinical setting in China, oral fumagillin is not commercially available, which precluded it as a practical therapeutic option [4]. Albendazole, in contrast, is widely accessible, has a well-established safety profile, and remains a first-line agent recommended in various guidelines for the treatment of intestinal microsporidiosis, including that caused by E. bieneusi.

Although the clinical presentation—fever and hematochezia—was not the classic chronic watery diarrhea typically associated with intestinal microsporidiosis. It is noteworthy that the patient was placed on a nil-by-mouth regimen due to active gastrointestinal bleeding, which may have suppressed intestinal motility and altered fluid secretion, potentially masking the typical diarrheal symptoms.

The patient’s recovery is attributed to the combined multimodal therapy initiated on day 8, which included daptomycin, albendazole, antiviral treatment, and immunosuppression adjustment. The rapid clinical improvement following this regimen suggests targeting E. bieneusi with albendazole was a necessary component, though its individual contribution cannot be isolated from the other concurrent interventions.

Second, the case illustrates the complexity of polymicrobial infections in transplant patients. The presence of a suspected duodenobiliary fistula likely served as a nidus for bacterial translocation, leading to bacteremia with ESBL-producing organisms. The subsequent detection of E. bieneusiand herpesviruses underscores the need for broad diagnostic considerations beyond typical bacterial pathogens.

Third, the management strategy successfully balanced aggressive antimicrobial therapy with careful immunomodulation. The reduction of calcineurin inhibitors and the temporary intensification of steroids, while counterintuitive for infection control, may have helped stabilize the patient’s overall inflammatory response and organ function, particularly in the context of improving renal function.

A key limitation of this report is the diagnosis of Enterocytozoon bieneusi infection based solely on mNGS, without confirmatory testing via targeted PCR, histopathology, or special staining. This was due to the patient’s unstable clinical status and high bleeding risk, which precluded invasive diagnostic procedures. While mNGS is a highly sensitive tool, the possibility of contamination or detection of non-viable nucleic acid cannot be entirely excluded. However, several factors supported the biological and clinical relevance of the mNGS finding. First, E. bieneusi is an obligate intracellular parasite. The detection of its nucleic acid in peripheral blood (185 reads), though not a direct measure of organism burden, is a significant finding in itself, suggestive of disseminated infection. Second, the presence of a suspected duodenobiliary fistula provided a plausible anatomical conduit for this intestinal parasite to enter the systemic circulation. Third, and most importantly, a marked clinical improvement, particularly in fever and gastrointestinal symptoms, was temporally associated with the initiation of albendazole, a drug with specific activity against microsporidia. While the improvement occurred in the context of a multimodal regimen, this therapeutic response provides supportive, though not definitive, evidence that E. bieneusi was a contributing pathogen. Therefore, within the constraints of the acute clinical setting, the mNGS result provided a crucial diagnostic clue that directly informed and appeared to validate a targeted therapeutic intervention.

Additionally, the patient was concurrently infected with multiple established pathogens, including ESBL-producing E. coli and K. pneumoniae, E. faecium, CMV, and HSV-1, all of which could contribute to the clinical presentation. However, three factors supported considering E. bieneusi as a clinically significant co-pathogen rather than an incidental finding. First, the persistent high-grade fever continued despite empiric therapy with meropenem, which provided adequate coverage against the identified gram-negative bacteria. Second, a marked clinical improvement, particularly in fever and hematochezia, was temporally associated with the initiation of albendazole, a drug with specific activity against microsporidia. Third, E. bieneusi is a recognized cause of disseminated infection in severely immunocompromised hosts. Therefore, while the patient’s illness was undoubtedly polymicrobial, the diagnostic and therapeutic focus on E. bieneusi was justified and appears to have been a critical component of the successful multimodal management strategy.

Finally, the interim diagnosis of COVID-19 emphasizes the vulnerability of this population to superimposed viral infections and the importance of continuous vigilance.

The management of immunosuppression in this case underscores the delicate balance required in transplant recipients with severe, polymicrobial infections. The decision to withhold mycophenolate mofetil was driven by profound cytopenias to mitigate bleeding risk and reduce net immunosuppression. The temporary withdrawal of cyclosporine, while increasing rejection risk, was necessitated by uncontrolled sepsis, SARS-CoV-2 co-infection, and concern for nephrotoxicity, with a clear plan for its monitored reintroduction upon infection resolution. The escalation of corticosteroids was reserved for specific, evidence-based indications—refractory septic shock and COVID-19 pneumonia—rather than as a blanket anti-inflammatory measure. This experience highlights the need for a structured, phased approach: initially reducing immunosuppression to prioritize infection control during the critical phase, followed by a carefully monitored re-escalation during recovery to safeguard allograft function. Success in such complex cases hinges on daily multidisciplinary reassessment, dynamically weighing the risks of infection against those of rejection.

In conclusion, this case underscores that successful outcomes in complex immunocompromised patients rely on a multidisciplinary approach, the early application of advanced molecular diagnostics like mNGS, and a dynamic treatment strategy that addresses both the infectious insult and the underlying immune dysregulation.
