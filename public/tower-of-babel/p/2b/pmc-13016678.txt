Narcolepsy type 2 is an unstable diagnosis and idiopathic hypersomnia has the potential for remission—long-term and cross-sectional observations from the Bern Sleep–Wake Registry
Authors: Rocco G Cavalli, Alice E M Tamagni, Julia van der Meer, Anelia Dietmann, Corrado Bernasconi, Claudio L A Bassetti, Elena S Wenz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStudy ObjectivesThe boundaries between narcolepsy type 1 (NT1), narcolepsy type 2 (NT2), and idiopathic hypersomnia (IH) and the evolution of these diseases over time are poorly understood. This retrospective cohort single-center study with a cross-sectional follow-up aims to assess changes of diagnosis and test findings over time.MethodsRetrospective and cross-sectional data collection: We assessed demographic characteristics, symptoms, sleep–wake tests, and diagnosis changes in patients with NT1, NT2, or IH seen at the Bern Sleep–Wake Epilepsy center between 2000 and 2021. In addition, we obtained information about changes in symptoms and diagnosis by contacting patients seen between 2005 and 2017 by telephone. The two data sources were combined to analyze the frequency of diagnosis changes.ResultsA total of 214 patients (NT1 n = 122, NT2 n = 37, IH n = 55) were included. In 17% (30/179) of patients with follow-up, the diagnosis changed during the observational period. The most unstable diagnosis was NT2 (17/30, 57%), most commonly transitioning to NT1 (n = 5) and IH (n = 9). Five patients with IH went into remission. NT1 was mostly stable (104/107). We confirmed a poor retest reliability of the MSLT in patients with NT2 and IH.ConclusionsFrequent diagnosis changes in patients with NT2 and IH challenge the current diagnostic criteria, in addition, IH can remit.

Introduction

The diagnosis of narcolepsy type 2 (NT2) and idiopathic hypersomnia (IH) is challenging because of overlapping symptoms and the lack of biomarkers. Due to the limited availability of long-term observational analyses, the disease course is not well understood. Like narcolepsy type 1 (NT1), NT2 is a rare and debilitating primary sleep disorder. NT1, NT2, and IH belong to the group of central disorders of hypersomnolence (CDH) and are considered to be chronic. Their main symptom is excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS). Most patients with NT1 show the pathognomonic symptom of cataplexy—episodes with a partial or generalized loss of muscle tone, mostly triggered by strong emotions, during which the patient remains fully conscious. If determined, low levels of cerebrospinal fluid Hypocretin-1 (CSF Hcrt-1, ≤110 pg/mL) are required for the diagnosis of NT1. This specific biomarker can replace the presence of cataplexy and the fulfillment of electrophysiological criteria in unclear diagnosis (see below). Patients with NT1 and NT2 often report sleep paralysis, hypnagogic hallucinations, and fragmented sleep. Patients with IH usually report sleep drunkenness and nonrefreshing sleep, and a subgroup typically shows a prolonged nocturnal sleep time (>11 h). However, apart from cataplexy, the symptoms are not specific and often overlapping, making the diagnosis difficult [1].

NT1, NT2, and IH are diagnosed according to the current International Classification of Sleep Disorders 3rd edition (ICSD-3). Importantly, the distinction between NT2 and IH rests mainly on the number of sleep onset REM periods (SOREMPs) in multiple sleep latency tests (MSLT) or during polysomnography (PSG). Two or more SOREMPs (one of which can be replaced by a nocturnal SOREMP during PSG) are required to diagnose NT2, and no more than one SOREMP allows the diagnosis of IH [2, 3].

Recent studies have questioned the diagnostic criteria for CDH and in particular the value of the MSLT. While the MSLT is an important part of the current diagnostic criteria, it seems to lack sensitivity and specificity [2, 4–6]. An mSL <8 min and ≥2 SOREMPs can be observed in 1%–6% of healthy adults [7]. Various factors, such as sex, age, circadian misalignment, sleep restriction, sleep apnoea and depression, are known to affect the number of SOREMPs [7, 8]. Furthermore, in past studies, not all patients with a typical history of IH would today meet the current ICDS-3 criteria of an MSLT sleep latency of <8 min [9, 10]. Finally, the MSLT appears to be poorly reproducible, which has been shown to lead to formal changes of diagnosis, mainly between NT2 and IH [11–13].

The distinction between the different CDH is additionally complicated by their unstable clinical presentation over time. For this reason, some authors support the hypothesis of “fluid boundaries” between the different CDH [14]. Others have proposed that NT2 and IH without long sleep time be considered as a single disease entity or that the diseases could be lumped together in a different and more symptom-led way [4, 5, 15]. In addition, the clinical evolution of some cases of NT2 into NT1 and the suggestion of an autoimmune process targeting Hcrt-producing neurons in some patients with NT2 has raised the suspicion that NT2 could be an incomplete or early stage of NT1 [2, 16–19]. Based on our clinical experience, this might also be true for some cases of IH. For these reasons, some diagnoses become clear only in the long-term disease course or can be subject to change over time and with repeated diagnostic testing [20]. Spontaneous remissions have also been reported [10, 20, 21]. Long-term follow-up studies of IH or NT2 have reported inconsistent findings, with both diseases having the potential for remission [10, 20–23].

In this retrospective cohort study with a cross-sectional assessment, we aimed to identify changes of diagnosis and remissions in patients with NT1, NT2, and IH over the disease course. We further aimed to assess the impact of repeated electrophysiological examinations, such as the MSLT, on the diagnosis.

Materials and Methods

Screening and eligibility

We reviewed the clinical patient registry of the Sleep-Wake-Epilepsy Center of the Inselspital, University Hospital of Bern (Bern Sleep–Wake Registry). All patients with mentioned NT1, NT2, or IH among the primary differential diagnoses, who consulted the center between 2000 and 2021, were screened for eligibility. We considered patients with either a first diagnosis or an already established diagnosis if they came for follow-up or for a second opinion between 2000 and 2021. For patients consulting our center before the publication of the current diagnostic criteria, compliance with ICSD-3 diagnostic criteria was assessed retrospectively based on the patient’s medical records. We included all patients with a definite diagnosis, meeting the ICDS-3 criteria after the diagnostic workup (= baseline). We also included patients with a probable diagnosis. These did not fully meet ICDS-3 criteria but presented with suggestive clinical features according to the assessment of a senior physician and expert in sleep medicine. Patients with comorbidities potentially causing EDS, such as obstructive sleep apnoea syndrome (OSAS) or mild psychiatric conditions, were included if they were being effectively treated or considered to be of minor clinical relevance. Studies on parts of this cohort have previously been published [6, 24].

Retrospective data collection

We reviewed the following sources: clinical records, examinations performed in the sleep laboratory, HLA typing, Hcrt-1 levels in the cerebrospinal fluid, some standard questionnaires (Epworth Sleepiness Scale, Fatigue Severity Scale) and a clinical questionnaire of 110 questions on sleep and wakefulness (Berner Schlaffragebogen), specific to our sleep center. As follow-up information, we only considered changes of diagnosis and repeated sleep laboratory examinations. All information was systematically entered into a RedCap electronic data capture tool hosted at the University of Bern [25, 26].

Telephone interview (cross-sectional data collection)

In 2020 and 2021, all patients in our cohort who had visited our center between 2005 and 2017 were contacted (Figure 1). We did not include patients who visited our center before 2005 for the last time (they mostly lacked up-to-date contact information) and patients diagnosed after 2018. In this subgroup, we considered the information in the medical records to be sufficiently recent. During a semi-structured telephone interview, we asked the patients about the validity of their last documented diagnosis at our center, about a potential new diagnosis, and about current sleep–wake symptoms. It was not possible to assess the exact time point of diagnosis change, which is why we defined the time point of the telephone interview as the time of “documented diagnosis change.”

Sleep–wake tests

Sleep stages were assessed according to standard criteria valid at the time of the patient’s visit [3, 27, 28]. The patient’s sleep duration per 24 h was estimated by actigraphy over 1–2 weeks prior to the PSG recording, as most PSGs were not performed with ad libitum sleep due to the standard operating procedures in our hospital. The MSLT was in most cases performed the day after PSG, with four to five test sessions over the day. The first test started 1.5–3 h after awakening, and there were intervals of 1.5–3 h between the tests. The standard operating procedure at our hospital generally requires the interruption of stimulants or sodium oxybate and, if possible, of psychopharmacological treatment prior to sleep laboratory testing.

Change of diagnosis or remission

We documented a change of diagnosis or remission in our database if either:

a new diagnosis or remission was documented in the medical records during the observation time,

Information from clinical documentation and telephone interviews was combined to evaluate the frequency of diagnosis changes. If a telephone interview was performed, this information was taken as the basis for the analysis.

Data analysis

The statistical analysis was performed with SPSS Statistics 27 Software and R (v4.3.0). Since most of the data were not normally distributed, numerical values are presented as median and interquartile ranges (IQR). Categorical measurements are expressed as percentages. To identify differences between patient groups for numerical and ordinal data, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used. For categorical data, Pearson’s chi-squared tests were used and Fisher’s exact tests in case of small sample sizes, according to the default settings of the statistical package gtsummary. The analysis was exploratory and statistical tests were conducted at the 5% significance level without adjustment for multiplicity. We calculated rates of diagnosis change at the longest time of follow-up and visualized them in a survival plot. The incidence of diagnosis changes at 5 years postdiagnosis was estimated by the Kaplan–Meier method. We also represented the changes of diagnosis in a Sankey diagram and with bar charts, the latter to visualize the relation with the duration of the observation period.

To assess patterns and their reproducibility in the PSG and MSLT, we adapted the concept of electrophysiological “phenotypes” introduced by Lopez et al. 2017 [12]. For all four phenotypes, both MSLT and PSG results were considered.

Ethical statement

All participants included in this study either provided a general consent for the use of their data for research purposes by the University Hospital of Bern or a specific informed consent for this study. The study was approved by the Swiss cantonal ethics committee (KEK-number 2019-02446).

Results

Patient selection

The first screening identified 391 patients out of more than 10 000 patients in the Bern Sleep–Wake Registry, whose data were reviewed for eligibility.

All had a differential diagnosis of NT1, NT2, or IH mentioned at any time point during the clinical evaluation. Details of the selection process with the reasons for exclusion are provided in Figure 1. A total of 214 patients with available baseline information were included in the study: 197 patients (92%) had a definite and 17 (8%) a probable diagnosis. Probable diagnoses were assigned to four patients with NT1 and typical cataplexies but without available MSLT information, nine patients (NT1 n = 2; NT2 n = 1; IH n = 6) with MSLT mSL >8 min but typical clinic for the condition, and four patients with NT1 and a history of typical cataplexy but presenting without SOREMPs. A total of 165 patients had a documented follow-up in the clinical records. All patients who visited our center for an initial diagnosis, second opinion, or follow-up, between 2005 and 2017, including those with a documented follow-up, were contacted by telephone (n = 140, 65%), and 76 participated in the telephone interview. In summary, our follow-up analysis included 179 patients, 103 with only retrospective follow-up data, 14 with follow-up data from the telephone interview only, and 62 with both types of follow-up information available (Figure 1). If available, information from the telephone interview was always taken as a basis for analysis.

Among patients with NT1, 99% (93/94) were HLA DQB1*06:02-positive, compared to 50% (14/28) of patients with NT2 and 29% (7/24) of patients with IH (p < .001). Thirty-five patients in our cohort had known Hcrt-1 levels as determined by a radioimmunoassay. Among tested individuals with NT1, 97% (29/30) were Hcrt-1 deficient. Observation time

The median total duration of the observation period was 8 years (IQR 2, 16) for NT1, 5 years (IQR 2, 10) for NT2, and 6 years (IQR 3, 9) for IH, calculated as time from diagnosis to last in-hospital follow-up visit or, if available, to the telephone interview.

Changes of diagnosis

The diagnosis of 30/179 (16.8%) patients changed during the observation period (clinical records: n = 23, telephone interview: n = 7). As shown in Figure 2 and Figure 3, changes in diagnosis occurred most often in patients with NT2 (17/30, 56.7%), followed by those with IH (10/42, 23.8%) and NT1 (3/107, 2.8%).

From the 17 patients initially classified as NT2 with diagnosis change, five patients developed cataplexy during the disease course (diagnosis change to NT1 after 0.5–5 years [median 2 years]). In one patient, the diagnosis was confirmed by reduced Hcrt-1 levels, and all five patients were HLA DQB1*06:02-positive. In nine patients, the diagnosis changed to IH, either after repeated MSLT (n = 6, disappearance of SOREMPs) or after clinical reevaluation (n = 3, nonrefreshing long sleep duration and sleep drunkenness, no MSLT repetition). In three patients, psychiatric evaluation resulted in a change of diagnosis to hypersomnia associated with psychiatric disease.

From the 10 patients initially classified as IH, who had a diagnosis change, 5 patients reported complete resolution of symptoms and discontinuation of any treatment during the telephone interview. One patient developed NT1 with proven Hcrt-1 deficiency and new onset of cataplexy and SOREMPs after 2 years. In two patients, depression was diagnosed, and the diagnosis was changed to hypersomnia associated with a psychiatric disease within the first 2 years of follow-up. In one patient, sleepiness resolved after improvement of sleep hygiene and sleep duration, and the diagnosis changed to insufficient sleep syndrome. This patient did not work shifts at baseline. In one patient, the diagnosis changed to NT2 after clinical reevaluation, despite missing SOREMPs in the initial MSLT. This patient presented with increasing episodes of sleep paralysis, no sleep drunkenness and a positive family history of NT2.

From the three patients initially classified as NT1 with a diagnosis change, two reported complete disappearance of cataplexies during clinical follow-up and were re-diagnosed as NT2. The initially reported cataplexies were re-evaluated to be “atypical.” The third patient reported complete disappearance of all symptoms after discontinuation of treatment during the telephone interview. It is noteworthy that no CSF Hcrt-1 measurements were performed in those patients at the time of diagnosis, but they all met ICSD-3 criteria.

In summary, 18 of the 30 patients (60.0%) changed between the included diagnosis groups (NT1, NT2, or IH), six patients changed to another CDH diagnosis (one to insufficient sleep syndrome; five to hypersomnia associated with psychiatric disease) and six patients went into remission (one with initial definite NT1, five with initial definite IH; Figures 2–4). Of the 18 changes within our included diagnosis groups, 14 (78%) diagnoses were fully ICSD-3 compliant. The remaining four changes between NT2 and IH were based on clinical reassessment only (without repetition of the MSLT, and thus not fully compliant to ICSD-3 criteria).

After 5 years, the stability (“survival”) of the diagnoses was lowest for NT2 (49% stable diagnosis) intermediate for IH (84%) and highest for NT1 (99%).

Subgroup analysis: changes in diagnosis after repeated electrophysiological testing and electrophysiological phenotype

With the aim of investigating the cohort specifically with respect to electrophysiological markers and their possible impact upon changes to the diagnosis, we analyzed the subcohort of 53 patients (24.7%; NT1 n = 27 [22.1%], NT2 n = 16 [44.4%], IH n = 10 [17.9%]) who had repeated the MSLT. The median interval to repetition was 2.1 years (0.1–20.8 years). Of these patients, 22 also repeated the PSG. Eight patients repeated the MSLT a third time (NT1 n = 3, NT2 n = 3, IH n = 2, with one PSG repetition). For our analysis, we considered the first and the last examination of a total of 61 repetitions of the MSLT and 23 repetitions of the PSG.

Reasons to repeat the MSLT were: (1) systematic reassessment (eg, for a second opinion after the first diagnostic workup at an external center) (n = 21); (2) uncertain diagnosis (n = 17); (3) evaluation of the effect of therapy (intravenous immunoglobulins: n = 5; ineffective or not tolerated stimulant therapy: n = 5); (4) reevaluation after treatment of concomitant OSAS (n = 6); (5) change of core symptoms or worsening (n = 5). In two cases, the reason for the reassessment was not clearly stated.

The initial electrophysiological phenotypes and the changes after repeated MSLT are shown in Figure 5. To summarize, 53% (n = 28) kept their electrophysiological phenotype after reassessment. Narc-EP was the most stable phenotype, maintained by 18 patients (18/29, 62%), followed by Norm-EP (1/2, 50%), Hyper-EP (9/21, 43%), and REM-EP (0/1, 0%). The stability of Narc-EP was better in patients with NT1, of whom 77% (13/17) maintained their initial phenotype, than in patients with NT2 (42%, 5/12). Discussion

We determined the stability of the diagnoses NT1, NT2, and IH in a “real-world” retrospective cohort study using clinical patient data collected in the Bern Sleep–Wake Registry from 2000 to 2021 and performed a cross-sectional follow-up by telephone interview in a subgroup of patients. The median total observation time was 8 years for NT1, 5 years for NT2, and 6 years for IH. The diagnosis changed in 17% of patients with any follow-up (30/179), and the probability of a diagnosis change was highly dependent on the initial diagnosis (NT2 > IH > NT1). To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate the stability and fluctuations of these three CDH diagnoses over time.

We confirmed that NT2 is an unstable diagnostic entity [5, 15, 19, 21]. Its longitudinal profile has rarely been studied, although its existence in relation to NT1 and IH has been questioned [2, 4]. In our cohort, we observed a change from NT2 to NT1 (appearance of cataplexy) or IH (disappearance of SOREMP and/or development of symptoms more typical of IH) in more than 50% of patients. Taking this into account, together with the low specificity, sensitivity, and reproducibility of the MSLT, we suggest that new, more specific disease markers are needed for diagnosing NT2, or NT2 as a separate disease entity should be reevaluated [2, 4–6, 11–13]. The patients identified as having NT2 according to the current classification, could in fact belong to two subcategories, one representing an early stage of NT1 and the other corresponding to another central disorder of hypersomnolence with false-positive MSLT and a less typical phenotype (eg, IH with shorter sleep duration and refreshing naps, previously undetected psychiatric comorbidity).

There appeared to be some potential for remission in patients with IH (5/42 (12%) patients with follow-up). The heterogeneity of its prognosis has been noted in previous studies [10, 21–23].

However, remissions were only detected by telephone interview, as these patients did not return for follow-up after experiencing clinical improvement. Future studies on the topic should therefore be prospective, or at least also include some kind of cross-sectional follow-up. In our cohort, IH was also the CDH most likely to be reassessed as hypersomnia associated with psychiatric disease or insufficient sleep syndrome. The diagnostic boundaries between IH with mild psychiatric symptoms and hypersomnia associated with psychiatric disorders are poorly defined, which might promote reclassifications. As reported in previous studies, the diagnosis of NT1 is very stable over time and only rarely subject to change (2.8% in our cohort) [21, 29, 30]. Unfortunately, the two patients whose diagnosis changed from NT1 to NT2, due to a different interpretation of cataplexy by another physician, did not undergo lumbar puncture to determine CSF Hcrt-1 levels. Due to missing clinical follow-up, no further clinical information about the patient who reported total remission during the telephone interview is available. However, we consider the initial diagnosis of NT1 legitimate in this case, since the patient presented with cataplexy several times a week, an mSL of 4.5 min, 4 SOREMPs during MSLT and a positive HLA status. Few cases of remission of patients with NT1 confirmed by hypocretin measurements have been described in the literature [29, 30].

Our findings highlight the importance of good history-taking, with regard to cataplexy, and to determine Hcrt-1 in the CSF. Despite being pathognomonic, cataplexy is not always easy to identify, especially if it presents atypically or infrequently, hampering differentiation between NT1 and NT2.

(1) A distinct change in the symptoms included in the ICSD-3 criteria (eg, appearance of cataplexy, diagnosis of a relevant comorbid psychiatric disorder, disappearance of symptoms/remission).

Our study allowed to assess formal diagnosis changes, but not the underlying pathophysiology. We are therefore unable to determine whether only the diagnosis (based on one of the three named factors above) or the pathophysiology of the diseases changed over time. Some changes in diagnosis might rather reflect issues with the application of diagnostic criteria (mainly concerning NT2 and IH) or an initial diagnostic inaccuracy (eg, deferred evaluation by a psychiatric specialist, difficulty excluding insufficient sleep syndrome) [31]. However, we assume this difficulty to reflect typical diagnostic dilemmas in clinical practice.

In our cohort, we confirmed the poor reliability of the MSLT and the low reproducibility of the electrophysiological phenotype in patients with an initial diagnosis of NT2 or IH after reassessment [11–13]. Based on those results, the value of the MSLT as basis for any diagnosis or change to a diagnosis of CDH presenting without cataplexies should be carefully reevaluated. Interestingly, in all 53 patients with repeated MSLT, despite 25 changes of the electrophysiological phenotype only 12 clinical diagnosis changed afterwards (Figure 5). We conclude that changes in diagnosis following a complete reevaluation were often rather based on the clinical presentation than MSLT results.

The cohort characteristics at baseline (Tables 1 and 2) are mostly in line with previous studies [10, 15, 22, 32–34]. An exception is the median age at diagnosis, which in our study is rather high for patients with NT1 (30 years for NT1; 23 years for NT2 and 24 years for IH). The main reasons might be our position as a tertiary referral center and that our center only treats adult patients.

Consistent with previous literature, the sex distribution in our cohort at baseline differs significantly between narcolepsy and IH [7, 32]. Furthermore, percentages of patients with HLA DQB1*06:02-positivity are comparable with those reported in the literature [1]. The relatively higher percentage of HLA DQB1*06:02-positivity in patients with NT2 compared to IH (not significant) supports the hypothesis of a “continuum” with NT1. This might be true for at least some patients with NT2.

We noted a rather high number of patients with IH and a positive family history for sleep disorders (44%). A positive family history for IH has previously been described in different series for up to 39% of patients, but the genetic background is unclear [10, 22, 35–39]. A positive family history of 12% for NT1 in our cohort seems in the range of previous reports [40]. We were able to confirm a high prevalence of fatigue in all three disease groups (Table 1) [9, 23, 32, 41, 42].

Whereas the differences between the electrophysiological parameters (Table 2), mainly concerning PSG and MSLT sleep latencies (NT1 < NT2 < IH) and occurrence of SOREMPs (NT1 > NT2 > IH) are expected, we notice only a few differences in symptoms between NT2 and IH (Table 1). This has been discussed in the past, underlining current diagnostic difficulties, and raises the question, whether some patients with NT2 and IH have the same disease and are only arbitrarily divided by MSLT results [5].

Both NT2 and IH patients report longer sleep times on the weekend than during the week. This could suggest a degree of underlying sleep insufficiency in some patients. Our PSG standard protocol does not allow assessment of hypersomnolence, because patients are usually woken up between 6 and 7 am. The “ad libitum” sleep protocol was irregularly applied, which is why the total sleep time during PSG cannot be meaningfully interpreted.

Interestingly, patients with NT1 were started on medication after clinical workup more often than patients with NT2 and IH, which could be related to diagnostic uncertainty, symptom severity, and the fact that most medications for NT2 and IH are used off-label.

Unfortunately, only 16% of patients in our cohort had Hcrt-1 measurements. This might partly be due to the long observation period, when it was less common to measure Hcrt-1 early on. The high number of diagnosis changes in this study and the heterogeneity of disease presentations underline the importance of determining this objective and specific parameter, not only in patients with a clear clinical presentation of NT1 but also if NT2 is a differential diagnosis.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of our study is the high number of included patients based on a comprehensive clinical evaluation (integration of clinical and paraclinical data) and the long-term follow-up. Nevertheless, the number of cases for some specific questions remains rather low.

This “real-world” retrospective cohort study with a cross-sectional follow-up (telephone interview) tried to take into account the complexity of the clinical reality affecting diagnostic processes. Various factors need to be considered when interpreting our results. The retrospective assessment relies on the data quality and completeness of the clinical records and can be subject to reporting and selection bias. Since the beginning of the observation period of this study, there were not only changes in infrastructure and responsible personnel, but also in knowledge, diagnostic criteria, and treatment guidelines. However, diagnoses were always made by senior physicians with longstanding expertise in sleep medicine.

There are some remaining questions that we could not answer with the available data. For example, it would have been interesting to analyze the association between sleep duration in patients with IH (short vs. long sleep time) and diagnosis stability.

Not all patients agree to undergo all diagnostic tests, especially lumbar puncture. In Switzerland, depending on their insurance policy, patients often carry the costs of examinations. This may discourage some from repeating expensive examinations, particularly if no therapeutic consequences are anticipated. Further, MSLT is often repeated because of diagnostic uncertainty or unclear clinical course, possibly leading to a selection bias when analyzing the reproducibility. However, Lopez et al. did not discover an effect of the clinical decision to repeat the PSG-MSLT evaluation in their study [12].

Other comorbidities (eg, OSAS, psychiatric illnesses) might complicate the interpretation of electrophysiological results. Moreover, the chance for a patient to get a diagnosis like hypersomnolence associated with a psychiatric disorder could depend on whether or not the patient gets a psychiatric assessment. We do not know how many patients in our study had a psychiatric evaluation between baseline and follow-up.

Since many diagnosis changes were reported by patients during the telephone interview, the exact time of those changes is unknown, and the changes have not been verified by a clinical assessment. Additionally, early censoring for many patients led to low sample numbers for longer times of follow-up (Figure 4). Thus, diagnosis survival times should be interpreted with caution, especially for follow-up times over 5 years. Despite the limitation of combining information from two different data types (clinical records and telephone interview) to assess diagnosis changes and the reduced time frame for eligibility for the telephone interview (2005–2017) for practical reasons (see methods), the telephone interview allowed the follow-up of a much higher number of patients and the identification of patient-reported disease remissions.

Conclusion

The results of our study support the idea of a possible biological or pathophysiological continuum between patients with NT1 and some patients with NT2 and IH [22]. Our findings call into question the current diagnostic criteria, which give excessive weight to the MSLT and less to clinical features. This study suggests considering IH and NT2 as uncertain diagnoses, possibly requiring a more longitudinal follow-up and careful evaluation and weighting of comorbidities early in the diagnostic process (eg, psychiatric disorders, sleep deficiency) [5, 22]. To improve the diagnosis and treatment of CDH, we still need to improve the understanding of the underlying pathophysiologies and develop more specific biomarkers and prognostic factors [6].
