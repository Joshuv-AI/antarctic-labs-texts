Cognitive & Behavioral Assessment Toolbox (CBAT): An open‐source platform supporting experiments in computational psychiatry
Authors: Asako Toyama, Yoshihiko Kunisato, Yuichi Yamashita, Shinsuke Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsLarge‐scale behavioral experiments are increasingly recognized as essential in psychiatric research based on a transdiagnostic dimensional view of mental disorders. However, advanced programming skills and substantial financial resources are usually needed to conduct such experiments, posing a barrier for new researchers. In this study, we introduce and validate an open‐source platform, the Cognitive & Behavioral Assessment Toolbox (CBAT), which enables researchers to conduct large‐scale behavioral experiments without the need for advanced programming expertise.MethodsTo validate the CBAT, we conducted an online behavioral experiment involving 1309 Japanese participants. Participants completed four behavioral tasks that can be deployed using the CBAT: the attention network test, the beads task, the probabilistic reward task, and the delay and probability discounting task. In addition, demographic information and self‐report questionnaires, including psychiatric symptom scales, were collected.ResultsThe behavioral data successfully replicated key findings in prior studies that employed the four behavioral tasks. Specifically, we observed classic attentional network effects in the attention network test, conservative information sampling in the beads task, expected response bias in the probabilistic reward task, and robust delay and probability discounting in the delay and probability discounting task.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate the empirical validity and broad utility of the CBAT. By facilitating behavioral experiments, the CBAT provides a flexible infrastructure to support transdiagnostic psychiatric research and investigations in other related fields. As it does not require advanced programming skills or significant financial resources, the CBAT lowers the barrier to conducting high‐quality research and promotes open science in psychiatry.

The current diagnostic categories of mental disorders (e.g. schizophrenia or major depressive disorder) have been challenged. For example, the same clinical label can emerge from many distinct biological pathways, a phenomenon known as equifinality. 1 , 2 , 3 Conversely, a single cause can lead to different diagnoses, a pattern termed multifinality. 1 , 4 High rates of comorbidity further blur categorical boundaries among mental disorders. 5 Moreover, growing evidence has suggested that diagnostic categories are often not predictive of treatment response or symptom development. 6 These findings imply that the categorical diagnoses may not reflect clinically or biologically relevant aspects of mental disorders.

In recent years, there has been a growing trend for the dimensional view of mental disorders to complement the categorical view. The dimensional view posits that mental disorders should be characterized as a continuous spectrum on a high‐dimensional space over transdiagnostic axes. For example, the hierarchical taxonomy of psychopathology (HiTOP) framework proposes a hierarchical dimensional classification of mental health problems. 7 , 8 By analyzing large‐scale cohort data collected by structured interviews with the Diagnostic Interview Schedule, Caspi et al. 9 identified a ‘p factor’, capturing general psychopathology across disorders. In support of the p‐factor concept, Soda et al. 10 reported that the general factor was linked to a history of diverse diagnoses.

The National Institute of Mental Health (NIMH) in the United States proposed another framework, the Research Domain Criteria (RDoC). 6 This framework aims to understand mental disorders across multiple dimensions of fundamental neurobehavioral functions, termed domains (e.g. negative valence systems, positive valence systems, cognitive systems). RDoC has promoted studies spanning health and disorders. 11 , 12

Integrating such approaches, studies in computational psychiatry have examined relationships between transdiagnostic dimensions of psychiatric symptoms and a single domain of neurobehavioral functions in humans. 13 Leveraging large‐scale online experiments, they have identified latent factors (dimensions) underlying the questionnaire data of psychiatric symptoms and their relationships with a neurobehavioral function, such as model‐based planning, 14 , 15 reinforcement learning, 16 , 17 , 18 and metacognition. 19 , 20 , 21 For instance, our previous research 18 elucidated how a transdiagnostic psychiatric dimension related to compulsive and intrusive thoughts was related to reinforcement learning, a subdomain of the positive valence systems in RDoC.

However, to date, few studies have established a comprehensive correspondence between the multiple dimensions of fundamental neurobehavioral functions documented in RDoC and the various symptom dimensions observed in psychopathology. This scarcity of research could be due, at least in part, to the challenges involved in implementing multiple experimental tasks and questionnaires online. While large‐scale online experiments are recognized as efficient tools for collecting large‐scale data in psychiatry, 22 the implementation of tasks often requires advanced programming skills. This highlights the need for a battery of experimental tasks and questionnaires that can be easily implemented online.

Relatedly, from the perspective of open science, the demand for research transparency, reproducibility, and societal impact is growing. However, obtaining the information necessary for replication and extension of experiments often requires substantial time and effort, and crucial details – such as the precise specifications of experimental tasks – are frequently unavailable. Moreover, scientific discoveries tend to remain confined within academic circles, where only specific researchers can fully interpret and utilize the findings. To address these limitations, a research infrastructure that is accessible not only to researchers but also to people outside academia and does not require advanced technical skills is needed.

In this study, we introduce a battery of behavioral tasks and questionnaires for online experiments, named the Cognitive & Behavioral Assessment Toolbox (CBAT). The CBAT includes experimental tasks that correspond to a range of RDoC domains, as well as questionnaires that cover various psychiatric symptoms. Although similar batteries are available in English (see ‘Comparison with similar tools’ in the next section), this is the first in Japanese and simplifies experiment implementation. This is particularly important for cross‐cultural psychiatric research, as many studies focus on WEIRD populations, whose findings may not be generalizable across cultures. 23 The CBAT also provides English content, contributing to more inclusive and generalizable models of behavior and mental health.

In the following sections, we first introduce the characteristics of the CBAT and its potential contributions. Then, we present an example of an online experiment that can be conducted using the CBAT and discuss the challenges and future directions of this framework.

Cognitive & Behavioral Assessment Toolbox (CBAT)

The CBAT provides several scripts for questionnaires and behavioral tasks, written using jsPsych (https://www.jspsych.org/latest/) 24 ; or lab.js (https://lab.js.org/) 25 ; and stored on GitHub (https://github.com/) (Fig. 1a). Users can select the necessary components via the CBAT platform to construct experiments, which can then be run using JATOS – a free and open‐source tool for online experiments (https://www.jatos.org/). 26 CBAT has the potential to become a powerful tool when used with databases such as the Computational Psychiatry Research Map (CPSYMAP; RRID:SCR_018942; https://ncnp-cpsy-rmap.web.app/) 27 (see Research connectivity and extensibility section).

(b) The panel shows short instructions on how to use the CBAT. With respect to mental health questionnaires, owing to copyright restrictions, only a few that have no copyright issues, as well as some demo questionnaires, are currently available in the CBAT.

Several types of content, including behavioral tasks, have already been deployed on the CBAT site (https://cbat.cpsy-lab.com/). Unfortunately, at present, most existing questionnaires cannot be made available on the CBAT due to copyright restrictions. However, a few questionnaires that have no copyright issues, as well as some demo questionnaires, are already available.

Features of the CBAT

Minimal requirements, quick start

The only requirement to use the CBAT is to register on the CBAT website (https://cbat.cpsy-lab.com/) with a Google account. We also recommend downloading and installing JATOS, which is useful for conducting experiments using CBAT components.

The most distinctive feature of this tool is its block‐based modular design, which allows users to easily combine consent forms, questionnaires, and tasks into experimental protocols. Currently, the tasks of the CBAT support only desktop and laptop use, with mobile compatibility under development.

Free access, free skills (and free customization)

The CBAT is freely available and requires no specialized programming skills, so it is accessible to a wide range of users – including researchers, students, and citizens. For users with programming experience or basic knowledge of jsPsych or lab.js, the code downloaded from the CBAT can be customized or extended. Modifications such as changing the texts or replacing stimulus images can be made directly in the downloaded files. Although the amount of content currently available in the CBAT is still limited, we are actively working to expand it. In addition, we have developed GUI‐based applications (Shiny applications) that enable users to create custom questionnaires and informed consent forms that are not included in the CBAT, without requiring advanced programming skills. The generated files can be used in combination with the behavioral tasks provided in the CBAT. Detailed instructions and links to these applications are available at https://cba-toolbox.github.io/CBAT/en/usage.html. These applications are based on an R package developed for the CBAT (cbat4r; https://github.com/cba-toolbox/cbat4r). Accordingly, the same functionality is also available directly within the R environment.

The CBAT is free to use. Experiments can be run locally at no cost, but online experiments require a server for data storage, which may incur additional costs.

Research connectivity and extensibility

CBAT is further enhanced when used in combination with external resources. CPSYMAP is a database that maps research papers onto a multidimensional space defined by neuroscience, psychiatry, and computational modeling approaches, allowing users to identify relevant tasks, constructs, and models related to a given research question. For example, researchers using CBAT tasks can refer to CPSYMAP to explore how similar tasks have been linked to specific RDoC constructs or computational models in the literature.

CBAT is primarily designed for cross‐sectional experiments. It does not currently provide built‐in functionality for longitudinal tracking. For studies requiring longitudinal tracking, we have developed a complementary platform, CEMA (Computational Ecological Momentary Assessment: see the section of Challenges of the CBAT framework and its contribution to computational psychiatry). In addition, a forthcoming computational model toolbox (Computational Model Toolbox, CMOT) will facilitate the application of computational models – such as reinforcement learning and Bayesian inference – to behavioral data obtained using CBAT. By using CBAT with CMOT, users will be able to analyze data using standardized modeling pipelines, thereby improving comparability and reproducibility across studies.

Language

Most of the instructions and questionnaires are written in Japanese, which allows for the collection of high‐quality data from Japanese‐speaking populations and promotes cross‐cultural studies. The source code is available upon request to the authors, so it can be adapted and implemented in other countries. Furthermore, some English versions are also prepared to support cross‐cultural comparisons.

Comparison with similar tools

Several existing platforms share some conceptual features with the CBAT. For example, Millisecond Inquisit (https://www.millisecond.com/) provides an extensive test library but requires a paid license for data acquisition, and users need to learn Inquisit's proprietary scripting language to create or modify experiments beyond basic templates. The Experiment Factory (https://www.expfactory.org/) 28 , 29 is another platform that inspired the development of the CBAT. It offers many open‐source behavioral experiments and provide flexibility and modularity. However, it requires programming skills and a technical setup, including the use of Docker, which may be a barrier for some users.

In contrast, the CBAT combines open accessibility with a block‐based modular interface that allows users to construct and run experiments without programming skills while still supporting code‐based customization. This combination of usability and flexibility makes it well suited for open, collaborative research. The CBAT also features RDoC‐based task tagging and plans for linking with the CMOT, making it particularly well suited for research in computational psychiatry.

Demonstration of an Online Experiment

Here, we demonstrate an example of an experiment that could be conducted using the CBAT. We collected data from more than 1300 participants through an online experiment, which included four behavioral tasks. The behavioral tasks used and their English versions are available in the CBAT. As shown later, the validity of the obtained data was generally supported through comparisons with previous studies.

We note that the tasks used in the present demonstration do not represent a fixed set of components available in the CBAT. Although new tasks and questionnaires are currently uploaded by the authors, users can freely modify the code for their own use.

Methods

Participants

Participants were recruited through a crowdsourcing service of CrowdWorks, Inc., Japan. A total of 1,397 participants completed all the tasks and questionnaires. No personally identifying information (name or address) was collected. Two dummy questions were included in the questionnaires to confirm whether participants carefully read the sentences, and 72 participants who failed to answer at least one of them were excluded. An additional 16 participants were excluded because of technical issues. Consequently, data from 1,309 participants (768 females, 538 males, and 3 not reported; mean age = 41.03 years, range = 18–85) were analyzed. All participants provided their informed consent online.

Participants received monetary compensation of 1001 Japanese yen via the CrowdWorks platform after completing the study. This study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the National Center of Neurology and Psychiatry (A2022‐062).

Behavioral tasks

Figure 2 provides an overview of the four behavioral tasks used in this study, including the number of trials and the total duration of the experiment. These times are provided just as a reference, as participants were allowed to take breaks at any time. Two tasks are related to the cognitive systems domain of the RDoC: the attention network test (ANT) and the beads task (BT). In that domain, ANT is closely related to the attention construct, and BT includes the cognitive control construct. The other two tasks are related to the positive valence systems domain: the probabilistic reward task (PRT) and the delay and probability discounting task (DPDT). PRT is mostly related to the reward learning construct, and DPDT is mostly related to the reward valuation construct. See Methods S1 for details on these tasks.

Demographic characteristics and self‐report questionnaires

We collected demographic information, including age, sex, level of education (middle school graduate, high school graduate, college graduate, postgraduate), occupation (company employee, public servant, self‐employed, company executive, freelancer, full‐time homemaker, student, part‐time/temporary worker, unemployed, others), height, weight, and history of mental illness (yes/no, if yes, choose from schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, depression, anxiety disorder including panic disorder, obsessive‐compulsive disorder, developmental disorders including autism spectrum disorder, ADHD, and pervasive developmental disorders, and other/unknown). All the participants were also asked to complete 11 self‐report questionnaires. Most of them were psychiatric symptom rating scales such as measures of depression and anxiety.

The questionnaires were administered on participants' personal computer screens during the online study. No downloadable copies were provided, and item texts were not included in the article or Supporting information. Furthermore, questionnaire‐based analyses, including factor analysis, were outside the scope of this article and are therefore not presented. In addition, none of these questionnaires are currently included on the CBAT website.

Analysis

We conducted basic analyses to confirm expected task effects, using task‐specific metrics such as reaction times (ANT, PRT), choice accuracy (ANT, BT), number of draws (BT), and indifference points (DPDT). Details are provided in Methods S2.

Experimental procedures

The participants accessed the experiments via a web link, and after providing informed consent, they completed behavioral tasks and questionnaires through their web browser. Additional details are provided in Methods S3.

Results

The data included a total of 1,309 participants. The prevalence of self‐reported lifetime history of any psychiatric disorder was 18.5%, which is slightly lower but comparable to that reported in interview‐based epidemiological studies in Japan (22%), 30 while the potential lifetime prevalence may be higher. 31 Table S1 shows a summary of the demographic information.

Results of behavioral tasks

The results of each task are shown in Figure 3. In the following sections, to confirm the validity of the behavioral tasks, we calculated the main indices of the four tasks and compared them with the previous findings.

Attention Network Test (ANT)

Response times (RTs) from correct trials were used to calculate indices for the three types of attentional networks: alerting effect (effortful processing), orienting effect (response to orienting cues), and executive effect (response to the interference of competing cues) (Fig. 3a). Data from one, two, and two participants were excluded from the analyses because the corresponding indices could not be computed due to a lack of correct trials.

The mean alerting effect, obtained by subtracting the mean RT for the double‐cue condition from the mean RT for the no‐cue condition, was 51.5 ms (SE = 0.7). The mean orienting effect, obtained by subtracting the mean RT of the spatial cue from the mean RT of the center cue, was 14.2 ms (SE = 0.5). The executive effect, obtained by subtracting the mean RT of congruent conditions from the mean RT of incongruent conditions, was 70.5 ms (SE = 0.9). Each of the three effects significantly differed from zero on the basis of a one‐tailed paired t test (Ps <0.001). In the study by Fan et al., 32 the independence of the networks was supported. However, we found very weak but significant correlations among them using Pearson's correlation tests (all Ps <0.05). It is possible that the significant correlation we observed is due to the large sample size allowing a small effect to be detected. For example, Fan et al. 32 reported a nonsignificant correlation between orienting and executive effects using data from 40 participants, whereas we found a significant correlation with a much larger sample, even though its correlation coefficient in our study was smaller (r = −0.07) than their finding (r = −0.12).

Beads Task (BT)

In this task, 14 participants (1%) did not make decisions after 60 draws. The following analyses were conducted on the remaining participants. The average number of draws taken before a decision was made was 5.79 (SD = 6.82). This value is not substantially different from that reported in the original task by Garety et al., 33 where healthy controls made an average of 5.38 draws (SD = 3.15), especially considering that the maximum number of draws allowed in the current task was three times greater than that in the original task. In our data, one draw was most frequent and three draws second, whereas Garety et al. 33 reported that three draws was most frequent and one draw second. Furthermore, participants who chose the incorrect jar (72 participants; 6% of the total) stopped drawing earlier than those who chose the correct jar did (1,223 participants; 94%), with mean numbers of draws of 2.9 and 6.0, respectively. This reflects a tendency toward ‘jumping to conclusions’ in erroneous decisions.

Next, we calculated the normative Bayesian estimate of the belief for a particular jar, which was the same for all participants, as the sequence of majority‐ and minority‐colored beads was identical across participants. It was 85% and 97% after the first and second draws, respectively, under the assumption of a 50:50 prior. On the other hand, the cumulative percentage of participants who stopped after their first draw was 22%, and that of participants who stopped after their second draw was 34% (Fig. 3b). These results suggest that participants tended to be more conservative than would be expected under a normative Bayesian model, which is consistent with previous findings. 33 , 34 , 35

Probabilistic Reward Task (PRT)

A total of 30% of the trials were excluded by the exclusion criteria (trials less than 150 ms or more than 2500 ms; for each subject, trials with RT (after natural log transformation) outside the 3SD). The data from all the participants were subsequently utilized for the analyses. We calculated four indices following the original study: accuracy, RT, response bias, and discriminative ability (Fig. 3c). The calculation of response bias and discriminability followed Wooldridge et al. 36

For accuracy and RT, two‐way analyses of variance (ANOVAs) with condition (rich or lean) and block (1–3) as factors were conducted. For both indicators, we found significant main effects of condition and block, with no interaction effect between them (Ps < 0.001). Participants responded quickly and accurately in the rich condition compared with the lean condition.

For response bias and discriminability, we first confirmed that both indices were significantly greater than zero using one‐tailed t tests (P = 0.01 and P < 0.01, respectively). Next, one‐way ANOVAs were conducted with block (1–3) as a within‐subjects factor. The analysis revealed no significant effect of block on response bias, unlike the results of Pizzagalli et al. 37 In contrast, a significant main effect of block was found for discriminability. Interestingly, post hoc tests of the main effects of block revealed a similar pattern to that of accuracy (block 1 > block 2, block 1 > block 3, block 2 > block 3). This pattern was not observed in Pizzagalli et al. 37 and may reflect a loss of concentration among the participants in the latter blocks in our online experiment, which included many tasks.

Delay and Probability Discounting Task (DPDT)

With respect to delay discounting, indifference points could be estimated from the data of 93% of the participants (86 participants had at least one missing point). With respect to probability discounting, indifference points could be estimated from the data of 91% of the participants (115 participants had at least one missing point). Notably, a coding error in this task was identified after data collection (briefly, the indifference point tended to converge at 950 due to an algorithmic error). However, given that the collected data still allow for meaningful interpretation of delay and probability discounting tendencies, the results are reported below.

Figure 3d illustrates the discounting curves under both the delay and probability conditions. The black dots denote the median indifference points. The red curve and blue curve show hyperbolic and exponential discounting models fitted to the data, respectively. With respect to the probability condition, consistent with the original study, 38 the hyperbolic function provided a slightly better fit than the exponential function did (r2 = 0.992 and 0.954, respectively). On the other hand, regarding the delay condition, the hyperbolic function provided a slightly worse fit than the exponential function did, although the difference is again very small (r2 = 0.893 and 0.907, respectively) and looks neglectable visually.

Next, we fitted a hyperbolic discounting model to each participant's data separately for the delay and probability discounting conditions. For the subset of participants for whom the parameters were estimated and were positive in both conditions (n = 904), we calculated the Pearson correlation between the parameters reflecting the steepness of discounting (after natural log transformation). The results revealed a significant positive correlation, r = 0.36 (P < 0.001), which is consistent with the findings of Richards et al., 38 although the coefficient was much weaker than that previously reported (r = 0.75).

Discussion

We developed a free experimental toolbox, the CBAT, to facilitate the implementation of behavioral experiments that combine various questionnaires and behavioral tasks without the need for any coding skills. In particular, it can meet the demands of current computational psychiatry. In addition, we collected large‐scale online data using various tasks that are posted on the CBAT. Key findings from previous studies were replicated, confirming that the CBAT works as we intended.

Rationale behind the development of the CBAT

The CBAT was developed to simplify the process of conducting behavioral experiments, particularly in the field of mental health, which has recently been shifting toward a dimensional approach. By supporting large‐scale online data collection, the CBAT will foster more efficient and accessible research. Notably, the CBAT is the first such battery available in Japanese, helping to expand behavioral and psychiatric research and contributing to cross‐cultural psychiatry. In addition, the CBAT was designed to be used in combination with platforms developed by the authors, such as CPSYMAP, with the aim of facilitating research in computational psychiatry.

Demonstration‐based validation of the CBAT

Our demonstration of an online experiment showed the validity of the behavioral tasks that are now included in the CBAT.

The results of the behavioral tasks largely replicated basic trends from previous laboratory studies, supporting the validity of the tasks used. For example, in the ANT, we found positive effects of all three attention networks – alerting, orienting, and executive control – introduced by Fan et al. 32 With respect to BT, most participants drew much more than expected on the basis of Bayesian norms, which is consistent with prior studies. 35 , 39 With respect to the DPDT, we were able to estimate the delay and probability discounting curves (showing greater discounting with longer delays and lower probabilities). With respect to the PRT, the accuracy and RT indices showed condition‐based differences as intended, and the existence of response bias and discriminability were confirmed, although the effect of block differed from Richards et al. 38

Notably, we observed effects that had not been reported in prior studies. In the ANT, for example, there were significant correlations among the three attentional networks that were not reported in the original study by Fan et al. 32 ; however, these correlations have been partially reported in a subsequent study using integrated datasets. 40 One likely explanation is the large sample size in our online study, which leads to greater statistical power to detect even small effects. In the DPDT, the delay discounting curve appeared to be shallower than that reported in a previous study. 38 These differences from previous findings may reflect variations in participant populations, including cultural or demographic factors. Recruiting large and diverse samples can help resolve inconsistencies across studies and identify key factors underlying observed differences. This is one of the key purposes for which the CBAT was developed. Moreover, publicly sharing such large datasets enables secondary analyses from diverse perspectives.

The current demonstration also highlights several methodological considerations specific to conducting behavioral experiments online. First, controlling visual stimuli is challenging in online experiments. In the current experiment, ANT and the PRT use response time to and discrimination of visual stimuli, respectively, as key measures. Therefore, the position or size of the stimulus on the retina can play a critical role. They were not fully controlled in the present study, and they may pose limitations for replicating laboratory results. As a solution, future implementations could include adjustments to standardize the visual experience across participants. Instructions are also crucial. For example, 1% of the participants in the BT continued sampling beads more than 60 times and failed to make a final decision, suggesting that they may not have understood the task instructions. Effective solutions include incorporating comprehension checks or gamification elements to help participants naturally understand the rules. Such approaches also improve the reliability of task measures 41 and help ensure the collection of high‐quality online data.

Challenges of the CBAT framework and its contribution to computational psychiatry

The CBAT framework has some limitations, and part of them overlap with issues discussed for a similar toolbox, the Experiment Factory. 28 First, since the code used in the CBAT is built on jsPsych or lab.js, it needs to remain compatible with updates to these libraries. Another issue concerns the management framework for continued maintenance and the incentives to encourage contributions to the CBAT. At present, only the authors are uploading the CBAT components. However, if the platform becomes more open and allows broader community contributions, it will be necessary to establish a management framework for quality assurance, licensing, and administrative oversight.

In addition, although it is not a limitation of the CBAT itself, owing to copyright restrictions, many existing questionnaires cannot be publicly shared at present. This limitation highlights the need to develop open‐source questionnaires and promote open access to established assessments. To overcome this challenge, initiatives such as the HiTOP project have begun to develop openly accessible, transdiagnostic instruments, such as the HiTOP self‐report measures (HiTOP‐SR). 42 We are participating in the HiTOP Japan project (Japanese HiTOP‐PRO), and we anticipate that, in the near future, many commonly used questionnaires will be complemented by HiTOP‐based and other openly available, transdiagnostic measures, thereby enabling more open, reproducible, and transparent research.

From the perspective of computational psychiatry, the CBAT provides an important foundation for future data‐driven and theory‐driven research. When used together with platforms such as CPSYMAP, the CBAT enables researchers to position behavioral tasks and experimental findings within the broader landscape of computational psychiatry. In addition, the planned connection with the forthcoming CMOT will allow direct links between experimental paradigms and computational models, including reinforcement learning and Bayesian inference. Furthermore, for studies requiring longitudinal tracking, we have developed a complementary platform, CEMA (https://cpsycolloq.github.io/CEMA-en/). At present, only the Japanese version is available upon request. This platform supports smartphone‐based experiments and longitudinal follow‐up assessments, incorporating CBAT library tasks as assessment components. This longitudinal extension is particularly important for computational psychiatry, as it enables the investigation of within‐individual dynamics and symptom fluctuations over time, as well as the computational factors underlying these fluctuations. Together, these developments position the CBAT as a flexible and extensible infrastructure for future computational psychiatry research.

Conclusion

We developed and introduced the CBAT, a toolbox designed to facilitate the conduct of experiments with behavioral tasks and questionnaires. We validated and showed the potential of the CBAT by collecting large‐scale data and replicating key findings from prior studies. The CBAT promotes accessible and reproducible research in computational psychiatry by lowering technical barriers and supporting RDoC‐based task organization. The CBAT's design reflects our belief in promoting scientific development through a shift from competition to collaboration. Future integrations with CPSYMAP and the CMOT will further enhance the CBAT's utility for investigating transdiagnostic symptom dimensions and neurobehavioral functions.
