Mediating Factors and Well-Being Differences by Gender Among Academic Physicians
Authors: Miriam T. Stewart, Mihriye Mete, Mariah Quinn, Chantal Brazeau, Kristine Olson, Susannah G. Rowe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This survey study compares factors that mediate associations of gender with occupational well-being among male and female physicians,

Introduction

Occupational distress is highly prevalent among physicians and its harms to physicians, patients, and health care organizations have been well-documented.1,2,3 Given these harms, clinician well-being has been conceptualized as one of the aims in the quintuple aim of the health care system,4 alongside improving population health, enhancing the patient experience of care, reducing health care costs, and promoting health equity. Physician turnover and attrition from the field of medicine threatens to exacerbate existing and projected shortages in both primary care and specialty care physicians,5 reducing patient access to care,6 generating high costs for health care organizations,7,8 and increasing health care expenditures for the US public.9 Given the significant harms associated with physician occupational distress, there is increasing investment in interventions to reduce occupational distress10 and indeed there is early evidence that interventions may have some success.11,12

Interventions to improve occupational well-being are most effective when they are informed by an understanding of the range of factors that influence well-being, particularly among the highest risk cohorts. Female physicians have been found to have higher occupational distress than physicians who are male.13,14,15 These differences begin in training16,17 and continue across the career span.18 Given the high prevalence of occupational distress in female physicians, a more nuanced exploration is needed that accounts for systemic contributors rather than solely individual factors. Attempts to understand the difference in occupational well-being suggest that factors associated with occupational well-being vary by gender.19 In a study of physicians at a single academic medical center, Rotenstein20 et al found that after controlling for demographic factors and academic rank, multiple workplace culture factors as well as self-compassion attenuated the association of gender with burnout. In a study of general surgeons, the difference in burnout was fully mediated by differences in professional fulfillment and control over schedule.21 Female physicians are also more likely than male physicians to report experiencing mistreatment, which is associated with higher burnout rates.22,23 However, prior studies have been limited in their scope—only assessing a single institution, a single specialty, or a single factor associated with burnout. A broader understanding of the factors associated with burnout for female physicians is needed to inform the development of interventions targeting these factors, with the goal of reducing the negative downstream consequences of occupational distress for female physicians, their patients, and health care organizations.

Our study examines key factors associated with occupational well-being for female physicians by identifying the factors that mediate the differences between male and female physicians in a large, multicenter, multispecialty cohort of physicians, using a tripartite model of occupational well-being to assess the relative contribution of factors in 3 domains: workplace culture, ease of daily work, and individual factors such as personal resilience.24 While prior work has examined gender differences in specific contexts or single institutions, to our knowledge, this is the first study to employ a large, multicenter dataset with a mediation model to examine how factors associated with occupational well-being differ by gender across multiple domains.

Methods

This survey study was determined to be exempt by the Stanford institutional review board based on use of deidentified, administratively collected data pooled from across sites. This study adhered to reporting guidelines for cross-sectional studies as described in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline.25

Between October 2019 and July 2021, 15 organizations participating in the Healthcare Professional Well-Being Academic Consortium (Healthcare PWAC, previously known as the Physician Well-Being Academic Consortium) collected cross-sectional survey data on occupational well-being from attending physicians (including health care professionals without medical doctorates or doctorates of osteopathic medicine but who bill independently, such as dentists and doctorate-level psychologists). All participating organizations collected the same self-reported demographics and validated measures of professional fulfillment, burnout, and factors associated with occupational well-being, as previously described.26 Each organization was also able to include additional measures and questions. Participation was voluntary and respondents were invited to answer or skip questions at their own discretion; clicking start on the survey was considered informed consent. An independent survey vendor administered the survey, pooled data, and performed deidentification to remove potentially identifying information. The deidentification protocol prioritized retention of data as follows: medical practice specialty, gender, race, ethnicity, and age category. When combined demographic information yielded a group of fewer than 5 respondents, these data were removed. A deidentified and cleaned dataset was provided to the study team by Healthcare PWAC.

Measures

Demographics

Participants self-reported information on race, ethnicity, gender, age, medical specialty, and hours worked per week. Race was categorized as Asian, White, other (American Indian or Alaska Native, Black or African American, Middle Eastern or North African, Native Hawaiian or Other Pacific Islander, or any race not otherwise specified), or missing. Ethnicity categories included Hispanic, non-Hispanic, and missing or deidentified. Race and ethnicity were included as questions in the survey to allow for analyses focused on the impact of race and ethnicity on the experience of physicians.

Professional Fulfillment and Burnout

Burnout and professional fulfillment were measured using the Professional Fulfillment Index, which includes 16 items, each scored on a 5-point Likert scale and reported as an overall score from 0 (lowest) to 10 (highest). This index has been described elsewhere.27

Measures Assessing Workplace Experience

Organizations used a wide range of measures assessing workplace experiences that have been associated with occupational well-being. For this analysis, we selected the 5 measures that were utilized by the greatest number of participating institutions. These included (1) supportive leadership behaviors,28 a 9-item measure assessing the degree to which the respondent’s direct supervisor engages in supportive leadership behaviors; (2) personal-organizational values alignment,29 a 3-item measure assessing input into decision-making and alignment between individual and organizational goals; (3) control over schedule, a 5-item measure assessing the respondent’s sense of control over aspects of their schedule; (4) electronic health record (EHR) helpfulness, a 4-item measure assessing ease of using the EHR; and (5) self-valuation,30 a 4-item measure assessing self-care and self-compassion. These measures are all scored on a 5-point Likert scale and reported as an overall score from 0 (lowest) to 10 (highest).

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed from April to September 2023. Data were summarized using descriptive statistics such as mean (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. Published cut points were used to dichotomize burnout (burnout positive if scored >3.325; range, 0-10) and professional fulfillment (professional fulfillment positive if scored >7.5; range, 0-10) to determine percentage of respondents scoring positive for burnout and professional fulfillment. Participant characteristics and scores on measures of workplace experience were compared between female and male physicians using 2-sample t tests and χ2 tests as appropriate. Effect sizes (Cohen d or Cohen h) were reported when relevant. A P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

A mediation framework was chosen to explore workplace experiences which may explain the differences between male and female physicians in professional fulfillment and burnout. We based this mediation model on the Stanford University framework for occupational well-being, which is comprised of 3 domains: culture of wellness, workplace efficiency, and individual factors.24 Within each domain, we included dimensions of workplace experience that are known to be associated with occupational well-being. These dimensions are leadership support, values alignment, and control over schedule in the domain of workplace culture; EHR helpfulness in the domain of workplace efficiency; and self-valuation in the domain of individual factors. Mediation analyses were conducted using the structural equation modeling procedures in Stata version 16 (StataCorp) and were run separately for burnout and professional fulfillment, measured as continuous variables. To minimize the deletion of missing observations from all equations of the structural model, maximum likelihood with missing values option in Stata was used as the estimation method for structural equation modeling. This method assumes joint normality and missingness at random and does not perform listwise deletion.31 Each model was adjusted for work hours (entered as an ordered categorical measure [1-4] based on 4 categories of weekly work: <46 hours, 46-54 hours, 55-59 hours, and ≥60 hours). R2, comparative fit index (ideal if 10 >0.95) and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA; ideal if ≤0.05) were used to assess model fit.

Results

The survey was administered to 37 511 physicians and 19 088 completed the survey (response rate of 50.8%). A total of 16 731 respondents self-reported their gender as either female (8197 respondents [43%]) or male (8534 respondents [45%]) and were included in this analysis. The remaining respondents (2357 respondents [12%]) did not provide gender information or reported an identity other than male or female. Respondents’ ages ranged from younger than 29 to older than 60 years, with 40 to 49 years being the most common age bracket for both female (2788 participants [34%]) and male (2448 participants [29%]). Discussion

In this large, multicenter, multispecialty survey study of physicians, female physicians experienced high burnout and low professional fulfillment, consistent with multiple prior studies. Female physicians had lower scores than male physicians across 4 dimensions of workplace experience (leadership support, values alignment, control over schedule, and self-valuation). Female physicians had a small but statistically significant higher mean score than male physicians on EHR helpfulness. However, the difference was so small that it is unlikely to be meaningful despite being statistically significant and may be a statistical artifact of the large sample size.

In seeking to better understand the high rates of occupational distress in female physicians, we developed a mediation model to identify key contributors to the differences in workplace experience between female and male physicians. In this mediation model, the difference in burnout between female and male physicians became nonsignificant, suggesting that this difference in burnout is not related to intrinsic qualities of gender, but rather is shaped by differences in experience. In this mediation model, self-valuation accounted for the majority of the gender-related difference in burnout. The difference in professional fulfillment between female and male physicians remained significant in a second mediation model including these same 5 dimensions of workplace experience, suggesting that there are factors not included in our model which contribute to the differences in professional fulfillment.

The self-valuation measure assesses self-care behaviors (2 questions) and attitudes of self-compassion (2 questions). An association of self-valuation with burnout has already been demonstrated, as has a difference in self-valuation between female and male physicians.30 However, ours is the first study, to our knowledge, to demonstrate that the difference in self-valuation is the largest mediator of the gender-related difference in burnout when modeled with other factors known to be associated with burnout.

While experienced at the level of the individual, self-valuation is shaped by systemic and cultural forces that constrain self-care and self-compassion. For this reason, it is not surprising that female physicians are more impacted by low self-valuation because they experience different constraints in the both the home and work environment. At work, female physicians receive 24% more patient messages in the EHR,32 likely due to being perceived as more accessible. Communication patterns also vary by gender. Patients speak more, disclose more psychosocial as well as biomedical information, and speak more assertively to female physicians, and also interrupt female physicians more often.33,34 Female physicians are expected to33,35 and do spend a longer amount of time with patients.32,33,35,36,37,38,39,40 While this relational approach to care likely contributes to higher patient satisfaction37,38,39,40 and better outcomes,41,42,43 as has been demonstrated in multiple studies, it also reduces the amount of time available to female physicians for self-care activities. At home, female physicians do a disproportionate amount of household labor,44 amounting to as much as 100 minutes more per day compared with male physicians45—a total of 11 extra hours of work per week. Female physicians also bear a greater fraction of the mental load.46,47 These disparities were exacerbated during the pandemic; in one study of physicians, physician mothers were 30 times more likely than physician fathers to have taken on additional caregiving responsibilities.48

Organizations can promote self-valuation and reduce gender-related differences by establishing job-related structures and resources that mitigate the high professional and personal cost of family caregiving for all physicians. In work settings where family caregiving is better supported for all genders, evidence suggests that work-family conflict is reduced and family caregiving responsibilities are more evenly shared.49,50,51 Strategies include flexible work models, access to childcare, and equitable leave policies that support caregivers to continue being professionally productive while attending to family responsibilities.49,50,51

The second aspect of the self-valuation measure focuses on self-compassion, which is commonly understood to be an internal set of attitudes about oneself in relation to mistakes and failures. In this domain, as in the domain of self-care, female physicians’ workplace experiences likely influence their lower self-compassion scores. Despite having similar or better outcomes,40,41,52 multiple studies have found that female physicians receive more negative feedback53,54,55,56 and worse online patient reviews.57,58,59 Female surgeons receive fewer referrals,60,61 female physicians are less likely to be promoted into leadership roles,62,63 and female physicians are less likely to receive awards from national medical associations.64,65 Female physicians are more likely to be mistaken for other health care team members66,67 and are more likely to experience harassment.23,68,69,70 Patients are more likely to refuse to be cared for by a female physician.23 In addition, female physicians experience more negative professional consequences for errors. In a study exploring the association of surgical outcomes with primary care physician referrals to surgeons, primary care physicians were found to reduce their referrals to female surgeons after a surgical complication at a higher rate than for male surgeons. Primary care physicians were also less likely to refer a patient to any female surgeon after a surgical complication by one female surgeon, a phenomenon not observed to be the case for male surgeons.71 In an environment in which female physicians receive more negative feedback for similar or better outcomes than physicians who are men, lower self-compassion scores may reflect an accurate understanding that mistakes have higher consequences for female physicians.

Self-valuation was also a significant mediator of the gender-related difference in professional fulfillment, along with organizational-personal values alignment. However, in this mediation model, the difference in professional fulfillment remained significant, suggesting that there are factors mediating the difference between female and male physicians that are not captured in the model. Further research is needed to identify additional domains that impact the difference in professional fulfillment between female and male physicians. Further research could focus on areas where gender differences have been identified, including perceived appreciation,20 compensation,72,73 mentorship,74,75 and professional advancement opportunities.62

Limitations

This study has limitations. The response rate of 50.8% represents a limitation because it introduces nonresponse bias. Responders may differ from nonresponders in ways that we cannot measure, which could influence the validity of the conclusions. A proportion of respondents did not disclose their gender (12.3%), which could have resulted in selection bias leading to either overestimation or underestimation of the impact of gender on these markers. Due to the deidentification protocol for this dataset, we were not able to obtain data on respondents who reported an identity other than male or female given their relatively small numbers. Data on the experience of this population has been published elsewhere.76 We did not have data on individual respondents’ race and ethnicity, precluding an intersectional analysis to better understand the interplay between gender and other axes of identity. The absence of intersectional data is a major limitation. Future work should examine how facts of identity intersect with occupational well-being, as these factors likely amplify disparities. We also did not have data on every dimension of workplace experience associated with occupational well-being for physicians, and so there are likely factors impacting the experience of female physicians that were not included in our model.

Conclusions

In this multicenter survey study of physicians from US academic medical institutions, female physicians experienced higher burnout and lower professional fulfillment compared with male physicians. The difference in burnout between female and male physicians became nonsignificant when accounting for the indirect effects of leadership support, values alignment, control over schedule, self-valuation, and satisfaction with the EHR, while the difference in professional fulfillment persisted. Self-valuation was the most significant mediator of the gender-related difference in burnout. Interventions to improve occupational well-being for physicians should prioritize reducing systemic barriers to self-care and self-compassion, rather than misplacing the burden on individuals. Further research is needed to identify factors that influence professional fulfillment for female physicians.
