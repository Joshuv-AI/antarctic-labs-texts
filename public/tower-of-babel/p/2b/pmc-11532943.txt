TAVI complication: Prosthetic valve leaflet dislodgment after post-dilatation
Authors: Haitham Abu Khadija, Mohammad Alnees, Gera Gandelman, Jacob George, Alex Blatt
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Background and significance

Transcatheter aortic valve Implantation (TAVI) is a minimally invasive procedure that has emerged as a valuable alternative to surgical aortic valve replacement in high-risk patients with symptomatic severe aortic stenosis [1,2]. One of the major predictors of poor prognosis following TAVR is the presence of significant paravalvular regurgitation (PVR) [3].

Rationale for procedure optimization

To mitigate the risk of significant PVR and improve clinical outcomes, additional procedural maneuvers such as balloon post-dilatation may be necessary during the index TAVI procedure [4,5]. These supplementary techniques aim to optimize valve positioning and minimize residual regurgitation.

Objective of the case report

This case report describes a rare but serious complication that can occur following TAVI with post-dilatation - acute free aortic regurgitation due to prosthetic leaflet dislodgment. Early recognition and prompt intervention are crucial to manage this life-threatening event and achieve the best possible patient outcomes.

Case presentation

Patient information

A 77-year-old active and lucid female patient was referred to our tertiary care center due to severe, symptomatic aortic stenosis complicated by heart failure symptoms. The decided treatment strategy was to perform transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI).

Clinical findings

The patient presented with exertional dyspnea, fatigue, and decreased exercise tolerance, consistent with New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class III heart failure symptoms. Physical examination revealed a systolic ejection murmur best heard at the right upper sternal border.

Diagnostic assessment

The pre-procedural workup for the TAVI procedure included transthoracic echocardiography (TTE), cardiac computed tomography (CT), CT angiography, and coronary angiography. TTE confirmed severe aortic stenosis with an aortic valve area of 0.7 cm2 and a mean transaortic gradient of 45 mmHg. Cardiac CT demonstrated extensive calcification of the aortic valve leaflets. Based on the findings, an ACURATE neo™ medium size M SV 25 mm aortic bioprosthesis (Boston Scientific) was selected, as the patient's aortic annulus area measured 457.3 mm2.

Interventional management

The TAVI procedure was performed under local anesthesia combined with conscious sedation. As per the device's protocol, an aortic balloon pre-dilatation was carried out using a VACS® Balloon 22.0 mm/40 mm (Osypka). The self-expandable valve was then deployed under gentle pacing. To address the residual PVR, post-dilatation was performed using a VACS® Balloon 25.0 mm/40 mm (Osypka) in an attempt to change the directionality of the regurgitant jet (more transversal across the aortic root). However, this did not result in a reduction in PVR severity.

Complications

Aiming to further reduce the PVR, a more aggressive post-dilatation was performed using a VACS® Balloon 26.0 mm/40 mm (Osypka). Immediately after this maneuver, the patient became hypotensive with a very low diastolic pressure of less than 20 mmHg, and severe valvular aortic regurgitation was observed on TTE. Outcomes and follow-up

To address this hemodynamically life-threatening situation, a bailout valve-in-valve procedure was performed, successfully implanting a CoreValve Evolute Pro 29 mm (Medtronic™) prosthesis. This completely resolved the free aortic insufficiency and the residual PVR (Fig. 4), as confirmed by subsequent TEE. The patient's hemodynamics stabilized, and she required a permanent pacemaker implantation two days later due to complete atrioventricular block. Discussion

The moderate paravalvular leakage (PVL) observed in this case was likely a consequence of the unsealing of the underlying heavily calcified aortic valve leaflets. Post-dilatation using an appropriately sized balloon or an oversized balloon is a common maneuver to reduce PVL after transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI). However, this additional procedure carries the potential for serious complications.

In the present case, transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) clearly identified the morphological loss of function of one of the prosthetic aortic valve leaflets, which was likely provoked by the second, more aggressive post-dilatation. The repeated ballooning with increased volume may have directly injured the leaflet, leading to its dislodgment.

Acute severe aortic regurgitation after TAVI can have various etiologies, including prosthetic valve malfunction, a stuck valve, or crashed or dislodged leaflets [6,7]. Dislodgment of leaflets post-TAVI is a rare but critical complication that should be considered in the differential diagnosis of moderate or higher degrees of PVL after valve implantation. Additionally, conditions related to clotting events, such as Behçet disease, may contribute to leaflet dislodgment, complicating the clinical scenario further. Recognizing these potential causes is essential for effective management and treatment after TAVI [8,9].

Another important consideration is the potential role of pre-implantation calcium fragmentation using intravascular lithotripsy (Shockwave Medical Inc.) to prevent significant PVL [10]. This technique may help modify the calcified aortic valve and facilitate a better sealing of the prosthetic valve, thereby reducing the risk of residual PVL.

Our case highlights the complexities and challenges of managing severe aortic insufficiency and its subsequent complications following transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI). The decision to perform a bailout valve-in-valve procedure highlights the need for timely intervention in cases where traditional valve repair methods (the post-dilatation management) may fail or when patients present with significant hemodynamic instability.

The successful implantation of the CoreValve Evolute Pro 29 mm prosthesis not only addressed the immediate concern of free aortic insufficiency but also enhanced the patient's overall hemodynamic condition. This is consistent with current literature that endorses the effectiveness of valve-in-valve procedures in patients with previous valve interventions that have led to complications. Research indicates that these procedures can result in substantial enhancements in both functional status and quality of life [11,12].

However, the need for a permanent pacemaker two days post-procedure underscores the potential for new conduction abnormalities following valve replacement, particularly in patients with pre-existing cardiac conditions or those undergoing complex interventions. The incidence of complete atrioventricular block in such scenarios is a known risk, necessitating close monitoring and follow-up [13].

The patient's discharge four days after the TAVI procedure reflects advances in postoperative care and the increasing safety of transcatheter interventions.

Conclusion

In this extreme clinical case, we successfully managed a rare yet potentially life-threatening complication that occurred during a transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) procedure. Through our analysis, we identified the possible underlying causes, which included the second, more aggressive post-dilatation maneuver as well as the role of the heavily calcified aortic valve substrate. Additionally, we considered the potential value of implementing novel technologies, such as intravascular lithotripsy (Shockwave Medical Inc.), to help modify the calcified aortic valve and potentially reduce the risk of significant paravalvular leakage during TAVI procedures.

This case highlights the importance of vigilant monitoring and prompt recognition of acute complications following TAVI, as well as the need to carefully assess the potential benefits and risks of adjunctive interventions to optimize procedural outcomes. Continued research and innovation in TAVI techniques and technology may help mitigate rare but serious complications in the future.
