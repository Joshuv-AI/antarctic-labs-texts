The association between triglyceride glucose–body mass index and all–cause mortality in critically ill patients undergoing cardiac surgery: a retrospective analysis from the MIMIC–IV database
Authors: Yuanfang Jia, Junwei Zhang, Lei Zhang, Aijie Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSeveral studies have indicated that several million cardiac surgeries are performed worldwide each year. Recognized for reflecting insulin resistance, the triglyceride glucose-body mass index (TyG-BMI) index has been demonstrated a strong association with the incidence of cardiovascular events. Although the TyG-BMI index has been extensively investigated in the context of metabolic disorders, its prognostic value in patients undergoing cardiac surgery remains insufficiently defined. The study investigated whether TyG-BMI at Intensive Care Unit (ICU) admission predicts all-cause mortality in critically ill post–cardiac surgery patients.MethodPatient records of individuals who had undergone cardiac surgery were obtained from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV) database, a comprehensive source of critical care data. Based on their TyG-BMI values, patients were stratified into four distinct categories. This study focused on all-cause mortality at 30 and 365 days as the main outcomes, while 90- and 180-day mortality were treated as secondary measures. To assess differences in clinical outcomes, subjects were grouped into TyG-BMI quartiles, and corresponding Kaplan–Meier(KM) curves were constructed. Additionally, the relationship between the TyG-BMI index and the study outcomes was assessed using Cox proportional-hazards regression and restricted cubic splines (RCS).ResultsThe study cohort comprised 2,624 critically ill patients following cardiac surgery, with a mean age of 66.54 ± 11.28 years; males accounted for 73.17% (n = 1,920). Stratification of patients into TyG-BMI quartiles revealed marked differences in all-cause mortality at 30-, 90-, 180-, and 365-day follow-up intervals. Interestingly, as the TyG-BMI index increased, survival rates at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days did not follow a consistently upward trend, but rather showed an initial improvement followed by a gradual decline. However, following the decline, the survival rate was similar to that observed in patients exhibiting the lowest range of TyG-BMI. A nonlinear U-shaped relationship was observed linking TyG-BMI levels with mortality over the 30- to 365-day period, as shown by spline-based modeling. The inflection points for these relationships were 235.7, 238.25, 238.2, and 238.25, respectively. The results from the Cox regression analysis matched up with what we found using the RCS analysis.ConclusionIn critically ill patients following cardiac surgery, the TyG-BMI index exhibited a U-shaped association with all-cause mortality at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days. The TyG-BMI index could be a useful tool for evaluating and managing patients in the ICU following cardiac surgery.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05075-y.

Introduction

Cardiac surgery is a vital approach for treating serious cardiovascular diseases [1]. As reported by the American Heart Association, more than one million individuals receive cardiac surgeries each year [2]. Over the past few decades, advancements in surgical techniques and postoperative management have improved survival rates and prognosis for patients [3]. However, postoperative mortality remains a significant concern, influenced by factors such as comorbidities, surgical complexity, and postoperative management [4]. Traditional risk assessment models, including the European Cardiac surgery Risk Evaluation System (EuroSCORE), have demonstrated some value but also exhibit limitations [5]. The EuroSCORE has been a commonly utilized tool for forecasting mortality rates after cardiac surgery. However, its accuracy and dependability are diminishing as the field of cardiac surgery advances and changes continually [6]. Within patients undergoing degenerative bicuspid mitral valve repair, EuroSCORE II demonstrates poor discrimination, accuracy, and calibration scores, as it overestimates the 30-day mortality rate [4]. A novel risk factor that facilitates straightforward and cost-effective clinical application, along with enhanced methods for predicting and preventing postoperative mortality following cardiac surgery, should be identified and proposed.

Previous studies have demonstrated that insulin resistance (IR) is closely associated with cardiovascular diseases [7]. Insulin resistance (IR) represents a defining metabolic abnormality in conditions such as metabolic syndrome and diabetes [8]. Currently, the hyperinsulinemic-euglycemic clamp (HEC) is considered the gold standard method to assess IR and can be used across different groups of people [9]. However, although the Homeostasis Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR) is broadly utilized and fairly precise, it still requires specialized equipment and trained personnel, is time-consuming and costly, involves multiple blood draws, and is poorly tolerated by patients. As such, HOMA-IR remains limited to research settings and is not feasible for large-scale clinical application. Moreover, the method relies on exogenous insulin infusion, which may be affected by bioavailability issues [10]. In recent years, the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index has emerged as a simple and reliable surrogate marker of IR and has been validated in forecasting the outcomes of different diseases, encompassing conditions like heart failure, acute MI, atrial fibrillation, and sepsis [11]. Emerging data indicate that combining the TyG index with obesity-related parameters, including general (e.g., BMI) and central (e.g., waist-to-height ratio, waist circumference) adiposity measures, may enhance its utility in evaluating insulin resistance [12]. Research indicates that the TyG-BMI index might be more effective than HOMA-IR in assessing IR and has demonstrated a strong link with the prevalence of hypertension [13, 14]. Nevertheless, it is still uncertain if the TyG-BMI index holds comparable predictive significance for patients who are undergoing cardiac surgery.

The aim of this study is to investigate whether TyG-BMI levels measured at ICU admission are associated with all-cause mortality in cardiac surgery patients. The findings of this study might help uncover novel methods for early identification and improvement of outcomes in patients after cardiac surgery.

Methods

Source of data

This study is a retrospective analysis. The MIMIC-IV (v3.1) database, developed by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT), was used to identify patients undergoing major cardiac surgeries such as coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), valve replacement, and combined procedures, as recorded by the relevant International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes in the MIMIC-IV database. Between 2008 and 2022, the ICU of BIDMC (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center) treated over 90,000 adult patients. In compliance with ethical requirements, we completed the National Institutes of Health(NIH) training on human subject protection and obtained Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative(CITI) certification prior to data acquisition (certification number 66351605). Given that the database lacks protected information and the patients are anonymized, informed consent was not required.

Study design and participant

This study included patients who had their first ICU admission following cardiac surgery. A total of 9,836 patients who had cardiac surgery were categorized using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) and Tenth Revision (ICD-10) codes. Patients over 18 years old who underwent cardiac surgery, identified by International Classification of Disease (ICD) codes (Supplemental Data TableS1), were included from the MIMIC-IV Database (version 3.1). The following criteria were used for exclusion: (1) patients whose ICU stay was shorter than 24 h (2) patients with multiple ICU admissions, only the information from the first ICU admission for cardiac surgery was retrieved; (3) missing or implausible values in key variables, including triglycerides, body weight, and height (4) Individuals with confirmed malignant neoplastic diseases. A total of 2,624 patients were included and categorized into four groups according to TyG-BMI index quartiles (Fig. 1).

Data compilation

Data extraction was performed using Structured Query Language (SQL) with Navicat Premium (v17.1.7). Extensive data were compiled for each patient at the time of admission, including age, sex, body weight, height, body mass index (BMI), and smoking status, etc. Baseline comorbidities included hypertension, acute renal failure, cerebrovascular accident (CVA), chronic kidney disease (CKD), diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia (HLD), heart failure (HF), myocardial infarction (MI), and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Vital signs on admission comprised heart rate(HR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), oxygen saturation (SpO2). Medications of interest included aspirin, clopidogrel, warfarin, β-blockers, statins, dexmedetomidine, and agents targeting the renin–angiotensin system, such as angiotensin-converting enzyme(ACE) inhibitors and angiotensin II receptor blockers(ARBs). Laboratory parameters measured were low-density lipoprotein(LDL) cholesterol, serum triglyceride levels, total serum calcium, glucose, potassium, sodium, absolute lymphocyte counts, hemoglobin, platelet count, red cell distribution width (RDW), erythrocyte and leukocyte counts, alanine aminotransferase(ALT), aspartate aminotransferase(AST), creatinine, blood urea nitrogen(BUN), creatine kinase (CK), lactate dehydrogenase (LD) and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), cardiac troponin I, international normalized ratio (INR), and prothrombin time (PT). Illness severity was assessed using the Acute Physiology Score III(APS III), Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores. Length of stay (LOS) in both the ICU and the hospital was recorded. Outcomes included mortality at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days. Laboratory measurements were obtained during the patient’s initial ICU admission. Parameters were excluded from analysis if missing data exceeded 40%, in order to uphold data accuracy.

Outcomes

The MIMIC-IV database includes mortality outcomes at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days for patients undergoing cardiac surgery. The primary endpoints were all-cause mortality at 30 days and 1 year after surgery, whereas mortality at 90 and 180 days was considered secondary.

Method for calculating the TyG index

The TyG index was calculated as the following formula: ln [fasting glucose (mg/dL) × fasting triglycerides (mg/dL)/2] [15]. Body mass index (BMI) was defined as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m²). The TyG-BMI index was derived by multiplying the TyG index by BMI: TyG index × BMI [13].

Statistical analysis

Initially, the distribution of continuous variables was evaluated to determine normality. For variables with a normal distribution, comparisons were made using Student’s t-test or one-way ANOVA, with values reported as mean and standard deviation. For data not conforming to a normal distribution, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed, and values were expressed as medians accompanied by interquartile ranges. Comparisons of categorical variables were conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, and outcomes were expressed as numbers and their respective proportions.

For the analysis of primary and secondary outcome rates, Kaplan-Meier (K-M) survival analysis was performed based on stratification by the TyG-BMI index. Univariate Cox models were employed to analyze the relationship between the TyG-BMI index and mortality at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days. Multivariate Cox models were constructed using variables of clinical importance or those demonstrating a univariate association with the endpoints. Selection of variables for the final model took into account the number of events to maintain model stability. In Model 1, only the TyG-BMI index was included as a predictor. Model 2 incorporated additional adjustments for demographic variables (age, sex, race), vital signs (heart rate), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes), and laboratory parameters including prothrombin time (PT), platelet count, serum potassium, red and white blood cell counts, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and serum creatinine, as well as SOFA score. The reference group in both models consisted of individuals within the lowest TyG-BMI quartile. To investigate the association pattern between TyG-BMI levels and clinical outcomes, restricted cubic spline (RCS) models were constructed with TyG-BMI treated as a continuous variable. When evidence of nonlinearity emerged, a recursive algorithm was employed to identify inflection points in the association between TyG-BMI and all-cause mortality at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days. To explore potential effect modifiers, stratified analyses were carried out by age (< 75 vs. ≥75 years), sex, racial group (White vs. non-White), and comorbidities such as acute kidney injury and diabetes.

All statistical analyses were conducted using Decision Chain software and R software (version 4.4.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria) in the RStudio environment. Results were deemed statistically significant when the p-value was under 0.05.

Results

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics

Of the 9,836 cardiac surgery cases documented in the MIMIC-IV database, 2,624 patients meeting the inclusion criteria were selected for analysis. The study population had an average age of 66.54 ± 11.28 years, and the majority (73.17%) were male. Participants were categorized into quartiles based on their TyG-BMI values at admission, and baseline characteristics were compared across these groups (Table 2 for details). The TyG-BMI values were distributed as follows:199.89 (139.77–224.92), 242.31 (224.94–259.30), 277.84 (259.34–301.46), and 339.47 (301.49–414.83). Individuals in the lowest TyG-BMI quartile tended to be older, exhibited a higher incidence of myocardial infarction, and showed lower rates of diabetes, hypertension, acute kidney injury, chronic kidney disease, and cerebrovascular events. The usage of beta-blockers and aspirin was lower in this group. Levels of HR, SpO₂, glucose, hemoglobin, serum creatinine, BUN, serum potassium, WBC count, platelet count, and RBC count, HbA1c, triglycerides, length of hospital stay (LOS), and length of stay in the intensive care unit (ICU LOS) were lower, while levels of SpO₂, mean blood pressure (MBP), prothrombin time (PT), and international normalized ratio (INR) were higher. Meanwhile, the 30-day mortality rate (6.58% vs. 4.04% vs. 3.74% vs. 6.32%, P < 0.05), 90-day mortality rate (7.92% vs. 4.48% vs. 4.33% vs. 7.94%, P < 0.01), 180-day mortality rate (7.92% vs. 4.48% vs. 4.63% vs. 8.27%, P < 0.01), and 365-day mortality rate (8.07% vs. 4.63% vs. 4.63% vs. 8.27%, P < 0.01) were higher in Group Q1 than in Groups Q2 and Q3, and higher in Group Q4 than in Groups Q2 and Q3, with differences considered statistically significant at a P-value below 0.05. Association between TyG-BMI and clinical outcomes in cardiac surgery patients

Two Cox regression models were used to assess the independent impact of the TyG-BMI index on mortality, as detailed in Tables 2 and 3. In the fully adjusted model (Model 2),which accounted for demographic factors, comorbidities, laboratory values, and SOFA score, patients in the second and third TyG-BMI quartiles had significantly lower risks of 30-day all-cause mortality compared to the lowest quartile, with adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) [95% confidence intervals (CIs)] of 0.60 (0.37–0.96) and 0.55 (0.34–0.90), respectively. No significant difference was observed in the highest quartile (HR: 0.95, 95% CI: 0.62–1.46). A similar trend was observed for 365-day all-cause mortality, with adjusted HRs (95% CIs) of 0.62 (0.40–0.96), 0.55 (0.35–0.85), and 0.90 (0.61–1.32) for Q2, Q3, and Q4, respectively, compared with Q1 as the reference group. We found that cardiac surgery patients with a lower TyG-BMI index (< 224.93) had higher 30-day and 365-day all-cause mortality rates than those with a TyG-BMI index ≥ 224.93. However, patients in the Q4 group (TyG-BMI >301.47) exhibited higher all-cause mortality rates at 30 days and 365 days compared to those in the Q2 and Q3 groups (224.93 < TyG-BMI < 301.47). Discussion

This retrospective study is the first to investigate the relationship between TyG-BMI and all-cause mortality in individuals undergoing cardiac surgery. The analysis demonstrated a U-shaped pattern between TyG-BMI values and mortality during the 30- to 365-day follow-up periods. Postoperative mortality risk rose with both decreased and elevated TyG-BMI levels, pointing to a non-linear pattern between this marker and patient survival. The statistical significance of this nonlinear trend was further confirmed through spline-based regression modeling, highlighting the need to pay more attention to the dual-end risks of TyG-BMI in preoperative assessments.

Insulin resistance (IR) is frequently observed in association with both metabolic syndrome and obesity [16]. The presence of insulin resistance has been linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular complications and unfavorable prognoses after cardiac surgery [17]. This relationship is attributed to disorders of glucose and lipid metabolism, as well as oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, endothelial dysfunction, and ectopic lipid storage [7]. The TyG index, based on fasting plasma glucose and triglyceride concentrations, has been recognized as a useful surrogate for assessing insulin resistance [18]. The TyG-BMI index, along with BMI, offers a more thorough evaluation of metabolic disorders and obesity levels [18, 19]. Recent studies have shown that the TyG-BMI index is associated with poor outcomes in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) [20–22]. Chen et al. and Zhang et al. have also found that the TyG index may be a valuable predictor of adverse outcomes in diabetic patients who have undergone coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) [23]. Previous research has predominantly concentrated on the effects of elevated TyG or TyG-BMI on the risks associated with cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, and all-cause mortality [24].

Our study identified a U-shaped association between the TyG-BMI index and all-cause mortality within 30 to 365 days following cardiac surgery. We propose the following interpretations for this finding. First, as the TyG index is calculated using fasting triglyceride and glucose levels, a low TyG value may reflect either hypolipidemia or hypoglycemia. It is plausible that low fasting glucose contributes to the increased mortality observed at lower TyG levels. Previous studies have shown that hypoglycemia (≤ 4.0 mmol/L) is significantly associated with higher risks of atrial fibrillation, diabetes, stroke, and major adverse cardiovascular events [25]. Moreover, patients experiencing more than five hypoglycemic episodes per year have a 61% higher risk of cardiovascular events, including arrhythmia (65%), cerebrovascular accident (38%), and myocardial infarction (43%) [26]. Second, the cardiovascular impact of low lipid levels remains controversial. One prospective cohort study found that low LDL-C (< 70 mg/dL) and low triglyceride levels were associated with an increased risk of hemorrhagic stroke in women [27]. However, other studies suggest that cardiovascular benefits may continue to accrue with further reductions in LDL-C [28]. Interestingly, although obesity is a well-established risk factor for cardiovascular disease, numerous studies have reported a U- or J-shaped relationship between BMI and mortality [29]. In some contexts, individuals with lower BMI may face higher short-term mortality compared to those who are overweight [30]. This paradox may be partly explained by the limitations of BMI, which is calculated solely from height and weight and does not reflect fat distribution or body composition. As a result, individuals with low TyG-BMI may be misclassified as having low metabolic risk, while in fact they may suffer from malnutrition, sarcopenia, or frailty—conditions that independently increase the risk of mortality. Furthermore, we observed that the increase in mortality risk at higher TyG-BMI values was less pronounced than at lower values, consistent with the “obesity paradox”. This phenomenon has been documented in various cardiovascular populations but remains underexplored in ICU patients following cardiac surgery [31]. We propose that the inflection point of the TyG-BMI index (approximately 235–238) may represent a “metabolic intermediate zone”. This threshold warrants further investigation as a potential tool for risk stratification in future studies, but more clinical evidence is needed to validate our conclusions in the future.

In our study, the risks across all TyG-BMI quartiles were relatively close, with statistically significant associations observed only in certain subgroups. The reduced number of participants in some subgroups may have limited the statistical power, but other explanations may also exist. We found that the association between TyG-BMI and 30-day and 365-day all-cause mortality was more pronounced in male patients, non-White populations, patients with acute kidney injury, and those without type 2 diabetes mellitus, suggesting a certain degree of heterogeneity in the relationship between TyG-BMI and postoperative mortality risk. Clinically, insulin resistance (IR) is associated with several conditions, including obesity, T2DM, metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, and cancer [32]. IR is a hallmark feature of patients with T2DM, who are already in a high-risk metabolic state. Therefore, the prognostic stratification value of the TyG-BMI index in diabetic patients may not be as evident as in non-diabetic individuals. In addition, our study did not perform subgroup analyses based on different BMI levels (e.g., normal weight vs. overweight/obese), as this was not planned in the initial study design. Given that BMI is an integral component of the TyG-BMI index, obesity status may modulate its predictive performance. Future studies are warranted to further explore the utility of this index across different BMI categories, which may help identify the optimal populations for its application.

TyG-BMI is an index that can be calculated based on routine laboratory tests and physical measurements, with the advantages of being simple, cost-effective, and non-invasive [33]. This study highlights the potential of TyG-BMI as an informative metric for assessing preoperative risk [34]. Particularly for elderly cardiac surgery patients with multiple comorbidities, a TyG-BMI level within the appropriate range may represent a better metabolic state and balanced energy reserves, which can help improve postoperative survival rates [2]. Additionally, for patients with low TyG-BMI, nutritional support and overall health improvement can be enhanced preoperatively [35]; while for those with high TyG-BMI, metabolic control should be intensified and perioperative management optimized to achieve individualized interventions [12].

This study has the following strengths: First, it employed a systematic and diverse set of statistical methods, including Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis, Cox proportional hazards regression model, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression, and subgroup analysis. The association between TyG-BMI and all-cause mortality in cardiac surgery patients was investigated using diverse analytical approaches, which enhanced the rigor and credibility of the findings. Second, the RCS model uncovered a nonlinear, U-shaped relationship between TyG-BMI and mortality across short-, medium-, and long-term periods, successfully identifying risk inflection points that traditional linear models failed to detect. Third, the study covered postoperative mortality outcomes at multiple time points, covering follow-up periods of 30 to 365 days, providing a more comprehensive assessment. Furthermore, utilizing a substantial real-world dataset of individuals who underwent cardiac surgery, this study has good clinical representativeness and generalizability.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. Firstly, the study employs a retrospective observational approach. Although multiple factors have been adjusted for, it is still impossible to completely avoid the influence of confounding factors or to determine causality. Secondly, as an indirect metabolic indicator, TyG-BMI cannot distinguish between fat and muscle components through BMI alone, which may lead to some evaluation bias [36]. Thirdly, the MIMIC-IV database does not contain reliable data on cause-specific mortality. Therefore, only all-cause mortality was analyzed in this study. Future prospective research is warranted to investigate the association between TyG-BMI and specific causes of death, such as cardiovascular or infectious mortality, to further clarify its clinical implications. Additionally, direct comparison between TyG-BMI and existing risk scoring systems would help evaluate its additional predictive value. However, as this study is a retrospective analysis, and due to differences in the availability and calculation standards of each scoring system, we were unable to perform such comparisons. Future analyses using discrimination metrics (e.g., AUC), reclassification indices (e.g., NRI, IDI), or calibration curves would be beneficial in further assessing the incremental value of TyG-BMI in clinical decision-making. Meanwhile, as the TyG-BMI was calculated based on glucose and triglyceride values upon ICU admission, these parameters may have been influenced by surgical stress and acute-phase responses, potentially limiting its reflection of the patient’s baseline metabolic risk.

In summary, TyG-BMI may serve as a useful and accessible biomarker for early risk stratification in critically ill patients after cardiac surgery. Future prospective studies are needed to validate its predictive performance in broader populations. Similar metabolic indices have shown prognostic utility in various cardiovascular populations. For instance, Yılmaz et al. reported that triglyceride-based indices predicted in-hospital mortality in coronary ICU patients [37], while Aslan et al. associated the atherosclerotic plasma index with cerebrovascular events in patients with carotid artery disease [38]. These findings support the broader applicability of TyG-like indices in risk stratification. In addition, with the growing role of artificial intelligence (AI) in clinical prediction and personalized risk assessment, the integration of artificial intelligence (AI)-based modeling techniques may further enhance the predictive accuracy and clinical utility of metabolic indices such as TyG-BMI. Recent advances have demonstrated the potential of AI to improve risk modeling in cardiovascular medicine, suggesting promising directions for future research in this field [39].

Conclusion

In our analysis, TyG-BMI demonstrated potential as a predictor of all-cause mortality at 30, 90, 180, and 365 days among critically ill individuals who had undergone cardiac surgery. Furthermore, a U-shaped relationship was identified between the TyG-BMI index and the risk of all-cause mortality in these patients post-surgery. Future studies should investigate cause-specific mortality and examine specific subgroups of patients to validate and elaborate on our findings.
