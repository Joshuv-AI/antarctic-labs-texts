Capsular management in posterolateral total hip arthroplasty: impact on proprioception, balance, functional outcomes, and complications
Authors: Sahan Guven, Enejd Veizi, Ali S. Nazligul, Yasin Erdogan, Sahin Cepni, Kasim Kilicarslan, Ahmet Firat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe role of capsular management in total hip arthroplasty (THA) performed via the posterolateral approach remains debated, particularly regarding its effects on proprioception, balance, and postoperative stability. While capsular repair may reduce dislocation risk, partial capsular excision may facilitate surgical exposure. This study compared T-capsulotomy with capsular repair and partial capsular excision in terms of proprioception, balance, functional and radiological outcomes, operative time, and complications.MethodsA retrospective cohort of 177 patients who underwent primary THA between February 2019 and April 2024 was analyzed. Patients were assigned to either T-capsulotomy with capsular repair (n = 91) or partial capsular excision (n = 86). Proprioception was assessed using the Hip Joint Position Sense test; balance with the Berg Balance Scale; function with the Forgotten Joint Score (FJS-12) and Harris Hip Score (HHS); and pain with the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). Radiographic evaluation included acetabular anteversion, inclination, offsets, and leg-length discrepancy. Operative time and postoperative complications were recorded.ResultsNo significant differences were found between groups in proprioception, balance, FJS-12, HHS, or VAS scores (p > 0.05). Radiological parameters were similar. Operative time was significantly shorter in the partial excision group (70.5 vs. 80.1 min, p < 0.001). Complications were infrequent, and no dislocations occurred.ConclusionsAt one-year follow-up, no statistically significant differences were identified between the two capsular management techniques in terms of proprioceptive, functional, and radiological outcomes. Although partial capsular excision shortened operative time, it did not yield superior clinical results. Capsular management may be guided by surgeon preference and intraoperative considerations when accurate component positioning is achieved. Further prospective studies are needed to clarify early postoperative proprioceptive differences and long-term outcomes.Level of EvidenceLevel III (retrospective cohort study).

Background

In total hip arthroplasty (THA), particularly when the posterolateral approach is utilized, the management of the hip capsule plays a critical role in postoperative stability and the risk of complications. The literature indicates that dislocation rates are associated not only with the surgical approach itself, but also with multimodal factors such as component positioning, femoral head size, preservation of gluteal musculature, and capsular repair [1–3]. Capsular and soft tissue repair, in particular, appears to be effective in reducing the risk of early dislocation. While dislocation rates range from 4% to 8% in series using the posterolateral approach without capsular repair, this rate decreases to between 0 and 0.8% when capsular repair is performed [4].

In addition to the microscopic anatomy of the hip capsule, proprioceptive receptors within the capsule, including type I and III mechanoreceptors and free nerve endings, are believed to contribute to postoperative proprioception and balance control through afferent sensory input [5]. However, clinical studies at the macroscopic level remain limited; only a small number of randomized controlled trials have demonstrated that capsulotomy and repair performed via the anterior approach are superior to capsulectomy in terms of postoperative proprioception [2, 6].

Systematic reviews evaluating proprioceptive and balance abilities have emphasized that long-term impairments persist in both proprioception and balance performance after THA; this condition increases the risk of falls and highlights the benefits of adequately structured balance rehabilitation programs [1, 7]. However, most of these studies evaluate outcomes without distinguishing between surgical approach types or capsular management strategies. Specifically, the impact of capsular repair on functional, radiological, and proprioceptive outcomes remains unclear in the context of the posterolateral approach.

Retrospective studies and meta-analyses have demonstrated that dislocation rates are significantly lower in patients who undergo capsular repair, and that functional outcomes such as Harris Hip Score are superior in this group [8, 9]. However, no significant differences have been found between the capsular repair and resection groups in terms of operative time and blood loss [10]. Moreover, it has been suggested that capsulectomy provides a wider surgical field, potentially offering better visualization during the procedure. However, the literature has raised concerns that the pseudocapsular tissue developing after partial capsular excision may not provide the same functionality as the native capsule in terms of proprioceptive receptor density and balance control [6]. Although there are studies in the literature investigating the effects of different surgical approaches in THA on proprioception and balance, the specific impact of various capsular management techniques used in the posterolateral approach on these outcomes remains incompletely understood. In addition, few studies have simultaneously evaluated proprioceptive function, balance performance, radiological parameters, and clinical outcomes within the same cohort.

Accordingly, the main hypothesis of our study is that in THA performed via the posterolateral approach, the partial capsular excision technique may yield comparable results in terms of operative time, dislocation, and other complications when compared to the standard T-capsulotomy with capsular repair technique, while potentially being associated with inferior outcomes regarding joint position sense and balance perception. The aim of this study is to compare the effects of these two different capsular management techniques used in THA cases performed via the posterolateral approach for hip osteoarthritis on hip joint proprioception and balance sense, functional and radiological outcomes, operative time, and the incidence of dislocation and other complications.

Methods

In this retrospective cohort study, patients diagnosed with hip osteoarthritis who underwent THA via the posterolateral approach at single institution between February 2019 and April 2024 were reviewed. Patients who were diagnosed with primary hip osteoarthritis and underwent unilateral primary total hip arthroplasty via the posterolateral approach were included in the study. The study protocol was approved by the local institutional ethics committee (Approval No: TABED 2-25-1102) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Inclusion criteria were age 18 years or older, a minimum postoperative follow-up of 12 months, and availability of complete preoperative and postoperative clinical and radiographic data. Exclusion criteria were as follows: a history of previous surgery on the same hip in 6 patients, revision THA in 14 patients, bilateral THA in 24 patients, osteoarthritis secondary to developmental dysplasia of the hip in 18 patients, inflammatory arthritis in 11 patients, neuromuscular disorders that could affect proprioception or balance in 3 patients, and insufficient postoperative follow-up data in 12 patients. After applying exclusion criteria, a total of 177 patients who met the inclusion criteria were analyzed (Fig. 1).

Baseline demographic variables (age, sex, side, body mass index, etc.), American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status classification scores, operative time, femoral head diameters and intraoperative complications were collected from patient records.

Surgical technique

In our clinic, the posterolateral approach is routinely preferred for THA in patients with hip osteoarthritis. However, there are variations in capsular management techniques among surgeons in the literature when using the posterolateral approach [11]. In our clinic, we utilize two different capsular management techniques: T-capsulotomy with capsular repair and partial capsular excision. All patients underwent total hip arthroplasty in the lateral decubitus position under spinal anesthesia via the posterolateral approach. A skin incision measuring 10–15 cm was made, starting from the posterior border of the greater trochanter and extending toward the posterior superior iliac spine [12]. The subcutaneous tissues and fascia lata were incised along the line of the skin incision. The fibers of the gluteus maximus muscle were bluntly dissected to expose the short external rotator muscles. In both groups, the conjoint tendon comprising the piriformis tendon and the obturator internus with the gemelli muscles was carefully identified and released. The posterior joint capsule was then exposed.

Group 1: T-Capsulotomy and capsular repair

In Group 1 patients, a T-shaped capsulotomy was performed on the posterior capsule. A vertical incision perpendicular to the femoral neck was made first, followed by a horizontal incision parallel to the posterior wall of the acetabulum, resulting in a T-shaped configuration. After femoral neck osteotomy and removal of the femoral head, acetabular preparation was carried out, and the appropriately sized acetabular component was inserted in a press-fit manner and secured with two screws. Following femoral canal preparation, the appropriate femoral components were implanted. At the end of the procedure, the posterior capsule was repaired according to the configuration of the T-shaped capsulotomy. The vertical limb of the capsulotomy was repaired using a soft-tissue to soft-tissue side-to-side technique with non-absorbable sutures. The horizontal limb of the capsule was reattached to the greater trochanter. Subsequently, the released short external rotator muscles were reattached to the greater trochanter using non-absorbable transosseous sutures.

Group 2: partial capsular excision

In Group 2 patients, a partial capsular excision technique was performed. Following the release of the piriformis and other short external rotator muscles, an elliptical segment of capsular tissue was excised from the posterior joint capsule. A full-thickness elliptical capsular segment with an approximate maximal vertical length of 2 cm and maximal horizontal length of 5 cm was partially excised. Minor variations in excision size were allowed according to intraoperative visualization, patient anatomy, and soft-tissue conditions. This elliptical area was oriented with its long axis parallel to the posterior wall of the acetabulum and its short axis perpendicular to the femoral neck (Fig. 2). Acetabular and femoral components were implanted using the same technique as in Group 1. No capsular repair was performed in this group. The short external rotator muscles were reattached to the greater trochanter using the same transosseous suture technique as in Group 1.

All procedures were performed by four experienced arthroplasty surgeons. Two surgeons routinely performed a T-capsulotomy with capsular repair, while the remaining two surgeons routinely employed partial capsular excision. The choice of capsular management strategy was based on surgeon-specific practice patterns rather than predefined patient-related criteria. As such, capsular management technique and surgeon were partially confounded in this retrospective design. Both capsular management strategies were performed contemporaneously during the study period rather than sequentially. In all patients, the acetabular component used was the cementless Trilogy® Acetabular System (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA), and the femoral component was the cementless M/L Taper®Hip Prosthesis (Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN, USA). In both groups, a drain was placed following component implantation, and the surgical incision was closed in layers according to anatomical planes. Skin closure was performed using staples.

Postoperatively, a standardized rehabilitation protocol was applied to all patients. Prophylactic antibiotics were administered as 4 × 1 g cefazolin for 1 day postoperatively, and thromboembolic prophylaxis was provided with 4000 IU enoxaparin daily for 20 days. Full weight-bearing mobilization under the supervision of a physiotherapist was initiated within the first 24 h. Patients were instructed to avoid hip flexion beyond 90°, adduction, and internal rotation for the first 6 weeks.

Clinical evaluation

The proprioceptive sense of the hip joint was assessed using the Hip Joint Position Sense (HJPS) test. To eliminate visual input, patients were placed in the supine position with their eyes closed. The hip was passively moved to target angles (90° of flexion and 15° of abduction) and held in that position for 5 s before returning to the neutral position. The patient was then asked to actively replicate the same position. The absolute angular difference (in degrees) between the target and the reproduced position was measured using a goniometer. The test was repeated three times for each patient, and the mean value was recorded. This method has been described in the literature as a reliable test for evaluating hip joint proprioception [13, 14].

Postural balance performance was assessed using the Berg Balance Scale. This test consists of 14 functional tasks, each scored on a scale from 0 to 4, with a maximum possible score of 56. A score of 50 or above was considered indicative of good balance control [14, 15].

The Forgotten Joint Score (FJS-12) was used to evaluate patients’ awareness of their hip joint during daily activities. This self-reported questionnaire consists of 12 items, with scores ranging from 0 (constant joint awareness) to 100 (complete joint unawareness). Higher scores indicate lower joint awareness and better functional integration. The FJS-12 is considered highly sensitive for detecting subtle functional differences following total hip arthroplasty [16].

Pain and functional recovery were evaluated using the Harris Hip Score. This scale consists of four subcomponents: pain, function, range of motion, and deformity, and is calculated out of a maximum score of 100. Scores ≥ 90 are classified as ‘excellent’, 80–89 as ‘good’, 70–79 as ‘fair’, and < 70 as ‘poor’ [17].

Pain intensity was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). On the 10-point scale, 0 indicated no pain and 10 represented unbearable pain. Patients were asked to mark the average level of pain they experienced at rest and during walking over the past week [18].

All clinical evaluations were conducted during routine outpatient visits at 12 months postoperatively by one independent blinded observer.

Radiological evaluation

Radiographic component positioning parameters were systematically assessed for all cases to evaluate consistency of surgical execution across surgeons. Cup inclination, cup anteversion, stem alignment, and offset restoration were measured on standardized postoperative anteroposterior pelvic radiographs by an independent observer blinded to the surgical technique. Given that different posterior capsular management strategies may influence intraoperative exposure and component positioning, radiological parameters were evaluated independently to determine whether capsular management was associated with any measurable differences in component alignment or radiographic surrogates of hip biomechanics.

All radiological assessments were performed on standardized anteroposterior (AP) pelvic radiographs obtained at the 1-year postoperative follow-up, using the digital PACS system. During imaging, patients were positioned supine with the pelvis in neutral rotation and both lower limbs internally rotated approximately 15°. Symmetry of the sacrum and obturator foramina, along with alignment of the teardrop points, were used as references to ensure radiographic standardization.

Since postoperative computed tomography scans were not available for all patients, the acetabular anteversion (AA) angle was measured on standard anteroposterior pelvic radiographs using the method described by Widmer [19]. Accordingly, the long axis (D) and short axis (d) of the elliptical projection of the acetabular component opening were measured. The anteversion angle was calculated using the formula arcsin (d/D), as described by Widmer. Measurements were taken from the same edge of the liner opening, and no magnification correction was required due to the ratio-based nature of the calculation. The acetabular inclination (AI) angle was measured as the angle between the horizontal reference line passing through both teardrop points and the long axis of the acetabular component. The center of rotation (COR) of the hip joint was determined by fitting a circle to the contour of the femoral head using digital measurement software, ensuring the circle corresponded to the maximal diameter of the head. The center of this circle was defined as the COR. Acetabular offset was measured as the horizontal distance between the COR and the medial wall of the acetabulum, defined by the quadrilateral surface. Femoral offset was defined as the horizontal distance between the COR and the longitudinal axis of the femoral diaphysis [20]. Leg length discrepancy was calculated by measuring the distance between the inferior edge of the teardrop and the apex of the ipsilateral lesser trochanter on both sides. The discrepancy was recorded as the difference in measurements between the two sides, expressed in millimeters. All radiographic measurements were performed by two independent, blinded orthopedic surgeons, and interobserver reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC = 0.88, 95% CI 0.82–0.92).

On anteroposterior pelvic radiographs, the target range for acetabular anteversion (radiographic anteversion measured using the Widmer method) was considered to be 5–25°, and for acetabular inclination, 30–50°. These ranges have been recommended in the literature as optimal positions to reduce the risk of dislocation and wear [21]. Component positioning outside of these target ranges was considered malposition. For leg length discrepancy (LLD), a difference of less than 10 mm was regarded as clinically acceptable, whereas an LLD of ≥ 10 mm was classified as clinically significant and reported as ‘leg length discrepancy’ [22].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or as median (minimum–maximum), while categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of data distribution. For between-group comparisons, the independent samples t-test was used for normally distributed variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Correlation analyses were performed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient for normally distributed variables and Spearman’s rho for non-normally distributed variables. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The analyzed cohort consisted of 177 patients. T-capsulotomy with capsular repair (Group 1) was performed in 91 patients, while partial capsular excision (Group 2) was applied in 86 patients. The mean age was 57.5 ± 11.7 years in Group 1 and 58.1 ± 13.5 years in Group 2. Female patients accounted for 52.7% of Group 1 and 51.2% of Group 2. The right side was affected in 53.8% of Group 1 and 53.5% of Group 2. The mean body mass index (BMI) was 28.9 ± 5.1 in Group 1 and 28.8 ± 3.8 in Group 2. An ASA (American Society of Anesthesiologists) score of 2 was present in 81.3% of patients in Group 1 and 72.1% in Group 2. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of mean age, sex distribution, affected side, BMI, or ASA score (p > 0.05). (Table 1)

The mean operative time was 80.1 min in Group 1 and 70.5 min in Group 2. Operative time was significantly longer in Group 1, showing a statistically significant difference compared to Group 2 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). When complication distribution was analyzed, in Group 1, component malposition was identified in 8 patients (8.8%)—including high acetabular anteversion in 3 patients, high acetabular inclination in 3 patients, and low acetabular inclination in 2 patients. Additionally, leg length discrepancy was detected in 4 patients (4.4%). No dislocations were observed. In Group 2, component malposition was found in 2 patients (2.4%)—one with high acetabular inclination and one with low acetabular inclination—and leg length discrepancy was identified in 1 patient (1.2%). No dislocations were observed in this group either. Complication rates were numerically different between groups; however, these differences did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4).

Discussion

The most important finding of our study is that, in primary THA performed via the posterolateral approach, there were no statistically significant differences between T-capsulotomy with capsular repair and partial capsular excision techniques in terms of proprioception (Hip Joint Position Sense Test), balance (Berg Balance Scale), functional outcomes (Forgotten Joint Score and Harris Hip Score), or pain levels (VAS) at the 1-year postoperative follow-up. Additionally, no statistically significant differences were observed between the groups in radiological parameters, including acetabular anteversion, inclination, acetabular and femoral offset, and leg length discrepancy. However, the operative time was significantly shorter in the partial capsular excision group, while complication rates were low and comparable in both groups. Within the limitations of this study, no statistically significant differences were detected between the two capsular management techniques in terms of short-term clinical or radiological outcomes. However, operative time was significantly shorter in the partial capsular excision group, indicating that the only measurable disadvantage associated with capsular repair was a modest increase in operative time.

In this study, the FJS-12, HHS, and VAS scores at the first postoperative year were similar between the T-capsulotomy with capsular repair group and the partial capsular excision group in THA performed via the posterolateral approach. Although a meta-analysis published in 2020 reported that posterior capsular repair was associated with improvements in certain functional scores, it also noted heterogeneous effects on HHS scores and concluded that no clear superiority could be established [8]. Additionally, a systematic review conducted by Miranda et al. emphasized that there was no clinically significant difference in functional outcomes between the two approaches [2]. These findings are consistent with previous studies suggesting that capsular repair does not provide additional benefit in terms of functional recovery in the short term.

Partial capsular excision resulted in a significantly shorter operative time, likely due to the additional suturing and soft-tissue manipulation required during capsular repair. Although partial capsular excision may theoretically facilitate exposure and retraction, the present study did not demonstrate superiority in component positioning or other measured clinical and radiological outcomes. Similarly, Schwartz et al. (2021) and Curtin et al. (2023) reported that capsular repair significantly prolongs operative time [23, 24]. The time-saving advantage offered by partial excision may be considered a factor that can enhance operational efficiency, particularly in high-volume centers.

Prolonged operative time has been associated with an increased risk of surgical site infection and other short-term complications in arthroplasty, particularly when operative duration exceeds commonly reported thresholds such as 120 min [25, 26]. However, the approximately 10-minute reduction observed in the present study should be interpreted cautiously, as both groups had mean operative times well below these higher-risk thresholds. No difference in infection rates was observed between the groups; therefore, the shorter operative time should not be interpreted as evidence of reduced infection risk. Although shorter procedures may theoretically improve operating-room efficiency, the magnitude of the observed difference may not necessarily translate into clinically meaningful reductions in postoperative complications. In high-volume centers, however, even modest reductions in operative time may contribute to improved workflow efficiency and cumulative resource utilization.

The literature has shown that posterior capsular repair in primary total hip arthroplasty performed via the posterolateral approach can significantly reduce the rate of early postoperative dislocation [4, 8]. Nonetheless, several systematic reviews and cohort studies have highlighted that component positioning is a more critical determinant of dislocation risk than the selected capsular management technique [21, 27]. In our study, the incidence of complications remained low in both groups, and no dislocations were observed. This discrepancy compared to previous studies may be explained by several factors. First, the overall dislocation rate in our cohort was 0%, which limited the statistical capacity to detect differences between groups. In the present study, the findings suggest that regardless of the chosen capsular management technique, there was no difference in terms of postoperative dislocations. Although accurate component placement and standardized surgical technique are recognized contributors to hip stability, the present study was not designed or powered to determine the relative contribution of component positioning versus capsular management to dislocation risk. However, it should be noted that dislocation is a relatively rare event in contemporary total hip arthroplasty, and the present study was not specifically designed or powered to evaluate this outcome, as the groups were not stratified by component positioning (e.g., malpositioned vs. well-positioned). Therefore, the absence of a statistically significant difference in dislocation or other complications should be interpreted with caution and should be viewed as a clinical observation of procedural safety within this cohort rather than definitive evidence of statistical equivalence.

The hip joint capsule contains important mechanoreceptors that contribute to proprioception and postural control [28, 29]. Although the presence of mechanoreceptors in the capsule and in the postoperative pseudocapsule has not been definitively demonstrated histologically, it has been reported that preserving capsular integrity may offer functional benefits [3, 5]. Labanca et al. emphasized that proprioceptive and balance impairments following total hip arthroplasty can persist for up to five years, and that structured rehabilitation programs contribute to improvement during this period [7]. Frattura et al. reported that intraoperative manipulation of the capsule and surrounding structures temporarily impairs postoperative balance, but a tendency toward recovery is typically observed within 4–12 months [30]. In our study, no statistically significant difference was found between patients who underwent T-capsulotomy with capsular repair and those who underwent partial capsular excision in terms of JPS (hip joint position sense) and Berg Balance Scale scores at the end of the first postoperative year. This finding suggests that capsular repair was not associated with a measurable advantage in terms of proprioception and balance at the 12-month follow-up in this cohort. This finding may be more plausibly explained by preserved proprioceptive contributions from the surrounding muscular and tendinous structures rather than functional preservation by the pseudocapsule.

Although it was previously hypothesized that progressive reinnervation of periarticular soft tissues might contribute to proprioceptive recovery over time, current histological evidence indicates that mechanoreceptor reinnervation within reconstructed tissues and pseudocapsules is minimal or entirely absent [31, 32]. Therefore, recovery of proprioceptive function is likely driven by compensatory adaptation of surrounding intact structures rather than direct neural regeneration. The recovery trend reported in the literature, which is also observed in our results, implies that the effects of capsular management may become more pronounced over longer follow-up periods and that the sensitivity of measurement methods may influence these outcomes. In addition, because preoperative proprioceptive, balance, and functional measurements were not available, the present study could not evaluate the magnitude of postoperative improvement or determine whether baseline differences existed between the groups. Therefore, the postoperative-only comparison at 12 months should be interpreted as a cross-sectional assessment of late postoperative status rather than an evaluation of recovery trajectory or functional gain. However, given that the present study evaluated outcomes only at the 12-month follow-up, it is possible that early postoperative differences between capsular management techniques were not captured. Future studies incorporating earlier assessment time points, such as at 3 or 6 months postoperatively, may help to determine whether capsular repair provides an advantage in the early recovery phase, even if long-term outcomes are similar.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design introduces an inherent risk of selection bias, and the absence of randomization limits causal inference. In addition, capsular management strategy and surgeon were partially confounded, as two surgeons routinely performed one technique while the remaining two surgeons used the alternative approach. Because surgeons did not perform both techniques, surgeon-specific factors such as operative workflow, soft-tissue handling, component positioning preferences, and intraoperative decision-making could not be fully separated from treatment-related effects. This design may introduce surgeon-related bias. However, the absence of significant differences in radiographic component positioning parameters across surgeons suggests consistency in surgical execution, thereby minimizing one of the major sources of surgeon-related variability.

The follow-up period was limited to one year, which precludes assessment of long-term complications and late functional outcomes. Moreover, because only a single postoperative assessment at 12 months was available, early postoperative differences in proprioception, balance, and functional recovery between the groups could not be evaluated. Furthermore, the relatively modest sample size, particularly for subgroup analyses, may have reduced the statistical power to detect subtle differences between techniques. Additionally, the study may be underpowered to detect differences in rare complications such as dislocation, and no formal post-hoc power analysis was performed for these outcomes. In addition, preoperative baseline proprioceptive, balance, and functional outcome measurements were not available due to the retrospective design, which limited the ability to evaluate postoperative changes relative to baseline status. Finally, proprioception was assessed using validated clinical tests rather than laboratory-based motion analysis systems, which may limit the sensitivity and precision of the proprioceptive measurements. Future prospective studies involving multiple surgeons performing both techniques are warranted to further isolate technique-related effects from surgeon-specific factors.

The present study contributes to the existing literature by providing a comprehensive evaluation of capsular management techniques in posterolateral THA, incorporating proprioceptive, functional, and radiological outcomes within a single cohort. Although no statistically significant differences were identified between techniques in this cohort, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the methodological limitations and limited statistical power for certain outcomes.

Conclusion

Within the limitations inherent to the retrospective non-randomized design of this study, no statistically significant differences were identified between T-capsulotomy with capsular repair and partial capsular excision at one-year follow-up following primary total hip arthroplasty via the posterolateral approach. Although partial capsular excision was associated with a shorter operative time, this did not translate into superior clinical or radiological outcomes. Further prospective studies are required to confirm these findings.
