Automated measurement of left ventricular ejection time via contactless suprasternal notch laser vibrometry
Authors: Jonas Gonzalez-Billandon, Matilda Andersson, Benjamin Waubert, Anders Ekman, Lisa Bergin, Linette Hartzell, Johan Sahlén, Simon Gustafsson, Yuan Tan, Andreea Valdman, Mattias Windå, Mattias Nilsson, Jonas Spaak, Henrik Hellqvist
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLeft Ventricular Ejection Time (LVET) is a sensitive indicator of early cardiac dysfunction, yet its routine use in first-line population screening is hindered by the operational and time demands of operator-dependent modalities like echocardiography. In this study, we introduce a scalable, contactless approach using laser Doppler vibrometry (LDV) signals targeted at the suprasternal notch (SSN) to capture hemodynamic events without physical sensor attachment. We demonstrate in a cohort of 238 individuals that this rapid, zero-setup measurement yields LVET values statistically equivalent to pulsed-wave Doppler echocardiography (concordance within a 25 ms margin). Furthermore, an exploratory clinical analysis revealed that the LDV-derived LVET index (LVETI) was significantly prolonged in patients with aortic insufficiency and stenosis compared to low-risk controls, demonstrating the clinical plausibility of the measurement. To eliminate the bottleneck of expert interpretation, we developed and validated a deep learning model that automatically extracts LVET with high accuracy (mean absolute error of 8.08 ms). By combining easy LDV data acquisition with automated analysis, this approach may facilitate rapid, operator-independent cardiovascular assessment, potentially enhancing population-level screening and cardiovascular health management.Subject terms: Cardiology, Diseases, Engineering, Medical research

Introduction

Although advances in cardiovascular health assessment have enabled more accurate risk prediction and monitoring, cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of death1. It is estimated that 80% of cardiovascular disease cases can be prevented by adopting healthier lifestyle habits, such as quitting smoking, daily sports activity and maintaining a balanced diet. However, to effectively guide lifestyle interventions, it is essential first to assess cardiovascular health1. Traditional parameters used to evaluate cardiovascular function include cardiac output and stroke volume. Cardiac output represents the volume of blood the heart pumps through the systemic circulation per minute, while stroke volume refers to the amount of blood ejected from the left ventricle during each systolic contraction. Additionally, systolic time intervals–such as left ventricular ejection time (LVET) and the pre-ejection period (PEP)–offer convenient, non-invasive methods for studying changes in left ventricular performance. Several studies have shown that variations in LVET and PEP correlate well with changes in cardiac output and stroke volume, making these measurements suitable indicators for assessing cardiovascular health2.

Left ventricular ejection time is defined as the time interval from aortic valve opening to aortic valve closure and represents the systolic phase during which the left ventricle ejects blood into the aorta. Because LVET is inversely related to heart rate3, it must be corrected for heart rate to accurately interpret its changes, yielding the LVET index (LVETI) under the assumption of a linear relationship between LVET and heart rate. Moreover, LVET is influenced by sex and age; for example, females exhibit significantly longer LVET at all heart rates compared to males, and LVET tends to increase with age3. Consequently, LVETI should be further adjusted for sex, and normal ranges should be sex-specific when using it for diagnosis4. Since their introduction in the early 1960s, both LVET and LVETI have served as convenient, non-invasive methods to assess changes in left ventricular performance5. Notably, LVET has been associated with incident heart failure in individuals from the general population free from cardiovascular disease6, and recent studies have shown that LVET is an independent predictor of all-cause mortality in patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction7. A recent study by Pestelli et al. demonstrated a strong correlation between prolonged LVETI and severe aortic stenosis8, while earlier work linked prolonged LVETI to aortic regurgitation9. These findings collectively highlight LVETI as a clinically relevant marker for identifying specific cardiovascular diseases and aiding in the diagnosis of aortic valve abnormalities.

A clinical reference standard for measuring LVET is through pulsed-wave doppler (PWD) echocardiography exam, typically assessing flow at the left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) or across the aortic valve10. Echocardiography remains indispensable for comprehensive anatomical and functional diagnosis; however, its application for high-throughput primary care triage or large-scale screening is inherently limited by the requirement for specialized sonographers and prolonged examination times. Consequently, there is a clinical need for rapid, operator-independent tools that can act as gatekeepers–identifying isolated hemodynamic abnormalities and efficiently directing at-risk patients toward specialized echocardiographic evaluation. Consequently, various non-invasive techniques have emerged to estimate LVET, primarily through the analysis of arterial pulse wave characteristics at accessible peripheral sites. These methods generally rely on identifying specific waveform features corresponding to aortic valve opening and closure (e.g., the dicrotic notch). Examples include analysing pressure waves measured by cuff-based oscillometry at the brachial artery11, or photoplethysmographic (PPG) signals12,13. Another class of methods assesses cardiac mechanics through chest wall vibrations or impedance changes. Seismocardiography (SCG) uses accelerometers or gyroscopes placed on the chest to detect local vibrations related to cardiac events. SCG allows estimation of cardiac time intervals, including the PEP and LVET, by identifying fiducial points corresponding to aortic valve opening and closure14. Similarly, impedance cardiography has been explored for cardiac time interval estimation15. However, these are typically contact methods requiring sensor placement on the individual.

This motivates the exploration of non-contact measurement techniques. Laser doppler vibrometry (LDV) has gained interest for cardiovascular monitoring due to its non-contact nature and high precision in vibration measurement. Antognoli et al. investigated heartbeat detection using machine learning applied to LDV signals aimed at the carotid artery16. The carotid artery’s proximity to the heart reduces, but does not eliminate, the influence of arterial tree hemodynamics on the measured signal17. The suprasternal notch (SSN), the visible depression between the clavicles superior to the manubrium sterni, represents another candidate for an anatomical window for non-invasively assessing central cardiovascular dynamics due to its proximity to the aortic arch and great vessels18. This study proposes a novel approach by directing a LDV sensor at the SSN to directly measure the local surface vibrations associated with central hemodynamic events. We hypothesize that these high-fidelity, non-contact measurements contain distinct mechanical signatures corresponding to aortic valve opening and closure, enabling the estimation of LVET. This method differs from traditional SCG (contact sensor, different typical locations, lower fidelity sensors) and standard LDV applications (pulse wave velocity focus or peripheral waveform analysis), aims at combining the benefits of a non-contact, high-fidelity sensor with a physiologically relevant measurement site for direct assessment of cardiac timing intervals. Before exploring complex machine learning approaches for cardiovascular risk prediction, we need to establish the physiological validity of the contactless SSN signal. Therefore, we focus our initial efforts on extracting a single, well-established cardiac marker: LVET.

Contributions

In this paper, we make two key contributions. First, we demonstrate the feasibility of estimating the LVET using a vibrometer laser directed at the SSN. Unlike contact-based sensors that require physical attachment or echocardiography which demands expert probe manipulation, this optical approach enables a ’point-and-measure’ workflow. By targeting a visible anatomical landmark without skin contact, the method significantly simplifies acquisition, minimizing setup time and eliminating variability associated with sensor coupling pressure. Second, we introduce an automated deep learning framework for estimating LVET directly from the SSN signal, eliminating the need for manual extraction and expert intervention. Together, these components validate an end-to-end solution designed to transform LVET from a specialist metric into a scalable tool for accessible, primary care screening.

Methods

Study population

The study population consisted of individuals who underwent a cardio Neko health scan at one of the Neko clinics in Stockholm, Sweden, or who were separately invited due to known or suspected cardiovascular disease. The study was approved by the Swedish Ethical Review Authority and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. Data were collected from 691 study participants (male=409, female=282, age=50 ± 14 years) all underwent an LDV assessment, out of which 238 patients (male=146, female=92, age=58 ± 13) underwent an extended scan (due to increased risk of or known or suspected cardiovascular disease) that included an echocardiogram examination. To define a cohort with low cardiovascular risk, we started with a subset of 318 participants for whom complete clinical characteristics were available. We then sequentially excluded individuals based on several predefined risk factors (see Fig. 7 for a complete flowchart and exclusion criteria). This process yielded a final low-risk cohort of 168 individuals (84 male, 84 female, age= years). Stratified baseline characteristics are provided in Table 1. It is important to note that the observed age distribution, which shows a progressive decrease in sample size from the 18–39 age group to the>70 group, was incidental rather than designed. This pattern reflects the demographic profile of individuals who voluntarily seek preventative cardiovascular screening, a population that skews towards younger to middle-aged adults (18–55 years). Consequently, older age categories contain fewer participants, particularly those >70 years.

Hardware

Vibrometer laser

In this study, we used the VibroGo® portable laser vibrometer (Polytech, Waldbronn, Germany) to record surface vibrations at the SSN. This measurement captures the mechanical displacement of the skin surface induced by the propagation of the arterial pulse wave through the underlying great vessels. Consequently, the signal serves as a high-fidelity, non-contact surrogate for the central pressure waveform, allowing for the precise monitoring of hemodynamic timing.

Electrocardiogram

During the scanning procedure, the electrocardiogram (ECG) signal was acquired using the Dual Bio Amp FE232 system (ADInstruments, Dunedin, New Zealand). A single-lead ECG was recorded by positioning 3 electrodes on the chest for 60 seconds at 4000 Hz, with the signal time-synchronized to the SSN signal. To perform the ECG analysis and extract the R-peaks, we used the neurokit2 open-source library19.

Echocardiogram

Echocardiographic examinations were performed using the GE Vivid E95 cardiovascular ultrasound system, and the Viewpoint 6 software was used for offline analysis (GE HealthCare, Chicago, US). The system’s integrated ECG interface enabled simultaneous acquisition of a synchronised single-lead ECG during ultrasound imaging. This synchronisation facilitates the R-peak (onset) detection and computes the exact heart rate.

Experimental protocol

We collected LDV SSN signals during the scanning procedure developed at Neko Health. A vibrometer laser (see “Vibrometer laser” section) was aimed at the SSN. To standardize acquisition, the LDV was mounted overhead and targeted to the visible suprasternal notch anatomical landmark as illustrated in Fig. 1. The device’s autofocus function was used to optimize measurement depth for signal quality, while the operator visually monitored the signal and utilized a feedback camera to verify the laser spot positioning within the SSN. Recordings were made for 60 seconds at 4000 Hz while the patient remained in a supine resting position. At the same time, we acquired a time-synchronized one-lead ECG signal (see “Electrocardiogram” section).

LVET estimation from SSN signal

The pulse wave signal recorded by the laser at the SSN reflects the mechanical displacement induced by pulsatile blood flow. This signal results from the rhythmic expansion and contraction of tissues in response to each cardiac cycle, and also includes vibrations generated by the opening and closure of the aortic valve, as well as the reflected wave in the aorta. By capturing these subtle movements, the laser vibrometer provides a contactless, high-resolution depiction of cardiovascular dynamics. The hemodynamic patterns at the SSN allow inference of the timing of aortic valve opening and closure, with the pulse wave onset marking the beginning of systolic blood ejection (rapid upstroke) and the dicrotic notch indicating the moment of aortic valve closure (a small downward inflection following the systolic peak)20. Although under ideal conditions the measurements would exhibit negligible time differences, some discrepancies are expected. This is because hemodynamic parameters are known to vary according to the measurement site within the arterial tree17. The vibrometer signal is acquired at the SSN, whereas the echocardiogram exam records data directly from the LVOT.

For each participant, three non-consecutive pulse waves were manually selected from the approximate beginning, middle, and end of the recording, and annotated, ensuring each chosen wave was clean with clearly discernible onsets and dicrotic notches. We employed the time-synchronized ECG signals recorded during the scanning to extract the R-peaks, allowing us to segment the SSN signals into individual RR-intervals. The LVET was defined as the interval from pulse wave onset (systolic onset) to the dicrotic notch (systolic end), determined by visual inspection of the signal. LVET was computed as the difference between the systolic end and onset times, expressed in milliseconds. Pulse waves exhibiting clearly discernible features, as determined by the annotator, were chosen for analysis as illustrated in Fig. 2 (left). In addition, the R–R interval for each selected pulse wave was determined by measuring the time between the R-peak preceding the pulse wave onset and the subsequent R-peak. Both the displacement and acceleration components of the SSN signal were used to facilitate accurate estimation. The definitive SSN-derived LVET for each subject was obtained by averaging LVET values estimated from all of their respective annotated and segmented SSN heartbeat signals.

LVET from echocardiogram

LVET was measured from pulsed-wave Doppler recordings obtained in the LVOT, a critical region just proximal to the aortic valve where blood is ejected from the left ventricle into the aorta. The onset of systolic flow was defined as the point at which the Doppler envelope departs from the baseline, and the end of systolic flow was marked by the return of the envelope to the baseline (see Fig. 2). Concurrent ECG traces were used to confirm the timing of systolic events. Electronic calipers integrated within the ultrasound system were employed to quantify the elapsed time between these two points, providing a precise measurement of the LVET in milliseconds. For each Doppler measurement, values were obtained from one cardiac cycle.

Experiment

Signal processing

We first processed the raw SSN signals by applying a median filter to reduce noise. Next, we computed the displacement signal () by integrating the vibrometer’s velocity measurement and the acceleration signal () by differentiating this velocity signal. Since the vibrometer laser measures the target surface velocity, integrating this signal over time gives us the displacement at the suprasternal notch, which provides a detailed view of the blood flow gradient evolution. Taking the first derivative of the SSN velocity signal allowed us to calculate acceleration, and we observed that on noise-free signals, peaks corresponded with the onset and dicrotic notch (see Fig. 2). We detrended the SSN signals and ECG signals, removing any linear trend or baseline drift to ensure they oscillated around zero, then applied a low-pass filter at 200 Hz to remove high-frequency noise and a high-pass filter at 0.5 Hz to remove breathing artefacts. Finally, both signals were down-sampled to 500 Hz.

Extended echocardiogram scan

A subset of 238 patients (male=146, female=92, age=58 ± 13) underwent an extended scan that included an echocardiogram, which was performed sequentially on the same day as the initial LDV scan (see Sec. Experimental protocol). To ensure comparable physiological states between the two time points, both examinations were conducted with the patient in a supine position under resting conditions. The echocardiogram was performed using pulsed wave doppler to record the cardiac time intervals (see Sec Echocardiogram). This clinical exam, recognised as a reference standard for estimating LVET21, enabled us to directly record the LVET from the left ventricle outflow tract, thereby providing a ground truth measurement.

Dataset

From the study population used in the analysis (n=691), we constructed a dataset from LDV recordings obtained during the scan and their associated annotations. We split the LDV signals into samples segmented over individual RR-intervals. The dataset was partitioned using stratified sampling based on age and sex. Furthermore, subjects were assigned exclusively to either the training or testing cohort to prevent data leakage, resulting in 501 subjects (2260 samples, male=297, female=204, age=) in the training set and 190 subjects (790 samples, male=112, female=78, age=) in the test set.

Automatic LVET estimation

We used the LVET dataset (see “Dataset”) to train an ML model to estimate the pulse wave onset and dicrotic notch from an SSN heartbeat segmented signal using both and signals as input. Rather than having the model directly estimate the LVET duration, we focused on these specific event timings to ensure that the model’s outputs remain interpretable and allow medical experts to validate the detected events on the signals themselves, maintaining an essential human-in-the-loop for this critical diagnostic application.

Model and baselines

To establish baseline performance, we used both a mean regressor fitted on the training set and a peak detection algorithm augmented with a prior. The peak detection processes the signals and looks at the maximum peaks for the pulse wave onset and dicrotic notch (see Algo 2).

For the automated detection of pulse wave onset and dicrotic notch from the 1D SSN signals (displacement and acceleration), we employed a ConvNeXt architecture30 adapted for 1D data processing. The choice was motivated by ConvNeXt’s design, which integrates principles to capture dependencies across different scales within sequence data effectively. Furthermore, its computationally efficient architecture makes it suitable for real-time applications. To regress the timing of the onset and dicrotic notch, we use a probabilistic head to predict the location () and scale (b) parameters of a Laplace distribution for each event (see Fig. 5). We choose the Laplace distribution over the Gaussian distribution as the Laplace distribution has a sharper peak and heavier tails relative to a Gaussian, which can enhance robustness against outlier data points. This metric provides insight into the upper bound of the error for 95% of the predictions, thereby offering a measure of the worst-case performance. Discussion

We investigated a novel approach for non-contact LVET assessment using LDV targeted at the SSN. A key contribution of this work is the demonstration of statistical equivalence between our LDV-SSN method and pulsed-wave Doppler echocardiography in 238 individuals, achieving concordance within a 25 ms margin. To advance the practical application of this technique, we developed and validated a deep learning model capable of automatically extracting LVET from LDV-SSN signals with a high degree of accuracy (mean absolute error of 8.08 ms on test data). Furthermore, a clinical exploratory analysis revealed that the SSN-derived LVETI is significantly prolonged in patients with aortic insufficiency and stenosis (>90% probability) compared to low-risk controls, highlighting its diagnostic potential.

The development of such a non-contact, automated system is particularly relevant when considering the existing landscape of LVET estimation. While echocardiography systems remain the undisputed gold standard for investigating numerous parameters of cardiac mechanics with proven high precision, their widespread deployment for primary screening is constrained by the necessity for highly trained sonographers and prolonged examination times. The prospective clinical gain of the proposed method lies not in competing with the diagnostic breadth of echocardiography, but in providing an operator-independent, rapid-throughput triage tool. By automating the extraction of a key hemodynamic parameter, this system can act as a pre-clinical ’gatekeeper’. It effectively flags patients with abnormal systolic time intervals (such as a prolonged LVET), thereby optimizing the allocation of specialized and expensive echocardiographic resources for those who require a more detailed assessment. Recognising the clinical need for such scalable screening solutions, prior research has investigated various non-invasive modalities. Traditional acoustic methods like phonocardiography (PCG) provide accurate valve timing but require physical sensor contact15. Similarly, alternative non-invasive techniques like contact-based PPG12 and SCG15 have been explored for LVET estimation. More recently, camera-based approaches have emerged; for instance, Rahman et al.31 demonstrated ’Vision-SCG’ using smartphone video to track chest vibrations. However, such methods still rely on physical markers (e.g., QR codes). In contrast, the non-contact nature of our LDV-SSN approach offers marker-less acquisition, inherently offering potential advantages in protocol simplification and accessibility. To our knowledge, the present work is the first to specifically evaluate LVET estimation using LDV signals acquired at the SSN, validating the approach against a clinical standard in a comparatively large cohort. Our statistical analysis revealed a statistically significant mean difference between SSN-derived LVET and the reference echocardiography measurement (p=0.0001), with a mean absolute percentage error of 4.93%. Despite this difference in means, a formal equivalence test confirmed that the discrepancy was within our pre-defined 25-millisecond margin (p<0.05). Consistent with previous findings7, we also observed significant differences in SSN-LVET between males and females in our cohort. Similar to the study of Alhakak et al.23 we found a weak yet significant association between age and LVET. This likely reflects the large inter-individual variability in LVET alongside age-related physiological changes reported previously. Accordingly, we interpret this finding as supportive of a population-level tendency rather than a clinically decisive predictor in isolation. Further analysis focused on the heart rate-corrected index, LVETI, showed that for both males and females, SSN-derived LVETI has a statistical difference compared to echocardiography (p<0.05). For males, the SSN-derived LVETI was systematically underestimated by 5 ms and 7ms for females. Importantly, the mean percentage error for the LVETI estimation was low for both sexes (females: 3.42%, males: 3.29%). These findings contribute to the field by rigorously validating the SSN as a novel anatomical window for reliable, non-contact LVET and LVETI estimation via LDV, demonstrating its concordance with echocardiography. Moreover, this concordance–achieved using a protocol based on visual targeting of the anatomical landmark–suggests that the method is inherently robust to the minor variations in laser positioning and spot size that occur in standard clinical practice, maintaining accuracy without requiring sub-millimeter targeting precision.

While the validation of LVET derived from SSN signals is promising, its broader clinical utility depends on practical, automated implementation. Manual extraction of LVET by identifying the onset and dicrotic notch from the SSN signal demands significant expertise, a bottleneck for the adoption of this contactless and rapid measurement method. To overcome this challenge, we developed a machine learning model to automatically estimate these LVET-defining fiducial points. The application of machine learning to pulse waveform analysis is well-established; for instance, Antognoli et al.16 utilised LDV signals from the carotid artery for heartbeat detection, while other studies have employed pulse waveforms with ML to predict parameters such as arterial compliance32 or blood pressure from PPG signals33. These successes in related areas support the rationale for applying similar automated approaches to our LDV-SSN signals for LVET estimation.

Our specifically developed CNN-based model, designed to regress the onset and dicrotic notch timings, demonstrated superior performance compared to two baseline models. It achieved a mean IoU of 97% and a MAE of 8.08 ms for LVET predictions on the test set. The model also exhibited robustness across various subgroups with specific medical conditions, consistently outperforming the baselines. Although its performance was generally stable, a small decrease was observed in participants with moderate/high SCORE234, and in the older population (> 65 years old), indicating areas for future refinement. Nevertheless, these results underscore the significant potential of our deep learning model to accurately automate LVET extraction from LDV-SSN signals, paving the way for its integration into rapid and operator-independent cardiovascular assessment tools.

Having established a novel, automated, and non-contact method for LVET/LVETI estimation, the subsequent crucial question was its clinical relevance in detecting cardiovascular abnormalities. Given that abnormal LVETI values are known to be associated with certain cardiovascular diseases, we assessed the clinical plausibility of our SSN-derived measurements. Our investigation in patients with AS and AI demonstrated that their SSN-LVETI was significantly prolonged compared to a low-risk control group (with>90% probability). This concordance with established medical literature for these specific conditions8,9 underscores the potential clinical validity and diagnostic utility of our SSN-based method.

We acknowledge several limitations regarding the clinical applicability and scope of this technology. First, the deployment of this technology requires initial capital investment in high-fidelity laser vibrometry hardware. While we hypothesize that in a high-volume preventative screening context, these upfront equipment costs may be offset by the significant reduction in per-patient operational costs (due to the rapid, zero-setup workflow), the hardware costs could be a factor limiting immediate accessibility outside of specialized screening centers.

Second, the automated pipeline currently extracts a single parameter (LVET), providing a narrower assessment than echocardiography. However, this narrow focus was a deliberate methodological choice serving as a first-step validation. By demonstrating that an automated model can reliably extract a known, ground-truth physiological marker from the SSN pulse wave, we confirm that this contactless signal inherently contains valid and extractable mechanical signatures of the heart. Proving this physiological fidelity lays the foundation for further machine learning approaches.

Third, while our automated ML model achieved a low overall error (MAE 8.08 ms), its performance showed a marginal decrease in subgroups with conditions such as high cholesterol or high LDL. Additionally, we recognize that anatomical variations, particularly high body mass index (BMI) or increased neck adipose tissue, may attenuate surface vibrations at the SSN, potentially affecting signal quality in patients with obesity. This suggests that further model refinement is necessary to ensure robust clinical applicability across the full spectrum of patient profiles. We also acknowledge the imbalance in sample size across age groups, which resulted from the incidental demographics of the screening population. While this limits the generalizability of our findings in the older population (>70 years), we mitigated potential bias during the automated model development by employing stratified sampling for the training and testing sets (“Dataset” section). This ensured that the model performance remained robust despite the unequal group sizes in the raw cohort. Thirdly, our validation of SSN-LVETI prolongation in specific pathologies (aortic stenosis and insufficiency), though consistent with established literature, was based on a limited number of patients, particularly for aortic stenosis (n=8). Furthermore, we acknowledge that the LDV and echocardiography measurements were not acquired simultaneously. Although performed on the same day, sequential recording introduces potential variability in physiological states (e.g., heart rate, preload/afterload). While LVETI correction accounts for heart rate differences, minor hemodynamic fluctuations between measurements remain a potential source of residual error. Finally, the study population was predominantly Caucasian; consequently, the generalizability of our findings–including LDV signal characteristics and ML model performance–to more diverse ethnic and demographic groups requires explicit future confirmation.

These limitations naturally pave the way for several avenues of future research. A key priority will be to validating the current findings in larger, more heterogeneous patient cohorts which is crucial to definitively establish the generalisability and robustness of the SSN-LDV approach across diverse demographics. To enhance the clinical utility for specific conditions, studies with larger sample sizes for pathologies like aortic stenosis are also essential.

Having validated the foundational utility of the LDV-SSN signal, future work will move beyond predefined physiological intervals. We aim to leverage modern representation learning, allowing deep neural networks to autonomously extract complex features from the raw waveform to directly regress patient risk. These advanced representation learning techniques–such as pre-training on extensive unlabeled raw signal data followed by fine-tuning on annotated datasets–could significantly enhance model performance, generalisability, and robustness against signal variability. Ultimately, such refinements and broader validations will be pivotal in translating this promising non-contact cardiovascular assessment technology into widespread clinical practice.

Conclusion

Cardiovascular disease remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide, making the development of a fast, contactless protocol for assessing cardiovascular performance of high importance. This paper demonstrated the feasibility of end-to-end solution to estimate LVET using a vibrometer laser targeted at the suprasternal-notch. Our comparison against a clinical standard, echocardiography, in 238 participants established statistical equivalence within a 25-millisecond margin with a mean average error of 4.93%. Furthermore, the development and validation of a deep neural network for automated LVET extraction from these signals highlights the potential of this integrated contactless approach. In summary, these findings point toward a rapid, operator-independent method for targeted cardiovascular screening. The development of such an accessible and automated technology could significantly enhance preventative screening. By enabling the early, high-throughput detection of subclinical cardiac dysfunction alongside routine risk markers like ECG and blood pressure, this system functions as an efficient triage mechanism. Such proactive identification is key for timely interventions and effectively directing individuals towards further in-depth medical examinations and optimizing the utilization of comprehensive diagnostic tools like echocardiography.

To systematically isolate distinct cardiac phenotypes and mitigate confounding overlaps between concurrent valvular conditions, individuals were filtered based on an ordinal severity scale derived from echocardiographic assessments. Baseline or trace findings were assigned a score of 0, mild findings a score of 1, and moderate (including mild-to-moderate) to severe findings a score of 2. Following strict stratification by sex, the list below details the specific inclusion and exclusion thresholds applied to isolate the aortic cohorts:

Aortic Insufficiency (AI): Defined by the presence of at least mild aortic regurgitation (severity score of 1 or greater).
