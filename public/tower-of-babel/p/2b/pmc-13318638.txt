The Effect of Migration on Perceived Safety and Sleep Quality: An Analysis From Rural and Urban Indigenous Wixárika Communities in Jalisco, Mexico
Authors: Leela McKinnon, Eric C. Shattuck, Igor Martín Ramos Herrera, René Cristobal Crocker Sagastume, Quetzabel de la Cruz, Paritemai González Torrez, Thankam Sunil, David R. Samson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study explores how perceived safety influences sleep quality among Wixárika individuals in Mexico. We compared the sleep quality of those living in a rural town in northern Jalisco with those living in the Guadalajara Metropolitan Area (GMA). We aimed to assess differences in perceived safety, model its association with sleep outcomes, and test for gendered effects.MethodsSleep was measured using actigraphy and surveys. Perceived neighborhood safety (PNS) was measured with survey items. Sleep outcomes included efficiency, fragmentation, wake after sleep onset (WASO), self‐rated quality, and daytime dysfunction. Analyses used Mann–Whitney U tests, linear mixed effects models, and logistic regression.ResultsOur sample included 127 participants. In the rural location (n = 61), the mean age was 38.02 years (SD = 12.18, range = 18–65), with 72.13% comprised of women. In the GMA, the mean age was 29.59 years (SD = 8.43, range = 18–57), of whom 65.15% were women. PNS was significantly higher in the rural town than in the GMA. Gender moderated the association between PNS and sleep quality, with women showing improved sleep quality with greater PNS. Poorer mental health predicted lower sleep quality in rural participants and greater odds of daytime dysfunction in urban participants.ConclusionsIn our analyses, living in urban contexts is associated with reduced perceived safety, which interacts with gender to shape sleep. Women's sleep appears to be particularly sensitive to perceived safety, reflecting broader intersecting vulnerabilities and health inequities faced by Indigenous migrant women. Policies addressing housing and neighborhood safety, alongside culturally grounded supports, may improve sleep health in these communities.

Introduction

The Wixárika ethnic group is indigenous to the Sierra Madre Occidental region of northwestern Mexico. Wixárika individuals from rural, traditional towns are increasingly moving to cities, especially Tepic, Nayarit, and Guadalajara, Jalisco (Negrín 2019), to expand their economic opportunities. Such internal migration introduces significant changes to their living environments. These profound and rapid changes in socioecological environment may intensify the disproportionate health inequities faced by Indigenous migrants in Mexico (Crocker Sagastume et al. 2018; Gamlin 2016). The long‐standing socioeconomic and health inequities experienced by Mexico's Indigenous populations (Gamlin 2016) are further exacerbated for members of the Wixárika ethnic group because of their historical underrepresentation in Mexico's public health research (Crocker Sagastume et al. 2018).

This work focuses specifically on the effects of Wixárika individuals' perceived safety on their sleep. Environmental safety is an important factor that may be associated with sleep health in the context of rural to urban migration. Here, we compare sleep patterns between Wixárika individuals living in the Guadalajara Metropolitan Area (GMA) and in a small, rural town in northern Jalisco. Contexts of everyday living differ greatly between these locations, making them ideal comparative sites to examine the effects of perceived safety, migration, and sleep. Guadalajara is a large urban center with a population of 5,268,642 inhabitants as of 2020 (Guadalajara, n.d.), with high population densities, round‐the‐clock activity and traffic, and ubiquitous artificial light at night. In contrast, rural Wixárika communities tend to be small and geographically isolated. For instance, the rural community described in this study is home to approximately 2000 people. While homes do have electricity, this service is less reliable and more inconsistent than in the city, and in‐home lighting is relatively dim. Outdoor lighting is not as widespread as in the city, contributing to much lower levels of nighttime lighting. The sleep health of Wixárika individuals who have experienced changes to their physical and social environments due to rural to urban migration is of particular concern. People living in urbanized contexts report higher rates of insomnia than those in rural settings (Santos‐Silva et al. 2010; Stranges et al. 2012), which is likely related in part to reduced exposure to sunlight during the day and the use of artificial lighting at night (Chang et al. 2015; Wright et al. 2013). Furthermore, lack of perceived social support, which is reported at elevated rates in migrants due to separation from their traditional support structures (Gamlin 2016), is associated with shorter sleep duration and lower sleep quality (Cacioppo et al. 2002). Thus, better documentation and understanding of sleep in both traditional, rural Wixárika communities as well as urban communities with large populations of migrants is critical for identifying and more completely addressing health disparities experienced by Mexico's Indigenous populations.

Sleep Disturbance and Perceived Environmental Safety

The safety of living environments is an important consideration in the sleep health of migrants. Despite being necessary for growth, health, and physiological functioning (Durmer and Dinges 2005; Faraut et al. 2012; Xie et al. 2013), sleep is a behaviorally vulnerable state that increases the sleeping individual's risk of predation, victimization, and violence. Therefore, sleeping individuals are inherently at a greater risk of harm, which is especially notable during certain stages of the human sleep cycle. Sleep is segmented into nonrapid eye movement (NREM) and rapid eye movement (REM) stages. NREM is further divided into three stages, N1, N2, and N3. During sleep, humans cycle through the NREM and REM states approximately 4–6 times per night (Dement and Kleitman 1957; Patel et al. 2025). Arousal threshold, which here refers to the ease of awakening in response to external stimuli, varies considerably by sleep stage. NREM stage N3, or deep sleep, is characterized as the stage with the highest arousal threshold (e.g., the state from which it is most difficult to wake) (Colten et al. 2006). REM sleep can also be a highly vulnerable state: when broken down into its two sub‐states (tonic REM, characterized by parasympathetic activation and a relaxed body state; and phasic REM, in which sympathetic activation is observed, as well as rapid eye movements, muscle twitches, and respiratory variability), phasic REM has been described as the state with the most inhibition of external sensory stimuli (Ermis et al. 2010).

Studies in sleep anthropology have demonstrated that characteristics of human sleep may have evolved to maximize safety to achieve the optimal balance of deep, highly vulnerable, yet cognitively beneficial sleep architecture. Human sleep is shorter than would be expected based on comparison with other primates, yet a higher proportion of human sleep is devoted to the REM stage. These derived characteristics are the basis for the sleep intensity hypothesis, the idea that human sleep evolved to allow the maximum benefit of deep, restorative sleep in the shortest amount of time (Samson and Nunn 2015). By reducing total sleep time, it is thought that human sleep reflects selection for reducing time spent in this vulnerable state while simultaneously maximizing the cognitive and prosocial benefits of deep sleep and REM sleep (Nunn and Samson 2018; Samson and Nunn 2015). Human sleep architecture is also thought to have evolved protective mechanisms. Phasic REM occurs in short bursts and alternates with a frequent return to tonic REM, which may increase safety by minimizing time spent in high arousal threshold phasic REM. Thus, frequent returns to lower arousal threshold tonic REM may have had the evolutionary advantage of potential threat detection during sleep (Ermis et al. 2010). Furthermore, the alternation between NREM and REM sleep in human sleep cycles is proposed to serve a protective function, in which consecutive time in high arousal threshold REM and deep sleep is short, thereby minimizing time that an intruder can approach undetected (Voss 2004). Moreover, it may also be the evolutionary etiology for acute insomnia, which is argued to be a normal biopsychosocial response to a perceived or real threat, and thus adaptive in ancestral contexts (Nunn et al. 2016; Perogamvros et al. 2020).

These ideas are fundamental to the sentinel hypothesis, which describes the idea that brief interruptions of sleep allow for environmental threat scans, which may maximize the balance between sleep continuity and alertness for danger (Snyder 1966). Evolved mechanisms to increase safety during sleep are likely to be multifaceted. In addition to brief awakenings during sleep to serve a scanning function, Blume et al. (2018) propose that during sleep, human brains enter a “sentinel processing mode,” in which they are able to evaluate environmental stimuli during all NREM and even REM sleep stages (Blume et al. 2018). Depending on what stimuli are detected in these environmental scans, awakening may be initiated to respond to a potential, imminent threat. These multiple pathways that are proposed as serving a security function exemplify how physical safety appears to be a highly important evolutionary driver of modern human sleep physiology.

Adaptive responses to real or perceived environmental threats are likely to be observed across a wide range of sleep quality metrics. For the purposes of this analysis, sleep quality is operationalized as encompassing objective sleep measures including sleep efficiency, sleep fragmentation, and nighttime arousals, as well as subjective perceptions of the quality of one's sleep and feelings of restedness and alertness the next day (Nelson et al. 2022; Silva‐Caballero et al. 2023). Thus, we include several analyses that consider both objective actigraphy‐generated measures of sleep quality and survey‐based measures of subjective satisfaction with sleep and perceived daytime function.

Perceptions of home and neighborhood safety are known to influence sleep patterns, supporting the idea that light, low‐arousal threshold sleep has an adaptive component. Cross‐cultural studies have reported that individuals who perceive their neighborhoods as less safe experience shorter, lower quality sleep (e.g., DeSantis et al. 2016; Hill et al. 2016; Simonelli et al. 2015). Several recent studies have reported significant associations between perceived safety and sleep duration and quality, supporting the hypothesis that sleep is responsive to potential physical threats in the environment, and that feeling unsafe in one's environment is an important driver of sleep disruption. For example, perceived neighborhood safety (PNS) has been found to be associated with subjective sleep quality, where those who perceive their environments as safer also report significantly better sleep quality (DeSantis et al. 2016). Indeed, perceived and/or actual safety has also been found to be an important factor in sleep quality across groups with a range of demographic features. In adolescents in Florida, USA, for example, feeling unsafe at home and/or at school was associated with significantly higher odds of reporting short sleep duration (less than 7 h); higher odds of insufficient sleep were also associated with neighborhood disorder and having been the victim of bullying (Meldrum et al. 2018). Duncan et al. (2017) found that for sexual minorities (men who have sex with men [MSM]) living in Paris, France, living in neighborhoods that were perceived as unsafe during the day were more likely to have poor sleep quality, short sleep duration, problems falling asleep, and problems maintaining daytime alertness compared to those who reported feeling safe in their neighborhoods during the day (Duncan et al. 2017).

The majority of work examining the association between perceived or actual safety and sleep duration and/or quality use self‐report sleep measures, which do not always predict objective, biometrically generated data, including sleep duration and/or quality metrics derived from polysomnography (Cho et al. 2022) and actigraphy (Windmill et al. 2024). While self‐reported survey measures allow the analysis of large samples that have been instrumental in establishing robust results, a strong body of literature on the effect of safety on objective measures of sleep is lacking. McKinnon et al. (2023) presented results of actigraphic data on nighttime awakenings in a sample from the United States, finding that perceived neighborhood violence—but not noise—was associated with significantly more disrupted sleep, supporting the idea that safety specifically is a more salient factor in driving sleep disturbance than other, non‐safety related stimuli. This example demonstrates that actigraphy‐generated measures may also, in certain contexts, vary by perceived safety as is demonstrated in the numerous studies mentioned above that report on subjective sleep quality measures.

Fewer studies have presented data from samples outside of the industrialized Global North. Although more anthropological sleep studies are needed from culturally and socioecologically diverse contexts, the few studies conducted in nonindustrial, Global South populations generally support the association between safety and sleep duration and/or quality. Hill et al. (2016) presented data from Mexico, Ghana, South Africa, India, China, and Russia, notably expanding representation from countries beyond the Global North. The analysis, centering on PNS and self‐reported sleep outcomes, included outcome variables of self‐reported sleep duration and self‐reported sleep quality; overall, findings demonstrated that greater perceived safety was associated with lower odds of reporting insomnia symptoms and lower odds of reporting poor sleep quality in the majority of the populations sampled (Hill et al. 2016). Similarly, in a large sample from Argentina, Simonelli et al. (2015) found that feeling unsafe at home was strongly associated with poorer self‐rated sleep quality and shorter self‐reported sleep duration.

A limited number of studies using actigraphy in nonindustrial contexts have found similar associations between sleep quality measures and safety. For example, Samson et al. (2017) found that in groups of Hadza foragers, in nearly all sampled periods there was at least one member of the group who was awake. This finding was interpreted as providing support for the sentinel hypothesis (Snyder 1966), as chronotype variation that drives this sentinel‐like behavior would likely confer a group safety benefit (Samson et al. 2017). Additionally, in a comparative study across a rural to urban gradient, Silva‐Caballero et al. (2023) found that in adolescents in Mexico, sleep efficiency was higher in contexts where the sleeping environment was more buffered from potential environmental threats. The authors interpreted this finding as demonstrating that spaces that are more protected from potential environmental threats increase feelings of safety, leading to better sleep quality (Silva‐Caballero et al. 2023).

Given the underrepresentation of anthropological sleep studies from groups living outside of the industrialized Global North and the paucity of studies using objective sleep measures, more data from diverse socioecological contexts as well as those generated from objective (e.g., actigraphy) sleep measures are needed to better understand the relationship between sleep duration, quality, and safety.

Migration and Changes to Living Environment

Sleep is a critical piece of overall health that is especially important to consider for migrant health, given migrants' increased social vulnerability and the numerous factors that can affect sleep. Migrants are more susceptible to longer working hours and can also experience challenges with the quality and safety of their living and sleeping environments (Gamlin 2016; Yang et al. 2020), which can affect their ability to obtain adequate sleep. This can also be compounded by disruptions to social support systems that migrants experience when they move to new places; these social experiences could also exacerbate environmental effects on sleep and contribute to mental health problems that are known to be associated with worse sleep outcomes (Yang et al. 2020). Factors that can have detrimental effects on sleep duration and/or quality have been documented for immigrants, including racial/ethnic discrimination, difficulties accessing health care, social isolation, and disadvantageous work and/or living conditions (Almeida et al. 2025), all of which are relevant factors to consider for the sleep health of rural to urban internal migrants as well.

Gender and Safety in Mexico

Some studies have found that the association between perceived safety and sleep shows gendered patterns. When stratifying their analyses by gender, Simonelli et al. (2015) found that feeling unsafe at home was a significant predictor of decreased sleep quality for women but not for men. The experience of safety may be markedly different by gender, particularly depending on socioecological context. Violence against women is particularly concerning in Mexico (Gordon 2018), and the intersection of gender‐based violence and discrimination experienced by Indigenous migrant women (Gamlin 2016) makes them a uniquely vulnerable social group. Thus, the gendered effects of perceived safety on sleep are particularly important to consider for the sleep health of migrant Indigenous groups.

Aims and Hypotheses

Our primary objective is to explore associations between perceived safety and quantitative markers of sleep patterns from Indigenous Wixaritari living in a small, rural community in northern Jalisco and from Wixaritari (the majority of whom are migrants) living in the GMA. Our aims are to (1) quantify differences in perceived safety between rural and urban locations, (2) model the effect of perceived safety on sleep quality, and (3) assess whether there are gendered differences in the influence of safety on sleep outcomes in rural and urban Wixárika communities. We addressed these aims by testing the following hypotheses:Hypothesis 1 Urban migration is associated with perceived threats to safety. We predict that perceived safety in urban Guadalajara will be significantly lower than in rural northern Jalisco. Hypothesis 2 Perceptions of safety affect sleep quality. Here, we predict that perceived safety will be positively associated with sleep quality. Hypothesis 3 The effect of safety on sleep differs by gender, where we predict that perceived safety will be more strongly associated with women's sleep quality than with men's.

Urban migration is associated with perceived threats to safety. We predict that perceived safety in urban Guadalajara will be significantly lower than in rural northern Jalisco.

Perceptions of safety affect sleep quality. Here, we predict that perceived safety will be positively associated with sleep quality.

Methods

Participants and Recruitment

Participants were recruited via a cross‐sectional project focused on Wixárika sleep and health. We recruited participants from two groups: the GMA and a rural town in northern Jalisco. Most participants living in the GMA were migrants who had been born in rural communities in the Sierra Madre Occidental mountains. Two urban participants were born in the GMA, while three are missing survey responses for this question. Migrants reported that they had been living in the GMA for between less than a year and more than 25 years. Some of those living in the rural town had previously lived in cities before returning to live in the rural community. Data from the urban group were collected in June–July 2022 and February–March 2023 (n = 66); and data from the rural group were collected in March 2024 (n = 61). Two authors (PTG & QDC) are Wixaritari and have lived or currently live in Wixárika communities and assisted in community engagement, sharing information about the study, and recruiting participants. Further recruitment was conducted through snowball sampling.

We developed the protocol for this study in consultation with Wixárika members of our research team and adapted our research practices to align with culturally appropriate protocols. We discussed the study with community leaders in both locations and received written community permission to conduct recruitment in the rural community prior to beginning research activities. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Texas San Antonio (IRB #20–028), the Research Ethics Board of the University of Toronto (RIS Human Protocol #42074), and the Universidad de Guadalajara Centro Universitario de Ciencias Sociales record UDG‐CUCS 20–49‐2020.

Sleep Data

After explaining the study and receiving signed or verbal consent, we fitted each participant with a Motionwatch8 actigraph wristwatch (CamNTech). Actigraphs measure sleep via an accelerometer built into a device that is the shape and size of a standard wristwatch. Actigraphy is validated to a high degree of sensitivity (0.965) and accuracy (0.863) in comparisons with polysomnography, the gold standard for measuring sleep parameters (Marino et al. 2013). CamNTech Motionwatches are sturdy, waterproof, and are minimally invasive. The built‐in movement sensor captures movement in 60‐s epochs, providing outputs in binary sleep–wake states. These sleep–wake states are used to quantify nightly measures of sleep duration and sleep quality metrics such as sleep efficiency, the ratio of total sleep time to the duration of the sleep episode (i.e., time in bed), expressed as a percentage. This measure reflects sleep continuity, with higher percentages indicating more consolidated sleep (Reed and Sacco 2016). Another quantitative measure of sleep quality is fragmentation, the disruption of sleep continuity due to frequent awakenings during the sleep period. It is often quantified by the sleep fragmentation index (SFI), calculated as the total number of awakenings and shifts to lighter sleep stages (e.g., stage 1) divided by the total sleep time in hours. High sleep fragmentation indicates disrupted sleep architecture, leading to reduced restorative sleep and potential daytime impairments (Morrell et al. 2000). Lastly, we included wake after sleep onset (WASO), the amount of time spent awake between sleep onset and offset, which is used as a measure of sleep disruption due to nighttime awakenings.

Survey Measures

In addition to collecting sleep data using actigraphs, we also asked participants to complete a survey, which included questions about subjective sleep that were derived from the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (Buysse et al. 1989), including the questions “During the past month, how would you rate your sleep quality? (Very good, Fairly good, Fairly bad, or Very bad)”, “During the past month, how often have you taken medicine to help you sleep? (Not during the past month, Less than once a week, Once or twice a week, Three or more times a week)”, and “During the past month, how often have you had trouble staying awake while driving, eating meals, or engaging in social activity? (Not during the past month, Less than once a week, Once or twice a week, Three or more times a week).” Survey questions also assessed self‐reported physical (“How is your physical health currently?”) and mental health (“How is your mental/spiritual health currently?”) each rated on a scale from 0 to 4, with 0 corresponding to excellent health and 4 corresponding to poor health. These single‐item measures are widely used in health research due to the minimal risk of survey fatigue and burden placed on participants, as well as the documented associations between single‐item measures and health outcomes (Ahmad et al. 2014; DeSalvo et al. 2006). Finally, perceived safety was assessed. PNS (Hill et al. 2016) is a single score that is calculated as the mean of the responses to the questions “How safe do you feel when walking down your street alone after dark?” and “In general, how safe from crime and violence do you feel when you are alone at home?” These items are treated as quantitative variables, with 1 corresponding to feeling completely unsafe, and 5 corresponding to feeling completely safe, meaning that higher mean scores indicate higher perceived safety.

Survey questions were translated from English to Spanish for the printed questionnaires. Depending on participant preference and Spanish proficiency, Wixárika team members administering the surveys verbally translated the Spanish questions into Wixárika.

Data Analysis

We performed all data cleaning using the CamNTech sleep analysis software (CamNtech Inc., Cambridgeshire, UK) and performed data analysis and hypothesis testing using R version 4.4.1 (2024‐06‐14) (R Core Team 2020).

Perceived Safety by Location

We used Mann–Whitney U tests to compare PNS between populations to test Hypothesis 1's prediction that perceived safety in the urban location is significantly lower than in the rural location.

Linear Mixed Effects Modeling for Sleep Actigraphy Analysis

We used linear mixed effects models with sleep quality metrics (objective = sleep efficiency, fragmentation, and WASO) as the dependent variable to test associations with PNS score. We used the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015) to run linear mixed effects models, allowing us to treat each night of data as a separate data point by controlling for repeated measures per individual. We fit separate urban and rural mixed‐effects models for actigraphy outcomes and included the interaction term between gender and perceived safety to test the hypothesis that perceived safety has differential effects on sleep by gender. We controlled for self‐rated physical and mental health, age, and time in bed (for WASO only): WASO/Sleep Efficiency/Fragmentation ~ Gender × PNS + Physical Health + Mental Health + Age + Time in Bed + 1|ID.

Logistic Regression Analysis for Sleep Survey Variables

Responses for survey variables, which were originally grouped into four categories (“Very good”, “Fairly Good,” “Fairly Bad,” “Very Bad” for sleep quality and “Never,” “Less than once a week,” “Once or twice a week,” and “Three or more times a week” for daytime dysfunction), were grouped into binary responses. “Very good” and “Good” sleep quality outcomes were combined as “Good,” and responses of “Fairly Bad” and “Very Bad” were combined as “Bad.” Daytime dysfunction was grouped into “No dysfunction”, including responses of “Never” and “Less than once a week” and “Daytime Dysfunction,” including responses of “Once or twice a week” and “Three or more times a week.” We used logistic regression models to analyze the association between subjective sleep quality and daytime dysfunction: (Subjective Sleep Quality/Daytime Dysfunction ~ Gender × PNS + Location + Physical Health + Mental Health + Age).

Results

Descriptive Statistics

Our full sample included 127 participants (mean age = 33.64 years, SD = 11.19; range = 18–65), of whom 68.5% were women. Participants contributed 3–36 nights of data, with an average of 21.31 nights. Participants in the rural location were older on average than those in the urban location: in the rural location, the mean age was 38.02 years (SD = 12.18, range = 18–65), while in the GMA the mean age was 29.59 years (SD = 8.43, range = 18–57). Gender composition was similar across samples, with 72.13% of the sample consisting of women in the rural location and 65.15% of the sample consisting of women in the GMA.

Sleep Efficiency

Perceived safety was associated with significantly lower sleep efficiency in the urban location (estimate = −2.181, SE = 1.076, p = 0.047), but was not a significant predictor in the rural location (estimate = 1.173, SE = 1.256, p = 0.354). The interaction between gender and perceived safety was significantly associated with sleep efficiency in the urban location (estimate = 3.796, SE = 1.252, p = 0.004) but not in the rural location (estimate = −0.805, SE = 1.404, p = 0.568).

Women in the urban sample had significantly lower sleep efficiency than men (estimate = −11.584, SE = 4.435, p = 0.011). Worse self‐reported mental health was associated with lower sleep efficiency in the rural location (estimate = −2.062, SE = 0.816, p = 0.014) and is approaching significance in predicting higher sleep efficiency in the urban location (estimate = 1.584, SE = 0.849, p = 0.067). Sleep Fragmentation

The interaction between gender and perceived safety was a significant predictor of sleep fragmentation in the urban sample (estimate = −4.486, SE = 1.819, p = 0.016) but not in the rural sample (estimate = −0.254, SE = 1.955, p = 0.897).

Worse self‐reported mental health was associated with significantly more sleep fragmentation in the rural sample (estimate = 3.038, SE = 1.133, p = 0.009). Subjective Sleep Quality

None of the tested variables were significant predictors of subjective sleep quality in the past month, although perceived safety was nearly a significant positive predictor of past month sleep quality in the urban location (estimate = −1.094, SE = 0.649, p = 0.092). Worse self‐reported mental health significantly predicted greater odds of reporting daytime dysfunction in the urban location (estimate = 1.048, SE = 0.498, p = 0.035).

Discussion

Perceived Safety in Rural and Urban Wixárika Communities

Our analysis focused on perceived safety of urban and rural Wixárika individuals in Jalisco, Mexico. As predicted by our first hypothesis, we found that Wixárika participants living in the rural town in northern Jalisco reported feeling significantly safer than Wixárika people living in the GMA. Investigating differences in the subcomponents of the PNS score, we found that this difference was driven by perceived safety while alone at home, which was significantly higher in the rural town. We found no significant differences in perceived safety by location while walking alone at night. This finding suggests that migrants moving to urban locations may experience real or perceived declines in living environment security. Some participants in our urban sample may perceive their housing quality to be lower, a perception that could intensify feelings of insecurity in their neighborhoods, which is an interpretation consistent with the finding that migrants are at elevated risk of experiencing decreased housing quality and safety (Almeida et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2020). Future qualitative investigation should consider real and perceived housing and neighborhood quality for their role in driving perceptions of security for Wixárika people living in urban contexts.

Perceived safety for Wixárika migrants is ultimately situated in a complex and multi‐faceted sociocultural context, including low‐income and precarious employment, barriers to maintaining traditional food growing practices, prolonged separation from family, and disrupted access to cultural and/or spiritual spaces (Crocker Sagastume et al. 2025). Thus, addressing large‐scale structural barriers to safe living environments and the intersecting effects of ruptures to social networks and cultural spaces will necessitate attention to systemic policies (Hale et al. 2010). Migrants and immigrants experience structural vulnerability, a concept stemming from the theory of structural violence (Galtung 1969) that is expanded to capture systemic inequalities in access to economic, material, and political resources as well as vulnerabilities arising from cultural and/or ethnic discrimination and social exclusion (Quesada et al. 2011). Quesada et al. (2011) described how structural vulnerability uniquely affects Latino migrants living in the United States as a result of discrimination based on cultural values, legal status, and access to healthcare. Indigenous internal migrants in Mexico also experience similar structural vulnerability that is an important consideration in their health, including sleep. It is likely that Wixárika migrants living in Guadalajara experience structural vulnerabilities that those living in predominantly‐Wixárika rural communities do not, and that these social factors contribute to the sleep and health patterns we found in our analyses. In contrast to Wixárika participants living in traditional rural communities, many of the structural vulnerabilities identified by Quesada et al. (2011) in Latino migrants in the United States are likely experienced at an elevated rate by Indigenous internal migrants as well, including profound cultural discrimination and a history of anti‐Indigenous sentiment and negative stereotyping, cultural and linguistic marginalization, and economic precarity, and barriers to culturally appropriate health services (Gracey and King 2009; Montenegro and Stephens 2006).

Gender, Perceived Safety, and Sleep

We tested our second and third hypotheses with linear mixed effects models, which allowed us to simultaneously evaluate the influence of multiple factors on quantitative, actigraphy‐generated dimensions of sleep quality, as well as the interaction of gender and perceived safety to evaluate differential experiences between men and women. Our second hypothesis was rejected as, contrary to predictions, higher PNS in the urban sample was associated with lower sleep efficiency. Interestingly, these findings are in contrast to several studies that show the protective effect of safety on sleep duration and/or quality (DeSantis et al. 2016; Duncan et al. 2017; Hill et al. 2016; McKinnon et al. 2023; Meldrum et al. 2018; Simonelli et al. 2015).

However, we did find that when gender and location are considered, PNS interacts significantly with gender, indicating that the association between safety and sleep quality differs for men and women. Thus, our third hypothesis was supported by our finding that PNS has significant, differential effects on women's sleep quality compared to men's. However, this significant interaction was found only in the urban sample, with men's and women's sleep quality showing similar patterns by PNS in the rural location. In the urban location, we found that women's sleep quality increased significantly with greater perceived safety, while men's sleep quality decreased. Urban women's sleep patterns were consistent with our predictions, which were formulated based on elevated risks to safety experienced by women—particularly Indigenous women—in Mexico (Gamlin 2016; Gordon 2018), as well as the documented effects of PNS on sleep quality.

The existing literature is mixed regarding gender differences in safety‐related sleep processes. Our results showing a more pronounced benefit of safety on women's sleep quality are consistent with Simonelli et al.'s (2015) finding that home safety is associated with improved sleep quality for women but not for men. The authors interpret this finding as suggesting that women may have a higher sense of threat than men (Simonelli et al. 2015), which may be particularly pronounced in Latin America, where crime rates and violence against women are elevated (Gordon 2018). However, other work has found contrasting patterns. For example, participants from a South African neighborhood characterized by high crime rates found that fear for safety was associated with a higher likelihood of reporting poor sleep quality, and that this relationship was stronger in men (Correia et al. 2024).

The gendered pattern that we report here is important to interpret within its specific socioecological context and should be considered from the lens of intersecting vulnerabilities experienced by Indigenous migrant women in Mexico. Perceptions of security in Jalisco are low on a population level. By gender, 27.1% of adult men living in Jalisco reported feeling secure, compared to 19.5% of adult women living in Jalisco. While we were unable to locate data by urban versus rural locations, a 2024 survey found that women in all socioeconomic statuses in Jalisco reported lower perceived security than men (with men in the upper socioeconomic status reporting the highest perceived security of 30%), but that this gendered difference is most prominent in women of low socioeconomic status, of whom only 13.3% reported feeling secure (Instituto Nacional de Estadística y Geografía (INEGI) 2024; Zapopan, n.d.). Simonelli et al. (2015) further hypothesize that women may experience more pronounced changes in sleep quality related to safety due to a higher risk of depression and anxiety documented for women. Overall, these findings also highlight the ways that improved safety for migrant Indigenous women in Guadalajara could directly improve their health through sleep quality. We recommend more research that explicitly considers gender and safety in future sleep health studies, particularly in socioecological contexts outside of the Global North.

Our finding that men show significantly lower sleep quality measures associated with increased safety in the urban sample was unexpected and warrants further consideration. One possible explanation is that men in the urban location engage in social and recreational activities differently depending on their perceived safety. They may be more likely to engage in activities outside the house if they feel that (a) they are safer when spending time outside and/or returning home after dark or (b) when they feel that their homes are safe enough to leave their families at home alone during nighttime hours. These activities later in the evening and night may have detrimental effects on sleep quality. In support of this possible interpretation, a study of individuals living in the United States found that living in an inner city was associated with increased risk of short sleep duration, which was interpreted as possibly being related to more late‐night socializing in cities due to more social opportunities (Hale and Do 2007).

The second possibility is that housing safety is associated with increased costs, which could have differential effects on men's sleep quality. Men paying rent for more expensive, safer housing could have to work longer hours or additional jobs, or may have elevated economic worries that negatively impact their sleep. Number of jobs has a documented effect on sleep health, potentially through the disruption of healthy, consistent sleep schedules as well as an increase in stress and/or poor mental health, with evidence that immigrants who work two to three jobs report significantly worse sleep quality than those working a single job (Almeida et al. 2025). In the urban sample, both men and women typically engage in paid work. Women engage in paid artisan work in particular, in contrast to the rural sample where it is more common for women to engage solely in often unpaid domestic work. However, observations by members of our research team support the interpretation that migrant Wixárika men experience more work‐related stressors than women that may contribute to sleep disruption.

Ours is not the only study to find unexpected associations between perceived safety and sleep. For example, although Hill et al. (2016) generally found a relationship between safety and better sleep quality, when looking at each of the six countries included in their study individually, in their sample from India they found that greater perceived safety was associated with significantly greater odds of short sleep duration. While this specific finding was not explicitly interpreted by the authors, they did mention cultural differences as being potential explanatory factors that should be more fully considered in future work (Hill et al. 2016).

Subjective Versus Objective Sleep and Perceived Safety

We also assessed the odds of reporting poor subjective sleep quality and daytime dysfunction related to perceived safety. PNS was not significantly related to odds of reporting poor subjective sleep quality, but did near significance in the urban sample, where participants who feel safer in their neighborhood tend to have lower odds of reporting poor sleep. Although our actigraphy data do not generate sleep stage information, it is possible that perceived safety may be associated with deeper, more restorative sleep, leading to perceptions of having achieved a higher quality sleep.

Perceived safety was not associated with daytime dysfunction in either location. However, we found that mental health is a significant predictor of daytime dysfunction in the urban location, where participants who report poorer mental health have higher odds of reporting frequent problems staying alert while performing daily activities. These results are consistent with a number of studies reporting a link between depression and/or anxiety and sleepiness during the day, including evidence for an association between anxiety (Hasler et al. 2005) and depression (Chellappa and Araújo 2006) as predictors of daytime sleepiness, which may in part be related to the neurological bases for sleep processes and anxiety and depression (Alvaro et al. 2013).

Mental Health and Sleep

Mental health and stress are important factors to consider both in the urban and rural Wixárika contexts. For actigraphic sleep quality measures, better self‐rated mental health was associated with less WASO (i.e., fewer nighttime awakenings), higher sleep efficiency, and lower sleep fragmentation in the rural sample. Although not significant, better self‐rated mental health was associated at near‐significance with lower sleep efficiency in the urban location. The reason for the lack of association between mental health and actigraphy sleep variables in the urban location (and for variables approaching significance, patterns in the opposite direction) is unclear, but it is evident that mental health is a factor that plays a different role depending on location.

Indeed, the only significant predictor of either of our subjective sleep variables (sleep quality and daytime dysfunction) was mental health in the urban location, with poorer mental health predicting higher odds of reporting daytime dysfunction. This finding serves as a preliminary indicator of unique mental health stressors and needs in urban migrants. We hypothesize that there are numerous buffers and protective factors for mental health that are present in the rural location but lacking in the urban location, including stronger social support networks and access to culturally and/or spiritually relevant spaces. Profound disruptions in traditional social support networks, coupled with increased risk of experiencing discrimination and exclusion for Wixárika people living in urban settings, may exacerbate the adverse effects of poor mental health on other aspects of health, including daytime function and alertness.

A recent study reported that subjective sleep quality as measured by the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index does not predict objective, actigraphy‐measured sleep duration or quality, and that subjective sleep quality is strongly predicted by emotional state while objective measures are not (Windmill et al. 2024). The association between subjective sleep and emotional health should be considered in future qualitative and ethnographic work, particularly in populations experiencing elevated social stressors or marginalization. While a strength of our study is the combined use of subjective and objective sleep measures, given the common discrepancies between objective and subjective sleep markers, future work should also integrate objective health measures (e.g., stress and inflammatory biomarkers) into analyses of sleep and mental health in particular.

Limitations

One limitation of our findings is related to the nature of the self‐report data of the PNS. Our interpretation of the observed pattern that men show decreased sleep quality associated with higher PNS could be limited by men not accurately reporting their true perceptions of safety. Men may not have felt comfortable truly reporting if they felt unsafe in their homes or when out alone, in part related to cultural values surrounding expressions of masculinity. However, only men in the urban sample showed decreased sleep quality with higher PNS, and to our knowledge there is no reason to believe that men in the urban sample would be less likely than men in the rural sample to report true feelings of safety.

Second, the effect of migration history in the urban sample may have influenced the perceived environmental safety and the impact on sleep quality. The majority of urban participants were born in rural communities in the Sierra Madre Occidental mountains. The range of time spent living in the GMA was wide, with some having lived in the city for less than a year and others for more than 25 years. Thus, the experience of migration history was very heterogeneous and should be considered—especially through qualitative and/or mixed‐method work—in the future.

Third, our single‐item measure of mental health collapses anxiety and depression into a single variable, yet the experience of anxiety and depression may have profoundly different manifestations in sleep health measures (e.g., anxiety may be more likely to drive shorter or more disrupted sleep (Papadimitriou and Linkowski 2005), while some expressions of depression have been shown to be associated with longer or excessive sleep and insomnia, sometimes occurring in the same depressive bout (Zhang et al. 2022)). With our single, broad measure of mental health that includes a range of mental health experiences, the conclusions that we can draw about the relationship between mental health and sleep quality in this sample are limited.

Conclusion

This study analyzes how perceived safety relates to sleep in rural and urban groups of Wixárika people living in Jalisco, Mexico. Home and neighborhood safety are known to influence sleep duration and quality, and have been linked to the likelihood of reporting insomnia (Hill et al. 2016; Simonelli et al. 2015). Given the profound changes to the living environment (including safety) that occur with rural to urban migration and high rates of internal migration in Wixárika communities, sleep is an important consideration in the conversation about Indigenous and migrant health. This study importantly fills a gap in the anthropological sleep literature by reporting on sleep from groups living outside of the industrialized Global North. Furthermore, we report on objective, actigraphically generated sleep measures, which have been used less frequently than subjective survey measures. Thus, we advance understanding of sleep in diverse socioecological contexts as well as through the use of a wider range of methodologies.

As predicted, we found that the rural location is perceived as significantly safer than the urban location, suggesting that migrants moving to cities may experience declines in the safety of their living environments. The effect of perceived safety on sleep shows gendered effects, with women in Guadalajara showing the most benefit to their sleep associated with increased safety, which is an important insight into the sleep health of Indigenous Mexican women, who experience elevated rates of violence and intersecting vulnerabilities (Gamlin 2016).

Our results suggest that improvements in neighborhood and/or housing safety could be a promising area of intervention for urban Wixárika migrants. Social interventions have been identified as potential areas to increase perceptions of neighborhood satisfaction and security (DeSantis et al. 2016), as well as interventions that address factors such as noise levels in neighborhoods (Hale et al. 2010). However, addressing migrant sleep health is challenging and is situated in a complex, multi‐faceted context including precarious employment, disruptions to social support structures, and a lack of access to cultural and/or spiritual spaces (Crocker Sagastume et al. 2025). Addressing and improving sleep health will necessitate addressing similarly complex structural barriers to safe living environments. We advocate for policies and programs that promote access to culturally grounded sources of wellbeing, including spiritual spaces and Wixárika community networks in an effort to improve a range of health and wellbeing outcomes, including sleep.
