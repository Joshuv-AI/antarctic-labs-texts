The gut-brain axis in avian appetite regulation: Integrating peripheral signals with central neurocircuitry
Authors: Kimia Mahdavi, Morteza Zendehdel, Elham Ghashghaei, Hai Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Avian appetite relies on a specialized gut-brain signaling network.•Signal effects differ between central and peripheral administration.•Targeting gut-brain axis can improve poultry feed efficiency.Keywords: Appetite regulation, Gut-brain axis, Gut hormones, Hypothalamus, Neurotransmitters

Introduction

The regulation of energy homeostasis represents a fundamental biological process that demands precise integration of peripheral metabolic signals with central neural circuits (Brüning and Fenselau, 2023). In modern poultry production, such integration is critical for achieving rapid growth and improved feed efficiency, as disruption of energy balance predisposes high‑yield broilers to metabolic disorders such as ascites and sudden death syndrome (Hossain and Akter, 2022; Kalra et al., 2021.(

In mammals, the gut–brain axis (GBA) has been extensively characterized as a bidirectional communication network that orchestrates feeding behavior, nutrient partitioning, and overall energy balance through hormonal, neural, and microbial signaling (Sun et al., 2024). This mammalian model often emphasizes the role of long-term adiposity signals like leptin. However, avian species exhibit distinct anatomical and physiological adaptations, including a relatively simplified gustatory system, the absence of a functional T1R2 sweet taste receptor, and unique gastrointestinal morphology, which necessitate a species‑specific examination of appetite regulation that moves beyond a direct mammalian analog (Niknafs and Roura, 2018; Roura and Foster, 2018.(

In birds, the GBA functions as a sophisticated metabolic surveillance system in which signals arising from the gastrointestinal tract, pancreas, liver, adipose tissue, and gut microbiota are continuously monitored and relayed via endocrine and neural pathways to central command centers in the hypothalamus and brainstem, ultimately dictating meal initiation or termination (Blanco et al., 2021). Each component provides specialized signals: the gastrointestinal tract and pancreas release key peptides for rapid nutrient feedback (Saneyasu, 2024), while the liver monitors longer-term metabolic status, integrating nutrient status and releasing hepatic factors that communicate hepatic energy status to central appetite circuits (Fujita et al., 2017; Saffar et al., 2025(.

The gut microbiota further refines this axis by fermenting dietary components to generate short‑chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which stimulate the release of anorexigenic peptides such as glucagon‑like peptide‑1 (GLP‑1) and peptide YY (PYY) and interact with vagal afferents, thereby linking microbial activity to central satiety pathways (Rehman et al., 2007; Blanco et al., 2021). Environmental stressors, particularly heat stress, can disrupt this integrated system by increasing intestinal permeability, altering microbial composition, and inducing systemic inflammation, which together compromise feed intake and growth performance (Rostagno, 2020(.

At the central level, hypothalamic nuclei such as the arcuate nucleus (ARC), paraventricular nucleus (PVN), and ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH), together with the brainstem nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS), act as key integrators that translate peripheral hormonal and nutrient‑related signals into coordinated behavioral outputs through neurochemical effector pathways including neuropeptide Y/agouti-related peptide (NPY/AgRP) and pro-opiomelanocortin (POMC)-derived melanocortins (Boswell and Dunn, 2015; Honda, 2021). This integrated framework provides the conceptual basis for understanding appetite regulation in poultry. However, a comprehensive synthesis of how divergent peripheral signals, often exhibiting route dependent and context specific effects, are encoded and interact with central neurochemical circuits is still lacking. Therefore, this narrative review aims to: (1) systematically synthesize current knowledge on both peripheral and central mechanisms of the avian GBA, highlighting species specific complexities; and (2) link these mechanistic insights to practical strategies for enhancing feed efficiency, metabolic health, and resilience in commercial poultry production.

Peripheral signaling: Messages from the enteric system and associated organs

Sensing and taste perception

Avian species, despite having a relatively simple gustatory system, show fine discrimination for essential dietary components such as amino acids, fatty acids, and minerals (Niknafs and Roura, 2018). Notably, chickens lack a functional mammalian‑type sweet taste receptor T1R2 and instead rely on T1R2‑independent glucose‑sensing mechanisms in the gut, representing a major evolutionary divergence in early nutrient detection (Roura and Foster, 2018). This may reflect a shift towards post-ingestive, rather than pre-ingestive, evaluation of carbohydrate value. Luminal chemosensing is primarily mediated by enteroendocrine cells that couple the presence of nutrients to the rapid release of gut hormones, ensuring that the initial phase of feeding is tightly linked to post‑ingestive physiological responses (Mendez‑Hernandez et al., 2025). This architecture positions the upper gastrointestinal tract as the first peripheral "gatekeeper" that translates environmental nutrient availability into hormonal signals for the GBA.

Gut derived peptides with context dependent effects

A defining feature of the avian GBA is the profound context-dependency of several key gut-derived signals, particularly in their central versus peripheral effects. This suggests a sophisticated filtering mechanism where the route of signal arrival critically informs its central interpretation.

Ghrelin. Ghrelin is predominantly secreted from the proventriculus, with significant expression also detected in the duodenum and pancreas of birds, highlighting a broader gastrointestinal origin compared to mammals (Mahdavi et al., 2025). It is classically described as a potent orexigenic signal in mammals, acting via hypothalamic GHSR‑1a (Abdalla, 2015; Barrile et al., 2026). In birds, however, the effects of ghrelin are complex and highly context-dependent. Peripheral administration can produce variable orexigenic, anorexigenic, or null outcomes depending on species, strain, dose, and environmental conditions such as lighting (Kaiya et al., 2013). For instance, intraperitoneal (IP) injection of lower doses of rat ghrelin (0.4–0.9 nmol/100 g body weight) stimulated feeding in adult Japanese quail, whereas a higher dose (2.4 nmol/100 g body weight) suppressed it (Shousha et al., 2005). In chicks, intravenous (IV) injection of chicken ghrelin was found to suppress feeding (Geelissen et al., 2006), while other studies reported no effect (Kaiya et al., 2007). Plasma ghrelin concentration increases after food deprivation in chicks, similar to mammals (Kaiya et al., 2007). In striking contrast, central administration consistently induces robust hypophagia. This central anorexigenic effect is mediated through corticotropin‑releasing factor (CRF)‑dependent and monoaminergic pathways, including serotonergic (5‑HT2C), dopaminergic (D1), adrenergic, cannabinoidergic, opioidergic, and GABAergic systems (Saito et al., 2005; Zendehdel et al., 2013, 2020; Romero‑Picó et al., 2013; Farrokhi et al., 2021; Baghaeikia et al., 2022; Jonaidi et al., 2012). This central-peripheral dichotomy and context-dependency underscore a sophisticated filtering mechanism within the avian GBA, where the physiological meaning of a signal is defined by its point of entry and the metabolic state of the animal.

Peptide YY (PYY). Peptide YY shows a striking central–peripheral dichotomy in birds. Its highest mRNA expression is detected in the pancreas and jejunum rather than the large intestine (the major site in mammals), representing a fundamental shift in its primary production site within the avian GBA (Kewan et al., 2021; Aoki et al., 2017). Intravenous PYY acts as a potent anorexigenic factor, likely via NPY2 receptors, whereas central administration stimulates food intake, and in ovo PYY improves early post hatch growth and feed efficiency, probably by enhancing intestinal nutrient absorption (Aoki et al., 2017). This opposite action profile depending on compartment (blood vs. brain) underscores a unique signaling logic where the same peptide can convey different messages based on its point of entry into the regulatory network.

Somatostatin. Somatostatin is produced in the stomach, intestine, pancreas, and within the CNS, particularly in hypothalamic nuclei such as the ARC and PVN, exerts complex, state dependent effects on feeding in mammals, but in birds its central role is much more consistent. Intracerebroventricular (ICV) somatostatin reliably and potently stimulates food intake in both layer and broiler chicks via NPY1 receptors and downstream opioidergic and adrenergic pathways, establishing it as a clear central orexigenic signal in the avian GBA (Tachibana et al., 2009; Yousefvand et al., 2018; Mizrahi et al., 2021). The specific effect of peripheral somatostatin administration on avian food intake remains to be fully characterized.

Canonical satiety signals

Cholecystokinin (CCK). Cholecystokinin is released from the duodenum in response to dietary lipids and proteins and acts as a key short term satiety signal in birds (Wan et al., 2023). Peripheral CCK rapidly and dose dependently suppresses food intake via vagal afferents projecting to the nucleus of the solitary tract, while central CCKs also induce hypophagia through interactions with CRF, dopaminergic D1, and NMDA type glutamatergic receptors; moreover, CCK synergizes with leptin to couple meal related satiety with longer term energy status (Covasa and Forbes, 1994; Tachibana et al., 2012; Ebrahimnejad et al., 2025; Jelokhani et al., 2022). CCK thus represents a conserved, unambiguous stop signal acting through both neural and central pathways.

Proglucagon-derived peptides. The proglucagon gene, expressed primarily in the pancreas and intestinal l-cells, gives rise to a family of peptides including glucagon (pancreatic α-cells), GLP-1 (intestinal l-cells), GLP-2 (intestinal l-cells), and oxyntomodulin (intestine). These peptides collectively provide diversified feedback to central appetite circuits (Richards and McMurtry, 2008; Honda, 2016). In chicks, central administration of glucagon potently suppresses food intake and elevates plasma glucose via adrenergic and partially hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis-dependent mechanisms (Honda et al., 2007; Honda, 2016). While also anorexigenic, central GLP-1 acts via a distinct CRF-dependent pathway, lowering glycemia without elevating corticosterone (Furuse et al., 1997). Notably, peripheral administration (IP or IV) of GLP-1 also suppresses food intake in birds, such as in Japanese quail and broiler chicks, suggesting a dual role as both a central neurotransmitter and a peripheral satiety hormone (Shousha et al., 2007). Avian GLP-2 induces CRF-independent hypophagia, and similar to GLP-1, peripheral IV administration of chicken GLP-2 significantly reduces food intake, supporting its potential role as a postprandial satiety signal (Honda et al., 2015b, 2015c). Central administration of OXY suppresses feeding while increasing both glucose and corticosterone levels, an effect likely mediated via glucagon receptors (Honda et al., 2014aa). Importantly, the anorexigenic effect of OXY in chicks appears to be mediated through the glucagon receptor, a mechanism distinct from mammals where it acts via the GLP-1 receptor (Honda, 2016). Furthermore, a novel glucagon-like peptide (GCGL) and its receptor, found in birds but lost in mammals, have been identified in the chicken brain and also exert an anorexigenic effect following central administration (Wang et al., 2012; Honda et al., 2014b). This family exemplifies functional diversification and evolutionary innovation within avian appetite control systems.

Amylin (IAPP). Amylin, secreted from pancreatic β cells, acts as a potent satiety hormone in birds. Central and peripheral amylin dose dependently suppress food intake in broiler chicks and Japanese quail, mediated by activation of the area postrema and NTS, reduced activity in the lateral hypothalamus, and upregulation of calcitonin receptor complex components and POMC within the ARC, with genetic background modulating sensitivity between low and high body weight lines (Cline et al., 2008, 2010; Yuan et al., 2017(.

Serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine; 5-HT). Serotonin is a classical monoamine neurotransmitter and a peripheral satiety signal primarily synthesized and secreted by enterochromaffin cells (ECs) of the gastrointestinal mucosa (Lyte et al., 2022). Peripheral serotonin can influence central appetite circuits via vagal afferent pathways, particularly through 5-HT3 receptors on vagal terminals (Hwang and Oh, 2025). IP administration of gonadotropin-inhibitory hormone (GnIH) promotes feeding and weight gain in chickens, an effect that is significantly exacerbated by pharmacological blockade of the 5-HT2C receptor, indicating that peripheral serotonin acts via this receptor as a negative feedback regulator to modulate orexigenic signals (Song et al., 2024). At the central level, ICV injection of serotonin potently and dose-dependently suppresses food intake in both broiler and layer chicks (Rahmani et al., 2021; Zendehdel et al., 2017b). Long term metabolics

Insulin and insulin like growth factors (IGF 1, IGF 2). Insulin, a peptide hormone secreted primarily by the pancreatic β‑cells, functions as a potent central anorexigenic signal in birds rather than as a classic long‑term adiposity hormone. In broiler chicks, peripheral insulin administration does not alter feed intake, leading to the hypothesis that this may reflect a state of peripheral or central insulin resistance, potentially linked to the hyperinsulinemic phenotype of fast-growing lines (Liu et al., 2016). However, a definitive conclusion regarding the underlying mechanism requires further investigation. This contrasts sharply with the potent, dose-dependent hypophagia induced by central insulin administration in both layer and broiler chicks (Honda et al., 2007; Shiraishi et al., 2008). This hypophagic effect is mediated primarily via the hypothalamic melanocortin system, with upregulation of POMC‑derived alpha‑melanocyte‑stimulating hormone (α‑MSH) and downregulation of NPY, and is finely tuned by NPY receptor subtypes, D1- dopaminergic, and β2-adrenergic receptors (Shiraishi et al., 2008, 2011; Yousefvand et al., 2018, 2020; Zarei et al., 2025; Seyfi et al., 2026). This stark compartmentalization of action further reinforces the central theme of route-specific signal interpretation and functional divergence in the avian GBA.

The avian insulin like growth factors (IGF) system, while sharing general features with mammals, is now clearly implicated in appetite control. IGF-1 and IGF-2 are mainly produced in the liver (though also in other tissues), and their circulating levels are modulated by nutritionally regulated IGF-binding proteins (IGFBPs). Central administration of IGF-1 or IGF-2 potently suppresses food intake in broiler and layer chicks, respectively, effects linked to the naturally hyperglycemic state of birds and to hypothalamic Akt signaling (Honda et al., 2021; Fujita et al., 2019). Notably, peripheral administration also contributes to satiety: intravascular injection of IGF-1 significantly suppresses food intake in chicks (Fujita et al., 2017). This anorexigenic effect is supported by postprandial rises in plasma IGF-1 levels and nutritionally regulated hepatic IGF-1 mRNA expression, positioning IGF-1 as a peripheral satiety hormone that complements its central actions in the avian gut–brain axis (Fujita et al., 2017). Thus, the IGF system may serve a more direct and conserved role in coupling nutrient status to appetite in birds compared to the enigmatic leptin system.

Leptin. Although a leptin-like gene has been identified in chickens (Farkasová et al., 2016), its physiological role remains enigmatic and distinct from mammals. Early studies reported leptin expression in both liver and adipose tissue (Taouis et al., 1998; Ashwell et al., 1999), leading to the development of a chicken leptin RIA (Dridi et al., 2005). However, subsequent genomic analyses failed to confirm the classical leptin gene, and the 'avian leptin paradox' emerged, where plasma leptin levels do not correlate with adiposity as they do in mammals (Denbow et al., 2000; Honda, 2021). Peripheral leptin administration can reduce food intake in birds, but this effect is highly dependent on age, strain, and route of administration. For example, IP injection of recombinant chicken leptin significantly reduced food intake in 56-day-old layer chickens (by 38%) and 9-day-old layer chicks (by 15%), but had no significant effect in 9-day-old broiler chicks (Cassy et al., 2004). This suggests greater leptin sensitivity in older birds and slower-growing strains, potentially linked to developmental increases in brain leptin receptor expression (Cassy et al., 2004; Ohkubo et al., 2008). Hepatic leptin expression is modulated by hormones like insulin and nutritional state, aligning with the liver's central role in avian metabolism (Chuang et al., 2020). The discovery of a GC‑rich leptin gene with highest expression in the brain and pituitary, rather than adipose tissue, challenges its classical role as an adipostat (Seroussi et al., 2016). Collectively, the current evidence suggests that avian leptin likely functions not as a primary adiposity signal, as it does in mammals, but as a context-dependent metabolic modulator. Its activity appears to be closely linked to hepatic energy status and may be integrated with other liver-derived factors, such as IGF-1, to form a composite metabolic signal. This positions the liver, rather than adipose tissue, as the key energetic sentinel communicating with the avian brain.

Mechanistic studies show that leptin induced hypophagia in broilers is mediated via specific neurotransmitter systems, including adrenergic and glutamatergic circuits. Pharmacological blockade of β adrenergic receptors attenuates leptin’s anorexigenic effect, and antagonism of NMDA and AMPA receptors markedly reduces leptin induced hypophagia, implicating glutamate dependent JAK–STAT signaling within hypothalamic networks (Zendehdel et al., 2020; Adeli et al., 2019).

Liver expressed antimicrobial peptide 2 (LEAP2). LEAP2, derived mainly from the liver and gastrointestinal tract, acts as an endogenous antagonist/inverse agonist of the ghrelin receptor GHSR 1a and thereby counterbalances ghrelin’s actions. The dynamic ghrelin/LEAP2 ratio appears to function as a short term metabolic checkpoint that reflects immediate energy status (Holm et al., 2025; Perelló, 2025; Chen et al., 2026). Central administration of LEAP2 in broiler chickens induces robust, dose dependent hypophagia, and pharmacological studies indicate that this effect involves μ opioid receptors, serotonergic pathways, and cannabinoid CB1/CB2 receptors, with modulation by ghrelin receptor antagonism confirming functional interaction at the level of GHSR 1a (Saffar et al., 2025; Rahmania et al., 2026). Studies on the effect of peripheral LEAP2 administration on avian food intake are currently lacking. LEAP2 represents a key component of a liver‑centric short‑term energy sensing module, further emphasizing the hepatic hub in avian metabolism.

Additional peripheral modulators with central actions

The galanin family and the related peptide spexin. The galanin peptide family, comprising galanin (GAL) and galanin-like peptide (GALP, discussed later in the section on central neurocircuitry), along with the related peptide spexin, exhibits marked functional diversification in avian appetite regulation. GAL, a peptide produced both in the gastrointestinal tract and the CNS, acts as a potent central orexigenic signal; its ICV administration stimulates food intake in both layer and broiler chicks. This effect is mediated via interactions with adrenergic α2 receptors and opioid μ receptors, as co-injection with their antagonists (yohimbine and β-funalirexamine, respectively) attenuates the feeding response. The orexigenic response to GAL differs between layer and broiler chicks, highlighting genetic influences on neuropeptide sensitivity (Tachibana et al., 2008; Ando et al., 2000). The effect of peripheral GAL administration on feeding in birds is not well documented. In contrast, spexin (SPX) functions as a potent anorexigenic factor. In birds, spexin mRNA is robustly expressed not only in the CNS and pancreas but also along the gastrointestinal tract, including the proventriculus and small intestine, positioning it as a broader gut-derived signal. Peripheral SPX injection reduces feed intake, while central administration induces dose-dependent hypophagia via NPY1 and GalR3 receptors (Farzin et al., 2022). SPX activates GALR2/GALR2L receptors and MAPK/ERK signaling, and modulates hypothalamic neuropeptides by downregulating AgRP and MCH while upregulating CART expression (Meng et al., 2023). This receptor-specific dichotomy underscores the complex integration of these related peptides within the avian GBA.

Bombesin-like peptides. Bombesin-like peptides, including gastrin-releasing peptide (GRP, produced mainly in the gut and pancreas) and neuromedin B (NMB, expressed in the brain and gut), act as anorexigenic signals through bombesin receptor subtypes, such as BB2. Peripheral administration of bombesin suppresses feeding in young chickens and turkey poults (Denbow, 1989), suggesting a potential satiety role for the endogenous bombesin-like peptides via peripheral receptors. Central administration of bombesin or its related peptides (GRP/NMB) also suppresses food intake in chicks in a dose-dependent manner (Bungo et al., 2004) without elevating plasma corticosterone, indicating that their central satiety effects are direct and not secondary to generalized stress activation (Tachibana et al., 2010a). These peptides primarily signal through bombesin receptor subtypes, such as BB2.

Neuromedin U (NMU). Neuromedin U, despite sharing the "neuromedin" prefix with NMB, belongs to a separate peptide family. NMU is produced both in the gut and the brain and exerts powerful hypophagic actions that are tightly coupled to stress pathways: central NMU administration reduces feeding while simultaneously increasing plasma corticosterone and upregulating hypothalamic mRNA for corticotropin-releasing factor (CRF) and arginine vasotocin (AVT) (Kamisoyama et al., 2007). The anorexigenic effect of NMU is conserved across broiler and layer chicks (Honda et al., 2015aa). In chickens, NMU signals through specific G protein-coupled receptors, NMUR1 and NMUR2. NMUR1 is widely expressed in peripheral tissues like the gastrointestinal tract, while NMUR2 expression is predominantly central, with high levels in the hypothalamus (Wan et al., 2017). Central NMU administration also induces characteristic wing-flapping behavior in chicks, a stress-related response not typically associated with bombesin-like peptides (Honda et al., 2015aa). This distinct behavioral and neuroendocrine profile underscores NMU's unique role in tightly coupling satiety with stress responses within the avian GBA, a function that differentiates it from the bombesin-like peptide family.

Signals from non-gastrointestinal peripheral tissues. Several anorexigenic peptides are synthesized in peripheral tissues outside the classic gut-pancreas-liver axis. While their physiological roles as circulating hormones in appetite regulation remain largely unexplored in birds, central administration studies have revealed their potent anorexigenic effects, suggesting they may act as neuropeptides or reach central receptors to influence feeding.

•Epidermal growth factor (EGF), a key ligand of the evolutionarily conserved epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) system, is produced in multiple peripheral tissues of birds, including the ovary (Onagbesan et al., 1994), bone growth plate (Ren et al., 1997), and developing skin and feather follicles (Wang et al., 2025). Despite its well-characterized roles in peripheral growth and development, EGF also functions as a central anorexigenic signal. Central EGF administration dose dependently suppresses food intake, and pharmacological antagonism implicates dopaminergic D1 and adrenergic α2 receptors, revealing novel cross talk between growth factor signaling and classical monoaminergic appetite pathways (Jafari Ardakan et al., 2025). The effect of peripheral EGF administration on avian food intake has not been reported.

The gut microbiota axis and neural relays

Microbiota derived metabolites and barrier function. The gut microbiota constitutes a dynamic, bidirectional component of the avian GBA that affects host metabolism and appetite through microbial metabolites and immune modulation. Cecal microbes ferment dietary fibers into short‑chain fatty acids (SCFAs) such as butyrate, propionate, and acetate, which activate intestinal G protein coupled receptors and stimulate the release of anorexigenic peptides including GLP 1 and PYY, thereby linking microbial fermentation directly to central satiety pathways (Rehman et al., 2007; Blanco et al., 2021). SCFAs also enhance gut barrier integrity and exhibit anti-inflammatory properties, providing an additional route by which microbiota composition and activity can modulate systemic and central signals relevant to feeding behavior (Inan et al., 2000.(

Microbiota composition is shaped by diet, age, and host genetics and can be deliberately modulated via prebiotics, probiotics, postbiotics, and early life interventions. In ovo supplementation and microbial transplantation have been shown to promote beneficial taxa such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, improve gut development, and enhance metabolic resilience, suggesting that targeted manipulation of the microbiota in critical windows may program long term appetite regulation and feed efficiency in poultry (Gibson et al., 2004; Bednarczyk et al., 2016; Oliveira et al., 2014; Yan et al., 2017; Aguilar Toalá et al., 2018). Specifically, prebiotics such as galactooligosaccharides (GOS) and raffinose-family oligosaccharides (RFO) serve as fermentable substrates that selectively stimulate the growth of beneficial bacteria, enhance SCFA production, and strengthen intestinal barrier function (Gibson et al., 2017; Ricke, 2018). Notably, in ovo administration of prebiotics (e.g., at doses of 0.88–3.5 mg/embryo) has been shown to effectively modulate early microbial colonization, improve broiler performance, and prime gut–brain communication, offering a practical alternative to prolonged post‑hatch supplementation (Bednarczyk et al., 2016). These prebiotic-induced shifts in the microbiota not only support local gut health but also generate systemic metabolic and neural signals that influence central appetite circuits, highlighting the potential of targeted microbial intervention to fine-tune feed intake and energy homeostasis in poultry (Clavijo and Flórez, 2018; Pourabedin and Zhao, 2015).

Vagal and spinal afferent pathways. Transmission of gut derived information to the brain is mediated predominantly by the vagus nerve, which carries chemo and mechanosensory signals from gastrointestinal receptors to the NTS in the brainstem. Vagal afferents encode inputs such as gastric distension and CCK release, and NTS neurons integrate these signals before relaying them to hypothalamic centers including the ARC and PVN for incorporation into feeding decisions (Denbow, 1994; Schwartz, 2000; Blanco et al., 2021). Spinal afferents also contribute, particularly for nociceptive and some nutrient related signals, although their specific role in normal avian appetite regulation remains less defined than in mammals (Denbow, 1994). Vagal sensitivity itself shows circadian variation, with higher responsiveness during inactive phases, suggesting that temporal modulation of gut–brain signaling contributes to aligning feeding patterns with energy demands (Page, 2021).

Together, the microbiota, gut derived metabolites, and neural relays form an integrated peripheral interface through which environmental and nutritional stimuli are continuously translated into the central language of appetite regulating circuits.

Central neurocircuitry and effector pathways

The peripheral signals described above converge on central networks that compute energy status and generate coherent feeding behavior. These networks are organized primarily within the hypothalamus and brainstem and operate through tightly interconnected orexigenic and anorexigenic neurochemical systems. At the heart of this network, the ARC functions as the primary decoding hub, where often contradictory peripheral messages are integrated to formulate a coherent drive for or against feeding.

Core homeostatic hub: Arcuate nucleus (ARC)

Strategically positioned near the third ventricle, the ARC enables direct sensing of circulating metabolic signals and forms the primary homeostatic hub of appetite control in birds, as in mammals (Saneyasu, 2024). Within the ARC, two major neuronal populations function as antagonistic yet coordinated drivers of feeding behavior: the orexigenic neurons expressing NPY and AgRP, and the anorexigenic neurons expressing POMC and CART (Boswell and Dunn, 2015(.

NPY/AgRP neurons (orexigenic drive(. Neuropeptide Y (NPY) is the most potent central orexigenic signal identified in avian species; its hypothalamic mRNA expression increases sharply during fasting and rapidly falls with refeeding, underscoring its role as a sensitive detector of energy deficit (Kewan et al., 2021; Saneyasu et al., 2011). Central NPY administration robustly and dose dependently stimulates food intake, primarily via NPY1 receptors in downstream sites such as the PVN, and NPY receptors serve as critical integrators for multiple other modulators, including adiponectin, phoenixin 14, and gastric inhibitory polypeptide (GIP) (Yousefvand and Hamidi, 2020; Mahdavi et al., 2024; Gharaie et al., 2026(.

Agouti related peptide (AgRP), co expressed with NPY, acts as an endogenous antagonist of melanocortin 3 and 4 receptors (MC3R and MC4R) and provides a tonic brake on melanocortin mediated satiety (Boswell and Dunn, 2017). AgRP expression increases during fasting and chronic food restriction and remains elevated during stress or incubation related anorexia, suggesting that it contributes to longer term metabolic adaptations beyond acute meal to meal control (Dunn et al., 2013, 2015; Lindskoog et al., 2020(.

POMC/CART neurons (anorexigenic brake). POMC neurons produce melanocortin peptides such as α‑melanocyte‑stimulating hormone (α‑MSH), which consistently suppress food intake when administered centrally in chicks, firmly establishing the melanocortin pathway as a primary satiety system in birds (Kawakami et al., 2000; Honda et al., 2012; Carr, 2026). Hypothalamic POMC expression is nutritionally regulated and serves as a key mediator of peripheral hormones including insulin and leptin, although strain specific differences in responsiveness to different MSH isoforms have been documented (Shiraishi et al., 2008; Boswell and Dunn, 2015).

Cocaine and amphetamine regulated transcript (CART), expressed in the ARC and PVN, is another critical anorexigenic neuropeptide that potently inhibits food intake and counteracts NPY induced hyperphagia (Tachibana et al., 2003). Its expression is upregulated by leptin and CCK, positioning CART as an important integrative node linking peripheral satiety signals with central melanocortin circuits (Akieda Asai et al., 2014; Kristensen et al., 1998).

Downstream command centers: PVN, VMH, and NTS

Once processed in the ARC, metabolic information is relayed to higher‑order hypothalamic command nuclei (PVN, VMH) that orchestrate behavioral and autonomic outputs. In parallel, visceral signals are integrated in the brainstem NTS, which then communicates with hypothalamic centers to link acute satiation with homeostatic and hedonic control of feeding. Paraventricular nucleus (PVN). The PVN functions as a principal second order integration hub, receiving dense projections from ARC NPY/AgRP and POMC/CART neurons and integrating direct inputs from peripheral hormones such as leptin and insulin (Yousefvand et al., 2020; Sutton et al., 2016). The hypophagic effects of leptin and insulin are largely mediated in the PVN via activation of MC4R and modulation of NPY1 receptors, placing the PVN at the crossroads of melanocortin and NPY signaling (Ahima et al., 2000; Shiraishi et al., 2008; Yousefvand et al., 2018(.

Ventromedial hypothalamus (VMH(. The VMH has long been considered a “satiety center” and acts as a dynamic computational node balancing orexigenic and anorexigenic inputs. It integrates excitatory signals from NPY, orexin, and endocannabinoids via receptors such as NPY1/NPY5, OX1R, and CB1R, with inhibitory inputs from α MSH, CRF, leptin, insulin, and dopamine acting through MC4R, CRF2, leptin receptors, insulin receptors, and D1 receptors (Sánchez Lasheras et al., 2010; Bellocchio et al., 2013; Hillebrand et al., 2005; Ramos Lobo and Donato, 2017; Chao et al., 2012; Passani et al., 2011; Iigaya et al., 2019). This convergence allows the VMH to couple energy status with broader behaviors such as arousal, stress responsiveness, and thermoregulation.

Nucleus of the solitary tract (NTS). The NTS in the brainstem serves as the primary relay station for viscerosensory information conveyed by the vagus nerve. It integrates signals related to gastric distension, nutrient content, and gut hormones such as CCK and amylin, and projects to hypothalamic nuclei including the ARC and PVN as well as limbic forebrain regions that govern motivated aspects of feeding (Denbow, 1994; Schwartz, 2000; Blanco et al., 2021; Cline et al., 2008). Through these connections, the NTS links acute satiation and visceral feedback with homeostatic and hedonic control of intake. The NTS is crucial for processing unambiguous peripheral 'stop' signals like CCK, providing a fast neural pathway to inhibit feeding independently of slower hormonal feedback to the ARC (Chen et al., 2020; Schwartz, 2000).

Integrated control of feeding behavior

Overall feeding behavior in birds arises from a dynamic, species-specific equilibrium between central orexigenic and anorexigenic neurochemical pathways (Boswell and Dunn, 2015). This core balance, centered on the antagonistic actions of hypothalamic NPY/AgRP and POMC/CART neurons, is continuously and precisely modulated by the integration of a diverse array of peripheral signals discussed throughout this review (Honda, 2021). We propose that the avian GBA employs a prioritized processing strategy: rapid, unambiguous signals (e.g., CCK) take a direct route via the brainstem to quickly inhibit feeding, while more complex, state-dependent signals (e.g., ghrelin, PYY, leptin) are integrated in the hypothalamic ARC, where their meaning is interpreted based on physiological context and route of arrival.

Simultaneously, visceral feedback is relayed to the central nervous system via neural pathways. Gut derived signals related to gastric distension and nutrient induced hormone release (e.g., CCK, amylin) are transmitted via vagal afferents to the NTS in the brainstem. The NTS integrates this information and projects it to higher hypothalamic command centers such as the PVN and VMH, thereby linking acute satiation with homeostatic feeding decisions (Denbow, 1994; Schwartz, 2000; Blanco et al., 2021). This dual‑pathway architecture (fast neural vs. slower hormonal) allows for both reflexive meal termination and adaptive, longer‑term adjustments.

The gut microbiota adds another critical layer of regulation. Microbial fermentation of dietary components produces SCFAs, which stimulate the release of gut satiety peptides (e.g., GLP-1, PYY) and enhance intestinal barrier function. Through these endocrine and immune modulatory effects, the microbiota generates systemic signals that influence central appetite circuits, effectively linking microbial activity to host feeding behavior (Rehman et al., 2007; Blanco et al., 2021).

Environmental stressors, particularly heat stress, can disrupt this integrated axis by increasing intestinal permeability, altering microbial composition, and activating the HPA axis. These perturbations collectively compromise feed intake and growth performance (Rostagno, 2020). This vulnerability highlights the axis's role as an interface between the external environment and internal metabolic set points, and underscores how the state‑dependent interpretation of signals can be maladaptively shifted under stress.

In commercial poultry production, all strategies to influence feed intake, including nutritional formulation, genetic selection, and microbiota management, ultimately converge on this shared gut brain axis circuitry. A deeper, mechanistic understanding of the prioritized and state‑modulated processing model of how peripheral signals are encoded and interact with central neurochemical circuits is therefore essential. Such knowledge provides the foundation for developing targeted interventions to optimize feed efficiency, mitigate metabolic disorders like ascites, and enhance overall bird welfare and resilience in modern poultry production systems (Kalra et al., 2021). Interventions should consider not just the presence of a signal, but the physiological context that determines its final interpretation by the central nervous system.

Conclusion

The avian GBA functions as a sophisticated, specialized bidirectional network, integrating peripheral metabolic, hormonal, and microbial signals with central neural circuits to precisely regulate feed intake and energy homeostasis. As reviewed, peripheral signals often exhibit pronounced route‑dependent (central versus peripheral) and context‑specific effects, which we interpret not as inconsistencies, but as features of a sophisticated, state‑modulated control system. Central to this regulation is the dynamic interplay between hypothalamic orexigenic and anorexigenic systems, which are modulated by a wide array of neurotransmitters and neuromodulators and fine‑tuned by diverse peripheral inputs. As delineated in our model, we propose that the avian GBA is optimized for rapid adaptation to fluctuating nutrient availability and prioritizes short-term, liver-centric energy sensing over the long-term adiposity-based regulation dominant in mammals.

This integrated framework and the proposed model offer significant translational potential for poultry science. By strategically targeting key nodes within this axis with an appreciation for signal context and route, through dietary formulation, microbiota modulation, environmental management, or selective breeding, it becomes possible to develop more precise tools to enhance feed efficiency, mitigate metabolic disorders such as ascites, and improve overall bird welfare.

Realizing this potential fully requires focused future research to resolve critical knowledge gaps, particularly in avian leptin physiology, receptor-specific signaling, and causal microbiota-brain communication. Crucially, future studies must test the integrated, state-dependent model proposed here, moving beyond descriptive phenomenology to a predictive understanding of avian appetite control. Crucially, research must move beyond descriptive pharmacology to test integrated models of how signal context shapes feeding outcomes. A multidisciplinary approach that bridges advanced neurobiology and applied poultry research will be essential to develop innovative, sustainable strategies that optimize both productivity and metabolic health in commercial poultry production.
