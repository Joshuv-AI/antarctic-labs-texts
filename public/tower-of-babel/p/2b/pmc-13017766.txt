Cortical alpha changes during visuospatial attention: a deep learning–enriched EEG analysis
Authors: Elisa Magosso, Davide Borra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCovert visuospatial attention in anticipation of a stimulus is known to topographically modulate alpha-band (8 to 14 Hz) brain activity. However, the specific cortical regions involved remain unclear. Here, we conducted a whole-cortex analysis of alpha-band changes by examining source-level electroencephalographic (EEG) signals during a cued visuospatial attention task via a novel approach integrating conventional alpha power analysis with a deep learning technique based on an interpretable convolutional neural network (CNN). Conventional metrics contrasting alpha power between leftward and rightward attention suggested a robust, selective involvement of the left parietal lobe (superior and inferior parietal cortices) and a broader involvement of right hemisphere regions with less parietal contribution. The CNN-based approach, which discriminated the attention direction from the source-level EEG signals and identified the most discriminative regions in alpha band, refined these findings, corroborating the dominant role of the left parietal lobe and limited involvement of the right parietal lobe restricted to the supramarginal gyrus. The obtained findings are interpreted in terms of a more tonic engagement (disinhibition) of right parietal lobe, leaving less dynamic range for condition-dependent alpha modulation. This study not only improves the characterization of alpha-band attention-related changes but also presents a novel combined approach to investigate brain oscillations.Keywords: anticipatory visuospatial attention, alpha power, EEG source reconstruction, interpretable convolutional neural network, parietal cortex

Introduction

Covert visuospatial attention refers to the human ability of voluntarily orienting attention to a specific location in the visual space without moving the eyes or head, and it is essential for normal visual function. Deployment of covert visuospatial attention allows the selection and prioritization of relevant visual information that is not currently under fixation, such as peripheral items in the visual field, and leads to improved visual stimuli processing at the attended locations relative to ignored locations (see for reviews Posner et al. 1980; Carrasco 2011).

Concerning the neural implementation, a large body of evidence—mainly based on electroencephalographic (EEG) and magnetoencephalographic (MEG) studies—supports a functional link between brain oscillations, in particular in the alpha band (8 to 14 Hz), and mechanisms of selective attention (Klimesch 1999; Clayton et al. 2015; Frey et al. 2015). Attention-driven changes of alpha power in brain regions have been observed across a variety of attention domains, eg spatial attention, temporal attention, feature-based attention, and internal attention, and across different sensory modalities, eg visual, auditory, and somatosensory (see for reviews Clayton et al. 2015; Frey et al. 2015; van Diepen et al. 2016). The dominant view is that alpha oscillations act as a general mechanism to support and direct attention, by selectively implementing inhibitory and disinhibitory influences over cortical activities (Klimesch et al. 2007; Jensen and Mazaheri 2010; Mathewson et al. 2011; Frey et al. 2015; van Diepen et al. 2016). In particular, an increase in alpha power has been widely observed in cortical regions devoted to processing “unattended” or “to-be-ignored” signals and is associated with an increase in inhibition of the corresponding neural circuits. Vice versa, alpha power decrease has been associated with release of inhibition and enhancement of cortical excitability in the neural circuits responsible for processing the attended signals.

In the domain of covert visuospatial attention, oscillatory alpha-band activity over posterior sites has been consistently found to be topographically modulated according to the direction of attention. When visuospatial attention is covertly oriented to one hemifield, alpha power over parietal and occipital areas shows lateralization, resulting from an increase in the hemisphere ipsilateral to the attended side and/or a decrease in the contralateral hemisphere (Worden et al. 2000; Yamagishi et al. 2005; Kelly et al. 2006; Thut et al. 2006; Rihs et al. 2007; Doesburg et al. 2009; Gould et al. 2011; Händel et al. 2011; Liu et al. 2016; Sokoliuk et al. 2019). Such alpha power adjustments are typically observed in attentional cueing paradigms, after the presentation of the central cue indicating the “to-be-attended-location” and before the onset of the peripheral target/distractor stimuli. These modulations have been reported both in early time windows after the cue presentation (around 400 to 800 ms postcue, early shifting of attention) (Rihs et al. 2009; Doesburg et al. 2016) and in late (>800 ms) and longer intervals lasting up to several seconds postcue and extending until the target presentation (later sustained attention) (Kelly et al. 2006; Thut et al. 2006; Rihs et al. 2009). In line with the general view on alpha activity, these anticipatory alpha-power modifications are assumed to enhance the neural responses to impending stimuli at attended locations and to suppress the neural responses to possible imminent distractors at unattended locations. Interestingly, aberrant modulations of posterior alpha power during visuospatial attention are observed in attention-related disorders, such as Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) (ter Huurne et al. 2013; Vollebregt et al. 2016), and in patients with spatial neglect after stroke (Lasaponara et al. 2019).

Although it is well established that covert attention toward a location in space produces topographically organized modifications of posterior alpha power, the identification of the specific parietal and/or occipital regions primarily involved in these alpha-band changes is scarcely explored. The majority of studies, indeed, examined the alpha-band EEG (or MEG) correlates of visuospatial attention at the scalp level (Worden et al. 2000; Kelly et al. 2006; Thut et al. 2006; Rihs et al. 2007; Gould et al. 2011; Händel et al. 2011; Gallotto et al. 2020), consistently showing involvement of posterior scalp sites. Nevertheless, they do not reveal how the underlying regions in the parietal and occipital cortices—and more generally across the whole cortex—are differentially implicated in alpha-band changes. Some works combined EEG with repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) (Capotosto et al. 2012; Capotosto et al. 2017) or used simultaneous EEG and functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI) (Liu et al. 2016; Green et al. 2017) to assess the role of specific regions in the modulation of scalp occipitoparietal alpha activity during cued visuospatial attention tasks. These combined EEG-rTMS and EEG-fMRI studies are highly valuable for highlighting top–down attention-related influences from higher-order parietal and frontal regions (or influences from subcortical structures) to occipitoparietal alpha activity; however, they do not elucidate how the alpha power adjustments vary spatially across the different cortical regions. Other studies adopted techniques of EEG-/MEG-source reconstruction to image and analyze alpha-band power changes at cortical level during covert visuospatial attention (Yamagishi et al. 2003; Yamagishi et al. 2005; Siegel et al. 2008; Doesburg et al. 2009; Doesburg et al. 2016; Sokoliuk et al. 2019). However, most of them limited the analysis to a selection of a few cortical regions based on a priori predictions or earlier results, thus not performing a quantitative evaluation of the alpha power adaptations across all cortical regions.

Due to the insufficient description at whole-cortex level, some important questions remain open. Are low-level sensory regions in the occipital lobe or rather associative regions in the parietal lobe more robustly implicated in alpha power tunings associated with visuospatial attention shifts? How are the other regions, in the frontal and temporal lobes, involved in alpha-band changes? Do the two hemispheres exhibit a symmetric behavior, with homologous regions in the left and right hemisphere emerging as the most significantly implicated, or rather do hemispheric asymmetries exist?

A comprehensive investigation of these issues may benefit from the application of recent techniques of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI). Indeed, artificial intelligence techniques (such as deep learning) can be designed to automatically discriminate neural input data into classes, without performing a priori selection (in time, space, or frequency) within the data; from the neural data, these approaches directly learn features that are the most informative for discriminating brain states (for example, leftward vs. rightward attention) in a data-driven manner. By making these techniques also explainable, the abstract and often opaque nature of the learned features is alleviated, enabling their interpretation in neurophysiological terms (eg in terms of involved neural oscillations and brain regions). XAI approaches can be broadly classified into 2 categories: intrinsically interpretable models, in which interpretability is inherent to the model design and the learned features are directly interpretable, and post hoc explanation methods, in which explanations of the model decision toward the output brain states are obtained by applying algorithms that identify the traits of the input or internal representations strongly influencing the output (Retzlaff et al. 2024). XAI approaches can supplement traditional analytical methods to enrich and enhance the investigation of the neural mechanisms underlying specific brain states, and may even offer increased sensitivity to distributed, task-related neural patterns compared to conventional methods.

The goal of the present study is to characterize alpha-band modifications related to covert direction of visuospatial attention, performing a whole-cortex analysis via EEG source-level signals, by applying a combined approach that integrates conventional analysis with an XAI technique. This work is intended to provide a manifold contribution. On one hand, it aspires to contribute to a more complete characterization of cortical alpha-band changes associated with anticipatory visuospatial attention shifts, helping to fill the existing gap and to address the previous questions. On the other hand, this study intends to demonstrate the added value of an XAI method for the study of visuospatial attention, moving beyond the standard black-box nature of data-driven approaches and also offering a methodological framework with potential general applicability to the investigation of other cognitive brain states. To achieve these aims, EEG signals acquired during a cued visuospatial attention task—in which participants covertly shifted and maintained attention to either the left or right hemifield—were projected to the source space, estimating the activity at cortical regions spanning the entire cortex. The so-obtained source-level signals were analyzed in a meaningful time interval separating the presentation of the stimuli (target/distractors) from the preceding instructive cue and mostly capturing alpha modulatory effects induced by attention direction, in order to identify the key regions engaged in the alpha-band changes. Crucially, we adopted an innovative analysis strategy that combines conventional spectral analysis with an XAI approach based on deep learning. The conventional spectral analysis quantified the alpha power across the cortical regions and identified the regions showing significant differences between the leftward and rightward attention conditions by using metrics commonly used in the literature. The XAI approach consisted of a deep learning technique based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs). In the last decade, CNNs have gained significant attention in neuroscience, showing promise in EEG decoding and classification, thanks to their ability to handle multidimensional time-series EEG data and to automatically learn the optimal features for discriminating between different cognitive or motor conditions (Craik et al. 2019). However, their typical black-box nature, stemming from highly layered architectures and a vast number of parameters, hampers the interpretability of the underlying features that drive network decisions. Here, we employed an inherently interpretable CNN that we previously developed and applied to EEG motor classification (Borra et al. 2020), characterized by a shallow design and incorporating interpretability in the architecture itself. Specifically, the CNN enables a direct interpretation of the spectral features (bands of frequencies) and spatial features (brain regions) that drive classification. In this study, the interpretable CNN was trained to discriminate between the leftward and rightward attention conditions based on the cortical EEG signals. While the application of deep learning models to the decoding of visuospatial attention states has already been addressed in prior studies, the previous decoders provided no or limited feature interpretability (Farahat et al. 2019; Kaushik et al. 2022; Chugh and Aggarwal 2024; Bajool and Seeber 2025). In contrast, as a key point of novelty, the present study leverages an inherently interpretable deep learning architecture, linking classification performance to neurophysiologically meaningful spectral and spatial features. In this approach, by analyzing the learned interpretable features in the spectral domain and in the spatial cortical domain, we identified which cortical regions across the entire cortex contributed more, as to their alpha-band activity, in discriminating between the 2 attention conditions. Then, the results obtained using the conventional approach and the CNN-based approach were integrated by examining the convergence of their findings, and the cortical regions that were consistently identified across both approaches were considered the most robustly implicated in alpha power modifications associated with anticipatory direction of covert visuospatial attention (key regions). Thus, the adopted deep learning approach, going beyond a purely performance-driven decoder by explicitly addressing feature interpretability, served as an interpretable, complementary tool that reinforced and refined the findings obtained from the conventional analysis.

Overall, we expected our analysis to reveal distinct alpha-band activity modifications across lobes and hemispheres associated with anticipatory visuospatial attention, contributing to a clearer depiction of attention-related alpha-band mechanisms, and at the same time to showcase how a combined approach—integrating conventional methods with interpretable deep learning—may empower EEG analysis, contributing to methodological advances in brain oscillations investigation.

Materials and methods

Participants

Thirteen healthy volunteers (10 male, 1 left handed, age: M = 24 years, SD = 2.38 years) participated in this study. Similar sample sizes were used in many previous EEG/MEG studies investigating alpha brain oscillations in covert visuospatial attention tasks (Worden et al. 2000; Yamagishi et al. 2003; Yamagishi et al. 2005; Kelly et al. 2006; Thut et al. 2006; Rihs et al. 2007; Siegel et al. 2008; Yamagishi et al. 2008; Doesburg et al. 2009; Gould et al. 2011; Händel et al. 2011). All participants had normal or corrected-to-normal vision and no history of neurological disorders. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Bioethics Committee of the University of Bologna (protocol number 29146, year 2019). Written informed consent was provided by each participant before the beginning of the experiment, and all data were analyzed and reported anonymously.

Experimental protocol and EEG acquisition

The participants were seated in a quite dark room in front of a 27-inch monitor placed at a distance of approximately 50 cm and performed a cued visuospatial attention task (see Fig. 1a). During the task, black visual stimuli were presented on the monitor against a gray background. Throughout the entire task, the participants were instructed to maintain fixation on a cross placed centrally. Each trial started with the brief presentation (100 ms) of an arrow cue (directional cue), appearing just below the fixation cross, pointing either to the left or right, indicating that the participant was to covertly direct attention to the box located either in the lower-left or lower-right visual field. The 2 arrow cues were equiprobable. The cue was followed by an interval of random duration (2,100 to 2,500 ms, “cue–probe interval”), after which the probe was presented, consisting in the brief presentation (100 ms) of a symbol (“+” or “x”) either in only 1 box or in both boxes (8 possible probe configurations, each equiprobable). The trial ended with an interval of random duration (2,000 to 2,500 ms, “post-probe interval”), during which the participants were instructed to press a keyboard key with the right hand only if they detected a target configuration, that is only if they detected the “+” symbol in the cued location (see Fig. 1b). Participants were asked to respond as accurately and rapidly as possible, minimizing head and limb movements.

A central fixation cross and 2 lower lateralized squares, serving as position markers for directing attention, were continuously displayed on the monitor. This paradigm was used in previous works (Doesburg et al. 2008; Doesburg et al. 2016) and originally adapted from an earlier paradigm (Worden et al. 2000). It was adopted here as it aimed at maximizing the attention orienting to the cued location, since the participants never had to respond to stimuli appearing at the noncued location, but rather, they had to suppress their possible interference. It is worth noticing that although our paradigm was inspired by some previous works (Doesburg et al. 2008; Doesburg et al. 2016), here, we adopted a cue–probe interval having a longer duration (2.1 to 2.5 s) than in those previous studies (1 to 1.2 s), which primarily aimed at investigating oscillatory brain modulation in an early postcue phase (300 to 800 ms, early shifting of attention). Indeed, other works (Kelly et al. 2006; Thut et al. 2006; Rihs et al. 2009; Wang et al. 2016) have successfully employed longer cue–target intervals (2 s or even longer) to investigate visuospatial attention, showing that alpha lateralization can occur over more prolonged time periods. Thus, our goal was to investigate alpha-band modulation during a more extended anticipatory interval (see also the Time–Frequency Wavelet Decomposition and Preliminary Time-Resolved Alpha Power analysis section in Materials and Methods and the Preliminary Time-Resolved Alpha Power Analysis section in Results, for a preliminary assessment of the cue–probe interval as to the phenomenon of interest).

The presentation of all visual stimuli was controlled by a custom MATLAB script (version R2023a, The Mathworks Inc., Natick, MA, United States) that also served to collect the keyboard responses for assessing response accuracy and reaction time.

The trials were acquired in 10 blocks, separated by a break of about 2 min, to reduce participants’ fatigue. Each block included 80 trials (40 trials for each directional cue, with 5 trials for each of the 8 probe configurations), resulting in a total of 400 trials per directional cue. The presentation order of both the directional cue and probe configuration was chosen pseudo-randomly in each block.

During each block, EEG was recorded continuously using the g.HIAMP80 system (g.tec Medical Engineering GmbH, Schiedlberg, UA, Austria) from 60 active scalp electrodes (g.Scarabeo) placed according to the 10/10 system. An electrode placed on the right earlobe was used as a reference, and the ground electrode was placed in AFz. The sampling rate was 512 Hz, and a notch filter (stopband 48 to 52 Hz) was applied during the recording. Electrode impedance was kept below 50 kΩ.

Our EEG analysis focused on the cue–probe interval, during which the participants covertly oriented and maintained the attention toward the left or right visual space, and we contrasted the 2 conditions (in the following denoted as “Left Cue” and “Right Cue”) using a conventional approach and a CNN-based approach, in order to investigate the alpha-band changes between leftward and rightward anticipatory attention at EEG-source level.

Behavioral data

Participants were instructed to press the response key at the probe presentation only in case the symbol “+” appeared in the cued location (target trials, ie trials with target configurations of the probe, see Fig. 1b). This circumstance occurred in 30 out of 80 trials per block—15 occurrences for each cue direction per block—overall resulting in 300 target trials out of 800 total trials across the entire experiment. Because of a technical problem with the keyboard used for collecting the responses, the behavioral responses were not recorded in 3 blocks for 2 subjects, in 2 blocks for 1 subject and in 1 block for 3 subjects (that is, we missed the responses in 11 out of 130 blocks, overall).

To assess behavioral performance, we computed the mean reaction time (RT) based on the correct responses to the target trials and the balanced accuracy (bACC). The latter was computed as the mean between sensitivity (proportion of correctly detected target trials) and specificity (proportion of correctly rejected nontarget trials). This metric provides an unbiased estimate of performance when the 2 classes (target and nontarget trials) are unequally represented, as occurring in our experiment. These performance metrics were obtained over the entire experiment, ie collapsing the responses across all blocks, to verify that the participants were engaged in the task and allocated covert attention toward the cued hemifield. We also tested for differences between the 2 attention conditions, Left Cue and Right Cue, for balanced accuracy and RT separately, using a 2-tailed permutation test based on the t-statistic, and the P-values were corrected for multiple comparisons (2 comparisons) using false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995; Benjamini and Yekutieli 2001). For each metric, the permutation test was performed on the 13 participant-level differences between the 2 attention conditions, by randomly swapping the Left Cue and Right Cue labels for each participant (which is equivalent to randomly inverting the sign of the within-subject difference), and a total of 5,000 random permutations were replicated.

E‌EG preprocessing and cortical source estimation

The preprocessing pipeline was applied offline to the EEG signals of each participant and was consistent with our earlier studies investigating cortical power in other cognitive and motor tasks (Borra et al. 2023b; Magosso and Borra 2024). It consisted of the following steps: (i) linear detrending, applied to the EEG signals of each block; (ii) band-pass filtering between 1 and 60 Hz and notch filtering at 50 Hz, applied to the EEG signals of each block. Notch filtering was also adopted offline to remove the residual power line interference; (iii) identification of bad channels in each block using the random sample consensus method (Fischler and Bolles 1981); (iv) concatenation of the EEG signals across all blocks; (v) removal of channels that were labeled as bad at least in 1 recording block (step iii) from the concatenated signals. On average, 3.3 channels per subject (standard deviation, SD = 3) were removed; (vi) removal of artifacts via independent component analysis (ICA). ICA was computed using the extended Infomax algorithm (Lee et al. 1999) applied to the concatenated signals (after excluding bad channels). The time pattern, power spectral density, and scalp topological map of each IC were visually inspected, and ICs likely having artifact origin were eliminated. On average, 12.9 ICs per subject (SD = 3.9) were removed. The eliminated ICs were identified as mainly corresponding to eye blinking, EMG artifacts likely due to contraction of cranium, faces and neck muscles, cardiac artifacts, and single-channel noise. The relatively high number of removed ICs reflects the substantial length of the concatenated signals subjected to ICA, approximately lasting 4,400 s (about 73 min) in each participant; (vii) spherical spline interpolation of the bad channels removed in step (v); (viii) common average re-referencing; and (ix) epoching into variable-length epochs, with each epoch starting 0.5 s before the cue onset and ending 0.5 s after the probe onset; this way, the epochs included the entire cue–probe interval, with variable duration between 2.1 and 2.5 s, thus having an overall variable length between 3.1 and 3.5 s.

At the end of the preprocessing stage, for each participant, 400 epochs per condition (Left Cue and Right Cue, respectively) were obtained, with 60 scalp EEG signals per epoch.

For each participant and each epoch, the activity at the cortex level was estimated starting from the 60 scalp electrode signals, using the Brainstorm Matlab Toolbox (version November 2024, Tadel et al. 2011). We used the standard realistic head anatomy MNI ICBM 152 distributed with Brainstorm. The source space was restricted to the cortex and discretized into 15,002 vertices. The forward problem was solved using the boundary element method with Brainstorm default parameters. The inverse problem was solved using sLORETA (Pascual-Marqui 2002) with an identity noise covariance matrix and source current dipoles constrained to be perpendicular to the cortex. Therefore, each cortical vertex was associated with 1 source signal (overall, 15,002 signals).

The cortical vertices were then grouped into 68 regions of interest (ROIs, 34 per hemisphere) according to the Desikan–Killiany Atlas (Desikan et al. 2006). The Desikan–Killiany ROIs are represented in Fig. 2 and listed in Table 1. The source signal representative of each ROI was computed by averaging the signals of all vertices belonging to that ROI. To prevent signal cancellation in case of many vertices within the ROI having dipole orientations in opposite directions, the signs of source signals that were not oriented as the ROI’s “dominant direction” were flipped before averaging (Ghumare et al. 2018). The ROI’s dominant direction was computed as the first principal direction of the orientations of the dipoles belonging to the ROI.

At the end of this stage, for each participant, 400 variable-length epochs per condition (Left Cue and Right Cue, respectively) were obtained, with 68 cortical signals per epoch (1 signal per ROI). As described in the following, a preliminary analysis was applied to the signals of the variable-length epochs to identify which alignment, if cue-locked or probe-locked, mostly captured the alpha-band changes between the 2 attention conditions.

Time–frequency wavelet decomposition and preliminary time-resolved alpha power analysis

Time-resolved alpha power

For each participant, continuous wavelet transform using the Morlet wavelet as a mother wavelet was applied to the ROI signals downsampled to 128 Hz, epoch by epoch. The resulting wavelet coefficients were squared to obtain time–frequency power estimates for each ROI in each epoch. The alpha power for each ROI was then computed by integrating the squared wavelet coefficients within the alpha frequency band (8 to 14 Hz). In this way, we obtained the temporal evolution of the alpha power of each ROI throughout each epoch.

These data were subsequently used for a preliminary time-resolved analysis of alpha-power differences between the 2 attention conditions during the cue–probe interval. This analysis aimed to assess whether the selected cue–probe interval was indeed relevant for the phenomenon under investigation and to determine the alignment (cue-locked or probe-locked) that more effectively captured attention-related alpha-band modulations. To assess cue-locked alignment, from each variable-length epoch, the alpha power of each ROI (derived from the wavelet) was extracted between −0.25 and 2 s relative to the cue, and then averaged separately across the 400 Left Cue epochs and the 400 Right Cue epochs. In this way, for each participant, we obtained an average time pattern of alpha power in each attention condition per ROI, along the interval −0.25 to 2 s relative to the cue. Finally, for each attention condition, the alpha power of each ROI was averaged over 250-ms time windows spanning the cue-locked interval (9 time windows). This same procedure was replicated to assess the probe-locked alignment. In this case, from each variable-length epoch, the alpha power of each ROI (derived from the wavelet transform) was extracted between −2 and 0.25 s relative to the probe. The same steps were then performed obtaining, for each attention condition, the alpha power of each ROI over 250-ms time windows spanning the probe-locked interval (9 time windows).

Statistical analyses

Effect sizes (Cohen’s d) were used to quantify the overall magnitude of alpha-power differences between the Left Cue and Right Cue conditions, for the cue-locked alignment and the probe-locked alignment. Specifically, for each alignment (cue-locked and probe-locked), we computed Cohen’s d for the Left Cue vs. Right Cue contrast in each ROI and each 250-ms time window, disregarding the first time window in the cue-locked alignment (precue window) and the last time window in the probe-locked alignment (postprobe window). To obtain a global measure of the effect magnitude for each alignment, the absolute effect sizes were averaged across all ROIs and across the 8 time windows, separately for the cue-locked and probe-locked cases. This summary measure was used to identify the alignment with the overall larger effect size to be used in the following analyses. To complement the effect size analysis, for each alignment, we also performed 2 one-tailed permutation tests based on the t-statistic, contrasting the 2 attention conditions, ROI by ROI and for each time window (whether Left Cue greater or less than Right Cue). The obtained P-values were corrected for multiple comparisons (68 ROIs) within each time window, using FDR correction. One-tailed rather than two-tailed tests were adopted as we were interested in distinguishing the direction of the changes. All permutation tests were conducted on the 13 participant-level differences between the 2 attention conditions. For each ROI and time window, the permutation tests were performed by randomly flipping the sign of the difference in alpha power Left Cue–Right Cue for each participant, and a total of 5,000 random permutations were replicated. Following the results of the permutation tests, for each alignment, the overall number of ROIs with significantly different alpha power between the 2 conditions was computed across all time windows, as further evidence of the alignment more effectively capturing the phenomenon of interest.

Based on the results of this preliminary analysis (see the Preliminary Time-Resolved Alpha Power Analysis section), the probe-locked alignment was selected. Consequently, all subsequent analyses (both using the conventional approach and the XAI-based approach) were conducted on 2-s epochs spanning from −2 to 0 s relative to probe onset.

Approach based on conventional alpha power indices

The time-resolved analysis described above revealed alpha-power changes over the cortex spatially coherent throughout the selected 2 s preprobe interval (see also Fig. 6 in the Preliminary Time-ResolvedAlpha Power Analysis section in Results). Therefore, the conventional analysis approach was based on the computation of 2 metrics (Alpha Power Difference and Alpha Modulation Index) obtained by collapsing alpha power on the selected 2 s-length interval, ROI by ROI, and evaluating the difference of the so obtained alpha power between the 2 attention conditions at each ROI, as described below.

Alpha Power Difference (APD) and Alpha Modulation Index (AMI) of the ROIs

From the epoch-level wavelet decomposition, the alpha power of each ROI was computed within each 2-s preprobe epoch by integrating the squared wavelet coefficients over this time interval and over the alpha frequency band, obtaining a single alpha power value for each epoch and ROI. These metrics are commonly used in the literature (both at the scalp and cortex level, eg see Siegel et al. 2008; Händel et al. 2011; Liu et al. 2016; Lasaponara et al. 2019; Sokoliuk et al. 2019; Gallotto et al. 2020) with previous studies typically selecting one or the other. Here, we employed both metrics to provide a more comprehensive description, as they highlight different nuances. Moreover, the use of both metrics allows the identification of the ROIs showing significant effects both as to absolute (APD) and relative alpha power changes (AMI), thus eliminating potential bias due to the use of either one or the other metric. Note that in the following, to avoid ambiguity, the acronym APD is used only to denote the alpha power difference computed on the overall 2-s preprobe interval and not to denote the time-resolved alpha power difference.

Statistical analyses

Each metric was separately subjected to statistical tests. For each metric, we applied 2 one-tail permutation tests based on the t-statistic for testing whether the metric was greater or less than 0, ROI by ROI; the results were then corrected for multiple comparisons (68 comparisons, 1 per ROI) using FDR. For each metric and for each ROI, the permutation tests were performed on the 13 participant-level values of the metric. Permutation tests were conducted by randomly flipping the sign of the metric value for each participant, and 5,000 random permutations were performed. We used one-tailed tests in order to emphasize the regions showing the effects in a specific direction, ie showing alpha power (either absolute in case of APD or normalized in case of AMI) greater when attending left than right or when attending right than left.

From these analyses, we identified the ROIs with statistically significant APD and the ROIs with statistically significant AMI.

Approach based on an interpretable convolutional neural network

In addition to the metrics based on conventional spectral analysis described in the Approach Based on Conventional Alpha Power Indices section, we computed a deep learning–based alpha-band metric at the ROI level, accounting for diverging alpha-band activity across the 2 attention conditions, termed “Alpha-Band Discriminatory Relevance of the ROIs.” This measure was extracted from an interpretable CNN trained to predict the 2 attention conditions from the source-level EEG, by learning interpretable spectral and spatial features. In this section, the architecture of the interpretable CNN is first presented, together with the adopted training and evaluation strategies. Then, we describe the computation of the metric and the performed statistical analyses.

The interpretable CNN

An interpretable CNN that we proposed previously—"Sinc-ShallowNet” (Borra et al. 2020)—was used here to classify Left Cue vs. Right Cue attention condition from single-epoch cortical-level signals, preliminarily downsampled to 128 Hz to reduce the computational cost. As detailed above and in line with the conventional approach, the CNN was applied to 2-s preprobe epochs. The network consists of a shallow architecture that processes the input neural signals , where represents the number of ROIs and represents the number of time samples (see the Network Training and Decoding Performance section for the definition of ), and returns the associated label as output. A high-level representation of the network architecture is reported in Fig. 3, and the details of the network layers (main hyperparameters, number of trainable parameters, and output shape) are listed in Table 2.

The network first processes the input using a sinc-based temporal convolutional layer. This layer forces the learning of a bank of ideal bandpass filters (modeled via the composition of sinc functions), by reparametrizing the coefficients of each convolutional filter in terms of the 2 cutoff frequencies. Each filter has a shape and cutoff frequencies and (). The cutoff frequencies and are optimized during training and represent the interpretable spectral features of the network. Then, a spatial depthwise convolutional layer is applied, optimally weighting the information in space across all brain regions, by learning a set of spatial filters with shape for each bandpass filter (ie resulting in 64 spatial filters, in total). These frequency-specific spatial filters (), optimized during training, represent the interpretable spatial features of the network. Neurons are activated via exponential-linear functions (ELUs), and batch normalization is included after each convolutional layer. Remarkably, the combination of the sinc-based temporal convolution with the spatial depthwise convolution realizes an “interpretable spectral and spatial feature extractor,” which automatically identifies the most discriminative frequency bands and the most discriminative brain regions tied to each frequency band, for distinguishing between the 2 attention conditions. Finally, average pooling is applied [pool size and stride , , respectively] to reduce the computational cost and keep the model size limited, and dropout (dropout rate ) is included to improve generalization. Feature maps are flattened and provided as input to the output fully connected layer composed of neurons, 1 per class (ie Left Cue and Right Cue). Output neurons are activated via softmax activation, providing as output the conditional probability , .In addition to dropout and batch normalization, the parameters of the spatial depthwise convolutional layer and of the fully connected layer are constrained to have maximum norm (1 and 0.25, respectively), to further improve generalization. The network hyperparameters are set as in the reference publication (Borra et al. 2020) except for the temporal dimension of the filters (convolutional and pooling), which is reduced according to the shorter length of the input time series considered in this study (see the Network Training and Decoding Performance section). See the reference publication for additional details about the network architecture (Borra et al. 2020).

Although the CNN is general-purpose (ie not restricted to a specific EEG decoding task), it was originally applied to classification of motor execution and motor imagination, showing to outperform state-of-the-art CNNs (Borra et al. 2020). Since EEG-based motor classification relies on the network’s ability to learn and discriminate oscillatory patterns—such as alpha, beta, and gamma rhythms—we deemed this CNN well suited for decoding covert orientation of attention, which similarly involves oscillatory activity changes in different brain regions.

Network training and decoding performance

We adopted a within-subject training strategy employing a cross-fold validation partitioning scheme for each participant’s dataset, thus we trained participant-specific interpretable CNNs (ie 1 CNN per fold per participant). Each participant’s dataset consisted of 800 source-level epochs (400 epochs per condition, each epoch lasting 2 s) and was divided into a training set and a test set using 5-fold cross-validation. For each fold, we reserved 20% of the training set as a validation set, thus resulting in 512, 128, and 160 training, validation, and test epochs. To increase the number of training examples, we cropped the neural signals by extracting 0.5 s-length chunks from the 2 s-length epoch with a stride of 0.1 s, and we used them as input for the network (Lashgari et al. 2020). Therefore, the number of time samples of each input processed by the network was . With this cropping procedure, the examples were augmented 16 times; thus, we used 8,192, 2,048, and 2,560 training, validation, and test examples. The network trainable parameters were optimized for 500 training epochs (1 training epoch corresponding to 1 full pass through the entire training set), using the cross-entropy as loss function and adaptive moment estimation (Kingma and Ba 2017) as optimizer (learning rate of 1e-4, mini-batch size of 64). The parameters at the training epoch with maximum validation accuracy were returned and used for inference and feature analysis. For each trained network (65 in total), ie for each participant (13) and cross-validation fold (5), the decoding performance on the test set was derived by computing the area under the ROC curve (AUROC). The AUROC was then averaged across folds, obtaining a decoding performance measure for each participant.

Processing of the interpretable features and Alpha-band Discriminatory Relevance (CNN-ADR) of the ROIs

The interpretable features of the trained networks—ie the cutoff frequencies of the temporal filters and the frequency-specific spatial filters—were extracted and processed. The processing was applied to the features of each trained CNN (1 CNN per fold per participant, 65 in total). Furthermore, we applied the same processing to nontrained CNNs (65 in total), ie randomly initialized CNNs, representing a control condition. This control condition was used to perform a comparative analysis with the features obtained with the trained CNNs, in order to discard potential aspects related to noise.

i) Computation of the relevance of the frequency components for the discrimination between the 2 visuospatial attention conditions. This analysis was performed mainly to check that in the deep learning approach, the alpha-band frequency components resulted the most important for the discrimination between the 2 classes, as expected from the neurophysiological knowledge. From the interpretable spectral features and , , (ie from the learned cutoff frequencies of the temporal filters), we derived the probability that a frequency component was processed by the network (the higher its probability, the greater its relevance for the class discrimination). To do so, the frequency axis was discretized into points within the range [1,60] Hz, and for each filter , we assigned the value to each frequency inside the bandwidth (ie between and ) and 0 otherwise (in other words, we converted the frequency response of each learned band-pass filter into a uniform probability density function). Then, we averaged across the filters, obtaining the “spectral probability density function” (1 scalar value for each frequency component within the range [1,60] Hz). Moreover, to summarize the results, from the spectral probability density function we computed the probability that the network processed the frequency components belonging to delta (1 to 4 Hz), theta (4 to 8 Hz), alpha (8 to 14 Hz), low-beta (14 to 20 Hz), high-beta (20 to 30 Hz), and low-gamma (30 to 60 Hz) bands (“band-level probability”), by integrating over the corresponding range of frequencies.

To do so, the frequency axis was discretized into points within the range [1,60] Hz and for each frequency component, we averaged together the spatial filters linked to the bandpass filters containing that frequency component. This way, we obtained a representation () quantifying the contribution of each ROI to the discrimination, frequency by frequency. Finally, this representation was averaged across the frequency bins belonging to the alpha band, obtaining a scalar value for each ROI representing the alpha-band discriminatory relevance of the ROI.

Statistical analyses

The following statistical analyses were conducted on the participant-level quantities obtained in the Processing of the Interpretable Features and Alpha-Band Discriminatory Relevance (CNN-ADR) of the ROIs section, in order to identify the frequency band and the associated ROIs most relevant for the discrimination between the 2 attention conditions.

Statistical analyses on the band-level probabilities. First, we compared the band-level probabilities obtained with the trained CNNs vs. nontrained CNNs (control condition). To this aim, a two-tailed permutation test based on the t-statistic was performed separately for each band, and the P-values were corrected for multiple tests (6 in total) using the FDR correction. For each band, the permutation test was conducted by randomly flipping the sign of the band-level probability difference (between the trained and nontrained CNNs) for each participant, and 5,000 random permutations were performed. This statistical analysis served to reveal how the network learned to process the spectral information in the various bands to distinguish between the 2 attention conditions. Subsequently, to verify the relative importance of the alpha band for the discrimination, two-tailed permutation tests based on the t-statistic were performed, in the case of the trained models, to compare the alpha-band probability with the probability of each other band, and the P-values were corrected for multiple tests (5 in total) using the FDR correction.

Statistical analyses on the Alpha-band Discriminatory Relevance of the ROIs. Then, we run a statistical analysis inspired by our recent work (Borra et al. 2023a)—in which deep learning was applied to classify source-level EEG recorded during a different cognitive task (face/body inversion)—to identify the brain regions most relevant for the discrimination between the 2 attention conditions, based on their alpha-band activity. Specifically, these regions were identified according to the computed CNN-ADR values, as the ones that met all the following selection criteria. See also the reference publication (Borra et al. 2023a) for further details about these selection criteria.

i) Criterion 1: Discriminatory relevance vs. mean discriminatory relevance. We first computed the average discriminatory relevance across the 68 ROIs, separately for each participant. Then, we performed a pairwise comparison between the so obtained mean value (13 samples) and the discriminatory relevance of each ROI (13 samples). This criterion was met by the ROIs having discriminatory relevance significantly higher than the average.

i) Criterion 1: Discriminatory relevance vs. mean discriminatory relevance. We first computed the average discriminatory relevance across the 68 ROIs, separately for each participant. Then, we performed a pairwise comparison between the so obtained mean value (13 samples) and the discriminatory relevance of each ROI (13 samples). This criterion was met by the ROIs having discriminatory relevance significantly higher than the average.

ii) Criterion 2: Discriminatory relevance vs. the highest discriminatory relevance. At first, we identified the ROI with the maximum relevance score (on average across participants) among the 68 ROIs. Then, we performed a pairwise comparison between the discriminatory relevance of the ROI with maximum relevance (13 samples) and the relevance (13 samples) of each of the other ROIs (67 remaining ROIs). This criterion was met by the ROI having the maximum relevance and by all other ROIs comparable to it.

ii) Criterion 2: Discriminatory relevance vs. the highest discriminatory relevance. At first, we identified the ROI with the maximum relevance score (on average across participants) among the 68 ROIs. Then, we performed a pairwise comparison between the discriminatory relevance of the ROI with maximum relevance (13 samples) and the relevance (13 samples) of each of the other ROIs (67 remaining ROIs). This criterion was met by the ROI having the maximum relevance and by all other ROIs comparable to it.

iii) Criterion 3: Discriminatory relevance of trained CNNs vs. discriminatory relevance of control CNNs. We performed a pairwise comparison between the discriminatory relevance (13 samples) of each ROI obtained with the trained CNNs vs. the same obtained with the nontrained CNNs, representing a control condition. This criterion was met by the ROIs having discriminatory relevance significantly different than the control condition.

iii) Criterion 3: Discriminatory relevance of trained CNNs vs. discriminatory relevance of control CNNs. We performed a pairwise comparison between the discriminatory relevance (13 samples) of each ROI obtained with the trained CNNs vs. the same obtained with the nontrained CNNs, representing a control condition. This criterion was met by the ROIs having discriminatory relevance significantly different than the control condition.

All previous pairwise comparisons were performed using two-tailed permutation tests based on the t-statistic, and FDR correction was used to correct for multiple tests. According to this procedure, the ROIs identified as the most relevant in the alpha band were those having alpha-band discriminatory relevance statistically: (i) superior to the average discriminatory relevance across all ROIs, (ii) comparable to the highest discriminatory relevance, and (iii) different from the discriminatory relevance obtained in the control condition.

It is important to note that the 3 criteria above, adopted here to identify the most relevant cortical ROIs, were not defined ad hoc for this study; rather, to give more general validity and avoid potential bias, we employed the same criteria as in our previous study (Borra et al. 2023a) where a different CNN was used to discriminate between different cognitive conditions (perception of upright vs. inverted faces/bodies) from source-level EEG signals.

Integration of the 2 approaches and identification of the key ROIs in the alpha band

The results of the conventional approach and of the CNN-based approach were combined together by assessing their convergence. The cortical ROIs that were consistently identified by both approaches—ie those showing statistically significant APD and AMI in the conventional approach, as well as significant ADR (according to the 3 criteria) in the CNN-based approach—were considered the most robustly associated with alpha-band power changes during anticipatory visuospatial attention. These regions were designated as “key ROIs.”

Converging results across the 2 approaches

A fundamental finding is that some of the ROIs emerging from the conventional approaches (resulting significant as to both the conventional metrics, APD and AMI), were also among the ROIs most informative in the alpha-band activity for discriminating between the 2 attention conditions, as revealed by the CNN-based approach (compare panels b in Figs. 7, 8, and 11). These ROIs were the inferior and superior parietal cortices in the left hemisphere (IPL, SPL) and the supramarginal gyrus in the right hemisphere (SMGR). Thus, these 3 regions were identified consistently across the different analytical approaches as the regions mostly involved in alpha-band activity changes depending on attention direction and denoted as the key ROIs.

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed source-level EEG signals to characterize region-specific changes in alpha-band activity associated with covert directional changes of anticipatory visuospatial attention. Indeed, although the neural correlates of visuospatial attention have been extensively investigated, studies offering a comprehensive whole-cortex assessment of alpha-band modifications induced by directional allocation of anticipatory visuospatial attention remain relatively scarce. A central point of novelty of our study lies in the adopted methodology, which employs interpretable deep learning—using a CNN that learns interpretable features in the frequency and spatial domains—to complement and refine the results obtained via conventional alpha power analysis based on commonly applied indices. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has adopted such an integrated approach for EEG analysis, combining interpretable deep learning with traditional signal processing techniques.

Preliminary time-resolved alpha power analysis

To identify the time interval most relevant for the phenomenon of interest, to be subjected to the combined conventional and XAI-based approach, we applied a time-resolved alpha power analysis on 2 s-length epochs of the anticipatory attention interval (cue–probe interval, overall duration ranging from 2.1 to 2.5 s) either taken after the cue (cue-locked alignment) or before the probe (probe-locked alignment). In both cases, only a short time window of 100 to 500 ms, either immediately preceding the probe or immediately following the cue, was excluded from this analysis. The results of these time-resolved analyses deserve some comments. Previous studies (Rihs et al. 2009; Doesburg et al. 2016) suggest that different phases are involved during anticipatory attention to lateralized spatial positions, occurring at different timepoints after the attention-directing cue: an early phase of attention orienting, occurring in a time window of 400 to 800 ms after the cue, and a later phase (after 800 ms) of sustained attention to maintain stable the focus of attention. In both cases of alignment, the analyzed interval included timepoints of early attention orienting (approximately the time windows from +500 to +1,000 ms for the cue-locked alignment and from −2,000 to −1,500 ms for the probe-locked alignment) and subsequent timepoints of sustained spatial attention. When contrasting leftward vs. rightward attention direction, we observed that all these timepoints (see Figs. 5 and 6) were similarly characterized by alpha power in either hemisphere, tending to be higher for attention directed ipsilaterally than contralaterally. These results agree with previous data in the literature showing opposite sign of alpha-power difference (across attention conditions) in the left vs. right hemisphere during both earlier and later phases of anticipatory attention (Kelly et al. 2006; Thut et al. 2006; Rihs et al. 2009; Doesburg et al. 2016), either at the scalp or cortex level. Interestingly, our data matched not only the time pattern of alpha-power difference across attention conditions but also the time pattern of absolute alpha power in each attention condition, considered separately (see Figs S1 and S2 in Supplementary Material). Indeed, in agreement with previous works (Rihs et al. 2009), we observed that both Left Cue and Right Cue conditions were associated with a posterior alpha power decrease relative to precue baseline in the early orienting phase, followed by a subsequent posterior alpha increase in the later sustained phase, which was slightly larger in the hemisphere ipsilateral to attention direction. Crucially, despite these phase-specific differences in absolute alpha dynamics, the contrast between the 2 attention conditions (ie alpha power difference Left Cue–Right Cue) consistently revealed the same pattern of hemispheric alpha lateralization across both early and sustained phases.

Overall, 2 main conclusions can be derived from the previous observations. First, our data reproduced alpha-band signatures of both early and late phases of anticipatory attention, either in absolute terms, when considering absolute alpha power in each attention condition, or in relative terms, when considering the difference between the 2 conditions. Second, the early attention orienting and later sustained attention shared the same pattern of alpha-band difference across attention conditions, suggesting that they may rely on similar modulatory mechanisms.

Since the global effect size resulted larger in the probe-locked alignment than in the cue-locked alignment (see the Preliminary Time-Resolved Alpha Power Analysis section in Results), the 2-s interval preceding the probe was used in the core analyses conducted by means of the combined conventional and XAI-based approach. This interval, as described above, still contained early attention orienting phase (at least partially) and sustained attention phase. Thus, the key regions emerging from the application of the proposed approach to the selected time interval could be common to both attentional phases.

Conventional approach: insights about alpha-band modulations in visuospatial attention

Some interesting aspects can be first derived from the 2 conventional metrics, APD and AMI, computed on the entire selected 2-s preprobe interval. The pattern of these metrics across the entire cortex (Figs. 7a and 8a) reproduced the ones observed in the time-resolved analysis, assuming larger values at parietal–occipital regions with opposite sign in the two hemispheres, and aligned with prior observations (Worden et al. 2000; Kelly et al. 2006; Thut et al. 2006; Siegel et al. 2008; Rihs et al. 2009; Gould et al. 2011; Händel et al. 2011; Sokoliuk et al. 2019; Gallotto et al. 2020). Noteworthy results arise from the statistical analyses (Figs 7b and 8b). Different from the time-resolved patterns, where no right hemisphere ROI emerged as significant, these metrics, collapsed over the entire interval, yielded significant ROIs in both hemispheres. Specifically, higher-level parietal, temporal, and frontal ROIs appeared significantly implicated in alpha-power modifications across the two hemispheres. Conversely, low-level occipital regions resulted in being less implicated (only 1 significant ROI in only 1 metric). These findings can be interpreted in light of the established involvement of posterior parietal and frontal regions in large-scale attention networks. For example, the intraparietal sulcus with the adjacent inferior and superior parietal lobule, together with the frontal eye fields, are considered part of the dorsal attention network (DAN), while the temporo-parietal junction (involving the supramarginal gyrus, the angular gyrus, and the superior temporal gyrus), together with the ventral frontal cortex, is assumed to form the ventral attention network (VAN) (Corbetta and Shulman 2002; Corbetta et al. 2008; Vossel et al. 2014). Although traditionally DAN and VAN are considered to have strictly distinct functional roles—ie voluntary goal-directed attention orienting and involuntary stimulus-driven attention orienting, respectively—many studies suggest a collaborative interaction between the two systems to promote attentional processes (Corbetta et al. 2008; Vossel et al. 2014). The involvement of frontal, parietal, and temporal ROIs in our results suggests that alpha-power increase/decrease may reflect not just sensory gating at early visual stages but the orchestration of higher-order processes in frontal and parietal ROIs, which, in turn, may send top–down alpha-band modulatory influences to lower-level occipital ROIs (Liu et al. 2016). Other source-level EEG/MEG studies have previously reported strong alpha-power dynamics in frontoparietal ROIs during visuospatial attention shifts (Siegel et al. 2008; Lobier et al. 2018; Sokoliuk et al. 2019). As a second notable result, a clear hemispheric asymmetry emerged. Indeed, the inferior and superior parietal regions were consistently identified by both power-based metrics in the left hemisphere, with the 2 metrics diverging only as to 1 ROI (lateral occipital cortex). In contrast, significant regions in the right hemisphere were more broadly distributed and showed less consistency across the 2 metrics. The supramarginal gyrus was the only ROI consistently identified in the right parietal lobe, along with a few additional ROIs in the right frontal and temporal lobes. A possible interpretation is that the left parietal cortex occupies a predominant role in the dynamic regulation of the alpha power, relative to the right parietal cortex. We will return to this point later, after discussing the results of the CNN-based approach.

CNN-based approach: attentional states decoding

Deep learning approaches, in particular CNNs, have emerged as extremely powerful tools for EEG decoding and classification, able to automatically learn features from the input data relevant for between-class discrimination (Craik et al. 2019; Roy et al. 2019). Interpreting the features learned by the deep learning–based EEG decoders is of utmost importance as it transforms the models from black-box classification tools into novel analysis tools, able to shedding light on the EEG correlates underlying the discriminated classes (Roy et al. 2019). Here, we adopted a compact interpretable CNN, previously developed by our group (Borra et al. 2020), specifically designed to provide direct interpretability of the spectral features (the cutoff frequencies of a bank of passband filters) and spatial features (the spatial filters tied to each bandpass filter). It is important to remark that, in our previous study (Borra et al. 2020), we only introduced the network architecture and applied it to motor imagery decoding from EEG. In that study, we only analyzed the learned features to validate the model as a neural decoder of motor imagery conditions (neural decoding purpose). In contrast, in the present study, we employed the interpretable CNN to complement and enhance the analysis of the modulations of EEG oscillations underlying visuospatial attention (neural analysis purpose). Therefore, the application of our interpretable CNN design to EEG analysis represents a novel aspect of the current study. The adoption of such an interpretable CNN offered an independent tool for the analysis of the alpha-band activity modifications related to visuospatial attention direction.

Our interpretable CNN achieved good classification accuracy (AUROC of 64.7%, on average across participants, see Fig. 9), well above the chance level, successfully discriminating between the 2 attention conditions from the input signals. Even though decoding performance was moderate, each decoder performed significantly above the chance level (50%) in a cross-validation evaluation framework, suggesting a consistent generalization capability of the classifier across participants. The moderate level of performance was mainly due to the challenge posed by the addressed decoding problem. Attentional state decoding was performed during the cue–probe interval, ie prior to the probe presentation. Previous research has demonstrated that anticipatory attentional states can be decoded from EEG oscillatory patterns during the cue–probe interval, for example, considering alpha-power spatial modulations (Desantis et al. 2020; Noah et al. 2020). However, these internally generated neural states are inherently weaker than poststimulus brain responses, which typically yield a higher signal-to-noise ratio in EEG. As a result, relatively limited performance is achieved, for example, with accuracies from approximately 35% to 45% in Noah et al. (Noah et al. 2020) (decoding of anticipatory attention towards face vs. scene vs. tool). It is important to highlight that other studies decoding the direction of attention from the EEG (Kaushik et al. 2022; Bajool and Seeber 2025) have reported higher classification performance, for example, with accuracy from 65.7% to 92.5% in Bajool and Seeber (Bajool and Seeber 2025) (decoding of left vs. right attended audio track). However, these studies did not target the anticipatory phase of the task (ie the cue–probe interval); instead, the decoding was performed during the stimulus presentation, when participants were actively attending to an external target (eg listening to a specific audio stream, played from a specific direction). In such paradigms, attentional state decoding may have been contaminated from the presence of externally driven sensory responses in addition to internal attentional modulations, likely contributing to enhancing classification performance.

Besides differences in the addressed neural decoding problem, the present study also differs from prior works in terms of the neural decoding approach employed. First, state-of-the-art methods for decoding attentional states from the EEG have been mainly applied to scalp-level activity, while decoding of attentional states from cortex-level EEG has been more limitedly addressed. Second, the state of the art has predominantly relied on either traditional machine-learning techniques—such as linear discriminant analysis or support vector machines (Thiery et al. 2016; Desantis et al. 2020; Noah et al. 2020; Kaushik et al. 2022) or deep-learning models, including convolutional neural networks and long short-term memory architectures (Farahat et al. 2019; Kaushik et al. 2022; Chugh and Aggarwal 2024; Bajool and Seeber 2025). While these approaches in some applications have demonstrated high decoding performance, they are typically treated as black-box models or only partially interpretable models, offering limited insight into the neural features driving classification. When partially interpretable, interpretability is confined only to the spatial domain (eg spatial filters in standard CNNs or in common spatial patterns combined with linear discriminant analysis), and the interpretation of specific frequency contributions is less direct and requires post hoc analyses. In contrast, the present study employs an interpretable neural decoder that, by design, enables direct inspection of the spectral and spatial EEG features contributing most strongly to the discrimination between attentional states. This glass-box approach allows decoding performance to be linked explicitly to neurophysiologically meaningful oscillatory patterns and cortex topographies, rather than solely to abstract model parameters. As such, our approach moves beyond purely performance-driven black-box decoding and provides a perspective on how anticipatory attentional states are represented in EEG activity.

To better contextualize the decoding performance attainable in the challenging neural decoding problem addressed in this study, we trained and evaluated 3 state-of-the-art machine-learning and deep-learning models previously proposed for decoding oscillatory neural modulations from EEG signals. The state-of-the-art algorithms and results are presented in the Table S1 of Supplementary Material. As expected, all state-of-the-art neural decoders achieved decoding performance above the chance level (50%), albeit with relatively limited performance. Specifically, the AUROC ranged from 59.4 ± 0.7% to 63.1 ± 1.1% across the state-of-the-art approaches (mean ± SEM across participants). Notably, our interpretable CNN, Sinc-ShallowNet, significantly outperformed each state-of-the-art approach (see Supplementary Material for additional details). These results indicate that our interpretable CNN achieved a decoding performance that exceeded that of existing state-of-the-art methods. At the same time, the overall moderate performance levels across all models underscore the intrinsic difficulty of the neural decoding problem addressed in this study.

CNN-based approach: insights about alpha-band modulations in visuospatial attention

Successful decoding of attention orientation by our CNN-based decoders indicates that they captured neural features meaningful for discriminating between leftward and rightward attention orientation. As a first central result, the analysis of the learned interpretable features revealed the alpha band as the most relevant band for the classification (Fig. 10). While the pivotal role of alpha band in visuospatial attention is well established, it is likely the first time that this result has emerged in a totally automatic and data-driven way, simultaneously considering all frequency components of the input signals (within 1 to 60 Hz). Since the automatic emergence of the alpha band as the key band is central to our approach, it is crucial to ensure that this result of the frequency-resolved CNN analysis was robust and not driven by methodological biases. Regarding this, a potential bias in the data could have been introduced by computing the time series representative of each cortical ROI as the average of the time series of all vertices inside the ROI (see the EEG Preprocessing and Cortical Source Estimation section in Materials and Methods). Indeed, as time-domain averaging tends to operate as a low-pass filtering, this procedure might attenuate oscillatory content and power, especially at high frequencies and thus introduce some bias in the ROI-level data (processed by the CNN), potentially favoring lower-frequency bands (such as the alpha band) at the expense of higher-frequency bands (such as beta and gamma). Thus, the higher relevance that the CNN automatically attributed to alpha-band activity in discriminating the 2 attention conditions could, in principle, reflect the bias introduced in the input signals rather than a genuine neural mechanism. In order to rule out this possibility and to ensure the reliability of the CNN results in terms of relevance of spectral components for the discrimination, we performed 2 control analyses whose results are reported in detail in the Supplementary Material (see Figs. S3 and S4 in Supplementary Material). Briefly, we verified that:

computing time–frequency power before or after spatial averaging within ROIs yielded highly similar Left Cue–Right Cue contrasts, with alpha-band activity emerging in both cases as the dominant band expressing the difference between the 2 attention conditions, with no other comparable effects emerging in other frequency bands (Fig. S3).

computing time–frequency power before or after spatial averaging within ROIs yielded highly similar Left Cue–Right Cue contrasts, with alpha-band activity emerging in both cases as the dominant band expressing the difference between the 2 attention conditions, with no other comparable effects emerging in other frequency bands (Fig. S3).

using single-vertex time series instead of across vertices–averaged time series as input to the CNN (ie using the time series of the central vertex of each ROI as ROI representative) did not alter the spectral relevance profile obtained by the frequency-resolved CNN analysis, with the alpha band remaining the most informative frequency range for classification (Fig. S4).

using single-vertex time series instead of across vertices–averaged time series as input to the CNN (ie using the time series of the central vertex of each ROI as ROI representative) did not alter the spectral relevance profile obtained by the frequency-resolved CNN analysis, with the alpha band remaining the most informative frequency range for classification (Fig. S4).

Together, these control analyses indicate that time-domain averaging across vertices to obtain ROI-level signals did not introduce a bias in the ROI-level data and did not affect the features automatically learned by the CNN and the resulting spectral relevance profile.

The previous results, confirming the reliability of the CNN-based spectral analysis, justified focusing the subsequent analysis of the spatial features learned by the CNN specifically within the alpha-band—designing the Alpha-band Discriminatory Relevance (CNN-ADR)—to identify the ROIs most relevant, as to their alpha-band activity, for the discrimination of the 2 attentional states. From this analysis, a small subset of posterior cortical ROIs emerged as the most relevant ones for distinguishing leftward vs. rightward attention condition; these were all left parietal and fronto-parietal ROIs with the only exception of the right supramarginal gyrus. Therefore, the CNN-based approach revealed a pronounced hemispheric asymmetry, which was in line with the results of the conventional approach, and further reinforced the view of a left-hemispheric parietal dominance in the dynamic regulation of alpha-band activity as a function of anticipatory attention direction. Indeed, compared with the conventional approach, the CNN-based approach identified 2 additional left ROIs other than the left inferior and superior parietal cortex (ie the left supramarginal gyrus and the left paracentral lobule), while in the right hemisphere, it retained the right supramarginal gyrus and disregarded right frontal and temporal ROIs. Thus, this approach turned out to be more selective in the right hemisphere, identifying only a focal parietal zone.

Converging results from conventional and CNN-based approaches

Overall, the different approaches provided support in favor of an asymmetrical cortical organization of alpha-band regulation related to attention direction, with 3 parietal ROIs—the left superior parietal cortex, the left inferior parietal cortex, and the right supramarginal gyrus—consistently identified by the different approaches and thus emerging as the regions mostly involved in the alpha-band power adjustments. These findings suggest a hemispheric asymmetry, with a larger contribution of the left hemisphere in the dynamic regulation of alpha-band activity as a function of anticipatory attention direction. However, we claim that these results do not imply that the left hemisphere is more implicated overall than the right hemisphere in visuospatial attention mechanisms. Rather, the nonsignificant emergence of some right hemisphere parietal regions (eg right parietal superior and inferior cortices) when contrasting their alpha-band activity between attention conditions could result from a tonic and more stable engagement (ie disinhibition) of these right regions across both attention conditions. Indeed, alpha-band synchronization and desynchronization are thought to act as general mechanisms for functional inhibition and disinhibition (ie engagement) of brain regions, depending on the task demand (Jensen and Mazaheri 2010). Right hemisphere regions may operate at a lower and more stable (less tunable) level of alpha activity than homologous left hemisphere regions because overall more implicated throughout all the attentional tasks and thus with a less dynamic range for further condition-dependent alpha-modulation. In other words, right parietal regions may show reduced alpha “tunability” because they are already tonically activated in attentional control (ie with alpha power at lower tonic level). This interpretation also harmonizes our results with one theory of visuospatial attention (hemispatial theory; Heilman and Van Den Abell 1980; Corbetta and Shulman 2002; Gallotto et al. 2020), which states that the right hemisphere is responsible for attending both left and right hemifields (while the left hemisphere would attend only the right one), and thus posits a right hemisphere dominance. Based on our interpretation, the right-hemispheric dominance may be expressed primarily through tonic engagement, reflected in lower absolute alpha power, whereas left-hemisphere regions may contribute more strongly to dynamic, condition-dependent alpha adjustments, since they are less tonically activated.

It is also interesting to consider our results and interpretation in light of recent studies (Schuhmann et al. 2019; Kasten et al. 2020; Kemmerer et al. 2022; Radecke et al. 2023) that used transcranial alternating current stimulation (tACS) in the alpha band—a technique shown to enhance the power of alpha oscillations at the stimulation site (Zaehle et al. 2010; Kasten et al. 2016)—to induce alpha modulation in right and left parietal cortices. These studies proved that alpha tACS over the left dorsal parietal cortex affected the performance of covert attention tasks compared to sham stimulation (Schuhmann et al. 2019; Kemmerer et al. 2022; Radecke et al. 2023), while alpha-tACS applied to the homologous right cortex showed no effect (Kemmerer et al. 2022; Radecke et al. 2023). One of these studies (Radecke et al. 2023) argued that the left dorsal parietal cortex plays a dominant role in the dynamic adjustments of alpha power, while the right one remains tonically more activated and with more stable (less tunable) alpha power, resulting in different susceptibility of the two homologous cortices to tACS-induced alpha modulation. As those results refer to tACS mainly targeting DAN regions (the superior parietal cortex and part of inferior parietal cortex), our findings and interpretation fit well with them.

Finally, it is worth mentioning that our data showed lower alpha power in the right relative to the left hemisphere consistently during the cue–probe period in both attention conditions (Left Cue and Right Cue), and in the precue period, as displayed by the absolute alpha power in the Figs S1 and S2 of the Supplementary Material. This pattern supports the hypothesis of a general higher disinhibition, ie a higher engagement, of the right hemisphere over the left one throughout all the tasks. Crucially, a very similar pattern, although observed at the scalp level, is reported by Rihs et al. (Rihs et al. 2007), who observed absolute alpha maps dominated by a left–right asymmetry in alpha-activity (left > right) already present in the precue period. Additionally, lower values of absolute alpha power in the right compared to the left hemisphere regions are reported in other studies too (Thut et al. 2006; Doesburg et al. 2008; Lasaponara et al. 2019).

Limitations and future directions

First, although our sample size is comparable to that of many previous studies (see the Participants section), it remains relatively limited. It would be highly relevant to apply the present combined approach to a larger sample size, for achieving a more robust validation of the asymmetric hemispheric involvement of parietal regions in alpha-band power adjustments. Second, we did not find robust relationships between alpha-power changes in the identified key regions and behavioral performance. This may result from the limited number of participants and also from the particular structure of the task that yielded a low proportion of trials (37.5%) requiring the response, limiting the amount of usable behavioral data. Furthermore, previous studies have suggested different roles for the lower alpha (7 to 10 Hz) band and upper alpha band (10 to 14 Hz) (Klimesch 1999; Lobier et al. 2018), and a very recent work (Cruz et al. 2025) found a spatial and temporal dissociation between the 2 sub-bands using MEG signals. Here, we tested whether our CNN-based approach was able to find spatial dissociation between the 2 sub-bands; to this aim, we applied the analysis of the spectral and spatial features of the CNN, considering separately the low-alpha (8 to 10 Hz) and high-alpha band (10 to 14 Hz) and deriving the low-alpha and high-alpha band discriminatory relevance values ROI by ROI. Our results, differently from the study by Cruz et al. (Cruz et al. 2025), show no substantial differences between the most relevant ROIs in the 2 sub-bands (see Figs. S5 and S6 in the Supplementary Material). These results may suggest that either our method is not sufficiently sensitive to disentangle the contribution of alpha sub-bands in EEG or that the different contributions of the 2 sub-bands did not emerge here because of the absence of a neutral condition (not requiring any shifts of attention) to be used for comparison with each attentional condition as done in the previous work (Cruz et al. 2025). Accordingly, future studies could apply the same combined approach used here to investigate alpha adjustments relative to a neutral condition, to better assess potential sub-band-specific effects.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this work investigated regional alpha-power changes during directional shifts of covert attention at the EEG source level, considering cortical regions covering the entire cortex and making use of a novel integrated approach that combines 2 complementary approaches, ie a conventional spectral analysis and an interpretable artificial intelligence technique based on deep learning. The latter used an interpretable CNN to decode leftward and rightward covert attention orientation, exploiting oscillatory-induced activity contained in source-level EEG signals, and analyzed the interpretable spectral and spatial features learned for the discrimination, automatically revealing the alpha band as the most important band for distinguishing the 2 attention conditions. The conventional metrics suggested a differential involvement of left and right hemispheres in alpha-power changes between the 2 attention conditions. The CNN-based approach, by identifying the regions whose alpha-band activity was more relevant for the discrimination, supported and refined the conventional approach results, confirming a left-biased parietal involvement and corroborating the implication of more extensively distributed regions across dorsal and posterior portions in the left parietal cortex (the superior and inferior parietal cortex) and a more focal region in the right parietal cortex, located more anteriorly (the supramarginal gyrus).

Overall, a 2-fold contribution may be drawn from this study:

i) A contribution to neurophysiological knowledge. Of course, continuously enhancing the comprehension of how alpha-band mechanisms operate in attention has implications in clinical neuroscience. Indeed, altered patterns of alpha-band adjustments have been observed in individuals with neurological disorders such as ADHD and visual neglect (ter Huurne et al. 2013; Vollebregt et al. 2016; Lasaponara et al. 2019), as well as in normal aging (Hong et al. 2015), and techniques aimed at reinstating intact alpha oscillatory dynamics may represent promising targets for reversing attentional deficits (Ros et al. 2017).

i) A contribution to neurophysiological knowledge. Of course, continuously enhancing the comprehension of how alpha-band mechanisms operate in attention has implications in clinical neuroscience. Indeed, altered patterns of alpha-band adjustments have been observed in individuals with neurological disorders such as ADHD and visual neglect (ter Huurne et al. 2013; Vollebregt et al. 2016; Lasaponara et al. 2019), as well as in normal aging (Hong et al. 2015), and techniques aimed at reinstating intact alpha oscillatory dynamics may represent promising targets for reversing attentional deficits (Ros et al. 2017).

ii) A contribution to EEG analysis methods, by showcasing the potential of an innovative approach that exploits an interpretable artificial intelligence tool to strengthen and sharpen the results of more standard EEG analysis methods. In particular, it is worth remarking that this combined approach may have a more general validity and can be applied to investigate the neural correlates underlying cognitive and motor tasks in contexts other than visuospatial attention.
