Emergency myelopoiesis in murine arthritis results in cell-intrinsic changes in dendritic cell development and function
Authors: Lin Tze Tung, Roselyn R. Jiang, Bianca Pozzebon, Mathieu Mancini, Lucie Droin, Mitra Yousefi, Joo Eun (June) Kim, Marwah Mousa, Yue Liang, Connie M. Krawczyk, Ines Colmegna, Danielle Malo, David Langlais, Silvia M. Vidal, Anastasia Nijnik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryRheumatoid arthritis affects ∼1% of adult population, results in joint inflammation and systemic comorbidities, and is driven by pathogenic hyperactivation of both innate and adaptive immune systems. We demonstrate that emergency myelopoiesis is induced in rheumatoid arthritis, enhancing innate immune cell production and generating functionally altered innate immune cells. Importantly, these mechanisms impact dendritic cells—antigen-presenting cells that bridge innate and adaptive immune responses. Such effects are intrinsic to hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) and persist ex vivo independently of inflammatory disease milieu. Dendritic cells derived from HSPCs of arthritis afflicted mice show global changes in gene expression, cytokine production, and induction of activation and checkpoint markers. Furthermore, such dendritic cells have altered capacity for T cell activation. Overall, this characterizes how chronic inflammation and induction of emergency myelopoiesis affect dendritic cell development and function in models of rheumatoid arthritis, with implications for other disorders of chronic inflammation.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis is a chronic autoimmune and inflammatory disease that affects ∼0.5%–1% of the adult population, with the incidence of the disease elevated among women, smokers, and first-degree relatives, as well as strongly increased with age.1,2 While joint inflammation is the primary feature of the disease, many rheumatoid arthritis patients develop extra-articular manifestations and associated comorbidities, such as hypertension, osteoporosis, interstitial lung disease, and others.3,4 Overall, rheumatoid arthritis is chronic and progressive, causing pain, impaired mobility, reduced quality of life, and premature mortality. Despite significant progress in the available therapeutic options, a cure for rheumatoid arthritis is still lacking.5

Immune and inflammatory tissue damage is the primary mechanism for arthritis disease onset and progression, with a hyperactivation of both innate and adaptive immune systems, release of inflammatory mediators, influx of activated monocytes and other myeloid leukocytes into the affected tissues, and a systemic loss of B and T cell tolerance.6,7 While these and other immunological disease mechanisms in rheumatoid arthritis are widely investigated, it is essential to consider that most immune cells are short-lived and rapidly replenished from hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs) in the process of hematopoiesis.8 Hematologic dysfunction is common in arthritis patients, with anemia and clonal hematopoiesis being most widely studied.9,10 We argue that the full understanding of immune dysfunction in rheumatoid arthritis and its roles in the disease pathophysiology requires not only the analyses of peripheral terminally differentiated immune cells, but also the understanding of the mechanisms of immune cell production, differentiation, and turnover.

Over the past decade, trained immunity has become established as an important feature of the innate immune system that allows the host to mount functionally altered immune responses based on their previous history of immune exposures, independently of adaptive immune memory.11,12 While some long-lived tissue-resident innate immune cells can act as mediators of trained immunity, we and others have shown that altered HSPC function through their long-term epigenetic, transcriptional, and metabolic reprogramming is the major carrier and mediator of trained immunity in murine models of Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) vaccination, various infectious diseases, and other pathologies.13,14,15,16,17 There is therefore a growing interest in the role of such mechanisms in systemic autoimmune and inflammatory disorders, including rheumatoid arthritis.18,19

Systemic hematologic dysfunction, with an induction of emergency myelopoiesis, is reported in recent studies in murine models of rheumatoid arthritis and related disorders, and has been shown to contribute to the disease pathogenesis.20,21 Furthermore, stable reprogramming of HSPCs in murine periodontitis was shown to increase the severity of murine collagen antibody-induced arthritis,22 indicating that at least in some settings trained immunity is capable of modulating immune cell functions and disease progression in arthritis. In human rheumatoid arthritis patients, we characterized cell-intrinsic changes in HSPC functions, including altered cell numbers, impaired ex vivo proliferation, altered signaling pathway activities, and accelerated telomere loss.23,24,25 In summary, rheumatoid arthritis is associated with altered HSPC functions and with the induction of emergency myelopoiesis to ramp up innate immune cell production. However, the cell-intrinsic impact of these effects not only on immune cell numbers but on the functional properties of the innate immune cells, independently of the inflammatory disease milieu, remains poorly understood.

Dendritic cells (DCs) are the major professional antigen-presenting cells of our immune system, defined by their capacity to activate naive T cells and induce antigen-specific adaptive immune responses.26 Based on their ontogeny and functional properties, DCs are broadly classified as conventional (cDC) and plasmacytoid (pDC), with monocyte-derived DCs (moDCs) developing in the periphery and sharing some properties of cDCs.26 Beyond their immunogenic functions, DCs also play essential roles in regulating T cell immune response polarization,27 and as mediators of immune tolerance through the induction of anergic and regulatory T cells (Tregs).28,29 Dysregulation of DC function is therefore an important component in the pathogenesis of autoimmune disorders, including rheumatoid arthritis. Notably, DCs are short-lived, with an estimated lifespan of 10–30 days in mice.30,31 They are therefore rapidly replenished from HSPCs, with cDCs arising from the common myeloid progenitor (CMP) together with monocytes and other myeloid leukocytes.32 Increased DC production is reported in infectious diseases33,34,35 and investigated as a form of immunotherapy for cancer.36 Furthermore, vaccination against fungal pathogen Cryptococcus neoformans37 or an intradermal inoculation with cholera toxin B subunit38 in murine models result in long-term changes in DC function. Overall, this indicates the significant modulation of DC development and function in some disease settings. However, in chronic inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis the cell intrinsic impact of the disease on DC development and function remains unknown.

In the current study, we demonstrate that emergency myelopoiesis induced in chronic rheumatoid arthritis acts not only to enhance innate immune cell numbers but results in the production of functionally altered innate immune cells. Importantly, such mechanisms impact DCs, altering their global transcriptional profiles, responses to immune stimulation, and capacity for T cell activation. Overall, this study characterizes the cell-intrinsic impact of chronic inflammation and emergency myelopoiesis on DC development and function in rheumatoid arthritis.

Results

Expansion of HSPCs and induction of emergency myelopoiesis in murine rheumatoid arthritis

SKG mice are a well-established model of rheumatoid arthritis, with the disease characterized as late onset, sustained, and progressive (RA-SKG), thus well reproducing the core features of human arthritis. Mechanistically, SKG mice carry a homozygous mutation in Zap70 that impairs T cell negative selection.39 However, the disease develops only following an innate immune trigger, such as injection of zymosan (Zym), highlighting the interplay of innate and adaptive immunity in the disease mechanisms.40,41 We previously validated the induction of arthritis by weeks 5–7 after a single injection of Zym in SKG mice,42 and have adopted this model and experimental workflow for the current study (Figure S1A).

Hematologic dysfunction and the induction of emergency myelopoiesis in RA-SKG relative to control SKG mice were confirmed in peripheral blood, with a major increase in monocyte and platelet counts, and a reduction in lymphocyte counts (Figure S1B). We also observed reduced hematocrit, hemoglobin concentration, mean corpuscular volume, and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration in RA-SKG mice, indicating a dysfunction of erythropoiesis (Figure S1B). Flow cytometry demonstrated an increase in monocytes in the bone marrow and in monocyte-derived macrophages in both bone marrow and spleen of RA-SKG mice (Figures S1C and S1D). Furthermore, the monocytes in RA-SKG mice had enhanced levels of activation markers CD40, CD86, MHCII, and checkpoint marker PD-L1 (Figures S1E and S1F). Gating strategies are provided in Figure S2A.

There was a significant expansion of HSPCs in the bone marrow of RA-SKG relative to control SKG mice, particularly affecting the latter multipotent progenitors MPP3-4, common myeloid progenitors (CMPs), granulocyte monocyte progenitors (GMPs), and megakaryocyte progenitors (MkPs) (Figures 1A–1E). The induction of emergency myelopoiesis was further supported by the increase in myeloid colony forming units in the bone marrow of RA-SKG mice (Figure S3A). Increased dendritic cell numbers and altered activation in murine arthritis

Conventional DCs (cDCs) are short-lived and replenished from CMPs, like other myeloid leukocytes.30,31,32 We therefore tested whether the induction of emergency myelopoiesis in murine arthritis also affects in situ DC numbers. Flow cytometry indicated an expansion of DCs in both bone marrow and spleen of RA-SKG relative to control SKG mice (Figure 2A). Further analyses demonstrated an increase in cDC1 and cDC2b cells in the bone marrow and spleen, as well as in cDC2a cells in the bone marrow, while the increases in cDC2a in the spleen did not reach significance, and pDC numbers were unaffected (Figure 2B). The numbers of pre-DCs in the bone marrow of RA-SKG mice also significantly increased (Figures S3B and S3C), indicating a systemic induction of DC production in murine arthritis. Further analyses of DC surface markers indicated altered DC activation in situ in RA-SKG mice, characterized by increased PD-L1 in the bone marrow and spleen, reduced CD40 in the bone marrow, reduced MHCII in the spleen, and increased CD86 in the bone marrow (Figures 2C and 2D). Overall, we conclude that the induction of emergency myelopoiesis in murine arthritis is associated with increased DC abundance and altered DC activation.

Cell intrinsic changes in DC development and function in murine arthritis: SKG model

To characterize the cell-intrinsic changes in DC development and function in murine arthritis, independently of the inflammatory disease milieu, DCs were derived ex vivo from the bone marrow of RA-SKG and control SKG mice (BMDCs).43 DC responses to immune stimulation were then analyzed following challenge with LPS, β-glucan, or poly(I:C) to mimic bacterial, fungal, and viral stimulation, respectively (Figure S1A). To focus on cell-intrinsic aspects of HSPC to DC differentiation, independently of changes in mature immune cell functions in RA-SKG mice, the bone marrow samples were depleted of cells expressing lymphoid, granulocyte, macrophage, and erythroid lineage markers (CD3+, CD19+, NK1.1+, Ly6G+, F4/80+, and TER119+), and the BMDC derivation protocols re-optimized for such lineage depleted marrow. Lineage depleted bone marrow efficiently generated BMDCs, with the marrow of arthritis-afflicted mice producing significantly higher BMDC yields (Figures S3D–S3G). This indicates that the induction of DC production in murine arthritis is at least in part intrinsic to HSPCs and independent of inflammatory disease milieu. Supplemental study used wild type BALB/c mice, corresponding to the SKG strain genetic background, with and without an equivalent single-dose Zym treatment. As expected, the wild type control mice did not develop arthritis, showed no induction of emergency myelopoiesis (Figure S5), and demonstrated no changes in DC responses to stimulation (Figure S6). All this definitively shows that the reprogramming of myelopoiesis and DC functions in the SKG model is the result of arthritis disease, and not a side effect of Zym treatment.

DCs derived from lineage depleted bone marrow of RA-SKG mice also produced lower levels of inflammatory cytokine IL-1β and Th1-polarizing cytokine IL-12, higher levels of immunoregulatory cytokine IL-10 and chemoattractant MCP1/CCL2, with some variation in the magnitude of these effects across the stimulation conditions (Figure 3C). It is interesting to note that reduced IL-1β and increased IL-10 are known to protect against arthritis in vivo in SKG mice.44 In contrast, the production of major inflammatory cytokines TNFα and IL-6 was unchanged between RA-SKG and control DCs (Figure 3C). Intracellular flow cytometry further showed reduced IL-12/IL-23 p40 production by RA-SKG DCs (Figures 3D and 3E). As RA-SKG DC supernatants showed reduced IL-12 but no detectable IL-23 (Figure 3C and not shown), we conclude that this reflects a reduction in IL-12 production. Overall, this establishes that changes in HSPCs under chronic inflammation in murine arthritis result in the production of DCs with a significantly altered capacity to respond to immune stimulation.

Altered DC development and function in murine arthritis: CIA model

To test whether the changes in DC development and function described here are specific to the SKG murine model or are a general feature of arthritis, independently of genetic background and disease induction methods, select studies were repeated with the collagen-induced arthritis model (CIA).45 In this model wild type mice of DBA/1 strain are induced intradermally with type-II collagen in CFA (Col-CFA) and compared to control DBA/1 mice, untreated or treated with CFA alone, as described in our recent work.42 Col-CFA but not control mouse cohorts developed progressive arthritis from day 18 post-injection and were euthanized for analyses at day 34. Arthritis-afflicted Col-CFA but not control mice demonstrated the induction of emergency myelopoiesis (Figure S7). Furthermore, DCs derived ex vivo from the bone marrow of arthritis-afflicted Col-CFA mice showed altered expression of costimulatory and checkpoint markers in response to stimulation, with reduced CD40 and enhanced PD-L1 induction (Figure S8). All this is fully consistent with our findings from the SKG model. Overall, this confirms the above conclusions and indicates that altered DC development and function in arthritis is conserved across murine models, genetic backgrounds, and disease induction methods.

Analyses of DC development and function with bone marrow transplantation models

To test if the altered DC differentiation from the HSPCs of RA afflicted mice can persist long-term, chimeric mice were generated. In one of the experiments, bone marrow from SKG mice with active RA (RA-SKG) or from healthy control SKG mice was transplanted into two separate cohorts of radioablated CD45.1+ BALB/c recipient mice (Figures S9A–S9C). In a second study, RA-SKG and control SKG bone marrow were first mixed in a 1:1 ratio with allotype-marked CD45.1+ marrow, and then transplanted into separate cohorts of radioablated CD45.1+ BALB/c recipients (Figures S9D–S9I). For some readouts, as labeled on the plots, chimeric mice were re-challenged with Zym, according to the protocol used throughout this study.42 The chimeric mice were analyzed at >20 weeks post-reconstitution, to allow for full replenishment of their immune system from donor HSPCs, measuring the contribution of CD45.2+ donor to blood and immune cells via flow cytometry.

We observed that when transplanted alone, RA marrow showed largely normal capacity to reconstitute hematopoiesis, as demonstrated by the high frequencies of donor-derived cells in recipients’ peripheral blood (Figure S9A), normal although variable numbers of donor-derived HSPCs in the recipients’ bone marrow (Figure S9B), and normal although variable numbers of donor-derived cDCs and frequencies of cDC subsets in the recipients’ spleen (Figure S9C). When directly competing against CD45.1+ allotype-marked control marrow within the same host, RA bone marrow was mildly impaired in its reconstitution capacity, as shown by reduced chimerism in peripheral blood (Figure S9D). In this setting, RA marrow demonstrated some increase in the contribution to the DC cell lineage specifically following re-challenge of the recipient mice with Zym, with an increase in RA-HSPC-derived DCs both in vivo in mouse spleen and ex vivo in the BMDC cultures (Figures S9E–S9G). Furthermore, RA-SKG DCs in these chimeric mice expressed constitutively higher levels of PD-L1 both in vivo and in BMDC cultures (Figures S9H–S9I). This is consistent with some of the phenotypes of RA-SKG DCs in studies above (Figures 2 and 3), suggesting at least partial persistence of RA-induced changes in DC development and function in this chimera model. Future studies should further explore how the competitive chimera setup with Zym-rechallenge of the recipients may contribute to the persistence of these phenotypes.

Analyses of the impact of murine arthritis on macrophage functions

To test if rheumatoid arthritis can also modulate the development and function of macrophages, we applied the bone marrow-derived macrophage model (BMDM), which has been used widely in our previous work to assess the impact of vaccinations and infections on innate immune functions.15,16 Bone marrow from arthritis afflicted and healthy control SKG mice, either with or without lineage depletion, was differentiated ex vivo. Resulting BMDMs were stimulated with LPS, β-glucan, or poly(I:C), as previously, to model bacterial, fungal, or viral challenge, and the cells analyzed for surface marker expression and cytokine production. The BMDMs derived in cultures of total non-depleted bone marrow of RA-SKG mice showed significantly altered responses to stimulation, with reduced induction of co-stimulatory markers CD40 and CD86 and increased induction of checkpoint marker PD-L1 (Figures S10A and S10B), as well as reduced production of inflammatory cytokines IL-1β, IL-6, and TNFα (Figure S10C), as compared to control BMDMs derived from total bone marrow of healthy SKG mice. Notably, the reduced production of IL-6 and TNFα by these RA-SKG BMDMs was confirmed through intracellular flow cytometry (Figures S10D and S10E). However, unlike in our studies with BMDCs (Figure 3), these effects were not observed when working with BMDMs derived in cultures of lineage depleted bone marrow (Figure S11). Overall, this suggests that the inflammatory environment of murine arthritis can drive the production of macrophages with an altered capacity to respond to immune stimulation, however such effects are not intrinsic to myeloid precursor cells but depend on their crosstalk with other immune cells within the bone marrow in the inflamed arthritis disease milieu.

Analyzing the expression of key genes corresponding to the previous readouts in our DC functional assays, RA-SKG DCs showed reduced induction of Il12b and enhanced induction of Il10 and Ccl2 (Figure S12A), fully consistent with our previous cytokine secretion analyses (Figures 3C–3E). Furthermore, genes encoding co-stimulatory markers CD80 and CD86 were downregulated and the gene encoding checkpoint marker Cd274/PD-L1 was upregulated (Figure S12B), further demonstrating the concordance of RNA-seq with previous flow cytometry analyses (Figures 3, S4, S6, and S8). In contrast, no downregulation of Il1b or Cd40 was observed (Table S1A), suggesting that their dysregulation in RA-SKG DCs in previous assays may be mediated via post-translational mechanisms.

Differential gene expression analyses comparing DCs derived from the marrow of RA-SKG versus control SKG mice, at fold change ≥ |2.0|, Benjamini-Hochberg-adjusted p value ≤0.001, and read count per million (CPM) > 5 in at least two samples, confirmed the significant shift in the gene expression profile between RA-SKG and control DCs under steady-state unstimulated conditions, with 23 upregulated and 47 downregulated genes (Figure 4C; Table S1B). Further transcriptional changes between RA-SKG and control DCs were observed with stimulation, with 109 upregulated and 71 downregulated genes under β-Glucan and 70 upregulated and 30 downregulated genes under poly(I:C) stimulation (Figure 4C; Tables S1C and S1D). Hierarchical clustering of the genes differentially expressed between RA-SKG and control DCs was performed to get a consolidated overview of the transcriptional changes. While the transcriptional differences between RA-SKG and control DCs were obvious at steady-state, these differences were mild relative to the transcriptional responses of either group to stimulation (Figure S12C), consistent with previous PCA analyses (Figure 4B). A prevalent pattern emerges whereby the genes upregulated at steady-state in RA-SKG relative to control DCs are either more strongly induced (cluster 1) or less strongly downregulated (cluster 3) with stimulation. Accordingly, the genes downregulated in RA-SKG DCs at steady-state are either less strongly induced (cluster 4) or more strongly downregulated (cluster 2) following stimulation. In summary, the overall profile of transcriptional response to microbial stimulation is preserved, however the magnitude of the stimulation-induced transcriptional signatures is significantly altered in RA-SKG DCs.

Gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA) comparing RA-SKG and control DCs demonstrated a constitutive activation of the transcriptional signatures of innate immune and inflammatory response in unstimulated RA-SKG DCs, including “antimicrobial response,” “response to IFNβ,” “type I IFN signaling,” “defense response to bacterium,” “acute phase response,” and “neutrophil chemotaxis” (Figure 4D; Table S1E). Furthermore, the induction of these transcriptional signatures was even more notable when comparing RA-SKG and control DCs following stimulation (Figure 4D; Tables S1F and S1G). The upregulated transcriptional signatures of unstimulated RA-SKG DCs also included “oxidative phosphorylation” and “mitochondrial respiratory chain” (Figure 4D; Table S1E), indicating that significant metabolic reprogramming may impact RA-SKG DC functions.46 Furthermore, the downregulated transcriptional signatures of unstimulated RA-SKG DCs included “cell junction organization,” “cell adhesion,” and “actin-based movement” (Figure 4D; Table S1E), suggesting possible changes in their migratory capacity. Overall, we conclude that inflammatory disease milieu makes a stable cell-intrinsic impact on DC differentiation, resulting in altered DC transcriptional profiles at steady state, as well as significantly modulating DC transcriptional responses to stimulation.

Altered response to microbial stimulation in monocyte-derived DCs of rheumatoid arthritis patients

To test if DCs derived ex vivo from human rheumatoid arthritis patients also exhibit altered responses to stimulation, monocytes were isolated from peripheral blood of patients and healthy controls matched by sex and age. Monocyte-derived DCs (moDCs) were generated over 7 days of culture with GM-CSF and IL-4. Successful derivation of moDCs was confirmed based on the expression of CD11c and DC-SIGN markers (Figure S13A), with no differences in moDC yields between patient and control samples (data not shown). moDCs were stimulated according to the protocols established above and analyzed for activation markers by flow cytometry. We observed an increased induction of PD-L1 on moDCs from RA patients versus matched healthy controls in responses to β-glucan and poly(I:C), with similar trends observed also for unstimulated and LPS-stimulated moDCs (Figures S13B and S13C). Differences in the induction of other activation markers CD40, CD86, and HLA-DR between moDCs of patients and controls were not statistically significant. We also observed reduced secretion of IL-12 p40 in response to β-glucan stimulation, as well as increased secretion of MCP1/CCL2 in unstimulated moDCs derived from patients versus healthy controls (Figures S13D and S13E). Overall, this supports the notion that DC differentiation and function is altered not only in murine RA but also in rheumatoid arthritis patients, with consistent changes in PD-L1, IL-12 p40, and MCP1/CCL2 induction observed for ex vivo differentiated DCs from both RA-SKG mice and human patients.

Cell intrinsic changes in the capacity of DCs to activate T cells in murine arthritis

The activation of T cells is the primary function of DCs as professional antigen-presenting cells. To analyze the impact of arthritis on the DC capacity for antigen presentation and T cell activation, independently of inflammatory disease milieu, DCs derived ex vivo from lineage-depleted marrow of RA-SKG and control SKG mice were activated with LPS, β-glucan, or poly(I:C) in the presence of antigenic protein ovalbumin (OVA). DCs were then co-cultured with CD4 T cells expressing a TCR specific for OVA, from TCR-transgenic, Zap70 wild type, BALB/c donor mice. T cells were analyzed at days 3–4 of co-culture, comparing T cell activation by RA-SKG versus control SKG DCs, and measuring cell surface markers of T cell activation, proliferation, and exhaustion, as well as cytokine production, and the expression of transcription factors specific to T-helper and Treg lineages.

CD4 T cell activation in the co-cultures with RA-SKG DCs was significantly altered across most of the parameters analyzed. There was a highly significant reduction in IL-10 producing T cells in co-cultures with RA-SKG DCs under all the stimulation conditions (Figures 5A and 5B). Additionally, reduced induction of IFNγ, IL-4, and IL-17A, corresponding to Th1, Th2, and Th17 cytokines, respectively, was observed in the co-cultures with RA-SKG DCs under some of the stimulation conditions (Figures 5A and 5B). We further analyzed the capacity of RA-SKG DCs to induce the expression of T helper cell transcription factors FOXP3, Tbet, GATA3, and RORγt, corresponding to Treg, Th1, Th2, and Th17 lineages, respectively. RA-SKG DCs were impaired in the induction of Treg transcription factor FOXP3 in CD4 T cells across all the stimulation conditions (Figures 5C and 5D), consistent with their previously established impaired capacity for IL-10 induction (Figures 5A and 5B; Figure S14A). Induction of Tbet was also reduced in the co-cultures with RA-SKG DCs under all the stimulation conditions, while reduced induction of GATA3 was observed under β-glucan stimulation only, and the induction of RORγt was not significantly affected (Figure 5C).

T cells co-cultured with RA-SKG DCs also showed reduced induction of the activation marker CD44, increased levels of naive T cell marker CD62L, but unchanged induction of the activation marker CD69 and exhaustion marker CTLA4, as compared to the T cells in co-cultures with control DCs (Figures S14B–S14E). T cells co-cultured with RA-SKG DCs also showed reduced induction of the proliferation marker Ki-67 (Figures S14D and S14E), although T cell proliferation as measured with a dye-dilution assay was variable across biological replicates and overall not significantly affected (data not shown). Thus, murine arthritis results in the production of DCs with profoundly altered capacity for T cell activation, with many of the changes predicted to be adaptive and antagonize disease progression, while others such as the reduced DC capacity for FOXP3+ and IL-10+ CD4 T cell induction could potentially contribute to the disease pathogenesis.

Discussion

In summary, in the current work we establish that the expansion of HSPCs and the induction of emergency myelopoiesis in murine rheumatoid arthritis is associated with enhanced DC production and significantly altered DC capacity for costimulatory and checkpoint marker expression, cytokine production, and T cell activation. Such modulation of DC development and function is at least in part cell intrinsic and can persist ex vivo independently of the inflammatory disease milieu, although its physiological role and persistence in situ require further investigation with long-term in vivo models.

Trained immunity, involving long-term changes in HSPC transcriptional, metabolic, and epigenetic profiles with downstream remodeling of innate immune cell differentiation and function,13,17 has become well established in vaccination and infection models.11,12 There is now a growing interest in its role in chronic inflammatory diseases.18,19 While many studies focused on monocytes and macrophages as mediators of trained immunity, in recent years similar mechanisms have been implicated for other innate immune cell lineages, including neutrophils,47,48,49,50 osteoclasts,18,51 and mast cell.52 Modulation of DC function through trained immunity mechanisms has been indicated in two recent studies in murine models, namely with C. neoformans strain H99γ immunization37 and an inoculation with cholera toxin B subunit.38 In both models, enhanced DC response to recall stimulation is reported, correlating with protection against virulent C. neoformans37 and enhanced antitumor immunity in a melanoma model,38 respectively. Our current work provides evidence for the modulation of DC development and function in murine rheumatoid arthritis, emphasizing the need for systematic evaluation of “DC training” in arthritis, as well as in other chronic inflammatory and autoimmune disorders.

The precise impact of the altered DC differentiation and function on arthritis disease mechanisms remains to be further investigated. Our initial analyses suggested an impaired capacity of DCs derived from the HSPCs of arthritis afflicted mice to respond to microbial stimulation, based on their reduced expression of costimulatory markers and inflammatory cytokines and the enhanced expression of PD-L1 and IL-10. Subsequent global transcriptional analyses of DCs confirmed these effects, however also demonstrated a strong induction of many inflammatory, immunogenic, and metabolic transcriptional signatures. Overall, we conclude that chronic inflammation in rheumatoid arthritis enhances DC production and triggers complex changes in DC transcriptome and effector functions, without a clear immunogenic or tolerogenic bias.

DCs are the most potent antigen-presenting cells with a unique capacity for the activation of naive T cells and for the priming of antigen specific immune responses.26,27 With the central role of autoreactive T cells in driving arthritis pathology in the SKG model and in human patients, it will be essential to establish the impact of altered DC functions in rheumatoid arthritis, as described in our current work, on both antigen specific and heterologous T cell activation. Several recent studies already report on the crosstalk between “trained immunity” and the adaptive immune system,53,54 primarily implicating T cells as an essential source of cytokines for the “innate immune training.” For example, BCG vaccination was shown to cross-protect against influenza A infection in mouse models by inducing memory CD4 T cells, with the T cell-derived IFNγ subsequently promoting antimicrobial training of alveolar macrophages.55 Similarly, the induction of “protective” alveolar macrophages during respiratory viral infections in mice was shown to be dependent on IFNγ production by CD8 T cells.56 In the current study, DCs derived ex vivo from the HSPCs of arthritis afflicted mice had a strongly altered capacity for CD4 T cell activation ex vivo. Future studies should apply long-term in vivo models to elucidate how such changes in DC functions in arthritis may modulate T cell-mediated immune responses and the course of arthritis disease progression.

Beyond their immunogenic function, the capacity of DCs to regulate the polarization of immune response needs to be considered when assessing their role in autoimmune disease pathogenesis.57 Arthritis onset and progression in SKG mice is linked to an expansion of autoreactive Th17 cells,58,59 and Th17-polarized response is also implicated in human rheumatic diseases.60,61,62 Therefore, it is notable that in the current study DCs derived ex vivo from the HSPCs of arthritis afflicted mice induced lower levels of Tbet+ but not RORγt+ CD4 T cells, although the induction of both IFNγ and IL-17A was reduced at least under some of the conditions. Overall, many of the changes in DC functions reported here are predicted to be adaptive and antagonize arthritis disease progression, however the reduced induction of IL-10+ and FoxP3+ CD4 T cells by RA-SKG DCs may contribute to disease pathogenesis. The overall impact of the modulation of DC development and function in rheumatoid arthritis on the disease progression in vivo remains to be further elucidated in future work.

As well as expanding the fundamental knowledge of immune dysfunction in rheumatoid arthritis, the current study may provide insights into the mechanisms of action of existing therapies and potentially suggest novel targets for therapeutic intervention. With the growing clinical application of autologous HSPC transplantation for severe autoimmune and rheumatic diseases,63,64,65 there is a critical need to understand how persistent cell-intrinsic changes in patients’ HSPCs affect the development and function of immune cells, including DCs. In the long term, it will be essential to establish whether such mechanisms favor disease remission or relapse following autologous HSPC transplantation in severe autoimmune and rheumatic diseases, and to develop approaches to either facilitate or mitigate such effects.

Limitations of the study

The following limitations of the current study point to important future directions for this work. While we established significant transcriptional and functional changes in DCs derived from HSPCs of arthritis afflicted mice, their underlying mechanisms remain to be characterized via comprehensive epigenetic analyses of cells along the full HSPC to DC differentiation trajectory. Furthermore, it will be important to establish how such changes in DC development and function affect arthritis disease mechanisms in vivo. This will entail more comprehensive mouse-to-mouse HSPC transplantation studies, analyzing the course of arthritis disease progression in the chimeric mice, and the activities of DCs in vivo in the disease context. Ultimately, the studies must expand to human arthritis patients, characterizing not only moDCs, but the HSPC to DC differentiation pathways and their functional, transcriptional, and epigenetic alterations in arthritis. Finally, future studies should explore whether similar mechanisms may affect DC development and function in other diseases of chronic systemic inflammation.

Experimental models and study participant details

Ethics statement

Mouse experiments were in accordance with guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care and protocols MCGL-7932 and MCGL-6029 approved by the McGill Animal Care Committee. Studies with human subjects were approved by the McGill University Health Center Ethics Review Board (ERB protocol number MP-37-2021-7495), and written consent was obtained from all the participants.

Mouse experimental models

SKG mouse line carries a homozygous G-to-T substitution at nucleotide 489 in the Zap70 gene on the BALB/c genetic background.39 Rheumatoid arthritis disease was induced in the mice with an intraperitoneal injection of zymosan (Millipore-Sigma, 58856-83-2) at 5 mg per mouse at the age of 8–10 weeks, as previously described.42 Control mice received an equal volume of PBS as vehicle control. The mice were analyzed at ≥5 weeks after the zymosan treatment when the animals had a clinical score of 8–16, indicating medium to severe arthritis.45 Collagen-induced arthritis (CIA) mouse model was established on genetically susceptible DBA/1 genetic background with an intradermal immunization of chicken type-II collagen (100 μg, Chondrex, 20011) emulsified in an equal volume of complete Freund’s adjuvant containing 4 mg/mL of heat-denatured Mycobacterium (Chondrex, 7001) administered at the base of the tail.42,45 Mice were maintained under specific pathogen free conditions, and matched by age and sex between all the experimental groups.

ARRIVE guidelines statement

Mouse experiments are reported according to ARRIVE guidelines. Experimental unit corresponds to a single animal. Studies used both female and male mice, sex-matched between experimental groups, however most animals were female to reflect the stronger predisposition of this sex to arthritis in both human patients and mouse models. Mouse cohort sizes weren’t set up for statistical power to disaggregate the data by sex, and this represents an important direction for future work. Test and control mice were bred as littermates and maintained in shared cages. The mice were 8–10 weeks of age at disease induction. Staff were not blinded to group allocations, and no a priori sample size calculations were carried out.

Human participants

Patients who fulfilled the following criteria were included in the study: age >18, rheumatoid arthritis classification according to the 2010 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism criteria, seropositive for rheumatoid factor and/or ACPA, and no coexisting systemic inflammatory disorders, infectious diseases, or cancer. Healthy controls matched by age and sex to the rheumatoid arthritis patients were included, selected based on having no systemic autoimmune diseases and no cancer that required treatment with chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Characteristics of study participants are summarized in Table S2. Briefly, 4 patients and 4 healthy controls were analyzed, with 3 females and 1 male in each group, with the mean age of 47 ± 7 for healthy controls and 44 ± 6 (mean ± S.D.) for the patients; of the patients two received Methotrexate, one received Upadacitinib/Leflunomide, and one received Prednisone. Cohort size was not set up for statistical power to disaggregate the data by sex; information regarding gender, ancestry, ethnicity, race, or socio-economic status are not available; such analyses represent an important direction for future work. All participants provided written informed consent.

Cell line and primary cell cultures

Cell line L-929 (ATCC, CCL-1) was used to produce M-CSF-containing L-cell conditioned media, but not as an experimental model for the studies. After purchase, the line was not independently validated or mycoplasma tested. The primary cell cultures include mouse bone marrow derived dendritic cells, mouse bone marrow derived macrophages, and human monocyte derived dendritic cells, and are described below.

Method details

Flow cytometry

Cell suspensions of mouse tissues were prepared in RPMI-1640 (ThermoFisher Scientific) with 2% (v/v) fetal calf serum (FCS, ThermoFisher Scientific), 100μg/mL streptomycin (Wisent), and 100U/mL penicillin (Wisent). The samples were stained for cell surface markers in PBS with 2% (v/v) FCS and Super Bright Complete Staining Buffer (SB-4401-75, 1:20, ThermoFisher Scientific) for 20 min on ice, using the fluorophore conjugated antibodies provided in the key resources table. Viability Dye eFluor 506 (ThermoFisher Scientific, 65-0866-14) was used to gate out dead cells. Compensation was performed with BD CompBeads (BD Biosciences) or UltraComp eBeads Plus (ThermoFisher Scientific). The data were acquired on BD Fortessa and analyzed with FlowJo software (TreeStar, BD Biosciences).

Bone marrow derived macrophage cultures

Murine bone marrow cells were resuspended at 107 per mL and seeded dropwise at 2 mLs per plate into the 150 × 25 mm non-tissue culture treated plates (Fisher Scientific) with 25 mL of complete DMEM media, containing 10% FCS (both from ThermoFisher Scientific), 30% L-cell conditioned media (produced from L-929 cell line, ATCC, CCL-1) and 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Wisent). All cultures were fed with fresh media at day 4 and harvested on day 7.

Bone marrow derived macrophage stimulations

Murine bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDMs) were harvested on day 7 and seeded at 2 × 105 cells per well into non-tissue culture treated 96 well plates (Falcon) with 200 μL of complete DMEM media to rest in an incubator at 37°C and 5% CO2 overnight. Stimulations were performed next day with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, E. coli O111:B4, Millipore-Sigma, 10 ng/mL), polyinosinic–polycytidylic acid (poly(I:C), Millipore-Sigma, 20 μg/mL), (1→3)-β-D-glucan from Alcaligenes faecalis (Millipore-Sigma, 5 μg/mL) or blank media as negative control for 12 h. Supernatants were collected for cytokine analyses, and BMDMs prepared for flow cytometry, staining with Fc blocker (TruStain FcX anti-mouse CD16/32 antibody, BioLegend 101320) and viability dye (Fixable Viability Dye eFlour 506, Thermo Fisher Scientific, 65-0866-14). For analyses of cell surface markers, BMDMs were stained with the fluorophore conjugated antibodies listed in the key resources table. For analyses of cytokine production, BMDMs were pre-treated with Monensin (BioLegend 420701) and Brefeldin A (BioLegend 420601) at the manufacturer’s recommended concentration (1:1000) for 4 h, and processed with BD Fixation/Permeabilization Solution Kit (BD Biosciences 554714) prior to intracellular staining.

Bone marrow derived dendritic cell cultures

Murine bone marrow derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were derived as previously described.72,73 Bone marrow cells were resuspended at 107 per mL and seeded dropwise at 75 μLs per well into 6-well non-tissue culture treated plates (Fisher Scientific) containing 3 mL/well of RPMI-1640 media, with 10% FCS (both from ThermoFisher Scientific), 2 mM L-glutamine (Wisent), 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Wisent), 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol (Millipore-Sigma), and 20 ng/mL granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF, #315-03, PeproTech, ThermoFisher Scientific).43 In many experiments, magnetic depletion of mature immune cells from the bone marrow samples was performed using the EasySep Streptavidin RapidSpheres Isolation Kit (#19860, Stem Cell Technologies) and biotin-conjugated antibodies against the major blood and immune lineage markers: CD3 (BioLegend #100244, 17A2), CD19 (BioLegend #115504, 6D5), NK1.1 (BioLegend #108704, PK-136), TER119 (BioLegend #116204), Ly6G (BioLegend #127604, 1A8), and F4/80 (BioLegend #123106). Resulting lineage depleted murine bone marrow cells were resuspended at 107 per mL and seeded dropwise at 70 μLs per well into the same plates and media as described above. BMDC cultures were fed with fresh media at days 3 and 6.

Bone marrow derived dendritic cell stimulation

BMDCs were harvested at days 8–9, counted, and seeded at 106 cells/mL for stimulation with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, E. coli O111:B4, Millipore-Sigma, 10 ng/mL), polyinosinic–polycytidylic acid (poly(I:C), Millipore-Sigma, 20 μg/mL), or (1→3)-β-D-glucan from Alcaligenes faecalis (Millipore-Sigma, 5 μg/mL) for 16 h. For the analyses of activation and checkpoint marker expression the cells were stained with fluorophore conjugated antibodies listed in the key resources table. For intracellular flow cytometry analyses of cytokine production, Brefeldin A and Monensin were added to block cytokine secretion over the last 4 h of stimulation (BioLegend #420701, #420601, 1:1000), BMDCs were fixed and permeabilized with the BD Fixation/Permeabilization Kit (BD Biosciences, #554714), and stained with PE anti-mouse IL-12/IL-23 p40 antibody (BioLegend #505204).

Antigen presentation and T cell stimulation assays

The assays were performed as previously described.72 Briefly, BMDCs were either untreated or stimulated with LPS (10 ng/mL, E. coli O111:B4, Millipore-Sigma), poly(I:C) (Millipore-Sigma, 20 μg/mL), or β-D-glucan (Millipore-Sigma, 5 μg/mL), together with whole ovalbumin (OVA, endotoxin-free, 0.25 mg/mL, Worthington, LS003061) for 16 h. OVA-specific CD4 T cells were isolated from the spleens of C.Cg-Tg(DO11.10)10Dlo/J mice (Jackson Laboratories, JAX 003303) using the EasySep Mouse CD4+ T cell Isolation Kit (Stem Cell Technologies, #19852). In some assays, T cells were pre-loaded with 10 μM Cell Proliferation Dye eFluor 450 (ThermoFisher Scientific), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. BMDCs were co-cultured with OVA-specific CD4 T cells in round-bottom 96-well plates for 3–4 days, in IMDM media with 10% FCS (ThermoFisher Scientific), 2 mM L-glutamine (Wisent), 100 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin (Wisent), and 50 μM β-mercaptoethanol (Millipore-Sigma), at 104 BMDCs and 105 T cells per well (1:10 ratio).

The cultures were analyzed by flow cytometry at day 3, for T cell activation markers CD44 and CD69, and for Cell Proliferation Dye eFluor 450. Alternatively, for the analyses of cytokine production, the cultures were stimulated on day 4 with Cell Activation Cocktail (BioLegend, #423302, 1:500), containing ionomycin and phorbol 12-myristate-13-acetate (PMA) for 4 h. Brefeldin A and Monensin were added to block cytokine secretion (BioLegend #420701, #420601, 1:1000) over the last 2 h. The cells were treated with the BD Fixation/Permeabilization Solution Kit (BD Biosciences, #554714), and stained for intracellular cytokines IFNγ, IL-4, IL-17, and IL-10. Alternatively, the cells were treated with the FoxP3 Transcription Factor Staining Buffer Set (ThermoFisher Scientific, #00-5523-00), and stained for transcription factors T-bet, GATA3, RORγt, and FOXP3 and for proliferation marker Ki-67. All flow cytometry analyses were conducted after pre-gating on live CD3+CD4+ T cells, and the antibodies used are listed in the key resources table.

Mouse bone marrow transplantation (or chimera) studies

For bone marrow transplantation experiments, also known as chimeras, cohorts of wild type recipients CByJ.SJL(B6)-Ptprca/J (JAX 006584, congenic for CD45.1) were irradiated with 2 doses of 3.75Gy, delivered 3 h apart, in RS-2000 irradiator (Rad Source). In one experiment, recipients were engrafted either with bone marrow from SKG mice with active arthritis (RA-SKG) or from healthy control SKG mice. In the second study, the recipients were engrafted with a 1:1 mix of bone marrow cells from CD45.1+ control allotype-marked mice and CD45.2+ SKG mice either with active arthritis (RA-SKG) or healthy controls (SKG). All marrow cells were delivered as intravenous injection in sterile PBS, and the recipient mice kept on neomycin in drinking water (2g/L, BioShop) for 3 weeks. Some cohorts of the chimeric mice were challenged with an intraperitoneal injection of zymosan (Millipore-Sigma, 58856-83-2) at 5 mg per mouse, as described previously.42 The analyses were conducted at >20 weeks post-reconstitution, and encompassed comparing the numbers and activation states of CD45.2+ versus CD45.1+ immune cells with flow cytometry, including DCs in lymphoid tissues and in BMDC cultures from the chimeric mice.

Fluorescence-activated cell sorting and RNA isolation

For the fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS), BMDCs pre-stimulated in culture for 12 h were harvested and stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies anti-CD11c APC (Biolegend, N418, #117310), anti-CD11b PerCP-Cy5.5 (Thermo Fisher, M1/70, #45-0112-82), and with the viability dye DAPI (BioLegend). Cell sorts were performed on the FACSAria Fusion (BD Biosciences) into the Mag-MAX Lysis Buffer (Ambion, AM1830). RNA was isolated using the Mag-MAX total RNA kit (Ambion, AM1830) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, and RNA yields and quality were assessed on Bioanalyzer (Agilent).

Myeloid colony forming units assays

Colony forming units (CFU) assays were performed with primary mouse bone marrow using MethoCult M3534 media following the manufacturer’s protocols (Stem Cell Technologies). Myeloid colonies were counted at 10–12 days of incubation, in duplicates.

Cytokine assays

Cytokine levels were analyzed by Eve Technologies Corporation (Calgary, AB, Canada) using the Mouse Cytokine Proinflammatory Focused 10-Plex Discovery Assay Array (MDF10) or the Human Cytokine Proinflammatory Focused 15-Plex Discovery Assay Array (HDF15).

Hematology analyses

Hematology analyses of mouse blood were performed by the Diagnostics Laboratory of the McGill Comparative Medicine Animal Resources Center (CMARC), as previously described.74

Human monocyte-derived dendritic cells

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from 40 mL of peripheral blood by gradient centrifugation using Lymphoprep and SepMate-50 IVD tubes (Stem Cell Technologies, #7811, #85450). Subsequently, monocytes were isolated with the EasySep Human Monocyte Enrichment Kit (Stem Cell Technologies, #19058), according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Monocytes were resuspended at 1 × 106 cells per mL in RPMI-1640 (ThermoFisher Scientific) with 10% (v/v) fetal calf serum (ThermoFisher Scientific), 100μg/mL streptomycin (Wisent), 100U/mL penicillin (Wisent), GM-CSF (100 ng/mL, ThermoFisher, PeproTech 300-03), and IL-4 (50 ng/mL, ThermoFisher, PeproTech 200-04). Monocytes were plated at 3 mLs per well into non-tissue culture treated 6-well plates, as a homogeneous cell suspension. The cell cultures were fed with fresh media on days 3 and 6, and harvested for stimulation on day 7. Stimulation was performed as with murine DCs, using lipopolysaccharide (LPS, E. coli O111:B4, Millipore-Sigma, 10 ng/mL), polyinosinic–polycytidylic acid (poly(I:C), Millipore-Sigma, 20 μg/mL), or (1→3)-β-D-glucan from Alcaligenes faecalis (Millipore-Sigma, 5 μg/mL) for 16 h. The cells were analyzed by flow cytometry using the antibodies listed in the key resources table.

RNA sequencing

Bulk RNA-seq was conducted as previously described.75,76 Briefly, rRNA depletion and library preparation were performed using the KAPA RNA HyperPrep Kit with RiboErase (Roche). Libraries were sequenced on Illumina Novaseq 6000 in a paired-end 100 bp configuration aiming for 50 × 106 reads per sample. The quality of sequencing reads was validated using FastQC tool (Babraham Bioinformatics), and low-quality bases were trimmed from read extremities using Trimmomatic v.0.36.66 Sequencing reads were mapped to the mouse UCSC GRCm38/mm10 reference genome assembly using Hisat2 v2.2.1.67,68,69 Gene expression was quantified by counting the number of uniquely mapped reads with featureCounts using default parameters (Subread package v1.5.2).70 Residual rRNA reads were removed, and only genes expressed above 5 counts per million reads (CPM) in at least 2 samples were retained, for a total of 11,307 genes. Dimension reduction analysis was performed using the Principal Component and Partial Least-Squares regression method.77 TMM normalization and differential gene expression analyses were conducted using the edgeR Bioconductor package.71 Genes with changes in expression ≥ |2.0| fold and Benjamini-Hochberg adjusted p values ≤0.001 were considered significant. Gene Set Enrichment Analyses (GSEA) were performed with GSEA v4.3.2 using MSigDB v2022.1, with the default configuration and permutation within the gene-sets, and with the m5.go.bp gene-set database and GOTERM_BP_DIRECT biological process GO-terms.78

Quantification and statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed with Prism 9.5.1 or 10.5.0 (GraphPad Inc.), using Student’s two-tailed t test to compare two datasets and ANOVA for multiple comparisons; p < 0.05 was considered significant. As already indicated in the text above, experimental unit throughout this study corresponds to a single animal (mouse). Further information on the statistical analyses for each dataset is provided in all the Figure Legends, with significance abbreviated as follows ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ns – not significant. Analyses of the bulk-RNA-seq data are described in the relevant subsection of method details.
