Care by Name, Not by Nature: Experiences of Older Adults Accessing a Home Care Package Including Food and Nutrition Services—An Exploratory Qualitative Study
Authors: Caitlin Wyman, Victoria Hoekman, Michael Lawless, Jolene Thomas, Alison Yaxley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo (1) explore the experiences and perceptions of older adults accessing an Australian Government Home Care Package (HCP), (2) understand how recipients prioritise their package expenditure, (3) understand the value that recipients place on food and nutrition services, and (4) understand the barriers and facilitators to spending on these services.MethodsSemi‐structured interviews were conducted with 15 HCP recipients aged 65 years or older located across Australia. Transcripts were analysed using Braun and Clarke's six‐phase reflexive thematic analysis.ResultsFour themes were developed from 15 interviews: (1) Care, By Name or By Nature; (2) We Know What We Need, Just Listen; (3) The Struggles of Prioritising and Spending; and (4) Nutritional (Im)Balance. Participants experienced a lack of person‐centred care in the HCP program, making it difficult to obtain services that met their care needs and preferences. For many, their package budget was insufficient, causing recipients to compromise on services required for safe, independent living. Nutrition was important to HCP recipients but was often not their main priority when prioritising their limited package funding. Nutrition was valued by HCP recipients but often deprioritised for more immediate care needs.ConclusionIt is apparent that changes to home care programs are required so recipients are at the centre of their care and have access to resources to help maintain their nutritional health and remain living independently at home. These insights can inform program and policy changes to better support older adults to age‐in‐place.Keywords: aged, diet, food and nutrition, home care services, qualitative research

Introduction

Globally, the fastest growing age group is people aged 65 years or older [1], projected to comprise 16% of the total population by 2050, up from 10% in 2022 [1]. This demographic shift has major implications for societal structure and function [2]. The importance of environments that support ‘ageing‐in‐place’, by enabling older people to live safely and independently in their homes and communities, has been emphasised by the United Nations (UN) [3] and the World Health Organization [4]. Ageing‐in‐place is increasingly associated with positive long‐term health and wellbeing outcomes [5] and aligns with older people's preference to remain living at home [4, 6]. Whilst population ageing coincides with an expected increase in health and long‐term care demand [7], economic benefits may be generated by facilitating healthy ageing [8] through systems which promote the capacity of older people to age independently [3]. A 2020 UN report found that countries with healthy ageing policies, such as in Europe, North America, Australia and New Zealand, had higher proportions of older people living independently [9].

Home and community‐based programs are designed to assist older people to remain living independently [10]. Many programs operate on consumer‐directed care (CDC) to foster choice and autonomy over healthcare and services received [10]. In Australia, the Government‐funded Home Care Packages (HCP) program, based on CDC, operated until November 2025, when it was replaced by the Support at Home program. The HCP program subsidised the costs of services and equipment to help older adults to age‐in‐place [11]. There were four package levels ranging from A$10,687 (Level 1, basic care needs) to A$62,013 (Level 4, high care needs) [12]. Before a HCP recipient could spend these funds, their provider charged administration fees: package management < 15% and care management capped at 20% [11]. In 2024, over 280,000 older Australians accessed a HCP [13], however, few studies have explored the experiences of recipients' package usage and expenditure [14, 15, 16].

Optimal nutritional health, including intake of adequate protein and nutrient‐dense foods, is important to promote healthy ageing, with evidence of beneficial effects on age‐related decline [17]. Yet, data suggest that a significant proportion of HCP funds were expended on personal care services, cleaning and household tasks, with only 2% spent on allied health services, such as dietetics [18]. Food and nutrition‐related services available via a HCP included shopping assistance, meal preparation, meal delivery, dietitian services and prescribed nutrition [11]. If optimised, nutrition can help maintain older people's physical health to live at home for longer [4]. To date, no research has explored the role of food and nutrition services in the HCP program. Although important, food and nutrition are only one aspect of care eligible for funding, so it is important to understand its place within the ‘big picture’ of HCP recipients' experiences. Hence, this study aimed to (1) explore the experiences and perceptions of older adults accessing a HCP, (2) understand how recipients prioritised their package expenditure, (3) understand the value that recipients placed on food and nutrition services and (4) understand the barriers and facilitators to spending on these services.

Methods

Design

An exploratory qualitative study of the experiences and values of older adults [19] related to their HCP, food and nutrition. Ethics approval was granted by Flinders University Human Research Ethics Committee (project number: 5263). All participants provided written informed consent. This study abided by the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines (File S1) [20] and was grounded in naturalistic inquiry [21], an exploratory approach in which researchers describe and interpret the experiences of the target population [22].

Recruitment

Purposive and convenience sampling were used to recruit adults [19] aged 65 years or older, receiving a HCP and able to communicate in English. Based on contemporary guidance [23] a sample size of 9–17 was identified as sufficient to achieve rich thematic insights [23], aligned with the principle of information power and determined through iterative data collection and analysis [24]. Recruitment aimed to maximise demographic reach with study information shared via social media, posters in community locations (e.g., libraries) and emails to 129 senior citizen clubs across all states and territories. Older adults who expressed interest were provided with further information via email or post.

Data Collection

Interviews were conducted face‐to‐face, by phone or videoconference per participant preference and location, audio recorded and transcribed verbatim. Both interviewers (CW and VH) were female, final‐year Bachelor of Nutrition and Dietetics (Honours) students who received appropriate training. A semi‐structured interview guide was developed and iteratively revised by the research team, experienced in aged care and qualitative research (Table 1). Field notes were documented after each interview. A demographic questionnaire collected information on participants' age, gender, location (postcode), HCP level and length of time receiving a package. To minimise participant burden in this exploratory study, detailed socio‐economic and cultural data were not collected.

Data Analysis

Interviews were the primary data source, with transcripts de‐identified and pseudonyms assigned. Transcripts were analysed using Braun and Clarke's six‐phase reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) [25], facilitated by NVivo software (Version 13) [26]. The inductive RTA approach aligned with the study's exploratory nature, starting with participants' narratives and interpreting their subjective meaning using a data‐driven approach [25]. The codes, categories and themes generated were discussed and validated by the research team to ensure trustworthiness [27]. An example of theme development and its relationship with the interview data are shown in Table 2. Consistent with RTA, an iterative assessment of information power [24] was used to deem when further data collection was unlikely to generate new insights beyond those identified. Demographic data were analysed using descriptive statistics and presented as median and range.

Results

Fifteen participants were recruited to this study. Interviews (30–78 min) were conducted with each participant between October 2022 and November 2023. Participants were mostly female (n = 11) with a median age of 75.5 years (range: 65–87 years). They had been receiving HCP services for a median of 3 years (range: 1 month–7 years), and more than half had a Level 4 package (n = 8) (Table 3). Four main themes were developed from the data: (1) Care, By Name or By Nature; (2) We Know What We Need, Just Listen; (3) The Struggles of Prioritising and Spending; and (4) Nutritional (Im)Balance; described in detail below, supported by illustrative quotations here and in Table 4.

Theme 1: Care, by Name or by Nature?

This theme explored participants' overall experiences with their HCP and the program, broadly relating to the first research aim. The sub‐themes highlighted the contradictions in care received, between financial gains and recipients feeling like they were a number versus the positive experiences of person‐centred care.

Perception of Providers' Priorities

More than half of participants mentioned or alluded to feeling like their providers were more interested in money than their care. In participants' views, providers were more focused on finances than working towards understanding and addressing their needs. Rosemary captured this perspective: ‘it is very much money motivated; it is not about care’. Participants commented on the costs added by their providers, in addition to care and package management fees. Nancy spoke of ‘hidden fees,’ with her cleaner receiving A$27/h, although her package was charged A$60/h. These additional fees reduced recipients' funds and consequently, the services they could access. Richard felt like ‘they value the dollar more than what they value you’, with many participants believing that their needs were not their provider's main priority.

The Number‐Focused Experience

Many participants experienced a lack of care throughout the HCP program including from the government, providers, case managers and support workers, highlighting the seemingly contradictory nature of the program. Helen expressed this bluntly; ‘she is not my care manager because she doesn't care’. Participants did not feel at the centre of their care. Alice felt like ‘just a number out there’ , while Nancy shared that her experience had ‘not [been] recipient focused, at all’. As most participants were reliant on HCP services to remain at home, it was important that they met their needs. Flexibility to choose the timing of service delivery was frequently constrained. Failing to provide a personalised time for services or workers arriving late was seen to disregard recipients' time, which was particularly impactful as they often had other appointments to schedule around.

The Person‐Centred Experience

Amongst all the negative experiences shared by participants, there were still stories of providers, managers and workers that were perceived to care. This was more often the case for self‐managed participants as they could choose their workers and develop relationships with them. Having someone that cared was compared with employing a daughter, ‘who knows what you need’ (Jill) and ‘like being a real person again’ (Audrey). Staff who took the time to listen to, understand and meet individuals' needs were highly valued by participants. Communication which facilitated participants to have choice and control over their package, to receive services that met their needs, contributed to a person‐centred experience.

Theme 2: We Know What We Need, Just Listen

This theme provided further insight into participants' HCP experiences, with sub‐themes focusing on their experience of fighting for their needs and trying to access services and information.

Not Being Heard

The feeling of not being heard was common, linking back to the impersonal experience of care (sub‐theme 1.2), although less so for self‐managed participants. Some participants explained knowing what support services they needed and how they wanted them delivered, but their individual requirements were not always considered. Nancy ‘could offer them so much in term[s] of [her] opinion of what was happening, but they wouldn't listen’. Participants expressed frustration with not being listened to or communicated with. Not only were their voices and immediate needs unheard, but their overall experiences of HCP services were not always understood; they ‘don't actually understand what it's like on the ground’ (Audrey). Their complaints and understanding of their situation, including how their care could be improved, were often ignored.

Fighting for Themselves

Participants shared experiences of not being treated as autonomous due to their age and the difficulties faced in implementing decisions about their care. Rosemary stated, ‘you're old, you're stupid, you've got government funds, and we'll tell you what you can spend them on’. Many participants spoke of fighting to get the care and services they needed to remain living independently at home, exemplified by Helen who expected the need to advocate for herself: ‘it's just another drama, another fight’. Due to perceived insufficiency of information and support, participants were often required to do their own research and advocacy, supporting each other to navigate the HCP system. As Hazel said, ‘there's a lot of things we don't know about’, with Barbara voicing the experience of multiple participants in having to find ‘a whole lot of things, I have to find out myself’. Some participants had to instigate conversations around their care, to ensure that their needs were met, instead of being told of their HCP entitlements. Engaging in Facebook forums dedicated to sharing HCP experiences and information was common amongst participants. There was an overwhelming sense of frustration from participants, but most had the capacity and resources to fight for their needs.

Theme 3: The Struggles of Prioritising and Spending

Participants faced challenges when prioritising and spending their HCP funds on essential services that enabled them to stay at home and maintain their health. This theme delved into some of the barriers and facilitators to spending, which is further explored in relation to food and nutrition services in Theme 4.

The Decision‐Making Process

Package expenditure was based on participants' needs, with funds spent on services which: (1) they could no longer physically do themselves, such as household maintenance and transport, (2) help them stay at home, such as domestic assistance, and (3) support their health, such as physiotherapy. For Alice, the HCP is ‘about keeping people in their homes… I need all those services to keep me here’. Participants had to budget for current and anticipated care needs. Some also mentioned being mindful of getting the most out of their funding, whilst still receiving quality products and services, like Hazel who wanted ‘to get the best quote so all [her HCP] money's not used up’. A few participants felt comfort in knowing they had a safety net of funds available if their health deteriorated in the future.

The Uncertainties of Spending

Confusion surrounding what participants could spend and claim under their HCP further contributed to their Struggles of Prioritising and Spending. Donald shared that the HCP manual was ‘very good on telling you what you can't spend money on [but] not about what you can spend money on’. The stress associated with these experiences of uncertainty and not knowing whether services or products would be approved by their provider was evident in participants' voices as they shared their stories. Doris felt ‘anxiety with a new provider [and] whether they're going to say yes or no’. Stress was also apparent for some participants who were unsure how they would manage at home if the products and services they needed were not approved. Consequently, three participants spoke of resorting to using their own funds, as ‘it would have taken months’ (Jill) to get HCP approval.

Going Without

For many participants, their funding did not meet all their needs, so they were faced with the difficult decision of cutting back on care. Participants were left to neglect basic services, such as cleaning and personal care, due to higher priorities, often at the cost of living comfortably. For example, Irene prioritised physiotherapy ‘because it's needed straight away, saying ‘I don't have to have a shower for couple of days’. Although Hazel's package funding was sufficient to cover massages and chiropractic care, she had to forgo the services because she ‘wasn't getting reimbursed in time to have [her] next appointment’. Participants who could not afford to supplement their HCP with their own funds, were left with no other choice than to go without.

Theme 4: Nutritional (Im)balance

This theme broadly related to the third and fourth research aims, with the sub‐themes exploring the role of food in participants' lives and the complex dynamics of relying on food and nutrition services within the constraints of HCP funding.

Food Is More Than Nutrition

All participants knew and valued the importance of food in providing good nutrition to support their overall health. However, they also desired palatable and tasty food, with a common preference for fresh meals and variety. Participants shared an attitude that beyond its nutritional benefit, food provided enjoyment. As stated by Irene, ‘we all have to eat to live and it has to be a somewhat pleasurable experience, particularly when you get old and there's nothing much left to have pleasures about’.

The social aspects related to food, particularly grocery shopping as an outing, were mentioned by a few participants, like Barbara who said, ‘ I try not to do a big shop … it's better for me to go several times a week’. If they were able, participants preferred to do their own shopping, despite grocery delivery being subsidised through their HCP, particularly to select their own fresh produce.

The Cost of Balancing Priorities

Despite being aware of the importance of nutrition, participants often spoke of struggling to access appropriate food to meet their requirements, as they had to spend their HCP funds on more urgent priorities. Audrey spoke of trying to ‘economise so I haven't been ordering as many meals … I've been eating, not as well’. Some participants had lost the motivation to cook for themselves, like Richard who ‘used to always cook but … stopped doing that because it's only me’. It was difficult for some participants to prioritise preparing and cooking their own meals due to fatigue, reduced mobility or age‐related decline. Consequently, multiple participants opted for simpler meals of lower nutritional value, such as toast, as it was easier to prepare and for some was a way to save money. Further, half of participants mentioned reduced appetites and eating smaller meals, likely at the cost of their nutritional health. This ranged from Helen, who had ‘sort of gone off food’, to Lois who ‘ couldn't care less whether [she ate] much at all ’. Although meal preparation is approved under the HCP, most participants that could have benefited from the assistance couldn't justify the staff hours as it was ‘quite time consuming ’ and ‘ wouldn't be cost effective ’, with the associated fees lessening funds available for other aspects of care.

Inadequate Products and Services

Nutritional imbalance was contributed to by pre‐prepared meal companies which did not cater to participants' dietary needs or food preferences. The meals could not be modified to individuals' dietary requirements, such as coeliac disease or intolerances, or there were limited options available. As Irene said, ‘there are a lot of things that are not catered for in those bulk prepared meals’. The taste, texture and frozen aspect of pre‐prepared meals, did not meet the standards of participants who valued the quality of their food (sub‐theme 4.1). It was common for participants to have tried multiple companies as they were not satisfied with the products or online ordering experience. However, participants living in regional or rural areas of Australia were not afforded the luxury of multiple options. For Helen, no companies delivered meals to where she lived, asking ‘How can you access them if they don't exist?’ Location also presented a barrier to finding workers for meal preparation. Lois told of returning home after a hospital admission and resorting to paying a friend with her own funds, after her unmet request for ‘assistance for someone to come and cook a meal for [her] at night’. Even in the case where a recipient has package funds available to spend on food and nutrition services, barriers still exist.

Discussion

This study is the first to explore where nutrition fits in the context of Australian HCP recipients, including their experiences with accessing a package and spending their funds. Participants expressed their perceived lack of care in the HCP experience, seeing it as not being personalised to their individual needs. They knew what support and services they needed but often did not feel heard, having to advocate for themselves. Although nutrition was important to participants, for most, it was not the main priority to help them stay at home when prioritising limited funds.

Experiences of feeling cared for were an uncommon occurrence, contradictory to the program name and CDC marketing, yet consistent with the few studies that have explored the experiences of Australian HCP recipients [14, 15, 16]. The theme of Struggles with Prioritising and Spending was also apparent in a previous qualitative study of HCP recipients, or their substitute decision‐makers, finding participants were confused and uncertain of their entitlements and package expenditure [16]. Similarly, a study requiring participants to simulate spending funds and explain their decision‐making process found they had to make trade‐offs between services due to limited funding [15]. A further qualitative study exploring HCP consumers' experiences identified a lack of access to reliable information, and that providers were not always delivering CDC and often had unreasonable fees [14], aligning with the theme Care, By Name or By Nature. Participants' difficulty in receiving services that met their needs is not unique to Australia's home‐based aged care system. The main theme of a systematic review of home care in Canada was the unmet needs of older adults, including their nutritional health, funding support and service satisfaction [28]. In Sweden, older adults told of feeling like they were unable to influence their home care and found it difficult to, or were prevented from, speaking up about their needs and preferences [29].

Contrary to the predominant lack of care felt by participants, were the experiences of self‐managed HCP recipients. These participants paid less administration fees and had more control over their services. However, not all older adults have the abilities or resources to self‐manage their HCP and, regardless, CDC should underpin both provider‐managed and self‐managed packages. These experiences were not just apparent in home care for older adults. A study of rural clients using Australia's National Disability Insurance Scheme (NDIS) found that they also had to advocate for their needs, and either battled with the NDIS or took on the burden of self‐management [30], to Fight for Themselves. This questions the role of self‐management in healthcare, as responsibility often falls onto the individual to meet their own needs and disregards the importance of environmental factors, including relationships and resources [31, 32, 33]. Furthermore, a difference exists between someone being able to make decisions about their own care and their ability to act on those choices [31]. Hence, ageing‐related policies and programs, such as Australian home care programs, must promote the capacity of older adults through creating supportive environments and communities [31].

In relation to food and HCP recipients, this study has created knowledge where there was none. Good nutrition as part of a healthy lifestyle can slow the ageing process and help to prevent age‐related loss of muscle mass and strength [17]. However, age‐related changes in physiological and metabolic function as well as eating behaviours, such as reduced appetite and consumption of smaller meals, place the older population at increased nutritional risk [34]. Hence, when older adults are further constricted in meeting their nutritional requirements, such as by the lack of funding, adequate products or accessible services reported by participants, this compromises their ability to live healthily at home. Thus, to support good nutrition, ageing‐related interventions must consider the broader context of older adults' lives [34], highlighting that there is no singular key to ageing‐in‐place.

Strengths and Limitations

This study is the first to explore where nutrition fits in the context of HCP recipients and adds more recent perspectives to a very limited understanding of how recipients experienced the program. Despite under‐representing people from culturally diverse backgrounds, a range of perspectives was provided from participants of varying age, health status, location and HCP level, including those who did and did not use food and nutrition services. The semi‐structured interviews and guiding questions enabled a large amount of data to be collected; hence, a nuanced narrative was obtained and was sufficient in achieving information power to answer the research question. A limitation of this study was that a single analyst coded the interview data, yet RTA acknowledges the subjective researcher role. Credibility was strengthened via team discussion throughout the analysis, including sharing preliminary codes to develop and refine themes based on the dataset, and by grounding interpretation in direct participant quotations.

Conclusions

This project captured the voices of a group of HCP recipients, providing insight into their experiences with using their package, including food and nutrition services. It has highlighted the need for better monitoring and regulation across home care programs to ensure all recipients are experiencing CDC. Further, results have identified a need to monitor the nutritional health of home care recipients, who often modify their meals due to financial constraints, fatigue or lack of motivation. A gap has been identified in the accessibility of appropriate meal providers and workers available for meal preparation. Research in the home care space is limited, with existing literature becoming outdated with changes to policies, programs and population demographics. Additional research is required to triangulate these findings with the perspectives of providers, case managers and support workers. Finally, capping additional fees associated with package management and services should be reconsidered to allow for a greater proportion of recipients' funds to be spent on their care needs.

This sample of HCP recipients experienced a lack of person‐centred care in the program, making it difficult for them to obtain services that met their care needs and preferences. For many, their package budget was insufficient to support their age‐related care needs, causing recipients to compromise on services needed to live safely at home. As such, the HCP program did not appear to be working as intended. Nutrition is important to HCP recipients but was often not their main priority in their limited package funding. Based on this research, it is apparent that changes are required to home care programs so recipients are truly at the centre of their care and have access to resources that can help them to maintain their nutritional health and remain living independently at home. With the reform of the Australian aged care system and introduction of the Support at Home program on 1 November 2025, it remains to be seen how recipients' experiences will change but this research provides a baseline from which to monitor progress in the new system.
