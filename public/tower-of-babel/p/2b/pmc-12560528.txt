Epidemiology, resistance profiles, and risk factors of multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae: experience from a low- and middle-income country
Authors: Banan M Aiesh, Nour Mousa, Ruba Jomaa, Yousef Zouneh, Dania Abuhalima, Motaz Saifi, Ashraqat Namrouti, Adham Abutaha, Moutaz W Sweileh, Samah W Al-Jabi, Ali Sabateen, Sa’ed H Zyoud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundKlebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae) is an Enterobacteriaceae and is also a normal gastrointestinal flora. Antimicrobial resistance is a growing public health concern, posing substantial challenges to modern medical practice. In this context, the aim of this study was to investigate the factors contributing to multidrug-resistant (MDR) K. pneumoniae and the prevalence and antimicrobial resistance patterns of K. pneumoniae strains isolated from patients in a major tertiary care center in Palestine. This study provides insights into multidrug-resistant pathogens, which is critical given their serious public health implications.MethodsFrom January 2022 to December 2023, a large tertiary care hospital hosted a retrospective, hospital-based study. The hospital information system provided the K. pneumoniae data. Patients of all sexes and all ages who had any kind of K. pneumoniae isolated from clinical specimens met the inclusion criteria. Patients from hemodialysis centers and outpatient clinics, as well as 20 people with insufficient medical data, were not included, resulting in a final cohort of 376 patients. This study analysed the demographic, clinical, and microbiological characteristics of patients with K. pneumoniae infections.ResultsThe mean age of patients with K. pneumoniae infections was 49.9 years, with a significantly lower mean age in the MDR group (48.5 vs. 56.4 years, p = 0.004). A greater proportion of patients were female across all subgroups. Common comorbidities included cardiovascular disease (43.4%), solid malignancies (25.8%), and diabetes mellitus (29%), although only hematological malignancies were significantly different among the subgroups (p = 0.013). Central line catheterization was significantly more common among ESBL-producing and CR-K. pneumoniae cases (p = 0.007). The most common culture sources were rectal swabs for CR-K. pneumoniae (56.6%) and urine samples for ESBL-producing strains (40%, p < 0.001). High resistance rates were observed against cephalosporins and ciprofloxacin, particularly in the ESBL and CR strains. Younger age was the only significant risk factor associated with MDR K. pneumoniae, whereas sex and comorbidities were not.ConclusionThe findings of this study highlight the significant prevalence of multidrug-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae, a pathogen responsible for various infections. These results emphasize the critical need for antimicrobial stewardship programs to evaluate and manage antibiotic usage effectively to help combat resistance. Additionally, stringent infection control measures are necessary to reduce the risk of cross-transmission.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Antimicrobial resistance, K. pneumoniae, Multidrug resistance, Palestine, Risk factors

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance remains one of the most critical and unsolved challenges worldwide. The World Health Organization (WHO) has recognized it as a prominent threat to both global health and sustainable development [1]. According to 2021 estimates, antimicrobial resistance is associated with 4.71 million bacteria, with a recent projection suggesting that these numbers will increase to 10 million by 2050 [2, 3]. The increase in antimicrobial resistance among bacterial pathogens represents a serious public health concern, creating significant challenges for contemporary medical practice and increasing healthcare costs [4]. Antimicrobial resistance arises through four primary mechanisms: reduced cell wall permeability, active efflux of drugs from bacterial cells before they take effect, enzymatic inactivation of antimicrobial agents (such as penicillinases or novel carbapenemases), and modifications of target sites, including changes to penicillin-binding proteins [5]. These mechanisms allow bacteria to withstand nearly every antibiotic developed [5]. Antimicrobial agent misuse and overuse have also contributed to the global emergence of antimicrobial resistance [6].

Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae), a member of the Enterobacteriaceae family, is commonly found as part of the normal human gastrointestinal microbiota [7]. K. pneumoniae can be acquired in hospitals or communities. It contributes to a range of illnesses, including pneumonia, urinary tract infections (UTIs), meningitis, peritonitis, and septicaemia [7]. In five of the six World WHO regions, over 50% of K. pneumoniae isolates from hospital settings exhibited resistance to third-generation cephalosporins [8]. K. pneumoniae-resistant strains have drawn increasing attention as a public health concern [9].

Extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing bacteria often display multidrug resistance. This resistance is mediated by bacterial plasmids, which can also carry genes conferring resistance to other antibiotic classes. Such cross-resistance restricts treatment options, increases the dependence on costly broad-spectrum agents such as carbapenems, and contributes to prolonged hospital stays. Ineffective treatment may also be prescribed since resistant strains may not be detected via standard drug susceptibility tests. Therefore, further studies are needed to monitor changes in the response of bacterial pathogens to antibiotics [10].

Carbapenems are still the most effective antibiotic agents against ESBL producers [11]. However, as time progressed, carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae (CRE) emerged. CRE infections have limited treatment options and a significant death rate [12]. Diagnosing, treating, and preventing such infections requires specialized expertise. Therefore, since antimicrobial resistance is a constantly evolving field, updating the latest information on resistance is crucial for providing optimal patient care. The adoption of robust antimicrobial stewardship practices is essential for minimizing the prevalence of resistant pathogens in communities [13]. In many low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Palestine, the burden of MDR K. pneumoniae is compounded by limited surveillance systems, widespread antibiotic misuse, and constrained healthcare resources [14–16]. Despite its clinical importance—ranging from urinary tract infections to life-threatening sepsis—there remains a significant gap in understanding the local resistance patterns and associated risk factors for MDR strains in tertiary healthcare settings across LMICs [17, 18]. Furthermore, the increasing prevalence of extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing and carbapenem-resistant (CR) K. pneumoniae strains has severely limited therapeutic options, often necessitating the use of last-resort agents such as polymyxins, which are themselves becoming less effective owing to increasing resistance [19, 20]. These challenges contribute to increased morbidity, longer hospital stays, and elevated healthcare costs, especially when resistant strains are not readily detected by standard susceptibility testing [21, 22]. Given these alarming trends and the scarcity of region-specific data, there is an urgent need to evaluate the resistance profiles of K. pneumoniae and identify potential risk factors for MDR infections in Palestine. Addressing this knowledge gap is critical for informing empirical therapy, guiding infection control strategies, and implementing effective antimicrobial stewardship programs.

Previous studies on K. pneumoniae in Palestine have been conducted mainly in Gaza and have focused largely on prevalence and molecular characterization [23, 24]. In contrast, our study was carried out at the West Bank at a major tertiary referral hospital in Nablus and provides updated epidemiological data on ESBL-producing and carbapenem-resistant isolates. In addition, it examines associated demographic and clinical risk factors, which were not comprehensively addressed in earlier research. This geographic and methodological distinction adds novel insights relevant to infection control and empirical therapy in Palestinian healthcare settings.

This study was conducted to evaluate the antimicrobial resistance profile and prevalence of K. pneumoniae isolates obtained from patients at a major tertiary care hospital in Palestine. This study also investigated the possible factors contributing to the emergence of multidrug-resistant strains of K. pneumoniae. Moreover, the study illustrates the demographic and clinical characteristics of the patients, including their medical interventions and distribution among hospital wards. This study provides insights into multidrug-resistant pathogens, which is critical given their serious public health implications.

Methods

Study design, setting, timeframe, and inclusion criteria

A retrospective cross-sectional study was carried out at An-Najah National University Hospital (NNUH) in Nablus, Palestine; patients who were admitted between January 2022 and December 2023 to different intensive care units and hospital departments were included. All ages, sexes, and patients with K. pneumoniae isolates were included. Patients with incomplete medical records and those from hemodialysis facilities and outpatient clinics were excluded. Data were gathered over a period of five months. A total of 376 unique K. pneumoniae isolates, with one isolate per patient, were included in the microbiological and resistance analyses. This number was greater than the 370 patients needed to improve the statistical power and reliability.

Data collection form

We collected all the required data via comprehensive Excel sheets, and our research focused primarily on the following key areas:

Section 1: We gathered demographic data, including age, sex, and any preexisting medical conditions, such as diabetes mellitus, solid tumors, hematological malignancies, neurologic disorders, cardiovascular disease, and renal disease. In addition, we identified the departments that treated the patients: pediatric, cardiology, general surgery, bone marrow transplantation, and several intensive care units (ICUs), including the surgical, pediatric, cardiac, and medical ICUs. Ultimately, we categorized each patient’s overall outcome as either death or discharge.

Ethical consideration

The study was granted ethical approval by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of An-Najah National University and received authorization from the Clinical Research Center at NNUH to access the data. All collected information was strictly limited to research purposes, handled with full confidentiality, and available solely to the study investigators. Patients were assigned numerical codes to safeguard anonymity.

Microbiological methods

Bacterial identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing were conducted via the VITEK® 2 Compact system (bioMérieux). Multidrug resistance (MDR) was defined as nonsusceptibility to ≥ 1 agent in ≥ 3 antimicrobial categories, and pandrug resistance (PDR) was defined as resistance to all agents in all categories; no PDR K. pneumoniae strains were identified [25].

The AST-N417 cards were used for routine testing. For MDR isolates, an extended antibiotic panel was applied. The system was employed to detect extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) production by comparing the growth of isolates treated with beta-lactam antibiotics with and without clavulanic acid [26–28]. Carbapenemase production was confirmed phenotypically via the modified carbapenem inactivation method (mCIM) and EDTA-modified mCIM (eCIM) [29–31].

In line with hospital policy, only isolates from clinical samples (e.g., blood and urine) underwent full susceptibility testing; rectal swab isolates were used solely for carbapenem-resistant species screening.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS version 25 was used for the data analysis. The microbial organisms involved, the organisms’ susceptibility patterns, the initial antimicrobial treatments, and the patients’ demographic and clinical characteristics were all compiled via descriptive statistics. The strains were divided into three groups: CR-K. pneumoniae, ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae, non-ESBL, and non-CR K. pneumoniae. Age is shown as the mean and standard deviation, and the results are displayed as frequencies and percentages. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used to evaluate the relationships between risk factors and mortality; a p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We first used the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test to determine whether the continuous variables were normal to ensure that the right statistical tests were chosen. To determine if the data are normally distributed, this test is frequently employed. We compared the means between two groups (MDR vs. non-MDR) via Student’s t test for variables that met the assumption of normality. For comparisons involving more than two groups, such as non-ESBL, non-CR K. pneumoniae, ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae, and CR-K. pneumoniae, we employed one-way ANOVA. This method is appropriate for comparing means across multiple groups when the data are normally distributed. These tests were conducted to ensure that the statistical methods chosen were suitable for both the distribution of the data and the number of groups involved.

Results

Baseline characteristics of patients with different K. pneumoniae strains

After excluding duplicate isolates, 376 unique K. pneumoniae isolates were included in the microbiological and resistance analyses. The mean age of patients with K. pneumoniae infections was 49.86 years (± 20.55), with a statistically significant difference between groups (p = 0.017); patients with non-ESBL and non-CR K. pneumoniae infections had a greater mean age (56.43 years) than those with ESBL-producing or CR-K. pneumoniae infections did. Females represented the majority of cases overall (55.9%), with no statistically significant sex differences among the groups (p = 0.737). With respect to comorbidities, cardiovascular diseases were the most common (43.4%), followed by diabetes mellitus (29%) and hematological malignancies (32.2%). A significant association was observed for hematological malignancies, which were more prevalent among patients with CR-K. pneumoniae infections (p = 0.013).

Among medical interventions, central line catheterization was significantly associated (p = 0.007), with ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae being more common (43.1%) than in the other groups. Intubation and urinary catheterization were not significantly different across the groups (p = 0.506 and p = 0.084, respectively). In terms of infection onset, 59.1% of the infections were present at admission, whereas 41% were hospital-acquired; however, the differences between the groups were not significant (p = 0.091). Prior hospitalization within the past 3 months showed a borderline significant difference (p = 0.049), with higher frequencies in the CR-K. pneumoniae group (58.7%). Prior antibiotic use was common (85.4% overall), with no statistically significant differences among the groups (p = 0.153). Additional details are provided in Table 1.

Ward-based patterns of K. pneumoniae infection

The prevalence of K. pneumoniae across different patient groups in the surgical intensive care unit (ICU) ranged from 6.2% to 9.1%, with no statistically significant differences observed (p = 0.733). Comparable patterns were noted in the general surgery, pediatric, and cardiac surgery departments. Similarly, the distribution of K. pneumoniae was not significantly different in the bone marrow transplant ward (p = 0.825) or the internal medicine ward (p = 0.984). In contrast, statistically significant differences were identified in the pediatric critical care unit (p = 0.007), where carbapenem-resistant K. pneumoniae (CR-Kp) was not detected, and in the medical ICU (p = 0.041), where ESBL-producing strains were more frequently encountered. Sources of K. pneumoniae isolates

Urine represented the most frequent source of Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates, particularly among non-carbapenem-resistant (non-CR) strains, and was significantly predominant among ESBL-producing strains (40%, p < 0.001). In contrast, rectal swabs were strongly associated with CR-K. pneumoniae, accounting for 56.6% of these isolates (p < 0.001). Blood cultures were more commonly associated with ESBL-producing strains (21.6%, p = 0.002), whereas sputum and wound samples also presented significantly higher proportions of ESBL-positive isolates (p < 0.001 and p = 0.023, respectively). Antibiotic resistance rates of K. pneumoniae strains

Antibiotic resistance varied significantly among the K. pneumoniae groups. Ciprofloxacin resistance was high overall (51.4%) and peaked in CR-K. pneumoniae. Cephalosporins also had high resistance rates, especially among ESBL strains. Carbapenem resistance was almost exclusive to CR-K. pneumoniae (p < 0.001). Significant differences were also observed for piperacillin-tazobactam and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (p < 0.001). In contrast, resistance to nitrofurantoin, fosfomycin, and norfloxacin remained relatively low, with minimal intergroup variation. The full details are presented in Table 4. Potential risk factors for multidrug-resistant K. pneumoniae species

Table 5 presents the evaluation of potential risk factors for MDR-K. pneumoniae, which were assessed by comparing the non-MDR and MDR groups, with ESBL-producing and CR-K. pneumoniae categorized as MDR. MDR status was not significantly associated with sex (p = 0.573). A younger age was observed in the MDR group (mean 48.46 vs. 56.43 years, p = 0.004). Among the comorbidities, solid malignancies were observed less frequently in the MDR group (p = 0.039), whereas other conditions, including renal disease, cardiovascular disease, and diabetes mellitus, were not significantly associated.

Discussion

Gram-negative bacteria with drug resistance—especially those producing ESBLs and carbapenemases—are increasingly recognized as critical global health threats. AMR has emerged as one of the most pressing and unresolved challenges confronting public health worldwide. The key drivers of this crisis include the misuse of antibiotics and the insufficient implementation of robust antimicrobial stewardship programs.

This study analysed a range of patient characteristics and risk factors across three categories of Klebsiella pneumoniae isolates: non-ESBL/non-CR, ESBL-producing, and carbapenem-resistant (CR) strains. The overall mean age of patients with K. pneumoniae infections was 49.86 years, with females comprising a slightly greater proportion (55.9%). This finding contrasts with a study conducted in New Delhi, where females accounted for a lower percentage of cases (45.2%) [32]. Consistent with findings from India, our study also identified urine as the most common source of ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae isolates (40%). This finding supports the literature that highlights the urinary tract as a primary site for infection or colonization by ESBL-producing bacteria. An Indian study reported that ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae isolates were predominantly detected in urine samples (40%), which aligns with our findings and is consistent with other studies indicating that urine is a key reservoir for ESBL-producing bacterial strains [33–35]. Recognizing local and institutional patterns of AMR is essential for informing empiric treatment strategies for UTIs and highlighting the broader AMR burden faced by healthcare systems.

Our investigation revealed a greater prevalence of gram-negative bacilli that produce ESBLs or exhibit resistance to carbapenems in the general surgery department and among emergency inpatients than in the ICU and medical ward. On the other hand, a Saudi Arabian study revealed that surgical intensive care units had the greatest percentage [36]. Furthermore, the surgical ward had the greatest proportion, according to another study in Palestine that focused solely on hospital-acquired diseases [33]. Moreover, diabetes mellitus was present in 29% of the patients in our sample, which is consistent with results from Missouri in 2018 [37].

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the prevalence of antibiotic resistance patterns among K. pneumoniae isolates from patients admitted to a large academic tertiary care hospital. Our findings demonstrated a notably high prevalence of MDR, with 82.2% of the isolates classified as MDR, including both ESBL-producing and carbapenem-resistant strains. In comparison, a study conducted in Iran by Atieh et al. [7], which was based on 250 culture-positive clinical samples of K. pneumoniae, reported a considerably lower MDR prevalence of 46.6% for K. pneumoniae. A comparison of our study results with those of another study by Lina et al. [38] revealed that urine samples from K. pneumoniae had MDR rates of 85%, with varying resistance rates compared with our study. A number of reasons, including improper antibiotic prescribing methods, regional genetic variations, and the complexity of cases handled at these facilities, may be responsible for the observed discrepancy in MDR rates between our study and earlier research.

In our study, high rates of resistance were detected in ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae strains, especially those to third-generation cephalosporins and fluoroquinolones, with resistance rates of 98.5% for cefotaxime, 93.9% for both ceftriaxone and ceftazidime, and 92.4% for ciprofloxacin. Similarly, the CR-K. pneumoniae strains exhibited considerable resistance to ciprofloxacin (42.3%), ceftazidime (42.7%), and piperacillin–tazobactam (42.3%).

In a study conducted in Jordan, 45% of the isolates were ceftazidime resistant, and 42% were ciprofloxacin resistant [39]. Notably, our study revealed that 50.8% of ESBL-producing bacteria were resistant to gentamicin, whereas previous studies reported that this incidence varied between 20% [40] and 54.1% [41]. Additionally, resistance to amikacin among the ESBL-producing K. pneumoniae strains in our study was relatively low at 3.1% and even slightly lower than the 7% reported in the Omani study [40]. This method can assist in selecting an empirical regimen for organisms that produce ESBLs, where amikacin is preferred owing to decreased resistance rates.

A study in Egypt focusing on K. pneumoniae isolated from catheter-associated urinary tract infections reported alarmingly high levels of multidrug resistance: 94.9% of the isolates were MDR, with complete resistance to imipenem, whereas amikacin had the lowest resistance, 42.4% [42]. These findings reinforce the critical need for targeted antimicrobial stewardship and the cautious use of last-resort agents such as imipenem when treating K. pneumoniae infections in similar hospital settings.

In our study, the resistance rate to amoxicillin-clavulanic acid was 41.5% across all K. pneumoniae isolates, but the strains were less resistant to imipenem (28.8%). In contrast, a study in northern Ethiopia (Gondar) reported an MDR prevalence of 57.1% among K. pneumoniae isolates, with high resistance to beta-lactams (100% to nalidixic acid, cefotaxime, and cefazolin) but preserved imipenem susceptibility at 85.7% and meropenem susceptibility at 79% [43]. Similarly, a study conducted in Khartoum, Sudan, reported an MDR prevalence of 80% among K. pneumoniae but a much higher rate of carbapenem resistance (41%), indicating the significantly reduced effectiveness of imipenem and meropenem compared with our findings [44]. These regional differences emphasize how crucial local resistance monitoring is to direct successful empirical treatment.

A high rate of antimicrobial resistance is associated with prolonged hospitalizations and increased morbidity and mortality. Addressing this issue requires collective efforts to curb inappropriate antibiotic use and mitigate the escalating threat of resistance. Key strategies include the implementation of effective antimicrobial stewardship programs and the regular updating of antibiograms to guide healthcare providers in choosing the most suitable empirical therapy. Standard laboratory tests for drug susceptibility may not detect these resistant bacteria, which could result in ineffective therapies. Antimicrobial surveillance studies help to track changes in the way bacterial infections react to medications. Effective surveillance and monitoring strategies are needed to prevent the emergence of resistant microorganisms linked to these severe infections and health burdens, as well as to put policy recommendations on appropriate antibiotic usage into practice [45].

In our study, risk factors for MDR K. pneumoniae infection, such as age, sex, medical interventions, comorbidities, and hospital stay, were examined. Sex was not a significant factor (p = 0.573). Although younger age was observed in patients with MDR K. pneumoniae (mean 48.46 vs. 56.43 years, p = 0.004), this association should be interpreted cautiously because of the retrospective design and absence of multivariable adjustment for potential confounders. Similarly, the apparent lower frequency of solid malignancies in the MDR group should not be considered a definitive protective factor. However, a multicenter study in Lebanon reported that male sex was a significant risk factor for MDR K. pneumoniae infections [46].

The relationship between comorbidities and MDR K. pneumoniae varied within our study population. Renal disease was not significantly associated with MDR status (p = 0.836), and other conditions, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, and malignancies, were also not significantly linked. In contrast, a study from China reported that cerebrovascular disease was a significant risk factor for MDR K. pneumoniae infections [47]. Additionally, there was no significant difference in the average length of hospital stay between the MDR and non-MDR groups (p = 0.503), a finding that contrasts with a Chinese study, which reported that longer hospital stays were strongly associated with MDR K. pneumoniae infections [48]. These differences may reflect varying healthcare practices, patient populations, or resistance mechanisms across regions.

Antibiotic-resistant bacteria are becoming a global problem as a result of ineffective treatment durations and poor antimicrobial selection [49]. This challenge is associated with high rates of morbidity and mortality. Furthermore, conventional antibiotics are no longer effective against infections caused by MDR bacteria. Accordingly, it is anticipated that by 2050, antibiotic resistance will rank among the primary causes of death [50]. To optimize patient clinical outcomes, antimicrobial stewardship programs can offer the appropriate antimicrobial of choice while focusing on the susceptibility profile of the causative pathogen. Numerous studies have also shown that antimicrobial stewardship practices reduce costs in medical settings and have several beneficial effects on the prudent use of antibiotics [51].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. The relatively large cohort of 376 patients enhances the robustness of the results, whereas the inclusion of demographic, clinical, and microbiological variables offers a comprehensive overview of multidrug-resistant K. pneumoniae in a tertiary hospital in Palestine. The retrospective, hospital-based design provides practical evidence regarding resistance patterns and prevalence, which may support the development of targeted control strategies. Nonetheless, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The wider application of these findings is limited by the retrospective design’s limitations in establishing causal inferences and the exclusion of hemodialysis and outpatient groups. Additionally, the omission of 20 patients due to incomplete records could have introduced selection bias, and the single-center setting limits the extent to which the results can represent the national healthcare context. Finally, the absence of molecular testing for carbapenem resistance mechanisms restricts a deeper understanding of the genetic basis of resistance. While we identified potential associations between patient characteristics and MDR K. pneumoniae infections, the retrospective cross-sectional design and reliance on univariate analyses limit causal inferences. Future studies with larger cohorts and multivariable regression models are warranted to adjust for potential confounders.

Conclusions

This study clarifies the transmission and resistance patterns of MDR Klebsiella pneumoniae in a Palestinian tertiary hospital setting. The data indicate an alarmingly high prevalence of MDR infections, particularly those caused by CR strains and ESBL producers. In particular, resistance to widely used antibiotics such as fluoroquinolones and cephalosporins is a concern. Notably, the prevalence was greater among younger patients and those undergoing invasive procedures such as central line placement, underscoring the role of hospital-related factors in the transmission of resistant infections.

Implications for clinical practice and further research

From a clinical perspective, these findings strongly support the need to increase antimicrobial stewardship, particularly in critical care settings such as emergency departments and operating rooms. When determining treatment regimens, clinicians should rely more heavily on localized antibiogram data and exercise caution when prescribing empirical broad-spectrum antibiotics. Updated prescribing guidelines should account for the encouraging finding that medications such as amikacin and fosfomycin continue to exhibit comparatively low resistance rates and may serve as effective treatment alternatives for MDR cases.

Routine screening of rectal swabs for CR K. pneumoniae should be a central component of institutional infection control initiatives, particularly among high-risk populations, such as patients in critical care or those with hematologic malignancies. Hospitals may be better equipped to detect and contain potential outbreaks through more proactive surveillance strategies, rapid diagnostics, and resistance profiling.

Although this study provides valuable insights, the generalizability of its findings is limited by its retrospective and single-center design. To better understand causal relationships and validate the risk factors identified herein, future research should include prospective, multicenter studies employing multivariable models. Additionally, genomic resistance testing and molecular typing could elucidate the mechanisms by which these resistant strains spread within hospitals and the broader community.

Further interventional research is needed to assess the actual impact of antimicrobial stewardship training programs, audits of antibiotic use, and enhanced infection control procedures on resistance rates in clinical practice. The development of a clinical risk scoring system may enable healthcare professionals to identify patients at greatest risk for MDR infections early in the treatment process. Finally, qualitative research exploring the prescribing practices and antibiotic knowledge of Palestinian healthcare professionals could inform more pragmatic and culturally appropriate policy approaches.

By addressing these knowledge gaps, healthcare systems will be better positioned to combat the growing threat of antimicrobial resistance, ultimately leading to improved patient care and outcomes, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.
