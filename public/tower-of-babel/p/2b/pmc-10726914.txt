Association Between Partisan Affiliation of State Governments and State Mortality Rates Before and During the COVID‐19 Pandemic
Authors: STEVEN H. WOOLF, ROY T. SABO, DEREK A. CHAPMAN, JONG HYUNG LEE
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Policy Points The increasing political polarization of states reached new heights during the COVID‐19 pandemic, when response plans differed sharply across party lines.This study found that states with Republican governors and larger Republican majorities in legislatures experienced higher death rates during the COVID‐19 pandemic—and in preceding years—but these associations often lost statistical significance after adjusting for the average income and health status of state populations and for the policy orientations of the states.Future research may help clarify whether the higher death rates in these states result from policy choices or have other explanations, such as the tendency of voters with lower incomes or poorer health to elect Republican candidates. ContextIncreasing polarization of states reached a high point during the COVID‐19 pandemic, when the party affiliation of elected officials often predicted their policy response. The health consequences of these divisions are unclear. Prior studies compared mortality rates based on presidential voting patterns, but few considered the partisan orientation of state officials. This study examined whether the partisan orientation of governors or legislatures was associated with mortality outcomes during the COVID‐19 pandemic.MethodsData on deaths and the partisan orientation of governors and legislators were obtained from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the National Conference of State Legislatures, respectively. Linear regression was used to measure the association between Republican representation (percentage of seats held) in legislatures and (1) age‐adjusted, all‐cause mortality rates (AAMRs) in 2015–2021 and (2) excess death rates during three phases of the COVID‐19 pandemic, controlling for median household income, the prevalence of four risk factors (obesity, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, heart attack, stroke), and state policy orientation. Associations between excess death rates and the governor's party were also examined.FindingsStates with Republican governors or greater Republican representation in legislatures experienced higher AAMRs during 2015–2021, lower excess death rates during Phase 1 of the COVID‐19 pandemic (weeks ending March 28, 2020, through June 13, 2020), and higher excess death rates in Phases 2 and 3 (weeks ending June 20, 2020, through April 30, 2022; p < 0.05). Most associations lost statistical significance after adjustment for control variables.ConclusionsMortality was higher in states with Republican governors and greater Republican legislative representation before and during much of the pandemic. Observed associations could be explained by the adverse effects of policy choices, reverse causality (e.g., popularity of Republican candidates in states with lower socioeconomic and health status), or unmeasured factors that predominate in states with Republican leaders.

State policies on topics ranging from tobacco taxes and medicaid expansion to early childhood education and parental leave can influence population health. Partisan influence on policy choices has intensified over time, a product of increasing political polarization in the United States that began in the 1990s and escalated with the Tea Party movement that followed the 2009 election of President Barack Obama and with the ideological division that accompanied the election of President Donald Trump. 1 , 2 , 3 The growing polarization of state policies has produced a demonstrable association with health outcomes. Studies show that increasing divergence between the policies of “red” and “blue” states has been associated with life expectancy, mortality rates, and other health outcomes. 4 , 5

This divergence became acute during the COVID‐19 pandemic, when the politicization of public health policy created a sharp divide in how states with Democratic and Republican leaders reacted to the crisis. 6 Although many states initiated lockdowns soon after the pandemic began in March 2020, Republican governors and legislatures were more likely than their Democratic counterparts to curtail lockdowns in June 2020 and allow businesses to reopen, to resist mandates to wear masks or prohibit social gatherings, and to publicly question the scientific evidence and safety of COVID‐19 vaccines and the veracity of public health officials. 7 , 8 , 9 The resulting polarization in state responses to COVID‐19, a disease that claimed lives within days or weeks of exposure, created a macabre natural experiment in which the association between partisan policy choices and mortality outcomes could be examined.

Prior studies of the association between partisan affiliation and health outcomes have generally used presidential voting patterns as the independent variable. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 However, the central question for this study is less about voting patterns or presidents than the political orientation of state elected officials. Seated governors and legislators have played a more direct role than presidents in shaping state policies affecting public health. During the COVID‐19 pandemic, they were central in creating the divergence in response plans that unfolded across the states. This study examined whether the partisan affiliation of governors and state legislatures was associated with mortality outcomes and, to our knowledge, is the first to examine such associations at the state level.

Methods

Study Aims

The aim of this study was to determine whether the party affiliation of governors or legislators (measured as the percentage of seats held in state legislatures) or party control over both branches of government was associated with mortality outcomes. The central question concerned mortality outcomes during the large surges that occurred during the COVID‐19 pandemic (March 2020 through April 2022); but to determine whether the association was confined to the pandemic, we also examined mortality patterns in the 5 years preceding the pandemic (2015‐2019). Because the study did not involve human participants, it was exempt from institutional review under 45 Code of Federal Regulations §46.101(b)(4).

Indicators

The partisan affiliation of governors was defined as a binary variable based on the party affiliation of the seated governor in the year of observation. The partisan orientation of the legislature was defined as a continuous variable based on the percentage of seated legislators affiliated with the Republican party. Party control over the branches of state government reflected whether the governor's party held control over both legislative chambers (unified government, or “trifectas”) or only one chamber (divided government).

Mortality outcomes included the states’ (1) age‐adjusted, all‐cause mortality rates (AAMRs) during five prepandemic years (2015‐2019) and 2020 and 2021, and (2) excess deaths during the first 25 months of the pandemic (March 2020 through April 2022). This 25‐month period included three phases of the pandemic in which partisan orientation had different implications: Phase 1 (weeks ending March 28, 2020, through June 13, 2020), representing the initial outbreak and policy response, during which public health strategies became politicized 17 , 18 ; Phase 2 (weeks ending June 20, 2020, through April 3, 2021), representing the prevaccine era, during which partisan orientation influenced positions on masking, social distancing, and other nonvaccine pandemic control measures; and Phase 3 (weeks ending April 10, 2021, through April 30, 2022), a period when COVID‐19 vaccines were available and the nation experienced several surges caused by coronavirus variants, during which partisan orientation influenced attitudes about vaccine safety and mandates. The observation period ended in April 2022, following the completion of the most recent major surge that began in Fall 2021.

The study examined yearly AAMR values for each year between 2015 and 2021 and temporal changes in mortality rates between 2015 and 2019 (to examine associations with increases unrelated to the pandemic) and between 2015 and both 2020 and 2021 (to examine associations with increases related to the pandemic). Excess deaths were calculated for each of the three phases of the pandemic and are presented as crude excess death rates (per 100,000) based on state populations.

Covariates included median household income, the prevalence of four risk factors (self‐reported obesity, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, coronary heart disease, and stroke) known to affect mortality, and state policy orientation.

Data Sources

Death counts and weekly excess deaths during 2020–2021 were obtained from the National Center for Health Statistics. 19 The methods used by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) to calculate excess deaths are described elsewhere. 20 Age‐adjusted mortality rates were obtained from CDC Wide‐ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (WONDER). 21 , 22 The prevalence rates for the four risk factors were obtained for each year (2015‐2021) from the Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance Survey. 23 The partisan orientation of governors and state legislators by year was obtained from the National Conference of State Legislatures. 24 Data on the partisan composition of state legislatures excluded Nebraska, which has a unicameral and nonpartisan legislature. Population counts 25 for calculating excess death rates by phase in 2020–2021 and median household income by state 26 for years 2015 through 2021 were obtained from the US Census Bureau.

The policy orientation of states on a conservative to progressive continuum was measured with the Grumbach scale for 2014. 2 The scale rates state policy orientations in 16 domains (abortion, campaign finance, civil rights and liberties, criminal justice, education, environment, gun control, health and welfare, housing and transportation, immigration, private sector labor, public sector labor, LGBTQ rights, marijuana, taxes, and voting) on a 0–1 scale, with 1 reflecting the most progressive policies. This study adjusted for the five policy orientation scores that—according to Montez and colleagues 27 —were most predictive of changes in state‐level life expectancy: tobacco taxes, labor, immigration, environment, and civil rights.

Data Analysis

Descriptive Analysis of Mortality Trends

For each year between 2015 and 2021, the AAMR was determined for the total population residing in states with Republican or Democratic governors, respectively, based on the party affiliation of the seated governor in the designated year. Alaska was excluded from calculations for 2015–2018, when its governor was an Independent, and for consistency with other calculations, Nebraska was excluded because of its nonpartisan legislature.

AAMRs were also obtained by year for the total population residing in states with four different levels of Republican representation in the state legislature: <33% (Group 1), 33%‐49% (Group 2), 50%‐66% (Group 3), and 67% or more (Group 4). Representation of ≥50% is necessary for party control, and the majority party can exert greater influence if it holds enough seats (usually ≥67%) to override a governor's veto. Accordingly, the groups were defined to capture Democratic control with (Group 1) or without (Group 2) veto override power and Republican control with (Group 4) or without (Group 3) veto override power.

Finally, the AAMR was obtained by year for the total population residing in states with four different levels of control over the branches of government, including two “trifecta” scenarios in which either Democrats or Republicans held the governor's seat and controlled both chambers or two scenarios of divided government in which the governor's party controlled only one chamber. Alaska, the one state with an Independent governor, was excluded from the analysis.

As shown in Appendices 1 and 2, the number of states varied by year for each of the three measures (number of governors, by party; level of Republican representation; and control over branches of government). For any group of states for which the AAMR was calculated, CDC WONDER was used to obtain the rate for the pooled population.

Regression Analysis of Associations

Simple and stepwise linear regression was used to measure the association between the Republican share of the legislature and three outcomes: (1) AAMR for each year between 2015 and 2021, (2) changes in AAMR between 2015 and 2019, 2020, and 2021, and (3) excess death rates during the three phases of the pandemic. Two regression exercises were performed.

First, for each outcome, a stepwise regression examined the association without adjustment for covariates (Model 1) and with the stepwise addition of median household income (Model 2), obesity (Model 3), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (Model 4), coronary artery disease (Model 5), and stroke (Model 6). Second, for each outcome, a stepwise regression adjusted for the Grumbach policy orientation score for tobacco taxes (Model 2), labor (Model 3), immigration (Model 4), environment (Model 5), and civil rights (Model 6) in each state. The slope and standard error, R2, and p values for each calculation are reported.

Mean excess death rates by gubernatorial party were compared with the Chi‐squared test, and 95% confidence limits are reported. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to test for differences in rates among states with unified and divided government. Dunn's test was used for pairwise comparisons of rates in states with different levels of Republication representation and partisan control of government. All calculations were performed using SAS (version 9.4) or R Statistical Software (version 4.3.0).

During 2015–2021, the population residing in states with Republican governors experienced a higher AAMR than those living in states with Democratic governors (Figure 1). The AAMR was relatively stable during 2015–2019 and increased substantially during the COVID‐19 pandemic (2020‐2021). The AAMRs differed significantly among states with different levels of partisan control over the legislature (Kruskal–Wallis, p < 0.01; Figure 2). Specifically, the AAMR in states with strong Democratic majorities (Republican representation of <33%) was significantly lower than in states with Republican majorities, including those with moderate (50%‐66%) and large (>67%) majorities (Dunn's test, p < 0.01). The AAMR in states with weaker Democratic majorities (Republican representation of 33%‐49%) did not differ significantly from that of states with moderate Republican majorities (50%‐66%; Dunn's test, p = 0.06) but was significantly lower than in states with large Republican majorities (>67%Dunn's test, p < 0.01).

The AAMRs also differed significantly among states with different levels of control over state government (Kruskal–Wallis, p = 0.02; Figure 3). States with unified Democratic leadership (Democratic governor and Democratic control of both chambers) had a lower AAMR than states with unified Republican government (Dunn's test, p < 0.01), as did states with Republican governors presiding over a divided legislature (Dunn's test, p = 0.02). The AAMR in states with Democratic governors and a divided legislature was higher than among states with unified Democratic leadership (Dunn's test, p = 0.02) and did not differ significantly from that of states under unified Republican control (Figure 3). Among states with divided legislatures, those led by Republican governors had larger Democratic majorities (lower Republican representation) in the legislature—and a lower AAMR—than those led by Democratic governors (Table 2).

Discussion

This study found that AAMRs in the 5 years preceding the COVID‐19 pandemic were higher in Republican‐controlled states—those with Republican governors, larger Republican representation in state legislatures, and Republican trifectas (Republican governors and control of both chambers)—than in Democratic‐controlled states. The association appeared to strengthen over time, particularly during 2020 and 2021. In states with divided legislatures and Republican governors, which had larger Democratic majorities in the legislature (Table 2), AAMRs were statistically equivalent (p = 0.15) to those of states under unified Democratic control.

The existence of higher death rates in states with Republican governors or a large Republican presence in statehouses is consistent with other studies that have reported poorer health outcomes in populations that voted preferentially for Republican presidential candidates. 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 15 , 16 , 28 Warraich and colleagues found that decreases in mortality between 2000 and 2019 were larger in counties that voted for Democratic presidential candidates than in counties that voted for the Republican opponent. 14 Studies have also reported higher COVID‐19 mortality rates in “red” states and in counties that voted for President Trump in 2020. 29 , 30 , 31 , 32

This study adds to the literature by demonstrating that the increase in AAMRs that occurred during the first 2 years of the COVID‐19 pandemic (2020‐2021) and the excess deaths that occurred during those years were significantly higher in states with Republican governors and with larger Republican majorities in the state legislature. The exception was in Phase 1, when excess death rates were significantly lower in Republican‐controlled states. This trend likely reflects circumstances at the onset of the pandemic, when COVID‐19 first struck highly populated states with Democratic governors and legislatures (e.g., New York, New Jersey). Thereafter, as the pandemic spread across states in Phases 2 and 3, the association reversed, and death rates were significantly higher in states with Republican leadership. Other studies have reported a similar reversal following the summer of 2020, after which COVID‐19 death rates were higher in counties that voted for President Trump 30 or in states with Republican governors. 29

Although the strong association between two continuous variables—mortality rates and the proportion of legislators—suggests that factors associated with partisan affiliation contributed to higher mortality rates before and during the pandemic, the role of confounding variables cannot be excluded. For example, some associations observed in this study lost statistical significance after adjustment for median household income, which exerts a large, independent influence on mortality rates. Income is inversely related to the prevalence of risk factors for disease and injury (e.g., tobacco use, obesity) and of chronic diseases. Income is lower—and poverty rates are higher—in conservative states and in counties that vote for Republican presidential candidates. 33 , 34 , 35 This study found that few associations (other than the increase in AAMR between 2015 and 2021) retained statistical significance after adjustment for median household income and the prevalence of obesity and other risk factors.

The simplest explanation for this loss of statistical significance is that low income (and its adverse influences on risk factors for disease) exerted greater influence on mortality than the policy choices of state officials or their party affiliation. Other studies have documented higher COVID‐19 mortality in counties with adverse socioeconomic conditions. 36 , 37 , 38 However, the potentially complex relationships among the variables measured in this study allow for more nuanced explanations. For example, labor market or tax policies that widen wealth inequality and disadvantage lower‐ or middle‐income households can reduce growth in median household income. Policies that relax regulations on tobacco, firearms, and other health hazards can increase the prevalence of disease and injury. As a result, the covariates examined in this study (income and health risk factors) may, like mortality, be products of policies that predominate in Republican‐led states, creating conditions of endogeneity that would invalidate multivariate adjustments as performed here.

The second modeling exercise circumvented some of these issues by not adjusting for variables that can be influenced by policy (i.e., income or health risk factors) and adjusting instead for policy orientation itself. The Grumbach scores captured state policy orientation in five domains predictive of life expectancy (tobacco taxes, private sector labor, immigration, environment, and civil rights). 4 Adjusting for these scores removed the statistical significance of the observed association, providing suggestive (but not causal) evidence that policy choices (or related factors) may play some role in the association between Republican representation and mortality.

Additional suggestive evidence that higher mortality is associated with policy choices comes from a recent study that examined the association between state COVID‐19 mortality rates and Republican control of the legislature, COVID‐19 policy responses, legislative professionalism, local government autonomy, and broad (non–COVID‐19) preemptions. The investigators found that states with unified Republican control were more likely to limit the emergency public health authority of state executives, governors, and state and local health officials and that these limits were associated with higher COVID‐19 mortality rates. 39 An earlier study showed that Republican‐controlled states imposed stay‐at‐home orders later and reopened sooner in 2020 than did states under Democratic control. 9

Alternatively, the causal pathway may operate in the opposite direction; covariates such as low income and poor health may be among the adverse conditions that drive voters to the polls and motivate them to elect Republican candidates. 40 Some data suggest that voters struggling with socioeconomic precarity or health complications, such as opioid addiction or disabilities, may be more attracted to the messages, ideology, and policies advocated by Republican candidates. Finally, the associations reported here could reflect unmeasured factors that independently influence income and/or mortality and that happen to predominate in states with Republican leaders. Evidence Gaps in Explaining Potential Interrelationships Among Observed Variables [Colour figure can be viewed at wileyonlinelibrary.com]

The figure illustrates the complexity in understanding the potential interrelationships between observed variables, particularly regarding associations between Republican representation (depicted in red box) and mortality (depicted in dark gray box). Multiple explanations exist, necessitating further research to discern causal relationships. For example, only two of the many potential socioecological factors are examined in this study. These two variables (depicted in light gray boxes) include the median household income of the state population, only one measure of socioeconomic status, and the prevalence of four adverse health conditions (obesity, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, coronary heart disease, and stroke), a small subset of the many health conditions associated with higher mortality rates. These include risk factors (e.g., tobacco use, hypertension), chronic diseases, mental illness, and substance abuse, among others. Extensive literature confirms that income and other socioeconomic factors affect the risk of adverse health conditions (link ①), which in turn have strong causal relationships with mortality (link ②) and morbidity. The association between Republican representation and mortality could be explained by its association with income and/or adverse health conditions, a possibility suggested when the associations observed in this study lost statistical significance after adjusting for these covariates. Republican governors and legislatures may be more likely to preside over populations with lower socioeconomic status or a higher prevalence of disease risk factors. To some extent, this might reflect policies favored by Republican officials that adversely affect income (link ③) or population health (link ④). However, the causal pathway may operate in the reverse direction. Republican officials may be more likely to be elected by voters struggling with low income and other forms of economic precarity (link ⑤) or with adverse health conditions (link ⑥).

This study has several limitations, notably that cross‐sectional associations, however significant, cannot prove causality. Linear regression, as used in this study, contributes to understanding but is inadequate for causal analysis. Other limitations include the failure to (1) include covariates for other factors that affect mortality, (2) examine health outcomes other than mortality, or (3) study temporal or lagged effects, an important consideration given that policies promoted by officials of either party rarely exert immediate effects on health outcomes, especially mortality.

Further research is needed, first to confirm the existence of the associations reported here and then studies to test for causality and explore potential mechanisms of action. Such research is needed not only to understand what occurred during the COVID‐19 pandemic but also to clarify the future influence of partisan policy on population health trajectories. Future research may elucidate the pathways by which policy choices influence the social determinants of health and ultimately distal outcomes like mortality.

The polarization of states continues; politicians continue to take increasingly partisan positions on issues ranging from reproductive rights and gun control to climate change and health outcomes at the state level continue to diverge. This may widen inequities in the health and longevity of Americans living in different states. As policy choices become more partisan, partisanship becomes a critical determinant of health for researchers to study. Well‐designed studies are needed to clarify the settings in which partisan choices affect health and the causal mechanisms involved, and the circumstances in which more important factors eclipse state policy as determinants of health.
