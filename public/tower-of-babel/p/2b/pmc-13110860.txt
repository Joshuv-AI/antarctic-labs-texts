Nasal humoral immunity: the first line of protection against respiratory pathogens
Authors: Lena Hansen, Jenna J Guthmiller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe nasal cavity is the first site of infection for respiratory pathogens, and control of pathogens at this site is critical for limiting disease progression. Mucosal immunity in the respiratory tract has historically been studied in the lungs despite lung infections typically arising as a result of lack of pathogen control in the upper respiratory tract. However, the generation of vaccines to prevent infection and block transmission requires an understanding of how immune responses are generated and maintained within the nasal cavity. Recently, a concerted research effort to understand immune responses in the nasal cavity and upper respiratory tract has revealed new insight into how local immune responses are generated and help to control respiratory pathogens. Here, we discuss these recent advances and outstanding questions in our understanding of humoral immunity within the nasal cavity and upper respiratory tract.Keywords: antibodies, humoral immunity, plasma cells

Introduction

The nasal cavity is a hollow bony structure covered in respiratory mucosa, which creates a large surface area to warm, humidify, and filter air. Additionally, the respiratory mucosa is critical for capturing and sequestering inhaled particulates, such as pathogens and allergens that trigger immune responses within the nasal mucosa. Immunity in the nasal cavity is multipronged and includes physical barriers, epithelial cell–intrinsic mechanisms of immune control, and the recruitment of innate and adaptive immune cells. These defense mechanisms work in coordination to help control pathogen burdens while generating immune memory within the nasal cavity that can be recalled upon reinfection.

Dr. Jenna Guthmiller is an Assistant Professor in the Department of Immunology and Microbiology at the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus. Dr. Guthmiller's research focuses on understanding how broadly protective humoral immunity against rapidly evolving pathogens develops and can be harnessed by next-generation vaccines. Her work has identified the epitopes targeted by broadly neutralizing antibodies and how distinct virus exposures and vaccines can drive broadly neutralizing antibodies.

Vaccines delivered intranasally seek to recapitulate these immune responses in order to mediate protection from infection in the nasal cavity, limit pathogen dissemination to other tissues, and prevent onward transmission. There is currently only one intranasal vaccine, the live attenuated influenza virus vaccine (LAIV), licensed in the United States. LAIV has been shown to have comparable or higher effectiveness compared to the seasonal inactivated influenza vaccine in young children (<5 years old) but lacks effectiveness in adults.1 Reinfection with antigenically diverse respiratory viruses such as influenza viruses, coronaviruses, and rhinoviruses is common, suggesting that incomplete protection is mounted within the nasal cavity. However, reinfections are typically caused by antigenically drifted viral variants or distinct serotypes, and reinfection with homologous viruses or bacterial serotypes is less common.2–4 Secreted IgA is associated with protection from colonization of pathogenic bacteria and controlling viral infections.5,6 Therefore, the induction of immune memory in the nose has the potential to be harnessed to mediate protection at the site of infection.

Numerous recent studies have provided new insight into how adaptive immunity and immune memory are generated and maintained at mucosal sites.7–10 Within this commentary, we will discuss mechanisms of immune-mediated protection in the nasal cavity with an emphasis on the induction of humoral immunity. We will also discuss evidence for how these processes can be harnessed by emerging vaccine approaches.

Cellular architecture of the nasal cavity and its associated lymphoid tissue

The nasal mucosa is covered by ciliated and nonciliated epithelial cells, basal epithelial cells, and goblet cells, which sit on top of structurally supportive lamina propria comprised of stromal cells, acinar glands, capillaries, nerves, and immune cells. The nasal lumen is covered in mucus produced by goblet cells, as well as by mucus cells in the submucosal glands. Submucosal glands are composed of mucus and serous cells, with the latter producing serous fluid, a watery substance, that helps to keep the respiratory tract moist (Fig. 1). Mucus and antimicrobial peptide production by both the epithelium and submucosal glands helps to defend the mucosal lumen of the nasal cavity from bacterial adhesion and invasion.11 Structurally, the nasal mucosa is supported by a network of stromal cells, mainly fibroblasts, and capillaries in the lamina propria. Immune cells are prevalent within the lamina propria, including dendritic cells, CD4 and CD8 T cells, and B cells. The majority of T cells in the submucosa are resident memory T cells (CD69+CD103+), which are positioned to respond to secondary exposures to a given antigen.12–14 B cells in the submucosa are comprised of resident memory B cells and antibody-secreting plasma cells. Naïve B and T cells are present in nasal turbinates and can be detected by nasal swabbing approaches.8 Data from the lung and nasal turbinate of humans support that IgA-producing plasma cells are largely congregated around submucosal glands.15,16

Organized secondary lymphoid organs, such as nasal-associated lymphoid tissue (NALT), are present within the nasal cavity. In humans, the Waldeyer ring is the main lymphoid tissue within the upper respiratory tract and is a ringed arrangement of tonsils in the pharynx, including lingual, palatine, tubal, and pharyngeal tonsils. The pharyngeal tonsil, commonly referred to as the adenoid, is located at the roof of the nasopharynx where the nasal cavity connects to the pharynx. The outer epithelial cell layer of tonsils has epithelial cells known as microfold (M) cells, which are present in specialized folds in the surface of the tonsil crypts.17 Once antigen enters into tonsils via M cells, innate and adaptive immune cells can become activated and drive the formation of germinal center (GC) responses. Due to the high intensity of exposure to air-derived antigens and microbes, adenoid and other tonsils have ongoing GC responses.8

Other large- to medium-sized mammals, including sheep, pigs, cats, and cows, have pharyngeal tonsils similar to those in humans.18,19 In contrast, rodents such as mice lack tonsils and instead possess NALT within the nasal cavity. In mice, NALT is situated atop the palate and resembles the anatomy of Peyer patches in the small intestine. It contains a subepithelial dome rich in immune cells, including dendritic cells, B-cell follicles, and T-cell zones, and M cells in mice are located within the epithelial layer above this dome.9,20–23 External stimuli, such as microbiota, and chemokine induction also drive the development of NALT and GCs within NALT.22,24 Further discussion of the role of adenoids and NALT, as well as tertiary lymphoid structures, in shaping the generation of immune responses in the nasal cavity are discussed below.

Inductive sites for humoral immunity in the nasal cavity

Humoral immunity within the nasal mucosa is believed to originate from immune responses within the nasal cavity.9,10,25 Plasma cells and resident memory B cells typically derive from GC reactions, which classically occur within secondary lymphoid organs such as tonsils in humans, NALT in rodents, and distal lymph nodes such as the cervical lymph nodes. In mice, NALT and cervical lymph nodes are well established to be important inductive sites for GC responses and for generating IgA-producing plasma cells.9,26 However, the requirement for the individual inductive sites may be context dependent. For example, a study using the pneumococcal vaccine found that cervical lymph nodes, but not NALT, are critical for generating antibody responses in the nasopharynx.27 Moreover, it was found that both the cervical lymph nodes and NALT were dispensable for generating protective immunity against influenza viruses.28 Therefore, NALT and cervical lymph nodes are not redundant in generating humoral immunity in the nasal cavity.

Studies of inductive sites of humoral immunity in humans have largely been restricted to studying surgically removed adenoids or, more recently, nasopharyngeal swabbing of the adenoid.8 Studies using surgically removed tonsils have revealed ongoing GC responses and memory B cells against respiratory pathogens, including severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2).29 Recently, several studies revealed that GC B-cell responses can be longitudinally tracked by swabbing the adenoid crypts following infection or intranasal vaccination.8,30 This adenoid swabbing approach has also revealed antigen-specific memory B cells, plasma cells, and T follicular helper cells.8 Additionally, adenoid biopsies revealed ongoing GC responses following SARS-CoV-2 infection.31 These emerging approaches will allow for the longitudinal sampling of immune responses in the nasal cavity, which can be used to understand the contribution of adenoids to secreted antibody responses. These approaches may allow the field to establish mucosal correlates of protection against infection and transmission.

A recent report identified tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) with active GCs in the nasal turbinates of influenza-infected and 4-hydroxy-3-nitrophenyl acetyl (NP)-hapten–immunized mice.7 Interestingly, this report found that the influenza-specific B cells and B1-8 B cells, a B cell specific for the NP-hapten, were more enriched within TLSs than in NALT.7 The TLS responses were associated with improved antigen-specific antibody responses in the nasal cavity, suggesting that they may be an important component of humoral immunity within the nasal cavity, independent of NALT and cervical lymph nodes. This study also reported that nasal swabs of the upper nasal cavity contained GC-like B cells, although the precise anatomical site of sampling of these swabbing efforts is not known.7 Despite this, TLSs with GC-like structures and accompanying plasma cells have been observed in nasal polyps of humans, suggesting that TLSs can form under pathological conditions.32

Role of plasma cells and antibodies in mediating protection in the nasal cavity

Mucosal antibodies in the nasal cavity play a critical role in mediating protection against pathogens and shaping the microbiome within the nasal cavity.33–37 Antibodies in nasal secretions are believed to derive from local plasma cell populations, rather than from circulating antibodies,10 although this has yet to be shown in humans. Dimeric secretory IgA (sIgA) is the major antibody isotype of antibody within the nasal mucosa.38–40 Humans possess 2 IgA subclasses, IgA1 and IgA2, with IgA1 as the dominant subclass in the nasal mucosa.41 sIgA can mediate protection via direct neutralization of foreign pathogens or engagement of FcαR1 on myeloid cells, and potentially NK cells, to promote antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, superoxide production, and cytokine release.42–44 sIgA-FcαR1 engagement can also lead to enhanced antigen presentation to T cells by macrophages,45,46 further supporting how sIgA can promote protective immunity. Moreover, sIgA and IgM have improved binding breadth to viral variants relative to IgG, likely due to their multimeric state, suggesting that sIgA may be more functional against variable pathogens in the respiratory tract,47–49 including SARS-CoV-2 and influenza viruses.

sIgA within the respiratory tract largely derives from IgA-secreting plasma cells within the submucosa of the nasal tissue,9,50 although plasma cells have also been found in the adenoid and olfactory epithelium.8,10 Upon selection within a GC, plasma cells upregulate CCR10, which promotes their migration to CCL28-producing tissues.9,16,51 The CCR10/CCL28 axis has been observed in the human lung16 and for NALT-derived plasma cells.9 In addition to CCL28, submucosal glands in the lung express A proliferation-inducing ligand (APRIL), which promotes the longevity of plasma cells by signaling through the B-cell maturation antigen (BCMA) and transmembrane activator and calcium modulating ligand interactor (TACI) receptors.16 Moreover, CXCR3+ plasma cells can migrate into the lungs and olfactory epithelium within the nasal cavity.10,52 Transcytosis of IgA and IgM into the nasal lumen occurs through the polymeric Ig receptor (pIgR), which is expressed at high levels in the nasal epithelium and at submucosal glands within the lungs of humans and serous acini of the mouse nose.9,16 Importantly, pIgR engaged with IgA and IgM is cleaved to allow for the release of soluble antibody into the nasal lumen, with the attached pIgR component becoming the secretory component. Locally produced IgG is likely transcytosed to the nasal lumen via high expression of FcRn within the nasal epithelium.53

Vaccine approaches to induce protective humoral immunity in the nasal cavity

Vaccines that elicit immune responses in the nasal cavity have the potential to prevent infection within the respiratory tract, limit the dissemination of pathogens to other organs, and block transmission to new hosts. Currently, only 6 intranasal vaccines are licensed in humans, including the LAIV and 5 intranasal or inhaled vaccines for SARS-CoV-2, reviewed in detail previously.54 The effectiveness of FluMist LAIV against influenza viruses is modest in children and poor in adults,1 likely due to poor immunogenicity and rapid clearance. Emerging live-attenuated vaccines, such as the deltaFlu platform, have improved immunogenicity.55 Overall, a better understanding of how immunity is orchestrated in the nasal cavity and how vaccines can trigger these responses is needed to make effective intranasal vaccines.

Several promising platforms and regimens are showing promise for inducing protective immunity in the nasal cavity. One common regimen is the “prime and pull” strategy.56 The prime and pull strategy primes immune responses within a draining lymph node via an intramuscular or subcutaneous immunization, and provides a booster vaccine at the mucosal site to pull reactivated systemic immune memory into mucosal tissues. This approach has shown improved protection against multiple respiratory pathogens, even in the absence of an adjuvant.57–59 While the prime and pull strategy works well for novel antigens, preexisting immunity to pathogens like influenza and SARS-CoV-2 could neutralize vectors, limit antigen exposure, and reduce immune recruitment to mucosal barriers. Viral vectored vaccines, such as adenovirus vaccines, express antigens from other pathogens and imitate natural infection to drive humoral immune responses in the respiratory tract and could overcome limitations of preexisting immunity.60,61

A major barrier for effective intranasal vaccines is the delivery and sensing of antigen within the nasal cavity. Several approaches have been taken to improve antigen uptake and immunogenicity, including FcRn hijacking approaches. FcRn is highly expressed by nasal epithelium and can transcytose IgG and albumin to both the lumen and lamina propria. This can be done by either making an antigen-Fc conjugate, antigen-albumin conjugate, or albumin-hitchhiking antigen-lipid conjugate vaccines.62–66 All of these approaches have been shown to promote improved antibody responses within the respiratory tract.

Adjuvants can facilitate uptake of vaccine antigens in the submucosa. Mucoadhesive adjuvants, which are typically comprised of mucus-adhering polymers, can improve antigen retention in the nasal cavity and improves IgA induction.67 Nanoemulsions take advantage of immiscible liquid combinations, such as oil and water, and surfactants to stabilize antigen and promote cell uptake and penetration of mucosal barriers. A phase 1 clinical trial of the seasonal influenza vaccine adjuvanted with the nanoemulsion adjuvant W805EC showed it induced a significant fold-rise in serum antibodies as well as nasal IgA antibody titers.68 Furthermore, a recent phase 1 clinical trial with recombinant H5 adjuvanted with W805EC and administered intranasally showed the induction of systemic and mucosal antibodies against divergent H5 viruses,69 suggesting that this vaccine could be effective against H5 virus spillovers into humans. However, mucoadhesive and nanoemulsion vaccines do not induce robust innate immune responses that can further potentiate immune responses in the nasal mucosa. Nanoemulsions combined with potent innate immune-stimulating adjuvants show improved humoral immunity in the nose.70 A recent study revealed that changing the osmolarity of a vaccine’s composition can enhance mucosal penetration and improve immune responses.71 Therefore, there are multiple complementary approaches to improve antigen uptake within the nasal mucosa to augment humoral immunity.

The development of adjuvanted intranasal vaccines has been challenging due to occurrence of adverse events, including facial nerve paralysis (Bell’s palsy), associated with heat-inactivated Escherichia coli and cholera toxins.72,73 This underscores the caution needed for the development and implementation of adjuvants in future intranasal vaccines. Despite this, clinical trials of the W805EC adjuvanted vaccine reported that this adjuvanted vaccine was well-tolerated, and no serious adverse events were reported.68,69

Concluding remarks and outstanding questions

Humoral immunity in the nasal cavity is critical for mediating protection against respiratory pathogens. While it has been long appreciated that local antibody production is critical for sustained antibody responses in the nasal cavity, recent studies have advanced our understanding of where these cell types mature and can be recruited into the nasal tissue. Moreover, emerging vaccine technologies have the potential to harness these immune responses to drive protective humoral immunity in the nasal cavity.

Despite this tremendous progress, many outstanding questions remain:

What are the contributions of distinct secondary lymphoid organs (ie NALT, lymph nodes) and tertiary lymphoid follicles in generating humoral immunity in the nasal cavity? For example, the net effect of adenoidectomies on immunity against multiple respiratory is largely neutral,74–76 suggesting that the adenoid is dispensable for nasal immunity or that there are compensatory mechanisms, such as cervical lymph nodes or tertiary lymphoid structures, that are critical for generating humoral immunity. Moreover, mucosal plasma cells can migrate from distal mucosal sites into other mucosal surfaces.66,77,78 Whether humoral immune responses generated outside of the nasal cavity can traffic and reside in the nose is unknown.
