Is Necrotizing Enterocolitis Totalis a Distinct Disease Process?
Authors: Pengjian Zou, Wenhai Fang, Peizhi Zhao, Junjian Lv, Tulian Lin, Zhe Wang, Wenjie Luo, Peipei Wu, Wei Zhong, Qiuming He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to characterize the clinical features of necrotizing enterocolitis totalis (NEC-T) in infants compared with NEC non-totalis (NEC-non-T) and to identify associated risk factors, thereby providing insights into its management for clinicians and parents.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted at the Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Centre and included infants with Bell’s Stage II or III NEC from January 2015 to March 2021. Infants were divided into NEC-T and NEC-non-T groups based on intraoperative findings. Clinical data were collected and compared between the two groups. Logistic regression was used to identify independent risk factors for NEC-T.ResultsAmong 195 infants who underwent surgery for NEC, 30 (15.4%) had NEC-T. Significant differences were observed in gestational age, time from onset to surgery (8 hours vs. 46 hours, p<0.001), survival, and intestinal failure. Risk factors for NEC-T included neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS) (OR, 12.85; p=0.004), ventilatory support after birth (OR, 13.36; p<0.001), bacteremia (odds ratio [OR], 10.98; p<0.001), transfusion (OR, 4.62; p=0.004), increased ventilatory support (OR, 7.34; p=0.001), and portal venous gas (PVG) (OR, 4.78; p=0.007).ConclusionNEC-T in neonates is characterized by sudden onset, rapid progression, and high mortality. NRDS, ventilatory support after birth, bacteremia, transfusion, increased ventilatory support, and PVG significantly increased the risk of NEC-T. Aggressive surgical decision-making may be a crucial approach to improving survival rates in patients with NEC-T.

INTRODUCTION

Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) remains a leading cause of gastrointestinal mortality in premature infants and predominantly affects preterm neonates. The incidence of NEC is estimated to be 5–7% among infants with a birth weight of less than 1,500 g [12]. Despite significant advances in understanding its pathophysiology and improvements in neonatal intensive care [345], the overall mortality rate associated with NEC remains unchanged at 25–40%. This condition spans a wide spectrum of severity, ranging from mild cases that can be managed medically to severe, life-threatening presentations requiring urgent surgical intervention.

Of greater clinical significance is the occurrence of near-total intestinal necrosis in approximately 10% of patients, identified intraoperatively as necrotizing enterocolitis totalis (NEC-T). This condition is associated with nearly 100% mortality due to extensive intestinal necrosis, systemic sepsis, and multi-organ failure [678]. Management strategies for infants with minimal residual bowel length after NEC remain controversial [9]. Although most neonatologists and surgeons (up to 90%) consider life-sustaining interventions ethically justifiable for infants with NEC-T, only 71% would actually recommend them [10]. Despite progress in managing intestinal failure, few patients with NEC-T survive beyond discharge, limiting data on long-term outcomes [1112]. Delayed identification and treatment may contribute to the development of NEC-T. In addition, parents are often poorly informed about treatment options and important outcome indicators related to NEC-T, which may hinder their involvement in treatment decisions [1314].

This study aimed to characterize the clinical features of NEC-T in infants compared with NEC non-totalis (NEC-non-T) and to identify associated risk factors, thereby providing insights into its management for clinicians and parents.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Ethical Guidelines for Biomedical Research Involving Human Subjects. It was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Guangzhou Women and Children’s Medical Centre (HREC number 224A01). We retrospectively reviewed the medical records of patients admitted between January 2015 and March 2021. Patients were included if they had a diagnosis of NEC with an initial Bell’s Stage II or higher, according to the modified Bell’s staging criteria. Infants with major abdominal malformations, incomplete medical records, or spontaneous intestinal perforation were excluded from the study. Operative notes were reviewed for all NEC patients who required surgical intervention. The diagnosis of NEC-T was defined as the presence of necrotic changes involving more than 75% of the small and/or large intestines, as identified intraoperatively [15]. Data were extracted through chart review and included basic demographic information, clinical and radiological markers of disease severity, comorbidities, predisposing factors, and outcomes such as mortality and surgical interventions.

As measures of disease severity, primary outcomes including the need for and type of surgical intervention, intraoperative findings, mortality, and subsequent development of intestinal failure were recorded.

Definitions

NEC cases were defined as infants diagnosed with NEC stage ≥IIA (Bell’s classification). Two experts independently reviewed NEC cases, and consensus was reached in all cases. Surgical NEC was defined as NEC requiring surgical treatment and/or peritoneal drainage [16]. Bacteremia was defined as the presence of positive blood or cerebrospinal fluid cultures prior to the diagnosis of NEC [17]. Exposure to red blood cell transfusion was defined as the administration of erythrocytes within 7 days before disease onset [18]. Respiratory symptoms (anoxia and/or increased ventilatory support) included increased periodic breathing, respiratory rate, and apnea, accompanied by increased initial self-limited desaturations and greater need for ventilatory support. We recorded only cases that required endotracheal intubation or desaturations requiring mild stimulation by nursing providers. Short bowel syndrome/intestinal failure (SBS/IF) was identified when an infant had undergone major bowel resection (<75 cm or <50% of expected small bowel length remaining) and required >90 days of parenteral nutrition [192021].

Statistical analysis

Univariate analysis of all baseline characteristics, clinical parameters, outcomes, and variables was conducted using the chi-square test for categorical data and the two-sided Wilcoxon two-sample test for continuous data to compare patients who developed NEC-T with those who did not. Variables with a p-value<0.05 in the univariate analysis were evaluated using stepwise logistic regression, with a significance level of p<0.05. The IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 22.0 (IBM Co) statistical software was used for all statistical analyses.

RESULTS

During the study period, a total of 533 infants were initially identified. Of these, 338 (63.4%) were excluded from the study. A total of 293 (55.0%) infants were excluded because they received conservative treatment, and 45 infants were excluded from the surgical group, including 13 (2.4%) with abdominal malformations and 32 (6.0%) with incomplete medical records. Consequently, 195 infants with a diagnosis of NEC underwent surgical intervention over the 6-year study period (Fig. 1). Among these, 30 (15.4%) were diagnosed intraoperatively with NEC-T, defined as necrosis involving 75–100% of the small bowel. No significant differences were observed between the groups in terms of sex, feeding patterns, or mode of delivery (Table 1). When NEC-T and NEC-non-T patients were compared, NEC-T infants had a lower gestational age and were diagnosed with NEC at an older age. In addition, a higher proportion of NEC-T cases required ventilatory support before NEC diagnosis compared with NEC-non-T cases (56.7% vs. 15.2%, p<0.01). Consistent with these findings, NEC-T infants were more likely to develop neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS) (86.7% vs. 32.7%, p<0.01) and bacteremia (76.7% vs. 23%, p<0.01) (Table 2).

Based on chart review and relevant radiologic reports, the highest initial stage of NEC was determined using the Modified Bell’s staging criteria. Infants with NEC-T were more likely to present with Stage III NEC than those with NEC-non-T (76.7% vs. 58.2%, p=0.041).

In the 24 hours preceding overt signs of intestinal dysfunction, 17 NEC-T cases exhibited a respiratory prodrome requiring increased respiratory support. This included 15 patients who required intubation and mechanical ventilation and 2 who required increased supplemental oxygen. The prodrome was characterized by decreased oxygenation, increased respiratory rate, and elevated PaCO2, often preceded by hypocarbia (Table 3). Except for portal venous gas, other radiographic findings did not differ significantly between infants with and without NEC-T.

The primary outcomes measured were survival to hospital discharge and the development of intestinal failure. NEC-T cases typically progressed rapidly and required urgent surgical intervention. Significant differences were observed between NEC-T and NEC-non-T cases in the time from onset to surgery (8.00 [4.00–17.00] hours in NEC-T vs. 46.00 [24.50–89.00] hours in NEC-non-T, p<0.001) and intraoperative findings (Table 4). Among the NEC-T group, six neonates underwent laparotomy, six had stoma formation only, and two underwent intestinal anastomosis after resection of necrotic bowel. Despite the limited number of NEC-T cases, fewer NEC-T infants had intestinal perforation during surgery than NEC-non-T infants (6.7% vs. 54.5%, p<0.001). In addition, a significant difference in survival was observed between NEC-T and NEC-non-T neonates (30% vs. 95.8%, p<0.001). Of the nine NEC-T survivors, eight underwent enterostomy, and one underwent intestinal anastomosis.

DISCUSSION

In this retrospective cohort study, we aimed to identify demographic and clinical factors associated with the development of NEC in preterm infants and to determine whether timely intervention could effectively manage NEC-T. However, the rapid progression of NEC-T often leads to fatal outcomes. The prevalence of NEC-T remains low. In our study, 15.4% of infants were diagnosed with NEC-T, consistent with the previously reported 10% incidence [2122]. Limited data on the clinical course of advanced NEC make it challenging for healthcare providers to manage these cases and counsel families on critical aspects of care, including the need for operative intervention, the risk of intestinal failure, and mortality.

NEC-T is the most severe form of NEC, characterized by extensive necrosis involving nearly the entire small bowel [152324]. Our study confirmed that many patients with NEC-T have underlying comorbidities contributing to disease progression, with NRDS, bacteremia, and transfusions within 7 days preceding diagnosis identified as significant risk factors [2526].

The pathogenesis of NEC likely involves inflammatory mediators released during systemic infection, which promote intestinal inflammation and contribute to disease onset [18]. Severe infections trigger systemic inflammatory activation, producing cytokines that directly or indirectly damage the intestinal mucosa. In addition, pathogenic bacteria, intestinal dysbiosis, barrier dysfunction, and toxic intestinal paralysis during sepsis may contribute to the development of NEC [2728]. A highly inflammatory state following infection may lead to intestinal microcirculatory disturbances, resulting in widespread intestinal necrosis.

Perinatal asphyxia is closely associated with the development of NEC, and the severity of NEC correlates with the severity of respiratory distress [182930]. In cases of respiratory distress or pneumonia, systemic hypoxia leads to redistribution of blood flow that prioritizes oxygen delivery to vital organs. This process involves vasoconstriction of the intestinal vessels, resulting in reduced intestinal perfusion and subsequent intestinal mucosal hypoxia and ischemic injury, which contribute to the pathogenesis of NEC.

In our study, patients with severe NEC-T frequently exhibited unexplained respiratory deterioration before gastrointestinal symptoms developed, often requiring increased ventilatory support. Among 30 patients, respiratory changes were observed within 24 hours before overt signs of bowel dysfunction, ranging from decreased PO2 levels managed with supplemental oxygen to severe respiratory collapse with hypoxemia and hypercarbia requiring intubation and mechanical ventilation.

These findings were particularly notable in premature infants who had previously recovered from NRDS. This vulnerable population, which has a limited capacity to meet increased metabolic demands, is susceptible to respiratory distress in the early stages of intestinal inflammation, even before abdominal distension and tenderness limit compensatory respiratory mechanisms [313233]. Close monitoring and detailed record-keeping in the NICU provide an opportunity to detect early respiratory changes, allowing identification of alterations in respiratory status before gastrointestinal dysfunction becomes apparent [34].

Identifying patients at risk for portal venous gas (PVG) is crucial, as PVG remains a relative indication for surgical intervention, particularly in hemodynamically stable patients [353637]. Operative decisions are typically based on a combination of clinical and radiographic findings, most commonly the presence of pneumoperitoneum or evidence of peritonitis. PVG originates from the fermentation of substrates in milk feedings by bacteria within the gastrointestinal lumen. Previous reports have consistently identified PVG as a significant radiological marker of severe neonatal NEC [3839]. Specifically, among infants with lower GA, PVG is less likely to occur but is associated with a higher mortality rate when present. In this study, analysis of baseline data suggests that PVG may independently predict the development of NEC-T. However, it remains unclear whether PVG results from diffuse impairment of intestinal mucosal integrity caused by specific pathogens or toxins—a question central to understanding NEC-T that remains unresolved.

Research indicates that bowel perforation, resulting from transmural necrosis, is a late complication of NEC and a common indication for surgical intervention, occurring in 10–12% of NEC cases. Bowel perforation typically occurs in the terminal ileum and at the ileo-caecal junction [4041]. These studies also show that perforation usually develops approximately 20–30 hours after symptom onset. In our study, the time from NEC onset to surgical intervention was significantly shorter in infants with NEC-T than in those with NEC-non-T (8 hours vs. 46 hours). Yet, intestinal perforation was rarely detected during surgery (only 6.7% of cases). We hypothesize that the disease course in NEC-T is highly aggressive, with rapid progression to near-total bowel involvement shortly before perforation, rather than extensive necrosis resulting from delayed diagnosis or treatment.

Management of neonates with NEC-T remains clinically challenging. Despite recent improvements in outcomes, decision-making for these patients remains complex and multifaceted. Extensive bowel resection invariably results in SBS, necessitating a balance between resection of necrotic bowel and preservation of maximum viable intestinal length while minimizing operative time. Traditional management strategies for extensive NEC involve segmental resection and multiple stomas, often requiring multiple operations [4243].

In our cohort, survival among infants with NEC-T was low (30%). Consistent with prior reports [121523], aggressive surgical intervention was associated with a mortality rate of 68.8%, compared with 95% in those receiving non-aggressive management (conservative treatment or exploratory laparotomy only). Surgical decisions are guided by clinician judgment and parental preferences. Given the potential for bowel growth in preterm neonates, our findings suggest that resection may be a viable option for managing pan-intestinal NEC [44]. Considering our findings of a shorter time to surgery in NEC-T and the reported high mortality of non-surgical NEC-T, aggressive surgical decision-making is crucial in the management of NEC-T.

NEC is the leading cause of SBS, which is characterized by significant loss of functional intestinal mass, resulting in impaired digestion and nutrient absorption, fluid and electrolyte imbalances, and compromised growth [45]. Evaluating long-term outcomes and quality of life in patients with severe surgical NEC requires assessment of their ability to achieve enteral autonomy. The combination of severe SBS and frequent prematurity-related comorbidities has led to significant variability in clinical practice regarding management options for parents facing potential NEC-T cases. In our study, intestinal failure was inevitable in seven of the nine surviving children. None of these patients underwent intestinal transplantation. Instead, enteral nutrition was gradually increased after an extended period of parenteral nutrition, a process that proved challenging. Our survey revealed that neonatologists and pediatric surgeons continue to face difficulties in providing appropriate guidance to families, given the numerous associated concerns, including healthcare costs, patient suffering, quality-of-life implications, and family burden.

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective nature introduces inherent biases and confounders, including the inability to conclusively establish distinct pathophysiological mechanisms between the two groups. Although this study demonstrated that both NRDS and the need for ventilatory support were independent risk factors for adverse outcomes in NEC-T, the rarity of the condition limited our sample to 30 infants. Consequently, the corresponding odds ratios (NRDS: OR, 12.85; 95% CI, 2.29–72.04; ventilatory support: OR, 13.36; 95% CI, 3.71–48.16) are accompanied by wide confidence intervals, indicating reduced precision in the effect estimates. Nonetheless, the point estimates remained well above the threshold of clinical irrelevance (OR=1), and the lower bounds of the intervals exceeded 2, suggesting that a clinically meaningful association is highly probable in the real-world population. Multicenter collaborations with larger cohorts are needed to validate these findings and achieve narrower confidence intervals. In addition, the long study duration may have influenced outcomes because of changes in treatment approaches over time.

The management of patients with NEC-T and resulting IF is complex and requires an experienced multidisciplinary team. The availability of high-quality comparative data would bring clarity to complex discussions regarding the pursuit of aggressive surgical therapy versus comfort care and help providers guide parents in making informed choices that are consistent with their values.

Conclusion

The management of NEC-T remains ethically and clinically challenging because of the high mortality rate, significant morbidity associated with severe disease, and lack of established guidelines both within institutions and nationally. NRDS, postnatal ventilatory support, bacteremia, and blood transfusion were independent risk factors for NEC-T, as were increased ventilatory support and PVG after the onset of NEC-T. Aggressive surgical decision-making is a powerful approach to treating patients with NEC-T. In the future, a large-scale, multicenter prospective study is needed to determine the optimal management of this subgroup of NEC, to identify risk factors for NEC-T and establish a reliable early disease prediction model.
