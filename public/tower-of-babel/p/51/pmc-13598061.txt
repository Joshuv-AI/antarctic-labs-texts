HMGB1 ubiquitination inhibition and extracellular secretion, mediated by m6A modified-exosomal lncRNA MALAT1, creates a communication between NK92-MI cells and tumor cells to aggravate acute lymphoblastic leukemia
Authors: Xiaofang Hong, Daiyan Yang, Jianhao Xing, Huihui Li, Jifu Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMALAT1 has been validated to favor the progression of acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), but its detailed mechanism remains obscure. This study explored the functional roles of ALL cells-derived exosomal MALAT1 in chemoresistance and malignant growth of ALL cells, as well as its underlying mechanisms.MethodsThe expression of target molecules was evaluated by qRT-PCR, Western blotting, immunofluorescence, and immunohistochemical staining. CCK-8, EdU staining and flow cytometry were conducted to determine cell proliferation and apoptosis. RIP, RNA-pull down, Co-IP, and MeRIP were used to investigate molecular mechanisms. ALL cells were injected into nude mice to evaluate in vivo tumor formation.ResultsMALAT1 and methyltransferase‐like 14 (METTL14) were up-regulated in ALL, which exhibited a positive correlation. METTL14-mediated m6A modification raised MALAT1 stability and expression, and consequently facilitated ALL cell growth and apoptosis inhibition. Exosomal MALAT1 suppressed tripartite motif-containing 27 (TRIM27)-mediated ubiquitination of High-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) in NK92-MI cells, thereby leading to adriamycin resistance and malignant development of ALL cells.ConclusionALL cells-derived exosomal MALAT1 was up-regulated by METTL14-mediated m6A modification, and subsequently restrained HMGB1 ubiquitination and degradation in NK92-MI cells, which resulted in adriamycin resistance and malignant growth of ALL cells. Introduction

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is a blood tumor originated from precursors of B/T precursor cells in the bone marrow [1]. ALL is frequently occurred in children between ages 2–5 [2]. Despite the great improvement in medical diagnosis and treatment of ALL, the outcome of refractory and recurrent patients remains unsatisfactory [3]. The exact pathogenesis of ALL has not been fully clarified. Thus, it is important to elucidate its underlying pathogenesis to develop novel and effective therapies for ALL.

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a kind of RNAs more than 200 bp in size, which participate in the progression of ALL [4]. So far, multiple lncRNAs have been demonstrated to be modulators of ALL. For example, lncRNA PVT1 contributed to ALL cell growth and apoptosis inhibition through sponging miR-486–5p [5]. High expression of lncRNA GAS5 has been reported to cause treatment failure and relapse of B-cell ALL [6]. LncRNA MALAT1 has been identified as an oncogene in various malignancies [7]. Notably, our previous study revealed that MALAT1 favored development of ALL cells via regulation of miR-205/PTK7 axis [8]. Nevertheless, the detailed upstream and downstream regulatory mechanisms of MALAT1 in ALL remain largely unknown.

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification is a prevalent epigenetic modification in eukaryotic RNAs [9]. m6A modification has been considered to participate in the malignant development of diverse cancers, including ALL [10]. m6A modification can be dynamically regulated by m6A “writer”, “eraser”, and “reader” proteins. Methyltransferase‐like 14 (METTL14) is an m6A “writer” protein and its gene polymorphisms have been found to be correlated with the risk of ALL patients [11]. An et al. reported that METTL14-mediated m6A modification of miR-211 affected the proliferation of T-cell ALL via regulation of TCF12 expression [12]. Recently, METTL14-mediated m6A modulation of MALAT1 has been documented in various tumors. For instance, METTL14 up-regulated MALAT1 via m6A modification, which drove the growth of oral squamous cell carcinoma cells [13]. So far, whether MALAT1 can be regulated by METTL14-mediated m6A modification in ALL has not been clarified.

High-mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) belongs to the HMGB superfamily. HMGB1 has been identified to be highly expressed in cancer cells [14]. Existing evidence has proved that high expression of HMGB1 confers drug resistance of multiple human cancers, including ALL [15]. It has been recognized that lncRNAs can modulate protein stability via RNA–protein interactions [16]. This indicates that MALAT1 may influence HMGB1 protein stability in ALL cells. To date, no research has elucidated the role of MALAT1/HMGB1 axis in ALL.

In this study, we discovered that MALAT1 was up-regulated by METTL14-mediated m6A modification. MALAT1 could be packaged into ALL cells-derived exosomes by hnRNPA2B1. Exosomal MALAT1 interacted with HMGB1 to block tripartite motif-containing 27 (TRIM27)-mediated ubiquitination and degradation of HMGB1, which contributed to adriamycin resistance of ALL cells. Overall, our findings uncover the complicated regulatory mechanism of ALL cells-derived exosomal MALAT1 in adriamycin resistance and malignant progression of ALL cells.

Materials and methods

Clinical sample collection

Peripheral blood lymphocytes were obtained from 23 ALL patients and 23 healthy volunteers (served as normal controls) at The Second Affiliated Hospital, Jiangxi Medical College, Nanchang University. The recruited ALL patients did not receive any treatments before. Written informed consent was collected from all participators before our experiment. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital, Jiangxi Medical College, Nanchang University.

Cell culture and treatment

Human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), ALL cell lines Jurkat, Reh, CEM, and Molt4 were cultured in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, NY, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco). NK92-MI cells were cultured in MyeloCult™ H5100 (STEMCELL Technologies, BC, Canada) supplemented with 12.5% horse serum (Gibco). 293 T cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco) with 10% FBS. All cells were obtained from American Type Culture Collection (MA, USA) and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Primary human NK cells were isolated from human PBMCs using the NK cell enrichment kit (Miltenyi, Shanghai, China). In short, PBMCs (1 × 107) were added with 10 µL NK Cell Biotin-Anti-body Cocktail, followed by incubation with 20 µL NK Cell MicroBead Cocktail. After that, the labeled cells were isolated using the MidiMACS Separator (Miltenyi) and cultured with RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS and 1000 IU/mL IL-2. The CEM/ADR and Jurkat/ADR (adriamycin-resistant) cell lines were established by long-term treatment with continuous increasing concentrations of adriamycin as previously described [17]. NK92-MI cells were treated with ALL cells-derived exosomes or vehicle control PBS.

Cell transfection and lentivirus infection

Short hairpin RNA targeting METTL14 (sh-METTL14#1, #2), negative control shRNA (sh-NC), small interfering RNA targeting IGF2BP1 (si-IGF2BP1), si-IGF2BP2, si-IGF2BP3, si-hnRNPA2B1, si-HMGB1 and si-NC were provided by GeneChem (Shanghai, China). Sequences encoding MALAT1, hnRNPA2B1, HMGB1, TRIM27, HMGB1, ubiquitin were inserted into the pcDNA3.1 vector with or without Myc, HA, or Flag tag. ALL cells and 293 T cells were transfected with shRNA, siRNA, or constructed plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA).

Lentiviruses harboring sh-NC or sh-MALAT1 were packaged by GeneChem. For lentivirus infection, ALL cells were infected with the lentiviruses containing 10 μg/mL Polybrene for 48h, followed by selection with puromycin (1 μg/ml).

Transwell assay

Cell migration and invasion were determined by transwell chambers (Corning, MI, USA), coated without or with Matrigel invasion or migration, respectively. Briefly, a total of 1 × 105 cells suspended in 100 µL of culture medium without FBS were placed in the upper transwell chambers. The lower chambers were loaded with 600 µL of culture medium containing FBS. After 24h incubation, the migrated/invaded cells were fixed, stained with crystal violet and photographed.

Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assay for cytotoxicity analysis

LDH activity was analyzed using the LDH assay kit (Beyotime). NK92-MI and primary human NK cells (1000 cells/well) were planted into 96-well plates. After various treatments, the supernatant was removed. The cells were added with LDH release reagent, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1h, and centrifugation. The, LDH detection solution was added and incubated for 30 min away from light. The absorbance was measured at 490 nm with 630 nm as reference on a microplate reader.

IFN-γ release assay

The release of cytokine IFN-γ from NK92-MI cells and primary human NK cells was detected by the Human IFN gamma ELISA Kit (ab46025, Abcam), according to the manufacturer’s instruction.

Quantitative real‑time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher). Then, RNA samples (1 μg) were reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the Prime-Script RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, Osaka, Japan,). Next, we conducted PCR amplification using the TB Green Premix Ex Taq II (RR820A, TaKaRa) in the 20 μL reaction system. The target gene level was quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blotting

Protein samples were isolated using the RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Haimen, China). Protein concentrations were assessed using the Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime). Subsequently, the protein lysates (40 μg) were loaded onto sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and transferred to the nitrocellulose membranes by western blot system (Bio-Rad, CA, USA). After blocking in 5% skim milk, the membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibodies against METTL14 (ab309096, 1:1000, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), TSG101 (ab125011, 1:1000, Abcam), CD63 (ab193349, 1:1000, Abcam), hnRNPA2B1 (ab31645, 1:1000, Abcam), HMGB1 (ab79823, 1:1000, Abcam), HA (ab236632, 1:1000, Abcam), FLAG (ab205606, 1:1000, Abcam), Myc (ab206486, 1:1000, Abcam), perforin (sc-33,655, 1:1000, Santa Cruz, TX, USA), Granzyme B (ab317458, 1:1000, Abcam), Cyclin D1 (ab156448, 1:1000, Abcam), MMP-2 (ab92536, 1:1000, Abcam), MMP-9 (ab283575, 1:1000, Abcam), GAPDH (ab9485, 1:2500, Abcam) at 4 °C, followed by incubation with secondary antibody (ab6721, 1:2000, Abcam) for 1h. The protein bands were developed using the Pierce ECL Western blotting substate (Thermo Fisher).

Total m6A RNA methylation quantification

Total m6A level was measured using the EpiQuik TM m6A RNA Methylation Quantification Kit (Epigentek, NY, USA). Briefly, 200 ng RNA samples were added into the binding solution. Subsequently, the samples were incubated with the capture antibody and detection antibody. The m6A level was quantified by detection the absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher).

Methylated RNA immunoprecipitation (MeRIP)

The m6A modification level in MALAT1 transcript was evaluated by MeRIP. In short, Protein A/G magnetic beads were pre-bound with the m6A antibody (#56,593, CST, MA, USA) for 30 min. Thereafter, the RNA samples were incubated with the anti-m6A antibody coupled to magnetic beads at 4 °C overnight. The m6A antibody-bound RNAs were eluted from the beads using the Elution Buffer. The m6A level of MALAT1 was detected using qRT-PCR.

Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8)

ALL, NK92-MI and primary human NK cell proliferation was determined using the CCK-8 kit (GlpBio, CA, USA). The ALL, NK92-MI, and primary human NK cells were seeded into 96-well plates (3000 cells per well). At the indicated time points, the cells were reacted with 10 μL of CCK-8 reagent for 1h at 37℃. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured on a microplate reader.

EdU staining

Proliferation capacity of ALL cells was assessed by the EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China). After seeding in 24-well plates, the cells were incubated with the EdU medium (10 μM) for 2h, and then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with the staining reaction solution for 30 min, followed by Hoechst staining for 30 min. The stained cells were examined under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Apoptosis detection

The apoptotic cells were detected using the Annexin V-APC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit (KGA1107-100, KeyGEN, Nanjing, China). ALL cells were collected and resuspended in binding buffer, followed by staining with Annexin V‐APC (5 μL) and PI (5 μL) in the dark for 20 min. The stained cells were analyzed on a flow cytometer (BD, NJ, USA).

Isolation and identification of exosomes

ALL cells (1 × 106) were maintained in the vesicle-depleted RPMI-1640 for 2 days before exosome collection. The culture medium was collected and subjected to centrifugation at 300 × g for 10 min and subsequent 2000 × g for 20 min to get rid of cell debris. For exosome isolation, the supernatant was centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 30 min, followed by ultra-centrifugation at 100,000 × g for 80 min twice. The exosome pellets were re-suspended in PBS and stored at − 80 °C.

The isolated exosomes were observed under transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEOL, MA, USA). The size distribution of exosomes was evaluated by the NanoSight NS300 instrument (Malvern, UK). The presence of exosome protein markers (TSG101 and CD63) was confirmed by Western blotting.

Exosome labeling and tracking

Isolated exosomes were labeled with 10 μM PKH26 fluorescent dye (MCE) for 20 min. Subsequently, the labeled exosomes were added to NK92-MI cells for exosomes uptake assay. After uptake for 24h at 37 °C, NK92-MI cells were observed under a fluorescence microscopy (Olympus).

Immunofluorescence staining

ALL or NK92-MI cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100. After treatment with 5% goat serum and 3% bovine serum albumin for 30 min, the cells were incubated with primary antibodies against TRIM27 (ab277118, 1:100, Abcam), HMGB1 (ab79823, 1:250, Abcam), γH2AX (ab22551, 1:50, Abcam), perforin (s-33,655, 1:50, Santa Cruz), Granzyme B (ab317458, 1:50, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the cells were probed with Cy3-conjugated Goat anti-Rabbit IgG (AS007, 1:100, ABclonal) or FITC-conjugated Goat anti-Mouse IgG (AS001, 1:100, ABclonal) for 1h. After nuclear staining with DAPI, the fluorescence was observed under a fluorescence microscopy.

Flow cytometry

Primary human NK cells were collected and then permeabilized with permeabilizer for 20 min. Subsequently, cells were incubated with anti-granzyme B (12-8898-82, eBioscience, CA, USA) and anti-perforin (12-9392-82, eBioscience) for 30 min. The intracellular levels of granzyme B and perforin in primary human NK cells were then analyzed by flow cytometry.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The release of HMGB1 from ALL cells was evaluated using the commercial ELISA Kit for HMGB1 (SEA399Hu, USCN, Wuhan, China) following the instruction manual.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP)

The RIP kit (Millipore, MA, USA) was adopted to analyze the binding of IGF2BP1, hnRNPA2B1, or HMGB1 protein with MALAT1. In brief, the magnetic beads were pre-incubated with anti-IGF2BP1 (ab184305, Abcam), anti-hnRNPA2B1 (ab31645, Abcam), anti-HMGB1 (ab228624, Abcam) or anti-IgG (ab172730, Abcam). Then, cell lysates were incubated with the magnetic bead-antibody complexes at 4 °C overnight. RNA was extracted from the RNA–protein complexes after digestion with proteinase K. The enrichment of MALAT1 was measured by qRT-PCR.

RNA pull-down assay

RNA pull-down assay was conducted to verify the binding of MALAT1 to IGF2BP1/HMGB1 using the Pierce Magnetic RNA–Protein Pull-down Kit (Thermo Fisher). The biotin-NC probe and MALAT1 probe were provided by Biomics Biotechnology (Beijing, China). ALL cell lysates were incubated with the biotin probe pre-bound to magnetic beads at 4 °C overnight. Finally, the pull-down complexes were purified and subjected to qRT-PCR.

Co-immunoprecipitation assay (Co-IP)

To confirm the exogenous interaction between HMGB1 and TRIM27 proteins, 293 T cells were transfected with FLAG-HMGB1 and HA-TRIM27. After transfection for 24h, the cells were lysed with RIPA buffer. The lysates of cells were pre‐cleaned with protein A/G beads at 4 °C for 4h, followed by immunoprecipitation with protein A/G beads‐coupled anti-FLAG (ab205606, Abcam) or anti-IgG (ab172730, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. The bound protein A/G beads were washed with IP lysis buffer and detected by Western blotting.

For the detection of HMGB1 ubiquitination, NK92-MI cells were transfected with Myc-HMGB1 and HA-ubiquitin. After immunoprecipitation with protein A/G beads‐coupled anti-Myc (ab206486, Abcam) as described above, the protein levels of HA and Myc were assessed by Western blotting.

Nude mouse xenograft model

For the establishment of xenograft model, BALB/c nude mice (4–6 weeks old) were obtained from SLAC Laboratory Animal Center (Shanghai, China) and randomly divided into sh-NC, sh-MALAT1, and sh-MALAT1 + HMGB1 inhibitor (glycyrrhizin) groups (n = 8 per group). 5.0 × 106 Jurkat cells stably infected with lentiviruses harboring sh-NC or sh-MALAT1 were subcutaneously inoculated into the nude mice. The mice were intraperitoneally injected with glycyrrhizin (100 mg/kg, ALADDIN, Shanghai, China) or DMSO twice a week for 2 weeks. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = (Length × Width2)/2. Finally, all mice were subjected to cervical dislocation. The tumors were excised and weighed. All animal protocols were approved by the Ethic Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital, Jiangxi Medical College, Nanchang University.

Immunohistochemical staining

The tumor tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5 μm-sections. After deparaffinization and hydration, the sections were received antigen retrieval in EDTA buffer, followed by treatment with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 30 min. The sections were probed with primary antibodies against Ki67 (ab15580, 1:100, Abcam), HMGB1 (ab79823, 1:400, Abcam), and NK1.1 (ab289542, 1:100, Abcam) at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, the sections were probed with Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG (AS014, 1:50, ABclonal) for 30 min at 37 °C and developed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine. The images were photographed under a light microscope.

Statistical analysis

All measurement data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Comparison was conducted using Student’s t-test or One-Way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Bonferroni post hoc test using SPSS 21.0 software. Spearman correlation analysis was adopted to evaluate the correlation between measures. P value less than 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Positive association between MALAT1 and METTL14 in ALL samples

First, we discovered that the total m6A level was higher in ALL samples as compared with normal controls (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, the mRNA and protein abundance of m6A “writer” METTL14 was evidently enhanced in ALL patients (Fig. 1B, C). Besides, we observed an elevation in MALAT1 expression in ALL specimens (Fig. 1D). Of note, there was a positive correlation between MALAT1 and METTL14 levels in ALL samples (Fig. 1E). Therefore, high expression of MALAT1 and METTL14 in ALL might have close association with the development of ALL.

C METTL14 protein level was detected by Western blotting in peripheral blood lymphocytes from ALL patients (n = 3) and healthy controls (n = 3). D MALAT1 expression was assessed by qRT-PCR in peripheral blood lymphocytes from ALL patients (n = 23) and healthy controls (n = 23). E Spearman correlation analysis of the association between METTL14 and MALAT1 in ALL patients (n = 23). **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

METTL14 knockdown delayed ALL cell proliferation

Next, we investigated whether METTL14 regulated malignant growth of ALL cells. We observed that ALL cells exhibited a higher expression of METTL14 as compared with normal PBMCs, among which Jurkat and CEM cells exhibited the highest level and were selected subsequently (Fig. 2A, B). Moreover, the total m6A level was elevated in Jurkat and CEM cells (Fig. 2C). To verify the biological function of METTL14, we transfected sh-METTL14 into ALL cells. METTL14 expression in ALL cells was strikingly reduced after transfection with sh-METTL14#1 and #2 (Fig. 2D, E), and sh-METTL14#1 with a higher silencing efficiency was adopted. CCK-8 data indicated that the proliferation ability of ALL cells was lowered by METTL14 knockdown (Fig. 2F). Additionally, the number of EdU positive staining cells was reduced in sh-METTL14-transfeted ALL cells (Fig. 2G). Furthermore, METTL14 knockdown significantly restrained the migration and invasion of ALL cells (Fig. 2H). Besides, we further investigated the expression of markers related to cell cycle (Cyclin D1) and metastasis (MMP-2, and MMP-9) [18, 19] Western blotting indicated that the protein levels of Cyclin D1, MMP-2, and MMP-9 were reduced by METTL14 deficiency (Fig. 2I). To evaluate the regulation of METTL14 knockdown in DNA damage, immunofluorescent staining for DNA damage marker γH2AX was performed. The results showed that METTL14 silencing did not affect γH2AX expression in ALL cells (Supplementary Fig. 2). Thus, DNA damage repair pathways might not directly be involved in METTL14 silencing-mediated inhibition in ALL cell proliferation. Taken together, these observations suggested that high expression of METTL14 contributed to the malignant growth of ALL cells.

METTL14-mediated m6A modification increased MALAT1 stability and expression

Given the positive correlation between MALAT1 and METTL14 levels, we sought to validate whether m6A writer METTL14 modulated MALAT1 expression via m6A modification. SRAMP database predicted several m6A modification sites on MALAT1 (Supplementary Fig. 1). In addition, MALAT1 was highly expressed in ALL cells, especially in Jurkat and CEM cells (Fig. 3A). Me-RIP assay showed that the m6A level of MALAT1 was strikingly increased in ALL cells (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, METTL14 silencing remarkably reduced the m6A level of MALAT1 (Fig. 3C). We also observed a reduction of MALAT1 expression in METTL14-depleted ALL cells (Fig. 3D). Similarly, the stability of MALAT1 was declined after METTL14 knockdown (Fig. 3E). IGF2BPs are m6A readers that play crucial roles in m6A modification [20]. Thus, we further explored the regulation of IGF2BP1-3 in MALAT1 expression. We found that only sh-IGF2BP1 led to a remarkable decrease in MALAT1 expression, while si-IGF2BP2 or si-IGF2BP3 did not affect MALAT1 expression (Fig. 3F). Besides, IGF2BP1 was up-regulated in ALL cells (Fig. 3G, H). Furthermore, IGF2BP1 deficiency resulted in a reduction in MALAT1 stability (Fig. 3I). In addition, the direct binding of IGF2BP1 to MALAT1 was verified by RIP and RNA pull-down assays (Fig. 3J, K). To sum up, METTL14 stabilized MALAT1 via m6A modification with assistance of IGF2BP1.

MALAT1 overexpression reversed sh-METTL14-mediated influence on ALL cell proliferation and apoptosis

To examine the influence of METTL14/MALAT1 axis on malignant capacities of ALL cells, sh-METTL14 together with oe-MALAT1 plasmid were transfected into ALL cells. qRT-PCR validated the overexpression efficiency of oe-MALAT1 in ALL cells (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Functionally, the reduced growth capacity of METTL14-deficient Jurkat cells could be recovered after MALAT1 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 3B, C). Additionally, sh-METTL14-induced apoptosis of CEM cells was abrogated by MALAT1 overexpression (Supplementary Fig. 3D). Moreover, MALAT1 overexpression reversed METTL14 knockdown-mediated inhibition in migration and invasion of ALL cells (Supplementary Fig. 3E). Collectively, sh-METTL14-mediated proliferation inhibition and apoptosis were abolished by MALAT1 overexpression.

Extracellular MALAT1 was released via incorporation into exosomes

To determine whether MALAT1 was delivered via exosomes, the level of extracellular MALAT1 was detected after RNase treatment. qRT-PCR showed that MALAT1 level in ALL cell culture medium was not changed by treating with RNase alone, whereas combination with Triton X-100 strikingly lowered MALAT1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 4A), revealing that extracellular MALAT1 was packaged by the membrane rather than direct secretion. Additionally, there was no significant difference in MALAT1 expression in exosomes and whole culture medium (Supplementary Fig. 4B), suggesting the enrichment of MALAT1 in exosomes. Next, the exosomes were isolated from ALL cell culture medium and observed under TEM (Supplementary Fig. 4C). NTA analysis showed that the size distribution of exosomes was ranged approximately 100—150 nm (Supplementary Fig. 4D). The presence of exosome markers, TSG101 and CD63 in exosomes was confirmed by Western blotting (Supplementary Fig. 4E). Of note, MALAT1 level was higher in exosomes than that in ALL cells (Supplementary Fig. 4F). These data suggested that extracellular MALAT1 could be secreted through incorporation into exosomes.

hnRNPA2B1 mediated the packaging of MALAT1 into exosomes

Then we elucidated the specific modulator for the sorting of MALAT1 into exosomes. hnRNPA2B1 has been identified to facilitate the packaging of RNAs into exosomes [21, 22]. As assessed by RIP assay, MALAT1 could be enriched by hnRNPA2B1 antibody in ALL cells (Fig. 4A). In addition, RNA pull‑down assay proved that hnRNPA2B1 could bind to MALAT1 (Fig. 4B). Subsequently, ALL cells were transfected with si-hnRNPA2B1 or oe-hnRNPA2B1 to knock down or overexpress hnRNPA2B1 (Fig. 4C). Exosomal MALAT1 level was reduced in hnRNPA2B1-silenced ALL cells, but enhanced in hnRNPA2B1-overexpressed ALL cells (Fig. 4D). Our findings demonstrated that hnRNPA2B1 contributed to the sorting of MALAT1 into exosomes.

ALL cells transferred exosomal MALAT1 to up-regulate HMGB1 expression in NK92-MI cells

Natural killer (NK) cells exert key roles in innate immunity, especially for fighting against ALL [23]. We further examined whether ALL cells-derived exosomal MALAT1 regulated NK cell function. For this purpose, NK92-MI cells were incubated with exosomes labeled with PKH26. The intercellular trafficking of exosomes with green fluorescence in NK92-MI cells was achieved by fluorescence microscope (Fig. 5A). Of note, exosome treatment distinctly raised HMGB1 expression in NK92-MI cells (Fig. 5B). Besides, we did not observe difference in HMGB1 mRNA level of the supernatants and NK92-MI cells (Fig. 5C). Whereas, the protein level of HMGB1 was higher in NK92-MI cells than that in supernatants (Fig. 5D). Furthermore, the expression and cytoplasmic translocation of HMGB1 in NK92-MI cells were facilitated by exosome treatment (Fig. 5E, F). Accordingly, exosome treatment contributed to the release of HMGB1 from NK92-MI cells (Fig. 5G). Therefore, exosomal MALAT1 facilitated the protein expression and release of HMGB1 from NK92-MI cells.

ALL cells-derived exosomal MALAT1 inhibited HMGB1 ubiquitination via blocking the interplay between HMGB1 and TRIM27

Considering that exosomal MALAT1 enhanced HMGB1 expression at the posttranscriptional level, we speculated that ubiquitination-mediated degradation might be involved in this process. As anticipated, ALL cells-derived exosomes lowered the ubiquitin level of HMGB1 (Fig. 6A, B). Furthermore, cycloheximide (CHX) chase results indicated that the degradation of HMGB1 protein in NK92-MI cells was delayed by exosome administration (Fig. 6C, D). These data suggested that exosomes attenuated ubiquitination-mediated degradation of HMGB1. To further determine the detailed mechanism that regulated HMGB1 degradation, Co-IP assay confirmed a direct interplay between HMGB1 and TRIM27 proteins (Fig. 6E). Moreover, TRIM27 overexpression reduced HMGB1 protein abundance in exosomes-treated NK92-MI cells, which was abolished by MG132 (proteasome inhibitor), rather than CQ (lysosome inhibitor) (Fig. 6F), indicating that TRIM27 contributed to ubiquitination-mediated degradation of HMGB1. RNA pull-down assay showed that HMGB1 was markedly enriched by the MALAT1 probe in NK92-MI cells (Fig. 6G). RIP assay further showed that MALAT1 was significantly enriched by the HMGB1 antibody in NK92-MI cells (Fig. 6H). Moreover, immunofluorescence staining coupled with Co-IP revealed that MALAT1 weakened the interplay between TRIM27 and HMGB1 in NK92-MI cells (Fig. 6I, J). Taken together, MALAT1 repressed E3 ligase TRIM27-mediated ubiquitination of HMGB1.

ALL cells-derived exosomes harboring MALAT1 inhibited the function of NK92-MI and primary human NK cells, which was abolished by HMGB1 silencing

Next, we evaluated the influence of exosomal MALAT1/HMGB1 axis on NK92-MI cell function. Exosomes derived from ALL cells were co-cultured with NK92-MI cells with or without HMGB1 knockdown. Compared with NK92-MI cells cultured alone, NK92-MI cells co-cultured with exosome treatment (defined as exosomes derived from Jurkat and CEM cells) had significantly suppressed viability and cytotoxicity, which was reversed by HMGB1 depletion (Fig. 7A, B). Furthermore, the mRNA levels of perforin and granzyme B were reduced in ALL cells-derived exosomes-treated NK92-MI cells, whereas these alterations were reversed by HMGB1 silencing (Fig. 7C). The release of IFN-γ from NK92-MI cells was restrained by ALL cells-derived exosomes, which was abrogated by HMGB1 deficiency (Fig. 7D). Accordingly, immunofluorescence staining demonstrated that ALL cells-derived exosomes inhibited perforin and granzyme B expression in NK92-MI cells; however, HMGB1 knockdown neutralized the action of ALL cells-derived exosomes (Fig. 7E). Furthermore, we aimed to validate the above findings in primary human NK cells. As shown in Fig. 7F–I, ALL cells-derived exosome treatment weakened viability, cytotoxicity, IFN-γ release, perforin and granzyme B expression in primary human NK cells, which were counteracted by HMGB1 silencing. Collectively, ALL cells-derived exosomal MALAT1 resulted in dysfunction of NK92-MI and primary human NK cells via up-regulation of HMGB1.

Knockdown of HMGB1 in NK cells restrained adriamycin resistance of ALL cells

ALL cells may develop chemotherapy resistance, showing declined sensitivity to NK cells-mediated immune cytotoxicity [24]. To elucidate the influence of HMGB1-silenced NK92-MI cells on chemotherapy resistance of ALL cells, we established two adriamycin-resistant ALL cells. The IC50 value of adriamycin was evidently elevated in adriamycin-resistant group, suggesting the successful establishment of adriamycin-resistant ALL cells (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B). In addition, co-culture with HMGB1-depleted NK92-MI cells strikingly reduced adriamycin IC50 value of adriamycin-resistant ALL cells (Supplementary Fig. 5C, D). Thus, silencing of HMGB1 in NK92-MI cells effectively enhanced adriamycin sensitivity of ALL cells.

Silencing of MALAT1 repressed tumor growth and activated NK cells in vivo via inhibition of HMGB1

Finally, we explored the role of MALAT1/HMGB1 axis in the progression of ALL in vivo. We found that the tumor volume and weight were smaller in sh-MALAT1 group than those in sh-NC group, which was more obvious in sh-MALAT1 + HMGB1 inhibitor glycyrrhizin group (Fig. 8A–C). Besides, the expression of Ki67 and HMGB1 in tumors was reduced by MALAT1 knockdown, and this action was intensified by co-treatment with glycyrrhizin (Fig. 8D). Furthermore, MALAT1 deficiency enhanced the percentage of CD107a+ NK cells in tumor-infiltrating compartment of xenografts, which was further augmented by glycyrrhizin administration (Fig. 8E). In addition, the protein levels of perforin-1 and Granzyme B were raised, while the protein level of HMGB1 was reduced by MALAT1 knockdown, particularly observed in the group of co-treatment with glycyrrhizin counteracted these changes (Fig. 8F). Besides, the expression of NK1.1 (NK cell lineage marker) in tumors was evaluated by immunohistochemical staining. The results showed that NK1.1 expression was increased after MALAT1 knockdown, which was further enhanced by glycyrrhizin co-treatment (Fig. 8G). Collectively, inactivation of MALAT1/HMGB1 axis delayed ALL growth and activated NK cells in vivo.

Discussion

MALAT1, a well-established lncRNA, is up-regulated in multiple hematological cancers and acts as an oncogene to favor their malignant development [25]. MALAT1 was documented to be highly expressed in ALL, which suggested a poor prognosis of patients [26]. In line with this study, we identified an abnormal high expression of MALAT1 in ALL clinical samples. However, the regulatory mechanism for the high expression of MALAT1 in ALL has not been elucidated. m6A has been recognized to be one of modulatory mechanisms for lncRNAs [27]. As an m6A methyltransferase, METTL14 was found to methylate primary miR-211 in T-cell ALL12. It has been revealed that m6A modification can be regulated by m6A reader proteins, such as IGF2BP1/2/3, to exert specific biological function [28]. So far, whether METTL14 can affect ALL progression via m6A modification of MALAT1 remains undetermined. In this work, METTL14 was up-regulated in ALL and positively associated with MALAT1. Moreover, METTL14-mediated m6A modification raised MALAT1 stability via interaction with m6A reader IGF2BP1. Functionally, METTL14 knockdown restrained growth and triggered apoptosis of ALL cells via down-regulation of MALAT1. Our observations prove the contribution of METTL14/MALAT1 axis to the malignant growth of ALL cells. Because DNA damage repair can enable tumor cells to develop resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy [29, 30]. Moreover, targeting the factors involved in DNA damage repair is also an important strategy to overcome radiotherapy [31] or chemotherapy [32] resistance. Herein, we discovered that METTL14 silencing did not affect γH2AX expression in ALL cells, implying that DNA damage repair pathways were not involved in METTL14 silencing-mediated inhibition in ALL cell proliferation. Considering our result that METTL14 did not affect DNA damage repair, whether METTL14/MALAT1 axis influenced adriamycin resistance of ALL cells through DNA damage repair pathway was not examined in the current study. However, it will be a completely new perspective and direction for our subsequent research.

Mounting evidence has suggested that exosomes play pivotal roles in tumor microenvironment via delivering signaling RNAs, DNAs, or proteins to neighboring cells [33]. Cancer-derived exosomes may influence NK cell function via the regulation of target molecules, thereby inducing immunosuppression [34]. For instance, exosomal miR-552-5p from gastric cancer cells led to NK cell dysfunction via targeting PD-1/PD-L1 axis [35]. Another research indicated that multiple myeloma cells-derived exosomal NEAT1 repressed NK-cell activity through regulation of EZH2/PBX1 axis, which caused immune escape of multiple myeloma cells [36]. In this work, we discovered that MALAT1 was delivered from ALL cells to NK92-MI cells via exosomes. Next, we explored how MALAT1 was packaged into exosomes. hnRNPA2B1 has been verified to encapsulate RNAs to exosomes via binding to specific sequences [37]. Chen et al. documented that hnRNPA2B1 interacted with lncRNA LNMAT2 to facilitate its packaging into exosomes of bladder cancer cells [38]. Herein, we discovered that hnRNPA2B1 was responsible for the packaging of MALAT1 into ALL-derived exosomes. Therefore, MALAT1 was encapsulated to ALL cells-derived exosomes with assistance of hnRNPA2B1, and then transferred to NK92-MI cells.

HMGB1 has been verified to act as an extracellular protein to exert diverse roles in inflammation and oncogenesis [39, 40]. As an inflammatory mediator, HMGB1 protein can be released by NK cells [41]. Existing studies have suggested that HMGB1 overexpression contributes to the progression and lymph node metastasis of various malignancies [14]. As reported, miR-181a-mediated inhibition of HMGB1 delayed the proliferation of ALL cells [42]. Another study revealed that cytoplastic translocation of HMGB1 conferred chemotherapeutic resistance of ALL cells via autophagy induction [15]. In the current study, ALL cells-derived exosomes enhanced the release of HMGB1 from NK92-MI cells, and knockdown of HMGB1 in NK92-MI cells sensitized ALL cells to adriamycin.

Next, we investigated the mechanism through which exosomal MALAT1 facilitated HMGB1 expression and release in NK92-MI cells. It has been recognized that lncRNAs are involved in the regulatory mechanism of ubiquitination-mediated degradation of proteins [43]. For example, linc-AhRA functioned as a scaffold to favor proteasome-dependent TBK1 degradation in microglia [44]. TRIM27 belongs to the E3 ligase family, which recognizes its target protein for degradation [45]. Here, we found that the protein level, but not the mRNA level of HMGB1 was increased by ALL-derived exosomes. Furthermore, MALAT1 overexpression reduced the ubiquitin level of HMGB1 and blocked the interaction between TRIM27 and HMGB1. Therefore, exosomal MALAT1 enhanced HMGB1 protein stability via inhibiting TRIM27-mediated ubiquitination of HMGB1, thereby enhancing the release of HMGB1.

Taken together, we found that METTL14 enhanced MALAT1 expression in an IGF2BP1-m6A dependent manner. MALAT1 was transferred to NK92-MI cells via exosomes, which led to stabilization of HMGB1 protein via repressing TRIM27-mediated ubiquitination, thus causing adriamycin resistance and malignant development of ALL cells. Although the clinical sample size is limited in this study, and the clinical correlation of our results needs to be evaluated in more clinical samples in the future, our observations highlight the regulatory role of METTL14/MALAT1/HMGB1 axis in ALL and identify potential therapeutic targets for ALL.
