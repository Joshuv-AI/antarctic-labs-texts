Sperm DNA fragmentation: how to test, when to test, and what to do with abnormal results—a pragmatic mini-review for clinical practice
Authors: Sandro C Esteves, Peter Humaidan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSperm DNA fragmentation (SDF) represents strand breaks that may compromise embryo development, reproductive outcomes, and offspring health, particularly when the oocyte’s repair capacity is limited. This mini-review provides a clinically pragmatic framework addressing how to test, when to test, and whom to test for SDF, translating biological and laboratory insights into actionable andrology practice. The mechanistic basis of SDF is summarized, including the two-step hypothesis linking defective chromatin packaging to oxidative stress, and the combined influence of paternal and maternal aging on the creation and repair of DNA lesions. Contemporary methods for detecting both single- and double-strand breaks (DSBs), as well as DSB-specific platforms, are reviewed with emphasis on assay-specific cut-offs and clinical indications. Management strategies are outlined, prioritizing andrological optimization through lifestyle modification, antioxidant supplementation, treatment of genital tract infection, varicocele repair, and selected hormonal therapy, followed by retesting after one spermatogenic cycle. For persistently high SDF, selective use of testicular sperm for ICSI (Testi-ICSI) and laboratory adjuncts is discussed, supported by evidence of lower miscarriage and higher live-birth rates in defined high-SDF phenotypes. The limitations of the existing observational evidence base—particularly the paucity of phenotype-targeted randomized controlled trials—are highlighted. The review also underscores the value of embedding SDF testing within quality-managed programs and incorporating systematic audits of indications, treatment effects, and ART outcomes. Beyond ART, SDF assessment is positioned within preconception care, recognizing sperm chromatin integrity as a determinant of reproductive and intergenerational health. Future priorities include randomized trials comparing testicular and ejaculated sperm for ICSI in high-SDF cohorts, validation of DSB-specific assays, and longitudinal offspring follow-up to clarify intergenerational effects. This integrative approach promotes precision rather than proliferation of testing, aligning molecular insight with clinical prudence to improve reproductive and generational outcomes.

Introduction

Sperm chromatin integrity is fundamental to male reproduction as it safeguards the paternal genome and epigenetic cargo during transit, fertilization, and early embryogenesis (Carrell, 2012). When sperm chromatin lesions exceed the oocyte’s capacity for repair—particularly in the context of advanced maternal age—reproductive outcomes may deteriorate across natural conception, IUI, and ART (Nielsen et al., 2023).

Although the basic semen analysis remains the cornerstone of laboratory assessment (Björndahl et al., 2023), it largely represents a morphological proxy for fertilizing capacity and does not capture underlying genomic instability (Esteves, 2022). Sperm DNA fragmentation (SDF) testing directly interrogates this dimension by quantifying the frequency of DNA strand breaks (Esteves et al., 2021).

For clinicians, the practical question is therefore not whether SDF occurs, but how testing can be used meaningfully to improve care: how to measure it reliably, when to deploy testing, in whom it adds diagnostic or prognostic value, and how to manage persistently high SDF. As emerging technologies begin to differentiate specific types of DNA damage—particularly double-strand breaks (DSBs), which may be especially detrimental—the need for a coherent, clinically anchored approach has become increasingly urgent. At the same time, the rapid proliferation of new platforms demands caution to ensure that early data are not overinterpreted or applied beyond the limits of current evidence.

In this mini-review, we integrate mechanistic, diagnostic, and therapeutic perspectives to provide a concise yet comprehensive synthesis for everyday clinical use. Drawing on our group’s experimental, clinical, and meta-analytic work, as well as broader evidence synthesis and guideline developments, we aim to translate SDF research into clinically relevant guidance for patient-centered reproductive care. Importantly, this review does not advocate universal or routine SDF screening. Instead, SDF testing is positioned as a selective, question-driven tool to be used when results are likely to influence counseling or management beyond standard andrological evaluation. Throughout the manuscript, emphasis is placed on proportionality, prioritizing reversible factors, and carefully integrating SDF results into a couple-centered clinical approach to minimize unnecessary burden.

Mechanisms: origin and repair of sperm DNA breaks

The Aitken-De Iuliis two-step hypothesis

The molecular origin of DNA fragmentation in mature spermatozoa reflects the residue of several partly overlapping processes, elegantly captured by the Aitken and De Iuliis two-step hypothesis (Aitken and De Iuliis, 2010). In the first step, defective chromatin remodeling during spermiogenesis leaves regions of DNA incompletely protaminated or transiently cleaved by topoisomerase II. These physiological nicks are typically sealed before nuclear condensation, but when protamination is impaired—due to genetic variants, oxidative stress, or varicocele—unresolved breaks persist as vulnerable sites within the condensed nucleus.

The second step occurs post-testicularly, when superimposing oxidative stress in the epididymis or ejaculate—arising from leukocytes, immature spermatozoa, or environmental exposures—targets these susceptible domains. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) induce base oxidation (e.g. 8-oxo-deoxyguanosine) and sugar damage, potentially generating single-strand breaks (SSBs); clustered oxidative lesions can also evolve into DSBs. The result is a heterogeneous population of spermatozoa containing oxidative lesions, SSBs, and DSBs embedded within otherwise compact chromatin.

These mechanisms explain why infertile men with conditions associated with excessive oxidative stress, such as poor lifestyle, varicocele, genital-tract inflammation, or metabolic disease, frequently exhibit elevated SDF (Esteves et al., 2015a). They also clarify why SDF can be high in normozoospermic men and why lesions tend to cluster in protamine-deficient domains (Nasr-Esfahani et al., 2005; Andrabi et al., 2024).

Crucially, the clinical impact depends on lesion type and oocyte repair capacity. DSBs generally carry more severe reproductive consequences because their repair requires complex homology-dependent pathways that may exceed the repair capacity of the oocyte and early embryo (Casanovas et al., 2019; Ribas-Maynou and Benet, 2019). This model further explains why interventions that reduce oxidative exposure—varicocele repair, infection treatment, lifestyle optimization, weight loss, and even short abstinence intervals—can sometimes improve DNA integrity (Esteves et al., 2020; Lira Neto et al., 2021; Teixeira et al., 2021; Humaidan et al., 2022; Sørensen et al., 2023; Pereira et al., 2025), even though the underlying chromatin-remodeling defect remains.

Oocyte-mediated repair and its limitations

After fertilization, the oocyte becomes the sole custodian of DNA repair. Because spermatozoa contribute essentially no cytoplasmic enzymes, repair of paternal DNA relies entirely on maternally stored mRNAs and proteins supporting base-excision, nucleotide-excision, homologous recombination, and non-homologous end-joining pathways (Champroux et al., 2016). Experimental studies across mammals, together with emerging human evidence, indicate that the zygote can detect sperm-derived lesions, activate checkpoints (including ATM/ATR-mediated signaling), and attempt repair during the pronuclear stages and early cleavage divisions (Suganuma et al., 2005; Chabory et al., 2009; García-Rodríguez et al., 2018).

This capacity, however, is finite and variable. Oocyte repair efficiency appears to decline with advancing maternal age, likely due to depletion or dysfunction of stored repair factors, mitochondrial insufficiency, and reduced tolerance to additional genomic stress (Marinaro, 2023). When the burden of SDF exceeds this repair threshold—or when DSBs remain unrepaired—embryonic competence deteriorates, manifesting as developmental delay, mitotic errors, implantation failure, or early pregnancy loss (De Iuliis et al., 2009; Champroux et al., 2016; Esteves et al., 2021). This model explains the well-recognized clinical interaction between paternal DNA damage and maternal age: a level of SDF tolerated by a young oocyte may be clinically consequential in an older one with diminished repair reserve.

Paternal age also contributes to the accumulation of sperm DNA lesions (Evenson et al., 2020). Older men show a progressive rise in SDF, driven by cumulative oxidative stress, declining antioxidant defenses, impaired DNA-repair fidelity in spermatogonia, and ongoing replicative stress associated with lifelong spermatogenesis (Maher et al., 2018; Bertoncelli Tanaka et al., 2019; Aitken, 2023). Mitochondrial dysfunction further amplifies oxidative damage in the testis and epididymis, intensifying the second step of the Aitken–De Iuliis model (Aitken and De Iuliis, 2010). Telomere attrition, epigenetic drift, and reduced efficiency of spermatogonial quality control compound this instability (Aitken, 2023, 2024).

When paternal aging-related defects converge with diminished oocyte repair capacity in older partners, the result is a synergistic rise in embryonic arrest and miscarriage risk (Esteves, 2025). Consistent with this, animal and human studies associate advanced paternal age with elevated SDF levels and increased rates of aneuploid embryos (Fu et al., 2023; Geng et al., 2025). These effects, however, operate probabilistically rather than deterministically: not all older men with high SDF experience adverse outcomes, and the female partner’s reproductive potential remains a major modulating factor.

How to test: assays, thresholds, and quality control

Validated assays and what they measure

Four principal assays are currently used in clinical and research settings: TUNEL (Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP Nick-End Labeling), which enzymatically labels DNA breaks (Gorczyca et al., 1993; Duran et al., 2002; Henkel et al., 2004; Borini et al., 2006; Benchaib et al., 2007; Frydman et al., 2008; Mitchell et al., 2011; Sharma et al., 2016; Ribeiro et al., 2017; Santi et al., 2018); SCSA (Sperm Chromatin Structure Assay), a flow-cytometric measure of chromatin susceptibility to acid denaturation (Evenson et al., 1980, 1999; Bungum et al., 2004, 2007; Evenson, 2022); SCD (Sperm Chromatin Dispersion) (Fernández et al., 2005; Meseguer et al., 2011; Gosálvez et al., 2013; Feijó and Esteves, 2014; Esteves et al., 2015b, 2022; Vandekerckhove et al., 2016), a morphological analogue of SCSA; and alkaline Comet (Albert et al., 2016; Nicopoullos et al., 2019), a single-cell electrophoresis technique reflecting strand breaks (Table 1) (Esteves et al., 2021). They estimate global SDF and do not distinguish SSBs from DSBs. Although each interrogates slightly different aspects of chromatin integrity, cross-assay correlations range from moderate to strong for clinical classification, but absolute values are not interchangeable (Ribas-Maynou et al., 2013).

Laboratories are encouraged to adopt a single validated platform and establish internal reference ranges (Esteves et al., 2021). Basic semen analysis should be performed concurrently after 2–3 days of ejaculatory abstinence (Esteves, 2025), as SDF increases with longer intervals (Agarwal et al., 2016; Raditya et al., 2025). Biological variability is modest; for borderline results, a confirmatory test is advisable (Esteves et al., 2022). For diagnostic confirmation of borderline results, repeat testing should follow standardization of pre-analytical conditions, particularly the ejaculation abstinence interval. In line with the World Health Organization (WHO) guideline for the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of infertility, repeat semen-based assessments are ideally performed after approximately one spermatogenic cycle (∼11 weeks) to improve diagnostic reliability and reduce misclassification due to biological and analytical variability (Mburu et al., 2026).

Suggested thresholds and reporting

Assay-specific ranges link elevated global SDF levels with infertility risk (∼17–26%) and adverse ART outcomes (∼20–36%) (Table 1). Many centers adopt ≥20% as a clinically actionable threshold and ≥30% as a ‘high SDF’ zone where negative clinical signals strengthen (Santi et al., 2018; Esteves et al., 2021; Nielsen et al., 2023). Reports should specify assay type, abstinence period, internal quality control metrics, and the lab’s validated decision thresholds (Esteves, 2025). These values serve as decision-support tools, not absolute cut-offs, and must always be interpreted in the context of female characteristics and the wider clinical scenario.

DSBs: detection and clinical significance

Why DSBs matter

DSBs are regarded as the most biologically consequential form of sperm DNA damage (Li et al., 2025). By affecting both strands of the helix, they challenge accurate repair and increase the risk of deletions, chromosomal rearrangements, and other genome-level errors (Ribas-Maynou and Benet, 2019; Alvarez et al., 2023). Experimental models demonstrate that embryos exposed to high paternal DSB loads exhibit higher aneuploidy rates and often arrest before blastulation (Casanovas et al., 2019).

Clinically, DSB-enriched profiles may correlate more closely with adverse reproductive outcomes—such as recurrent pregnancy loss (RPL) and implantation failure—than total SDF alone (Ribas-Maynou et al., 2012; Cheng et al., 2021; Lee et al., 2025). While much of the evidence has historically been derived from relatively small or single-center studies, recent multicentre prospective data demonstrate that DSB-SDF independently predict live birth following IVF (Humaidan et al., 2026). These observations have stimulated interest in distinguishing the type in addition to quantifying the extent of DNA fragmentation.

Platforms for DSB detection

The Comet assay can detect different classes of damage. Under neutral pH conditions, it preferentially enriches for DSBs by preserving the double-strand configuration (Ribas-Maynou et al., 2012; Cortés-Gutiérrez et al., 2017; Humaidan et al., 2026) (Table 1). Neutral Comet studies have demonstrated stronger associations between DSB burden and poor embryo development or RPL, and more recently with live birth following IVF, supporting the notion that it captures a clinically critical subset of DNA damage (Ribas-Maynou and Benet, 2019; Drakeley et al., 2026; Humaidan et al., 2026).

Recently, new commercial platforms have emerged (Wang et al., 2023), including assays such as the R11 plus semi-automated systems, which enables rapid quantification of DSB load based on proprietary DNA-release kinetics (Table 1). Early results using this approach suggest that elevated DSB indices are associated with increased embryo aneuploidy and miscarriage risk (Wang et al., 2023; Lee et al., 2025). While promising, these findings should be replicated independently and validated against outcomes such as live birth.

Caveats and standardization

Despite the biological appeal, DSB-enriched assays require further standardization, external validation, and correlation with clinically meaningful outcomes before broad clinical adoption can be recommended. Neutral Comet and R11-type platforms require rigorous quality assurance and well-validated, laboratory-specific decision thresholds before they are broadly incorporated into clinical algorithms. Studies conflict on the superiority of any single platform in unselected infertility populations, and performance varies by protocol, scoring system, and phenotype distribution (Esteves et al., 2021). A prudent approach is to (i) maintain one validated assay for global SDF assessment within each laboratory; (ii) add a DSB-enriched read-out selectively when the pre-test probability is high (e.g. RPL, poor embryo development, advanced maternal age), particularly where results may inform individualized counselling regarding IVF success probabilities; and (iii) prospectively audit ART outcomes per initiated cycle and per transfer to ensure that improvements in SDF or DSBs indices translate into meaningful clinical gains. Until such audit data are robust and reproducible, clinicians should avoid overinterpreting isolated DSB measurements and continue to contextualize results within the broader clinical picture.

When to test: indications and timing

Before considering SDF testing, clinicians should prioritize basic andrological optimization, including standardization of ejaculation abstinence interval, assessment and treatment of genital tract infection or inflammation when suspected, optimization of metabolic and lifestyle factors, and correction of clinically significant varicocele where indicated (Esteves, 2025). In many men—particularly those with poor semen quality—shorter ejaculation abstinence intervals may reduce post-testicular DNA damage and should be considered prior to SDF testing (Lo Giudice et al., 2024).

Once these foundational measures have been addressed, SDF testing is most informative when applied selectively to clinical scenarios in which elevated SDF is either more prevalent or more likely to affect reproductive outcomes. These include idiopathic or unexplained infertility (Saleh et al., 2002, 2003; Simon et al., 2013; Feijó and Esteves, 2014), RPL (Zidi-Jrah et al., 2016; Carlini et al., 2017; McQueen et al., 2019; Tan et al., 2019; Inversetti et al., 2025), failure to achieve pregnancy after ≥2–3 appropriately conducted IUI cycles with adequate ovarian stimulation and satisfactory post-wash total motile sperm counts (Chen et al., 2019; Sugihara et al., 2020), implantation failure or poor embryo development after ART (Robinson et al., 2012; Zhao et al., 2014; Simon et al., 2017; Deng et al., 2019; Ribas-Maynou et al., 2021; Nielsen et al., 2023), clinical varicocele (Werthman et al., 2008; Zini and Dohle, 2011; Esteves et al., 2015a), advanced paternal age (Evenson et al., 2020; Gonzalez et al., 2022; Behdarvandian et al., 2023), genital tract infection, and systemic oxidative-stress states such as obesity, diabetes, or smoking (Gallegos et al., 2008; Roessner et al., 2012; Dupont et al., 2013; Mulya et al., 2024; Ma and Xi, 2025), exposure to environmental, occupational or recreational toxins (Bian et al., 2004; Krzastek et al., 2020; Kleshchev et al., 2021; Osadchuk et al., 2023), and in couples preparing for IUI or ART when SDF results may influence therapeutic management (O’Neill et al., 2018; Esteves et al., 2021; Lee et al. 2025; Humaidan et al., 2026). In these settings, observational and mechanistic data consistently show that SDF levels are higher than in fertile controls, that elevated SDF may reduce conception and increase miscarriage rates, and that the underlying contributor is amenable to andrological optimization. While most guidelines do not support routine SDF testing during the initial male infertility evaluation, recent updates recognize targeted indications—particularly RPL, unexplained infertility, failed ART, and clinical varicocele—as contexts in which SDF assessment may offer meaningful clinical insight (Bender Atik et al., 2023; Suker et al., 2024; Katz et al., 2025; Minhas et al., 2025). Contemporary guidelines also acknowledge that SDF testing can inform the selective use of testicular sperm for ICSI (Testi-ICSI) in non-azoospermic men with persistently elevated ejaculated SDF, especially after ART failure (Brannigan et al., 2024).

How to act: translating results into management

Elevated SDF should be interpreted as a marker of impaired spermatogenesis—most commonly driven by oxidative stress and/or defective chromatin remodeling—and its detection may warrant targeted andrological optimization whenever feasible before proceeding with ART (Esteves et al., 2020). In practice, this means identifying and treating modifiable contributors and repeating SDF testing approximately one spermatogenic cycle (∼10–12 weeks) after the initial test, allowing sufficient time for biological effects on newly produced spermatozoa to be observed.

Human studies show that lifestyle modification (Humaidan et al., 2022), antioxidant therapy (Gil-Villa et al., 2009; Nazari et al., 2020; Noegroho et al., 2022), weight reduction (Mir et al., 2018; Peel et al., 2026; Pereira et al., 2025; Santi et al., 2025), treatment of genital tract infection (Gallegos et al., 2008), varicocele repair (Lira Neto et al., 2021), and—in selected phenotypes—FSH therapy (Simoni et al., 2016; Santi et al., 2018) reduce SDF by ∼20–40%. These improvements are consistently observed across clinical contexts, including varicocele, metabolic syndrome, genital infection, and idiopathic infertility.

A note of caution is warranted concerning antioxidant therapy. The recently released WHO infertility guideline highlights the uncertainty surrounding its use in male infertility (Esteves et al., 2026; Mburu et al., 2026). Neutral or inconsistent reproductive outcomes may arise when antioxidant supplementation is applied in the absence of biological targeting, baseline confirmation of an underlying oxidative or redox-related disturbance, or assessment of validated biomarkers to demonstrate target engagement (de Ligny et al., 2025). Accordingly, antioxidant therapy should not be prescribed reflexively in response to an abnormal SDF result. A more defensible approach is mechanism-led and dose-aware, incorporating biomarker-informed selection and monitoring of treatment response (Moazamian et al., 2025). Future trial designs adopting this strategy—often extending supplementation to the couple rather than relying on a male-only intervention—may help clarify whether targeted redox modulation can translate into meaningful improvements in reproductive outcomes.

For each context, the table separates evidence for SDF reduction from evidence for reproductive outcomes and expresses certainty using adapted GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations) terminology. Although few interventions have been tested in controlled trials using reproductive outcomes as primary endpoints, observational cohorts consistently show that men who achieve a meaningful post-treatment reduction in SDF tend to have higher conception rates. This pattern has been described in men treated for infection (Gallegos et al., 2008), varicocele (Smit et al., 2013; Ni et al., 2014), and idiopathic infertility (Gallegos et al., 2008; Smit et al., 2013; Ni et al., 2014; Garolla et al., 2017). While this association does not establish causality, they suggest that SDF responsiveness may serve as an independent prognostic marker of improved sperm functional competence. Biologically, lowering the burden of DNA damage reduces the repair load placed on the oocyte, potentially facilitating fertilization, normal embryo development, and early genome activation.

For several common indications—including advanced paternal age, environmental or occupational exposures, or pre-ART evaluation in first-time ART candidates—direct human evidence linking treatment of the underlying contributor to improved pregnancy or live-birth outcomes remains limited or absent (Esteves et al., 2021). These uncertainties should be made explicit during counseling.

When SDF remains elevated despite appropriate optimization, alternative sperm-selection strategies may be considered to minimize the oocyte’s exposure to damaged paternal DNA. Options include the selective use of Testi-ICSI and the use of laboratory adjuncts (Quinn et al., 2018; Pujol et al., 2022; West et al., 2022; Hsu et al., 2023; Esteves et al., 2023). Such approaches may be particularly relevant for couples with advanced maternal age, in whom diminished oocyte repair capacity increases vulnerability to paternal DNA lesions (Kocur et al., 2023).

Because reproductive outcome data rarely allow attribution to individual SDF-targeted interventions, evidence is interpreted at the level of the clinical context rather than specific treatments (Table 2). This structure underscores that SDF testing should not be used as a screening tool, but rather as an adjunct to comprehensive andrological assessment when results are expected to meaningfully inform counselling or clinical decision-making.

Testi-ICSI in non-azoospermic men with high SDF

In non-azoospermic men, elevated SDF is frequently driven by post-testicular oxidative mechanisms. Consequently, identification and treatment of reversible contributors—such as infection, inflammation, varicocele, metabolic dysfunction, and lifestyle factors—should precede consideration of testicular sperm use. Testi-ICSI is therefore not proposed as a substitute for andrological optimization, but as a selective option for couples embarking on ART in whom SDF remains persistently high despite appropriate intervention.

Testicular spermatozoa are mainly protected from post-testicular oxidative stress and therefore generally exhibit lower SDF than ejaculated sperm (Esteves et al., 2015b; Xie et al., 2020). This biological advantage underpins the rationale for Testi-ICSI in non-azoospermic men whose ejaculated SDF remains high (>30%) despite appropriate andrological optimization (Muratori et al., 2015; Xie et al., 2020; Esteves, 2026). Although randomized controlled trials are lacking, observational studies and meta-analyses consistently report lower miscarriage rates and, in some analyses, higher live birth rates in carefully selected high-SDF phenotypes (Greco et al., 2005; Mehta et al., 2015; Esteves et al., 2015b, 2017; Bradley et al., 2016; Pabuccu et al., 2017; Arafa et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2019; Zhao et al., 2023; Khoo et al., 2024). However, the evidence base remains observational mainly, with methodological heterogeneity, variable SDF thresholds, and modest sample sizes. As such, these findings should be interpreted as low-certainty evidence and discussed transparently with couples.

Importantly, testicular sperm retrieval—either testicular sperm aspiration (TESA) or testicular sperm extraction (TESE), including its microsurgical variant (micro-TESE)—in non-azoospermic males is associated with a low complication rate (Esteves and Roque, 2019; Esteves 2026). Counseling should emphasize that Testi-ICSI is a selective option, currently supported by the joint guideline of the American Urological Association/American Society for Reproductive Medicine (AUA/ASRM) as a clinical-principle statement rather than a universal recommendation (Brannigan et al., 2024). Informed consent should address procedural risks, the possibility of retrieval failure, the need for backup ejaculated sperm, and the still-limited evidence base. Until adequately powered randomized trials are available, Testi-ICSI should be offered to carefully selected couples with persistently high SDF and a clear biological rationale after comprehensive counselling (Esteves, 2026).

Laboratory adjuncts

Laboratory-based sperm selection strategies provide additional means to reduce the oocyte’s exposure to damaged sperm DNA, especially when SDF remains elevated after andrological optimization.

Short ejaculatory abstinence is the simplest approach. Intervals ≤2 days appear to reduce oxidative burden and yield sperm populations with decreased SDF (Lo Giudice et al., 2024). Meta-analytic data indicate modest but consistent reductions in SDF levels and potential improvement in cumulative pregnancy and live-birth rates with this approach (Du et al., 2024). However, reproductive benefits have not been specifically confirmed in high-SDF cohorts (Sørensen et al., 2023; Du et al., 2024).

A hyaluronic acid (HA)-based sperm selection technique was evaluated in a large multicenter randomized controlled trial involving couples undergoing ICSI (West et al., 2022). Although overall live-birth rates were unchanged, HA selection significantly reduced miscarriage, and mechanistic analyses confirmed that HA-bound sperm exhibited lower SDF levels. Subgroup analyses suggest greater benefit in couples with poorer baseline sperm DNA quality or advanced maternal age—supporting a phenotype-specific value.

Microfluidic sperm sorting is supported by meta-analytic evidence demonstrating reductions in SDF and improvements in some per-embryo transfer outcomes. However, consistent improvements in live-birth rates have not been shown, most studies lack enrichment for high-SDF phenotypes, and direct comparisons with Testi-ICSI are virtually absent (Gisbert Iranzo et al., 2025). Evidence from other non-centrifugation-based devices remains limited, and device heterogeneity and protocol variability further limit generalizability.

At present, laboratory strategies may be most appropriately applied to men with intermediate SDF levels (∼20–29%), particularly first-time ART candidates. In contrast, testicular sperm may be considered when SDF remains high (e.g. >30%) despite an effort to optimize it and a plausible rationale exists.

Integrating findings into clinical practice

A practical, stepwise approach—summarized in Fig. 1—incorporates SDF results into decision-making by aligning paternal phenotype, maternal age, and the quality and strength of the available evidence. Crucially, counselling should transparently outline where evidence is more robust but not conclusive (e.g. SDF reduction after varicocele repair, Testi-ICSI), where signals are promising yet incomplete (e.g. centrifugation-free sperm selection, HA selection, short abstinence), and where evidence remains insufficient to confirm benefit. This ensures patient-centered decision-making while avoiding over-interpretation of existing data.

Beyond fertility: implications for offspring health

Although the primary focus of SDF research has been on fertility, mechanistic and emerging human data suggest potential consequences for the health of the offspring. Elevated paternal DNA damage—frequently linked to advanced paternal age (Evenson et al., 2020; Behdarvandian et al., 2023)—has been associated with higher de novo mutation rates, altered epigenetic imprinting, and increased risk of adverse obstetric and neurodevelopmental outcomes (Kong et al., 2012; Maher et al., 2018; Bertoncelli Tanaka et al., 2019; Aitken, 2022; Ashapkin et al., 2023; Stenqvist et al., 2025). It is important to distinguish between de novo mutations arising within the male germ line—largely driven by cumulative cell divisions, replication errors, and age-related dysregulation during spermatogenesis—and genetic or epigenetic alterations that may arise post-fertilization when the oocyte’s DNA repair capacity is limited. Interventions aimed at reducing SDF are therefore not expected to mitigate the germline mutation burden associated with advanced paternal age but may reduce oxidative DNA lesions that place additional demands on embryonic repair mechanisms.

From this perspective, the reported associations between paternal DNA damage and offspring outcomes should be interpreted as reflecting overlapping but biologically distinct pathways. Although these associations remain observational, they are consistent with experimental data showing that unresolved DNA lesions and epigenetic disturbances in the paternal germ line can influence developmental trajectories across generations, even in the absence of new germline mutations (Gao et al., 2019; Xavier et al., 2019; Zweifel and Woodward, 2022; Aitken, 2023).

These converging data argue for a broader view of male reproductive health: one that extends beyond achieving pregnancy to safeguarding the integrity of the paternal genome before conception. Sperm chromatin integrity is a critical determinant of offspring health, yet SDF testing remains absent from most preconception guidelines. When used selectively, SDF assessment provides the most direct available measure of the structural integrity of the paternal genome. It may help identify men who could benefit from targeted interventions to improve sperm DNA quality before attempting natural conception or ART.

Integrating SDF assessment into a comprehensive andrological approach to preconception care aligns with preventive principles in reproductive medicine. Further studies are required to determine whether preconception interventions that reduce SDF translate into measurable improvements in offspring outcomes.

Follow-up, audit, and research priorities

Structured follow-up and audit are essential for the responsible integration of SDF testing into clinical practice. Rather than functioning as an isolated assay, SDF assessment should be embedded within an evidence-based andrology framework that monitors patient selection, laboratory performance, and downstream treatment outcomes (Bento et al., 2025). Clinics should document the indication for testing, quantify post-intervention changes in SDF, and evaluate how results influence management decisions. Transparent reporting of sperm retrieval success, complication rates, and live-birth outcomes by sperm source enhances clinical governance and supports continuous improvement. For centers incorporating DSB-enriched assays, routine monitoring of assay precision and outcome correlations is a prerequisite before embedding such platforms into routine decision-making.

Several research gaps remain pressing (Barratt et al., 2010; De Jonge et al., 2024). Future studies should prioritize the validation of oxidative stress and redox biomarkers with established links to SDF that can guide the targeted use and monitoring of antioxidant therapy and demonstrate clear associations with reproductive outcomes. Randomized controlled trials comparing testicular versus ejaculated sperm for ICSI in high-SDF populations are also a top priority, as are comparative studies evaluating emerging sperm-selection technologies against testicular retrieval approaches, particularly in men with elevated DSB burdens. Equally important is the clinical validation of DSB-specific assays against reproductive and offspring outcomes, which will be essential to determine their prognostic value. Long-term cohort studies examining the genomic and epigenetic health of offspring conceived from high-SDF gametes are needed to clarify intergenerational implications.

Beyond ART settings, prospective studies should explore whether preconception interventions that lower SDF may also improve pregnancy and offspring health. Extensive, multidisciplinary collaborations across andrology, reproductive medicine, obstetrics, and pediatrics will be critical to define the full contribution of paternal genomic integrity to intergenerational health and to inform future preventive strategies.

Conclusions and future perspectives

SDF testing is no longer an experimental tool but a clinically useful adjunct when applied judiciously. The Aitken-De Iuliis two-step hypothesis provides a coherent biological model linking nuclear maturation defects to oxidative stress and contextualizes why reducing SDF can improve reproductive potential. The emerging focus on DSBs refines this further by highlighting the lesions most detrimental to fertilization, embryo development, and pregnancy success. A practical approach includes using a validated assay, correcting reversible conditions, retest after one spermatogenic cycle, interpreting results alongside female age, and considering Testi-ICSI or laboratory adjuncts for couples undergoing ART with persistently high SDF, with transparent counselling on evidence limitations (Fig. 1). Beyond ART, sperm DNA integrity is relevant to preconception care; integrating SDF testing into quality-management programs can help mitigate risks and encourage preventive strategies that extend to offspring’s health. Key knowledge gaps remain—particularly the need to validate DSB-specific assays, conduct comparative trials of microfluidics versus Testi-ICSI in high-damage phenotypes, and conduct long-term offspring follow-up. Addressing these gaps will be critical for moving from mechanistic plausibility to outcome-driven guidance. Ultimately, the goal is precision rather than proliferation of testing—using molecular insight to support better reproductive and intergenerational outcomes through a truly couple-centered approach.
