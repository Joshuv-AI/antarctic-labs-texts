Healthcare Professionals' Perceptions of Digital Counselling: A Qualitative Descriptive Study
Authors: Juulia Kaihlaniemi, Maria Kääriäinen, Pia Liljamo, Anna‐Maria Tuomikoski, Anne Oikarinen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThis study aims to describe healthcare professionals' perceptions of digital counselling, addressing a significant research gap in existing literature. As digital services become more common in healthcare, digital counselling has become part of healthcare professionals' work. Counselling is carried out using new methods, such as text‐based or video‐mediated communication, which are transforming the role of healthcare professionals.MethodsIn this qualitative descriptive study, healthcare professionals (n = 12) working in Digital Care Pathways were recruited for semi‐structured interviews using convenience sampling. Data were analysed using inductive content analysis.ResultsFive main categories were identified: facilitating the use of digital counselling applications, involvement of the patient in care, considering the patient's situation holistically, aiming for reciprocal understanding, and decision‐making related to care.ConclusionsDigital counselling enables text‐based or video‐mediated interactive applications, thus involving patients in their self‐care. It contains the same elements as traditional counselling, including consideration of the patient's needs, goal orientation, and interactivity, but it has its own characteristics.

Introduction

One of the main goals of the World Health Organisation's Global Digital Health Strategy is to support people‐centred healthcare systems based on health‐promoting digital services [1]. In healthcare, digital services have primarily been used to connect with rural areas and deliver medical care with satisfying results [2]. Digital services have shown a generally positive effect on population health across medical specialties and have been proven to help reduce healthcare costs and resource use [3].

Digital counselling is a relevant digital service to HCPs (healthcare professionals). In this study, HCPs refer to professional groups in nursing or rehabilitation, such as nurses, physiotherapists, and occupational therapists. They play a significant role in providing self‐care counselling to patients in digital environments and in counselling patients on the use of digital services [4, 5]. However, research on digital counselling from the perspective of HCPs is currently scarce. To develop digital counselling practices and strengthen HCPs' competence in digital counselling, more knowledge is needed about HCPs' perceptions of digital counselling.

Background

Digital counselling refers to counselling delivered via text‐based or video‐mediated interactive applications. Digital counselling is defined as an interactive, goal‐oriented action, linked to the context of the patient and the HCPs involved [6]. At its best, digital counselling is firmly patient‐centred, carefully planned based on the patient's knowledge and physical and psychosocial needs, and implemented with sufficient resources. Interaction is fundamental because a successful interactive counselling relationship is the key to appropriate action and the achievement of objectives [7, 8]. When counselling is conducted digitally, the interaction between the participating parties changes, unavoidably affecting the working practices of HCPs in different ways [9, 10, 11].

HCPs have mainly experienced digital counselling as a positive part of their work [4], highlighting benefits such as convenience, increased access to care, flexible delivery, enhanced productivity, better organisation, and opportunities to build confidence with virtual platforms [9, 10]. Moreover, multidisciplinary collaboration in digital counselling has helped HCPs to form a holistic view of the patient's care [10]. HCPs have recognised challenges related to digital counselling. The absence of physical contact challenges physical assessments [9], but it also encourages patients to express their concerns more actively, promoting engagement in their care [10]. The challenges related to interaction have been identified, for example, establishing a rapport [9], being present in the interaction situation [4], and meeting the patient emotionally [5, 11, 12]. Implementing digital counselling requires critical evaluation of patients' needs and, consequently, introduces new responsibilities for HCPs [4, 5, 13]. Factors like age [4, 10] or mental illness [13] can hinder participation. Thus, vulnerable patients may need support from their social network and HCPs to avoid digital exclusion [13].

Digital counselling requires versatile competencies from HCPs, including effective patient interaction [5, 9, 11, 14] and combining digital means with traditional methods [4]. Even though it is suitable for follow‐up visits, some situations require traditional counselling [4]. Strong clinical competence [4] and problem‐solving skills are essential to integrate clinical judgement with technology‐based decisions [15]. Previous research has not sufficiently discussed, from the perspective of HCPs, how the lack of physical contact affects the interaction and emotional presence in digital counselling. This is important to understand, as it ensures that digital counselling is patient‐centred. This study aims to describe HCPs' perceptions of digital counselling. The research question was: How do HCPs perceive digital counselling?

Methods

Design

The descriptive qualitative approach was adopted to provide a comprehensive summary of HCPs' perspectives on digital counselling. This approach is particularly suitable when prior research is limited, and the goal is to provide a rich, straightforward description of an understudied phenomenom without imposing a predetermined theoretical framework [16]. The study was planned and reported using the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research Checklist (COREQ) [17] (Supporting Information S1).

Study Setting and Recruitment

Five Finnish university hospitals have developed a Health Village patient portal, which includes Digital Care Pathways (DCPs). DCPs involve professionals from various disciplines, including HCPs and physicians. DCPs are secure communication channels between patients and professionals. Interaction happens either asynchronously via text or synchronously via video. DCPs provide instructions about various treatment phases and information on self‐care and rehabilitation programmes [18].

The study was conducted in a university hospital, in departments where DCPs were implemented. Participants were HCPs, for example, nurses, midwives, physiotherapists, and occupational therapists. The participants were recruited through convenience and snowball sampling methods [16]. HCPs received an email about the study and replied if they wished to participate. The first author informed the HCPs, who were the primary users of the DCPs, about the study during their remote meeting. HCPs who agreed to take part were asked to inform their colleagues about the study and the opportunity to participate. Participants provided informed consent by signing a consent form.

To ensure participants had appropriate knowledge and experience [19], the inclusion criteria required HCPs to have experience with digital counselling at least during the piloting phase of the DCP, and to be full‐ or part‐time HCPs, and working in a hospital setting. The exclusion criteria were as follows: not working as a HCP (e.g., administrative or non‐clinical roles), working outside a hospital setting, having no experience with digital counselling, and inability or refusal to provide informed consent.

Data Collection

The first author conducted the semi‐structured interviews (n = 12) between February and March 2021. The interviews were conducted remotely in Teams (digital platform; Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA). The interview guide (Table 1) was based on previous research and aligned with the research question. It covered the following themes: (1) counselling competencies in the digital environment; (2) patient orientation in digital counselling; (3) communication skills; (4) technological skills; (5) competence in information management; (6) ethical knowledge; and (7) competence in developing a DCP. The interview guide was pilot tested with three HCPs, and duplicate questions were removed. One pilot interview was included in the data [16]. To minimise disturbances, the first author conducted the interviews in a quiet, uninterrupted setting, and participants were advised to do the same at their workplaces. The interviews were audio‐recorded, and participants chose whether to use their cameras. Data saturation occurred after the eleventh interview, indicating that any new information would be expected from additional interviews [19]. The interviews lasted an average of 47 min, ranging from 33 to 84 min.

Data Analysis

Data were analysed using inductive content analysis, which enables categories to emerge directly from the data. This approach is essential when existing conceptualisations are fragmented, and no established theory sufficiently explains the phenomenon. The implementation of semi‐structured interviews rendered inductive content analysis suitable [19]. This design allowed us to build new insights into how HCPs perceive digital counselling in their clinical practice.

The first author analysed the data in collaboration with the research team (JK, AO, MK). Audio‐recorded data were transcribed verbatim by the first author. The transcription process generated 94 pages of text (Times New Roman, font size 12, line spacing 1). Then, in the preparation phase, the first author read the written material several times to familiarise herself with the data. The research question guided the coding process. The units of analysis, which were themes that answered the research question, were extracted from the data and classified as open codes (n = 936). During categorisation, open codes were grouped into 89 sub‐categories based on their similarities and named using content‐characteristic concepts. Sub‐categories were grouped by content into 15 categories, each with a descriptive concept. The abstraction process continued until the five main categories describing HCPs' perceptions of digital counselling were identified. An example of the content analysis process has been described in Table 2 [20, 21].

Ethical Considerations

The guidelines of the Helsinki Declaration were followed at different stages of the study [22]. Permission for the study was sought in accordance with the hospital district's practices. Research Ethics Committee approval was not required according to Finnish legislation, as the study did not involve minors, harm to participants, or clinical trials [23]. The participants were informed about the objectives, methods, and data handling, and were told that participation was voluntary. The participants' anonymity was ensured in the study report [24]. The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation was followed when processing the data. The anonymised data were saved on a password‐protected file for the duration of the study [25]. Language editing was supported by Microsoft Copilot and Grammarly (version 1.2) to improve clarity and grammar without altering scientific content.

Rigour and Reflexivity

To enhance the rigour of the study and to minimise potential bias, reflexive writing techniques were utilised and systematic, team‐based reflection was carried out at various stages of the research process [26]. During the data collection, the first author worked as a registered nurse and was the main user of one of the DCPs. Although the first author personally knew some participants, she sought to minimise potential subjectivity by conducting the interviews systematically following the interview guide. She also wrote field notes after the interviews. By the analysis phase, the first author had increased her knowledge of digital counselling and qualitative methods used during her PhD studies. The first author documented her preunderstanding and positionalities about digital counselling before the interviews and, as she gained experience, before the data analysis. Furthermore, the first author maintained a research diary throughout the research process. The research team met regularly, particularly during the analysis phase, to openly and confidentially discuss decisions. This allowed the first author to receive support from the other team members, who were experts in qualitative methods and counselling‐related research. These reflexive practices helped ensure transparency and strengthen the credibility of the findings.

Facilitating the Use of Digital Counselling Applications

Evaluating Capabilities and Recruitment to Digital Counselling

Participants reported that patients were recruited to the DCPs either by letter or in face‐to‐face meetings at the clinic. Many participants perceived their role as informing patients about the DCP. Participants knew use of the service was voluntary for patients, and this was considered when evaluating motivation to use the DCP. Most participants mentioned evaluating patients' digital capabilities and the necessary devices. A few of them also considered how a patient's functional capacity affects their use of the DCP, for example, if the patient is unable to type on a computer or use a mouse. Some participants perceived digital counselling as beneficial for families and young people, as they were used to digital services and expressing themselves through writing. Therefore, in the case of minors, guidelines intended for family members also had to be considered when parents managed the DCP on their behalf. One participant illustrated their experience of the recruitment process in the following way:You'll find out when you say we have an option like this. Is the patient interested? You'll find out that now it may be that the patient does not understand what this means, or whether they know how to use it. At that point, you must go to a concrete level and explain by example what kind of things can be handled through this. And then, go through the sign‐up process, the instruction paper, to sort of go through it all together (A7).

At that point, you must go to a concrete level and explain by example what kind of things can be handled through this. Following and Supporting the Use of Digital Counselling Applications

Participants perceived that following the patients' activity in the DCP was essential because, where necessary, patients were given more detailed support on how to use the DCP application. Some participants tried to discover why a patient was not logged in to the DCP. Some of them also attempted to solve problems with the application. Almost all participants perceived that their role as HCP included counselling on the use of the DCP. However, one participant felt the resources were insufficient, even though patients wished for support for using the DCP. Patients were supported in different ways. While some participants encouraged patients to reach out via the DCP in case of any ambiguity at the clinic, others reminded them to complete the questionnaires via text. Thus, supporting patients was perceived as significant in getting them to use the service. For instance, one participant shared her experience of providing counsel to patients on the utilisation of the DCP application during clinical appointments:It will be revealed when he comes to the first appointment; if he hasn't signed up there, the reason will be asked. If necessary, if it's just a case of not having found a place to log in, I have shown the place in the Health Village (A8).

Involvement of the Patient in Care

Empowering Self‐Care and Healthy Lifestyles

Most of the participants highlighted that counselling on disease symptoms was an essential aspect of digital counselling. Patients' symptoms were monitored, for example, with different symptom diaries, which one participant felt would help patients track their symptoms in their everyday lives. Many participants experienced that individual medication counselling was emphasised in digital counselling. It usually concerns medication monitoring, changes, and side effects. Moreover, supporting patients' self‐care was considered crucial to ensure appropriate treatment. Two participants shared that they counselled patients on bladder and bowel functions, wounds and skin care, and sexuality. Some perceived lifestyle counselling as important, for example, counselling on vaccination, dental care, nutrition, exercise, sleep, and substance use. Participants emphasised empowering patients to take responsibility for their own care, as illustrated in the following quotation:I would think that you can give through non‐real‐time remote connection better support and advice for helping things to move forward there, to cope better at home (A1).

I would think that you can give through non‐real‐time remote connection better support and advice for helping things to move forward there, to cope better at home (A1).

Providing Content to Support Self‐Care and Healthy Lifestyles

Participants reflected that DCPs contained counselling material on treating diseases and answers to frequently asked questions. They perceived that the material made it easier for patients to access information. Counselling material was versatile; a few participants described it as delivered in written, verbal, video, and picture formats. Participants perceived that different patient groups and ages were taken into account. For example, DCPs for children contained material expressly for children, young people, and their parents. Furthermore, those involved in developing the DCPs emphasised the importance of ensuring the materials are accessible. For instance, the text should be clear and concise, allowing patients to access it with a screen reader if necessary. According to several participants, patients can rehearse the guided topics through the counselling material. This aspect was discussed by one participant in the following way:There is a lot of that information, so when you are verbally told at the appointment, it's hard [for the patient] to remember it all. Then [the patient] can go there to read and refine the information at their leisure. Take time to go through things (A9).

There is a lot of that information, so when you are verbally told at the appointment, it's hard [for the patient] to remember it all. Then [the patient] can go there to read and refine the information at their leisure. Take time to go through things (A9).

Preparing for Healthcare Professional Meetings

Participants reported that DCPs enable patients to prepare for meetings with HCPs by prompting them to complete preliminary questionnaires. They described pre‐information surveys mapped, for example, disease progression, possible other diseases, medication, and the patient's needs for the appointment. In addition, some participants noted that other surveys, such as a pre‐admission anaesthesia survey and a functional ability meter, helped patients prepare for hospitalisation. Moreover, while some participants believed that digital counselling materials could effectively prepare patients for clinical examinations and surgical interventions, others perceived they enabled patients to prepare for appointments with HCPs. The digital counselling material included information about the hospital visit and preparation instructions. This created a basis for either a remote appointment or a hospital visit. One participant shared the following experience regarding the practicality of the digital counselling material in preparing patients for surgery:Those who have read the preparation instructions from the DCP, about nutrition and the other aspects, have come well prepared. It has gone, just as it should have gone (A4).

Those who have read the preparation instructions from the DCP, about nutrition and the other aspects, have come well prepared. It has gone, just as it should have gone (A4).

Considering the Patient's Situation Holistically

Preparing for Patient Meetings

Finding out the patient's situation was considered crucial in digital counselling. Participants shared, for example, that a patient's home situation, mood, and personal attitudes could be assessed through the messages exchanged. Participants also perceived that the results of the pre‐information surveys were significant because they provided patient‐centred information and enabled the patients' needs to be recognised. Some participants reflected that the transfer of information from the DCP to the patient health record was their responsibility. However, familiarisation with background information drawn from the patient health record was perceived as essential to ensuring patient‐centricity. It is worth noting that asynchronous communication enables the clarification of a patient's affairs before responding to the question, for example:You can search for information and think about how to formulate the answer to that question. And then search for it [information] and ask the colleague how this thing went and how to answer this question (A10).

You can search for information and think about how to formulate the answer to that question. And then search for it [information] and ask the colleague how this thing went and how to answer this question (A10).

Identification of Individual Counselling Needs

The participants agreed that identifying individual needs was the key to successful patient‐centred counselling. They emphasised determining patients' wishes either through their questions or directly asking what they want to know. The patient's level of knowledge was clarified through probing questions that targeted their existing understanding and personal context. Some participants highlighted that identifying the patient's capabilities was necessary for evaluating their level of internalisation and ensuring individual attention and counselling. As one participant noted, patient questions are of particular significance in identifying counselling needs when counselling is provided through text‐based communication:Probably not about anything else [identifying counselling needs] than if they ask questions, and we are always encouraged to be in touch … But I guess not, if there is a patient who doesn't ask anything, then probably nowhere at all (A11).

Probably not about anything else [identifying counselling needs] than if they ask questions, and we are always encouraged to be in touch … But I guess not, if there is a patient who doesn't ask anything, then probably nowhere at all (A11).

Solution‐Focused Activity

Participants reflected that digital counselling was a solution‐focused activity in which the patient's needs were considered, and the same subjects were not discussed with everyone. Participants agreed that they managed the patient's affairs and aimed to meet their needs. For example, one participant expressed the view that it was important to assess what kind of advice the patient needed. Another participant stated that they sought the information prior to the video‐mediated counselling, and the information communicated to different patients may vary. Solutions were considered together with the patient; for example, some participants involved reflecting on issues related to patients' needs, as well as on different approaches, solutions, and possibilities for change. Some participants found information sharing meaningful; for instance, sending individualised links via the DCP was described as easy and quick. They also considered it important to provide concrete instructions, such as assisting patients and offering advice and counselling to help them cope with different situations and issues. This aspect was described by one of the participants as follows:Even though usually there is some other way of doing things, for example, giving the anti‐seizure medicine. For their children, the seizure medication is given more frequently for this and that reason (A6).

Even though usually there is some other way of doing things, for example, giving the anti‐seizure medicine. For their children, the seizure medication is given more frequently for this and that reason (A6).

Aiming for Reciprocal Understanding

Meeting Synchronously or Asynchronously

Participants agreed that communication in digital counselling was conducted either via video‐mediated meetings or through messaging. Only a few participants had experience of real‐time meetings in video‐mediated counselling, but its implementation in the not‐too‐distant future was hoped for. They felt that video‐mediated counselling was comparable to traditional, face‐to‐face counselling because it allowed the patient to be observed and listened to in the moment. In text‐based counselling, patients targeted questions to participants, who responded asynchronously. The delay in responses was confusing, but it also allowed responses at the most convenient moment. Some participants noted that ready‐made phrases could be utilised in text‐based counselling, as one of the participants described:We have thought together how to get it more smoothly and could use some of these ready‐made phrases for similar things. What is perceived to be well articulated (A8).

We have thought together how to get it more smoothly and could use some of these ready‐made phrases for similar things. What is perceived to be well articulated (A8).

Confronting Emphatically

Participants considered empathy crucial, but some found it challenging when patients were frustrated or sent aggressive messages. They recognised that raising challenging questions when patients discussed their discomfort, problems, or distress was necessary. Most participants valued open, confidential, and honest interaction that respected patients and their affairs. Consequently, some emphasised that providing a personal answer to patients' questions is essential in text‐based counselling. On the other hand, one participant perceived that voice pressure, tone, and timbre were significant in video‐mediated counselling because these elements are crucial for conveying empathy. Participants agreed that showing empathy helped patients cope with their illness in everyday life. Most participants felt this was facilitated by motivational communication, while others emphasised listening to the patient, and some highlighted confronting with compassion. One participant illustrated her perceptions in the following way:When you're there alone, and it's difficult, at home or wherever you are, it can be that these encounters of ours are such that they give the strength to cope in the future (A2).

Clear Language and Comprehensible Interaction

Participants emphasised the need for clarity, conciseness, and comprehensibility when conveying information in digital counselling. Participants reported being urged to communicate with patients in an understandable manner and to present matters clearly from the patient's perspective. Concise was considered important in text‐based counselling, yet one participant found it challenging. Participants attempted to formulate responses to messages in the most efficient, detailed, and complete manner possible, thereby ensuring consistent responses to patients. Ensuring the patient's understanding was also perceived as essential. Some participants considered asking additional questions useful to ensure that guidance was understood, as one of the participants described:This verification that if the instructions were clear or you have any questions, these are good questions to ask if counselling is given, for example, in medication. And ensuring that the message has been understood correctly (A3).

Decision‐Making Related to Care

Assessment of Counselling Needs in Care Process

Most participants reported that their tasks included evaluating the information sent to the DCPs, which required professional medical skills when transferring it to the patient information system. Several participants pointed out that it was crucial to intervene in care‐related issues. They perceived that evaluating the need for follow‐up care, such as a physician's appointment, a nurse's home visit, or symptom monitoring, was critical in acute situations. However, some participants considered digital counselling a suitable approach for follow‐up care, such as blood tests or appointments. Moreover, participants directed patients to suitable social and health services, such as mental health services or peer support, when necessary. One participant highlighted the professional skills required to evaluate patient information:When I transfer information to the anaesthesia system, I must have professional skills … I transfer the information, I can react to that, what I need to deal with, what I need to do about that, or find out more if the children have some illnesses or any health‐related stuff (A5).

Identifying Challenges Related to Digital Counselling

Participants identified some challenges related to digital counselling that influenced their decision‐making. A few participants found it difficult that facial expressions and gestures are absent in text‐based counselling. Even in video‐mediated counselling, observing facial expressions is not always possible when a visual connection is unavailable. Moreover, confirming the identity of the person they were communicating with in text‐based counselling was essential but challenging, especially when the patient was a minor. Therefore, with intimate, broad, and challenging questions, some participants assessed the need for a telephone connection instead of text‐based counselling. One participant felt that having a phone connection provided a sense of safety in the interaction:The time spent answering and the length of the text are very limited in the DCP. It must be restricted; you can't start writing novels in response. But if there are things like that, you can contact [a patient] by phone. I would think that it brings security to communication (A2).

Participating in Multidisciplinary Co‐Operation

According to the participants, their professional skills were essential in digital counselling. Participants perceived that they counselled patients in line with their professional role, which remained unchanged from before. Nevertheless, they considered it necessary to identify the competence of other professionals. In unclear situations, participants usually consulted their colleagues, but they also forwarded patients' questions to other professional groups when needed. Participants reflected that nurses, physicians, rehabilitation counsellors, physiotherapists, and occupational therapists answered patients' questions in their areas of expertise. Physicians were consulted on questions participants found challenging, including those related to disease treatment, such as discussing symptoms, medication, appropriate painkiller dosage, or prescription renewal. It is worth noting that multidisciplinary co‐operation was perceived as beneficial in video‐mediated counselling, as one participant describes:The patient could be with them [local healthcare team] there, and we could be here, and we would talk and think about how to act and do things. At the same time, it would also make the patient responsible for himself and give information about the disease to those who care for them daily (A12).

Using the Available Information

Most participants emphasised that the counselling material provided through DCPs was a valuable tool for delivering digital counselling. In addition, some participants searched for information on the Health Village portal, while others retrieved different care recommendations in medicine and nursing. Information was also sought from other sources that participants found to be reliable, for example, various well‐known medical databases. Some participants emphasised that the use of contemporary, reliable information is crucial. Participants reflected that consistent instructions from the DCPs enabled uniform functionality. Using digital counselling materials ensured consistency and coherence throughout the counselling process. For instance, one of the participants shared her thoughts about how the digital counselling material clarifies the counselling:It might bring some kind of structure to it … somehow clarify the counselling. Maybe, even harmonising, what we as nurses counsel families to do (A6).

Discussion

This study contributes to research on digital counselling by showing that, from HCPs' perspectives, the principles of patient‐centred care—such as supporting patient involvement, considering patients' situations holistically, fostering reciprocal understanding, and enabling shared decision‐making—remain essential even when counselling is delivered in digital environments. Thus, digital counselling encompasses the key features of traditional counselling, including interaction, goal‐orientation, and patient‐centredness [6, 7, 8]. The results suggest that digital counselling has reshaped HCPs' roles by adding responsibilities, such as facilitating patients' use of digital counselling applications, to ensure appropriate care.

The first main finding indicates that HCPs were responsible for facilitating patients to use digital counselling applications. When assessing a patient's suitability for digital counselling, prior research [27] highlights the importance of their ability to access, evaluate, process, and apply web‐based health information, that is, digital health literacy. This competence is a central determinant of a patient's readiness to adopt and benefit from digital health solutions [27]. Patients' digital health literacy is influenced by their education and socioeconomic status, but it can be improved by providing social support [28], which emerged in the results of this study. HCPs reported that encouraging patients to use digital counselling applications at various stages of care enhanced their motivation to engage with the service. As shown in previous studies [14, 27], HCPs play a crucial role in promoting patients' involvement in digital counselling.

In line with previous studies [14, 15], the second finding revealed that digital counselling offers opportunities for patients to be involved in their own care. Counselling material was available to support self‐care, enabling patients to familiarise themselves with the relevant issues. This may strengthen patients' ability to seek and apply credible health information and to tackle health concerns more effectively [29]. While preparatory counselling material can reduce the anxiety of children before a hospital visit [30, 31], the interaction between families and HCPs in a digital environment is crucial [32]. This study complements existing knowledge by showing that digital materials and questionnaires alone are not always sufficient from the perspective of HCPs. Interactivity remains essential to ensure patient involvement in their care, even when counselling is provided digitally. This aligns with traditional counselling, which emphasises the interaction in engaging patients in their care [6, 7, 8].

The third main finding of this study indicated that HCPs aspire to a reciprocal understanding with patients. This finding confirms the existing understanding of counselling as an interactive process [6, 7, 8]. This study provided new insights from the HCPs' perspective on how empathetic and motivational interactions are emphasised in digital counselling. Building trust is one of the most important elements influencing the transparency and effectiveness of communication in digital environments [14, 29]. On the other hand, technology has created interactional challenges, especially in text‐based counselling, where misunderstandings may arise. These can be reduced by using precise language, asking follow‐up questions, and confirming patient understanding. Recent research consistently highlights the role of reciprocal communication in building shared understanding and supporting empathetic interactions between patients and HCPs [11, 14].

The fourth main finding concerns decision‐making related to care. According to the previous studies, HCPs are responsible for appropriate care delivery and decision‐making [14, 15], and collaboration among all professionals is key to ensuring quality in digital counselling [33]. The findings of this study emphasised that to ensure patient safety, it is necessary to recognise the difference between providing care and giving advice. Thus, HCPs placed a high value on awareness of their own roles and competence. They also highlighted the importance of recognising others' expertise to support effective multidisciplinary collaboration. Similar findings were reported in previous studies [15, 29]. Consequently, the study indicates that efficient multidisciplinary co‐operation and reliable information are essential factors in care‐related decision‐making.

The fifth main finding highlighted the importance of considering the patient's situation holistically. HCPs are responsible for coordinating care to deliver a continuous, integrated process [15]. Digital counselling created new ways of achieving this. HCPs aim to involve patients and consider their situation holistically. Despite the differences in interaction, a holistic approach remained essential for effective communication. Patients' counselling needs were determined by evaluating their overall needs and identifying their information needs. In fact, the elements that define counselling, that is, considering the patient's needs, goal orientation, and interactivity [6, 7, 8], were all present in the results of this study. Thus, as previous research on counselling has shown [34, 35], the patient can achieve goals through a successful interactive counselling relationship, even when counselling is delivered digitally.

Strengths and Limitations

The study's trustworthiness was evaluated based on its credibility, dependability, confirmability, authenticity, and transferability [36]. The study's credibility was enhanced through several means: the first author's and participants' experience with digital counselling, pilot testing of the interview guide, achieving data saturation during collection and choosing a theme representing HCPs' perceptions of digital counselling as the unit of analysis. Dialogue within the research group in different phases of the analysis increased dependability. Analytical processes have been presented in detail in the reporting of results to strengthen credibility and dependability. Moreover, authenticity was confirmed through citations from different participants. Transferability was enhanced by describing the sampling methods, the inclusion criteria, and the participants' demographic characteristics. The first author enhanced confirmability by keeping a research diary and writing field notes after the interviews [37].

The study had some limitations. Although the sample was small, the data reached saturation, and the analysis could be carried out reliably. Most participants were registered nurses, and they were encouraged to inform their colleagues about the study. Using snowball sampling may have influenced the diversity of participants and led to the underrepresentation of other professional groups. This may cause bias, as participants may share common characteristics or views, potentially narrowing the comprehensiveness of the findings [16]. Although their experience was mainly in text‐based digital counselling, reflections on the implementation of video‐mediated digital counselling and its significance did emerge from the data. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings, as they may constrain the transferability of results to other contexts and professional groups [37].

Implications for Practice and Research

This study will provide managers and leaders with insights into digital counselling and the requirements it places on HCPs. Digital counselling requires a clear allocation of tasks, an integrated approach to care pathways, and consistent criteria for assessing patient suitability and situations where face‐to‐face contact is necessary. Training should be properly targeted. Therefore, it is important to regularly assess the HCPs' digital counselling competence. According to this study, training in assessing patient suitability for digital counselling is needed. Further training is also required for interaction in digital environments, particularly regarding empathetic encounters and patient motivation. The study advances the field of caring science research by emphasising the importance of interaction, goal‐orientation, and patient‐centredness in digital counselling. Thus, the study offers new insights that can guide future research on how digital counselling supports ethically grounded, patient‐centred caring practices. For example, the development of a tool to assess HCPs' digital counselling competence has significant potential. This would facilitate the evaluation of the effectiveness of various interventions and the conducting of longitudinal studies to determine the development of digital counselling competencies over time and their impact on patient outcomes. Furthermore, there is a need to examine the experiences of different professional groups regarding digital counselling, as well as to implement multidisciplinary cooperation.

Conclusion

At its best, digital counselling allows patients to actively participate in managing their self‐care using interactive tools and support materials. It is tailored to patients' needs, promoting their involvement in self‐care through discussions and practical advice. The favoured approach emphasised empathy, clear communication, and ensuring patients' comprehension. However, it is essential to recognise that digital counselling may not be appropriate for every situation. Careful assessment of each patient's needs, consultation with other professionals, and the use of available information are essential. Additionally, identifying patients who may require practical support with digital counselling and providing the option of face‐to‐face counselling when necessary are crucial.
