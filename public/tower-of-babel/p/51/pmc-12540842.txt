Significant sensitivity of global vegetation productivity to terrestrial surface wind speed changes
Authors: Haohao Wu, Congsheng Fu, Lingling Zhang, Zelalem A. Mekonnen, Qing Zhu, Kailiang Yu, Philippe Ciais, Jianyao Chen, Dagang Wang, Huawu Wu, Guishan Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Decadal changes in terrestrial surface wind speed since the 1960s have been extensively documented, while the mechanism and the extent of their impacts on terrestrial ecosystem productivity remain unclear. Here, we systematically explore the impacts of wind speed changes on terrestrial gross primary production (GPP) using satellite-derived data, reanalysis datasets, flux tower observations, CMIP6 models, and exploratory simulation experiments. Our results show a negative sensitivity of terrestrial GPP to wind speed change, ranging from −156.67 to −65.82 g C m−2 yr−1 (m s−1)−1, across different data sources from 1983 to 2100. This is mainly attributed to the impacts of wind speed decline on stomatal conductance by reducing atmospheric dryness and soil drying. We find that during 1983 – 2010, wind speed decline is the most important factor, after rising atmospheric CO2 concentrations, in its contribution to the increasing trend in GPP (6.0% –7.8%). With further declines in wind speed, this contribution to GPP is projected to rank between second and third during 2031 – 2100. Among seven plant functional types, grasslands contribute most to the wind-induced changes in the GPP trend under current and future climates. These findings highlight the substantial effects of wind speed on centennial-scale global carbon dynamics.

Introduction

Wind exerts significant influences on the structure and functioning of terrestrial ecosystems1, with some effects comparable in magnitude to those from changes in temperature and water availability2. The global land surface wind speed has declined by 8% from 1960s to 2000s, a phenomenon commonly known as “terrestrial stilling” in previous studies2,3. Notably, this decline is particularly pronounced in the Northern Hemisphere3. Although increasing wind speeds were observed after 20104–6, terrestrial surface wind speed is projected to continue declining in the 21st century, starting around 20307,8. Decline in terrestrial wind speed has been reported to stimulate vegetation growth9, extend the timing of foliar senescence2, and significantly affect plant transpiration and carbon uptake10. For instance, wind speed was shown to be a primary driver of net ecosystem carbon balance in semi-arid and dry ecosystems globally11,12. Over the past decades, global terrestrial ecosystems have been a strong carbon sink from enhanced rate of carbon fixation13,14 driven by factors such as increasing air temperature14, rising atmospheric CO2 concentrations15, nitrogen deposition16, and large-scale climate variations (e.g., El Niño–Southern Oscillation)17–19. Due to the complexity of the impacts of wind speed change on plant photosynthesis, to date, it remains highly uncertain whether and how the wind speed changes, besides the factors mentioned above, contributes to enhanced photosynthesis rate of global terrestrial ecosystems.

Variations in wind speed are expected to influence photosynthesis through multiple biophysical mechanisms. Specifically, a decline in wind speed would increase boundary layer resistance3 and impede exchange of CO2 between plant and atmosphere, thus reducing the rate of photosynthesis. On the other hand, a decline in wind speed was also reported to reduce evaporation rate20 and mitigate water stress, thus promoting the rate of photosynthesis. Moreover, lower wind speed may increase air humidity and reduce vapor pressure deficit (VPD), which could enhance stomatal conductance and the rate of photosynthesis21,22. Existing findings on the wind speed sensitivity of terrestrial vegetation growth and corresponding carbon cycle were based on statistical analyses2,9,11. Unlike numerous laboratory and controlled field experiments conducted to examine the impacts of warming, drought, and free-air CO2 enrichment (i.e., FACE) on ecosystem carbon uptake23, field experiments focusing on wind speed remain largely absent, thus hindering us from understanding how wind speed variations affect photosynthesis without confounding with other climatic factors. In addition to the absence of experimental studies, to the best of our knowledge, there is also a lack of modeling studies that rigorously examine the relationship between wind speed and rate of photosynthesis. Exploring the effects of wind speed changes on terrestrial ecosystem productivity, as well as examining corresponding underlying mechanisms, is crucial for enhancing our understanding of how the projected decline in terrestrial wind speed may affect future photosynthesis rates.

Here, we use three satellite-derived gross primary production (GPP) datasets, FLUXNET observations, three wind speed reanalysis datasets, and outputs from six Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) models, to investigate the role of wind speed change on the interannual variability and long-term trend of global GPP (Fig. S1). Multiple statistical methods and model simulation experiments based on the Community Land Model version 5 (CLM5) are integrated to quantify the influences of wind speed change on GPP (see “Method”). We are particularly interested in (i) determining the sensitivity of GPP to wind speed change (hereafter “∂GPP/∂Wind”), (ii) exploring the underlying mechanisms driving this sensitivity and illustrating corresponding influence pathways, and (iii) evaluating the contribution of changes in wind speed to the global trend in terrestrial GPP under past and future climates.

Results

Decadal variations of terrestrial wind speed

From the reanalysis datasets and CMIP6 models, the global terrestrial surface wind speed exhibited a significant declining trend during 1983–2010, followed by a reversal from 2011 to 2030, and is projected to decline again from 2031 to 2100 (Fig. 1a; the identification of turning years is provided in “Methods”). Specifically, reanalysis datasets of the Climatic Research Unit (CRU)24, ECMWF Reanalysis v5 (ERA5)25, and the Modern-Era Retrospective Analysis for Research and Applications v2 (MERRA2)26 illustrated decreasing trends of −0.042, −0.017, and −0.031 m s−1 decade−1, respectively, in spatially averaged terrestrial wind speed from 1983 to 2010. Then, these datasets showed increasing wind speed trends of 0.039, 0.031, and 0.036 m s−1 decade−1, respectively, from 2011 to 2020 (Figs. 1a and S2a; regions affected by fire and human activities were excluded from all analyses in the study, see “Methods”). Consistent with reanalysis results, the spatially averaged terrestrial wind speed trend simulated by six CMIP6 models ranged from −0.070 to −0.009 m s−1 decade−1, with an average trend of −0.024 m s−1 decade−1, from 1983 to 2010. Six CMIP6 models projected average wind speed increases of 0.0041, 0.0058, 0.0092, and 0.0087 m s−1 decade−1 under the SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5 emission scenarios, respectively, from 2011 to 2030. These models projected wind speed declines of −0.0078 to −0.0101 m s−1 decade−1 from ≈2031 to 2100 (Figs. 1a and S3). (The six CMIP6 models were selected from a total of 16 based on their data availability and their ability to reproduce the increasing wind speed observed during the 2010s.) Globally, both the reanalysis datasets and CMIP6 simulations have depicted a widespread decline in wind speed from 1983 to 2010, covering 66.64% and 71.62% of the global land areas, respectively (Fig. S4). Sensitivity of GPP to wind speed changes over past decades

A Principal Components Regression (PCR; see “Methods”) approach, which could mitigate collinearity introduced by other environmental variables such as air temperature, air pressure, precipitation, shortwave radiation, VPD, and atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration, was utilized to quantify the sensitivity of GPP to wind speed change (∂GPP/∂Wind) in the study. The calculated ∂GPP/∂Wind values computed through the PCR method using satellite-derived datasets (1983–2014), FLUXNET site observations (cover varying periods within 1983–2014) and CMIP6 historical simulations (1983–2014) all exhibited predominantly negative values across the globe (Figs. 1b–f, S5, and S6), suggesting that decreasing wind increased GPP. The median values of globally averaged ∂GPP/∂Wind using these three types of datasets were −65.82 (± 62.56), −121.65 (± 104.69) and −84.18 (± 79.55) g C m−2 yr−1 (m s−1)−1, respectively (Fig. 1f). Extending the analysis periods from 1983–2014 to 1983–2016 (or 1983–2018) did not significantly alter the ∂GPP/∂Wind results (Fig. S7). The ∂GPP/∂Wind values based on different data sources showed similar spatial distributions (Figs. 1, S5, and S6). For instance, the ∂GPP/∂Wind derived from satellite-derived GPP datasets and CMIP6 historical simulations both revealed lower values around ~50° N – 60° N and near the equator (Fig. 1d). In relation to plant functional types (PFTs), the identified negative values of ∂GPP/∂Wind for Deciduous Broadleaf Forest (DBF), Needleleaf Forest (NF), and Grass were more pronounced than those for Evergreen Broadleaf Forest (ENF) and Shrub from 1983 to 2014. The pronounced negative values of ∂GPP/∂Wind for DBF, NF, and Grass were consistent across different datasets (Fig. S8a–c).

To further explore the sensitivity of GPP to wind speed change, we performed multiple simulation experiments using the CLM5 land surface model, driven by (i) baseline wind speed, wind speed uniformly (ii) increased by 1% and (iii) decreased by 1% and iv) detrended wind speed at each grid pixel for the period of 1983–2014 (The wind speed data used in these experiments was derived from the CRU JRA datasets; see “Methods”). Our simulation result shows that uniformly increasing wind speed by 1% led to a 0.40 g C m−2 yr−1 reduction of average terrestrial GPP (difference of GPP between experiments ii and i), while a 1% decrease in wind speed resulted in a 0.41 g C m−2 yr−1 increase of average GPP (difference of GPP between experiments iii and i) during 1983–2014 (Fig. 2c). The model simulated reduction in GPP with increasing wind speed (Fig. S9d) supported the negative ∂GPP/∂Wind derived from various data sources using the PCR approach (Fig. 1). Moreover, the overall terrestrial wind speed change by removing the long-term trend, i.e., wind speeds in experiment i) subtracted by those in experiment iv), was negative during 1983–2014 (Figs. 2a and S10b), verifying a widespread decline trend of wind speed during this period. This wind speed decline also induced increases of GPP (i.e., difference of GPP between experiments i and iv), especially from the late 1980s to 2005 (Fig. 2a) and over Northern Hemisphere (Fig. 2d and f). The spatially averaged value of the reduced GPP by removing trend in wind speed was 0.26 g C m−2 yr−1, close to the derived ∂GPP/∂Wind from experiments i and ii (not from the PCR approach) multiplied by the wind speed trend during 1983–2014 based on the default CLM5 simulation, i.e., 0.19 g C m−2 yr−1 (Fig. 2c). The GPP changes resulting from removing the wind speed trend in the simulation (Fig. 2d) also exhibited spatial patterns comparable to those obtained from ∂GPP/∂Wind × wind speed trend (Fig. 2f). Significant increases in GPP due to wind speed change occurred in 77.9% and 78.1% of terrestrial areas between 20° N and 60° N from the simulation and ∂GPP/∂Wind × wind speed trend, respectively (Figs. 2d–f and S11a). Mechanisms of wind speed effect on GPP

Wind speed changes affect GPP through several mechanisms, including (i) altering leaf boundary layer resistance (rb) and the CO2 transport between leaf and the surrounding air, (ii) affecting stomatal conductance (gs) through its effect on plant hydraulics, (iii) changing leaf temperature (TV) and iv) modifying photosynthetic capacity (Fig. 3a). In our study, the negative ∂GPP/∂Wind values were caused by either increases of GPP with decreasing wind speed or decreases of GPP with increasing wind speed. The former case was more widespread, while the latter case was generally observed in the tropics where wind speeds have increased (Figs. S4–S6). The widespread spatial distribution of the former case was reasonable, considering that declining wind speed occurred approximately over 60%–70% of global land areas (Fig. S4) and that decreasing wind speed basically resulted in increases of GPP (Fig. S12). From our experimental simulations using CLM5, a 1% decrease in wind speed led to an increase (median effect: −0.50%; difference between simulation experiments iii and i divided by value from experiment i, see “Methods”) in boundary resistance, which reduced rate of CO2 transport and decreased GPP (mechanism i), and it also caused an obvious increase (median effect: −0.07%) in stomatal conductance (mechanism ii), which subsequently enhanced GPP (Fig. 3a, b). In contrast, it had limited effects (median effects below 0.01%) on leaf temperature (mechanism iii) and photosynthetic capacity (mechanism iv). Although the percentage change in boundary layer resistance due to the wind speed decline (median effect: 0.50%) was larger than that of stomatal conductance (median effect: 0.07%), the sensitivity of GPP to stomatal conductance variations was greater than that to boundary layer resistance (Fig. S13). This is because stomatal conductance directly regulated the internal leaf CO2 partial pressures, exerting a more immediate and significant effect on the rate of photosynthesis within the leaf compared to boundary layer resistance (Fig. 3a). Overall, the enhanced GPP due to decreasing wind speed through mechanism ii was greater than the reduced GPP through mechanism i (Fig. S13). To further examine the mechanisms of how wind speed changes affect stomatal conductance (gs; mechanism ii), we focused on two main factors along the plant hydraulic gradient, including VPD and soil water content27 (Fig. 3a). From our CLM5 simulation experiments, a 1% decrease in wind speed led to a 0.02% enhancement in relative humidity (RH) of canopy air and a 0.06% decrease in VPD (see “Supplementary Note 1” for VPD calculation); meanwhile, it resulted in a 0.07% reduction in soil evaporation and a subsequent 0.03% increase in soil water content (SWC) at depth of 0–30 cm (Fig. 3b). While the changes in VPD and SWC were relatively small, their impacts on GPP were significant, with specific global average values being 0.17 g C m−2 yr−1 and 0.06 g C m−2 yr−1, respectively. These significant impacts were due to the high sensitivities of GPP to changes in VPD and SWC, which were as large as 107.39 g C m−2 yr−1 hPa−1 and 15.49 g C m−2 yr−1%−1, respectively (Fig. S14). Decrease of wind speed led to more pronounced decreases in atmospheric aridity (measured by VPD) and soil drying (measured by soil water content) in high-latitude regions compared to other geographical areas (Fig. S15d–f). Mediation analysis28 can be used to determine whether a significant relationship between an independent variable and a dependent variable is partially or entirely explained by an intermediate variable, known as the mediator (see “Supplementary Note 2”). In this study, mediation analysis was further conducted to investigate the relative importance of atmospheric aridity and soil drying in modulating ∂GPP/∂Wind (see “Methods”; Fig. S16). Results indicated that both reduced atmospheric aridity and soil moisture deficit exerted positive mediation effects on ∂GPP/∂Wind, namely, accelerating the negative impact of wind on GPP, and atmospheric aridity exhibited greater influences compared to soil drought, as shown by its larger spatially averaged mediation effect of 17% (Fig. 3c) and widespread positive values across the globe (Fig. S15g).

Contribution of wind speed decline to the increasing global GPP trend

The contribution of wind speed change to the GPP trend was calculated by multiplying the PCR-approach-derived ∂GPP/∂Wind by the trend in wind speed. We used wind speed reanalysis datasets, satellite-derived GPP datasets and historical and future simulation results from CMIP6. The CLM model experiments, which were designed to evaluate the influencing mechanisms, were not included in this analysis (see “Methods”). The increasing trend of GPP induced by wind speed decline during 1983–2010 based on satellite GPP datasets and the six CMIP6 model simulations was 0.25 and 0.18 g C m−2 yr−2, respectively. In contrast, the wind speed increase during 2011–2030 led to decreasing GPP trends of −0.057, −0.073, −0.033, and −0.18 g C m−2 yr−2 under the SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5 emission scenarios, respectively. The projected wind speed decline during 2031–2100 is expected to result in increasing GPP trends of 0.21, 0.19, 0.15, and 0.35 g C m−2 yr−2 under the four emission scenarios, respectively (Fig. 4a). Notably, the increasing future GPP trends induced by the projected wind speed decline during 2031–2100 could be compared to, or even larger than, that during the historical wind speed decline period (1983–2010), although the CMIP6 models predict a slowdown in the decreasing trend of future wind speeds compared to historical conditions. This is attributed to the projected larger GPP sensitivity to wind speed changes in the future, with an ensemble mean ∂GPP/∂Wind of −139.74, −141.86, −151.93, and −156.67 g C m−2 yr−1 (m s−1)−1 during 2031–2100 under SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5 emission scenarios, respectively (Fig. S17), compared to the historical period (−84.18 g C m−2 yr−1 (m s−1)−1 during 1983–2014). The varying contributions among different data sources and across the four emission scenarios (Figs. Remarkably, we found that decreasing wind speed emerged as the second largest contributing factor to the increasing terrestrial GPP trend during 1983–2010 (proportional contribution: 7.8%), secondary only to the dominant driver—the rising atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration, based on the satellite datasets analysis (Fig. 4b). The CMIP6 simulation could reproduce the second largest contribution of wind speed to increasing GPP trend based on the satellite datasets, with the specific proportional contributions being 6.0%. Meanwhile, the corresponding contribution is projected to be 17.7% and 7.4% under the SSP1-2.6 and SSP2-4.5 scenarios, respectively, during the projected wind speed decline period of 2031–2100 (Figs. 4b and S23). Furthermore, grasslands contributed most (26.8%–73.3%) to the wind-speed-induced GPP trend changes among the seven PFTs during both historical and projected wind speed decline periods, as well as during the wind speed reversal period (Fig. 4c). The substantial contribution of grasslands can be attributed to the significant trend in wind speed and the evident negative values of ∂GPP/∂Wind within grassland ecosystems (Fig. S8). Additionally, the global prevalence of grasslands further enhances their impact. The pronounced negative values of ∂GPP/∂Wind within grassland ecosystems stemmed from the fact that an increase in wind speed led to more severe atmospheric and soil droughts compared to other PFTs. These droughts resulted in a more pronounced reduction in stomatal conductance, as indicated by our simulation experiments (Fig. S24).

Discussion

In the study, we investigated wind speed change, in addition to the widely studied rising atmospheric CO2 concentrations15, increasing air temperatures29, changing water availability30, and more frequent extreme weather events31, in affecting terrestrial photosynthesis. Our results showed evidence of negative sensitivity of GPP to wind speed change (∂GPP/∂Wind), and revealed that wind speed changes affected GPP primarily through its effect on stomatal conductance. Notably, the historical wind speed decline was the second most important factor after rising atmospheric CO2 concentrations, in its contribution to the increasing trend in GPP during 1983–2010 (6.0%–7.8%). To date, studies examining the relationship between wind speed and GPP are limited, but recent studies revealed significant enhancement of vegetation growth (e.g., extended date of autumn foliar senescence in high northern latitude2 and enhanced global LAI9) due to decline in wind speed during past decades, which is consistent with our findings on the negative ∂GPP/∂Wind responses. Our analysis showed that the significant correlations between wind speed changes based on different reanalysis datasets and phenological indicators (including the Start of Season (SOS) and End of Season (EOS)) occupied less than 20% of global land areas (Fig. S25). However, the phenological changes caused by wind speed variations are prominent in high northern latitudes2. The latitudinal statistics revealed dominant positive correlations between wind speed and SOS and dominant negative correlations between wind speed and EOS, which suggested that declining wind speed could potentially lead to an earlier SOS and a delay EOS and subsequently increase GPP to some extent. Existing studies2,9 also showed that wind speed was comparable to temperature, precipitation, and solar radiation in explaining the interannual variations of LAI (i.e., 31%–33%) during 1980s–2010s. Our study directly demonstrated that wind speed change played a comparable or even more important role than temperature in affecting the long-term GPP trend during the past decades and under the low emission future climate change scenarios. Here, we acknowledge that the use of approximately 30 years of data (1983–2010) may introduce uncertainties into the trend analyses. It is also worth noting that both the present study and previous studies have revealed the dominant influence of rising atmospheric CO2 concentrations on the increasing trend in GPP14,32, although the contribution of wind speed changes to the long-term GPP trend was not highlighted in previous studies.

Currently, the terrestrial wind speed decline has been attributed to various reasons, including the increase in surface roughness due to urbanization and enhanced plant biomass33, reduced meridional temperature gradient resulting from polar amplification of global warming7 and decadal ocean-atmosphere oscillations such as the Tropical Northern Atlantic Index, the North Atlantic Oscillation, and the Pacific Decadal Oscillation4. Consequently, the causes behind the terrestrial wind speed decline remain poorly understood. In the study, we have conducted a Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) analysis (see “Supplementary Note 3”) to illustrate the influences of wind speed decline on GPP and the feedback of vegetation growth on wind speed. The SEM results revealed that while vegetation growth significantly increased aerodynamic roughness length globally (Fig. S26f–i), this increase only lead to a significant reduction in wind speed in 18.10% to 28.30% of the vegetated pixels globally (Fig. S26j–m). Moreover, the absolute value of the globally averaged standardized influence coefficient for wind speed on GPP (−0.18) was larger than that for surface roughness on wind speed (−0.04; Fig. S26a). For the pixels where surface roughness had a significant influence on wind speed (18.10%–28.30% of the vegetated area), the corresponding coefficients for wind speed on GPP and surface roughness on wind speed were −0.25 and −0.23, respectively. These findings indicate that, across the entire study area, the influence of wind speed on GPP was much stronger than the effect of surface roughness on wind speed. However, within the 18.10%–28.30% of the vegetated area, the strengths of these influences were comparable. As a result, decreased geostrophic wind speed and increased surface roughness jointly reduced surface wind speed in these regions, thereby enhancing GPP. The identified feedback loop of “GPP—vegetation growth—surface roughness—wind speed”, evident within 18.10%–28.30% of the vegetated area and particularly pronounced in the Northern Hemisphere, supported the findings of Vautard et al.32. For most of the study area (71.70%–81.90%), the nonsignificant influence of surface roughness on wind speed was consistent with the theories proposed by Karnauskas et al.7 and Zeng et al.4. The SEM results also aligned with the findings of Dunn et al.5, which suggested that decadal ocean-atmosphere oscillations drove trends in land surface wind, while vegetation growth, urbanization and instrumentation issues had less impact. Notably, the SEM analysis confirmed a negative influence of wind speed changes on GPP, regardless of whether surface roughness significantly influenced wind speed (Figs. S26b–e and S27). Even after excluding pixels where surface roughness significantly affected wind speed (18.10%–28.30% of the vegetated area), the contribution of wind speed changes to the global GPP increase during 1983–2010 decreases slightly from 7.8% to 6.9%, yet it remains the second largest contributor, following the rising atmospheric CO₂ concentrations.

Although terrestrial surface wind speed trends can vary significantly with weather systems, seasons, and wind speed ranges19,34–36, the globally averaged annual values exhibited a pronounced decline from the 1960s to the 2000s. The causes of wind speed changes do not affect the reliability of the study’s findings, as results obtained from different data using both statistical and model simulation methods (i.e., PCR, SEM, and CLM5 simulations) were consistent. Notably, the model simulation experiments were implemented in an offline mode, where only wind speed differed between the various simulation experiments. This approach allowed us to isolate the pure effects of wind speed changes on ecosystem productivity. Furthermore, both CMIP5 and CMIP6 models projected declines in the model ensemble mean of wind speed in the future7,8. The simulations from CMIP5 and CMIP6 could, to some extent, capture the declining trend in future wind speed associated with increasing plant biomass and polar amplification of global warming (Fig. S3). Moreover, the increasing wind speeds in the tropics (e.g., Amazon), combined with latitudinal patterns, suggest that this is a global phenomenon likely due to reduced meridional pressure gradients. Thus, with increased temperature amplification, we might expect further decreases in wind speeds at high latitudes and, consequently, enhanced GPP.

The increase in gross primary productivity (GPP) resulting from the decline in wind speed was particularly pronounced in regions dominated by grasslands (Fig. 4c). Interestingly, in another study9, grass was also reported to be the primary PFT that exhibited negative correlations between wind speed and annual LAI across the globe. More generally speaking, pronounced negative ∂GPP/∂Wind values were primarily observed in ecosystems where plant growth was highly dependent on atmospheric humidity, such as DBF, NF, and grass. Negative ∂GPP/∂Wind values were associated with significant positive correlations between Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) and atmospheric specific humidity (Fig. S28a). In contrast, ∂GPP/∂Wind was close to zero and even positive in regions where plant growth was not significantly enhanced (or even dampened) by higher atmospheric humidity (Figs. S5, S6, and S29b). For instance, plant growth in Amazon forests was restricted by excessive moisture and humidity during the wet season37,38, resulting in close-to-zero or even positive ∂GPP/∂Wind in this region (Figs. S5, S6, and S27b). Additional analysis was conducted to illustrate the influence of wind speed change on GPP via altering precipitation, and the results revealed that wind-induced precipitation changes contributed minimally (1.75%) to the total effect of wind speed on GPP at the global scale (Fig. S29).

Regarding influence pathways, the decline in wind speed resulted in increased soil moisture primarily due to reduced evaporation. This phenomenon is supported by global observations showing decreases in pan evaporation rates39. Furthermore, reduced wind speed lowered atmospheric water demand, such as VPD, thereby mitigating atmospheric drought and water stress40. Previous statistical investigations illustrated that both the wind-induced increase in soil moisture and decrease in VPD contributed to an extended vegetation growth in autumn over high latitudes9. By analyzing vegetation physiological processes based on our model simulations, we have explicitly quantified the effects of wind speed change on photosynthesis via different pathways, e.g., altering photosynthetic capacity, leaf temperature, boundary layer resistance, stomal conductance. Our study provided quantitative evidence that wind speed decline enhanced stomatal conductance and thus GPP primarily through increasing soil moisture (i.e., by reducing soil evaporation) and reducing VPD (i.e., increasing the relative humidity of canopy air). We showed that wind speed declines during past decades effectively reduced water stress and mitigated drought risks22,23,30,31,41,42, minimized stomatal closure that ultimately led to a significant enhancement of terrestrial ecosystem photosynthesis. Our findings are consistent with two previous studies that also highlighted the prominent role of wind speed variability in influencing the carbon balance of semi-arid and dry ecosystems11,12. In contrast to our findings, a field experimental study suggests that increasing wind speed enhances plant carbon uptake under high solar radiation due to more efficient convective cooling10. However, compared to the large spatial and temporal scales of our study, the short duration (i.e., 30 min) and specific conditions (i.e., high radiation and leaf temperature) of these experiments10 may underestimate the drying effect and overestimate the cooling effect of increased wind speed. Overall, our study highlighted the substantial significance and the mechanism through which wind speed changes affect terrestrial ecosystem productivity, and thus centennial-scale global carbon dynamics. Consequently, the present study can help to reduce the uncertainties in Earth system models’ predictions of future climate change impacts on ecosystem carbon sequestration capacity.

Methods

GPP and environmental variables

We obtained GPP and environmental data at eddy covariance flux tower sites from the FLUXNET Tier 1 database43 (http://fluxnet.fluxdata.org). The environmental variables include wind speed, surface air temperature, air pressure, precipitation, incoming shortwave radiation, VPD, and atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration. Only sites with continuous observations of no less than 5 years and statistical significance less than 0.001 in principal component regression models (see the “Principal Components Regression” section below) were considered. Eventually, 101 out of 205 flux tower sites were selected for investigation (Table S1). The FLUXNET data have undergone a rigorous standardized data processing using well-established and published methods43, including (a) thorough data quality checks; (b) calculation of friction velocity thresholds to filter low turbulence periods, allowing an estimate of the uncertainty from this filtering; (c) gap-filling of meteorological and flux measurements. Notably, the friction velocity thresholds were determined through combing two methods: a change-point-detection method proposed by Barr et al.44 and a modified version of the moving-point-transition method originally described by Reichstein et al.45 and Papale et al.46. The GPP data used in the present study is partitioned from measured net ecosystem exchange data using daytime partitioning method45.

Three satellite-derived GPP datasets, namely, GPP based on near-infrared reflectance vegetation index (NIRV GPP)47, GPP from an eddy covariance–light use efficiency model (EC-LUE GPP)21, and GPP using an optimality-based LUE model (P-model GPP)42, from 1983 to 2018 (to 2016 for P-model GPP) were used in the study (see “Supplementary Note 4”). Additional, global phenological indicators, including the SOS and EOS, were obtained from a global annual land surface phenology dataset48. These indicators were calculated based on AVHRR data using the amplitude threshold method, with methodological details outlined in Wu et al.48. The three satellite-derived GPP datasets and the phenology datasets were all aggregated to spatial resolution of 0.5° × 0.5°.

Global gridded environmental variables, including wind speed, surface air temperature, air pressure, precipitation, incoming shortwave radiation, VPD, specific humidity, and atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations (0.5° × 0.5°), during 1983–2014 were used. Air temperature, air pressure, and precipitation were obtained from the Climatic Research Unit Time-Series (CRU TS) version 4.0.449. Wind speed, shortwave radiation, and specific humidity, which were unavailable in CRU TS, were obtained from CRU and Japanese reanalysis (CRU JRA) version 2.224. Two additional wind speed reanalysis products were also analyzed, namely, the fifth-generation European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts Re-Analysis (ERA5)25 and the Modern-Era Retrospective Analysis for Research and Applications Version 2 (MERRA2)26. Atmospheric carbon dioxide concentration was obtained from Cheng et al.50. VPD51 was calculated based on air temperature, air pressure, and relative humidity from CRU TS 4.0.4 (see “Supplementary Note 1”).

The GPP and environmental variables from 1983 to 2014 were extracted from the “historical” experiment outputs (member id: “r1i1p1”) of six CMIP6 models: ACCESS-ESM1-5, CanESM5, CESM2, CMCC-ESM2, GFDL-ESM4, and INM-CM5-052. The projected GPP and environmental variables for the period 2015–2100 were derived from the same six CMIP6 model outputs under four future emission scenarios, including the Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSP) 1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5. The six CMIP6 models were selected from a total of 16 based on data availability and their ability to reproduce observed trends in global terrestrial wind speed, particularly the wind speed increase after 2010, referred to as “stilling reversal” in previous studies4,8 (Figs. 1 and S3). The remaining ten models, which failed to reproduce the wind speed increase after 2010, include AWI-ESM-1-1-LR, BCC-CSM2-MR, CAS-ESM2-0, EC-Earth3-Veg, GISS-E2-1-G, IPSL-CM6A-LR, KIOST-ESM, MPI-ESM1-2-LR, NorESM2-MM, and SAM0-UNICON.

Normalized Difference Vegetation Index (NDVI) product

We used a spatiotemporally consistent global NDVI dataset, the PKU GIMMS NDVI product, which was available from 1982 to 2020. Initially, this dataset was produced for the period of 1982–2015 based on the Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR) using a machine learning model. Subsequently, it was extended to year 2020 using a data consolidation method that relied on MODIS LAI data (2001–2020). A total of 3.6 million globally-dispersed Landsat NDVI samples were used to generate the PKU GIMMS NDVI product. More detailed information about the PKU GIMMS NDVI product can be found in Li et al.53 In this study, the NDVI dataset was aggregated to a monthly timescale at a spatial resolution of 0.5° from 1983 to 2014.

High disturbance regions and plant functional types

Regions with excessive anthropogenic disturbance were excluded in all analyses following the methods proposed by Yu et al.54. The criteria include a Human Footprint Index—HFI larger than 50%, a percentage of annual burn area larger than 30% and a coverage of cultivated and managed vegetation larger than 30%. We used the Global Human Footprint Dataset (HFI) of the Last of the Wild 220 Project, Version 2, 2005 (LWP-2), which had a spatial resolution one kilometer and was expressed as a percentage, to account for human activity (https://sedac.ciesin.columbia.edu/ data/set/wildareas-v2-human-footprint-geographic). Data from the Global Fire Emissions Database version 455 were used to exclude burned areas.

We extracted seven PFTs, including deciduous broadleaved forest, evergreen coniferous forest, evergreen broadleaved forest, deciduous coniferous forest, grassland, shrubland, and savanna, from the Historical Land-Cover Change and Land-Use Conversions Global Dataset. This dataset had a 0.5° × 0.5° resolution and was available at National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) National Climatic Data Center (http://www.ncdc.noaa.gov).

Identification of turning points in wind speed trend

Previous research4 has identified the year 2010 as a turning point for the reversal of global terrestrial wind speed trends from a decline to an increase. The reanalysis datasets and simulation results from the six selected CMIP6 models in this study clearly support this turning point (Figs. 1 and S3). Furthermore, projections from these CMIP6 models indicate another turning point after the 2010s, where terrestrial wind speed is expected to revert to a declining trend. To identify this turning point, i.e., the year when the downward trend may resume, we conducted a series of linear regressions for each scenario (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5). We varied the starting year from 2010 to 2080 while keeping the end year fixed at 2100 to calculate the trend in wind speed for each starting year. The starting year associated with the most negative trend was identified as the year when wind speed is likely to begin decreasing again. These starting years were identified as 2027, 2025, 2023, and 2038 for the four scenarios (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5), respectively, with an average of 2029.8. Based on these findings, we divided the study period into three phases: 1983–2010 as the historical wind speed decline phase, 2011–2030 as the reversal phase, and 2031–2100 as the projected decline phase.

We used “∂GPP/∂Wind” to denote the regression coefficient of wind speed in Eq. (4) in the study. The Thiel-Sen slope estimator method58 was used to determine the trends in annual wind speed and GPP. The GPP trend caused by wind speed change was estimated as the ∂GPP/∂Wind multiplied by the trend in wind speed, and dividing it by the GPP trend gives the contribution of wind speed change to the GPP trend. The wind-induced GPP trend and its relative contributions among different environmental variables were calculated for three identified periods: 1983–2010, 2011–2030, and 2031–2100. For the 2011–2030 period, ∂GPP/∂Wind was time-weighted (i.e., 2011–2014 versus 2015–2030) as the average of ∂GPP/∂Wind values derived from both historical simulations and future projections of CMIP6 models. The contributions of the other six environmental variables, i.e., air temperature, air pressure, precipitation, shortwave radiation, VPD, and carbon dioxide concentration, were calculated in the same way. The sum of contributions from all seven environmental variables is expected to be equal to (or close to) 100% (Fig. 4).

Physically-based mathematical models are robust tools for mechanism exploration59,60. The widely used land surface model—CLM5, which is the land component of the Community Earth System Model Version 2 (CESM2)61, was used to (a) calculate ∂GPP/∂Wind and validate the ∂GPP/∂Wind results derived from the statistical (i.e., PCR) analysis, and (b) to investigate the underlying mechanisms of changes in GPP driven by alteration of wind speed. The CLM5 was chosen because the model has explicit representation and parameterization of the biogeophysical and biogeochemical processes61, through which wind speed affects GPP. For instance, it explicitly represents plant hydraulic dynamics27 that was critical in regulating the effects of wind speed on GPP.

In this study, the CLM5 was run in the offline mode. To achieve carbon, water, and energy steady state at the start of the simulations61, CLM5 was run for 400 years in the “spin-up” mode with accelerated decomposition rates of soil organic matters, followed by an additional 800 years’ run in the “normal mode”. By the end of the spin-up, the total ecosystem C was drifting by less than 0.02 Pg C year–1 at global scale, much lower than the 0.1 Pg C yr−1 threshold recommended for the C4MIP by Jones et al.62. Then, experimental simulations were conducted using CLM5 for varying wind speed scenarios for the period of 1983–2014, including:

i) Control run: The simulation was forced by the default air forcing dataset of CLM5 – CRUNCEP version 7 meteorological data (6-h data; https://rda.ucar.edu/datasets), except for wind speed, which was replaced by data from the CRU JRA version 2.2 dataset, because the CRUNCEP7 wind speed revealed widespread increasing trend of terrestrial wind speed during 1983–2014, inconsistent with observations; in contrast, CRU JRA2.2 wind speed showed overall decreasing trend of terrestrial wind speed during the same period. All air forcing data had a temporal resolution of 6-h and a spatial resolution of 0.5° × 0.5°, and the simulation was conducted at 0.9° × 1.25° resolution. The atmospheric CO2 concentration in the simulation was set to a constant value of 367 ppm (default value in the model) to exclude effects of the rising CO2 concentration on GPP.

ii) Sensitivity run: All model settings and drivers were the same as the control run, except that the wind speed was uniformly increased/decreased by 1% and 8%, respectively throughout the simulation period. The extracted declining trends in spatially-averaged terrestrial wind speed during 1983–2014 were −1.48% and −1.95% per decade from CRU dataset and CESM2 simulation, respectively (Fig. 1a). Previous studies reported an averaged terrestrial wind speed decline of up to 8% over the past three decades3. Adjustments of 1% and 8% were determined to cover the range of the terrestrial wind speed changes. We haven’t further manipulated wind speed within the range of 1%–8%, because the responses of GPP to wind speed changes (ΔGPP) from −8% to +8% are essentially linear (Fig. S12).

iii) Detrended wind speed run: All model settings were the same as the control run, except that the long-term trend in wind speed was eliminated. The singular spectrum analysis63 was used to extract and remove the long-term trend in wind speed, and the extraction was carried out on a 5-day scale, instead of 6-h or daily scale, to deal with the requirements of substantial computational resources. Specifically, trend extraction was implemented using function “trenddecomp: in MATLAB for each terrestrial grid.

The uncertainty of the ∂GPP/∂Wind derived from principal component regression (PCR) analysis at each grid cell was evaluated based on 1000 Monte Carlo simulations. The global average relative uncertain of ∂GPP/∂Wind, calculated as the standard deviation divided by the absolute mean value, was 16.77%. Meanwhile, the global average coefficient of determination (R2) of the PCR fitting used in the study was 81.49% (Fig. S11b, c). The small relative uncertainty and the large R2 demonstrated the reliability and precision of the PCR analysis, respectively. To further increase the robustness of the ∂GPP/∂Wind derived from the PCR analysis, we compared the results obtained from model experiments and those derived by statistical analyses based on the output of the control run (Fig. S1). To illustrate the sensitivity of GPP to wind speed changes, i.e., ∂GPP/∂Wind, the differences in GPP between the perturbed and control runs were qualitatively compared with the results derived from statistical method. Furthermore, the differences in GPP between the control and detrend runs were quantitatively compared with the statistical results, i.e., ∂GPP/∂Wind × wind trend, to illustrate the GPP change induced by long-term trend of wind speed.
