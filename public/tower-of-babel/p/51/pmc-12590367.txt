Anomalous Left Coronary Artery From Pulmonary Artery Masquerading as Myocarditis in an Infant: A Case Report
Authors: Chun‐mei Gao, Hai‐tao Lv
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAnomalous origin of the left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA), or Bland‐White‐Garland syndrome, is a rare but life‐threatening congenital cardiac defect. Its clinical presentation, particularly in infants, is often nonspecific and can be mistaken for common conditions like myocarditis or dilated cardiomyopathy. We present a case of a 1‐month‐old infant where the initial presentation strongly suggested viral myocarditis, but characteristic electrocardiographic findings were pivotal in leading to the correct diagnosis of ALCAPA.

Case

A previously healthy 1‐month‐old female infant was transferred to our hospital due to a 10‐day history of cough and wheezing, with acute exacerbation over 24 h. There was no significant family history. On the second day of admission, she developed acute respiratory distress. Physical examination revealed tachycardia (heart rate 200 bpm), hypoxemia (oxygen saturation 85%), and signs of poor perfusion with cutaneous mottling. She was immediately transferred to the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU).

Emergency electrocardiogram (ECG) revealed sinus tachycardia, pathological Q waves in leads I, aVL, and V4–V5, reversed R‐wave progression across precordial leads V1–V3, ST‐segment elevation in leads I, aVL, and V2–V3, and low‐amplitude or inverted T waves in leads I, aVL, and V4–V6, whereas leads V1–V3 exhibited upright T waves (Figure 1). Serum cardiac troponin T level was markedly elevated at 165.8 pg/mL (reference range: 0–14 pg/mL). Chest radiography indicated bronchopneumonia accompanied by cardiomegaly.

Following a documented infection and subsequent fulminant clinical deterioration, the marked elevation in troponin T levels initially supported a diagnosis of myocarditis. However, detailed electrocardiographic analysis revealed evidence of extensive myocardial necrosis, injury, and ischemic changes predominantly affecting the anterior wall territory—consistent with left coronary artery perfusion. Echocardiography and coronary computed tomography angiography (CTA) confirmed the diagnosis of anomalous origin of the left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (Figures 2 and 3), with severely impaired left ventricular function (LVEF 27%).

The patient exhibited a favorable prognosis subsequent to undergoing a pedicled left coronary artery grafting procedure and was discharged 2 weeks after the surgery. Serial follow‐up ECGs at 10 days, 6 months, and 1 year post‐surgery (Figure 4) demonstrated complete resolution of the previously observed abnormal Q waves and T‐wave inversion. Concurrent echocardiograms documented a gradual normalization of LVEF, and the patient's heart rate stabilized within the age‐appropriate range.

Discussion

Anomalous origin of the left coronary artery from the pulmonary artery (ALCAPA), or Bland‐White‐Garland syndrome, is a rare but lethal congenital cardiac defect if left undiagnosed (Blickenstaff et al. 2023). We present a case of a 1‐month‐old infant whose initial presentation with heart failure and elevated troponin mimicked acute myocarditis, yet the ECG provided pivotal clues that led to the correct diagnosis. This case underscores the critical role of electrocardiography in distinguishing between common and life‐threatening rare conditions in infants.

The pathophysiology of ALCAPA involves hemodynamic changes that evolve postnatally. During fetal life, high pulmonary artery pressure permits adequate perfusion through the anomalously connected left coronary artery. After birth, the fall in pulmonary vascular resistance critically reduces perfusion pressure in the left coronary system, leading to myocardial ischemia in its territory—specifically, the left ventricular anterior wall, lateral wall, and anterior two‐thirds of the interventricular septum, primarily supplied by the left coronary artery. This phase is exemplified by the acute clinical deterioration and ischemic ECG changes observed in our patient. In some infants, the development of collateral circulation from the right coronary artery can lead to a “coronary steal” phenomenon, further exacerbating ischemia. Clinically, ALCAPA is divided into infantile and adult forms. Our case represents the classic infantile form, characterized by poor collateral circulation, which typically presents in early infancy with nonspecific symptoms of heart failure and carries a high mortality risk without intervention (Achim et al. 2023; Blickenstaff et al. 2023).

The diagnosis of ALCAPA requires a high index of suspicion. Although echocardiography is the first‐line imaging tool, its diagnostic accuracy is limited, with one series reporting a sensitivity of only 68.8% (Lin et al. 2020). This underscores the necessity for complementary diagnostic modalities. In our case, while myocarditis was the initial consideration due to the infectious prodrome and troponin elevation, the ECG findings were incongruent with a diffuse inflammatory process. Instead, the distinct pattern of anterolateral ischemia (pathological Q waves, ST‐T changes) specifically pointed toward a coronary perfusion abnormality. This electrocardiographic insight was instrumental in prompting definitive coronary imaging with CTA, which confirmed the diagnosis. This diagnostic sequence highlights that the ECG is not merely a screening tool but can be the key to initiating a correct diagnostic pathway.

ALCAPA is a surgical emergency, with up to 90% mortality in the first year of life without treatment, making early diagnosis paramount (Blickenstaff et al. 2023). In any infant with signs of left ventricular failure and elevated cardiac enzymes, the ECG must be meticulously scrutinized. The diagnosis of ALCAPA could be made if all the following criteria were met: (a) Q wave greater than 0.3 mV (3 mm); (b) Q wave duration longer than 30 ms; (c) QR pattern in at least one of leads I, aVL, V5, and V6; (d) No Q waves in any inferior lead (II, III, aVF) (Hoffman 2013). The presence of these features mandates urgent investigation with advanced coronary imaging.

Conclusion

In summary, ALCAPA is a great mimicker of infantile myocarditis. This case illustrates that the 12‐lead ECG provides a critical, low‐cost, and rapidly available clue that can redirect the diagnosis from a common inflammatory condition to a rare, surgically correctable congenital anomaly. Recognizing the characteristic electrocardiographic signs of regional ischemia is essential for triggering life‐saving intervention.
