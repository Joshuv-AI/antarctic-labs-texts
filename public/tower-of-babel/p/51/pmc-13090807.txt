Navigating Practical, Political, and Ethical Challenges of Long‐Term Human Biology Field Research: The Shuar Health and Life History Project
Authors: J Josh Snodgrass, Felicia C Madimenos, Melissa A Liebert, Samuel S Urlacher, Tara J Cepon‐Robins, Theresa E Gildner, Richard G Bribiescas, Lawrence S Sugiyama
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMajor advances in understanding human biology and health have been made possible by long‐term field research projects with Indigenous peoples, whose ecological settings and lifeways are distinct from majority populations in high‐income settings. Long‐term fieldwork is central to human biology, yet much about this research approach remains opaque. This paper considers a single long‐term study—the Shuar Health and Life History Project in Amazonian Ecuador—and discusses how we have navigated practical, political, and ethical challenges of initiating and maintaining a two‐decade long collaborative project with integrative field and laboratory components. By enhancing transparency and offering logistical and ethical insights, our goal is to provide guidance to researchers. We offer three sets of conclusions and recommendations. First, our highly integrative approach allows us to ask important scientific questions and answer them in innovative ways. We recommend investment in broad theoretical and methodological training and study design, as well as a project structure that nurtures students' ideas. Second, a project like ours is extremely challenging to operate, requiring major sustained commitment of time and funding to maintain the fieldsite and laboratories. We recommend that scientists initiating major field projects be cognizant of these enormous costs and challenges, develop supportive relationships among collaborating researchers, and incorporate flexibility and excess capacity. Finally, sustainable long‐term investment in research communities must be prioritized, and the science and outreach efforts require tailoring to local conditions and realities. We recommend that researchers invest deeply in communities, building trust and establishing long‐term relationships that are mutually beneficial.Keywords: anthropological practice, biomarkers, ethics, fieldwork, Indigenous peoples

Introduction

Many of the most significant advances in understanding human adaptation, biology, psychology, and health stem from long‐term field research projects with Indigenous peoples, whose ecological settings and lifeways are distinct from those of majority populations in high‐income contexts. The diversity of diets, physical activity patterns, social conditions, marriage and reproductive patterns, and exposures to physical environmental factors (e.g., high‐altitude hypoxia, extreme cold, and high parasite burden) offers a unique lens through which to explore novel scientific and societal questions that would be difficult, if not impossible, to investigate elsewhere. Research with Indigenous communities has generated groundbreaking insights across human biology and evolutionary anthropology, including on life history and demography (e.g., Lee and DeVore 1968; Hill and Hurtado 1996; Hawkes et al. 1997); physiological/energetic adaptations in extreme environments (e.g., Leonard et al. 2005; Beall et al. 2010); reproductive ecology (e.g., Konner and Worthman 1980; Ellison et al. 1989; Bribiescas 1996); foraging (e.g., Blurton Jones and Konner 1976; Hawkes et al. 1982); growth and developmental plasticity (e.g., Dietz et al. 1989; Bogin and Loucky 1997; Hewlett and Lamb 2005; Walker et al. 2006); and, psychology (e.g., Sugiyama et al. 2002; Marlowe 2004; Sell et al. 2009).

Following the tradition of intensive anthropological fieldwork established by early scholars like Franz Boas, Margaret Mead, and Bronislaw Malinowski, human biologists have often emphasized long‐term field research (i.e., conducted over months, years, and sometimes decades) in geographically, environmentally, and culturally diverse locations. While much of the practice of field research has changed in recent decades in recognition of the colonial legacy and exploitative aspects of earlier approaches (see e.g., MacClancy and Fuentes 2013), long‐term field studies remain a core feature of anthropology. In human biology today, long‐term field projects are typically highly collaborative, with well‐developed community partnerships, and careful attention to ethics. Much has been written about fieldwork in human biology and biological anthropology in a general sense (e.g., Lasker and Mascie‐Taylor 1993; Panter‐Brick and Eggerman 2010; MacClancy and Fuentes 2011), including fieldwork ethics and best practices (e.g., Stinson 2005; Turner 2005; Aagaard‐Hansen and Johansen 2008; Froment 2011; MacClancy and Fuentes 2013; Crittenden 2020; Adegboyega and Zhang 2023); the challenges and risks of fieldwork (e.g., Howell 1990; Clancy et al. 2014; Ice et al. 2015); the personal impact of fieldwork when balancing career and life/family (Brazelton et al. 2022); and, the difficulties of returning home from fieldwork (e.g., Sarma et al. 2022). However, there is a great deal about human biology field research that remains opaque, even to other human biologists, anthropologists, and scholars in closely related disciplines.

While human biologists may occasionally discuss these issues informally among themselves, our scientific presentations, papers, posters, and books seldom address the complex, nuanced, and site‐specific operational aspects of individual field projects—such as how and why a project was initiated, the individuals and institutions involved, and how researchers navigated the complex sociopolitical, cultural, and environmental landscapes of specific field sites. Additionally, critical details—including the financial and logistical hurdles of sustaining a project across time, the personal costs to familial relations and career trajectories, and the inherent limitations of such studies—are often overlooked. Few academic publications provide in‐depth accounts of these aspects of long‐term field‐based human biology research projects, although detailed discussions of collaborative research among the Akwesasne Mohawk Nation (Ravenscroft et al. 2015) and the Tsimane Health and Life History Project (Gurven et al. 2017) are notable exceptions.

Considering these limitations, this paper focuses on a single long‐term study—The Shuar Health and Life History Project (SHLHP [see Appendix 1 for a glossary of key abbreviations and acronyms]; https://www.shuarproject.org/)—to discuss the practical, political, and ethical challenges of initiating and maintaining a long‐term human biology and evolutionary anthropology field project. By enhancing transparency and offering logistical and ethical insights, we aim to provide valuable guidance for researchers preparing for fieldwork. To that end, we conclude with a set of recommendations for researchers. Importantly, this paper is not meant to be exhaustive and each of the core topics (i.e., practical, political, and ethical) could be expanded upon in greater detail; consequently, we may leave some readers hungry for more information. However, we feel that there is value in framing the project holistically, which allows an appreciation for its uniqueness and its historical contingencies. The paper is intentional in not centering participant (i.e., Shuar) experiences and interests—although our main research topics related to health were already of interest to local Shuar and health care collaborators prior to the start—but instead is written from the vantage point of the researchers. We are extremely fortunate to have been able to develop long‐term, mutually beneficial collaborative relationships with multiple Shuar leadership administrations and communities. As such, our use of the term “challenges” is not directed at any frustrations of working with Shuar but rather reflect the realities of an everchanging political, practical, and ethical landscape that we as researchers must navigate. Furthermore, we emphasize that working through these challenges has led to new project opportunities and directions, novel collaborative possibilities, and a constantly evolving but continuously relevant project.

The Shuar and the Shuar Health and Life History Project

Shuar are Indigenous Amazonians of Ecuador and northeastern Peru of the Aénts Chicham (formerly Jivaroan) linguistic group, with a rapidly growing population estimated at approximately 136 000 individuals in Ecuador (INEC 2023) (Figure 1). Their economy has historically been centered on household subsistence production, including swidden horticulture, hunting, and fishing (Karsten 1935; Stirling 1938; Harner 1984; Rubenstein 2001; Pohle and Reinhardt 2004; Zapata‐Ríos et al. 2009). While some Shuar have migrated to major cities and towns within Ecuador and abroad, the majority continue to live in small, rural communities scattered across the Upano Valley (UV) and Rio Zamora drainages at the eastern edge of the Andean Cordillera, and extending eastward across the rugged and remote Cordillera de Cutucú to the < 200 m lowlands of the Rio Morona region (Cross‐Cutucú region [CC]). Their inhabited territory spans roughly 30 000 km2 of neo‐tropical southeastern Ecuador, primarily within the Morona Santiago province (CODENPE 2012).

There is a complex relationship among Shuar, the Ecuadorian government, non‐Shuar Ecuadorians, and international corporate interests, which has intensified in recent decades in large part because of disputes over land rights and accelerating resource extraction (e.g., Watts 2017; Vacas‐Oleas 2024). Shuar are renowned for successfully repelling invading outsiders, including the Inca and Spanish empires in the 16th century. In 1964, Shuar established Latin America's first Indigenous federation as a means of dealing with agrarian reforms that were aimed at developing the Ecuadorian Amazon region (Salazar 1981; Rubenstein 2001). More recently, with the arrival of copper, gold, and oil mining companies, Shuar have not remained passive. Their legacy of independence and resistance has helped fuel the emergence of grassroots organizations dedicated to defending their lands from mining threats (Rudel 2018) but this has also caused significant internal divisions with respect to how to deal with these threats, especially from about 2018–2025 with regard to leadership of the Federación Interprovincial de Centros Shuar (FICSH).

The SHLHP was established in 2005 by LSS through collaboration with FICSH, Shuar partners at the community‐level, and Area 6 of the Ecuadorian Ministerio de Salud (Health Ministry) (Urlacher et al. 2026). The SHLHP has focused its fieldwork on the Morona Santiago province and grew out of prior work by LSS in the region (see below). In its first 2 years, the project expanded to include JJS as project co‐director, and initiated collaborations with other Ecuadorian organizations such as the Instituto Ecuatoriano de Seguridad Social (IESS), and graduate students from the University of Oregon. Subsequently, additional senior researchers and students from Harvard and Yale joined the SHLHP, and additional collaborations were initiated with partners such as the Ecuadorian Ministerio de Educación (Education Ministry). In 2019, the SHLHP leadership team was expanded to include MAL, FCM, and SSU as co‐directors given their growing and essential roles in the project across multiple years.

This paper's author team includes the five current co‐directors of the SHLHP (MAL, FCM, JJS, LSS, SSU), two scholars who completed their dissertation research as part of the project and have now transitioned into faculty positions (TJCR, TEG), and a senior member of the project with over a decade of project involvement (RGB).

The Seeds of the SHLHP

The basis for the SHLHP was LSS's collaboration with John Tooby and Leda Cosmides (University of California, Santa Barbara) that began in the early 1990s when he was enlisted in their vision to develop collaborative long‐term field sites with small‐scale, subsistence‐based societies that contrast sharply with populations in wealthy nations typically studied in human‐focused research. The overarching goal was to develop and test hypotheses about human adaptations and adaptability as part of a broader effort to unify the behavioral and social sciences with natural sciences through an integrated evolutionary framework (Tooby and Cosmides 1992). This vision was also motivated by an understanding that cross‐cultural ethnographic research could shed light on human adaptive challenges, the evolutionary responses to those challenges, and their behavioral and cultural sequalae (see e.g., Chagnon and Irons 1979; Symons 1979, 1992; Tooby and DeVore 1987). As part of this effort, Cosmides and Tooby funded travel and infrastructure (e.g., field freezers, solar panels, etc.) during the early years of the SHLHP. Prior to SHLHP, LSS conducted various studies with the Indigenous Shiwiar (Pastaza Province) (e.g., Sugiyama and Chacon 2000; Sugiyama et al. 2002; Sugiyama 2004), who are closely related to the Shuar in the Aénts Chicham language group.

In the early 2000s, the Shuar population was considerably larger than the Shiwiar (> 50 000 vs. < 500), permitting larger sample sizes and the involvement of multiple researchers without overburdening individual communities. While the Morona Santiago province was undergoing accelerated infrastructure development and market‐related changes, many Shuar still experienced conditions more closely resembling those to which humans are evolutionarily adapted, such as natural fertility, subsistence‐based economy, active lifestyle, and high pathogen exposure. These conditions made Shuar an ideal population for cross‐sectional and longitudinal study of the health effects of market integration (MI; increasing production for and consumption from a market‐based economy; Lu 2007), life history tradeoffs (i.e., using the branch of evolutionary biology known as life history theory to investigate resource allocation tradeoffs between competing reproductive and somatic demands (e.g., Blackwell et al. 2010; Urlacher et al. 2018)), and how psychological adaptations use local variables to compute adaptively relevant outputs (e.g., Cepon‐Robins et al. 2021). Additionally, the changing economic landscape allowed for testing major hypotheses about large‐scale health transitions, including the epidemiological shift from infectious to chronic diseases and evolutionary mismatch hypotheses (e.g., McDade et al. 2012; Liebert et al. 2013).

Goals of the SHLHP

Four overarching goals of the SHLHP emerged during the first few years of the project. First, the project aims to document patterns of health and disease and to investigate how social and environmental changes associated with MI affect Shuar health and well‐being, with particular attention to child growth and nutritional status, chronic conditions (e.g., cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and osteoporosis), and infectious/parasitic disease, topics of mutual interest with FICSH and the local Ministerio de Salud when LSS first discussed collaboration. This insight has broader implications for understanding not only contemporary humans but also those in the historical and evolutionary past. Second, SHLHP tests hypotheses derived from life history theory to investigate how phenotype and health over the life course are shaped by underlying variation in energy and resource allocation (i.e., trade‐offs) to competing functions such as maintenance, growth, reproduction, and physical activity. Third, the project seeks to provide insights into hypothesized cognitive adaptations using cross‐cultural tests, including the biological underpinnings of disgust, cooperation, mutual aid, attraction, and other aspects of sociality that are central to the evolutionary success of the human species. Finally, the project aims to provide health information to participants, other community members, and to partner organizations (e.g., FISCH and the Ministerio de Salud) to help with prevention and treatment efforts and respond to on‐the‐ground health needs through collaboration with Shuar leadership, local providers, and communities. Additional information on how these collaborations developed is outlined below.

Intellectual Origins of the SHLHP

The intellectual basis of the SHLHP derives from the integration of four research traditions: human biology; human behavioral ecology; evolutionary psychology; and evolutionary medicine (Table 1), as well as prior research conducted by project co‐directors LSS and JJS. Work by LSS among the Yora of Peru, the Yanomamö of Venezuela, and several Indigenous groups within Ecuador using human behavioral ecology, evolutionary psychology, and human biology frameworks (e.g., Sugiyama 2004; Sugiyama and Chacon 2000; Sugiyama et al. 2002; Walker et al. 1998) and by JJS with Indigenous Siberians (primarily the Yakut [Sakha], starting in 2001) (e.g., Snodgrass et al. 2007; Leonard et al. 2008; Sorensen et al. 2009), and immigrant farmworkers in Oregon (beginning in 2007; e.g., McClure, Martinez, et al. 2010; McClure, Snodgrass, et al. 2010; McClure et al. 2013; McClure, Snodgrass, et al. 2010; McClure et al. 2013; Squires et al. 2012) using human biology and evolutionary medicine approaches contributed to the initial development of the project's conceptual and methodological framework. Research by JJS in Siberia involved deep partnerships with Indigenous scientists and physicians that allowed a large‐scale collaborative project that balanced larger scientific goals of US and Russian scientists and local health needs; this effort included responding to emerging health needs of communities (e.g., autoimmune thyroid disorders (Cepon et al. 2011) and type 2 diabetes (Snodgrass et al. 2010)) and publishing research findings in Russian (Snodgrass et al. 2011).

When the SHLHP began in 2005, with full‐scale biomarker data collection starting in 2008, few long‐term human biology or evolutionary anthropology field projects, apart from the Tsimane Health and Life History Project (see Gurven et al. 2017), matched SHLHP in terms of its scope. The Kalahari Research Project, which had a broad human evolutionary approach, had already concluded, as had the Ituri Project, which was vital to the development of the fields of human ecology and reproductive ecology. The Tsimane Amazonian Panel Study (2002–2010) (Leonard et al. 2015) was integrative and broad but leaned more toward biocultural and economic perspectives than evolutionary ones. Hadza research, which began in the 1960s under the International Biological Program and was later advanced by James O'Connell, Nicholas Blurton Jones, Kristen Hawkes, and Frank Marlowe, among others, incorporated behavioral, ecological, and was beginning to include explicitly evolutionary psychological research (e.g., Blurton Jones et al. 1992; Wetsman and Marlowe 1999) but did not adopt an intensive biomarker component until much later (e.g., Raichlen et al. 2017). More recently, additional long‐term, integrative projects have emerged in human biology including the Orang Asli Health and Lifeways Project (Wallace et al. 2022), the Turkana Health and Genomics Project (Lea et al. 2021), and the Shodagor Longitudinal Health and Demography Project (Starkweather et al. 2024), all of which are contributing valuable insights into the evolution of human health and disease.

Measuring Social and Environmental Change: A Central Role for Market Integration (MI)

A key aspect of the SHLHP that shapes hypotheses and research design is operationalizing MI. Earlier studies often framed these kinds of changes occurring in Indigenous groups like the Shuar as “acculturation,” but this perspective is inaccurate as it implies a unilateral flow of influence from the dominant to minority population, overlooking the complexity, reciprocity, and feedback among cultural interactions. Initially, the SHLHP approached MI through regional comparisons between Shuar communities in the relatively more market integrated UV and those in the less market‐integrated and remote CC region (Figure 1). While this approach was and continues to be productive, it was initially driven by feasibility constraints and practical exigencies. Over time, our ethnographic observations and formal analyses revealed that MI was highly uneven in its effects and varied markedly both between and within communities in each region (Liebert et al. 2013; Gildner et al. 2020; Urlacher, Liebert, et al. 2016). These findings prompted a shift toward investigating MI at the household level and employing methods that capture its multifaceted and layered influences. Building on the ethnographic foundation established by LSS's earlier work in Latin America and JJS's research in Siberia that adapted a style of life approach from Bindon et al. (1997) (e.g., Snodgrass et al. 2005), we created a structured interview instrument to assess material style of life among Shuar. This tool was grounded in the typical sequence in which key market‐produced items that vary in ownership are acquired over time among Indigenous peoples of Ecuadorian Amazonia. From this, we developed multiple composite variables to capture traditional style of life (T‐SOL), market‐integrated style of life (M‐SOL), and household style of life (H‐SOL) (Liebert et al. 2013; Urlacher, Liebert, et al. 2016); this approach has been applied in subsequent papers.

In 2010, the project expanded to include non‐Shuar Ecuadorian Mestizos (Colonos) from the Upano Valley region, which provides an opportunity to examine the same variables in similar ecological conditions, yet among people from a different economic and cultural background (Barrett et al. 2025; Madimenos et al. 2015, 2026). The project has since been extended to include Shuar living in the peri‐urban context of the regional market center (Urlacher et al. 2021) as well as first generation Ecuadorian immigrants living in the US (Shattuck‐Heidorn et al. 2021).

Data Coverage

Balancing on‐the‐ground local interests and health needs with theoretically driven research questions, SHLHP has developed an extensive dataset. Data include standard anthropometric and functional measures (e.g., height, weight, grip strength), social, economic, infrastructure, educational, and behavioral measures, dietary information, reproductive and health histories, genealogies, and health metrics from point‐of‐care testing devices (e.g., HemoCue Hb201+ [for hemoglobin measurement] and Cardiochek PA [for glucose and lipid measurements]). Minimally invasive biomarker samples (finger prick dried blood spots [DBS], saliva, urine, and feces) are routinely collected and shipped to the University of Oregon Global Health Biomarker Laboratory (led by JJS), the Baylor University Human Evolutionary Biology and Health Lab (led by SSU), and the Yale Reproductive Ecology Laboratory (led by RGB) depending on the analysis. Additional analyses, particularly fecal sample assessments for parasite infections, are conducted in the field as needed. Other measures, applied to subsamples of participants, include bioimpedance analysis for body composition, accelerometry for physical activity, indirect calorimetry for resting metabolic rate, doubly labeled water for total energy expenditure, ultrasound for bone density, knemometry for short‐term linear growth, and behavioral observation for time allocation and related variables. The overall study design is a mixed‐methods approach, including quantitative and qualitative data collection and analysis, with both cross‐sectional and longitudinal elements. As of 2026, the project includes data from over 3500 Shuar and 390 Colono participants, ranging in age from 1 month to 90 years of age.

Since SHLHP's inception, in addition to sharing immediate individual results with participants (e.g., helminth infection, point‐of‐care, and anthropometrics), and de‐identified reports of results of particular interest to the FICSH, we have regularly and carefully shared de‐identified datasets with scientists and health practitioners who adhere to explicit privacy, data use, and publication agreements (see https://www.shuarproject.org/data‐sharing); the topic of data sharing is discussed in detail below.

Selected Key Findings and Applied Health Contributions

Over the past 20 years, the SHLHP has published across journals with an anthropological focus and in others emphasizing general science, medicine, nutritional sciences, microbiology, and experimental psychology. Some of the important intellectual contributions to date include: (1) clarifying the role that early life exposure to parasitic worms plays in shaping the immune system and influences patterns of growth and testing the Old Friends Hypothesis (Blackwell et al. 2010, 2011; Urlacher et al. 2018; Cepon‐Robins et al. 2019, 2025); (2) documenting that the effects of MI on cardiometabolic health and parasite burden, as well as on child growth and nutritional status, are highly variable across individuals and communities (Liebert et al. 2013; Urlacher, Liebert, et al. 2016; Cepon‐Robins et al. 2014; Gildner et al. 2016, 2020); (3) demonstrating that the widely‐used clinical marker of systemic inflammation (C‐reactive protein [CRP]) is highly variable over time among Shuar adults and children and shows no evidence of chronic systemic elevation (McDade et al. 2012; Urlacher et al. 2018; DeLouize 2025); (4) demonstrating that the disgust emotion functions to regulate pathogen exposure, showing the importance of evolved psychological mechanisms in disease avoidance (Cepon‐Robins et al. 2021); (5) showing that Shuar children do not spend more calories each day on a pound‐for‐pound basis than children living in the US or UK (Urlacher et al. 2019) and that MI does not clearly impact Shuar children's total energy expenditure (Urlacher et al. 2021), indicating a primary role of diet in driving trends in childhood obesity; (6) demonstrating that Shuar children's linear growth, while below international references (Urlacher, Blackwell, et al. 2016), is highly variable (Blackwell et al. 2009; Urlacher, Blackwell, et al. 2016) and appears to be primarily driven by energetic trade‐offs with immune activity (Urlacher et al. 2018, 2019); (7) revealing that the timing of early life history events (e.g., menarche) canalizes the adult bone density phenotype (Madimenos et al. 2012); (8) the identification of MI‐related differences in lifeways (e.g., housing structures) that are predictive of features of the gut microbiome (Stagaman et al. 2018); and (9) developing an integrative theoretical approach that unites physiological, behavioral, and social components of the immune response to infection (Schrock et al. 2020).

SHLHP data have also been used to establish physical growth references for Shuar children and adolescents (Blackwell et al. 2009; Urlacher, Blackwell, et al. 2016) (this was one of the original project goals based on early discussions with Shuar parents and leadership [see below]), and to provide population‐specific reference data for adults on bone density based on calcaneal ultrasound (Madimenos et al. 2011). Since its inception, the SHLHP has also contributed data and collaborated with cross‐site studies that have made unique insights into human biology and evolution, including on the universality of shame as a component of our species' cooperative biology (Sznycer et al. 2018), on variation in human preferences for sexually dimorphic faces (Scott et al. 2014), and the environmental calibration during childhood of risk and time preference (Amir et al. 2020). Overall, SHLHP has provided an additional social and ecological context of human variation that extends the advances made by other long‐term anthropological projects, and been groundbreaking in rigorously testing longstanding evolutionary ideas such as life history tradeoffs between growth and immune function and disgust as a context‐sensitive mechanism that reduces infection exposure.

A full list of SHLHP papers is available on the project website (https://www.shuarproject.org/).

Navigating Practical Challenges

In the following section, we describe some of the ways that our team has navigated issues from obtaining authorizations for fieldwork, getting supplies and equipment to and from participant communities, ensuring the safety and health of the research team, scheduling fieldwork among other career and life responsibilities, to selecting biomarkers.

Coordination, Shuar Research Authorization, and Getting to the Field Communities

SHLHP research activities are based in the southeastern Ecuadorian province of Morona Santiago (Figure 1) with Sucúa (population ~11 000) serving as a project's primary staging location. Sucúa offers strategic advantages: it is reasonably accessible from Quito by bus, hosts the Shuar Federation (FICSH), provides central access to UV communities and the interior CC region via road (from which riverine access is available), and is home to a network of long‐term Shuar collaborators, friends, and local health care providers. These Shuar and Colono collaborators and friends provide invaluable insights into ever‐evolving local political dynamics, short‐ and long‐term research assistance, lodging and secure storage for essential equipment (e.g., field freezers, stadiometers, scales, solar panels, electrical systems, and water purifiers), household items, and lab supplies (e.g., gloves and lancets) between field seasons.

Fieldwork and acquiring local permissions/authorizations and community agreements has been led primarily by LSS and SSU. This process involves coordinating (in Spanish) with FICSH and other agencies to conduct research across Shuar territory, identifying potential participant communities, reaching community consensus, and ensuring individual consent per human subjects guidelines (Institutional Review Board [IRB] approval; see below). To date, we have collaborated with several elected Shuar Federation administrations, with two serving multiple terms thereby allowing us to forge deeper trust and collaboration, and with whose then health directors we continue to work. These also include community health auxiliaries and/or nurses with whom we have worked (and lived). This consistency is unusual, given the frequent turnover in Shuar leadership and the historical pattern of international students and/or researchers conducting studies without returning, a source of irritation and initial mistrust of researchers by some Shuar leadership. Establishing trust requires significant and dedicated time and social investment, particularly given the Shuar's strong cultural value of independence and history of endemic intrinsic and extrinsic conflict (i.e., independent, warrior ethos culture).

One of the project's persistent challenges has been transporting equipment and supplies to Ecuador and into remote field communities, particularly in the CC. Early in the project, poor road infrastructure made moving equipment from Quito to Sucúa, and especially to CC communities, slow and inefficient. However, nationwide infrastructure improvements have since made travel to Sucúa and UV communities more manageable (although landslides, road closures, and failing bridges still occur). Transporting and operating equipment in CC communities remains an issue. Bulky and fragile equipment must endure long journeys by bus, truck, and motorized canoe—typically 5–9 h by road followed by 6–12 h on the river (Figure 2), with both heavy rains and dry season low water impeding travel. Some of these challenges are navigated by ensuring that equipment travels in waterproof containers (i.e., pelican cases and dry bags), but other aspects of often lengthy journeys can only be approached with patience. Once a CC community is reached, equipment must be physically carried, typically a considerable distance, over rough and muddy terrain. A further complication is the lack of stable power in CC communities, requiring the use of solar panels and power stations sourced from the US and Ecuador. Setting up, operating, and maintaining these systems demands basic electrical skills and careful pre‐trip planning, as mistakes can be hazardous, damage expensive equipment, and disrupt data collection (Figure 3).

Navigating Physical and Mental Health in the Field

Another set of challenges includes conditions that locals in the region face daily and yet are without the privileges and support that we, as US‐based researchers, have. These challenges involve the demanding environmental conditions of the Amazon, including extreme tropical heat and humidity, sun exposure, biting and stinging insects and snakes, fierce and potentially rabid dogs, lack of potable water, lack of toilets and/or latrines, limited food supply, and heightened risks of respiratory and gastrointestinal illnesses, parasites, and potentially life‐threatening infectious diseases (e.g., malaria). None of these challenges are insurmountable, but they require considerable preparation. This involves fostering open and respectful communication among team members before departing for Ecuador and throughout fieldwork, which creates a space for discussing concerns, sharing practical advice (e.g., on recommended vaccinations and antimalarials, water treatment options, camping supplies, and appropriate clothing and footwear), and preparing for emergencies. Knowledge of first aid and tropical medicine is useful for all, and mandatory for at least one team member per field season, as is making sure people understand that the kind of emergency service access typically available in the US is not available in most study communities. This also involves clear rules and expectations regarding issues that may occur, such as those involving non‐emergency “borderline” cases that, in the desire to continue research, might lead to an irrevocably bad decision (e.g., any bite by a dog that breaks the skin, without documented rabies vaccination, triggers evacuation to the nearest locale with available rabies vaccine). Ultimately though, the field team made up of people with vastly different levels of experience cannot be a democracy, so although input from everyone is considered, final decision‐making authority must rest with the senior, most experienced field researcher(s) leading the team. Of course, in field seasons with just a few seasoned researchers, decisions will typically appear communal. These proactive measures help the team navigate unexpected hurdles and support each other during challenging moments.

Beyond physical concerns, one of the most underappreciated aspects of population‐based research is the time and effort required to assess the local situation, maintain old and/or build new relationships, and prepare for fieldwork upon arrival in Ecuador, a challenge that can be particularly difficult for those US‐based researchers with little experience outside high‐income countries. Cultural differences in scheduling can be frustrating for those unfamiliar with them, and the extensive conversations, political discussions, social obligations, and events required before, during, and/or after data collection may cause anxiety or impatience for those eager to begin research, particularly students who may feel overwhelmed due to limited time and resources to initiate and complete projects. However, these relationships are essential for fostering genuine, collaborative partnerships, and it is important to always remember that we are guests in these communities and in the culture. Building this kind of trust, rooted in non‐extractivist and collaborative goals, takes time, requiring patience and sustained engagement, often working with or around others' schedules. For example, it initially took several years of relationship‐building with FICSH, community health centers, and local communities before the SHLHP team could reliably begin data collection within a few weeks of arrival. Sometimes this requires postponing or halting immediate research goals in the interests of long‐term project continuity. Even with our well‐established project today, it typically necessitates several weeks of on‐site communication, engagement, and preparation before data collection is ready to begin.

Our commitment to transparency and clear expectations in the field has also helped SHLHP foster a culture of support and respect among team members. Fieldwork in the Amazon presents numerous challenges, and our ability to navigate them effectively is rooted in our strong sense of teamwork and collaboration. We rely on each other not just for logistical support but also for physical and mental well‐being. The realities of fieldwork—shared hard work, managing dehydration, sleep deprivation, gastrointestinal issues, and other unforeseen hurdles—create strong bonds among team members. Open communication, mutual respect, and shared responsibility allow us to adapt to challenges and sustain our work over the long term. This collaborative approach has been essential to the longevity of our project, enabling us to continue conducting meaningful research in a demanding field setting. Despite here focusing on challenges, we should add that no fieldwork project endures without a core of field team members who fundamentally enjoy it, the culture and the people: a team‐based effort allows those who might otherwise not work in this type of setting alone to do so, but the long‐term project depends on those who thrive on fieldwork over the long haul, despite the costs (see below).

Three key points must be emphasized. First, preparation and flexibility are essential for working in these settings. It is crucial to anticipate various scenarios, develop contingency plans, and adapt to shifting local political conditions and unforeseen challenges. Second, while the project leadership (i.e., co‐directors) bears the primary responsibility for ensuring team safety, individual team members must also take responsibility for their own actions and preparedness and follow the project guidelines and advice of experienced researchers. Since the project's inception, we have carefully vetted potential team members through extensive dialogue. Once they have joined, team members undergo preparation, including what used to be called “the talk” which now includes reading and signing a document outlining project rules and information (SHLHP Fieldwork Expectations and Code of Conduct), familiarizing themselves with Shuar ethnohistories and local Ecuadorian politics, achieving basic conversational Spanish proficiency, and engaging in detailed discussions about the realities of fieldwork (including familiarity with the SHLHP Roles, Responsibilities, Policies, and Procedures document). Despite these efforts, the challenges of fieldwork can be shocking to new team members. Third and perhaps most important, we live and work in the context of Shuar culture, so being respectful of the local norms, customs and values must inform our behavior in the field. This means that understanding valued behaviors and roles among Shuar, and treating them respectfully and acting accordingly within one's role as a person, researcher, collaborator, and eventually friend within this cultural context, is expected. Indeed, nobody will last long working in Shuar culture unless they have a fundamental appreciation and respect for the culture. This should obviously be the case with all anthropological research, but we would be remiss to not emphasize it here.

Timing Is Everything—Competing Priorities in Work and Life

A major challenge for the SHLHP includes navigating pivotal shifts in availability and time for the project resulting from changes in jobs and life events (e.g., having and raising children, dealing with health issues, caregiving for relatives, navigating COVID‐19). Given the 20‐year duration of the project, it is unsurprising that all project team members have undergone major life changes that have shaped the amount and timing of involvement in the project, including our ability to participate in fieldwork. Most of us hold academic jobs with heavy teaching and service load demands, limiting both the time that can be committed to the project and our ability to travel. As Brazelton et al. (2022) note, career stage also affects how much time team members devote to fieldwork and other project components. Even for the most committed field researcher, sustained long‐term fieldwork can impose significant social costs, particularly on families. For those scholars launching large‐scale projects like SHLHP, it is crucial to anticipate these challenges by building in flexibility, ensuring cross‐training for different roles, and developing capacity so that other team members can step in to contribute to critical components of project goals when needed; this avoids bottlenecks or disruptions if someone becomes unavailable.

Another unexpected challenge is the difficulty of collaborative writing for publications and grant proposals when authors are in different physical places (e.g., across the US), at different career points, with disparate writing schedules, and with different writing styles (both differences in personal writing style but also writing differences rooted in disciplinary traditions and expectations [e.g., human biology versus evolutionary psychology]). Authors of this paper who as graduate students completed their PhD's on the project (TJCR, TEG, MAL, FCM, SSU) went on to academic jobs that introduced additional obligations, such as teaching and service responsibilities, thereby imposing constraints on their ability to collaboratively write during their early career stage.

Finally, the changing landscape of higher education now means that most of us are required to teach more students with less support, compounded by more compliance requirements and service responsibilities (APA 2024). These obligations impose additional challenges to scheduling and protecting time for research, running a lab, and severely limits time for writing papers and grant proposals.

Samples, Study Design, and Managing Data

The overarching research design of the SHLHP (i.e., working across multiple, often remote communities in several regions), Shuar social and demographic particulars (e.g., relatively small villages of typically < 200 closely related individuals, over half of whom are under 15 years of age), and the need for relatively large sample sizes to adequately power multivariate statistical models, have raised complexities for data acquisition. In addition to these data collection issues, there are also analytical challenges related to the non‐independent nature of some data, such as data from multiple members of the same household.

These data collection and analytical challenges are compounded by two additional obstacles. First, to financially support the SHLHP and to enable graduate students to collect data for their master's or PhD projects, we typically run multiple interconnected sub‐projects simultaneously. This approach often creates data collection complications and can result in competing priorities, yet it also enables students to collect pilot data quickly, and to complete projects which would be impossible without the support of all members of the field team. Second, discussion/collaboration with potential participant communities is partly based on our scientific objectives and practical ability to work there (e.g., who can be locally available that field season), but also shaped by collaborations with Shuar leadership and the communities, who have their own agendas, expectations, and constraints (see below). Therefore, project operations regularly change, which require day‐to‐day and year‐to‐year flexibility. While this is not unusual for human biology field projects, it is likely unfamiliar to students new to the field or even to seasoned academic veterans who have worked in different settings or on smaller‐scale projects.

Finally, two additional, interrelated challenges deserve mention when working with data from the SHLHP: Long‐term data management and the analytical demands posed by the breadth and complexity of the data and the research questions. From 2006 to 2010, Aaron Blackwell constructed and managed the SHLHP master database. Since 2010, MAL has taken over database management, bringing valuable fieldwork experience that enhances her ability to handle complex data; as the project has expanded, she has ensured consistency and data integrity across the SHLHP database. Our collective (by the project co‐directors) oversight and maintenance of the SHLHP database has involved building relationships with families to enable long‐term tracking of participant health and household census information inside and outside of the field, entering data from paper forms collected in the field, converting digital files from lab analyses, securely storing physical documents, and managing digital files across multiple sets of data and projects. These responsibilities require maintaining best practices for collecting, storing (securely and confidentially), and disseminating human subjects' data, ensuring consistency and integrity in longitudinal data collection from individuals, households, and communities across multiple years and different research projects. This process includes intensive data cleaning, often revisiting original paper forms to resolve inconsistencies, and organizing data for internal use and external collaborations. With > 3500 individuals in the database and several hundred variables, this represents a substantial and ongoing effort requiring meticulous and consistent attention.

Over the years, the SHLHP has transitioned between different platforms for database management, data sharing between team members, and statistical analyses; some of these choices are based on shifting preferences but others are dictated by institutional changes or the availability of software packages or new data analytic techniques. Managing a database and providing statistical oversight for a project of this scope demands specialized training and extreme attention to detail. For researchers embarking on similarly large, field‐ and lab‐based studies, it is essential to carefully plan and use data collection and management strategies that accommodate the complexities of multiple researchers' needs and adhere to best practices. While having a point of contact for data management helps standardize data entry and streamline data retrieval, multiple project members should have enough familiarity with the system that someone can efficiently take over if needed.

Biological Samples: Collecting, Shipping, and Analysis of Lab Samples

In addition to obtaining permission to collect biological samples described above, and the ethical issues described below, there are enormous practical considerations presented by the collection of biomarker samples in remote field settings in the Ecuadorian Amazon, with its limited electrical grid, barriers to access clean water, and hot and humid climatic conditions. Furthermore, once biological samples are collected, most require a cold chain, which necessitates portable freezers and a portable solar‐powered electrical system to power them, shipments on ice, and long‐term storage in ultracold (−80°C) freezers until analysis. Researchers initiating similar biomarker work must be attentive to all these details or they risk being unable to obtain quality data from samples; in the worst‐case scenario, researchers might collect participant samples and then be unable to analyze them successfully, which unnecessarily burdens participants, breaks agreements with collaborators, and is financially inefficient (and a waste of research time and effort). In the following section, we detail some of the challenges encountered in the collection, processing, transport, and analysis of biological samples.

Collecting Biological Samples

The SHLHP regularly obtains finger prick blood, urine, and fecal samples that can provide immediate health results through point‐of‐care testing (Gildner et al. 2022; Madimenos et al. 2022) and can also be collected and analyzed later in the lab for various minimally invasive biomarkers (Urlacher et al. 2022). We also periodically collect saliva samples for hormonal analyses. We discuss the political and ethical challenges of biological sample selection in detail below, but it is worth emphasizing here that biospecimen selection is a careful balance among what is possible (i.e., feasible in remote locations with limited infrastructure and extreme temperature conditions), culturally acceptable/preferred (e.g., avoiding collection of hair samples, which pilot collection and subsequent dialogue showed to not be preferred [see below]), most useful to Shuar participants and communities (e.g., health measures such as parasite infection, cholesterol, and blood sugar/diabetes risk), and what is most interesting scientifically (i.e., what motivates our research and allows us to test our hypotheses).

Regarding point‐of‐care testing, the main challenge has been keeping the instruments (e.g., HemoCue Hb201+ and Cardiochek PA) in working order given their typical sensitivity to high heat and humidity and their risk of damage during travel to remote tropical settings. To minimize exposure to the elements and obtain high‐quality data, point‐of‐care instruments are stored and transported in sealed plastic bags with desiccant, packed in boxes/containers (and typically in dry bags) and are ideally used for data collection during the coolest time of day and in the coolest locations (i.e., protected by shade). Under the hottest conditions, which we have encountered more frequently in other locations beyond Ecuador (e.g., work by JJS in Bangladesh as part of the World Health Organization's World Health Survey Plus), using ice packs and portable fans helps keep instruments cool during use. Decisions regarding what instruments to employ in the field are partly driven by these climatic constraints. For example, the SHLHP has not used the A1cNow+ device for measurement of glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) in finger prick blood because the instrument and test cartridges are very sensitive to temperature and are bulky, creating difficulty with transport and storage. Pilot testing with other devices (e.g., the HemoCue White Blood Cell analyzer) has led to avoidance because they proved unreliable under Amazonian field conditions.

For dried blood spots (DBS), we follow standard practices to collect finger prick blood onto commercial filter paper cards (Whatman 903) (McDade et al. 2007). High humidity in the Amazon presents a singular challenge as it can saturate unused DBS with moisture if exposed to air for more than a few minutes. To mitigate this, we store unused cards in sealed plastic bags with desiccant and prepare them immediately (< 1 min) before use. Once collected, we use picnic nets to protect DBS cards from insects during drying (Figure 4). Following standard protocols, we immediately store collected DBS cards in a portable freezer after a four‐hour drying period. For optimal blood collection, after pretesting more delicate lancets, we decided to primarily use BD Microtainer Contact‐Activated Lancet (Blue; catalog number 366594), which are more likely to penetrate thick, calloused fingers and ensure adequate capillary blood flow.

Field finger prick blood collection station showing Cardiochek PA and HemoCue point‐of‐care instruments, blood collection supplies, and a picnic net protecting blood spot cards as they dry. Photo by Melissa Liebert.

Other biological sample collections, such as urine and fecal samples, also demand considerable investment and care. Urine samples for hormone analyses are typically collected over the course of the day (24‐h) or first thing upon waking (morning void). Urine collection for doubly labeled water is more flexible and easier to accomplish. Our fecal sampling strategy for determining helminth species and abundance via microscopy follows the protocol outlined by Cepon‐Robins et al. (2014), which involves ensuring samples are fresh and are from first morning passage (Figure 5). Fecal samples for microbiome analysis are collected in a similar manner, except with RNAlater or ethanol added. An overarching, though likely obvious, point is that successfully collecting different biomarker samples requires meticulous attention to practical details (including educating participants about the biomarker collection process), underscoring the importance of robust and thoughtfully designed field protocols, with the particular logistics of sample collections tailored to the community's physical layouts and circumstances.

Field microscopy setup for parasite analysis, showing Tara Ceon‐Robins processing a fecal sample. Photo by Lorenzo Sugiyama.

The SHLHP has also encountered challenges associated with collecting saliva samples for hormone analysis (e.g., cortisol and testosterone). Saliva collection requires precise timing, particularly when documenting diurnal cortisol patterns, which involves collecting multiple samples per day for consecutive days. As described by Liebert et al. (2025), our data collection process to measure diurnal cortisol is labor‐intensive and involves in‐person sampling (at waking, 30 min post‐wakeup, and in the evening) to ensure compliance, often necessitating pre‐dawn hikes to participants' homes to arrive prior to participants waking. Researchers coordinate with participants ahead of time to avoid misunderstandings (and ferocious guard dogs and mean turkeys) when arriving before dawn. This approach was adopted based on best practices at the time we initiated this collection (2011), dovetailing with the logistical constraints around participant activity patterns, and designed to generate high‐resolution data while minimizing participant burden.

Storing and Shipping Biological Samples

Dried blood spot and other biological samples are typically stored and transported within Ecuador using portable −20°C freezers, powered by deep‐cycle batteries or vehicle 12 V plugs when reliable power is not available. At the conclusion of each field season, samples are transported in these portable freezers to Quito, where they are shipped on dry ice to the US through a contracted shipping company, as several sample types require a complete cold chain (see Bribiescas et al. 2025 for additional details). This significantly increases project costs, with a typical shipment from Quito to our lab destinations in the US costing approximately $5000. Despite the cost, not only are samples shipped via guaranteed cold chain by an experienced provider (though it should be noted that making shipping arrangements can sometimes take several weeks), but the local shipping company office handles export and customs office inspections and scheduling. Once samples reach their destination in our labs in the US, they are transferred to long‐term −80°C or −20°C freezer storage (depending on sample requirements).

Laboratory Issues: Space, Assay Availability, and Equipment

Our ability to maintain lab spaces is made possible through our universities and specifically because of our sustained academic productivity and external grant funding; even then, there have been frequent institutional challenges in obtaining and retaining functional lab space. Sample storage and preservation require constant monitoring, management, and procurement of resources.

The long‐term storage of biological samples, even in well‐equipped laboratories at major research universities in the US, has posed significant challenges. Periodic power interruptions caused by natural or human‐made disasters have necessitated investments in remotely monitored alarm systems to protect samples. In addition, freezers have a limited lifespan; since 2007, three ultracold freezers in the Global Health Biomarker Laboratory and Human Evolutionary Biology and Health Lab have failed. Replacing this equipment long after startup funds have been exhausted is particularly challenging (see below), as these freezers typically cost in the range of $15 000 to $20 000, excluding maintenance contracts. This extends to point‐of‐care devices, since some of those instruments (e.g., Cardiochek PA) have a lifespan of only 1–2 field seasons.

A further complication is that DBS assays often require kits and materials designed for plasma or serum and must therefore be validated for the small quantities of blood and red cells present on filter paper cards. The Global Health Biomarker Laboratory and the Human Evolutionary Biology and Health Lab have expertise in DBS, and many SHLHP studies have undertaken this DBS validation process (e.g., Urlacher et al. 2018; Snodgrass 2022), a time‐consuming and costly step that is essential before analyses can begin. Biomarker selection for DBS assays involves balancing scientific goals with practical considerations such as the availability of commercially validated assays or the feasibility of developing and validating new ones. Assay development and validation require a rigorous and systematic process, including evaluation, modification, and formal validation (see e.g., Eick et al. 2019), which adds further costs and time. Furthermore, we have not been able to successfully validate immunoassay‐based DBS protocols for certain analytes (e.g., interleukin‐10 [IL‐10]), which limits research possibilities.

Researchers embarking on long‐term projects that include substantial biomarker components must carefully consider the financial demands and near‐pathological vigilance required with equipment. A point‐person must always be available (24/7, 365 days a year) to monitor freezers and respond to emergencies to prevent catastrophic loss of irreplaceable samples. This level of vigilance and preparedness is essential to the success of such studies.

Funding to Keep the SHLHP Running: Field and Lab

As with other long‐term field studies, the SHLHP requires considerable sums of money to operate, which has necessitated obtaining internal and external research grants and partnering with collaborators with their own funding. Two key points warrant discussion. First, most grant opportunities from federal (e.g., National Science Foundation) and private sources (e.g., Wenner‐Gren Foundation, Leakey Foundation) are designed to address specific research questions rather than provide general support for field communities or lab methods. Without understating the impact of targeted input and specific mechanisms like National Science Foundation Broader Impacts or the Wenner‐Gren Engaged Anthropology Grant, where some funds are allocated to broader project and community needs, there are always critical and often unpredictable fieldwork costs not covered by grants. These include community requests or needs (e.g., purchasing school supplies) or addressing acute medical emergencies unrelated to our study but occurring during our community stays (e.g., paying for emergency hospital transport, private physicians, or medication). Similarly, no granting agency provides funds to offset the numerous individual requests for assistance from long‐term friends and community leaders who have provided a wealth of political, logistic, and infrastructural support over the years. While small, flexible university research accounts occasionally fill these gaps, we frequently rely on co‐director personal funds. That is the unfortunate reality of the scientific funding environment. SHLHP has never had large‐scale, long‐term funding (such as from a National Institutes of Health R01 award) but instead we have flexibly cobbled together money from many different sources. The amount of funding, however, varies substantially year to year. Some years we might have ~$50 000 available from multiple active external research grants to support extensive fieldwork (e.g., 5–6 people for 2–3 months), while in other years we have no external grants and we pull together approximately $10 000 from small university accounts and personal funds to support a smaller team (e.g., 2–3 people, and/or for shorter periods, e.g., 5–6 weeks) to focus on core data collection and maintaining community relationships. These figures, however, do not include costs of field equipment that last for multiple years (e.g., microscopes, solar panel setups, point‐of‐care devices, and ultrasonometers) or the cost of analyzing biomarker samples in our US labs. What it does show is how much can be accomplished even in years with minimal financial resources, especially when we share costs across the team such that those with greater funding effectively subsidize those with less funding.

The second major issue involves the costs of maintaining our labs themselves. Most grant funds are used to cover assay‐related supplies such as buying immunoassay kits and consumables (e.g., pipette tips, gloves). Grant funds rarely extend to other critical lab costs, such as those involved with training lab staff, graduate students, and undergraduates to run assays, addressing mistakes or failed kits that require reanalysis, repairing/maintaining equipment, or covering unpredictable inflation‐driven increases in supplies. Sadly, very little of our federal grant “indirect” (F&A) funds make it back to contribute to lab infrastructure costs. Furthermore, given the scale of biomarker analysis in the SHLHP, having a dedicated lab manager in each of our main labs is critical to oversee operations and train students. However, hiring a professional lab manager is costly, and this has only partially been accomplished in the Global Health Biomarker Laboratory and has not been possible in the Human Evolutionary Biology and Health Lab. The Yale Reproductive Ecology Laboratory maintains a full‐time laboratory manager that is shared with other biological anthropology labs; they are responsible for logistics including hazardous waste disposal, equipment maintenance, ordering, shipping, and emergency responses (e.g., freezer failures). Researchers embarking on lab‐intensive human biology research projects should plan strategically and creatively to navigate these many complexities, planning for a level of lab infrastructure that can be maintained for a decade or more.

Navigating Political Challenges

In this section, we discuss several of the important political issues encountered by the SHLHP team, including developing collaborations, selection of and constraints on biomarker choice and/or survey questions, enrolling study participants, and navigating co‐authorship on publications.

The Politics of Local Collaboration and Selection of Communities and Participants

As discussed above, SHLHP has collaborated with the Shuar Federation (FICSH), Ecuadorian ministries, and the leadership and members of multiple Shuar communities. Each year, we have multiple discussions to update research agreements based on newly planned projects, or renegotiated after considering political or logistical changes on the ground. The Shuar political landscape is constantly evolving, shaped by internal dynamics of a traditionally egalitarian society that organized into communities and adopted multi‐level democratic organizations to deal more effectively with state and non‐state, non‐Shuar actors (e.g., Salazar 1981; Rubenstein 2001), and in response to changing Ecuadorian policies and commercial mining activities that continue to challenge and reshape Shuar autonomy, politics, and ecology. Even so, Shuar culture places high value on personal autonomy and independence. Consequently, each field season the SHLHP coordinates with the appropriate Shuar leadership, selecting participant communities based on a balance of political, logistic, social, and scientific considerations. Additionally, in each community where we are invited or seek to work, senior project members coordinate with community leaders who organize (or include on an already planned meeting schedule) a community‐wide meeting to introduce the team and our proposed research. These meetings provide an opportunity for potential participants to ask questions, voice concerns, and discuss pressing health or other issues affecting their communities, and discuss whether they wish (via consensus) to have the research in their community. The formality of these meetings varies a bit depending on the size of the community and how long we have worked there in the past. However, they are part of current Shuar culture and how Shuar communities approach all community‐wide coordination and decisions, regardless of how seemingly important or minor they might seem to outsiders. They are not something we introduced or specific to our research program, and only upon community consensus can our presence and research in a community go forward.

Our community‐centered approach takes different forms and here we describe three general types of collaboration. However, in reality these are overlapping. The first type of collaboration involves how research is developed, planned, and carried out in direct coordination with FICSH and/or community leaders/members. An example can be seen in our collaboration on an existing child health diagnostics project that had begun in 2005 as a partnership between FICSH, the Ministry of Health, and UNICEF. Our role was to assess/optimize methods, enter data, and create graphs for presentation of results to FICSH, other local Indigenous organizations, participant communities, and publications (e.g., Blackwell et al. 2009), as well as to jointly plan future research. Subsequent development of Shuar‐specific growth curves (Urlacher, Blackwell, et al. 2016) and related work on child growth was born of discussions with Shuar parents and the initial community‐driven research efforts. A second type of collaboration is centered on issues of mutual interest to ourselves, FICSH, community leaders, and/or participants; past examples include work on prevalence of helminth infection (e.g., Cepon‐Robins et al. 2014), metabolic syndrome (e.g., Liebert et al. 2013), and anemia (DeLouize et al. 2022). In these cases, potential direct benefits of participating include both immediate informational benefits and the facilitation of medical access via our network of local providers (e.g., organization of anthelmintics) or from the US (e.g., point‐of‐care glucose test kits). The third kind of collaboration initially appears as a form of direct reciprocal collaboration in which research on topics of scientific interest are not directly related to existing interests of FICSH, community leaders, and/or participants. Conversely, communities have goals for projects not directly tied to those of the SHLHP. We collaborate on their project and they on ours. In some cases community plans are generally related to our larger project (e.g., medical supplies for their health promotor or school supplies), but in others community goals focus on things that are largely unrelated (e.g., acquiring funding for communal house infrastructure, purchasing trashcans for a community to support an eco‐tourism project, giving English lessons at the community school, or assisting with water quality efforts [Figure 6]). Over time this becomes more akin to generalized collaborative reciprocity. Planning typically requires significant discussion, first with leaders, then in open community meetings, to make sure that there is consensus all around, and that we can actually provide ‘value added’ to their larger project. Examples of where we have engaged in research based on this reciprocal collaboration include work on facial attractiveness (e.g., Scott et al. 2014), risk preferences (Amir et al. 2020), and shame (Sznycer et al. 2018).

Felicia Madimenos with a Shuar friend/collaborator testing water quality as part of an infrastructure project. Photo by Lorenzo Sugiyama.

Another significant challenge faced by SHLHP is the relatively limited health infrastructure and availability of medical providers in Shuar communities, creating difficulties for us to consistently collaborate with in‐country scientific and medical professionals. Many doctors working in remote and rural areas are rural service doctors rotating through their service year, so they do not have a long‐term presence or investment (see e.g., Romero‐Alvarez et al. 2023). This contrasts with several other large human biology studies that rely on long‐term partnerships with local doctors and researchers. Despite this limitation, SHLHP creates pathways for medical care and/or treatment whenever necessary including coordinating transport to the nearest health centers (puestos or centros de salud), covering costs of travel and treatment, accompanying a participant to facilitate communication with local health workers and, in extreme cases, arranging a medical evacuation through a governmental or mission service (using a two‐way radio or satellite phone).

An important decision in the early years of the project was whether to expand beyond the Shuar to include non‐Indigenous residents in the Morona Santiago region. In 2010, we began a multi‐year sub‐study focused on Colonos, starting with research led by FCM to investigate the role of reproductive factors in bone density. This dual focus on Shuar and Colonos living under similar socioecological conditions has provided valuable opportunities to investigate how key lifestyle factors, such as reproductive patterns and physical activity, contribute to differences in biological parameters of interest (Madimenos et al. 2011, 2026).

Selecting Sample Types and Biomarkers (and Recognizing Those That Are Off‐Limits)

A local ethical and political issue for SHLHP involves the selection of sample types and biomarkers for analysis. In accordance with a long‐term understanding with Shuar leadership across several administrations, we do not analyze human genetic samples. This decision is informed by Shuar politics, as well as concerns about cell‐line patenting and other forms of commercial profiting. Other (non‐SHLHP) researchers have now published such work, but for our purposes the research benefits do not outweigh the future long‐term risks to the project or their being used in internal Shuar politics (e.g., for gossip and/or competing views to instigate internal Shuar conflict). Furthermore, for many of our health conditions of interest, the influence of social and environmental factors far outweigh likely causes associated with within‐population genetic variation (although in other circumstances the questions would be well worth asking).

Cultural sensitivities surrounding other biological samples and measures have also shaped our research practices, sometimes in unexpected ways. For example, we explored the collection and analysis of hair samples for cortisol concentration (Mequanint et al. 2017), expecting that children would prefer it to weekly repeated finger prick blood sampling for a study of children's growth. However, after parental consent and child assent, some kids nevertheless seemed hesitant, and parents asked whether we could just get the same information using minimally invasive bloodspots (with which they were already accustomed); for our purposes, bloodspots were easier and more useful to collect anyway, so we dropped the hair sampling. This experience, as well as others we have encountered, highlights the balance required in human biology research between scientific inquiry, practical limitations, respect for cultural differences, and ethical considerations. Researchers must appreciate these nuances in order to conduct respectful and meaningful studies in diverse settings.

Navigating Publication Among Collaborators: Authorship and Other Important Decisions

Over the years, SHLHP has encountered many challenges in disseminating project results, particularly in establishing fair and transparent guidelines for authorship and author order. One of the key difficulties stems from the breadth and duration of data collection—most publications draw on data from multiple field seasons, collected by numerous individuals. Tracking these contributions over time can be complex, making it difficult to create a simple, universal set of guidelines. Our project authorship guidelines have evolved over time, from fairly simple guidelines when the project was small to more nuanced and flexible as the project grew, researchers transitioned in and out, and students shifted into senior roles.

From the outset, we have taken an inclusive approach to authorship, recognizing that the success of this project depends on the collective effort of many individuals. Authorship is not limited to those who actively write the manuscript; it extends to those who play critical roles in data collection, lab analysis, and/or securing financial support for the research. This approach underscores the reality that no single individual can carry out this work alone. Each team member is not just a ‘worker bee’ but a vital part of a complex machine—without their contributions, the project could not function at the level it does. By including all who meaningfully contribute, we reinforce the collaborative nature of this work and ensure that contributions are acknowledged equitably.

Determining author order presents additional challenges, as we strive to balance the varied but essential contributions of team members. These contributions range from collecting data and performing lab and statistical analyses to securing funding, conceptualizing research, writing, and editing manuscripts. While first authorship is generally straightforward—typically assigned to the individual leading the paper and its underlying research—determining the last author is more complex. In the sciences, last authorship often represents the senior investigator or project leader, but within SHLHP, this has evolved over time. Until 2019, LSS was the last author on all publications emphasizing field research, while JJS served as the last author on all lab‐centered studies. However, as other project members have taken on leadership roles in both field and lab settings, supervised trainees, and become co‐directors, this model has necessarily shifted. The growing integration of lab and field components in publications further complicates traditional approaches to determining last authorship.

Authorship decisions become even more nuanced when data are shared beyond the SHLHP team. While we have a set of written guidelines to codify authorship policies, these guidelines are intentionally flexible, serving as a foundation rather than a rigid rulebook. In practice, the first author and co‐directors determine final authorship collaboratively on a case‐by‐case basis. This process generally works well, but challenges arise when individuals are inadvertently excluded or placed in a position that does not accurately reflect their contribution. Given the significance of publications in shaping academic careers and well‐being, authorship decisions must be approached with care, transparency, and a commitment to fairness. While no system is perfect, prioritizing an inclusive and collaborative authorship model strengthens the integrity of the project and recognizes the essential work of all contributors. Scientific research—especially field‐based projects like SHLHP—depends on teamwork, and our authorship practices are designed to reflect that reality.

In addition to the complexities of determining authorship and author order within the SHLHP team, there are authorship considerations regarding local collaborators, such as Ecuadorian scientists, health care providers, and non‐scientific contributors. With a few exceptions, we have not included local collaborators as co‐authors on publications, primarily because Shuar collaborators are not typically involved in the parts of the research for which authorship would normally be warranted and they are not typically medical or academic professionals who would value co‐authorship for career advancement. This approach differs from many other human biology projects (including ones that we have directed and/or been involved with), where local professionals or academics have direct research roles, and thus serve as co‐authors. Our local collaborators, therefore, benefit in other ways, or as part of their existing responsibilities, or we compensate them financially for their work and their contributions are acknowledged in publications. This approach involves careful consideration and dialogue and is beneficial for Shuar collaborators and partners as well as the SHLHP team. We emphasize the reality that there is no one‐size‐fits‐all collaborative approach that will work in all contexts; instead, the SHLHP arrangement reflects this particular situation and set of negotiations and relationships. Local collaborators whose contributions would merit authorship based on typical scientific standards are included in prior papers, and are co‐authors on some papers currently in progress for that reason.

Finally, aside from relaying findings to participants (described in the next section), our dissemination of findings beyond academic journals includes conference presentations, university press releases, and coverage in the popular media. While media coverage has occasionally been beneficial, it can pose challenges, such as needing to ensure accurate and appropriate representation of our work (e.g., when writers covering our research use language that perpetuates harmful stereotypes of Indigenous peoples)–a common struggle for scientists, especially since the research team is rarely permitted to fact check or edit popular articles before publication. Also, it is essential to note the important role that the SHLHP website (https://www.shuarproject.org/) has played in communicating project goals, sharing results, and forging dialogue with other researchers and potential collaborators.

Ethical Considerations

In this section, we outline several important ethical considerations encountered over the course of the SHLHP, including ethical review across different institutions, use of minimally invasive biomarkers and point‐of‐care testing, choosing representative samples of study participants, and the tensions between open data sharing policies and data sovereignty.

Ethical Review and Institutional Review Boards

Human subjects review, essential for ethical compliance and required by our universities and funders, has required major investment over the years. Our discussion of the challenges of managing human subjects reviews should not be interpreted as frustration with this essential process or a desire to avoid this step; there is a critical need for this safeguard and we appreciate that this process leads to improved ethical approaches and fundamentally, better science. While all methods employed in SHLHP involve no more than minimal risk to participants (allowing for protocols to undergo expedited review), some aspects of this process are complex. The scope of our project, employing numerous methods, including survey, biological samples, accelerometry, and ultrasonometry, among others, has necessitated many Institutional Review Board (IRB) amendments as we adopt additional methods. In addition, the long‐term nature of the project and shifting number of research team members mean that all approved methods cannot be included each year, so we have to compartmentalize recruitment, and consent and assent statements. This, and changes in compliance office staff or reviewers, means that upon annual renewal reviewers sometimes request changes or clarifications to already approved protocols. At the local level, the relevant study protocols are discussed with FICSH, and upon their authorization to conduct the work in FICSH communities, we discuss them with community leaders, and then community members (as discussed above). Adult participants (defined as ≥ 15 years based on the local age of consent) are then asked to provide informed verbal consent, while for participants < 15 years of age a parent provides verbal consent and the child gives assent, at the time of each data collection activity, after discussing any additional questions participants may have.

The University of Oregon has served as the primary institution for the project IRB, with LSS managing much of the IRB work since its inception. This has required a considerable effort to monitor, update, and navigate the approval process, especially given institutional changes to the IRB process. As the project expanded, with University of Oregon students completing degrees and joining other institutions, and as more collaborating researchers and students from other institutions came on board, the complexity of the IRB reviews and inter‐institutional agreements grew exponentially. Today, the project relies on assorted IRB approvals and inter‐institutional agreements with our various universities. Different IRB offices have distinct policies and review processes, leading to increased workloads, longer approval times, and greater unpredictability (e.g., certain institutions have not allowed the University of Oregon to serve as the institution of record for the purposes of IRB, so in some cases this has required separate approval for a team member to analyze already collected data, or a separate consent process for that researcher). Based on our experience with SHLHP, researchers conducting multi‐institutional studies should allocate ample time to address and navigate the evolving and often complex human subjects research compliance landscape.

Minimally Invasive Biomarkers and Health Information From Point‐of‐Care Testing

Since the project's inception, we have obtained biological samples using only minimally invasive methods. The focus on DBS, for example, was chosen partly due to practical issues (i.e., complexities in drawing venous blood, including needing a phlebotomist, specific equipment necessary to process blood, and more stringent storage requirements). More importantly however, DBS is ideal for minimizing risks to and burden on participants (McDade et al. 2007). Furthermore, finger prick blood is a more reasonable and acceptable method for collecting samples from children and adolescents, who have been a major focus of the SHLHP.

Finger prick blood samples are also easily used with most point‐of‐care devices that immediately produce clinically valid results, a clear benefit of this approach. Consequently, health results can be returned to participants instantaneously, along with in‐person explanations and descriptive pamphlets. This approach was adapted from the Indigenous Siberian Health and Adaptation project (co‐directed by JJS from 2001 to 2014; e.g., Snodgrass et al. 2010) and a long‐term study of Latino Farmworkers in Oregon (co‐directed by JJS from 2007 to 2016; e.g., McClure et al. 2013). In both projects, Hemocue Hb201+ and Cardiochek PA instruments were used to obtain immediate (< 2 min) health information on hemoglobin/anemia risk and glucose and lipids, respectively, and these results were returned to participants. In both cases, a process was in place for interpretation and discussion of clinically‐relevant results with, if necessary, follow‐up care—by participating physicians and regional health centers in Siberia and in partnership with a culturally and linguistically savvy community‐based organization (experienced with immigration issues) based in Oregon.

Point‐of‐care testing was integrated into the SHLHP in 2008, with the use of the Hemocue Hb201+ and Cardiochek PA instruments. This inclusion has allowed us to simultaneously collect health data for SHLHP research purposes (e.g., Liebert et al. 2013; DeLouize et al. 2022) and to provide health information to individuals. Furthermore, point‐of‐care testing offers an opportunity to provide aggregate, deidentified information on health issues to participant communities and to FISCH, who can use it to focus health efforts and/or solicit additional resources. The value of point‐of‐care was leveraged in a Wenner‐Gren Engaged Anthropology Grant to FCM that allowed SHLHP to do more structured “health days” where data were returned and more general community‐level implications provided and discussed (https://wennergren.org/article/engaged‐anthropology‐grant‐madimenos/) (Figure 7). While the benefits of point‐of‐care testing in the SHLHP field setting and for other remote, resource‐limited settings have been described elsewhere (Madimenos et al. 2022), the ability to provide information to participants, target health education recommendations, engage people with their own health, and foster collaborative partnerships with communities are just a few of the advantages of point‐of‐care testing.

Community health presentation by Shuar colleagues and local health care provider. Photo by Theresa Gildner.

The implementation of health testing in SHLHP has been successful in several important ways but at times it has also been challenging. We have generally had adequate supplies for testing, our collection protocols have produced high quality results, participants and other community members have generally been interested in obtaining results (i.e., few people have opted out), and we have not encountered any adverse results despite working with thousands of people. However, despite our intention to do so, we rarely have a local health care provider available onsite to provide services or help interpret findings. This is perhaps unsurprising given that most Shuar communities in which we worked did not have basic medical services within the community. So, in situations where results suggest participants might benefit from seeing a local health provider, we facilitate access to services and treatment, including transporting people to the nearest clinic/hospital for further evaluation, or establishing pathways for participants to get evaluations, care, and medicines. Another significant issue is that as chronic conditions such as insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes have become increasingly common in Ecuador and globally because of diet and lifestyle change associated with MI, for those with modestly elevated (but subclinical) biomarker levels, providing one‐time health results coupled with general information about healthy diets and lifestyles often feels wholly inadequate—and frustratingly so. Furthermore, while one‐time health results can encourage participants with levels that exceed clinical cut‐offs to seek treatment from local health providers, unfortunately even those who are diagnosed with diabetes by local doctors, including those who are prescribed insulin, often cannot follow local recommendations to return to clinics weekly to monitor their levels. Although we have provided home testing kits for the few participants diagnosed with diabetes, this was an unsustainable solution, and only solved when frequent bus transport became available in those communities. While there are moments when our work clearly benefits individuals, such as identifying anemia in children or pregnant women potentially caused by parasites, the rapid pace of health transitions in these communities underscores the need for approaches that incorporate more long‐term and sustainable health care practices, while also considering traditional Shuar knowledge systems.

One obvious potential solution is to integrate local clinical partners to assist with follow‐up care. The public health system does provide such care, but as noted above, travel, financial, and infrastructural challenges impose barriers to people accessing continuing treatment. As chronic conditions become more prevalent among Shuar who live outside town, this is an enormous challenge, given on‐the‐ground infrastructural limitations and inter‐cultural barriers. Moreover, in projects spanning many years or decades, external factors inevitably shift the landscape, requiring constant adaptation. In some settings, ongoing collaboration with trained health providers is feasible but in other locations like Morona Santiago—Ecuador's poorest province—limited resources, a shortage of already overworked health providers, and inadequate clinical infrastructure restricts the potential for such collaboration. Increased implementation of plans to station physicians in local multi‐community health units may help in those communities, but to date we have not worked in those communities since services began. In these under‐resourced regions, providing health information becomes even more critical because of greater need, particularly since most public health providers do not have time or resources to commit to primary health education.

Participants and Non‐Enrolled Community Members

The process of recruiting study participants is shaped by local dynamics, presenting unique challenges. Two key issues are determining inclusion criteria and navigating the ethical considerations of providing health information to individuals who are either unable or unwilling to participate in the full study.

The first challenge lies in selecting a truly random sample from the communities. While this is generally the ideal approach scientifically, it is often impractical due to the lack of up‐to‐date census information and the political and logistical considerations that influence participation which make it unfeasible (there are > 100 000 Shuar in Morona Santiago, scattered across hundreds of communities). As a result, recruitment involves careful negotiation to ensure representation while acknowledging these constraints.

The second challenge involves ethical considerations surrounding the dissemination of health‐related information. Sometimes people seek health insights—e.g., related to anemia and glucose levels—without being enrolled in the full study, which may occur if they do not meet eligibility criteria (e.g., if a sub‐study is focused on non‐pregnant women). Denying this information, particularly when no similarly accessible alternative health information option exists, poses an ethical issue. While these individuals are not formally part of that particular study, our approach has been to provide them with health information whenever possible, and we plan for this possibility in selecting participant communities and bringing enough extra supplies to cover all community members who might want this information. In some cases, financial constraints arise, as grants typically do not cover the costs of providing health information to non‐participants, so again we ultimately cover these costs ourselves. Although we have worked to allocate funds for this purpose, doing so requires navigating complex and changing situations.

A related ethical issue is our initial decision to focus on Shuar, and not Shuar in cities or towns, or Colonos, despite their potential interest in participating and receiving health information. This decision was based largely on feasibility (time, effort and financial) constraints and the focus of FICSH at the time. Since we worked exclusively in Shuar communities, the number of married‐in Colonos could be counted on one hand (lands within global Shuar territory could not be sold to Colonos, so Shuar communities were almost exclusively Shuar, although this is changing). Over time, we reconsidered this approach, ultimately deciding to include a comparative sample of Colonos from a particular town parish. This decision has not only broadened access to health information but also enhanced our research design, allowing for comparative analyses between Shuar and Colono individuals living in similar environments.

Tensions Between Open Data Access and Data Sovereignty

A major issue that is becoming increasingly acute is the balance between the ethics of public/open data access and data sovereignty. Receiving US federal grant funding and publishing in scientific journals is increasingly requiring open data sharing through an online repository to ensure transparency and scientific integrity, as well as to make data more widely and equitably available. However, to other researchers, especially those collaboratively working with Indigenous and other oppressed groups, these open access policies—even with deidentified data—pose significant ethical challenges, including commodifying data, undermining data sovereignty by reinforcing colonial/neocolonial power hierarchies, and threatening original collaborative research agreements developed with communities (Tsosie et al. 2021; Mulligan et al. 2022; Fuentes 2023; Getz et al. 2025), as well as relevant participants reasonable understanding at the time of collection on how data will be used based on interpersonal trust built with the field research team. The recent licensing of artificial intelligence (AI) learning models using academic content by journal publishers such as Wiley and Taylor & Francis to private technology companies such as Microsoft adds an additional layer of complexity that merits awareness and considerable vigilance by researchers (Dutton 2024).

The SHLHP takes these issues very seriously, and we work hard to balance these different responsibilities, which are often in tension. We are committed to transparency, collaboration, and data access in order to ensure scientific rigor, and we have a formal data sharing process—and a track record of collaboration and data sharing—in which data are requested through an online Data Request Form (https://www.shuarproject.org/data‐sharing) that is then considered for approval by the project co‐directors. However, we are very clear that our prime directive is to protect the privacy and safety of our participants and the collaborating communities, and to ensure that data are not used in extractive and/or unethical ways. Furthermore, our research agreements specify that deidentified data can only be shared upon request and that the requesting researcher agrees to privacy, data use, and authorship expectations that the SHLHP has established to ensure the welfare of our participants and their communities. Throughout this process, we recognize our role as stewards of these Shuar data, not owners. Other approaches to data sharing and sovereignty have been used elsewhere, such as those where Indigenous scientists and leaders have developed their own repositories (e.g., Native BioData Consortium, a non‐profit located within a sovereign American Indian nation [the Cheyenne River Sioux Reservation]; https://nativebio.org/). We respect and are inspired by these approaches. However, due to infrastructural and political realities specific to our collaboration (e.g., at one point the main FICSH headquarters was raided by national police, offices and living quarters ransacked, and records destroyed; in another incident the FICSH headquarters was firebombed in a dispute linked to mining issues and conflict over rightful FICSH leadership) so similar repositories are not viable or appropriate options for our collaboration with Shuar at this time. This is not a limitation that we consider permanent, and we believe that it is more transparent and ethical to acknowledge these constraints than to pursue a model that does not fit our current context. As with much of this kind of work, there is no one‐size‐fits‐all solution to data sharing and data sovereignty.

Conclusions and Recommendations

Based on our 20 years of the SHLHP and over 125 years of cumulative SHLHP experience, we offer three sets of conclusions with recommendations. Our hope is that these points are useful to scientists initiating or maintaining a field‐based research project in human biology or a related discipline. We also hope this article is useful in providing a transparent, behind the scenes perspective on how and why we have made decisions on certain aspects of the project design and approach.

Conclusion and Recommendation #1: Train Students Broadly, Foster Innovative Ideas, and Create a Strong Project Foundation

First, the highly integrative project design and methodological richness with an innovative and multifaceted theoretical perspective have allowed the SHLHP to have many scientific successes. The project has been able to ask important research questions and to answer them in innovative ways. An example of one successful research project, briefly mentioned earlier, is our work on the etiology of stunting, which affects hundreds of millions of children globally. By using a longitudinal data collection protocol with multiple minimally invasive biomarkers, we were able to map physiological tradeoffs between linear growth (in lower leg length and overall height) and immune activity (with CRP, immunoglobulin E [IgE], and immunoglobulin G [IgG]) (Urlacher et al. 2018). This research would not have been possible without the foundation and infrastructure created by the SHLHP, including establishment of the field site and well‐developed social relationships nurtured over many years. Furthermore, this research required access to a biomarker lab that was able to support assay development, as well as storage and analysis of biological samples.

Another example, which illustrates the power of a research design at the intersection of human biology, evolutionary psychology, human behavioral ecology, and evolutionary psychology, and which allows us to connect behavior, physiology, and health using evolutionary and biocultural theoretical lenses, is our work on disgust and health (Cepon‐Robins et al. 2021). This research project tested the hypothesis that disgust is an emotion evolved to regulate the avoidance of pathogen exposure, a proposal that dates to Darwin (1872) but which had never been fully tested. Using a unique dataset that included psychological measures, detailed information on MI, minimally invasive biomarkers, and measures of parasite burden, we found support for Darwin's hypothesis, as well as evidence that local, social, and environmental conditions calibrate the disgust mechanism (Cepon‐Robins et al. 2021). Both studies were published in high‐profile journals and received considerable media attention.

Our recommendations that follow from Conclusion #1 include that projects like the SHLHP require integrative training, a robust data collection and database strategy, and an overarching project structure that allows for innovative ideas. We recommend exposing students to diverse methods and theoretical perspectives early in their training, supporting them in finding their niche, and firmly connecting their skills and interests to the rest of the project team; the more connected and cross‐trained they are the better. Relatedly, creating a supportive, collaborative team that allows flexibility to pursue innovative ideas as well as assistance navigating the various research challenges is crucial. An effective strategy that we have used since the beginning of SHLHP is to have broad overarching project goals (established by LSS and JJS), but then fostering and supporting a grassroots approach that harnesses the exciting ideas coming from students—who receive initial training and guidance assisting others with their data collection—that then determines the year‐to‐year emphasis and investment in the project. The initial ideas for the two projects discussed in the previous paragraphs came from graduate students developing their doctoral research (TJCR at the University of Oregon and SSU at Harvard). Finally, given the complexity and heterogeneity of the project data, we emphasize the need for database strategies and project personnel who can design and manage data and assist others in its analysis for projects of this scope. Starting off with this systematic approach—and appreciating its importance—will minimize problems later and will help avoid the need for backtracking or losing valuable data.

Conclusion and Recommendation #2: Anticipate the Costs and Challenges of Fieldwork and Plan Accordingly

Second, it is extremely challenging to keep a project like SHLHP operating year after year—it takes a team! Major sustained commitment of time to the project by many different people is essential, as is a continued infusion of funds to maintain the fieldsite and the lab. Over the course of the project, all main project personnel have been pulled in different directions by other job‐related and personal demands, as well as having their lives disrupted and fieldwork plans canceled by the COVID‐19 pandemic; this has made it difficult to consistently dedicate adequate time to all the various components required to maintain the SHLHP. This is particularly true regarding the challenge of continued presence at the fieldsite, which essentially requires putting our personal lives on hold for weeks or months at a time, year after year. We are the first to recognize our own limitations and the project's missed opportunities. We are sometimes frustrated by not being able to do more to scale up the project and to be more successful in our health outreach efforts, both in terms of our desire to reach more people and to better provide support and continuity of investment over time. We have also struggled to collect health information that requires high‐resolution data (e.g., diet and reproductive hormone profiles in women) because of the investment it would require doing so and the challenges imposed in this environment. To a certain extent this has limited our science, something that has been noted by grant and manuscript reviewers over the years. However, we recognize that larger structural issues (in addition to personal demands) impose restrictions on what we can and cannot do: there are tradeoffs to everything. Furthermore, we have been careful not to impose research models used in other settings that could potentially create problems among Shuar; for example, potential conflicts could arise with a larger, more permanent presence in one but not another Shuar community. Ultimately, this carefully conceptualized and managed collaborative project and series of research and outreach efforts have worked for our Shuar collaborators and partners and for us.

Our recommendation that follows from this second conclusion is when starting a major fieldwork project, it is critically important to embark on this work with eyes wide open to the enormous costs and challenges. It is also essential to anticipate and, whenever possible, plan for disruptions created by the ebbs and flows of the lives of the researchers on the team, and to be realistic with participants regarding the likelihood of when you will return. While predicting the next pandemic or the future shifts in university budgets is impossible, what can be done is to expect new challenges to emerge, pursue creative strategies for partnerships and funding, and to build in flexibility and excess capacity in terms of human power. Another important point is to recognize that individual researchers and even collaborative teams cannot do everything, and there will inevitably and regularly be choices that must be made about where to place energy and resources.

Conclusion and Recommendation #3: Fieldwork Should Be Tailored to Local Customs, Concerns, and Histories

It is critical to prioritize long‐term investment in research communities while recognizing the need to tailor science and outreach to local conditions. Our experience over 20 years with the SHLHP shows that while fieldwork experience in different locations and familiarity with best practices are useful as starting points, they are no substitute for experience in a particular region and with a particular group of people. There really is no one‐size‐fits‐all approach to human biology (or any) fieldwork, and there is no way we could have effectively initiated this project without the underlying ethnographic fieldwork and local relationships cultivated by LSS nor continued it without the social relationships and participant observation by members of the SHLHP team. We have invested heavily in understanding Shuar culture and local conditions and constantly work to balance local needs and our own interests and desires as researchers, within the broader context of Shuar culture. This has included a prime directive of not doing harm, which has meant avoiding extractive research and carefully thinking through benefits and negative consequences of our presence at participant, community, and larger Shuar population levels. More than anything, we have attempted to build respectful, trusting, and mutually beneficial relationships with communities and individuals.

Our final recommendation has two parts. The first is to emphasize the importance of local conditions and that you can only effectively work at the community level when people want you there. This means investing in communities from the outset and taking the time to understand a region's people without expecting that they will want you there. This requires an understanding of local customs, concerns, and histories, and then building trust and establishing relationships that are mutually beneficial. This process takes time, and is ongoing, and it takes recognition that you must walk away if your presence is not welcome or causing problems (even if it has nothing to do with your presence or research, per se). A second part of this recommendation is to be flexible and to expect and adapt to regular change. Nothing stands still for very long—field communities will change over time because of social and economic shifts, emigration and immigration, global events such as pandemics, and political relationships between communities and countries. The scientific and health questions will also change over time both because of scientific advances and practices, but also because of shifts in disease patterns and health concerns. Success over time will depend on the ability to pivot based on new ideas, harness ideas from students and community members, and to respond to the needs, desires, and wisdom of collaborating communities and leadership.
