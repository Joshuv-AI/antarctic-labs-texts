Introduction

Definition of hemophilia

Hemophilia is a recessive X-linked bleeding disorder caused by a deficiency in blood-clotting factors. Severity is classified based on factor levels: severe (<1 IU/dL), moderate (1–5 IU/dL), and mild (>5 IU/dL). Hemophilia A (HA) results from mutations in the Factor VIII (F8) gene, an 186 kb DNA sequence on Xq28, encoding a protein of 2,351 amino acids organized into A1-A2-B-A3-C1-C2 domains. Each domain plays a distinct role in the coagulation cascade, and mutations disrupting these interactions impair normal clotting.[1]

Genetic basis and inheritance

HA and hemophilia B (HB) are X-linked recessive disorders caused by mutations in the F8 and F9 genes, respectively. HA affects approximately 1 in 5,000 males, while HB affects 1 in 10,000. Males with a single mutated X chromosome express the disease fully, whereas females are typically carriers, with potential mild symptoms due to X-chromosome inactivation. Sporadic cases often arise from de novo mutations in gametes, highlighting the importance of genetic testing for accurate family counseling.[23456]

X-linked inheritance and role of sex chromosomes

The pattern of X-linked inheritance explains why affected males transmit the gene to all their daughters, but not to their sons. Skewed X-chromosome inactivation in females can occasionally result in symptomatic carriers, emphasizing the need for careful evaluation in clinical practice.[78]

Genetic variants in hemophilia A and B

Mutations range from large deletions, nonsense mutations, and intron inversions in F8 to missense and promoter variants in F9. The type of mutation correlates with disease severity and guides treatment planning. Databases such as European association for hemophilia and allied disorders (EAHAD) catalog thousands of pathogenic variants, serving as essential resources for precision medicine.[9]

Clinical and patient benefits of this study

This study provides significant implications for both practitioners and patients by bridging molecular genetics with clinical management of hemophilia. For clinicians and laboratory practitioners, understanding the underlying genetic variants of the F8 and F9 genes enables more accurate diagnosis, stratification of disease severity, and prediction of inhibitor development risk. The identification of specific mutations allows for precision-based therapeutic planning, optimizing factor replacement or non-factor therapies tailored to each patient’s molecular profile. Moreover, integrating genetic data into clinical decision-making enhances the accuracy of carrier detection, guides prenatal and preimplantation genetic diagnosis, and improves genetic counseling for affected families.

For patients and their families, this study contributes to earlier and more accurate diagnosis, reducing the diagnostic delay often associated with bleeding disorders. Genetic confirmation of hemophilia type and variant informs personalized treatment regimens that improve efficacy and reduce treatment-related complications such as inhibitor formation. Additionally, awareness of carrier status among female relatives enables proactive reproductive choices, and early monitoring for potential bleeding symptoms. By promoting targeted interventions and family-based screening, this study’s findings ultimately improve patient outcomes, enhance quality-of-life, and reduce the overall disease burden in affected communities.

Methods and Materials

Genetic testing and diagnosis

The genetic assessment of HA and HB can be performed using two principal approaches: linkage analysis and direct mutation detection.

1. Linkage Analysis

Linkage analysis is employed primarily in families with multiple affected members (familial hemophilia). This method investigates microsatellite variable number tandem repeat (VNTR) markers or single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the FVIII or FIX gene to track inheritance patterns. When applied in familial cases, linkage analysis can achieve up to 99% accuracy. However, in sporadic cases, it primarily serves to exclude carrier status in females.[10]

The heterozygosity of polymorphic markers in the index mother is essential for informative linkage analysis. Sequential evaluation of multiple FVIII gene polymorphisms, considering population-specific heterozygosity rates, is necessary due to ethnic and geographic variation in allele frequencies.[111213]

2. Direct Mutation Detection

Direct mutation detection identifies the exact pathogenic variant in the FVIII or FIX gene. It has an accuracy approaching 100% and is applicable in both familial and sporadic cases.[1415] Techniques include polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the gene followed by mutation screening using DNA sequencing, denaturing high-performance liquid chromatography, long-distance PCR, multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA), or direct sequencing.[1617181920] Combining these techniques allows high detection rates across severe, moderate, and mild hemophilia cases.

3. Molecular Genetic Testing

Targeted Mutation Analysis: Specific inversions such as the F8 intron 22-A inversion account for ~ 40% of severe hemophilia cases. Detection uses long-range or inverted PCR and, in some cases, Southern blotting.[18]Sequence and Variant Analysis: Large deletions, insertions, frameshifts, splice site modifications, nonsense, and missense mutations are commonly detected. Sanger sequencing and Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) cover coding regions, intron-exon boundaries, and regulatory regions. MLPA confirms copy number variations (CNVs).[3720]

Genetic Testing

Affected males’ genetic testing

The following justify genetic testing in guys with hemophilia.

The diagnosis of hemophilia through prenatal testing of male fetuses is essential for managing labor and delivery effectively. Understanding mutations in hemophilia is crucial, particularly in carriers with no family history, as it helps predict disease severity. Genotyping the carrier, fetus, or child allows anticipation of the clinical phenotype, facilitating early diagnosis and management. Additionally, distinguishing between mild HA and type 2N von Willebrand Disease (vWD) is critical, which can be achieved by analyzing the relationship between von Willebrand Factor (vWF) and factor VIII, factor VIII binding genotyping, or factor VIII genotyping. A cost-effective approach involves genotyping to evaluate factor VIII or the vWF factor VIII binding domain. Furthermore, identifying mutations in factor VIII or factor IX genes can assess the likelihood of developing factor VIII and factor IX inhibitors. Lastly, genotyping HA and B patients is vital for providing affordable carrier testing to at-risk female family members.

DNA testing on high-risk females

The following justifies genetic testing in hemophilia-affected females.

In families with hemophilia, genetic testing is often conducted to determine the genotype for future prenatal diagnosis rather than focusing solely on assessing carrier status in mothers and daughters of affected men. This testing is also performed on at-risk relatives, such as siblings with HA, maternal aunts, or maternal cousins, to establish their carrier status for both familial and sporadic hemophilia. Such evaluations are essential for providing optimal prenatal counseling and testing when necessary. Additionally, genotyping is crucial in predicting the severity of disease in symptomatic carriers without a family history of hemophilia if they have a male child who develops hemophilia.[49]

Hemophilia A genetic testing approach

Genetic test approach for affected males

Affected males underwent the following genetic testing.

A systematic approach to genetic testing is necessary to confirm a diagnosis of HA. Pre-test counseling should be provided to discuss the testing process’s risks, benefits, potential outcomes, costs, and clinical value. If severe blood clotting issues are observed and no intron 22 inversion mutation is detected, testing for an intron 1 inversion mutation should be conducted. For patients exhibiting moderate to severe symptoms without evidence of inversion mutations, it is appropriate to analyze relevant regions of the factor VIII gene, including the promoter, splice junctions, and coding areas. Post-test counseling is crucial to review the test results, their applicability, and their clinical significance, ensuring patients and families understand the findings and their implications.[50]

Genetic testing methods for at-risk females

When factor VIII activity is low in at-risk females, it typically indicates carrier status but does not confirm it. Molecular genetic tests are necessary for diagnosis. Families with known hemophilia usually share the same mutations, while about 30% of sporadic cases involve novel mutations. Thus, focused carrier screening for at-risk females is essential, especially if a male relative has the same genotype [Figure 1].

An algorithm for genetic testing of hemophilia patients at risk of transporter. If testing shows that neither the proband nor the carrier has a mutation, request linkage analysis.[51]

A stepwise approach is essential to confirm a family member’s diagnosis of HA. Pre-test counseling should thoroughly address the estimated risks of carrying a mutation, the likelihood of detecting it, and the clinical relevance of genetic testing findings. Targeted testing for the specific factor VIII mutation is recommended for at-risk females with a known familial genotype. If the family genotype is unknown, efforts should focus on determining the clinical phenotype. If the clinical phenotype is unclear or indicates a severe condition, testing for the inversion mutation of intron 22 should be performed, followed by intron 1 inversion testing if the results are negative. Further analysis of the factor VIII gene is warranted if the phenotype suggests mild to severe disease or if inversion tests are negative. For asymptomatic female adoptees with an uncertain family history of HA or B, testing for clotting factors VIII and IX is advised, starting with protocols for HA and progressing to HB if results are negative. Post-test counseling is crucial to explain the results, their clinical significance, and their application in ongoing patient management.[52]

Differential diagnosis

Hemophilia must be distinguished from Fabry disease, scurvy, von Willebrand disease, Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, and platelet function disorders. Testing for von Willebrand factor and antigen levels, as well as assessment of coagulation factor interactions, is essential.[53]

Results

Clotting factor replacement therapy

Factor VIII replacement (Hemophilia A)

HA, caused by factor VIII (FVIII) deficiency, affects approximately 1 in 5,000 male births and is characterized by recurrent joint and muscle bleeding, leading to arthropathy, and reduced quality-of-life.[23] Clotting factor replacement therapy, administered as plasmatic FVIII or recombinant FVIII, remains the cornerstone of treatment. Conventional therapy includes on-demand administration during bleeding episodes, as well as prophylactic regimens to prevent recurrent bleeds.[5455] Historical developments, from whole-blood transfusions to plasma fractionation and at-home cryoprecipitate infusions, have significantly improved patient outcomes.[565758]

Factor IX replacement (Hemophilia B)

HB results from factor IX (FIX) deficiency, a vitamin K-dependent serine protease. FIX replacement therapy involves prophylactic or on-demand infusions to maintain target plasma levels: 20%–40% for mild bleeds and 60%–80% for severe bleeds or surgery [Table 1].[5960]

Prophylactic vs. On-demand Treatment

Prophylactic therapy

Prophylactic therapy aims to maintain clotting factor trough levels above 1 IU/dL, distinguishing between mild (5%–40%), moderate (1–5%), and severe (<1%) hemophilia. Mortality correlates with disease severity: severe hemophilia has a 43.6% death rate, whereas mild and moderate cases have 17.4%, and 21.7%, respectively.[51616263646566676869]

On-demand therapy

On-demand treatment focuses on promptly stopping bleeding to prevent long-term joint damage. Despite improved clinical outcomes, therapy is often guided by clinician judgment rather than standardized protocols, with dosing variations between 3.7 and 30 IU/kg depending on bleed severity.[7071]

Bypassing agents for inhibitor patients

Persistent inhibitory antibodies against FVIII or FIX complicate therapy in 20–30% of HA patients and nearly all HB patients. Bypassing agents such as recombinant FVIIa (rFVIIa) and FEIBA are used to circumvent inhibitor activity, as conventional replacement therapy is ineffective. The highest risk of inhibitor formation occurs within the first 50–100 exposure days.[72]

Gene therapy

Gene therapy has shown significant efficacy in treating monogenic hemophilia. In a study cohort of 100 patients across the severity spectrum—45% severe, 30% moderate, and 25% mild—the median annualized bleed rate (ABR) decreased from 6.72 before advanced interventions to 1.32 following gene therapy among treated patients (n = 20). Inhibitor formation occurred predominantly within the first ~ 50 exposure days (EDs), accounting for about 20% of inhibitor-positive cases, and declined sharply after 100 EDs. Patients receiving prophylactic regimens or pharmacokinetic-based dosing experienced fewer breakthrough bleeds compared with historical on-demand protocols. Overall, gene therapy led to approximately 90% reductions in ABR, Annualized Spontaneous Bleeding Rate (AsBR), and Annualized Joint Bleeding Rate (AJBR), substantially improving quality of life for patients without inhibitors, although ethical and cost considerations remain, particularly for patients with liver disease, children, or pre-existing antibodies [Table 2, Figures 2–4].[73747576777879]

Supportive care

Pharmacokinetic-based prophylaxis

Individualized prophylaxis based on pharmacokinetics, age, and activity level optimizes FVIII and FIX levels, preventing bleeds and joint damage.[80]

Imaging for surveillance

Early detection of joint pathology is recommended annually for adults and biennially for children. Imaging modalities include X-ray (WFH Pettersson and Arnold-Hilgartner scores), MRI for soft tissue evaluation, and ultrasound scoring systems like HEADUS.[80]

Physiotherapy and physical therapy

Physiotherapy is critical for joint health, including exercise guidance, RICE protocols, and strength/proprioception training. Despite its importance, access to physiotherapy remains inconsistent across countries.[808182]

Discussion

Advances in molecular genetics and prenatal diagnosis of hemophilia

Modern molecular genetics has revolutionized the diagnosis and prevention of HA and B. Prenatal testing methods—such as chorionic villus sampling (CVS), amniocentesis, and percutaneous umbilical blood sampling (PUBS)—allow early identification of fetuses carrying F8 or F9 gene mutations.[9] However, their invasive nature poses fetal risks. Non-invasive prenatal diagnostics (NIPD), using cell-free fetal nucleic acids (cffNA) from maternal plasma, now provide safer and earlier disease detection, as early as the fifth week of gestation.[2324] Genetic testing remains fundamental to effective management. Molecular characterization of F8 and F9 mutations confirms diagnosis, predicts severity, and guides individualized treatment.[52] Differentiation from disorders with overlapping symptoms—such as von Willebrand disease or Ehlers-Danlos syndrome—is vital.[53] Targeted genotyping of at-risk females allows accurate carrier identification and reproductive counseling, forming a cornerstone of personalized care.

Clinically, hemophilia presents with spontaneous bleeding, hemarthrosis, and deep muscle hematomas, leading to arthropathy if untreated.[252741] Severe cases may involve central nervous system or gastrointestinal hemorrhages with significant morbidity and mortality.[3739] Laboratory assays, including aPTT, chromogenic FVIII/FIX assays, and Bethesda inhibitor testing, remain essential for diagnosis and monitoring.[4345]

Therapeutic advancements have centered on replacement therapy using plasma-derived or recombinant factors.[5758] Prophylaxis, maintaining FVIII/FIX trough levels above 1 IU/dL, effectively prevents spontaneous bleeding and joint damage.[6364] However, inhibitor development—occurring in 20%–30% of patients with HA—remains a major challenge.[72] In such cases, bypassing agents like recombinant activated factor VII (rFVIIa) and FEIBA provide effective hemostasis. Gene therapy marks a paradigm shift in hemophilia treatment. Adeno-associated virus (AAV)–mediated gene delivery of F8 or F9 has shown durable expression and substantial reductions in bleeding rates in phase III trials.[7378] While promising, challenges persist regarding long-term efficacy, vector immunity, and cost.[747576] Ongoing post-marketing surveillance and optimization of dosing protocols are crucial for safety and equity.

Comprehensive care extends beyond pharmacotherapy. Physiotherapy and musculoskeletal imaging using MRI or ultrasound (HEADUS scoring) are essential for managing arthropathy.[8082] Pain management should combine factor replacement with non-opioid or, when necessary, opioid analgesics to preserve function.[8283] Multidisciplinary care—including hematologists, genetic counselors, physiotherapists, and psychologists—optimizes both physical and psychosocial outcomes.[80848586]

Conclusion

Molecular genetic innovations have revolutionized the diagnosis, prevention, and treatment of HA and B. Early genetic screening, prenatal and preimplantation diagnostics, and targeted counseling now empower families to make informed reproductive choices. Therapeutically, the transition from plasma-derived concentrates to recombinant and gene-based treatments has dramatically enhanced survival and quality-of-life.

Yet, significant challenges persist—particularly inhibitor formation, unequal access to therapies, and the high economic burden of advanced treatments. Continued research into cost-effective prophylaxis, improved gene therapy vectors, and accessible physiotherapy services is essential. The integration of precision medicine, patient education, and comprehensive multidisciplinary care offers the most promising path toward achieving optimal long-term outcomes for individuals living with hemophilia.
