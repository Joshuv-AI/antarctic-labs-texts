Genome-wide identification and immunological expression analysis of the C3HC4-type zinc finger protein genes in the silkworm, Bombyx Mori
Authors: Jiang Liu, Xin An, Jiahui Jiang, Shidong He, Yu Ming, Meng Chen, Irfan Muhammad, Yanlu Qi, Gaiqun Huang, Guoqing Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ubiquitous C3HC4 nucleic acid-binding motif is widely distributed across diverse organisms. C3HC4-type RING finger genes constitute a large family and are essential for plant development as well as responses to biotic and abiotic stress. Zinc finger protein domains exhibit considerable variation, indicating that most proteins have unique binding sites and functions. However, the binding sites and functions of most C3HC4-ZF proteins are still largely unknown. In this study, 32 C3HC4 genes were identified in the silkworm (B. mori). Phylogenetic analysis showed that C3HC4-ZF protein genes in the silkworm can be grouped into four main clusters, with the 32 genes distributed across 18 chromosomes. Tissue-specific expression patterns were observed for some C3HC4-ZF protein genes in larval tissues of silkworms. Notably, several C3HC4-ZF genes were found to be expressed explicitly during silk gland development. Four genes—BMZFPC3HC43, BMZFPC3HC411, BMZFPC3HC413, and BMZFPC3HC417 were selected for real-time quantitative PCR analysis in the silk gland. The results showed distinct expression patterns among these genes. Additionally, given the high expression of C3HC4 zinc finger protein genes in the fat body, qRT-PCR was performed to investigate the expression profiles of six C3HC4-ZF genes in the fat body after exposure to pathogenic microorganisms. The results indicated significant differences in their expression patterns, suggesting a potential role in the immune response. These findings provide a foundation for further functional studies of C3HC4-ZF genes in Lepidoptera insects. Moreover, the results offer valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying silk gland development and immunity in the silkworm.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-12605-1.

Introduction

Transcription factors (TFs) are key regulators of gene expression [1]. They are defined by the presence of DNA or RNA-binding domains, with some also containing additional effector domains. TFs participate in various biological processes, including development, metabolism, apoptosis, and autophagy [2–5]. The RING (Really Interesting New Genes) finger is a unique zinc finger domain [6], found in proteins involved in signal transduction and regulatory pathways [7–8]. The RING finger domain is classified into typical and modified types based on their cysteine and histidine residue arrangements. The C3HC4-type RING finger is a subclass following the canonical RING finger structure. It is a cysteine-rich domain consisting of 40–60 amino acid that coordinate two zinc ions [9–11]. The C3HC4 zinc finger (C3HC4-ZF) protein exhibits a relatively simple structural organization [12]. It is characterized by four pairs of double ligands that facilitate the binding of two zinc ions. This domain exhibits versatile molecular interactions, allowing it to bind not only to DNA but also to RNA, proteins, and lipids [13]. Moreover, C3HC4-ZF proteins are involved in crucial biological functions, such as gene transcription, recombination, and intercellular signaling. They play significant roles in protein-protein interactions and activation processes. Many C3HC4-ZF proteins act as E3 ubiquitin ligases, facilitating the transfer of ubiquitin to specific target proteins [14].

C3HC4 zinc finger protein genes represent an important class of regulatory factors that have been extensively studied in both plants and vertebrates; however, their biological functions in insects remain poorly understood [15–16]. Recent research indicates that members of the C3HC4 zinc finger protein gene family are extensively involved in mammalian physiological and pathological processes. For example, in humans, RBCK1 has been identified as a critical E3 ubiquitin ligase. Loss of RBCK1 function leads to abnormal polyglucosan accumulation, resulting in progressive cardiomyopathy. Most affected patients require heart transplantation and generally do not survive beyond early adulthood [17–19]. Blake et al.. further identified donor splice site variants in RBCK1 through whole-genome sequencing, confirming that such mutations cause loss of functional protein [20], thereby promoting glycogen accumulation and congestive heart failure. In addition to its role in metabolic regulation, RBCK1 is also implicated in tumorigenesis. Zhang et al.. reported that under hypoxic conditions, HIF-1α can induce RBCK1 expression, which then promotes the degradation of PICK1 via the ubiquitin proteasome pathway, thereby facilitating the growth of nasopharyngeal carcinoma [21]. Moreover, RBCK1 can interact with MTSS1 to mediate the ubiquitination and degradation of p65, negatively regulating the NF-κB signaling pathway and thus suppressing tumor development [22]. Other members of this family also participate in various key biological processes. For example, Rnf112 plays an important role in mouse embryonic development and adult brain function, influencing motor coordination, spatial learning, and memory [23]. Znf230 may function as a germ cell-specific transcriptional regulator involved in the precise regulation of gene expression during spermatogenesis [24]. ZNF645 is proposed to act as an E3 ubiquitin ligase, contributing to the production and quality control of human sperm [25]. Although members of the C3HC4 zinc finger protein gene family are involved in many aspects such as metabolism, cardiac function, tumorigenesis, neural development, and reproductive regulation, systematic research and functional analysis in insects are still lacking.

The silkworm, Bombyx mori, is an insect of significant economic importance and has been domesticated in China for over 5,000 years [26]. As a well-established model organism, it contributes notably to research in fields such as microbiology, physiology, and genetics [27–28]. In this study, we identified the C3HC4-type zinc finger (C3HC4-ZF) protein gene family in the silkworm through bioinformatics and molecular biology approaches. We systematically investigated the evolutionary relationships, gene structures, physicochemical properties, conserved domains, tissue-specific expression patterns, and stress responses of its members. Our findings provide valuable insights into the C3HC4-ZF protein gene family and establish a foundation for further functional characterization. Additionally, this work provides valuable insights into the roles of transcription factors in silkworms and other Lepidopteran insects.

Materials and methods

Identification and analysis of the C3HC4-ZF genes in B. mori

The genome sequence was obtained from the silkworm genome database Kaikobase (http://sgp.dna.affrc.go.jp/index.html) [29]. The hidden Markov model (HMM) of the C3HC4-ZF (PF00097) domain was downloaded from the Pfam database (https://pfam.xfam.org) [30]. The HMMER software (http://www.hmmer.org/), an online tool, was used to search for the C3HC4-ZF protein in the silkworm genome, with a predicted value lower than 0.01 was used [31]. The candidate proteins containing C3HC4-ZF domains were identified using the SMART online tool (http://smart.embl-heidelberg.de/) [32]. A BLASTP search was performed on the non-redundant (nr) protein database to annotate the C3HC4-ZF protein genes.

Chromosomal distribution and domain analysis of the C3HC4-ZF protein genes

Chromosomal distribution was mapped using MapChart 2.32 software [33]. Conserved domains were analyzed with the scan program [31]. The C3HC4-ZF protein sequences of B. mori, Danaus plexippus, Drosophila melanogaster, Caenorhabditis elegans, and Mus musculus were obtained from NCBI based on the hidden Markov model (HMM) profile of the C3HC4-ZF domain, using default parameters. The identified insect zinc finger protein sequences were aligned using ClustalX [34], while the MUSCLE program was employed for multiple sequence comparison [35], a phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA 7.0 software [36]. The comparison results were derived using the bootstrap method with 1,000 replicates [37].

Analysis of gene structure, domain organization

Information regarding B. mori C3HC4 genes, including coding sequences (CDS), chromosomal locations, the number of exons and introns, ORF length, and amino acid (AA), composition, was obtained from the KAIKO database. The molecular weight (kDa) and isoelectric point (PI) of each gene were calculated using the online ExPASy tools (http://www.expasy.org/tools/) [38]. The exon-intron structure was visualized utilizing the Gene Structure Display Server (GSDS) (http://gsds.cbi.pku.edu.cn/) [39]. Protein sequence motifs were identified through the MEME online platform (http://meme-suite.org/index.html), followed by downloading the conserved sequences and visualizing the results [40].

Expression profiling analysis

The genome-wide microarray data were downloaded from the SilkDB Gene Expression Atlas (MtGEA) Project website (https://silkdb.bioinfotoolkits.net/). The expression levels across multiple tissues from the third day of the fifth instar larvae, including head, fat body, testis, ovary, integument, midgut, hemocytes, malpighian tubules, the anterior, middle and posterior silk glands, were obtained. The expression array figures were created using the Pheatmap program from the R package [41].

Total RNA extraction and qRT–PCR

The B. mori P50 strain, provided by the Anhui Key Laboratory of Resource Insect Biology and Innovative Utilization, was reared in our laboratory. Larvae were reared following the standard method described by Liu et al. [42]. Anterior/middle and posterior silk gland (tissues collected at different times) were obtained by dissecting P50, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 ℃ for RNA extraction. Total RNA was extracted using Trizol Reagent (Takara, Dalian, China), and first-strand cDNA was synthesized using the Prime Script™ One Step RT-PCR Kit Ver. 2 (Takara, Dalian, China), then stored at -80 ℃ for later use. The primers for qRT-PCR are listed in Table 1. qRT-PCR was performed using the PerfectStart™ Green qPCR SuperMix (TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) in a 20 µL system. mRNA expression levels were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method [43]. The B. mori 18 S rRNA gene served as an internal reference for normalization. Each experiment included three independent biological replicates.

Immune challenge assays

Fifth-instar B. mori larvae on day 3 were anesthetized on ice for 20 min and randomly divided into five groups of 30 larvae each [44]. The gram-negative bacterium E. coli Trans1-T1 (5 µL, 109 cfu/mL), the gram-positive bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (5 µL, 109 cfu/mL), the fungus Coccidioides albicans (5 µL, 109 cfu/mL), and B. mori nuclear polyhedrosis virus (5 µL, 109 PIBs/mL) were heat-inactivated and injected into the abdomens of the larvae respectively. The control group was injected with an equal volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). After injection, wounds were immediately sealed with Vaseline and kept at normal conditions, and larval fat bodies were collected at 1.5, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h, respectively. The fat bodies collected at each period were mixed and three replicates were taken. Finally, total RNA was extracted and quantitative RT-PCR was performed for transcript analysis.

Data analysis

The calculated data were processed using Excel software, and ANOVA was performed with SPSS. The average and standard deviation from at least three separate experiments were used to describe each. Statistical significance was evaluated using the T-test and one-way ANOVA. A significant difference was found at P < 0.05.

Results

Identification and chromosomal distribution of the C3HC4-ZF protein gene family

In this study, a total of 32 uniqueC3HC4-ZF protein genes were confirmed in B. mori and named in an abbreviated format as BMZFC3HC4 (B. mori C3HC4-type zinc fingers). A comparative analysis across species showed a wide variation in the number of these genes, with 127 in M. musculus, 44 in D. plexippus, 28 in D. melanogaster, and 25 in C. elegans (Fig. 1). The data indicate that closely related species tend to have a more similar number of C3HC4-ZF genes. This pattern not only reflects the evolutionary diversity of this protein family but also supports the accuracy of our identification method.

A chromosomal distribution map of the C3HC4-ZF protein genes was created for the silkworm, based on their annotated start positions. As shown in Fig. 2, members of the BMZFC3HC4 protein genes family were spread widely and unevenly across the silkworm’s 28 chromosomes. Notably, a significant clustering of BMZFC3HC4 protein genes was observed on chromosome 10, which covers about 19.76 mega bases (MB) and contains 6 C3HC4-ZF protein genes, making up nearly one-fifth of BMZFC3HC4 protein genes. In contrast, only one C3HC4-ZF protein gene was found on each of chromosomes 1, 3, 7, 8, 12, 17, and 22. Additionally, no C3HC4-ZF protein genes were detected on nine chromosomes, while the remaining 10 chromosomes each contained 2 BMZFC3HC4 protein genes, for a total of 20 genes across those chromosomes. This distribution pattern highlights the non-random organization of BMZFC3HC4 protein genes and hints at possible functional or evolutionary importance related to their chromosomal localization.

Structural analysis and phylogenetic tree of the BMZFC3HC4 family

Using online analysis tools, the chromosomal positions of the BMZFC3HC4 genes were identified, and the genes were systematically numbered from BMZFC3HC4 1 to BMZFC3HC4 32. Detailed information for each BMZFC3HC4 protein gene, including gene name, protein ID, chromosome location, amino acid length, isoelectric point (pI), molecular weight, subcellular localization, number of C3HC4 zinc finger motifs, and exon count, is provided in Table 2. This variation in protein length aligns with observations in other animal and plant species, such as Medicago truncatula [45]. The isoelectric points of these proteins also exhibited a broad range, from 4.50 (BMZFPC3HC417) to 10.53 (BMZFPC3HC44), with approximately 50% of the proteins having pIs between 6.0 and 8.0. This suggest that most of the C3HC4-ZF proteins in B. mori are composed of neutral amino acids. Similarly, the molecular weights of these proteins varied considerably, ranging from 26.21 kDa (BMZFPC3HC49) to 173.66 kDa (BMZFPC3HC45). Interestingly, only one protein, BMZFPC3HC46, contained three C3HC4-ZF motifs, while all other proteins possessed only one. Subcellular localization analysis predicted that approximately 80% of the C3HC4-ZF protein family members are localized in the nucleus. This finding directly supports the accuracy of the identified protein sequences and highlights their potential roles in transcriptional regulation. Altogether, these findings provide valuable insights into the structural and functional diversity of the C3HC4-ZF protein family in B. mori. In this study, the C3HC4-ZF motif was identified in all 32 predicted C3HC4-ZF proteins in B. mori. Additionally, the number of C3HC4-ZF protein motifs in D. plexippus, D. melanogaster, C. elegans, and M. musculus was also determined (Fig. 3). As shown in Fig. 3, a total of 34 C3HC4-ZF motifs were found across the 32 C3HC4-ZF proteins in B. mori. This number is comparable to those in D. melanogaster (44 motifs), D. plexippus (28 motifs), and C. elegans (25 motifs), but much lower than in M. musculus (127 motifs). According to the spacer numbers between the Cys residues, the C3HC4-ZF motif could be divided into different patterns. The most prevalent C3HC4-ZF motifs observed across the species include C-X2-C-X12-C-X-H-X2-C-X2-C-X10-C-X2-C, C-X2-C-X11-C-X-H-X3-C-X2-C-X10-C-X2-C, and C-X2-C-X12-C-X-H-X2-C-X2-C-X12-C-X2-C, with corresponding percentages of 25.7%, 57.1%, and 17.2% in B. mori, Interestingly, the motif pattern C-X2-C-X11-C-X-H-X2-C-X2-C-X14-C-X2-C was found only in an M. musculus C3HC4-ZF protein. Comparing of the four C3HC4-ZF motif patterns among B. mori, D. plexippus, D. melanogaster, and C. elegans shows that the number and types of C3HC4 motifs are very similar across these species. This indicates a high level of conservation in the structural arrangement of C3HC4-ZF motifs, emphasizing their potential functional importance across different organisms.

The phylogenetic tree was constructed to further investigate the evolutionary relationship among the 32 C3HC4-ZF proteins using the neighbor-joining method [46]. The finding revealed that the protein sequences could be classified into four major groups: (I) BMZFPC3HC45/7/8/9/10/11/13/16/17/20/21/22/24/25/27/30/, (II) BMZFPC3HC42/6/14/17/19/26/29, (III) BMZFPC3HC44/15/18/23/32, (III) BMZFPC3HC41/3/28/31 (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, an analysis of gene structure was performed to elucidate the evolutionary processes underlying gene diversity (Fig. 4B). The analysis indicated significant variability in the number of exons and introns among C3HC4-ZF protein genes, ranging from 1 to 22, suggesting low conservation of gene structure among different subfamilies. However, genes clustered within the same subgroup exhibited similar exon and intron configurations, although intron lengths varied considerably. Notably, BMZFPC3HC412 contained an exceptionally long exon (~ 3 kb), distinguishing it from the other genes. Protein structural characteristics are critical for assessing the conservation of amino acid residues. Motif within the C3HC4-ZF proteins were identified using MEME online software, and sequence logos for motifs 1, 2, and 3 were generated (Fig. 4D) [47]. The results demonstrated that the overall protein structures within the C3HC4-ZF family were highly conserved, with three motifs being prevalent across all 32 C3HC4-ZF proteins (Fig. 4C). Except for BMZFPC3HC415 and BMZFPC3HC428, which contained only two motifs, all other proteins possessed three motifs. Interestingly, although the motifs showed minimal variation among the 32 C3HC4-ZF proteins, each group displayed a consistent motif organization, highlighting the evolutionary conservation and functional importance of these structural features.

Tissue expression profile of BMZFPC3HC4 genes

Based on the previously reported silkworm microarray data [48], the expression patterns of BMZFPC3HC4 genes in various tissues of the 3rd day of the 5th instar silkworm larvae were examined. The expression profile analysis showed that the 32 BMZFPC3HC4 genes were active in at least three larval tissues during this developmental stage (Fig. 5). Notably, the C3HC4-ZF protein gene displayed distinct differential expression patterns across multiple tissues in both the fifth instar and third-day larvae. The expression of the C3HC4-ZF protein gene in the fat body, testis, ovary, and hemolymph is significantly higher. The fat body and hemolymph, which are primary sites of antimicrobial peptide synthesis in insects, are essential for insect immunity [49]. Additionally, the expression of gene BMZFPC3HC43/11/13/17 in silk glands is significantly higher than that of other genes, indicating that these genes may be involved in silk thread formation. Overall, these finding demonstrate a tissue-specific expression pattern of the C3HC4-ZF protein genes and suggest that BMZFPC3HC4 genes might function as regulators in the development of multiple tissues.

Analysis of the BMZFPC3HC4 gene expression in the silk gland developmental period

Based on the above analysis, it was found that BMZFPC3HC43/11/13/17 genes exhibited specific and significant up-regulation in the silk glands. Their expression patterns in the anterior, middle, and posterior silk gland tissues were examined using qRT-PCR from the 3rd day of the 4th instar to the 6th day of the 5th instar larvae. Overall, the BMZFPC3HC43 and BMZFPC3HC417 genes displayed a similar upward expression pattern. Their relative expression levels initially increased, peaked on the 0th day of the 5th instar, and subsequently decreased across the silk gland regions (anterior, middle, and posterior). Notably, the expression level of BMZFPC3HC43 was significantly higher than that of BMZFPC3HC417. The BMZFPC3HC411 and BMZFPC3HC413 genes showed similar expression patterns in the anterior and posterior silk glands, with their relative expression levels first increasing and then decreasing, peaking concurrently. However, in the middle silk gland, their patterns diverge: the expression of BMZFPC3HC411 increases and then decreases, whereas that of BMZFPC3HC413 increases continuously throughout the developmental stage (Fig. 6).

Induced expression patterns of BMZFPC3HC4 genes in the fat body

The insect immune system is mainly governed by innate immunity, which has developed unique features through continuous evolutionary processes. This immune defense system includes two main components: cellular immunity and humoral immunity [50–51]. The humoral immune response is mediated by antibacterial proteins produced and secreted by the fat body, hemolymph, and extra-embryonic cells [52–53]. Based on this analysis, genes BMZFPC3HC43/11/13/17/26/28 showed significantly higher expression levels specially in the fat body. Additionally, the expression profiles of 6 C3HC4-ZF protein genes in the fat body after exposure to pathogenic microorganisms were examined using qRT-PCR. The results showed distinct expression patterns in response to different pathogenies: the expression of six genes in larvae treated with B. thuringiensis decreased notably, reaching its peak at 1.5 h (Fig. 7). After E. coli injection, the expression of BMZFPC3HC43/11/13/17/28 was significantly up-regulated at 1.5 h, and then gradually declined. In contrast, the expression of BMZFPC3HC426 remain relatively stable. When larvae were treated with B. bassiana, BMZFPC3HC428/13/17 were strongly upregulated at 1.5 h, and stabilized after 6 h. Meanwhile, the expression levels of BMZFPC3HC43/11/13 tended to remain steady. Following BmNPV infection, BMZFPC3HC43/11/13/17/26/28 expression was upregulated significantly at 48 h. However, the expression level of the BMZFPC3HC417 gene did not fluctuate significantly. These differential expression patterns suggest that these genes may play crucial roles in pathogen resistance, with distinct regulatory mechanisms operating against various microbial challenges.

Discussion

Zinc finger proteins, despite their widespread presence in animals, remained relatively understudied, particularly regarding their diversity and functions. Current research has primarily focused on C2H2 and CCCH-type zinc finger proteins, with limited investigation into C3HC4-type variants. Notably, the study of C3HC4-ZF proteins in B. mori. Remains unexplored, representing a significant gap in our understanding of this protein family. In this study, B. mori, D. plexippus, D. melanogaster, C. elegans, and M. musculus were identified as having 32, 28, 44, 25, and 127 C3HC4-ZF protein genes, which accounted for the 0.19%, 0.18%, 0.29%, 0.09% and 0.49% of the total genes, respectively. The results demonstrate a positive correlation between species complexity and the number of C3HC4-ZF protein genes, consistent with previous observations in the identification of C2H2 zinc finger protein genes [54]. Meanwhile, the C3HC4 protein motif was identified, and 3 types of C3HC4 motif are found in B. mori (Fig. 3). Similar to other insect species, the C-X2-C-X11-C-X-H-X3-C-X2-C-X10-C-X2-C motif is the most abundant pattern (55.88%), which is comparable to D. melanogaster (61.36%), C. elegans (40%), and D. plexippus (42.29%). However, C-X2-C-X12-C-X-H-X2-C-X2-C-X10-C-X2-C accounted for the highest proportion in mice. Compared with other primates, some C3HC4 motifs of B. mori are lacking. For example, compared with M. musculus, the C-X2-C-X11-C-X-H-X2-C-X2-C-X14-C-X2-C motif is not detected in B. mori. Previous research has demonstrated that these proteins are target genes specific to primates and are key transcription factors for the development of the pancreas, eye and neocortex [55]. Beyond the C3HC4 motifs, a plethora of additional regulatory domains, such as RING, WD40, and SPRY, are also found in B. mori C3HC4 proteins. The extra domains are nearly all involved in interactions with RNA, DNA, or proteins [56–58]. This domain diversity suggests that C3HC4 proteins may participate in multiple cellular functions. Domain analysis revealed that, except BMZFPC3HC46, all identified zinc finger protein sequences contained a single C3HC4 domain, contrasting with the multi-domain structure characteristic of C2H2 zinc finger proteins [59]. Furthermore, the 44 C3HC4-type zinc finger protein genes of D. melanogaster produced 44 C3HC4-ZF proteins. Same as D. plexippus, C. elegans, and M. musculus, the number of C3HC4-ZFP genes is consistent with the number of C3HC4 domains. Therefore, the domain of the C3HC4 zinc finger protein is conserved. A one-to-one correspondence analysis between C3HC4-ZF protein genes and C3HC4 domains across D. melanogaster, D. plexippus, C. elegans and M. musculus indicates strong evolutionary conservation of the C3HC4 zinc finger protein domain. A total of 32 zinc finger protein genes were identified, with their amino acid sequence lengths showing significant divergence. BMZFC3HC45 encodes the longest protein (1,567 aa), whereas BMZFC3HC411 encodes the shortest (184 aa). Despite this overall variation, protein lengths remained relatively conserved within the same subfamily. Analysis of isoelectric points revealed that approximately 50% of the protein members fell within the range of 6.0 to 8.0, indicating that most C3HC4-ZF proteins in B. mori are neutral amino acids. This contrasts with tomato C2H2 zinc finger proteins, which are predominantly basic amino acids [59]. Gene structure analysis demonstrated considerable variation in intron/exon organization among B. mori C3HC4 genes, with the number of exons ranging from 1 to 22. The majority of sequences contained fewer than 10 exons, with an average of 8.66 exons per gene (Fig. 4B). Additionally, exon length displayed substantial variation across genes. This structural diversity suggests potential functional divergence among C3HC4 genes. However, within each subfamily, gene structure and domain organization remained relatively conserved, implying possible functional redundancy among subfamily members [44]. Chromosomal localization analysis revealed that C3HC4 gene family members are widely distributed across the genome, with members of the same subfamily typically dispersed on different chromosomes. This distribution pattern aligns with the diverse functional roles attributed to the C3HC4 gene family. The C3HC4-ZF genes exhibited distinct tissue-specific expression patterns in silkworm larvae, with significantly higher levels found in the fat body, testes, ovaries, and hemolymph. The strong expression in the gonads (testes and ovaries) is align with their role in gametogenesis—a process that requires continuous cell division and differentiation, which is tightly regulated by numerous genes and proteins. These findings align with the established functions of zinc finger proteins in coordinating diverse biological processes, including cell differentiation, development, and metabolism [60]. Notably, over 93% of the BMZFC3HC4 genes were expressed in the silk glands, with four genes showing significant up-regulation. Functional annotations of these genes suggest potential regulatory roles. Among these, BMSK0005865, an ortholog of RNF5, has been implicated in multiple cellular processes, including the regulation of selective mitochondrial autophagy [61], control of CFTR degradation [62], and modulation of cGASd activity in innate immune responses [63]. Another notable gene, BMSK0005414 (Ring Finger Protein 10), has demonstrated inhibitory effects on cell growth in both vascular smooth muscle [64] and neurons [65]. The BMSK0001024 (RNF113A) gene has been associated with promoting proliferation and survival of cervical cancer cells, enhancing cellular migration and invasion capabilities, while inhibiting apoptosis [66]. Additionally, BMSK0006484 (RNF146), an E3 ubiquitin ligase, plays a neuroprotective role by preventing PARP1-dependent neurodegeneration in Parkinson’s disease [67]. These genes exhibited specific expression patterns in the silk glands. The RNF146 (BMSK0006484) gene encodes an E3 ubiquitin ligase whose activity prevents poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase 1 (PARP1) dependent neurodegeneration in Parkinson’s disease [67]. The specific expression of these multifunctional regulators in the silk gland underscores the potential complexity of its cellular processes. To further characterize this silk gland-specific expression profile, we analyzed the spatial and temporal expression patterns of these genes in the anterior, middle, and posterior silk glands across the 4th and 5th instar larvae using qRT-PCR. The relative expression of BMZFPC3HC43/17 in the silk glands of the anterior, middle, and posterior fexhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease, reaching the highest at 5th 0 d instar. Notably, in the posterior silk gland, its expression increased significantly, peaking between day 3 of the 4th instar and day 0 of the 5th instar, before declining sharply. Significant differences in expression levels were also observed across the different gland regions at various developmental stages. In contrast, while BMZFPC3HC411 and BMZFPC3HC413 showed similar expression patterns in the anterior and posterior silk glands, their expression trends in the middle silk gland were distinct. These expression patterns align with the well-documented staged development of silkworm silk glands [68–71] and further underscore the functional diversity of C3HC4-ZF protein genes in silk gland development and function. Zinc finger proteins are crucial regulators of plant growth and development, stress responses, and other physiological processes [72–73]. While significant zinc finger proteins have been identified in rice, Arabidopsis, and other plants, their roles in silkworms remain poorly understood. Unlike vertebrates, insects lack an adaptive immune system and depend on a robust innate immune system to combat pathogenic invasions [74–75]. The fat body is particularly vital in the insect immune response [54]. Consequently, BMZFC3HC4 protein genes, which are highly expressed in the tissue, are likely pivotal in immune regulation. To explore this, six BMZFC3HC4 protein genes were selected for examination of their expression under microbial immune challenges. The study aimed to elucidate their roles in immune responses and provide a foundation for future functional research. qRT-PCR analysis revealed significant variations in the expression patterns of these genes across different microbial challenges, reflecting the diverse regulatory mechanisms of zinc finger proteins. For example, the BMZFPC3HC426 (MYLIP) gene, associated with cytoskeletal protein clusters, is involved in cell movement, migration [76], and dyslipidemia treatment [77]. The BMZFPC3HC428 (BIRC7) gene promotes the invasion and metastasis of papillary thyroid carcinoma cells by inhibiting autophagy [78]. Additionally, the BMZFPC3HC43/11/13/17 genes are critical for tissue development and immune responses. In summary, these genes likely contribute to the immune regulation of silkworms through various mechanisms, highlighting their potential importance in innate immunity.

Conclusions

In this study, the C3HC4-type zinc finger proteins of the silkworm, B. mori, were comprehensively characterized and analyzed. The findings offer valuable insights into the functional roles of zinc finger protein transcription factors (TFs) in the developmental and immunological processes of B. mori and other Lepidopteran insects. Furthermore, this research provides a significant resource for elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying silk gland formation in B. mori.
