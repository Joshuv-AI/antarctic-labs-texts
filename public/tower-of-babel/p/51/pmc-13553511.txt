CPX-351 in combination with ruxolitinib for advanced-phase myeloproliferative neoplasms: results of a phase 1 study
Authors: Shivani Handa, Yazan Madanat, Kelly Pugh, Kaitlyn Dvorak-Kornaus, Qiuhong Zhao, Alice Mims, Kristin Koenig, Andrew Srisuwananukorn, Rachel Cook, Ronan Swords, Uma Borate
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR:

Accelerated phase myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN-AP; 10%-19% blasts) and blast phase myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN-BP; ≥20% blasts) remain among the most challenging myeloid malignancies, with no established standard of care. Median overall survival (OS) in MPN-BP has ranged from 3 to 6 months with intensive chemotherapy.1, 2, 3 Less-intensive approaches, including hypomethylating agents (HMAs) with/without venetoclax, offer improved tolerability and modest survival benefit over historical controls but rarely durable remissions.4 Allogeneic stem cell transplantation (allo-SCT) remains the only curative option, although long-term survival is suboptimal (3-year OS of 36%).5

CPX-351, a liposomal formulation of daunorubicin and cytarabine delivered at a fixed 5:1 molar ratio, improved survival compared with conventional 7+3 in AML with myelodysplasia-related changes.6 However, patients with antecedent MPNs were excluded from the pivotal CPX-351 studies.6,7 Current data for CPX-351 in MPN-BP are limited to a single retrospective series, and its combination with ruxolitinib has not been explored.8 Furthermore, although JAK inhibition has been studied in combination with HMAs, no prospective trials have evaluated the combination of intensive induction with JAK inhibition.9, 10, 11

Therefore, we conducted an investigator-initiated, multicenter phase 1 study to evaluate the safety and feasibility of CPX-351 combined with ruxolitinib in patients with MPN-AP/MPN-BP. This study was approved by institutional review boards at participating centers, and all patients provided written informed consent.

CPX-351 was administered at a fixed dose of 100 U/m2 IV on days 1, 3, and 5 during induction. Ruxolitinib was administered from day 6 through day 28 at 1 of 2 dose levels (DLs): 10 mg (DL1) or 20 mg twice daily (DL2). A Bayesian optimal phase 2 design guided dose escalation to determine the recommended phase 2 dose. Dose-limiting toxicities (DLTs) were assessed during cycle 1. Bone marrow evaluation occurred between day 35 and day 42. Patients with >5% blasts could receive reinduction, whereas responders could proceed with up to 2 cycles of consolidation with CPX-351 (65 U/m2 on days 1 and 3) followed by ruxolitinib (days 4-28) at the assigned DL. The primary end point was incidence of DLTs. Secondary end points included safety, response, and OS. Responses were assessed using 2017 European LeukemiaNet (ELN) AML criteria,12 with composite complete remission (CR) defined as CR plus CR with incomplete hematologic recovery (CRi). OS was estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method.

Between March 2019 and January 2025, 12 patients with MPN-AP (n = 6) and MPN-BP (n = 6) were enrolled, with 6 treated at each ruxolitinib DL. The median age was 68.5 years (range, 45-73). After induction, 5 patients (41.7%) achieved a composite CR (CR, n = 3; CRi, n = 2), and 1 patient (8.3%) achieved a partial response. Five patients (41.7%) had stable disease, and 1 died before response assessment. Three patients had a TP53 mutation/complex karyotype, none of whom achieved a response. Two patients had previously progressed on azacitidine, and 1 had progressive disease after decitabine plus ruxolitinib, none of whom achieved a response to the trial regimen. Among responders (CR/CRi), the median duration of response was 2.9 months (range, 0.8-13.5), comparable with 3.4 months reported with the decitabine and ruxolitinib combination.9 No patient underwent reinduction; 1 patient received 2 consolidation cycles followed by 8 cycles of ruxolitinib-only maintenance. Six (50%) patients received an allo-SCT, although only 2 were bridged directly after response to the trial regimen. All patients are now off study. Reasons for discontinuation included progressive disease (n = 1), physician discretion (n = 3), alternative therapy (n = 2), transplant (n = 2), and persistent thrombocytopenia (n = 1). One DLT occurred at each DL: a grade 5 hemorrhagic stroke at DL1 and a grade 4 intraparenchymal hemorrhage at DL2 in the setting of grade 4 thrombocytopenia. Hematologic toxicities were common, as expected with intensive induction therapy in this population. The most frequent grade 3 to 5 adverse events were neutropenia (67%), febrile neutropenia (58%), thrombocytopenia (67%), and anemia (25%). The incidence of febrile neutropenia is similar to that reported with CPX-351 in the phase 3 study (68%) and the previous retrospective study in MPN-BP (58%).6,8 Median time to platelet recovery (>50 × 109/L) was 27 days (range, 18-99), whereas absolute neutrophil count recovery (>1 × 109/L) was prolonged at 50 days (range, 18-99). Grade 3 to 5 nonhematological toxicities were uncommon, with acute kidney injury, congestive heart failure, hypoxia, and respiratory failure reported in 1 patient (8%) each. Importantly, no unexpected synergistic toxicities were observed.

With a median follow-up of 17.3 months, median OS was not reached (NR) for responders (95% confidence interval, 0.6 to NR) and was 18.7 months (95% confidence interval, 2.7 to NR) for nonresponders. One patient died within 30 days because of hemorrhagic stroke. Estimated 1- and 2-year OS rates were 57% and 38%, respectively. Although limited by sample size, these outcomes compare favorably with historical cohorts and likely reflect the availability of subsequent therapies for nonresponders.1, 2, 3

To our knowledge, this is the first prospective study evaluating CPX-351 combined with ruxolitinib in MPN-AP/BP. Serious bleeding events represented the principal DLT at both ruxolitinib DLs, consistent with the known increased risk of grades 3 to 5 bleeding reported with CPX-351 vs 7+3 (11.8% vs 8.6%).6 The observed CR rate of 25% is comparable with that reported with 7+3 or similarly intensive regimens (28%-35%) as well as with CPX-351 alone (25%).4,8,13

This study was originally planned to assess 4 escalating DLs of ruxolitinib (up to 40 mg twice daily) with a fixed dose of CPX-351 but was closed early because of slow enrollment and a changing treatment landscape moving toward less-intense approaches in MPN-AP/BP. Ruxolitinib combined with decitabine has shown CR/CRi rates ranging from 8% to 41%.9, 10, 11 The utility of Bcl-2 inhibition in MPN-BP is still unclear. Although real-world analysis has shown improved CR/CRi rates (∼40% vs 20%) with the addition of venetoclax to HMA, no difference in OS was appreciated across multiple smaller retrospective studies.4,14, 15, 16, 17 A recent phase 2 trial of decitabine-venetoclax combination demonstrated improved 1-year event-free survival (32% vs 15% with HMA alone in historical controls) and median OS of 10.8 months in an exclusive MPN-BP population.18 With allo-SCT being the only potential curative option, using lower-intensity treatment with less risk of complications may be favored as a “safer” bridging strategy. Moreover, many patients are not transplant candidates because of age/comorbidities, making HMA-based regimens, administered in a cyclical manner, a more practical approach for disease control in a noncurative setting. Nonetheless, given that fewer than half of the patients with MPN-AP/BP respond to HMA with/without venetoclax, intensive regimens such as 7+3 or CPX-351 remain relevant for carefully selected, fit patients for whom remission can be consolidated by allo-SCT. Although ELN 2024 criteria can aid risk stratification in MPN-AP/BP treated with less-intensive therapy (PMID 39969200), it remains unclear whether ELN 2017/2022 criteria can identify patients most likely to benefit from intensive chemotherapy in this setting. Future studies are needed to refine patient selection, develop newer agents, and explore combination strategies incorporating less myelosuppressive JAK2-selective inhibitors, such as momelotinib or pacritinib.

Overall, our findings demonstrate the feasibility of combining CPX-351 with ruxolitinib in MPN-AP/BP, with careful monitoring for bleeding complications. Continued JAK-STAT inhibition during intensive induction may help mitigate cytokine rebound and preserve spleen and symptom control while achieving cytoreduction. However, given the small sample size and nonrandomized design, the extent to which ruxolitinib contributed to antileukemic efficacy beyond CPX-351 alone is unclear. The therapeutic role of JAK inhibition in AML arising from antecedent MPNs with previous JAK inhibitor exposure remains to be defined.
