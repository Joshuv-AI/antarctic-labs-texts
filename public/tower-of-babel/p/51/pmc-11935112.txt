Chinese expert consensus on the application of pegylated recombinant human granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor during concurrent chemoradiotherapy (2023 edition)
Authors: Jun Wang, Baosheng Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeutropenia is the most common hematological toxicity of concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT), and leads to subsequent treatment delays and/or dose reductions. Neutropenia often advances to febrile neutropenia and serious infections, which can affect the prognosis and safety of patients. The reasonable prevention and management of neutropenia is vital for patients with malignancies undergoing CCRT. Pegylated recombinant human granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor (PEG‐rhG‐CSF), a long‐acting recombinant human granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor, can prevent and treat neutropenia in more convenient clinical settings. Based on relevant guidelines and the most recent clinical data, the Chinese Association for Therapeutic Radiation Oncologists, China Society for Radiation Oncology, and Chinese Association of Radiation Therapy have evaluated the safety and efficacy of PEG‐rhG‐CSF during CCRT, clearly defined the clinical pathway and route of administration for the prevention and treatment of neutropenia, and formed a Chinese expert consensus on PEG‐rhG‐CSF application during CCRT, with the goal of promoting the reasonable clinical use of this treatment.Keywords: clinical consensus, concurrent chemoradiotherapy, neutropenia, pegylated recombinant human granulocyte colony‐stimulating factor

INTRODUCTION

Concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) is an important treatment modality for oncological malignancies. 1 , 2 Neutropenia is the most common hematological toxicity that occurs during CCRT and can often lead to febrile neutropenia (FN), toxic shock, and even death, which not only seriously impact the treatment and survival of patients with cancer, but also increase their medical expenses. 3 , 4 , 5 Granulocyte‐colony stimulating factor (G‐CSF) is the main agent used in the prevention and treatment of neutropenia, with recombinant human granulocyte‐colony stimulating factor (rhG‐CSF) and pegylated recombinant human granulocyte‐colony stimulating factor (PEG‐rhG‐CSF) being commonly used. PEG‐rhG‐CSF is a long‐acting form of rhG‐CSF that was developed by cross‐linking a 20‐kDa polyethylene glycol molecule to the N‐terminus of rhG‐CSF and subsequent purification. PEG‐rhG‐CSF has long‐term maintenance of efficacy and is administered once per chemotherapy cycle, making the treatment convenient. 6 PEG‐rhG‐CSF was first approved for commercial use in China in 2011, which marked the start of the era of long‐acting G‐CSF treatment.

Based on relevant guidelines and the most recent clinical data, the Chinese Association for Therapeutic Radiation Oncologists, China Society for Radiation Oncology, and Chinese Association of Radiation Therapy updated the Chinese expert consensus on the application of PEG‐rhG‐CSF during CCRT (2020 edition) 7 and provided recommendations and references for Chinese radiation oncologists, with the goal of promoting the reasonable clinical use of this treatment. OVERVIEW OF CCRT‐INDUCED NEUTROPENIA

Neutropenia is the most common adverse event (AE) associated with myelosuppressive therapies (e.g., chemotherapy and radiotherapy) and is also the most serious hematological toxicity and dose‐limiting toxicity associated with chemotherapy. Meanwhile, the degree of damage to bone marrow, a radiation‐sensitive normal tissue, is related to the dose, range, location, and duration of radiation, which mainly affects the granulocyte lineage, especially during and after radiotherapy.

Definition and grading of neutropenia

Neutropenia is defined as an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) of < 2.0 × 109/L in the peripheral blood. Neutropenia is classified into four grades according to the Common Terminology Criteria AEs version 5.0 (CTCAE v5.0): grade 1, 1.5 × 109/L ≤ ANC < 2.0 × 109/L; grade 2, 1.0 × 109/L ≤ ANC < 1.5 × 109/L; grade 3, 0.5 × 109/L ≤ ANC < 1.0 × 109/L; grade 4, ANC < 0.5 × 109/L.

FN is considered severe neutropenia accompanied by fever, which is defined as an oral temperature of >38.3°C, >38.0°C for more than 1 h, and ANC < 0.5 × 109/L or ANC < 1.0 × 109/L with a trend to drop below 0.5 × 109/L within the next 48 h. 8

Epidemiology of neutropenia

The risk of FN and the incidence of neutropenia in patients receiving chemoradiotherapy is influenced by various factors, such as tumor type, chemoradiotherapy regimen, and patient characteristics, many of which also correlate with the selected chemoradiotherapy regimen. A meta‐analysis of 19 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving 2 728 patients with stage I–III non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) showed that the incidence of neutropenia in CCRT was 3.53‐ and 1.38‐fold higher than that in radiotherapy alone and sequential chemoradiotherapy cohorts, respectively. 9 A stratified analysis of radiotherapy‐induced myelosuppression reported by Mac‐Manus et al. found that the volume of marrow irradiation and CCRT were the main risk factors for moderate and severe FN. 10 In particular, CCRT had the highest impact on FN, and its odds ratio was as high as 42.1. The incidences of grade ≥3 neutropenia and FN can be as high as 77% and 16% in patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC receiving vinorelbine plus cisplatin CCRT, respectively. 11 The CONVERT trial found that elderly patients (>70 years) with small cell lung cancer (SCLC) who received CCRT were more likely to experience grade 3–4 neutropenia compared to younger patients (84% vs. 70%; P = 0.02). 12 In a multicenter, phase II RCT, 99 patients with stage T46 esophageal cancer were randomly assigned to receive cisplatin + 5‐fluorouracil (5‐FU) in combination with concurrent radiotherapy (CRT group) or docetaxel + cisplatin + 5‐FU in combination with chemotherapy (CT group) as initial induction therapy. The results indicated that the incidences of grade >3 neutropenia (41% vs. 94%) and FN (18% vs. 52%) were significantly lower in the CRT group than in the CT group, whereas R0 resection rates were similar in both groups. 13

It was previously reported that hematological toxicities were the primary cause (88%) of CCRT interruption and could impact the local control rate and survival of patients with treatment interruption. 14 Furthermore, overall survival was significantly lower in patients with myelosuppression‐induced CCRT interruption than in those without CCRT interruption. 15 , 16 Therefore, the prevention and management of neutropenia during CCRT are extremely important for the survival of patients with malignant tumors.

Based on the relevant guidelines and existing evidence, the present consensus classifies the commonly used CCRT regimens for malignant tumors into three groups according to the risk of FN: high‐risk regimens (>20% incidence of FN), intermediate‐risk regimens (10–20% incidence of FN), and low‐risk regimens (<10% incidence of FN). EFFICACY AND SAFETY OF PEG‐RHG‐CSF DURING CCRT

With the continuous advancement of clinical research on G‐CSF and refinement of radiotherapy technology, clinical expert opinions regarding the safety of CCRT with G‐CSF administration are constantly evolving. CONVERT is an open‐label, phase III RCT that investigated the safety and efficacy of the prophylactic administration of G‐CSF in CCRT through secondary analyses. A total of 487 patients were enrolled in this study, among whom 180 received prophylactic administration of G‐CSF for at least one cycle, with 60 receiving it as primary prophylaxis and 120 as secondary prophylaxis. The results indicated that the incidence of FN was lower in treatment cycles with the prophylactic administration of G‐CSF than in those without (10% vs. 22%, P = 0.002). 18 Moreover, no significant differences were observed in radiation‐related toxicity, treatment‐related mortality rates, or survival outcomes. Based on the results of the CONVERT study, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) clinical practice guidelines for hematopoietic growth factors (version 1.0, 2023) recommend that G‐CSF should be administered with caution during CCRT. 8 , 32 In addition, the Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) Guidelines for the Standardized Management of Oncology Radiotherapy‐Induced Neutropenia (version 2021) recommend the use of G‐CSF for the prevention and treatment of CCRT‐induced neutropenia and FN, with reference to the principles of primary and secondary prophylaxis of chemotherapy‐induced neutropenia, under the close monitoring of the patient's hematological indicators. 33

Simultaneously, evidence supporting the prophylactic administration of PEG‐rhG‐CSF during CCRT has increased. In a retrospective cohort study, 80 patients with SCLC who had received two cycles of etoposide‐cisplatin chemotherapy and experienced grade 3 or worse neutropenia received prophylactic PEG‐rhG‐CSF within 48 h of completing chemotherapy during CCRT. 34 Meanwhile, 80 patients with similar characteristics who received only rhG‐CSF upon myelosuppression without PEG‐rhG‐CSF administration were selected through propensity score matching as the control group. The results showed that the experimental group had a significantly lower incidence of grade 3/4 neutropenia (23.75% vs. 67.5%) and FN (2.5% vs. 16.25%) than the control group, without significant acute esophagitis or pulmonary toxicity. 34 However, the prophylactic administration of PEG‐rhG‐CSF did not confer a significant survival benefit to patients. 34 Apart from thoracic tumors, evidence supporting pelvic radiotherapy, such as CCRT for cervical cancer, is also gradually increasing. An RCT conducted by Li et al. demonstrated that the prophylactic administration of PEG‐rhG‐CSF during CCRT for cervical cancer was effective in preventing neutropenia and related AEs with a favorable safety profile. 35

Immunotherapy is an emerging therapeutic strategy. However, there is currently a lack of clinical data on neutropenia associated with the combination of immunotherapy and CCRT. Further clinical studies are required to evaluate its safety and feasibility in various cancers, such as head and neck, esophageal, and lung cancers. Additionally, the safety of PEG‐rhG‐CSF during CCRT in combination with immunotherapy warrants further assessment.

Theoretical basis for prophylactic application

In a clinical study of 163 patients with esophageal and lung cancer receiving CCRT, 36 prophylactic PEG‐rhG‐CSF administration significantly reduced the incidence of neutropenia‐related hospitalization (4.44% vs. 14.62%, P = 0.002), FN, antibiotic use, and chemotherapy delay (5% vs. 18%, P = 0.001) compared with the delayed administration of rhG‐CSF. This randomized controlled clinical study included 60 patients with cervical cancer who received CCRT, in which the experimental group was treated with prophylactic PEG‐rhG‐CSF, and the control group was treated with rhG‐CSF. The results showed that the incidence of grade 3/4 neutropenia (10% vs. 77.78%), FN (0% vs. 16.67%), and the delay rate of chemotherapy (12.5% vs. 61.11%) in the experimental group were significantly lower than those in the control group, indicating a manageable safety profile. 30

As the mean half‐life of PEG‐rhG‐CSF is 47 h, the effect of treatment can last for 12 days, and only a single administration is required per chemotherapy cycle, which can reduce pain and inconvenience for patients receiving repeated injections and improve treatment compliance. Meanwhile, due to the unique neutrophil‐mediated clearance mechanism, PEG‐rhG‐CSF is characterized by on‐demand mobilization and a steady increase of neutrophils, which can maintain ANC within a stable range. 6 The current clinical application of rhG‐CSF in China involves delayed and early administration, but both of these measures result in poor patient adherence. 37 Therefore, under non‐special conditions, prophylactic PEG‐rhG‐CSF should be considered during CCRT for malignant tumors, as it effectively ensures the safety of patients and the implementation of treatment at a sufficient dose.

TREATMENTS FOR CCRT‐INDUCED NEUTROPENIA

PEG‐rhG‐CSF treatment

Recommendation 4: Therapeutic PEG‐rhG‐CSF is defined as the use of PEG‐rhG‐CSF to treat patients who experience neutropenia or FN during CCRT. Therapeutic PEG‐rhG‐CSF should be considered for patients with one or more of the following conditions: (1) sepsis; (2) age >65 years; (3) ANC <1.0 × 109/L; (4) possibility of prolonged serious neutropenia; (5) complicated by pneumonitis or other infections; (6) invasive fungal infections; (7) fever during hospitalization; and (8) previous FN 38 (category 2A recommendation).

Theoretical basis for therapeutic application of PEG‐rhG‐CSF

In patients with esophageal and lung cancers who experienced grade 4 neutropenia during CCRT, the neutrophil proliferation rate and ANC in the PEG‐rhG‐CSF treatment group were significantly higher than those in the rhG‐CSF treatment group at different time points. The clinical effect of PEG‐rhG‐CSF was observed within 12–24 h after administration, and neutropenia improved by 36 h. 39 Another clinical study compared the efficacy and safety of PEG‐rhG‐CSF with and without rhG‐CSF in the treatment of grade 3/4 neutropenia after CCRT for malignant tumors. There were no statistically significant differences in ANC, neutrophil proliferation rate, and recovery time at different time periods after salvage therapy, suggesting that PEG‐rhG‐CSF monotherapy could effectively alleviate CCRT‐induced grade 3/4 neutropenia. 40 In more complex clinical scenarios, the use of a combination of PEG‐rhG‐CSF and rhG‐CSF for the treatment of CCRT‐induced grade 3/4 neutropenia should be thoroughly analyzed and clinically applied according to guideline recommendations and specific patient conditions to maximize patient safety.

Notably, PEG‐rhG‐CSF promoted the proliferation and differentiation of neutrophils, and the effective concentration of a single injection was maintained for 12 days. However, weekly chemotherapy regimens can lead to serious hematological toxicities, and many newly generated naïve neutrophils may be killed by chemotherapeutic drugs, resulting in bone marrow dysfunction. Therefore, PEG‐rhG‐CSF therapy is not recommended for CCRT with weekly chemotherapy regimens.

RADIOTHERAPY ALONE

Chemotherapy and radiotherapy are important treatment modalities for malignant tumors, but may result in the development of myelosuppression during therapy. Notably, radiotherapy alone has been associated with a lower incidence of myelosuppression. However, if patients with a moderate‐to‐high risk of FN receive multiple cycles, sufficient dose, and full course chemotherapy regimens upfront, they can experience a decreased bone marrow reserve and prolonged recovery duration. In such patients, subsequent radiotherapy (especially when bone tissues have hematopoiesis in the irradiated field) may increase the risk of myelosuppression. Therefore, individualized treatment or the prophylactic administration of PEG‐rhG‐CSF should be considered. The NCCN Guidelines for Hematopoietic Growth Factors (version 1.0, 2013) recommends the therapeutic use of PEG‐rhG‐CSF for radiotherapy‐induced neutropenia, and PEG‐rhG‐CSF may be considered for patients who received ≥2 Gy whole or partial body exposure, have an ANC ≤ 0.5 × 109/L, and will likely have prolonged periods of neutropenia. 8 A retrospective study that included 43 patients treated with sequential adjuvant radiotherapy after post‐operative chemotherapy for breast cancer reported that prophylactic PEG‐rhG‐CSF was effective in preventing radiotherapy‐induced grade III neutropenia (not occurring vs. 13.6%), compared to the control group, and facilitated the successful application of radiotherapy. 41

Recommendation 7: PEG‐rhG‐CSF may be considered for patients who have received ≥2 Gy whole or partial body radiation exposure, have an ANC ≤0.5 × 109/L, and will likely have prolonged periods of neutropenia (category 2A recommendation). For patients undergoing radiotherapy alone, prophylactic use of PEG‐rhG‐CSF may be considered depending on factors such as myelosuppression, radiotherapy regimen, and previous treatment (category 2 B recommendation).

MANAGEMENT OF CHEMORADIOTHERAPY‐ASSOCIATED NEUTROPENIA DURING THE CORONAVIRUS DISEASE 2019 (COVID‐19) PANDEMIC

In the context of the rapidly evolving COVID‐19 epidemic, the impact of COVID‐19 on the outcomes and treatment recommendations of relevant clinical studies (including previous national and international studies) should be considered. The following recommendations made herein are based on domestic and international evidence‐based consensus guidelines: 42 , 43 , 44 (1) In general, it is recommended to delay the initiation of anti‐tumor treatment for patients with COVID‐19 infection, and therapy should be performed according to the discretion of the oncologist if tumor control fails; (2) if patients contract COVID‐19 after use of PEG‐rhG‐CSF or develop neutropenia after infection with COVID‐19, close monitoring of relevant indicators and empirical management on a case‐by‐case basis is suggested; (3) appropriate enhanced primary prophylaxis should be advised after the patients have recovered from COVID‐19 infection and initiated anti‐tumor treatment, and PEG‐rhG‐CSF is used as primary prophylaxis for the development of neutropenia or FN and is available for treatment regimens with an intermediate risk (10–20%) of FN.

HANDLING OF AES

The type, incidence, and severity of AEs caused by PEG‐rhG‐CSF were similar to those caused by rhG‐CSF; however, the difference was not significant. 45 , 46

Bone pain

Bone pain is the most common AE; it is generally mild‐to‐moderate and does not require treatment. The incidence of bone pain was similar between the PEG‐rhG‐CSF and rhG‐CSF groups (19.7% and 19.0%, respectively). Nonsteroidal anti‐inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen or naproxen can be used as symptomatic treatments in patients who cannot tolerate pain. It was previously reported that post‐PEG‐rhG‐CSF administration of naproxen (500 mg, 2 times/day) for 5–8 days can effectively decrease the incidence and severity of PEG‐rhG‐CSF‐induced bone pain. 47

Allergic reactions

Allergic reactions include skin, respiratory, and cardiovascular reactions. These events are relatively rare and do not require routine anti‐allergic therapy.

Splenomegaly and (or) splenic rupture

The exact mechanisms of G‐CSF‐induced splenomegaly and/or splenic rupture are poorly understood, and several patients with hematopoietic dysfunction may be at risk of transient splenomegaly after G‐CSF treatment. The incidence of splenic rupture is extremely low and is considered a rare AE. For patients with abdominal pain (especially in the upper left abdomen), nausea, vomiting, and gradual exacerbation of anemia, changes in the splenic volume should be carefully monitored to provide timely treatment.

CONCLUSIONS

Neutropenia is the most common hematological toxicity of CCRT, and its incidence is associated with factors such as tumor type, chemotherapeutic drug, dose intensity, radiation dose, irradiation volume, and irradiation site. Neutropenia can lead to dose reductions, treatment delays, and even CCRT interruptions, consequently impacting patient prognosis. The hematological parameters of patients should be closely monitored during CCRT. Prophylactic or therapeutic PEG‐rhG‐CSF should be administered to patients with CCRT‐induced neutropenia to maximize treatment safety and efficacy. However, there are unresolved issues surrounding the use of PEG‐rhG‐CSF during CCRT, including its long‐term safety in combination with novel drugs in the context of precision medicine. Key unanswered questions will be addressed stepwise by accumulating evidence from prospective and real‐world studies.
