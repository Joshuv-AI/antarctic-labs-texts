Evaluating a case-based lecture to educate internal medicine interns about depression and suicide risk assessment
Authors: Shivani Kumar, Fabiana S Araujo, Julie Oyler, Marie Tobin, Nancy Beckman, Kristen C Jacobson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough primary care providers are often the first line in identifying and treating mental illness, there is limited published mental health curriculum for trainees in internal medicine. The goal of this single-arm prospective longitudinal study was to evaluate the impact of a case-based lecture on the evaluation and management of depression and suicidality.MethodsThe lecture was delivered to 72 internal medicine interns at a single academic medical center between 2019 and 2021. A 9-item survey measuring confidence in assessing patients with depression and suicidal ideation (SI) and confidence in managing SI was administered pre-lecture, immediately post-lecture, and 6 months after the lecture. Knowledge of depression symptoms was assessed at all three time points among the 2020–2021 participants.ResultsRepeated effects regression models revealed a significant effect of time for both confidence in assessment (F[2,83] = 32.91, p < 0.001) and management of SI (F[2,83] = 130.55, p < 0.001) of patients. Pairwise comparisons indicated that participants scored significantly higher on both confidence measures immediately after and at 6 months following the lecture; however, there was a significant decrease in confidence in management of SI across the 6-month follow-up period. Knowledge of depression symptoms did not change across time (F[2,43] = 0.55 p = 0.58).DiscussionSignificant increases in confidence suggest both immediate and lasting positive effects of the curriculum for internal medicine interns. However, the significant decrease in confidence in managing SI across the 6-month follow-up period may indicate the need for a booster session. Overall, a plurality of participants accurately identified all symptoms of depression at all time points, suggesting that the curriculum works to improve confidence in already achieved skills.Keywords: Medical education, Depression, Suicide, Primary care, Internal medicine

Background

Mental health and substance use disorders account for 7.4% of the global burden of disease [1] and are among the top ten leading causes of global disease burden [2]. Recent data from the United States (US) revealed in 2021, 21.0 million (8.3%) adults had a major depressive episode in the past year [3]. A significant long-term consequence of severe psychiatric illness is suicide, and people with depression are more than ten times as likely to attempt suicide [4]. Suicide is the 11th leading cause of death across all ages and the 2nd leading cause of death in young people, ages 10–34 [5, 6]. In 2021, over 12 million adults had serious thoughts of suicide, 1.7 million attempted suicide [3], and over 48 thousand people died by suicide [5].

Despite these staggering numbers, only 61% of adults with a major depressive episode received treatment in 2021 [3]. Nearly half of people who did not seek out mental health care cited cost as a barrier and over a third cited not knowing how to access care [3]. The Health Resources and Services Administration has demonstrated a shortage of mental health providers in the US [7]. Given the growing shortage of mental health practitioners in the US [8], low reimbursement rates for behavioral health care [9], and behavioral health-related stigma [10], primary care clinicians (PCCs) are often the first line in identifying and treating mental illness. Indeed, screening for depression is recommended by the US Preventative Services Task Force in all adults [11]. In addition, 80% of people who die from suicide had contact with a PCC in the year of their death, and 44% saw a PCC within one month of their death [12].

Despite the key role that PCCs have in the evaluation and management of depression and suicide, there is limited published mental health curriculum for trainees in internal medicine. For example, mental health competency is evaluated as part of the Internal Medicine (IM) Certification Examination; however, the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education does not set clear guidelines for mental health curriculum for IM residents, nor does it require rotations in psychiatry. Moreover, surveys of non-psychiatric health care providers showed that less than one-third reported feeling knowledgeable about suicide risk warning signs and only about one-third “strongly-agreed” when rating their self-confidence or comfort in treating patients with elevated suicide risk [13]. Lack of confidence and discomfort in suicide risk assessment is connected to lower likelihood of conducting a suicide risk assessment among emergency room physicians [14]. Therefore, there is urgent need for evidenced-based curriculum on depression and suicidality among PCCs.

The purpose of this paper is to report on the impact of a case-based lecture focusing on evaluation and management of depression and suicidality delivered to IM interns in the 2019–2020 and 2020–2021 academic years. The goal of this lecture was to increase knowledge and improve confidence regarding the evaluation and management of depression and suicide risk.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study used a single-arm prospective longitudinal design to test the effects of a single lecture on participants’ medical knowledge and confidence. The sample included IM interns from an urban, academic medical center in the Midwest US. The lecture was a part of the IM interns’ Ambulatory Medicine rotation. The Ambulatory Medicine rotation includes both a clinical and educational component. The IM interns work in both urgent care and outpatient clinical settings. They have one half-day of protected time for lectures each week, including lectures on behavioral medicine. The lecture on Depression/Suicide is one lecture in this curriculum. IM interns were eligible to participate in the study if they received the Depression/Suicide lecture. The lecture was delivered to a total of 72 IM interns across the 2019–2020 (AY20) and 2020–2021 (AY21) academic years. The percentage of interns who received the lecture was 34/40 (85%) in AY20 and 38/41 (93%) in AY21.

Intervention

The one-hour lecture was delivered in person in a classroom at the medical center. The lecture was delivered to all IM interns during one of their 2-week ambulatory medicine rotations in the fall term of each year. The lecture was delivered three times in each academic year to approximately one-third of the IM intern cohort. The same lecture was given to all 6 groups of IM interns across the two years. The lecture involved presentation and discussion of two cases: a patient with Bipolar II disorder with a current major depressive episode and a patient with current suicidal ideation.

For the patient with Bipolar II disorder, the patient presents to a primary care physician with symptoms consistent with a major depressive episode. The IM interns are prompted to identify the symptoms and signs present in the case and ask for additional information. After they come up with an initial treatment plan for the patient, the patient re-presents with symptoms consistent with a hypomanic episode. The IM interns are then prompted to identify missed information in the prior presentation of the case and propose an alternative treatment course. This first case is designed to illustrate the overlap of mood disorders based on Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders V (DSM-V) criteria [15] and the importance of differential diagnosis in evaluation and treatment of psychiatric illness.

The second case presents a patient with severe depressive symptoms and hopelessness. The IM interns are prompted to inquire about suicidal ideation, identify risk factors for suicide based on the Columbia Suicide Severity Rating Scale [16], and assess the overall risk for completed suicide. The lecture includes a discussion of the multifactorial nature of suicidal ideation.

Measures

Confidence in Depression and Suicide Assessment and Management (C-DASAM). A 9-item instrument was developed to assess specific confidence in clinical skills regarding assessment and management of depression and suicidality (Table 1). The items were modeled on similar instruments to assess general confidence in psychiatric skills [17, 18] but were targeted towards the specific topics covered during the lecture. Items were pilot-tested in a previous cohort of IM residents to assess content validity and feasibility. Each item was scored on a 5-point scale from Strongly Disagree (1) to Strongly Agree (5). Items were designed to measure confidence in assessing depression symptoms (Items 1 and 2), confidence in assessing for suicidal ideation (Items 3 and 4), and managing SI (Items 5–9). Previous studies suggest that confidence in mental health items tend to cluster in two domains: confidence in assessing and confidence in managing mental health symptoms [18].

Depression Knowledge. In AY21 a second questionnaire was added to assess knowledge of symptoms of major depressive disorder. This questionnaire was developed by the first author, an expert in mood and anxiety disorders. The scale consists of a list of 19 criteria, including the eight valid diagnostic criteria of a major depressive episode as defined by the DSM-V, and eleven distractors. The distractors included diagnostic criteria from other closely related psychiatric conditions (e.g., anxiety, social phobia) which are not present in a major depressive episode. Participants were asked to select True or False to indicate whether or not each item was a diagnostic criterion for a major depressive episode. Each item was scored as correct (1) or incorrect (0). At each time point, items were summed to create a single knowledge score with possible range 0–19.

Procedures

The project was reviewed by the local institutional review board and was considered exempt under protocol #IRB23-2024. All interns who received the lecture were invited to participate in this study. Participation was not mandatory. Subjects were not compensated. In AY20, pre- and post-surveys were delivered in person immediately before (T1) and after (T2) the session. A follow-up survey (T3) was delivered in person 6 months after the initial lecture to the first cohort. The latter two cohorts of AY20 completed the T3 follow-up survey 6 months after the initial lecture electronically due to COVID19 precautions. In AY21, all pre-, post-, and follow-up surveys were delivered electronically immediately before (T1), immediately after (T2), and 6 months after (T3) the initial lecture. Survey responses were linked within person using an anonymized ID created by each participant.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses used SAS software, version 9.3 for Windows (Copyright © 2002–2010, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). Significance levels were set at p ≤ 0.05, two tailed.

Number of required subjects. The number of required subjects was calculated assuming a correlation of r = 0.50 across time points and an effect size of 0.30. A total of 71 subjects were needed to achieve 80% power.

Psychometric properties of the C-DASAM scale. Exploratory factor analysis of the 9-item confidence scale at T1 was used to assess construct validity and to determine the underlying number of dimensions, using promax rotation and maximum likelihood estimation. The number of significant factors was determined by Kaiser’s criteria [19]. The internal consistency of each identified factor was calculated using Cronbach’s alpha. Composite factor scores were calculated by averaging the items that corresponded to each factor.

Testing effects of the intervention. To evaluate changes in the outcome variable across the three time points, repeated effects general linear models were fitted with PROC MIXED given its flexibility in handling missing data and unbalanced groups and the ability to model the residual covariance structure directly. Model parameters were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood. Analyses were run for each outcome separately, using all non-missing data. The assessment period (TIME) was included as a categorical fixed effect to allow for non-linear change across the three time points. Time was also treated as a repeated effect. Different covariance structures for residuals were tested, including unstructured, heterogeneous compound symmetry, compound symmetry, and variance components. The best-fitting model was selected by comparing Akaike’s Information Criteria-Corrected (AICC).

For the confidence outcomes, after modeling the main effects of TIME, a second set of models added academic year (YEAR) as a fixed effect to determine whether there were any differences in outcomes across the two years (including any effects from changing to all online data collection in AY21). The interaction between TIME and YEAR was also included to test whether the effects of the intervention differed across academic year.

For all outcomes, significant effects of TIME were probed with post-hoc pairwise comparisons using t-tests. Effect sizes are presented as standardized mean differences between time periods.

Results

Participants

All 72 interns who received the lecture completed the assessment survey at one or more time points. Across AY20 and AY21, a total of 65 participants completed the confidence survey at T1, with 57 responses at T2, and 35 responses at T3. Over three-quarters of interns (N = 62, 86.1%) had non-missing data at both T1 and at least one of the follow-up assessments.

Psychometric properties of the C-DASAM scale

Factor analysis of the 9 confidence items at the baseline T1 assessment returned two factors with an inter-factor correlation r = 0.42. Table 1 presents the factor loadings for each factor. Variables with factor loadings ≥ 0.40 were considered significant contributors to the factor. The first factor was defined by the four items measuring confidence in identifying symptoms of depression and SI and a fifth item measuring comfort in asking questions about SI. This factor was labeled confidence in assessment and had Cronbach’s α = 0.76. The second factor was defined by the remaining four items which measured clinicians’ knowledge and confidence regarding suicide risk assessment and management following a positive screen. This factor was labeled confidence in management of SI and had Cronbach’s α = 0.79.

Effects of the intervention on outcomes

For confidence in assessment and confidence in management of SI, a model allowing for a heterogeneous compound symmetry structure for errors had the lowest AICC value. This model allowed for different residual variances at the three time points but constrained residual covariances across time points to be equal. For knowledge of depression symptoms, a model allowing for compound symmetry of errors had the lowest AICC value. This model assumed residuals have equal variance and equal covariance across time points.

Figure 1 presents the estimated marginal means for each of the three outcomes at each time point, based on results from the regression models. Analyses revealed a statistically significant effect of TIME for both confidence outcomes (Assessment: F[2,83] = 32.91, p < 0.001; Management of SI: F[2,83] = 130.55, p < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons indicated that participants scored statistically significantly higher at T2 compared to T1 for both confidence measures ([Assessment] t83 = 8.03, p < 0.001; [Management of SI] t83 = 16.14, p < 0.001; see Fig. 1a and b). At T3, both confidence scores remained statistically significantly higher compared to T1 ([Assessment] t83 = 4.05, p < 0.001; [Management of SI] t83 = 5.90, p < 0.001). There was an observed drop in confidence scores between T2 and T3 for both outcomes (see Fig. 1), but this decrease was statistically significant only for confidence in management of SI (t83 = 5.69, p < 0.001). The effect of YEAR was not statistically significant for either confidence outcome (both p > 0.10). Likewise, neither of the TIME x YEAR interactions were statistically significant (both p > 0.40).

The standardized mean difference between T1 and T2 was 0.90 (95% CI: 0.66; 1.13) for confidence in assessment and 1.49 (95% CI: 1.29; 1.69) for confidence in management of SI. The standardized mean difference between T1 and T3 was 0.59 (95% CI: 0.30; 0.88) for confidence in assessment and 0.70 (95% CI: 0.45; 0.94) for confidence in management of SI. The effect size for the decrease in confidence between T2 and T3 was 0.27 for confidence in assessment (95% CI: −0.04; 0.57) and 0.72 (95% CI: 0.47; 0.97) for confidence in management of SI.

For knowledge of depression symptoms, the effect of TIME was not statistically significant (F[2,43] = 0.55, p = 0.58). The mean knowledge scores were high across all three time points (T1 Mean = 17.87, SE = 0.21, Time 2 Mean = 17.98, SE = 0.23, T3 Mean = 18.17, SE = 0.25, see Fig. 1c). Effect sizes for differences in knowledge of depression symptoms across paired time points were all less than 0.30 and the 95% confidence intervals for each pairwise comparison included zero.

Discussion

This study adds to a growing body of research demonstrating the effectiveness of using interdisciplinary education to enhance the knowledge and skills of participants outside of their specific fields. This paper reports on a case-based lecture to enhance confidence in assessing symptoms of depression and suicidal ideation and in the management of SI. The significant increases in confidence scores between T1 to T2 suggest immediate effects of adding a behavioral medicine lecture for IM interns. Moreover, the observed effect sizes of 0.90–1.49 suggest large effects of the intervention on intern confidence. In addition, confidence scores at T3 remained statistically significantly higher than T1 scores, suggesting that the effects of the intervention persist for up to 6 months; however, the more modest effect sizes (0.59 and 0.70), along with the statistically significant decrease between T2 and T3 for confidence in managing SI, may indicate the need for a booster session or structural component to this intervention.

Despite the significant increases in interns’ confidence, analyses did not show a statistically significant increase in medical knowledge of major depressive episode symptoms between T1 to T2 and T3. A number of factors could account for this result. First, although there was a slight increase in mean knowledge score over time, the knowledge survey was given only in the AY21 cohort. Thus, the non-significant results could be due to a lack of statistical power. However, the effect sizes for the increase were small (0.09 and 0.25), suggesting that increasing the sample size might not have altered results. We note that mean levels of knowledge were high at all three time points, and inspection of the individual data indicated that a plurality of participants (between 38.2 and 50.0%) at each of the three time points accurately identified all symptoms of a major depressive episode. This suggests that the analysis of change in knowledge may have been impacted by ceiling effects. Taken together, our results indicate that the lecture works to improve confidence in already achieved skills. Future research is needed to test whether similar improvements in confidence would be seen in clinicians with lower levels of knowledge.

Previous studies have shown that both high confidence and content knowledge are correlated with high procedural knowledge, suggesting that the significant increase in confidence found in this study may be related to clinically relevant outcomes [20]. However, although confidence may be an essential component to the development of competent clinicians, confidence without knowledge could create reckless or inattentive clinicians [21]. Educational interventions that increase both confidence and knowledge could help improve clinical competency.

Because the study was conducted at single urban academic medical center in the Midwest, replication at other institutions is warranted. However, the intervention was delivered to two separate cohorts of IM interns with no significant differences across academic year, suggesting some reproducibility. Moreover, nearly 90% of the IM interns received the intervention and 86% of participants had at least one follow-up assessment. Thus, study results are unlikely to be affected by selection or attrition biases. Future studies are needed to test whether the intervention would show similar effects among other healthcare providers who work with general patient populations, such as nurses, physician assistants, or emergency room physicians. Nevertheless, demonstrating the effectiveness of the intervention among a group of trainees who will become PCCs is an important first step in addressing gaps in the identification and treatment of depression and suicidality, particularly among underserved patient populations with limited access to traditional behavioral health care.

Limitations

There are several limitations to consider. Regarding the lecture, only one example of a patient with suicidal ideation was included, meaning that participants did not see examples demonstrating the multifactorial nature of suicidal ideation. The lecture was also based on discussion of hypothetical patients; previous work has demonstrated the benefit of using standardized patients for increasing confidence and clinical competence [22]. Approximately half of the respondents did not complete the T3 survey. Moreover, as part of the broader behavioral health curriculum during the IM internship year, all interns also received longitudinal supervision from mental health professionals in clinic throughout the year, which may have affected their knowledge and confidence. Thus, conclusions about the long-term effects of the intervention should be interpreted with caution given the smaller sample size and potential confounding factors. Future studies using a control condition could separate the impact of the lecture versus other clinical activities. Finally, we note that knowledge is only one of several competencies needed to provide psychiatric care, and our knowledge questionnaire was focused solely on symptomatology of depression. Thus, we do not know if the intervention increased knowledge of suicidal ideation, risk factors, or management. Nevertheless, this study provides initial evidence that educational interventions can be used to improve the confidence of IM interns in evaluating and managing depression and suicidal ideation.

Conclusion

The study showed that the delivery of a single case-based lecture had a significant impact on the confidence in IM interns’ assessment and management of suicidality and depression. Moreover, there is evidence that these effects persisted beyond the immediate short term. This suggests that educating IM interns about depression and suicide risk assessment in the primary care setting is a viable way of addressing the shortage of mental health providers.
