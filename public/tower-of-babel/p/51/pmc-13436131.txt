Multimodal ultrasonography for the prospective evaluation of soft-tissue changes after anterior cervical discectomy and fusion
Authors: Yuting Wu, Ruiqian Guo, Sujiao Cao, Li Qiu, Yuanjiao Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) traditionally employs closed-suction drainage, a “no-drain” strategy is increasingly adopted despite concerns regarding hematoma and soft tissue swelling. This study utilized multimodal ultrasound to objectively evaluate postoperative recovery in non-drained ACDF patients.MethodsIn this prospective observational study, 47 patients undergoing ACDF without drainage were assessed on postoperative Day 1 and Day 7. High-frequency ultrasound (HFUS) measured residual fluid volume and tissue thickness (skin and sternocleidomastoid muscle), while shear wave elastography (SWE) quantified tissue stiffness. Comparisons were made between surgical and contralateral sides and across time points.ResultsResidual fluid volume significantly decreased from (34.71 ± 45.4) mL on day 1 to (6.68 ± 10.04) mL on day 7 (P = 0.003). Skin thickness was significantly greater on the surgical side compared to the contralateral side, whereas muscle thickness showed no significant bilateral differences. Skin elasticity values changed significantly over time, reflecting reduced stiffness during recovery, while muscle elasticity remained stable. No correlation was found between tissue thickness and elasticity.ConclusionMultimodal ultrasound is helpful for assessment on postoperative residual fluid, soft tissue swelling, and muscle tension in patients without drainage tube placement after ACDF, supporting the safety of the “no-drain” strategy in carefully selected patients and providing objective biomarkers for optimizing perioperative management.

Introduction

Cervical spondylosis is a highly prevalent degenerative spinal disorder that significantly impairs patients’ quality of life. Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) remains the most widely performed surgical intervention for symptomatic cases refractory to conservative management [1]. Traditionally, closed-suction drainage is routinely placed following ACDF to prevent fluid accumulation. However, emerging evidence suggests that postoperative drainage may prolong hospitalization, increase the risk of retrograde infection, and potentially delay wound healing—thereby adversely affecting recovery and nursing care efficiency. As a result, an increasing number of surgeons are adopting a “no-drain” strategy in carefully selected patients [2, 3].

Nevertheless, the omission of drainage raises legitimate concerns regarding potential complications, including postoperative hematoma, localized soft tissue swelling, and elevated paraspinal muscle tension—all of which may contribute to dysphagia, neck pain, or even airway compromise in severe cases [4]. High-frequency ultrasound (HFUS) offers a safe, cost-effective, and radiation-free modality capable of real-time, high-resolution visualization of postoperative residual fluid and subcutaneous edema. Complementarily, shear wave elastography (SWE) enables quantitative, objective assessment of tissue stiffness [5, 6].

Accordingly, this prospective observational study employs multimodal ultrasound—integrating HFUS and SWE—to dynamically monitor and comprehensively evaluate residual fluid volume, skin and soft tissue swelling, and muscle biomechanical properties in patients undergoing ACDF without prophylactic drainage. By characterizing the temporal evolution of these parameters during early recovery, we aim to establish objective imaging biomarkers that can facilitate early detection of complications, optimize perioperative management, and ultimately improve clinical outcomes in the “no-drain” era of anterior cervical spine surgery.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient characteristics

The prospective study was approved by the ethics committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (No.20221251) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Recruited patients with cervical spondylosis who did not undergo placement of a drainage tube following ACDF. On the first postoperative day and the seventh day, high-frequency ultrasound was utilized to assess the volume of residual fluid in the cervical surgical region, as well as the thickness of the skin layer and sternocleidomastoid muscle in both the surgical area and the corresponding contralateral region of the neck. Shear wave elastography was employed to measure the shear wave elasticity values of the skin layer and sternocleidomastoid muscle in the cervical surgical area and its contralateral counterpart. The differences in thickness and shear wave elasticity values between the bilateral regions were analyzed. Furthermore, dynamic monitoring was conducted to evaluate the recovery of residual fluid in the surgical area. The relationship between soft tissue thickness and shear wave elasticity values was also investigated.

For patients who meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria, their age, gender, height, weight, and operation time are recorded.

Multimodal ultrasound examination

The prototypic US system equipped with SWE (RUBI, prototype for Aixplorer; SuperSonic Imagine, France) was used. A 4–15 MHz linear array probe is selected, configured under the “Superficial MSK” mode. The focus depth is adjusted dynamically to optimize imaging quality at different soft tissue levels based on the specific examination site.

The patient is placed in a supine position on the examination bed, with both sides of the neck fully exposed. The examination focuses on the surgical incision site and the corresponding anatomical area on the other side of the neck.

Initially, high-frequency ultrasound is employed to measure the length, width, and thickness of the residual fluid within the surgical area. The volume of the residual fluid is subsequently calculated using the formula: length × width × thickness / 2 (Fig. 1).

Subsequently, high-frequency ultrasound is utilized to measure the thickness of the skin and the sternocleidomastoid muscle on both sides of the neck.

Finally, the instrument is switched to the shear wave elastography (SWE) mode. The region of interest (ROI) is carefully positioned to the appropriate location, and the Scale is adjusted to a range of 0–300 kPa. Once the image has stabilized for approximately 5 s, it is frozen.

Based on the measured thickness of the skin and the sternocleidomastoid muscle, the diameter of the sampling box (Q-box) is adjusted. Consequently, the system automatically generates the mean, maximum and minimum shear wave velocity value of the skin and the muscle (Fig. 2).

Statistical methods

All standard statistical analyses were performed with SPSS IBM Version 23 and GraphPad Prism 10.1 software. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Normally distributed measurement data were expressed as x ± s (mean ± standard deviation), and comparisons between two groups were made using t-test or t’ test. For multiple groups of normally distributed data with homogeneity of variance, analysis of variance was used. Non-normally distributed measurement data were expressed as median and interquartile range, and comparisons between groups were made using rank sum test. The correlation between normally distributed measurement data was analyzed using Pearson correlation coefficient test. The correlation between non-normally distributed measurement data and ordinal data was analyzed using Spearman correlation coefficient test.

Results

Patient demographics

47 patients of 26 male and 21 female were included in the study, with a mean age of (52.89 ± 10.93) years, with a mean height of (162.48 ± 6.44) cm, with a mean weight of (64.89 ± 11.06) kg.

Discussion

The results of some studies support the view that “no drain” is safe and effective after ACSS under strict patients’ selection and discreet operation [2]. However, there is a lack of comprehensive assessment research on postoperative residual fluid, soft tissue swelling, and muscle tension in patients without drainage tube placement after ACDF. Ultrasound examinations are widely used for the assessment of residual fluid and soft tissue swelling [7–9]. Ultrasound elastography has also been successfully applied to various tissues and organs [10, 11].

Our findings demonstrate a significant reduction in postoperative residual fluid volume between postoperative Day 1 and Day 7, decreasing from a mean of (34.71 ± 45.4) mL to (6.68 ± 10.04) mL. This rapid resolution suggests that the body’s natural absorptive mechanisms—mediated by lymphatic drainage and capillary reabsorption—are highly effective in clearing modest fluid collections in the early postoperative period. The consistent downward trend across the cohort supports the notion that small-to-moderate postoperative fluid accumulations are typically self-limiting and may not necessitate routine drainage. These results align with recent evidence indicating that prophylactic closed-suction drainage after anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) offers limited clinical benefit, particularly when meticulous hemostasis and strict patient selection are employed [12].

The increased skin thickness on the affected side compared to the contralateral side on Day 1 likely reflects acute postoperative inflammatory responses, including edema, capillary leakage, and soft tissue trauma induced by surgical dissection or retraction. This asymmetry serves as a quantifiable marker of localized tissue injury and early wound response. Furthermore, the significant reduction in skin thickness on the affected side at the later time point indicates progressive resolution of edema and tissue remodeling during the early recovery phase. These findings underscore the dynamic nature of postoperative soft tissue changes and highlight high-frequency ultrasound as a sensitive, non-invasive tool for objectively monitoring peri-incisional swelling. Clinically, such quantitative assessment may help differentiate normal postoperative edema from pathological fluid accumulation or infection, thereby guiding decisions regarding drainage, compression therapy, or early mobilization.

Muscle thickness measurements showed no statistically significant difference between the affected and contralateral (unaffected) sides at either postoperative time point, suggesting that the surgical intervention did not induce appreciable acute atrophy or edema within the evaluated musculature during the early recovery period. Nevertheless, longer-term follow-up would be necessary to assess whether subtle changes in muscle quality (e.g., fatty infiltration [13] or fibrosis), undetectable by thickness alone, emerge beyond the early postoperative window [14, 15].

Interestingly, skin elasticity exhibited a consistent trend across both the affected and contralateral sides at the two postoperative time points. However, a statistically significant difference in elasticity was observed between the two sides on Day 1, which became smaller on Day 7. This transient asymmetry likely reflects acute localized tissue responses to surgical trauma—such as edema, inflammatory infiltration, and microvascular disruption—which temporarily reduce dermal compliance and elasticity on the operated side [16, 17]. The normalization of elasticity by Day 7 suggests effective resolution of these acute changes, possibly due to restored lymphatic drainage, reduced inflammation, and early tissue remodeling. These findings highlight skin elasticity as a sensitive biomechanical indicator of early soft tissue recovery. Moreover, the absence of persistent elasticity differences implies that the surgical approach did not cause lasting dermal or subcutaneous fibrosis within the first week, supporting its minimal invasiveness. Quantitative assessment of skin elasticity via ultrasound elastography may thus serve as a valuable objective tool for monitoring postoperative wound healing and guiding early rehabilitation strategies.

Only the skin elasticity on the affected (surgical) side exhibited a significant change between postoperative Day 1 and Day 7, whereas no such temporal variation was detected in the contralateral skin or in the underlying sternocleidomastoid muscle on either side. This finding highlights a layer-specific response to surgical trauma, wherein the superficial integument demonstrates dynamic biomechanical adaptation during early healing, while deeper muscular tissues maintain relative stability [18]. The increased stiffness (or decreased elasticity) of the surgical-side skin on Day 1 likely reflects acute inflammatory processes—such as localized edema, fibrin deposition, and transient collagen fiber disorganization—commonly triggered by tissue manipulation, retraction, and incision. By Day 7, the observed reduction in stiffness (i.e., increased elasticity) may signify the resolution of acute inflammation, initiation of remodeling, and restoration of dermal hydration and microcirculation.

Interestingly, shear wave elastography revealed no significant inter-side difference in muscle elasticity at either postoperative Day 1 or Day 7. This finding suggests that, despite localized surgical trauma, tissue handling, and potential inflammatory responses on the affected side, the biomechanical integrity and stiffness of the cervical musculature were preserved symmetrically during the early recovery phase. Several factors may account for this observation. First, the anterior approach to the cervical spine typically involves blunt dissection and retraction of the sternocleidomastoid and strap muscles rather than direct incision or denervation, thereby minimizing structural disruption to contractile elements. Second, compensatory neuromuscular mechanisms or rapid resolution of transient edema may prevent sustained alterations in muscle tone or stiffness. Third, the absence of drainage—while potentially increasing local fluid accumulation—may not be sufficient to induce measurable changes in deep muscle elasticity detectable by SWE within the first week.

This bilateral symmetry in muscle elasticity contrasts with the transient asymmetry observed in skin elasticity or thickness, underscoring that superficial and deep soft tissues respond differently to surgical stress. Clinically, the preservation of muscular biomechanical balance may contribute to reduced postoperative neck pain, earlier mobilization, and lower risk of muscular imbalance–related complications [19]. Importantly, our result supports the notion that ACDF without drainage does not induce significant unilateral muscle stiffening or spasm in the early postoperative period, reinforcing the safety of this management strategy from a musculoskeletal perspective. Nevertheless, longer-term assessments beyond one week may be warranted to rule out delayed fibrotic changes or adaptive remodeling.

A notable finding of our study is the absence of a significant correlation between tissue thickness and shear-wave elastography (SWE)-derived elasticity values—both in the skin and underlying muscles—across time points and between the affected and contralateral sides. This lack of association implies that structural dimensions (e.g., edema-induced thickening) do not directly dictate tissue mechanical behavior in the early postoperative phase. For instance, while skin or muscle thickness may increase due to fluid accumulation, the resulting change in stiffness or elasticity appears to be influenced by other factors such as collagen organization, inflammatory cell infiltration, microvascular integrity, or neuromuscular tone [20, 21]. Conversely, tissue may regain elasticity through biochemical and cellular remodeling even before visible thinning occurs. This dissociation underscores a critical limitation of relying solely on morphological imaging (e.g., B-mode ultrasound thickness) to infer functional tissue status. Instead, it highlights the complementary value of quantitative elastography as an independent biomarker of tissue quality and recovery.

Conclusion

Multimodal ultrasound is helpful for assessment on postoperative residual fluid, soft tissue swelling, and muscle tension in patients without drainage tube placement after ACDF. And, these findings highlight the limitations of relying solely on conventional B-mode imaging and advocate for the integrated use of quantitative elastography to provide a more comprehensive, functional evaluation of soft tissue healing after cervical spine surgery.
