SHIP2–PLK1 crosstalk promotes sensitivity to dual inhibition in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Ana Raquel Ramos, Giacomo Bregni, Nadia Gillet, Kunie Ando, Cyril Bodart, Alizée Vercauteren Drubbel, Louison Descampe, Fabiana Moresi, Xavier Bisteau, Quentin Verheye, Sheleya Pirard, Christophe Erneux, Benjamin Beck
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDrug synergy in cancer therapy has gained attention for its potential to enhance efficacy and minimize adverse effects. Synergy, where the combined effect of drugs exceeds the sum of their individual effects, is beneficial in addressing tumor heterogeneity and drug resistance in oncology. Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (eSCC), accounting for over 85% of esophageal cancer cases, has a poor prognosis and limited therapeutic options. Targeted therapies have shown inefficiency in eSCC due to resistance mechanisms, making drug combination an interesting option.MethodsTo investigate new therapeutic startegies in eSCC, we utilized a combination of multiple human eSCC cell lines, xenotransplant models, primary mouse eSCC model, CRISPR/Cas9-based genetic manipulation and RNA sequencing analysis.ResultsWe report the role of SHIP2, a phosphoinositide (PI) 5-phosphatase, in eSCC. Amplification of the INPPL1 gene, encoding SHIP2, was prevalent in eSCC, linked to elevated SHIP2 transcript levels. SHIP2 knockdown and pharmacological inhibition demonstrated its influence on eSCC cell survival, proliferation, and adhesion, partially by decreasing AKT phosphorylation and perturbing cell cycle-associated transcripts. SHIP2’s impact was not restricted to cells harboring INPPL1 amplification, implying its potential activity across diverse eSCC subtypes. Compensation mechanisms emerged in SHIP2-deficient eSCC cells, highlighting the necessity of combinatory approaches to counteract resistance. RNA sequencing identified PLK1 as a prominent downregulated transcript upon SHIP2 inhibition, revealing synergistic potential with PLK1 inhibitors.ConclusionsOur data support the use of specific SHIP2 inhibitors, in combination with PLK1 inhibitors, as a promising strategy for stalling eSCC growth, amplifying treatment response, and circumventing resistance. This study unravels the intricate interplay of SHIP2 within the PI3K/AKT pathway and cell cycle in eSCC, paving the way for targeted interventions with the potential to transform the therapeutic landscape of this challenging malignancy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-025-02454-z.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer ranks as the eighth most prevalent cancer globally and is associated with a dismal survival rate due to its aggressive nature. It manifests as either squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) or adenocarcinoma. Esophageal SCC (eSCC) accounts for over 85% of esophageal cancer cases worldwide [1]. The 5-year survival rate for metastatic esophageal cancer remains low (5% in the United States) [2], showing little improvement over the past two decades. This low survival rate is a consequence of intricate mechanisms of primary and secondary resistance to treatment. Enhancing the survival rate of these patients necessitates an improved understanding of the molecular underpinnings of this disease, coupled with translating this knowledge into innovative approaches for targeted therapy design.

The phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/AKT pathway ranks as the most frequently activated pathway in human cancers [3]. Operating downstream of several common receptor families, the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway assumes a central role in fostering cancer cell growth, survival [4], and metabolic processes [5]. Frequent cancer-associated alterations encompass (i) activating mutations in PIK3CA, the gene encoding the p110α catalytic subunit of Class IA PI3K, and (ii) loss-of-function mutations and deletions in PTEN (phosphatase and tensin homolog) [3]. Both alterations stand among the most prevalent drivers of human cancer, leading to constitutive PI3K/mTOR/AKT pathway activation. Subsequent production of phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate (PI(3,4,5)P3), and phosphatidylinositol 3,4-bisphosphate (PI(3,4)P2), trigger AKT phosphorylation at T308 by phosphoinositide-dependent protein kinase 1 (PDPK1) and at S473 by mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 2 (mTORC2) driving kinase activation [6–11]. Given the critical role of the PI3K/AKT pathway in cancer progression, multiple inhibitors have been designed to selectively dampen its activation in cancer cells. However, due to the pathway’s ubiquity and its influence on vital physiological functions, these inhibitors elicit side-effects that can compromise treatment efficacy and patients’ quality of life [12].

The production of PI(3,4)P2 via PI(3,4,5)P3 dephosphorylation plays a pivotal role in the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [13]. Dephosphorylation of phosphoinositides (PIs) is governed by a complex interplay involving 3-, 4-, and 5- PI-phosphatase activities [14–16]. Notably, PI 5-phosphatases, including OCRL, INPP5B, SHIP1/2, Synaptojanin 1/2, INPP5E, INPP5J, and INPP5K, catalyze the dephosphorylation of PI(3,4,5)P3 and PI(4,5)P2 [17, 18], to PI(3,4)P2 and PI4P respectively. PIs, such as PI(3,4)P2 and PI(4,5)P2, serve as crucial signaling molecules, notably influencing cell adhesion, migration, and invasion [19–21].

PI 5-phosphatases, particularly SHIP1/2, are pivotal enzymes capable of catalyzing PI(3,4)P2 formation by acting on PI(3,4,5)P3 thus controlling the pool of PI(3,4,5)P3 [22–24]. These enzymes may influence AKT activity, thereby regulating cancer cell survival. Notably, SHIP2 engages in scaffolding complexes with multiple proteins, thereby governing critical cellular mechanisms such as receptor docking at the plasma membrane [25, 26] and Erk pathway activation [27]. Interestingly, the role of SHIP2 in cancer cells exhibits context-dependent characteristics. While SHIP2’s role appears pro-tumorigenic in some cancer types like colorectal cancer [28], breast cancer [29–31], cervical cancer [32], laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma [33, 34], glioma [35], and hepatocarcinoma [36], it plays an anti-tumorigenic role in gastric cancers [37] and glioblastoma cells [38]. However, there are virtually no data regarding SHIP2 in esophageal cancer cells.

In this study, we unveil that esophageal SCC represents the cancer type with the highest frequency of amplification of INPPL1, the gene encoding SHIP2. Leveraging SHIP2 knockdown in vitro, we establish the regulatory influence of SHIP2 expression on eSCC cell proliferation, apoptosis and adhesion. Furthermore, our results underscore that pharmacological inhibition of SHIP2 phosphatase activity effectively suppresses eSCC cell survival both in vitro and in vivo. This was achieved by modulating AKT phosphorylation and perturbing numerous transcripts linked to cell division. However, we observe that the absence of SHIP2 in eSCC cells triggers diverse molecular mechanisms to compensate, prompting exploration of combination strategies to circumvent SHIP2 inhibition resistance. We identified PLK1 as a key downregulated transcript following SHIP2 inhibition and demonstrated a synergistic effect between SHIP2 and PLK1 inhibitors in eSCC cells, underscoring that specific SHIP2 inhibitors could represent a promising avenue for hampering eSCC growth while augmenting response to complementary anti-neoplastic treatments.

Results

INPPL1 is frequently amplified in human esophageal squamous cell carcinoma

Analysis of whole-genome sequencing data and copy number alterations (CNAs) in 96 samples of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (eSCC) from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) highlighted frequent amplification of the 11q13 locus (Fig. 1A). This frequent CNA results in the amplification of CCND1 (11q13.3) and INPPL1 (11q13.4), which encodes the SH2 containing inositol 5-phosphatase 2, known as SHIP2 (Fig.1B and Fig. S1A). In these eSCC samples, the amplification of INPPL1 is associated with reduced methylation of the gene and elevated expression of SHIP2 mRNA (Fig.1C and Fig. S1B, C). SHIP2 functions as a phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-trisphosphate 5-phosphatase (PI(3,4,5)P3 5-phosphatase) that could regulate the PI3K/AKT pathway (Fig. 1D). We examined CNAs in genes encoding PTEN, PI 4-phosphatases (INPP4A, INPP4B) and PI 5-phosphatases (SYNJ1, SYNJ2, INPP5D, INPP5K, INPP5J, and OCRL) and found that these genes are less frequently altered than INPPL1 (Fig. 1E).

Immunostaining for SHIP2 in tissue microarray (n = 60 samples) revealed high expression in human esophageal mucosa and heterogeneous expression in eSCC tumor epithelial cells (Fig. 1F and Fig. S1D). While SHIP2 expression appeared higher in well-differentiated tumors compared to non-differentiated ones (Fig. 1G), there was no apparent correlation between SHIP2 expression intensity in the tumor and its stage (Fig. 1H).

Analysis of data from the Cancer Cell Line Encyclopedia (CCLE) confirmed the positive correlation between SHIP2 mRNA expression and the absolute copy number of INPPL1 in a panel of 22 different human eSCC cell lines (Fig. 1I). Based on the INPPL1 copy number and SHIP2 mRNA expression level, three profiles can be delineated: (i) cells lacking INPPL1 amplification with low SHIP2 mRNA expression (KYSE-30, KYSE-180, and TE-11), (ii) cells with INPPL1 amplification and high SHIP2 mRNA expression (COLO-680 N, KYSE-520, and TE-14), and (iii) several eSCC lines such as the KYSE-410 line had an intermediate phenotype, characterized by an intermediate SHIP2 expression as well as the absence of INPPL1 amplification. Western blot confirmed that cells with INPPL1 amplifications had a high SHIP2 protein expression level (Fig. 1J). Importantly, transcriptomics data unveiled that SHIP2 mRNA is more abundant than any other PI 5-phosphatase. Conversely, the SHIP2 homolog, SHIP1 (encoded by INPP5D), is virtually absent from these cells (Fig. 1K). We thus aimed at determining the role of SHIP2 in eSCC cells.

SHIP2 knockdown decreases human esophagus squamous cell carcinoma cell survival

In order to assess the impact of SHIP2 on eSCC cell growth, we measured cell survival 72 h after siRNA-mediated SHIP2 knockdown (KD) across various eSCC cells (Fig. 2A). First, we focused on the KYSE-410 cell line, which harbors an intermediate level of SHIP2 expression. Treatment with two different siRNA targeting SHIP2 (siSHIP2 and siSHIP2#2) led to a reduction of KYSE-410 cell survival as compared to control siCT (Fig. 2B). The strongest impact was obtained with siSHIP2 and we focused on this siRNA for the subsequent experiments. A similar effect on survival was observed in 6 additional eSCC cell lines (Fig. 2C and Fig. S2A). The most sensitive lines being the ones with a low basal SHIP2 expression as defined above (Fig. 1J). To ascertain whether SHIP2 influences the PI3K/AKT pathway [39], we assessed AKT phosphorylation 48 h after transfection with either control siRNA or siSHIP2. We focused on the most sensitive lines (KYSE-410 and KYSE-30), and observed that in both, SHIP2 knockdown leads to a reduction in SHIP2 protein, as well as a notable decrease in AKT S473 phosphorylation (Fig. 2D). To further characterize the role of SHIP2 in eSCC cells, we profiled KYSE-410 and KYSE-30 lines 48 hours post-SHIP2 KD using RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) (Fig. 2E). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of RNA-seq data revealed a significant downregulation of transcripts associated with ‘intrinsic component of cell membrane’ and ‘cell-cell adhesion’ Hallmark gene sets following SHIP2 KD (Fig. 2F, G). These gene sets were the most significant negatively enriched in eSCC cells. Most dysregulated transcripts exhibited specificity to each cell line, and only 137 downregulated transcripts were consistent among the two profiled lines (Fig. 2H). Among these, we found tens of transcripts associated to cell-cell adhesion such as Cadherins and Protocadherins. In the same line, we observed that SHIP2 KD KYSE-410 cells had a round shape compared to control cells (Fig. S2C). Trancriptomics data also revealed the downregulation of many transcripts related to cell division such as BIRC5, TOP2A and MKI67 (Fig. 2I). In line with this observation, SHIP2 KD resulted in reduced KYSE-410 cells proliferation as measured by EdU uptake (Fig. 2J). The same impact on cell proliferation was observed in 4 additional eSCC cell lines (Fig. S2D). Moreover, pharmacological inhibition of AKT alone was sufficient to stall eSCC cell growth in vitro (Fig. S2E). Hence, SHIP2-mediated regulation of AKT phosphorylation may contribute to the modulation of cell proliferation.

Collectively, these findings suggest that SHIP2 modulates both eSCC cell adhesion and proliferation, exhibiting variable effects on the transcriptome in vitro depending on the cellular context.

Pharmacological inhibition of SHIP2 Inhibits human esophagus squamous cell carcinoma cell survival and proliferation in vitro and in vivo

To assess the potential impact of SHIP2 pharmacological inhibition to decrease human eSCC cell growth in vitro, we treated our range of eSCC cell lines with K149, a potent SHIP1/2 inhibitor [28, 40], or with AS1949490, a specific albeit less potent SHIP2 inhibitor [20, 41, 42] (Fig. 3A). Given the virtual absence of SHIP1 (encoded by INPP5D) in eSCC cells (Fig. S3A), K149 can be regarded as a SHIP2-specific inhibitor in our model. Our results indicate that a 72-hour treatment with K149 within the micromolar range led to reduced eSCC survival across seven distinct cell lines (Fig. 3B, C, Fig. S3B). Similarly, a 72-hour treatment with AS1949490 led to reduced eSCC survival across multiple cell lines (Fig. S3D).

We assessed AKT phosphorylation after a 48-hour treatment with K149 (2µM) or vehicle alone (DMSO). We focused on the most sensitive lines (KYSE-410 and COLO-680 N), and observed that in both, SHIP2 inhibition leads to a notable decrease in the global level of AKT S473 phosphorylation (Fig. 3D). Inhibition of EGF-induced pAKT S473 was also observed following K149-mediated SHIP2 inhibition in three other eSCC lines (KYSE-520, TE-11 and TE-14), but not in two lines where growth is not stalled (Fig. S3C), thus suggesting that SHIP2 influences eSCC cell survival by modulating the PI3K/AKT pathway.

To deepen our understanding of SHIP2 enzymatic activity inhibition in eSCC cells, we profiled the KYSE-410 cells after treatment with K149 or with AS1949490. Transcriptome profiling of KYSE-410 cells treated with SHIP2 inhibitors K149 (2µM) or AS1949490 (20µM) for 48 hours revealed consistent changes (Fig. 3E). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) of RNA-seq data revealed a significant downregulation of transcripts associated with ‘Mitotic cell cycle’ Hallmark genes with both compounds (Fig. 3F). By comparing KYSE-410 cells treated with K149 and AS1949490, we determined that up to 50% of the transcripts deregulated by AS1949490 were also affected by K149 (Fig. 3G). Among the most dysregulated transcripts, we found components of the AP-1 complex (FOSB, FOSL1, JUN), as well as several actors of cell cycle (BIRC5, MKI67, TOP2A). To determine which transcripts may be specifically regulated by SHIP2, we treated in parallel the same batch of KYSE-410 cells with K149 or siSHIP2 and found a core of about 100 downregulated genes, including genes like BIRC5, CDK1, MKI67, PLK1, TOP2A, thus pointing to a central role of SHIP2 in the regulation of cell proliferation (Fig. S3E). Utilizing an EdU incorporation assay upon K149 treatment, we substantiated that SHIP2 inhibition resulted in decreased cell proliferation in KYSE-410 and COLO-680N eSCC cells (Fig. 3H, I) as well as four other eSCC lines (KYSE-180, KYSE-30, TE-11, and KYSE-520) with various SHIP2 expression levels (Fig. S3F). Furthermore, K149 mediated SHIP2 inhibition also induced apoptosis in KYSE-410 and COLO-680N eSCC cells (Fig. 3J and Fig. S3G).

SHIP2 pharmacological inhibition induces apoptosis in eSCC cells in vivo

Concurrently, we conducted in vivo experiments by transplanting eSCC cells with an intermediate SHIP2 expression level (KYSE-410) into NOD/SCID mice and treating them with K149 for one week (Fig. 4A). Our results demonstrated that this treatment effectively reduced tumor epithelial cell proliferation as measured by Ki67 staining (Fig. 4B, C and Fig. S4A-C) and induced apoptosis (active-Casp3) (Fig. 4D, E and Fig. S4A-C). The same experiments were conducted with the second SHIP2 inhibitor AS1949490 and provided similar results on proliferation and apoptosis (Fig. S4A-E). These encouraging results led us to determine whether cells within a primary esophageal tumor developing in an immunocompetent animal would be sensitive to SHIP2 inhibition as well.

To this end, we treated Krt5CreER: p53flox/flox: RosaYFP mice [43] with tamoxifen to induce p53 loss of function in esophageal epithelium to promote carcinogenesis (Fig. 4F, Fig. S4D). Then, we treated these animals with 4NQO in the drinking water to induce eSCC development. Once the animals had developed tumors, we treated them with K149 or DMSO for a week. We collected the tumors and assessed apoptosis using cactive-caspase3 staining in tumor epithelial cells labeled with Keratin14 or E-cadherin. The same experiments were conducted with the second SHIP2 inhibitor AS1949490. These experiments revealed that SHIP2 inhibition with both drugs induced a significant increase of apoptosis, as observed in human eSCC xenografts (Fig. 4G, H, Fig. S4D, E).

Collectively, these results underscore the potential of pharmacological SHIP2 inhibition as an innovative strategy for curtailing eSCC cell survival/proliferation both on cells in vitro and in mice in vivo.

SHIP2 Inhibition in eSCC unravels compensatory mechanisms

To assess the viability of blocking SHIP2 as a potential strategy for eSCC treatment, we characterized the outcomes of SHIP2 knockout (KO). Leveraging CRISPR/Cas9 technology, we generated four distinct KYSE-410 SHIP2 KO eSCC lines originating from individual cell colonies (Fig. 5A). SHIP2 protein expression remained undetectable in all four KO lines (Fig. 5B). Seeking to identify potential adaptive mechanisms to SHIP2 loss-of-function, we subjected the four KO lines and a control line (transfected with the same plasmid but devoid of guide RNA) to RNA-seq analysis. The data revealed a cluster comprising three SHIP2 KO lines (D4, G10, and H3) exhibiting distinct transcriptional profiles from the control line, while a fourth line (G4) displayed a comparable profile (Fig. 5C and Fig. S5A). Comparative analysis across these lines highlighted their individual differences (Fig. 5D). Employing the K-means method, we observed global modifications in ion transport and cell differentiation processes in these three lines (Fig. 5D). Remarkably, the three lines with the greatest divergence from the control line (D4, G10, and H3) displayed an upregulation of another PI 5-phosphatase (INPP5J) and several regulators of calcium signaling (STIM1, TRPC6 and TRPV6), as well as a downregulation of transcripts associated with the Notch pathway (HES2, HEY2) and squamous differentiation (EDAR, FLG, KRT6B, and OVOL1). (Fig. 5E).

In these SHIP2 KO lines, AKT phosphorylation levels displayed a variable reduction in three out of the four lines (Fig. 5F). We focused on the KO lines with the highest pAKT S473 expression (G4 and G10) and did not measure variations in their growth rate compared to the parental line (Fig. 5G). In these KO lines, where the impact of SHIP2 loss-of-function on cell growth and AKT phosphorylation is compensated, we did not detect variations in the expression of PI 5-phosphatases at the mRNA level except for INPP5J (Fig. 5H). However, INPP5J protein levels were low and showed only a marginal increase in SHIP2 KO cells (Fig. S5C). Therefore, it remains unclear whether INPP5J upregulation alone is sufficient to compensate for the absence of SHIP2. The G10 line was characterized by a significant upregulation of INPP4B and PIK3C2A that may modify the PI(3,4)P2 patterns [44] (Fig. S5D). In this line, devoid of SHIP2 mRNA and protein, we observed that the SHIP2 inhibitors K149 and AS1949490 had virtually no impact on cell survival thus demonstrating that at the concentration used in this study, these drugs are SHIP2 specific in our model (Fig. S5E). In addition, even at a concentration of 20 µM, AS1949490 had virtually no impact on AKT phosphorylation in SHIP2 KO cells (Fig. S5F). Interestingly, the G4 line, which has pAKT S473 levels similar to the control line, displayed no variation in the expression of the main enzymes involved in PIP3/PIP2 metabolism, suggesting other types of compensation. SHIP1 (INPP5D) was not detected in any of these engineered lines (Fig. 5H). Together, these findings suggest the potential for compensatory mechanisms following SHIP2 loss, at least in vitro, and that exclusive SHIP2 inhibition (monotherapy) may prove ineffective for sustained eSCC treatment over extended periods due to the development of secondary resistance.

SHIP2 Inhibition sensitizes eSCC cells to PLK1 inhibitor

In an effort to uncover vulnerabilities induced by SHIP2 pharmacological inhibition, we conducted a screening of the most prominently downregulated transcripts subsequent to SHIP2 inhibitor treatment for 48 h (Fig. 6A). Within the top 30 most significantly downregulated genes following K149 treatment, 16 were directly associated with cell division, including polo-like kinase-1 (PLK1) (Fig. 6A). Of note, PLK1 is downregulated following treatment with AS1949490 (Fig. S6A), but also following siRNA-mediated SHIP2 knockdown (Fig. S3E). The impact of SHIP2 pharmacological inhibitors K149 and AS1949490 on PLK1 expression was confirmed by Western blot in two distinct cell lines KYSE-410 and COLO-680N (Fig. 6B).

Motivated by these findings, we sought to discern whether concurrent SHIP2 and PLK1 inhibition could have additional effects on cell survival in vitro. Volasertib, a potent PLK1 inhibitor that showed clinical activity in a phase I/II study in adult patients with relapsed/refractory acute myeloid leukemia [45], emerged as a salient candidate for exploration. To this end, we exposed KYSE-410 cells to range of concentrations of K149, Volasertib, or a dual application of both compounds (Fig. 6C). Leveraging SynergyFinder 3.0 [46], analyses revealed ZIP scores consistently exceeding 10, indicative of robust synergistic interactions (Fig. 6C and Fig. S6C). These results prompted us to quantitatively assess the synergy with other inhibitors. We swapped the SHIP2 inhibitor (AS1949490) or the PLK1 inhibitor (GSK461364) and obtained ZIP scores exceeding 20, confirming robust synergistic interactions between SHIP2 inhibiton and PLK1 inhibiton. The same synergy between K149 and Volasertib was observed in two other eSCC cell line (KYSE-30 and COLO-680N) (Fig. 6D). To challenge this result, we then treated KYSE-410, KYSE-30 and COLO-680N cells with low concentrations of Volasertib and K149. Strikingly, in KYSE-410 cells, while each drug alone had virtually no impact on eSCC cell survival, the combination of the two inhibitors decreased cells survival by 25% (Fig. 6E). In the same line, a K149 concentration insufficient to significantly attenuate cell survival was sufficient to enhance Volasertib’s impact in TE-11 and KYSE-30 cells (Fig. 6E and Fig. S6D). In KYSE-410 cells, a low concentration of K149 significantly enhanced Volasertib’s impact on both cell proliferation and apoptosis (Fig. S6E-H).

To elucidate the mechanistic basis of this synergistic interaction, we investigated whether PLK1 inhibition affected SHIP2 expression. Western blot analysis showed that treatment with the PLK1 inhibitors Volasertib and GSK461364 decreased SHIP2 protein levels in two distinct esophageal cancer cell lines (KYSE-410 and COLO-680N) (Fig. 6F). These findings suggest a reciprocal regulatory relationship between SHIP2 and PLK1 (Fig. 6G), which may explain the synergistic effect observed when targeting both proteins.

Discussion

The PI3K/AKT pathway is overactivated in esophageal cancer

In esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (eSCC) we identified copy-number alterations showing significant co-amplification of CCND1 with either PIK3CA or INPPL1 (SHIP2). INPPL1 amplification is likewise frequent in head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). The 11q13 locus that contains INPPL1 is amplified in approximately 30% of HPV-negative HNSCC cases according to the TCGA PanCancer Atlas (cBioPortal). By contrast, INPPL1 amplification is uncommon in colorectal and breast cancers, even though SHIP2 protein is highly expressed in experimental models [28, 31, 40, 47]. These data suggest that INPPL1 may be a passenger event associated with 11q13 gain. Because eSCC and HNSCC share major risk factors such as alcohol consumption and tobacco use [48, 49], these exposures might also promote INPPL1 amplification.

Oncogenic activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway reprograms cell metabolism to meet the energy needs of rapidly proliferating cancer cells [50]. This suggests that the PI3K pathway could promote eSCC cells growth that have elevated Cyclin D1 expression. Active AKT phosphorylates a wide array of target proteins, thereby influencing numerous biological processes like cell growth, proliferation, survival, and metabolism. It is particularly noteworthy that AKT phosphorylation was significantly higher in eSCC tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (90.4% vs. 27.7%), underscoring the pathway’s importance in tumor development [51]. While the role of AKT in sustaining cell survival across cancers is well known, little is known about its interaction with SHIP2 and the role of SHIP2 in promoting tumour growth. This prompted us to study the role of SHIP2 in a large panel of eSCC cells.

SHIP2 has a pro-tumorigenic role in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma

ESCC is the cancer type in which INPPL1 is most frequently amplified according to TCGA data [52]. The inositol polyphosphate or PI 5-phosphatases (INPP5Ps) constitutes a large family of enzymes in the human genome. These enzymes are responsible for the dephosphorylation of PI(3,4,5)P3 and PI(4,5)P2 at the 5-phosphate position of the inositol ring [17, 18], thereby acting as potential tumor suppressors. Consistent with their potential tumor-suppressive role, SHIP2 has been found to inhibit AKT activation in the U-87 glioblastoma cell line [38] and gastric cancer cells [37]. However, elevated SHIP2 expression has also been reported in non-small cell lung cancer [53], hepatocellular cancer [36], colorectal cancer [28, 47], laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma [33] and basal breast cancer [31], where it is associated with poor patient outcomes. Pharmacological inhibition of SHIP2 with K149 in breast [40] and colorectal cancer [28] models has further demonstrated its role in regulating AKT signaling and promoting cell survival. In the context of eSCC, our results indicate that SHIP2 may have a pro-tumorigenic role also. Specifically, both SHIP2 knockdown and pharmacological inhibition by two distinct SHIP2 inhibitors led to a reduced cancer cell survival concomitant with a decrease in AKT phosphorylation levels. Notably, even cells lacking INPPL1 amplification and exhibiting low SHIP2 expression levels were still sensitive to both SHIP2 knockdown and pharmacological inhibition, both in vitro and in vivo. Together, these results suggest that targeting SHIP2 could benefit a broad spectrum of eSCC patients, not just those with INPPL1 amplification.

Potential catalytic and non-catalytic functions of SHIP2

PI(4,5)P2 serves as the second major substrate for PI 5-phosphatases, leading to the production of phosphatidylinositol 4-phosphate [54]. PI(4,5)P2 functions as a signaling molecule in various biological processes such as endocytosis, exocytosis, cellular migration, adhesion, and ion channel regulation. Its levels can be modulated by different members of the PI 5-phosphatase family, including SHIP2 [55–57]. For example, SHIP2 targets PI(4,5)P2 in certain glioblastoma cell lines, while predominantly affecting PI(3,4,5)P3 in others [19].

On one hand, our research reveals that SHIP2 knockdown drastically alters the expression of transcripts related to cell adhesion, resulting in a distinct, rounded cell phenotype as opposed to the flat, spread-out shape of control cells. Interestingly, this phenotype is not observed when cells are treated with a SHIP2 pharmacological inhibitor, suggesting that SHIP2’s non-catalytic interactions with other proteins might play a crucial role in regulating cell adhesion. On the other hand, pharmacological inhibition of SHIP2 suffices to reduce eSCC cell survival across multiple lines. One hypothesis is that SHIP2’s regulation of AKT is fundamental for cell survival. Meanwhile, AKT-independent functions of SHIP2 may govern processes like cell adhesion and migration in eSCC.

SHIP2 is at the crossroad of several signaling pathways

SHIP2 is a critical regulator of cellular signaling pathways, specifically affecting the PI3K/AKT and Erk/MAPK pathways. It is also implicated in the ligand-induced uptake of a wide range of G-protein-coupled receptors, including adrenergic and dopaminergic receptors, and various receptor tyrosine kinases like EGFR, HGFR, VEGFR, PDGFR, NGFR, and IGF1R [58]. Importantly, SHIP2 serves as a scaffolding complex, interacting with multiple proteins to control vital cellular functions such as receptor docking at the plasma membrane and activation of the Erk pathway [27]. In this line, recent findings indicate that a specific lung cancer mutation in EGFR (P1019L), when phosphorylated at Y1016, exhibits enhanced interaction with SHIP2. This interaction further amplifies SHC1 engagement with the mutant receptor in an EGF-dependent manner, leading to sustained ERK and AKT signaling in A549 human lung cancer cells, thereby promoting cell migration and invasiveness [59]. This suggests that SHIP2, acting as an adaptor, may facilitate the signaling shifts responsible for the oncogenic capabilities of mutant EGF receptors. Depending on the cellular context, SHIP2’s role therefore extends beyond merely regulating the PI3K/AKT pathway through its PI 5-phosphatase activity.

In colorectal cancer models, SHIP2 knockdown (KD) has minimal impact on cell adhesion [28], but it does reduce AKT signaling without affecting cell proliferation. On the opposite, our data reveal that inhibition of SHIP2 in eSCC cells hampers AKT phosphorylation upon EGF stimulation, decreasing cell proliferation. To better understand this phenomenon, future work should focus on evaluating how SHIP2 affects these signaling pathways, including investigating potential changes in EGFR expression and its interaction partners. Moreover, to identify which functions are attributable to the catalytic domain or the adaptor activity, further experiments will be necessary to create eSCC cell lines with various SHIP2 alterations and explore their impact on signaling and cellular physiology. This field would require further investigations, notably by using catalytically inactive mutants and domain (i.e. SH2, SAM, proline rich sequences) deletions to clarify SHIP2’s mechanistic roles in eSCC.

The functional role of SHIP2 in cancer cells appears to be context-dependent and may be influenced by its subcellular localization. The dynamic cellular localization of PI 5-phosphatases, including SHIP2 and SKIP, varies in response to external stimuli [60]. These enzymes are found in a variety of cellular locations such as plasma membranes, ruffles, the endoplasmic reticulum, and even the nucleus [60]. Given this, studying the subcellular localization of SHIP2 in esophageal SCC could offer valuable insights into its role in cell survival. Although our data suggest that SHIP2 is at the crossroads of several pathways, we found that its loss can be compensated by different means, raising the question of its use as a therapeutic target.

SHIP2 as a pharmacological target in esophageal cancer

An important feature we observed in esophageal cancer is that pharmacological inhibition of SHIP2 hinders eSCC cell survival and proliferation both in vitro and in vivo, emphasizing the importance of PI 5-phosphatase activity in these cells. Targeting SHIP2 could, therefore, offer promising clinical implications for treating this form of cancer. In line with this concept, SHIP1-selective inhibition has been shown to effectively eradicate multiple myeloma in mice [40]. Given that pharmacological targeting of the PI3K/AKT pathway often results in cellular resistance mechanisms, such as genomic and/or epigenetic modifications [61], preventing such resistance during SHIP2 inhibition will be a challenge. The balance between PI kinases and phosphatases tightly regulates the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway. For example, miR-508 has been reported to suppress various phosphatases, including INPP5J, PTEN, and INPP4A, leading to the sustained activation of PI3K/AKT signaling [62]. Similar mechanisms could possibly contribute to resistance against PI 5-phosphatase inhibitors in esophageal cancers.

Our data show that SHIP2 knockout (KO) single-cell colonies are transcriptionally heterogeneous, with some clones resembling the parental line and displaying normal levels of AKT phosphorylation. The molecular mechanisms underlying this compensation are likely multiple and complex. Future research should employ complementary approaches such as lipidomics (to quantify phosphoinositides like PI(3,4,5)P3 and PI(3,4)P2), proteomics, and phosphoproteomics to refine our understanding of these adaptive processes. Additionally, it will be important to determine whether resistance to pharmacological inhibitors involves mechanisms similar to those driving genetic compensation in SHIP2 KO cells.

A second unexpected feature of our study is that concurrent inhibition of SHIP2 and PLK1 effectively blocks tumor cell growth in vitro. This is consistent with a study on CACO-2 and HCT116 colorectal cancer cells, which found that K149 sensitizes these cells to 5-FU-induced cell death [37]. Using lower concentrations of each drug in the combination also maintains the anti-proliferative effect while potentially reducing toxicity, an important consideration given that drugs are usually administered at their maximum tolerated doses. Such lower-dose combinations have shown promise in animal models and in vitro studies of ovarian clear cell carcinoma [63] and non-small cell lung carcinoma [64]. It will be important to determine whether concurrent inhibition of SHIP2 and PLK1 is effective specifically in tumors with INPPL1 amplification, such as eSCC and HNSCC, or whether this strategy is also effective across other SHIP2-high tumors, including breast and colorectal cancers.

In line with this, characterizing tumor sensitivity to the SHIP2/PLK1 inhibitor combination could help identify predictive biomarkers for cancers most likely to respond to this strategy. These biomarkers may be genetic, such as INPPL1 amplification, or transcriptomic, including elevated SHIP2 expression. However, response is likely to be cell-type dependent and influenced by the expression patterns of related enzymes, as well as by the mutational status of key signaling components such as PI3K and AKT.

Molecular basis of synergy between SHIP2 and PLK1 inhibitors on cell growth

Drug synergy occurs when the combined effect of two or more drugs is greater than the sum of their individual effects, potentially leading to improved patient outcomes in cancer treatment [65]. Our findings show that inhibiting SHIP2 enhances the sensitivity of cells to PLK1 inhibitors. This enhancement appears to be mediated by a reduction in PLK1 expression levels and a decrease in AKT phosphorylation following SHIP2 inhibition. Utilizing the thymidine block method to arrest cells in the G2/M phase has been shown to increase sensitivity to Volasertib [66]. Moreover, co-treatment with a PI3K/AKT pathway inhibitor, LY294002, yields additive effects when used with Volasertib in AML cell lines. This points to a potential interaction between G2/M progression and the PI3K pathway [66]. In addition, it has been reported that PLK1 phosphorylation by the PI3K/AKT pathway encourages mitosis, whereas inhibiting this pathway delays the transition from metaphase to anaphase [67]. A recent study also demonstrated that TC/miR-22 and Volasertib synergistically inhibit the PI3K/AKT pathway in osteosarcoma [68]. Collectively, these results indicate that co-inhibiting the PI3K/AKT pathway with SHIP2 inhibitors and with PLK1 inhibitors could be a powerful strategy for cell cycle regulation. While current SHIP2 inhibitors are not yet suitable for clinical trials [69], this combination strategy appears promising for halting the growth of eSCC and perhaps other cancers in future studies. Accordingly, future research should prioritise the development of highly SHIP2-selective inhibitors with lower hydrophobicity than AS1949490, capable of achieving good systemic exposure in vivo while sparing the SHIP1-dependent hematopoietic compartment [70, 71].

In conclusion, our research delineates the role of SHIP2 in human eSCC cells and establishes a foundation for innovative therapeutic approaches. These approaches would involve the co-inhibition of both SHIP2 and PLK1, highlighting the need for the development of new SHIP2-specific inhibitors.
