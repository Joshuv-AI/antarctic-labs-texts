Changes in inflammatory edema and fat fraction of thigh muscles following a half‐marathon in recreational marathon runners
Authors: Dingbo Shu, Jianping Wang, Fanjing Meng, Siyu Dai, Zhenhua Zhao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIt is known that microtrauma exists in the thigh muscles after long‐distance running such as the half‐marathon. Moreover, training characteristics of long‐distance runners may influence the specificity of the distribution of muscle fiber types in the thigh and affect muscle responses to lipid metabolism. However, the specific changes in microtrauma and intramuscular lipid in thigh muscles after a half‐marathon are unknown. A cohort of 20 healthy recreational marathon runners was recruited to complete a half‐marathon. MRI T2 mapping and 6‐echo q‐Dixon sequences were employed at baseline (P1), 2–3 h after running (P2), and 1 day after running (P3). Inflammatory markers (the T2 values) and intramuscular fat fraction (the proton density fat fraction, PDFF) were measured in thigh muscles to detect microtrauma and intramuscular lipid changes, respectively. One‐way analysis of variance showed significant time effects for T2 values and PDFF. Post hoc analysis of the 14 datasets collected at three time points revealed significantly higher T2 values in all thigh muscles after running (all p < 0.05). Significant differences in T2 values persisted for all thigh muscles at P3 compared to P1 (all p < 0.05). The PDFF of the vastus lateralis and vastus medialis was significantly decreased at P2 compared to P1 (p < 0.05). No significant differences in PDFF were observed for the thigh muscles at P3 compared to P1. The manifestations of inflammation edema and intramuscular lipid investigated through MRI may offer valuable insights for recreational marathon runners regarding the lower limb movement characteristics during half‐marathon running.

INTRODUCTION

The sport of distance running is increasingly prevalent and growing in popularity worldwide, providing both physical and psychological benefits to the runners (Shu et al., 2021; Wirnitzer et al., 2021). Over the past 2 decades, the number of participants in long‐distance running races has increased, with half‐marathon finishers outnumbering those in marathon races (Nikolaidis & Knechtle, 2023; Wirnitzer et al., 2021). This prolonged exercise induces a series of physiological changes, such as microtrauma to the thigh muscles and depletion of intramuscular lipids, which can affect the runner's training and recovery (Brechtel et al., 2001; Higashihara et al., 2020; Hooijmans et al., 2020). Thus, gaining insights into microtrauma and intramuscular lipid changes in thigh muscles following long‐distance running may unveil novel perspectives on their movement characteristics.

Several prior studies have concentrated on damage or altered mechanical properties of lower limb muscles. For instance, MRI studies have found segmental differences in inter and intramuscular microtrauma within thigh muscles post long‐distance running (Higashihara et al., 2020; Hooijmans et al., 2020). Concurrently, ultrasound elastography studies have revealed heterogeneous changes in lower limb muscle stiffness after prolonged running (Inami et al., 2019; Nakagawa et al., 2018). In addition, the composition of muscle fiber types may undergo alterations based on the exercise type (Bergh et al., 1978). Variations in the intramyocellular lipid (IMCL) content among different fiber types can result in disparities in their storage and utilization patterns (Zehnder et al., 2006). Particularly in endurance exercise, lipid utilization may be influenced by the lipid stored within muscle cells (Larson‐Meyer et al., 2008). Numerous studies have established that a substantial amount of intramuscular lipid in skeletal muscle serves as an important fuel source during moderate‐intensity exercise lasting 90 min or more (Essén, 1977; Hurley et al., 1986; van Loon, 2004).

Muscle functional MRI allows noninvasive examination of specific physiological conditions of muscles. For example, MRI T2 mapping sequence has been applied to detect exercise‐induced muscle inflammatory edema changes reflected by T2 values and to monitor recovery from the microtrauma (Higashihara et al., 2020; Miyamoto & Kawakami, 2014; Shu et al., 2021). Moreover, the MRI q‐Dixon sequence, capable of differentiating fat and water within the muscle and creating a fat fraction map based on collected data, provides a precise quantification of the intramuscular fat fraction (Dixon, 1984; Huber et al., 2020).The reliability of this technique in detecting intramuscular lipid rivals that of muscle biopsy (Smith et al., 2014), offering a broader scope of examination and superior repeatability (Burakiewicz et al., 2017).

Therefore, the objective of this study was to evaluate inflammatory edema and intramuscular lipid changes in thigh muscles after a half‐marathon using T2 mapping and 6 echo q‐Dixon sequences, respectively. We hypothesized that inflammation edema in specific thigh muscles would progress and intramuscular lipid would decrease after running, with distinctive trends emerging after a day of rest.

METHODS

Participants

Following the effect size of 0.5, α of 0.05, and power of 90%, our calculations determined that a minimum of 12 participants were necessary for this one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with repeated measures. We successfully recruited 20 recreational marathon runners, 17 males and 3 females, from the Zhejiang University Entrepreneurs Outdoor Association, which is cooperating with our Institute of Sport Medicine. Informed consent was procured from all participants prior to any data acquisition.

Demographic details and relevant running‐related information, including name, age, gender, monthly running distance, and the pace during half‐marathon, were collated through questionnaires completed by the participants.

Recreational marathon runners were defined as runners who had not undergone formal half‐marathon running training and whose occupation was unrelated to running. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a history of regular long‐distance running for more than 1 year, (2) engagement in three or more regular weekly running sessions, (3) monthly running distance no less than 100 km and, (4) participation in a formal half‐marathon or longer race at least once in the past year. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) contraindications to MRI and (2) musculoskeletal injuries of the lower limbs.

Study design

Participants were rigorously restricted from long‐distance running (more than 5 km) for a minimum of 7 days and refrained from any form of exercise for at least 5 days preceding the baseline MRI scan. During the examination period, participants adhered to their regular dietary habits, consuming customary foods, dishes, and ingredients without stringent dietary controls. MRI data were collected from all participants at three times: baseline (P1), 2–3 h after half‐marathon running (P2), and 1 day after half‐marathon running (P3). Participants lay on the MRI examination table for a duration of 30 min prior to each scan. The half‐marathon (21.0975 km) was a constructed race that was completed on the flat roads near our laboratory. To capture running related information, including average speed, time, and distance, a mobile application (Ledong Information Technology Co., Ltd.) was employed. Subjects were asked to keep their RPE scale level below 15 throughout the half‐marathon running.

MRI imaging acquisition

All MRI data were collected using a 1.5 T MRI scanner (Magnetom Aera, Siemens Healthcare) equipped with an 18‐channel body coil. Participants assumed a supine position, extending both legs fully inside the coil. To circumvent motion artifacts, placed sponges filled the gaps within the coil in the coil in order to prevent movement artifacts.

Axial MRI images from the greater trochanter to the medial condyle of the femur were collected. The imaging protocol commenced with the acquisition of axial plane T1‐weighted anatomical scans (TR = 850 ms, TE = 12 ms, FOV = 400 mm, thickness = 3.5 mm, average = 2, and scan time 2 min 34 s). T2 mapping sequence (TR = 2000 ms, TE = 25, 50, 75, and 100 ms, and FOV = 400 mm; thickness = 10 mm; average = 1; and scan time 3 min) was also collected. Subsequently, 6‐echo q‐Dixon sequence (TR = 15.62 ms, TE = 2.38, 4.76, 7.14, 9.52, 11.90, and 14.28 ms, FOV = 400 mm, slice thickness = 3.5 mm, average = 1, and scan time 21 s) was collected. The selection of different slice thicknesses was done to ensure image quality while keeping the scan time short.

Image analysis

T2 mapping and 6‐echo q‐Dixon sequences were employed to study the inflammatory edema and intramuscular fat fraction within the thigh muscles following the half‐marathon. Initially, the midpoint line for the left thigh region (from the greater trochanter to the medial condyle of the femur) was used as the anatomical reference (Higashihara et al., 2020). The T1 sequence served as anatomical reference for T2 mapping and q‐Dixon images. Subsequently, four most adjacent slices to the midpoint line (two above and two below) within the T2 mapping sequence as well as 12 most adjacent slices to the line (six above and six below) within the q‐Dixon sequence were further processed. This approach ensured that the processed T2 mapping images (total thickness: 40 mm) and q‐Dixon images (total thickness: 42 mm) represented approximately the area situated in the middle of the thigh.

For T2 and q‐Dixon images processing, thigh muscles, including biceps femoris long head (BFL), adductor magnus (AM), semimembranosus (SM), semitendinosus (ST), vastus intermedius, vastus lateralis (VL), vastus medialis (VM), and rectus femoris (RF), were manually segmented on the selected T2‐weighted and fat fraction images using the ImageJ software (NIH). Care was taken to avoid including any noncontractile tissue.

The obtained T2 values were averaged over four selected slices of each muscle and reported as T2 values for each participant; the obtained proton density fat fraction (PDFF) from q‐Dixon images was averaged over 12 selected slices of each muscle and reported as intramuscular fat fraction for each participant. Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS version 20.0 (IBM Corp.). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to verify the normal distribution of the T2 values and PDFF. A paired sample t‐test was carried out to analyze if there were significant differences between pre‐ and post‐run for T2 values and PDFF. In addition, a repeated measures ANOVA was performed on the parameters of each of the 14 participants who completed the follow‐up. Bonferroni adjustment was applied as needed for post hoc analysis. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Quantitative MRI parameters

In the analysis of the 20 datasets encompassing pre‐ and post‐run testing, paired t‐tests revealed a significant increase in T2 values across all thigh muscles post‐run (all p < 0.05). Concurrently, PDFF exhibited a significant decrease in all muscles (p < 0.05), except RF (p = 0.092), ST (p = 0.133), SM (p = 0.073), and AM (p = 0.086).

Among the 14 datasets completing assessments at three time points, post hoc analysis demonstrated significantly higher T2 values in all thigh muscles at P2 compared to P1 (all p < 0.05, Figure 2). Furthermore, a persistent significant difference in T2 values was observed for all thigh muscles at P3 compared to P1 (all p < 0.05).

DISCUSSION

In this investigation, we employed MRI T2 mapping and 6‐echo q‐Dixon to noninvasively assess inflammatory edema and intramuscular lipid content in thigh muscles following a half‐marathon. Our findings revealed a significant elevation in T2 values after completing a half‐marathon, and this elevation persisted until 1 day after the run. Meanwhile, a decrease in PDFF was observed only in specific muscles (VL and VM). These results suggest that all examined thigh muscles developed inflammatory edema following the half‐marathon, persisting until the day after the run. Furthermore, intramuscular lipid depletion and subsequent recovery were observed in only a subset of thigh muscles.

The T2 values of the thigh muscles were significantly higher at P2 compared to P1, consistent with previous findings that observed elevated T2 values in thigh muscles post‐run (Miyamoto & Kawakami, 2014; Shu et al., 2021). The T2 values serve as a marker of muscle inflammation, closely associated with the severity of muscle damage (Maillard et al., 2004). This might be the result of muscle microtrauma (inflammatory edema) caused by eccentric contractions involving the forced active lengthening or stretching of muscles during the half‐marathon running (Hooijmans et al., 2020; Shellock et al., 1991). As for the post‐running period, previous studies have suggested that T2 values peak 24–72 h after marathon exercise (Higashihara et al., 2020; Maeo et al., 2018; Yanagisawa et al., 2011), and our observations confirm that T2 values in the eight muscles remained significantly higher at P3 than P1. The T2 value is believed to correlate with intra‐tissue water content (Park et al., 1990; Summers et al., 1998). It is influenced by a wide range of physiological alterations likely driven by inflammation and/or edema (Fulford et al., 2015; Hazlewood et al., 1974; Hooijmans et al., 2020). The mechanical effects of eccentric contractions during the half‐marathon running cause immediate muscle microtrauma (Jones et al., 1987; Shellock et al., 1991). Moreover, due to mechanical and/or biochemical factors, the muscle microtrauma gradually increased in the following days and manifested as elevated T2 values on MRI (Hooijmans et al., 2020; Jones et al., 1987; Shellock et al., 1991).

In terms of intramuscular fat fraction, our observations indicated lower PDFF in only VL and VM at P2 compared to P1. The muscles constituting the muscular system contain varying proportions of muscle fiber types (Tamura et al., 2008). Histological studies have indicated that type I fibers contain three times more IMCL than type II fibers (Essén et al., 1975). Tamura et al. discovered that the soleus muscle contained three times more IMCL than the tibialis anterior muscle (Tamura et al., 2008), potentially due to the soleus being rich in type I muscle fibers, while the tibialis anterior predominantly contains type II fibers (Polgar et al., 1973). Therefore, the differences in PDFF of VL and VM from other thigh muscles observed in this study may be explained by differences in muscle fiber content and IMCL. However, this point has not been supported by histological studies of fiber type differences between thigh muscles. Besides anatomical differences, training characteristics also play a role. Clarkson et al. proposed that the muscle fiber composition may vary based on the training characteristics of individuals (Clarkson et al., 1980). Additionally, Methenitis et al. found variations in the ratio of type II to type I fibers in the VL among sedentary, endurance, power, and strength groups (Methenitis et al., 2019). These variations in muscle fiber composition and intramuscular lipid content may contribute to differences in intramuscular lipid utilization rates (Zehnder et al., 2006). These findings indicate that the differences in thigh muscle PDFF changes observed in recreational marathon runners in this study might also result from the specificity of long‐distance running training. Specific movement characteristics will result in transitions in the type of muscle fibers, leading to differences in the distribution of muscle fiber types (Gehlert et al., 2012). After rest, in both VL and VM, we noted that PDFF at P3 was no longer significantly different compared to P1, indicating a general restoration of intramuscular lipid content. This finding aligns with prior research demonstrating that IMCL levels can be restored within a day after exercise (Boesch et al., 1999; Décombaz et al., 2001; Larson‐Meyer et al., 2002).

It is noteworthy that the postexercise physiological surge in muscle water content may contribute to a potential reduction in the fat fraction, reflecting a relative decline in lipid content due to an overall increase in muscle volume. Nevertheless, our analysis indicates that while PDFF had reverted to baseline levels, T2 values remained elevated 1 day after the running session. This discrepancy suggests that the marginal augmentation in muscle water content post‐run may contribute weakly to changes in muscle PDFF.

Strengths and limitations

This study utilized MRI T2 mapping and 6‐echo q‐Dixon sequences to noninvasively investigate inflammation edema and intramuscular lipid changes in thigh muscles subsequent to half‐marathon running. Nonetheless, there remain a few limitations. PDFF measurements of total intramuscular lipid may obscure subtle variations in IMCL, and comparative analyses of the differences in intramuscular lipid changes detected by the 6‐echo q‐Dixon and MRS sequences can help underscore this disparity. Additionally, dietary intake was not strictly controlled due to participant compliance, potentially influencing intramyocellular lipid content, especially considering the impact of dietary fat (Tamura et al., 2008). Including compliant elite athletes in future studies may help to minimize this bias. Finally, significant PDFF changes were only observed in specific muscles in this study, whereas other muscles may have insufficiently utilized intramuscular lipids due to the running distance. Future studies could extend the running distance and utilize more measurements, such as serology and biopsy, to directly observe changes in muscle inflammation and PDFF to further investigate the movement characteristics of the lower limb during long‐distance running.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, this study revealed the increase of T2 values within thigh muscles subsequent to a half‐marathon, indicative of the presence of inflammatory edema. Moreover, the observed decline in PDFF in VL and VM exhibited recovery after 1 day of rest, suggesting dynamic alterations in intramuscular lipid content. This may offer valuable insights for recreational marathon runners regarding the lower limb movement characteristics during half‐marathon running.
