Are we overlooking the power of community in saving hearts?
Authors: Jose Eric M Lacsa
Published: 2025

Dear Esteemed Editor,

I read with great interest the recent study by Sébastien Pugnale et al., which examined the impact of the Volunteer Community Responders (VCR) Network on out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) outcomes in the Swiss Canton of Fribourg.1 Despite higher rates of pre-EMS CPR and AED use in the VCR group, the study found no significant independent association with return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), hospital admission, or discharge, highlighting the critical need to rethink responder deployment strategies. This insight holds particular resonance for low- and middle-income countries like the Philippines, where formal emergency medical services (EMS) often arrive too late and the first responder is typically a neighbor, not a medic.

In our congested cities and far-flung barangays, ambulances may be scarce, and minutes can mean the difference between life and death. But communities are rich in people willing to help. What we lack in equipment, we can begin to address through systems and trust. If neighbors, barangay health workers, and civic volunteers are trained and geolocated, they can be mobilized through basic SMS or mobile alerts to respond quickly and effectively.2

Singapore’s “myResponder” app is one such model that improved bystander intervention through digital coordination.3 In India, CPR training programs for laypersons have already empowered ordinary citizens to make life-saving decisions.4 For the Philippines and similar settings, a localized and low-tech adaptation of these models, grounded in community trust and supported by public health agencies, may prove more impactful than mimicking complex systems that don’t translate well into fragile infrastructures.

The lesson from Switzerland is not that community responder networks fail, but that they must be optimized within their context. When responders are delayed or deployed ineffectively, even well-intentioned networks fall short. In our case, where traffic, distance, and underfunded health systems widen the survival gap, investing in community capacity and communication can narrow it.

In the Philippines, the response to out-of-hospital cardiac arrest is hindered by multiple systemic challenges. Bystander CPR rates remain critically low, often below 5 %, due to limited public training and awareness. Automated External Defibrillators (AEDs) are scarce outside major urban centers and are infrequently used by laypersons because of limited accessibility and confidence. Return of Spontaneous Circulation (ROSC) and survival to hospital discharge rates are correspondingly low, with some hospital studies reporting ROSC rates under 10 %. Contributing factors include prolonged ambulance response times caused by traffic congestion, fragmented emergency medical services, and the absence of a national cardiac arrest registry. While local initiatives to improve community CPR training and first responder networks exist, these remain insufficiently scaled. This context underscores the urgency of mobilizing trusted community responders and developing low-tech, culturally grounded emergency response systems tailored to the Philippines.

Globally, OHCA remains a leading cause of preventable death.5 The challenge before us is not just technological or logistical, it is structural and relational. We need systems that activate people already there, already willing, and already trusted. Because sometimes, the hand that saves your life is not from an ambulance, it’s from the house next door.
