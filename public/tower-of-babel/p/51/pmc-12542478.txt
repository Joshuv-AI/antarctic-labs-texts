Comparative and phylogenetic analyses of Stipa L. (Poaceae) species based on the complete chloroplast genome sequences
Authors: Dongqing Yan, Xiaoyan Cao, Yang Ma, Jianming Niu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStipa L., a genus of Poaceae, comprises over 150 species and is mainly distributed in Eurasia. Stipa species dominate the open grasslands and steppes in a variety of climates and habitats. The phylogenetic relationships within this genus are still controversial and unresolved. In this study, we sequenced and downloaded chloroplast genomes of 22 and 16 Stipa species, respectively. Subsequently, we conducted comparative analysis and constructed the phylogenetic tree using 38 plastid genomes (plastomes).ResultsStipa chloroplast genomes demonstrated relatively conserved typical quadripartite structure, with length ranged from 137,120 bp to 137,859 bp. In total, the chloroplast genomes of Stipa species contained 131 genes, comprising 85 protein-coding genes, 38 tRNAs, and eight rRNAs. A total of 1,496 simple sequence repeats (SSRs) were identified and nine mutational hotspots were screened out, including psbA, psbZ-trnG-GCC, trnG-UCC-trnT-GGU, petN-trnC-GCA, rbcL, rpl16, ndhH, ndhF and rpl32. The phylogenetic tree revealed three strongly supported clades in Stipa. Species distributed in alpine grassland, such as Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau, consistently formed one clade. Stipa breviflora, S. przewalskyi and S. bungeana, distributed mainly in Loess Plateau, consistently formed another clade. In the third clade, the uncertain placement of subclades complicates the determination of the precise phylogeny within the genus Stipa.ConclusionsWe reported the complete chloroplast genomes of 22 Stipa species and elucidated the structural characteristics of the chloroplast genome of 38 species of Stipa, and developed SSRs. Stipa species grouped into strongly supported clades within phylogenetic trees and were in accordance with their spatial distributions in Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau and Loess Plateau, respectively, though conflicts of species relationships demonstrated by topology among subclades were explored. The results indicate the importance of molecular markers for reconstructing the phylogeny of Stipa species. Our results illustrate the complex evolutionary history among the Stipa species in the eastern and central Eurasian steppe, which can be used to study the taxonomy, biogeography, diversification, evolution and origin of autochthonous species in the Eurasian steppe.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07487-6.Keywords: Stipa, Eurasian steppe, Chloroplast genome, Comparative genomic, Phylogeny

Background

Stipa L., an important genus of Poaceae, is distributed in the Eurasian steppe [1–4]. In the western Eurasian steppe, for example, Stipa lessingiana Trin. & Rupr., S. pennata L., and S. orientalis Trin. ex Ledeb. are the main dominant species, and in the Mongol-Chinese steppe, S. baicalensis Roshevitz, S. krylovii Roshevitz, and S. caucasica subsp. glareosa (P.A. Smirnov) Tzvelev occupy dominances. However, some species, such as S. capillata L. occur in both steppe regions above mentioned [5]. Stipa species can also adapt a wide variety of precipitation and temperature conditions, including the typical steppe and desert steppe in eastern Asia, desert in central Asia, and alpine steppe in Qinghai-Tibetan Plateau (QTP), etc. [6, 7]. Since Linnaeus first published the genus Stipa with only three species (S. pennata, S. juncea L., and S. avenacea L.) [8], the group of Stipa members has grown significantly. Although Stipa was considered to have a worldwide distribution, encompassing about 680 species [9–12], most researchers now accept the narrower concept of Stipa L., i.e., the typical genus of the tribe Stipeae Dumort. [13], as comprising about 150 species [14, 15]. Due to the highly variable morphology, the subtle diagnostic features, and the phenotypic plasticity of different Stipa species, it would be more credible to provide support and validation for species relationships inferred from morphological traits by utilizing molecular data [2, 16, 17]. Using several chloroplast and nuclear DNA markers, previous studies investigated the tribe Stipeae, organizing a framework for understanding its species composition, genus division and geographical distribution [1, 15, 18, 19]. In recent decades, the sequencing technology has developed rapidly and genetic markers have been used. Genome information for Stipa has been reported and used for species discrimination [20, 21], phylogeny reconstruction [13, 20, 22, 23], and detecting speciation events [24–27], which deepens our understanding of this genus. Nevertheless, low sampling and using limited molecular markers has restricted the application of testing broader concepts among species restricted to eastern Asia. Evolutionary relationships have not always been elucidated clearly and/or fully. Consequently, additional genome information is still needed. High resolution genomic data could play an important role in facilitating a more in-depth clarification of species’ phylogenetic relationships.

Chloroplasts are semi-autonomous organelles in plants that plays a critical role in various processes such as plant photosynthesis and the biosynthesis of starch, fatty acids, amino acids, hormones, vitamins, and secondary metabolites [28, 29]. They are believed to have originated from the endosymbiosis relationship between photosynthetic cyanobacteria and the ancestor of today’s eukaryotic plant cells [30]. Most angiosperms have circular chloroplast genomes ranging from 120 to 160 kb, and these genomes are compact in size and have many copies per cell [31, 32]. They have typical quadripartite structure consisting of a large single copy region (LSC) and a small copy region (SSC), separated by two inverted repeat regions (IRs) [33]. Chloroplast genomes contain approximately 130 genes, including 80 protein-coding genes, four rRNA genes, and 30 tRNA genes [34]. They are widely applied in molecular marker studies, species identification, divergence dating, and phylogeny construction because of their moderate evolutionary rate, lack of recombination, and uniparentally inherited nature [29, 35, 36].

In the present study, we collected and sequenced 22 chloroplast genomes of species of Stipa. Nine of them, including S. klemenzii Roshevitz, S. kirghisorum P.A. Smirnov, S. sareptana A.K. Becker, S. subsessiliflora (Ruprecht) Roshevitz, S. regeliana Hackel, S. tianschanica Roshevitz, S. roborowskyi Roshevitz, S. aliena Keng and S. capillacea Keng, were sequenced here for the first time. The accession number, taxon, and sampling information are given in Additional file 1. Moreover, we used 22 sequenced and 16 previously published chloroplasts genomes of species in Stipa (they are predominantly distributed in the eastern and central Eurasian steppe) to perform genome structure analysis, comparative analysis, repeat sequence analysis, and phylogenetic analyses. We aimed: (1) to reconstruct a more comprehensive phylogenetic tree for revealing the evolutionary relationship among the species of Stipa; and (2) to screen potential regions in cp genomes that can be used for the phylogeny exploration and species classification. The results enrich the genetic resources and will improve our understanding of the phylogeny of Stipa species.

Results

Structural features of the chloroplast

We analyzed the chloroplast genomes of 22 species of Stipa and found that the length of the genomes ranged from 137,120 bp (S. subsessiliflora) to 137,859 bp (S. grandis P.A. Smirnov), and that the typical quadripartite structure consisted of a large LSC (81,003‒81,734 bp) and a small SSC (12,771‒12,875 bp) separated by two IRs (43,272‒43,326 bp) (Table 1 and Fig. 1). The average GC content was about 38.80%. In the cp genome of Stipa species, there are 131 genes, including 85 protein-coding genes, 38 tRNAs and eight rRNAs (Table 1). The majority of these genes occurred as a single copy in LSC or in SSC regions.

Complete chloroplast genome features of Stipa taxa

Chloroplast genome map of Stipa species. The full circle represents common genes annotated for the 22 Stipa species sequenced in this study. The bottom of the figure exhibits the differences in the genes annotated in the region for Stipa krylovii

The expansion and contraction of the IR borders are common results of evolutionary events, leading to the size differences among the chloroplast genomes. In species of Stipa, the boundaries of the IRs were different slightly (Figure S1 in Additional file 2), except for S. krylovii, where a complete inversion of SSC region was detected (Fig. 1). Two ndhH genes located at the junctions of SSC/IRa and SSC/IRb, respectively, while no genes straddled the boundaries of either LSC/IRa or LSC/IRb. The ndhH genes of 38 species spanned across the SSC/IRb boundary from 3 to 46 bp in SSC region and across SSC/IRa boundary from 191 to 192 bp in IRa region. The distance between ndhF gene in SSC region and SSC/IRb boundary changed from 13 to 124 bp, while the distance between gene rps15 in IRa region and SSC/IRa boundary was around 328‒347 bp. The gene rps19 was duplicated in IR region, and situated in 36‒43 bp away from LSC/IRs boundary. LSC region contained gene psbA and rpl22, which were located in 90 to 121 bp and 41 to 48 bp away from the LSC/IRa and from the LSC/IRb boundaries, respectively.

To verify the differences of the chloroplast sequences of 38 Stipa species, we performed mVISTA analysis taking S. breviflora Griseb. as a reference. The result showed a high sequence similarity (Figure S2 in Additional file 2), indicating that the plastomes are highly conserved. Relatively great differences, however, were identified in S. przewalskyi Roshevitz, S. penicillata Handel-Mazzetti, S. roborowskyi, S. subsessiliflora and S. klimesii M. Nobis. The highly variable regions were mainly concentrated in conserved non-coding sequence (Figure S2 in Additional file 2).

Codon usage

The codon usage frequency and relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) of the 38 Stipa cp genomes are shown in Fig. 2, Table S1 and S2 in Additional file 2. All the protein-coding genes were composed of 20 coded amino acids. In detail, leucine (Leu; 10.71%‒10.93%) was the most common amino acid, encoding from 1,690 times in S. klemenzii to 1,823 times in S. aliena. Cysteine (Cys; 1.03%‒1.07%) was the least universal amino acid, encoding from only 163 times in S. klemenzii to 179 times in S. capillacea and S. eriocaulis Borbás. Similar RSCU patterns of species were shown in Fig. 2, indicating the consistent trends of the codon use preferences. No usage bias (RSCU = 1) was found in methionine (Met) and tryptophan (Trp) (Table S2 in Additional file 2). RSCU values for about half of the codons (29) were greater than one, and the majority of them ended with A and U (Fig. 2). The RSCU value of 30 codons were less than one, and three codons ended with A, 15 with C and 12 with G. It appeared that the overall codon usage pattern in Stipa cp genomes tended towards A- or U-ending.

Nucleotide polymorphism analysis

We excluded S. krylovii in this analysis due to its inversion in SSC region. We estimated the divergence levels and detected the high variation hotspots within 1000-bp windows, display an excessively high peak in this region (Figure S3 in Additional file 2). The total nucleotide variation of 37 Stipa species was 0.00143, while Pi value ranged from 0 to 0.00693. IR regions, with the lower nucleotide polymorphism, were more conserved than LSC and SSC regions (Fig. 3). Nine hotspots were screened from the cp genomes, including eight (psbA, psbZ-trnG-GCC, trnG-UCC-trnT-GGU, petN-trnC-GCA, rbcL, rpl16, ndhF and rpl32) distributed in LSC and SSC regions, and one (ndhH) in the boundaries of the SSC and IRs (Fig. 3).

Repeat sequence analysis

The number of simple sequence repeats (SSRs) identified in 38 Stipa chloroplasts ranged from 34 (S. narynica M. Nobis and S. lipskyi Roshevitz) to 44 (S. orientalis), and they were mainly distributed in the LSC region (Fig. 4a and b). Four types of repeats patterns, including mono-, di-, tri- and tetra-nucleotides SSR motifs, were detected in all samples (Fig. 4a and Table S3 in Additional file 2). However, pentanucleotide SSRs only found in S. penicillata, and hexanucleotides SSRs were exclusive to S. tianschanica (Fig. 4a).

Comparison of simple sequence repeats among 38 Stipa chloroplast genomes. a numbers of SSRs detected in Stipa chloroplast genomes and (b) numbers of SSRs identified in LSC, IR and SSC regions

The most abundant SSR motifs were mononucleotide repeats, accounting for 46.4% of the total SSRs, followed by tetranucleotide (28.1%), dinucleotide (17.9%), and trinucleotide (7.8%) repeats. Almost all mononucleotide repeats were composed of A/T motif, whereas C/G was rare. The repeats mainly composed of A/T base combinations were also dominated in dinucleotide (AT/AT, 12.8%) and trinucleotide (AAT/ATT, 5.1%) (Table S3 in Additional file 2). The REPuter analysis demonstrated that Stipa’s chloroplast genomes lacked any reverse or complementary repeats (Figure S4 in Additional file 2). The number of palindromic and forward repeats, however, varied among different species ranged from 20 (S. breviflora, S. bungeana Trinius and S. przewalskyi) to 27 (S. capillaceae, S. capillata, S. purpurea Grisebach, and S. arabica Trin. & Rupr.), and from 40 (S. bungeana) to 54 (S. capillacea and S. penicillata), respectively (Figure S4 in Additional file 2).

Phylogenetic analysis

Four phylogenetic trees were constructed using two datasets (70 CDs and complete chloroplast genomes) of 39 species, including 38 Stipa species and Brachypodium distachyon (L.) P.Beauv. as outgroup species, based on maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) methods. The CDs dataset and the complete chloroplast genome dataset had aligned length of 48,012 and 136,845 bp, respectively. Topological conflicts were observed in four trees (Figs. 5 and 6). In general, Stipa species in eastern and central Eurasian steppe categorized into same sections in terms of morphological characters were not always aligned into one clade (Figs. 5 and 6). We identified three strongly supported clades, i.e. clade A, B and C. Specifically, topologies of clade B in both CDs tree and cp tree were same and with high support. For clade C, largely incongruent topologies existed in four trees (Figs. 5 and 6).

Discussion

Chloroplast genome variation and evolution

This study sequenced and reported the chloroplast genomes of 22 species within the genus Stipa, nine of them were sequenced for the first time (Additional file 1). The genome size of these 22 species ranged from 137,120 bp (S. subsessiliflora) to 137,859 bp (S. grandis) (Table 1), which is similar to the genome size of other species in the genus [13, 20, 21]. We compared these genomes with 16 published chloroplast genomes downloaded from GenBank. On the whole, the chloroplast genomes of Stipa are generally conserved except for S. krylovii that contained an inverted SSC region (Fig. 1). Plastid genomic rearrangement is a relatively common phenomenon, possibly triggered by recombination between inverted repeats or tRNA genes [37], transposition via transposable elements [38], lateral DNA transfer [39], improper DNA repair [40], etc. Owing to the SSC region of S. krylovii is intact and long, we speculate that transposable elements are likely to contribute to this inversion. Nevertheless, further studies are necessary to explore the detailed reasons. The IR/SC boundary regions showed similar characteristics, with only slight differences observed in the length flanking or away from genes, i.e., rpl22, rps19, ndhH, ndhF, rps15, and psbA. Almost all high variation regions in the cp genomes of Stipa species were in the LSC and SSC and the low nucleotide polymorphism was observed in IR regions (Fig. 3), which has also been reported in other plant species [41, 42]. The duplicative feature of IRs could provide enhanced copy-correction activity which would decrease the mutation load [43, 44]. Consequently, chloroplast genomes of Stipa species have conserved IR regions.

SSRs are widely used as molecular markers for determining genetic variations across species. In the present study, identified SSRs were vastly distributed in the LSC region (Fig. 4b). We also found that SSRs in Stipa species primarily consist of A/T repeats while tandem G or C repeats were rarely observed (Table S3 in Additional file 2), indicating the abundant of poly-A or poly-T repeats exist in the cp genome. This is also a common phenomenon in other plant species [42, 45, 46]. Furthermore, due to the relatively conserved structure and organization of chloroplast genomes, SSRs in Stipa chloroplasts will provide valuable resources for estimating genetic diversity and conducting phylogenetic analyses across species and genera, especially coupling with other informative nuclear genome SSRs.

Phylogenetic relationships

In the present study, four different phylogenetic lineages were retrieved from CDs and chloroplast genomes of the 38 species (Figs. 5 and 6). Three major clades were identified with high support, but the hierarchical branching order within the major lineages were unresolved without support (Figs. 5 and 6). It is necessary to note that a group of QTP Stipa species (clade A) (i.e., dominants of alpine grassland, distributed mainly in QTP and adjacent high mountains) were always separated from remaining Stipa species even though this group of species belongs to different sections. Similar phenomenon occurred in other studies with different sample size [1, 13, 15, 23]. As a center of origin and diversity for many plant species, QTP provides unique environment and geographic isolation that may promote species diversification through multiple mechanisms such as natural hybrids, selection, local adaptation, etc. [47]. Therefore, the QTP species of Stipa probably have a common origin [20]. Additionally, we found that S. purpurea has close phylogenetic relationship with species in section Pseudoptilagrostis and section Regelia rather than section Barbatae (Figs. 5 and 6), which was found in previous studies [2, 13, 23].

Previous studies found species in clade B have a morphology and molecular conflict. Baiakhmetov, et al. [47] detected that S. bungeana in section Leiostipa is genetically allied with S. breviflora in section Barbatae using population genetic data. We also found a close relationship between S. bungeana and S. breviflora. Therefore, proper taxonomic placement will require reassessment based on a more comprehensive investigation of S. bungeana and S. breviflora. These two species, and closely related S. przewalskyi, are primarily distributed in Loess Plateau growing in loess soil. This result suggested that these three species may have originated in this habitat.

The clade C involved four Stipa sections: Barbatea, Stipa, Leiostipa and Smirnovia. Clade C is comprised of weakly supported internal nodes, and incongruent topologies among and within subclades (Figs. 5 and 6) is seen. The topological inconsistency might be attributed to a shared geography since all are found in the Mongolia Plateau and Central Asia, where has been shown to harbor frequent hybrid species [24, 48]. Previous studies reveal that numerous Stipa species have emerged within a quite short period of time. The putative origin age of Stipeae is estimated to be 28.09 million years ago (Mya) (95% interval: 22.94‒36.04 Mya) [10], and the feather grass (Stipa species) likely originated more recently, between mid-Miocene to Pliocene [49, 50]. This time span is probably too short for species to accumulate enough genetic mutations to establish species boundaries and complete reproductive isolations. Furthermore, the species of Stipa hybridize (ca. 30% Stipa species might be hybrid origin) and backcross easily, which complicates phylogenetic relationships and confuses species delimitation [47]. For instance, S. heptapotamica Golosk. is thought to have originated via hybridization between S. richeriana and S. lessingiana [51]. Whereas S. bungeana might be the result of hybridization between S. krylovii and S. glareosa [47]. Population-scale data from multiple disciplines and from a wider range of taxa is necessary to clarify the phylogeny and elucidate evolutionary relationships.

Establishing reliable phylogenic relationships needs to involve most species of a taxon [52]. Our study included chloroplast genome data from 38 species of Stipa, representing the majority of Stipa species from eastern Eurasian steppe. These species are primarily distributed across the Mongolian Plateau, the Loess Plateau and the QTP. From the perspective of administrative boundaries, China and Mongolia cover the main region of the eastern Eurasian steppe. According to the recent records of 37 Stipa species in the two countries [53, 54], this study involving 24 species of them, accounting for 65% of the total. Central Asia, bordering on the western part of the research area, harbors 76 Stipa species [2], of which about one-third (24 species) are included in this study. Previous studies paid more attention to central and western Eurasian steppe [1, 20]. Even though a report has been conducted on the eastern Eurasian steppe, the sample size remained insufficient [25]. However, the sample size variation is unlikely to affect the identification of distinct clades, in which Stipa species are derived from geographical regions on large scale [13, 20, 22], indicating that Stipa phylogeny can be reliably conducted in detail for a specific region as long as the appropriate samples are acquired. Therefore, this study will enhance our understanding of the evolutionary relationships among Stipa species especially in the eastern Eurasian steppe. Furthermore, with the ongoing discovery of new Stipa species [21, 55, 56] by extensive field investigations, additional chloroplast genomes need to be sequenced and incorporated to improve the validity of phylogeny reconstruction of Stipa.

Conclusions

We sequenced and assembled 22 complete cp genomes in Stipa. By incorporating an additional set of 16 Stipa cp genomes obtained from the NCBI database, we performed comparative analyses and constructing phylogenetic trees. The overall structure and gene organization were relatively conserved across the species. Mutation hotspot regions and SSRs found in this study can be utilized for molecular marker development, phylogenetic analyses, and population genetic studies. Three strongly supported clades (A, B & C) were identified. Stipa species derived from clade A and clade B were consistent with their spatial distribution in QTP and Loess Plateau, respectively. The branching order (topology) is inconsistent in clade A and clade C in trees deriving using 70 coding genes and those derived from the whole cp genome. The phylogenetic trees not only reflect the phylogeny of Stipa species from eastern Eurasian steppe but may interest researchers in taxonomy, evolution, biogeography and floristics.

Methods

Sampling, DNA extracting and sequencing

Fresh leaves of 22 Stipa species were sampled in summer of 2021. These samples were all identified by taxonomist Prof. Liqing Zhao at Inner Mongolia University. The voucher specimens were preserved in the Herbarium of Inner Mongolia University (HIMC; Additional file 1). Nine Stipa species were firstly sequenced in this study (Additional file 1). Plant materials were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen in the field. Then, the samples were stored at −80 °C in the lab. Genomic DNA was extracted by a modified cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) protocol [57]. The quantified DNA was delivered to BioMarker company (Beijing, China) to construct library and sequenced using Illumina Hiseq 2500 platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). Raw data derived from each sample were produced with the length of 150 bp pair-end reads.

Sequence assembly and annotation

Raw data were filtered to remove adaptors and low-quality reads using Trimmomatic v0.39 [58]. In order to acquire the highly abundant reads, BBNorm (https://sourceforge.net/projects/bbmap/) was used to correct errors and to normalize the clean data with the default setting but targeting 50 × reads depth. Then, the reads were assembled into chloroplast genome using the SPAdes genome assembler v3.13.0 [59]. Assembled results were visualized and checked by Bandage [60].

Plastome annotation was performed using the Annotation of Organellar Genomes (GeSeq; https://chlorobox.mpimp-golm.mpg.de/geseq.html) [61], and the chloroplast genome maps were generated using CPGAVAS2 [62]. The annotation results were further confirmed by comparing published cp genomes of the genus Stipa species. Manual corrections of start/stop codons and intron/exon boundaries were performed in Geneious v8.0.2 [63]. All the data we sequenced in this study are available from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI), and the accession numbers were recorded in Additional file 1.

Genome characteristics, structure and comparative analysis

We calculated the length of IR, LSC and SSC regions in 22 chloroplasts, and computed the GC content of the genomes using seqkit [64]. Subsequent analyses were conducted for all 38 Stipa species. The mVISTA program was used to compare the genomes in Shuffle-LAGAN mode [65] by taking S. breviflora cp genomes as a reference. We used JSHYCloud (http://cloud.genepioneer.com:9929) online program to investigate the expansion/contraction of IR regions by comparing the positions of SC/IRs junctions and their adjacent genes. DnaSP v6.12.03 was used to calculate the nucleotide diversity (Pi) and to screen interspecific high variation sequences (hotspots) with a step size of 50 bp and sliding window length of 1000 bp [66].

Codon usage analysis

To explore the codon usage bias, we ran MEGA X [67] to investigate codon usage preference based on the RSCU ration and to compute the codon frequencies.

Repeat sequences and SSR identification

SSRs were identified using the web tool of MIcroSAtellite (MISA; https://webblast.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/) with the following thresholds: ten repeat units for mononucleotide, six repeat units for dinucleotide and five repeat units for tri-, tetra-, penta- and hexa-nucleotide SSR motifs, respectively. REPuter (http://bibiserv.techfak.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer/) [68] was applied to detect and calculate the size and the position of the forward, complement, palindromic and reverse repeats within the Stipa chloroplast genomes with the parameters settings as the minimal repeat size of 30 bp, the hamming distance of three and maximum computed repeats of 5,000.

Phylogenetic analyses

We used the maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) methods to infer the phylogenetic relationship of Stipa species. Two datasets originated from 38 Stipa species were generated for constructing phylogenetic trees: (1) the common 70 coding sequences (CDs), and (2) the whole chloroplast genomes (cp). B. distachyon were chosen as outgroup to root the tree. Sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7.505 [69] and then were trimmed by trimAl v1.2 with the settings: -gt 0.8 [70]. ML trees were constructed using the IQ-TREE [60] in the PhyloSuite v1.2.3 [61], employing 1,000,000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates [62]. The best-fit models were selected by ModelFinder [63] implemented in IQ-TREE: TPM3u + F + I for the CDs dataset and TN + F + I + G4 for the cp dataset. BI analysis was implemented using MrBayes v3.2.7 [71] under the GTR + F + I model for both CDs and cp datasets. The optimal model was selected by Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) using ModelFinder [72]. These analyses were performed with four parallel Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) runs for six million generations, sampling every 1,000 generations and with a 25% burn-in. Convergence of the runs was assessed by examining the average standard deviation of split frequencies (ASDSF; ASDSF < 0.01) to ensure they had reached the stationary distribution.
