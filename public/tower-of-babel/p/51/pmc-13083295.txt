Characterization of the host-parasite relationship of Polymorphidae (Acanthocephala) in Nannopterum brasilianus (Aves, Phalacrocoracidae) in the Soure Marine Extraction Reserve, Pará
Authors: Elaine Lopes de Carvalho, Gerônimo da Silva Cabral, Ricardo Luis Sousa Santana, Tiago Paixão Mangas, Elane Guerreiro Giese
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The Neotropical Nannopterum brasilianus is a piscivorous bird feeding on aquatic organisms, can ingest various infectious forms of helminths, such as those from the phylum Acanthocephala. In their adult stage, these parasites attach to the intestinal mucosa of their definitive hosts, causing varying degrees of tissue damage. Between 2020 and 2023, 35 individuals of N. brasilianus were analyzed, and 20 of them (57.1%) presented acanthocephalan infection. Morphological and histopathological analyses identified the species as Southwellina hispida, we described the tissue alterations associated with its presence in detail. The results contribute to the taxonomic knowledge of the parasite and provide important information for diagnosing and monitoring of infections in birds housed at Wildlife Triage and Rehabilitation Centers (WTRC).

Introduction

Nannopterum brasilianus (the Neotropic cormorant) has a wide geographic distribution, ranging from the southern United States through Central America to various regions of South America. It is a migratory species (AVIBASE, 2003). However, its helminth fauna remains only partially known, as do the immunopathological mechanisms associated with infection by these parasites (Monteiro et al., 2011). These wild birds play a significant ecological role in maintaining the helminth life cycle by acting as reservoirs and facilitating the dispersal of these parasites in the environment (Dezfuli et al., 2021).

The acanthocephalans that parasitize the intestines of birds in the family Phalacrocoracidae are represented by the species Andracantha tandemtesticulata (Monteiro et al., 2006) occurring in Guaíba, Rio Grande do Sul – Brazil (Monteiro et al., 2006, 2011); Andracantha phalacrocoracis (Yamaguti, 1963) in South Bohemia and South Moravia – Czech Republic (Moravec and Scholz, 2016) and Baia de São Vicente – Chile (González-Acuña et al., 2020); Southwellina hispida Van Cleave (1925); Wittenberg, 1932, in Mexico (Violante-González et al., 2011) and South Moravia – Czech Republic (Moravec and Scholz, 2016); Corynosoma arctocephali (Zdzitowiecki, 1984) in São Vicente Bay - Chile (González-Acuña et al., 2020); Profilicollis altmani (Perry, 1942) in São Vicente Bay - Chile (González-Acuña et al., 2020). Andracantha gravida, parasite of Phalacrocorax auritus in Mexico (García-Varela et al., 2009), Pallisentis sp. in Phalacrocorax niger from Palistan (Abro et al., 2017) and Andracantha leucocarboi from Phalacrocorax punctatus (Bennett et al., 2023).

In the Otago region of southern New Zealand, other acanthocephalan species have been reported on other cormorant species, including: Andracantha leucocarboi on Phalacrocorax punctatus, Leucocarbo chalconotus, and Microcarbo melanoleucos; Andracantha sigma on L. chalconotus and P. punctatus; Corynosoma hannae on L. chalconotus and P. punctatus; and Profilicolis nozaezelandensis on M. melanoleucos and P. punctatus (Presswell and Bennett, 2021). In the Czech Republic, Tovačov and Záhlinice S. hispida on Phalacrocorax carbo (Heneberg and Sitko, 2026).

Acanthocephalan transmission occurs when the definitive host (DH) ingests an infective cystacanth encysted in arthropods, mollusks, fish, etc., which are the intermediate and/or paratenic hosts. After ingestion by the DH, the cystacanth settles in the jejunum and, soon after its complete sexual maturation, migrates to the ileum (Aznar et al., 2004; Schuster and Krone, 2016; Caballero-Viñas and Sánchez-Nava, 2020). According to Castro (1989) and Fairweather (1997), acanthocephalans induce changes in the structure of intestinal tissue at the mucosa, muscularis, and serosa levels of the aforementioned organ and lead to changes that affect its normal functioning.

Based on this information, the objective of this study was to describe the host-parasite relationship between species of the Polymorphidae family and the species N. brasilianus from the Soure Marine Extractive Reserve, Pará.

Material and methods

Ethical aspects

This research has received a license from ICMBio/SISBIO n° 98376 and authorization from the Ethics Committee on the Use of Animals – CEUA, n° 6309230520.

Sampling area and sample collection

From 2020 to 2023, 35 specimens of N. brasilianus were examined. They were obtained from the coastal zone of the municipality of Soure in the Soure Marine Extractive Reserve, Marajó Island, Pará, Brazil (Latitude: 0.742862°, Longitude: 48.507732°). The birds were used as an alternative food source by local fishers, who kindly provided the gastrointestinal tract (GIT) used in this research. Each GIT was individually packaged in plastic bags and transported to the laboratory in isothermal boxes with ice.

In the laboratory, the organs were individually placed in Petri dishes containing 0.9% NaCl saline solution and analyzed under a Leica ES2 stereomicroscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany). The parasites were collected, cleaned with saline, quantified, and stored in A.F.A. solution. (93 parts 70% ethyl alcohol, 5 parts formaldehyde, 2 parts glacial acetic acid). Gut fragments containing embedded acanthocephalans were collected and fixed in 10% formaldehyde for histological analyses.

Light microscopy (LM)

The parasites were clarified in 90% Amann's Lactophenol solution and examined under a Leica DM2500 microscope (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany) attached to an opaque camera. They were then stored in glycerin alcohol (50 parts 70% ethyl alcohol and 50 parts pure glycerin). Live acanthocephalans were preserved in distilled water for 24 h under refrigeration to allow them to evert their probosces (Amato and Amato, 2010). Measurements are in micrometers unless otherwise indicated and are presented as minimum and maximum values.

Deposit in collection

Voucher S. hispida specimens were deposited in the Invertebrate Collection of the Museu Paraense Emílio Goeldi (MPEG), Belém, Pará, Brazil. These were 2 juvenile males MPEG.ACT 000014, MPEG.ACT 000015 and 2 juvenile females MPEG.ACT 000016, MPEG.ACT 000017. 2 adult males MPEG.ACT 000011, MPEG.ACT 000013 and 2 adult females MPEG.ACT 000010, MPEG.ACT 000012.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The acanthocephalans were fixed in A.F.A., washed in distilled water for 1 h, post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide (OSO4) for 2 h, and subjected to dehydration in an increasing ethanol series from 70% to 100% ethanol for 1 h in each alcohol battery. They were subsequently dried at a critical point of CO2, mounted on metal aluminum supports (stubs), metallized with gold + palladium, and observed in a scanning electron microscope model VEGA 3 (TESCAN, Brno, Czech Republic) according to Santana et al. (2023).

Taxonomic identification

Identification of acanthocephalans was based on Yamaguti (1963), Petrochenko (1971), Bhattacharya et al. (2002), and Naidu (2012). Published papers also assisted in species identification in scientific journals.

Histological processing

Fragments of the small intestine (ileum) were pre-fixed in 10% formalin, containing acanthocephalans adhered to the mucosa/musculature, and the adult male and female acanthocephalans underwent histological processing. They were dehydrated in increasing concentrations of ethanol from 70% to 100% for 1 h in each batch and subsequently clarified in xylene in two 30-min baths each. After this, paraffin infiltration was performed with three successive baths in liquid paraffin for 20 min each, in an oven at 60 °C, followed by embedding in histomolds (Tolosa et al., 2003; Carvalho et al., 2023). After embedding, the blocks were sectioned into 4-5 μm thick sections using a Zeiss Hyrax M25 microtome (Carl Zeiss™, Jena, Germany). The slides obtained were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and Gomory's trichrome. Images were obtained using a Leica DM 2500 microscope, with a DFC310 FX digital capture system and Leica Application Suite V4.4 software (Leica Microsystems GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany).

Statistical analysis

To determine epidemiological parameters, prevalence (%), average infection intensity (IMI) and average abundance were calculated according to Bush et al. (1997) and Bautista-Hernández et al. (2015).

Results

Statistical analysis

Between June 2020 and August 2023, 35 samples of N. brasilianus involving the head, respiratory system, and gastrointestinal tract were analyzed. Of the birds examined, 54.3% (n = 19) were females, of which 68.4% (n = 13) presented acanthocephalan infection, with intensity ranging from 16 to 30 acanthocephalans per host. Males represented 45.7% (n = 16) of the sample, of which 7 (43.8%) were parasitized, with intensity ranging from 13 to 27 acanthocephalans per host. Considering the total number of specimens analyzed, the overall prevalence of acanthocephalan infection was 57.1% (n = 20), with 424 acanthocephalans collected (259 females and 165 males). The mean intensity of infection (MI) was 21.2 parasites per infected host, and the average abundance was 12.1 parasites per host examined.

The acanthocephalan S. hispida was described based on the morphological analysis of 10 adult males and 10 females that was performed using LM and SEM (Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5). The specimens have a cylindrical proboscis with slight dilatation and numerous longitudinal rows of hooks. The neck is short and wide, and the double-walled proboscis receptacle is inserted at the base of this structure. The anterior portion of the trunk is dilated with hypodermic nuclei confined to this region, while the posterior portion is thinner. Both males and females have two fields of spines on the anterior region of the trunk. The lemnisci are not attached to the trunk wall at their distal ends. Histological analysis

The first tissue reactions observed in the parasite-host interaction in this study occurred primarily in the intestinal mucosal layer. Macroscopically, an extensive hemorrhagic area was identified in the intestinal wall, in addition to regions with evident scarring resulting from parasite detachment (Fig. 6). Another significant tissue reaction occurred in the muscular layer of the intestinal wall. As the helminths penetrated this region, progressive deposition of scar tissue developed around the parasites. Inflammatory aggregates containing red blood cells and connective tissue fibers were also observed around the proboscis hooks, with an inflammatory infiltrate in the adjacent tissues. In this region, the granulocytic infiltrate (eosinophils and other granulocytes) was less evident, with areas of necrosis, hyperplasia, and tissue changes consistent with neoplasia associated with the predominant presence of the parasite's proboscis (Fig. 7D).

Transfixion of the intestinal wall by the acanthocephalan proboscis was observed, reaching the bird's peritoneal cavity and being encapsulated by fibrous connective tissue. In some cases, irregular muscle tissue neoformation was observed after detachment or death of the parasite, accompanied by a localized chronic granulomatous reaction. This response was characterized by the accumulation of polymorphonuclear cells, multinucleated giant cells, macrophages, and a reduced number of eosinophils (Fig. 7C–D). Additionally, histological sections were performed in male S. hispida to confirm the number of cement glands and were also performed in females (Fig. 7E–F).

Discussion

Approximately 57.1% of the analyzed hosts were parasitized by juveniles and adult acanthocephalans throughout the intestine and MI 21.2, as was also observed by Monteiro et al. (2006), who recorded adult specimens of the genus Andracantha Schmidt, 1975 with 21.27% in the intestine of N. brasilianus in southern Brazil and MI 6.80 helminths/host. González-Acuña et al. (2020) recorded in the same host, 17.5% prevalence Andracantha phalacrocoracis (Yamaguti, 1963) and MI 4.11, 32.5% Profilicollis altmani Perry, 1942 and MI 25.04, and 11.25% Corynosoma arctocephali Zdzitowiecki, 1984 and MI 15.93. Carvalho et al. (2024) recorded 36.7% Andracantha sp. and MI 21.5, 30% Southwellina hispida, and MI 13 and 10% S. macracanthus and MI 26.7. García-Varela et al. (2023a) recorded seven species of Andracantha in phalacrocoracids, representing 78% of the biodiversity of the genus, suggesting that these birds could represent an ancestral host with a colonization event.

Amin et al. (2022) report S. hispida with lemnisci extending posteriorly to the end of the receptacle and lacks ligamentous insertions in the body wall. In our research, we were able to observe the presence of visible insertions in the LM, and add character to the species, since the partially everted, non-flattened copulatory bursa has visible cirumbursal papillae on the outer edge, as observed in the SEM.

In the present study, large areas of the small intestine with intense parasitic load by S. hispida were observed, adhered at different levels in the intestinal mucosa, muscular and serous layers, causing chronic encapsulating granulomatous reactions of fibroconnective tissue. Dezfuli et al. (2021) reported a similar pattern, with chronic granulomatous reaction and fibroconnective encapsulation, and the presence of eosinophils, due to injury caused by acanthocephalans, in crow (Corvus cornix and Pica pica).

In our research, we observed a high parasite load, with adult acanthocephalans located in the ileum. This finding is in line with Aznar et al. (2004), who reported that young acanthocephalans are mostly found in the jejunum and, as they mature, adults migrate to the ileum. The insertion of the proboscis and hooks of acanthocephalans into the small intestine of N. brasilianus triggered inflammatory reactions, hemorrhage, necrosis, and destruction of intestinal villi and crypts, which can compromise the physiological functioning of the host's alimentary canal (Friend et al., 1999). The findings of Dezfuli et al. (2021) showed a more severe host response to the proboscis, with the hooks perforating the entire intestinal wall of the birds and reaching the peritoneal visceral serosa. This was accompanied by the presence of macrophages, giant cells, eosinophils, and heterophils.

Dezfuli et al. (2021) found that villous necrosis occurs as a response of the host's immune system to injuries caused by the metasoma and the hooks of the parasite's proboscis. According to Juul-Madsen et al. (2008), granuloma formation (see Fig. 7E) is a significant innate response that increases as the bird matures. Additionally, a hemorrhagic condition in the lamina propria of the epithelial tissue has also been reported in birds of prey in the investigations of Santoro et al. (2010). This condition allows the parasite to remain attached to the intestine while its metasoma remains free in the lumen to absorb nutrients (Caballero-Viñas and Sánchez-Nava, 2020). According to Taraschewski (2000), areas of necrosis are mainly related to the tissue close to the parasite's body along with neoplastic processes, especially in the peritoneum. Furthermore, it has been shown that the intestine is completely perforated by the acanthocephalan proboscis. Further studies are needed to investigate the consequences of this injury, since literature on parasite-host interactions in cormorants is still scarce.

In the muscular layer, connective scar tissue was deposited around the helminths as they penetrated it, leading to severe inflammatory changes. The immune response to infectious agents is the primary factor preventing systemic infections. In the context of helminth infections, the immune evasion mechanisms and complex antigenicity of these parasites pose a significant challenge to the host immune system. This is largely due to the morphological diversity, multicompartmental life cycle, and metabolic plasticity of helminths, which contribute to antigenic variability and hinder the establishment of an effective and lasting immune response (Machado et al., 2004).

Paratenic hosts act as a trophic bridge and facilitate transmission between the intermediate host and the definitive host, but do not appear to play a central role in the population genetic structure of S. hispida in its distribution along the coasts of Mexico (García-Varela et al., 2023b). García-Varela et al. (2013) conducted a phylogenetic analysis encompassing a wide range of Polymorphidae species, establishing a solid phylogenetic basis to elucidate aspects of the evolutionary history of host-parasite relationships in this diverse group of acanthocephalans.

Conclusion

The description of the lesions caused by acanthocephalans in N. brasilianus in northern Brazil provided new information on the group. Furthermore, the addition of new characteristics to the species, such as the presence of papillae in the male's copulatory pouch observed under scanning electron microscopy, ensures that valuable information will be added to the literature.
