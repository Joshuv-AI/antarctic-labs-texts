The Constituent-Dependent Translocation Mechanism for PM2.5 to Travel through the Olfactory Pathway
Authors: Sheng Wei, Ting Xu, Miao Cao, Huan Wang, Yiqun Song, Daqiang Yin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe neurotoxic risk of PM2.5 is of worldwide concern, but the pathways through which PM2.5 gets to the central nervous system are still under debate. The olfactory pathway provides a promising shortcut to the brain, which bypasses the blood–brain barrier for PM2.5. However, direct evidence is lacking, and the translocation mechanism is still unclear. This study used the primary murine olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs) as an in vitro model to explore the translocation mechanism of PM2.5 in the olfactory system. We found that PM2.5 can be internalized into the OSNs via vesicle transportation. This process responds only to the water-insoluble compositions of PM2.5 (WIS-PM2.5) and cannot be affected by the water-soluble compositions of PM2.5 (WS-PM2.5). PM2.5 can further disrupt the integrity of the barrier constituted by the OSNs, and WS-PM2.5 plays a heightened role in inducing the damages. Our results suggested that both cellular and paracellular pathways are possibly involved in the translocation of PM2.5 in the olfactory system. More advanced microscopy techniques need to be developed to explore the whole translocation process in the olfactory–brain pathway in both in vitro and in vivo models.Keywords: particulate matter, olfactory system, central nervous system, endocytosis, vesicle transportation

Introduction

The potential of fine particulate matter (particles with aerodynamic diameter less than 2.5 μm, PM2.5) to increase the risks of neurological disorders has raised an urgent public concern.1−3 According to a global burden of disease study, ambient PM2.5 exposure is estimated to contribute to 28.7 million incidents of stroke.4 Although the deposition of PM2.5 in human brain tissues, like frontal lobe5 and midbrain,6 has been widely reported, the key issue regarding the exposure route through which PM2.5 invades the central nervous system (CNS) is still under controversy. The pulmonary route is the most studied pathway. Several studies indicate that PM2.5 can travel through the lower respiratory tract to the lung, transfer to the vascular system during the gas exchange process, and translocate to the CNS via blood circulation.7 In this route, PM2.5 needs to breach the blood–brain barrier (BBB) to reach the CNS, which hampers the invasion of PM2.5.8 The olfactory pathway is another potential pathway. It has been verified that synthesized nanoparticles can thread along the olfactory route, which is made up of the olfactory epithelium (OE) and the olfactory bulb (OB), and invade the CNS without the obstruction of the BBB.9,10 The question about how PM2.5 can get through the olfactory system to the CNS is not clarified, but the involvement of the olfactory pathway is supported by the observation of PM2.5 in the human olfactory nerves and OB.11−13 There also exists other potential pathways like the intestinal route,14 but until now no evidence has been provided that PM2.5 can invade the CNS via the gut-vagus or gut-spinal nerves like engineered nanoparticles.

The olfactory system is situated in the upper portion of the nasal cavity and directly links the CNS to the external environment. A recent study reported that intranasal instillation led to a higher accumulation level of silica nanoparticles (size around 500 nm) in the CNS in comparison with intravenous injection, indicating that the olfactory route may be the primary pathway for exogenous fine particles to access the CNS.15 Synthesized nanoparticles have been proposed to be internalized into the olfactory sensory neurons (OSNs) in the OE and then transported to the OB and other CNS regions via the olfactory nerve.9,10 The exogenous substances can also break down the tight junctions constructed by the OSNs and then translocate to the CNS via the olfactory paracellular pathway.16 Nevertheless, current knowledge about the mechanism through which PM2.5 gets through the olfactory pathway to the CNS is still void. Can PM2.5 get through the olfactory route via similar pathways? How can PM2.5 be internalized into the OSNs? The answers to these questions not only improve our understanding of the neurotoxicology of PM2.5 but also provide clues for reducing the health risks during haze days, like inhibiting the internalization of PM2.5 via proper medicine intervention.

In this study, we used primary murine OSNs as the in vitro model to explore the mechanism of translocation of PM2.5 in the olfactory system. Our results revealed the internalization of PM2.5 into the OSNs and the degeneration of the tight olfactory barriers, which indicated the potential cellular and paracellular transportation of PM2.5 in the olfactory system. We further found that the water-soluble and water-insoluble compositions of PM2.5 play different roles in the translocation process. This study provides new insights into how the olfactory system is involved in the translocation of PM2.5 in the olfactory–brain pathway.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture and Exposure Solution Preparation

The primary murine OSNs (also known as olfactory epithelial cells) and the corresponding culture medium (Cat #CM-M149) were purchased from Pricella Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (China). The primary OSNs were cultured at 37 °C in an incubator with 5% CO2.

Quartz membranes loaded with PM2.5 from Wangdu, China were graciously provided by Jianmin Chen from Fudan University. For details about the sampling information and the chemical compositions of PM2.5, please refer to the publication of Prof. Chen.17 The exposure solution of total PM2.5 (T-PM2.5), water-soluble components of PM2.5 (WS-PM2.5), and water-insoluble components of PM2.5 (WIS-PM2.5) was prepared according to the previously published article.18 Briefly, after ultrasonic extraction from the membranes, PM2.5 was resuspended with a sterile PBS buffer. Half of the suspension was used as T-PM2.5. The remaining half was shaken overnight and then centrifuged at 12 000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was used as WS-PM2.5, and the resuspension of the residues was used as WIS-PM2.5. The primary OSNs were exposed to the exposure solution for 24 h, unless otherwise mentioned.

Cell Viability Test

The primary OSNs were cultured in a 96-well plate, and the cell viability was examined via Cell Counting Kit-8 (KeyGEN BioTECH, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The experimental results were acquired from six independent biological replicates.

Assessment of the Cellular Internalization of PM2.5

The primary OSNs were cultured on the poly-l-lysine-coated glass bottom (diameter = 14 mm) of a 35 mm Petri dish (Beyotime, China). After 6 h of treatment with 0.5 μg/mL of PM2.5, the primary OSNs were stained with FM 4-64 (Invitrogen, USA) on ice for 1 min. The fluorescent images were acquired under the z-stack mode (15 sections, depth = 5 μm) with a confocal laser scanning microscope (Leica Stellaris 5, Germany). The reconstruction of the three-dimensional views of the z-stack images was conducted using LAS X (Leica, Germany). The transmission electron microscope (TEM) (Hitachi HT7800, Japan) was further used to obtain high-resolution micrographs of the ultrastructure of the OSNs.

Treatment with Dynole 2-24

Dynole 2-24 (CAS No. 1416313-72-8) was purchased from MedChemExpress (USA). Dynole 2-24 was dissolved with DMSO and then diluted to 2 μmol/L with culture medium (final DMSO concentration = 0.01%, v/v). The OE cells were pretreated with 2 μmol/L of Dynole 2-24 for 4 h and then exposed to T-PM2.5 for 6 h for the assessment of the internalization of PM2.5 or for 24 h for the RT-PCR test.

Assessment of the Tight Junction of the OSNs

The transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) value was used to represent the tight junction of the methanol-ionized nitride in the OSNs. The primary OSNs were first cultured in the 24-well transwell insert (pore size = 0.4 μm) for 48 h to form a tight epithelial barrier and then exposed to different compositions of PM2.5. An EVOM STX-2-electrode (World Precision Instruments, USA) was used to determine the resistance, and the TEER value was calculated using eq 1. Assessment of the Penetration of PM2.5 through the OSN Barrier

After 24 h of treatment with different compositions of PM2.5 in the 24-well transwell, the insets were transferred to a new 24-well plate. The insert was then added with 100 μL of WIS-PM2.5 (0.5 μg/mL), and the bottom chamber was added with 500 μL of fresh cell culture medium. After another 2 h of culture, the solution of the bottom chamber was examined with a C6 PLUS flow cytometer (BD, USA) to determine the penetrated PM2.5. The experiments were replicated four times.

Quantitative RT-PCR

The total RNA of the PM2.5-treated OSNs was extracted with RNAiso Plus (Takara, Japan) and reverse-transcribed into cDNA with the PrimeScriptTM RT reagent kit (Takara, Japan). A 7500 Real-Time PCR system (RT-PCR, Applied Biosystems, USA) was applied to perform RT-PCR, and the running procedure consisted of 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s, 57 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s. Gapdh and Actb were used as the housekeeping genes to calculate the relative expression levels of the target genes via the 2–ΔΔCT method. The primers used in this study are provided in Supporting Information Table S1. The experiments were replicated three times.

Western Blotting

The protein expression levels were determined as described in our previously published paper.19 Briefly, the total proteins of the OSNs were extracted with mixtures of lysis buffer, PMSF, and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, China). After blocking with QuickBlock Blocking Buffer (Beyotime, China) at room temperature for 30 min, the samples were further incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C and secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. A UVP ChemStudio Plus system (Analytik Jena, Germany) was applied to acquire the images, and the optical density was calculated by VisionWorks 9.1 (Analytik Jena, Germany). The antibodies used in this study are provided in Supporting Information Table S2. The experiments were replicated three times.

Immunofluorescence Staining

The primary OSNs were seeded on the poly-l-lysine-coated coverglass for 1 h, and the coverglass was then transferred to the 24-well plate for further culture. After exposure, the samples were fixed with ice-cold acetone for 10 min and blocked with 5% BSA for 1 h. The coverglasses were then incubated with the primary antibody Rabbit Anti-NFκB p65 (ABCAM, ab16502, dilution, 1:200) overnight at 4 °C and the secondary antibody Goat Anti Rabbit IgG (H&L)-Alexa Fluor 488 (Immunoway, RS3211, dilution, 1:100) for 1 h at room temperature. The Antifade Mounting Medium with DAPI (Beyotime, China) was finally added to the coverglasses. The fluorescent pictures were acquired using a Leica Stellaris 5 confocal laser scanning microscope (Germany) and analyzed with ImageJ. The experimental results were acquired from five independent biological replicates.

High-Content Imaging

The primary OSNs were cultured in a 96-well plate. After exposure, the nucleus, mitochondria, and lysosome of the OSNs were stained with Hoechst 33342, MitoTracker Green FM, and LysoTracker Red DND-99 (Invitrogen, USA) in the incubator for 30 min and then washed with 1 × PBS two times. A high-content imaging system (Molecular Devices ImageXpress Micro, USA) was applied to enable the rapid and high-throughput acquisition of the fluorescent data of the organelles. The fluorescence profile and colocalization analysis were further analyzed using ImageJ. The experimental results were acquired from six independent biological replicates.

Statistical Analysis

The experimental data from at least three independent biological replications were presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) using Prism 8.0 (GraphPad, USA). One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a postdoc Dunnett’s T3 multiple comparison test (SPSS 22.0, USA) was used to determine the significant difference. For all analyses, p < 0.05 was set as the criterion for significant difference.

Results and Discussion

The OSNs Internalized PM2.5 via the Clathrin-Dependent Endocytosis

We first carried out a preliminary experiment to determine a sublethal exposure concentration. The results showed that 5 μg/mL of the T-PM2.5 was the lowest concentration to significantly suppress the cell viability (Figure S1). Therefore, we set the tenth of 5 μg/mL (i.e., 0.5 μg/mL) as the exposure concentration. FM 4-64 is a dye that labels the cell membrane and can be used to visualize the vesiculation.20 After 6 h of treatment with PM2.5, vesicles enwrapping PM2.5 (diameter <1.5 μm) were observed at the edge of the OSNs in the T-PM2.5 and WIS-PM2.5 group (red arrows in Figure 1A). We also found that the red fluorescence faded after PM2.5 translocated into the cell body of the OSNs, which indicated that PM2.5 was released to the cytoplasm (black arrow in Figure 1A). However, we failed to record the entire process (the formation and the disassembly of the vesicle–PM2.5 complex) due to the instability of the dye and the absence of a live cell culture module on the used microscope. We further verified that PM2.5 penetrated the cell body instead of depositing on the cell surface in the T-PM2.5 and WIS-PM2.5 group via TEM and the reconstructed three-dimensional views from the fluorescent images (Figure 1B, Figure S2, and Video S1). In comparison, neither PM2.5 nor the formation of vesicles was found in the control and WS-PM2.5 group (Figure 1A). It should be mentioned that wrinkled nuclear membranes and malformed mitochondria (e.g., swelling and mitophagosome) were observed in the T-PM2.5 and WS-PM2.5 group (Figure 1B and Figure S2), indicating the sublethal dosage of PM2.5 probably impairs the OSNs via disrupting the function of the mitochondria.

The internalization of exogenous particles into the cells is mediated by various mechanisms, including endocytosis and phagocytosis.21 Given the observed vesicles and the internalized PM2.5 were mostly not larger than 200 nm (Figure 1A, B) and the scavenger receptor CD36 participating in phagocytosis22 showed an extremely low expression level in the OSNs (Figure S3), endocytosis might play a more important role in transporting PM2.5 into the OSNs. Clathrin-dependent endocytosis (CDE) and clathrin-independent endocytosis (CIE) are two major typical endocytic modes.23 We further investigated the gene expression involved in endocytosis. Cltc is a typical gene involved in the formation of the clathrin-coated pit (CDE mode), whereas Cav1 participates in the formation of caveolae (CIE mode).24 As shown in Figure 1C, the expression level of Cltc exhibited a significant upregulation in the T-PM2.5 and WIS-PM2.5 group, indicating the endocytosis of PM2.5 was mainly via CDE. Ap2a1, Ap2a2, and Ap2m1 encode the respective subunits of the assembly protein complex 2, which regulate the assembly of the clathrin-coated pit.25 The elevated expressions of these genes further confirmed the occurrence of clathrin-dependent endocytosis in the T-PM2.5 and WIS-PM2.5 group (Figure 1C). In comparison, the WS-PM2.5 could not elicit the formation of vesicles, which is consistent with the results of FM 4-64 staining and TEM. These results revealed that the OSNs could respond to and endocytose the solid part of PM2.5. We further applied Dynole 2-24, a selective inhibitor of dynamin GTPase which regulates the formation of clathrin-coated vesicles,26 to verify the endocytic mechanism. As shown in Figure 1D, E and Figure S4, the application of Dynole 2-24 significantly suppressed the endocytosis of PM2.5 into OE cells. The results confirmed the key role of CDE in the internalization of PM2.5 into the OE cells and implied that the appropriate medicine intervention, like chloroquine, which can affect the function of clathrin-coated pits,27 might hamper the invasion of PM2.5 into the OSNs during the haze days.

PM2.5 Destructed the Tight Junctions of the OSN Barriers

The OE is a tight barrier constituted by the OSNs that is responsible for the defense against exogenous substances.28 Since the cellular damages were observed under TEM (Figure 1B and Figure S2), we wonder whether the sublethal dosage of PM2.5 can impair the integrity of the OSN barrier. After 48 h of culture in the 24-well transwell insert, the OSNs constructed a tight barrier as evidenced by the TEER value being greater than 150 Ω·m2.29 However, the TEER of the OSNs significantly decreased by 12.3% after 24 h of exposure to T-PM2.5 (Figure 2A), indicating a damaged OSN barrier. Tight junction proteins, including ZO-1 and CLDN5, are the main components of the cell–cell adhesion structure, which are crucial for the maintenance of the OSN barrier. The suppressed expression levels of ZO-1 and CLDN5 further confirmed the degeneration of the OSN barrier (Figure 2B). These results were consistent with those reported in a series of studies conducted in Mexico. The residents who lived in heavily air-polluted Mexico City were found to have disrupted olfactory epithelium, e.g., epithelial dysplasia,11,30 and dogs from the same area were reported to have thinner olfactory epithelium.31 Although the TEER value only showed a slight decline after 24 h of exposure to WS-PM2.5 (decreased by 8.5%) and WIS-PM2.5 (decreased by 4.0%), the downregulated levels of tight junction proteins implied the potential of WS-PM2.5 and WIS-PM2.5 to damage the OSN barrier (Figure 2B). In addition, WS-PM2.5 seemed to exert a stronger influence on disrupting the OSN barrier since WIS-PM2.5 barely altered the expression level of ZO-1. The lesion of the OSN barrier may facilitate the paracellular transport of xenobiotics in the perineural spaces of the OE.16,32 We further verified that more PM2.5 could penetrate through the OSN barrier after exposure to T-PM2.5 and WS-PM2.5 via flow cytometry (Figure 2C). Therefore, these results implied that the paracellular pathway might also be involved in the transport of PM2.5 in the olfactory system, and WS-PM2.5 played an important role in this process.

WS-PM2.5 Induced the OSNs to Undergo Mitophagy and Inflammation

The results of the damaged OSN barrier highlighted the potential role of WS-PM2.5 in the OE paracellular pathway. Considering the observation of the mitophagosome in TEM (Figure 1B and Figure S2), we stained the lysosome and mitochondria to further dissect the influence of PM2.5 on the OSNs. As shown in Figure 3A, more overlap of the lysosome and mitochondria could be found in the T-PM2.5 and WS-PM2.5 groups. The fluorescence intensity profile and the high Pearsons’s correlation coefficient verified the high colocalization of the mitochondria and the lysosome in the T-PM2.5 and WS-PM2.5 groups (Figure 3B and Figure S5). Furthermore, the remarkably increased lysosome fluorescence signals confirmed the occurrence of mitophagia (Figure 3C).

The activation of TLR4-NFκB inflammation signaling is one of the important mechanisms for the induction of mitophagy.33,34 The transcriptional levels of genes related to TLR4-NFκB signaling were all markedly upregulated (Figure S6), indicating the activation of NFκB signaling in the T-PM2.5 and WS-PM2.5 groups. Transcription factor p65 (encoded by RelA) plays a crucial role in regulating the NFκB signaling, and the nuclear translocation of p65 marks the initiation of the NFκB cascade.35 Via immunostaining, we observed tremendous expressions of p65 in the nucleus of the OSNs treated with T-PM2.5 and WS-PM2.5 (Figure 3D, E), which further verified the activation of NFκB signaling. The mitophagy and inflammation induced by WS-PM2.5 may be responsible for the heightened effects on the OSN barrier compared to WIS-PM2.5. It has been reported that soluble heavy metals, including Zn, Cu, Mn, Pb, et al., are the main components of WS-PM2.5, whereas WIS-PM2.5 mainly consists of black carbon, insoluble metals (e.g., Fe and Al), and PAHs.36,37 The soluble metals like Mn and Pb are known to increase the level of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can break the redox balance of the cell and trigger inflammation.38,39 The inflammatory environment can further lead to the lesion of the biological barrier, including the olfactory epithelium.40,41 A recent research study reported that the elevated levels of soluble metals during atmospheric acidification could generate more ROS, thus enhancing the toxic potency of PM2.5.42 Therefore, the soluble metals on PM2.5 might be the primary contributors to the damaged OSN barrier. It should be mentioned that the observed result is, to some extent, different from the results of previous studies. WIS-PM2.5 was reported to induce stronger toxicity in rat cardiomyocytes36 and human lung epithelial cells (A549)18 than WS-PM2.5. Such a difference can be attributed to the different PM2.5 samples and the sensitivity of different cells. A previous research study found that PM2.5 from Wangdu (the same PM2.5 sampling city in this study) possessed a higher potency to generate ROS than PM2.5 from Beijing due to the higher load of soluble metals.43 Multiple solute carrier genes encoding metal transporters (e.g., DMT1, ZIP8, ZIP14, and ZnT1) are massively expressed on the OE cells and can facilitate the cellular intake of soluble metals,10 which may be the reason for the OE cells to be more sensitive to WS-PM2.5. More studies are needed to verify whether the OSNs are sensitive to WS-PM2.5, which can advance precise control strategies to diminish the health risks posed by PM2.5.

Future Perspectives

The pathway through which PM2.5 translocates to the CNS is a key issue that needs to be answered to fully understand the neurotoxic risk of PM2.5. Since the CNS is tightly protected by the BBB, the olfactory pathway provides a promising shortcut for the PM2.5.5,7,16,44 Multiple studies have reported the occurrence of PM2.5 in the olfactory system, including the olfactory nerve and olfactory bulb,11−13 but the translocation mechanism is still ambiguous. Using an in vitro model, we reported that the OSNs could respond to the water-insoluble part of PM2.5 (i.e., particles) and internalize PM2.5 via clathrin-dependent endocytosis, whereas the water-soluble part of PM2.5 could pose detrimental effects on the olfactory barrier. However, due to the current limited techniques, we failed to track the complete endocytosis process as well as the subsequent transportation of PM2.5 in the cell body. In addition, this study only provided the in vitro evidence, and in vivo studies are needed to get more conclusive evidence for the whole translocation process in the olfactory–brain pathway. Therefore, there is an urgent demand for the development of in situ high-resolution imaging (like multiphoton imaging and mass spectrometry imaging) that is suitable for long-time recording of PM2.5 transportation in both in vitro and in vivo models. In addition, whether the clathrin-dependent endocytosis of PM2.5 can be intervened by proper medicine administration should be further verified using animal models, which may provide some clues for preventing the health risks posed by the ultrafine particles during the heavily air-polluted days. In contrast, WS-PM2.5 was found to directly damage the OSN barrier via mitophagy and inflammation, and the soluble metals could be the primary contributors to the toxic effects. Therefore, diminishing the levels of heavy metals from the source (such as an industry emission source) or preventing the atmospheric acidification, which can reduce the bioavailability of metals, may also help decrease the health risks of PM2.5.

In summary, our study showed that PM2.5 can be internalized into the OSNs (mainly affected by the water-insoluble compositions) and can impair the integrity of the OSN barrier (mainly affected by the water-soluble compositions), indicating the involvement of both cellular and paracellular pathways in the translocation of PM2.5 in the olfactory system. These findings can shed some light on the mechanisms through which PM2.5 translocates to the CNS and highlight the crucial role of the olfactory system in the neurotoxic risks raised by PM2.5.
