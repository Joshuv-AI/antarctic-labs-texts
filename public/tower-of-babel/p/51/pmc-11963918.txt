Hypoperfusion Intensity Ratio as an Indirect Imaging Surrogate in Patients With Anterior Circulation Large‐Vessel Occlusion and Association of Baseline Characteristics With Poor Collateral Status
Authors: Omar Hamam, Julie Gudenkauf, Rawan Moustafa, Andrew Cho, Daniel Montes, Muhannad Sharara, Abdallah Moustafa, Mahla Radmard, Mehreen Nabi, Kevin Chen, Sadra Sepehri, Christopher Shin, Ishan Mazumdar, Minsoo Kim, Alireza Mohseni, Ajay Malhotra, Javier Romero, Vivek Yedavalli
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCollateral status (CS) plays a crucial role in infarct growth rate, risk of postthrombectomy hemorrhage, and overall clinical outcomes in patients with acute ischemic stroke (AIS) secondary to anterior circulation large‐vessel occlusions (LVOs). Hypoperfusion intensity ratio has been previously validated as an indirect noninvasive pretreatment imaging biomarker of CS. In addition to imaging, derangements in admission laboratory findings can also influence outcomes in patients with AIS‐LVO. Therefore, our study aims to assess the relationship between admission laboratory findings, baseline characteristics, and CS, as assessed by hypoperfusion intensity ratio in patients with AIS‐LVO.Methods and ResultsIn this retrospective study, consecutive patients presenting with AIS secondary to anterior circulation LVO who underwent pretreatment computed tomography perfusion were included. The computed tomography perfusion data processed by RAPID (Ischema View, Menlo Park, CA) generated the hypoperfusion intensity ratio. Binary logistic regression models were used to assess the relationship between patients' baseline characteristics, admission laboratory findings, and poor CS. A total of 221 consecutive patients with AIS‐LVO between January 2017 and September 2022 were included in our study (mean±SD age, 67.0±15.8 years; 119 men [53.8%]). Multivariable logistic regression showed that patients with AIS caused by cardioembolic and cryptogenic causes (adjusted odds ratio [OR], 2.67; 95% CI, 1.20–5.97; P=0.016), those who presented with admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score ≥12 (adjusted OR, 3.12; 95% CI, 1.61–6.04; P=0.001), and male patients (adjusted OR, 2.06; 95% CI, 1.13–3.77; P=0.018) were associated with poor CS.ConclusionsStroke caused by cardioembolic or cryptogenic causes, admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale score of ≥12, and male sex were associated with poor CS, as defined by hypoperfusion intensity ratio in the patients with AIS‐LVO.

The American Heart Association recently ranked stroke as the fifth cause of death among Americans. 1 , 2 Up to 34% of acute ischemic stroke (AIS) is attributable to anterior circulation large‐vessel occlusion (LVO). 3 Studies have shown collateral status (CS) is an important predictor of infarct growth, risk of postprocedural complication, and clinical outcomes for patients presenting with AIS secondary to LVO. Intravenous tPA (tissue‐type plasminogen activator) has been the standard treatment regimen for 2 decades; however, mechanical thrombectomy has become the standard of care for treating LVOs because of significantly improved outcomes. 4

CS has been shown to be an important biomarker of clinical outcomes in patients with LVO. Good CS is correlated with a low incidence of neurologic deficit, functional impairment, and mortality. In contrast, poor CS is associated with high mortality even after successful recanalization with reperfusion therapies. 5 , 6 , 7 Computed tomography perfusion (CTP) confers noninvasive quantification, estimation of the infarct core, and detection of salvageable tissue, also known as penumbra. Hypoperfusion intensity ratio (HIR) is a calculated CTP parameter previously validated as an indirect marker of CS. 8 Prior studies report that HIR is a reliable biomarker in assessing infarct tissue growth and functional outcomes in patients with AIS secondary to anterior circulation LVO. In addition, baseline patient characteristics and laboratory findings are associated with an increased risk of post–mechanical thrombectomy complications with worse functional outcomes in patients with AIS secondary to anterior circulation LVO. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12

Hemorrhagic transformation and cerebral edema are the worst consequences of AIS, which is frequently associated with a poor neurologic outcome. 13 It is characterized by a malignant progression of rapid neurologic deterioration accompanied by extensive cerebral edema, resulting in transtentorial herniation and death or poor functional prognosis. 14 CS plays an important role in the pathophysiology of cerebral ischemia. In addition, poor CS reportedly has a significant association with malignant middle cerebral artery evolution after reperfusion therapy and is related to the rate of early edema progression in patients with AIS. It is believed that poor CS accelerates the recruitment of ischemic penumbra into infarction, resulting in a larger infarct size; however, the exact mechanism underlying this association remains to be determined. 15 In patients undergoing recanalization, those with initially poor CS may develop greater early brain edema and have a higher early edema progression rate (1.6% edema progression rate per 1 collateral status point) 24 hours after AIS. 15 , 16 Poor collateral status is known to augment the progression of the infarct core and induce more proximal vascular occlusion, which are both associated with brain edema. 17 Poor CS relates to an increased degree of ischemic edema, which can increase interstitial pressure, resulting in an increase in the resistance of collateral arterioles and downstream perforating arterioles. 18

Baseline characteristics, such as metabolic and hematologic laboratory findings, as well as vital signs, have been shown to be associated with CS. For instance, admission systolic blood pressure has been reported to be inversely associated with the automated scoring of CS on baseline computed tomography (CT) angiography. 19 Recently, studies have reported an increased incidence of good CS with higher body mass index measurements. 20 , 21 , 22 Assessing the relationships between these baseline parameters with imaging biomarkers of CS can potentially aid in expediting emergent treatment for these patients.

Therefore, the present study aimed to determine which baseline characteristics can predict CS, as defined by HIR in patients presenting with AIS secondary to anterior circulation LVO.

Methods

Study Population

The authors declare that all supporting data are available within the article. The institutional review board–approved study included consecutive patients with anterior circulation LVO who underwent baseline CT angiography and CTP for their stroke management from January 1, 2017, to September 1, 2022, and waived the need for informed consent based on the observational nature of the study. Anterior circulation LVO was defined as an occlusion of the intracranial internal carotid artery, M1, or proximal M2 segments of the middle cerebral artery. 23

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Eligible patients were 18 to 85 years of age, both sexes, and had AIS of anterior circulation LVO. Other eligibility criteria included no evidence of intracranial hemorrhage on neuroimaging, as previously described. 24 Patients were ineligible if they had a midline shift or clinical signs of herniation, mass effect, high risk of hemorrhage, acute bilateral strokes, or multiple intracranial occlusions. Technical Parameters

Baseline comprehensive CT imaging was performed at the Johns Hopkins East Baltimore and Bayview Medical Centers using helical scanners on the Siemens Flash or Drive (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). For noncontrast CT: helical mode at 5‐mm slice thickness, 120 kVp, 365 mAs, rotation time 1 second, acquisition time 6 to 8 seconds, collimation 128×0.6 mm, pitch value 0.55, scan direction craniocaudal. For CTP: injection of 50 mL nonionic iodinated contrast with 30 mL saline flush at 5 to 6 mL/s with coverage of 70 to 100 mm at 5‐mm slice thickness. CTP parameters: 70 kVp, 200 effective mAs, rotation time 0.25 seconds, average acquisition time 60 seconds, collimation 48×1.2 mm, pitch value 0.7, 4‐dimensional range 114 mm×1.5 seconds. CTP images were then postprocessed using RAPID commercial software (Ischema View, Menlo Park, CA) for generating time to maximum (Tmax) maps, which were subsequently used to calculate HIR. For CT angiography head and neck: nonionic iodinated contrast with 50 to 70 mL injected at 5 to 6 mL/s from the aortic arch through the vertex using a bolus‐triggered method at 3‐mm slice thickness. CT angiography parameters: 90/150 kVp with an Sn filter, quality reference mAs 180, rotation time 0.25 seconds, average acquisition time 3 to 5 seconds, collimation 128×0.6 mm, pitch value 0.7, scan direction craniocaudal. All imaging was reviewed for central adjudication of Alberta Stroke Program Early CT Score (ASPECTS) values, occlusion location, perfusion measures, angiographic procedure success, hemorrhages, and malignant brain edema.

Data Collection

The baseline and clinical data for each patient were collected through manual medical record review. The variables collected for each patient included patient demographics, body mass index, admission National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score, and admission laboratory values, including the following: hemoglobin level, hematocrit, white blood cell count, platelet count, random blood glucose level, blood urea nitrogen level, and creatinine level; admission vital signs, including blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, and ASPECTS on noncontrast CT, were calculated by a board‐certified neuroradiologist (V.S.Y., 6 years of experience). The CS was quantified using the HIR and measured using the RAPID commercial software platform (Ischema View) after postprocessing the CTP images. The HIR values were dichotomized into poor CS and good CS. Poor CS was defined as an HIR of ≥0.4, whereas good CS was <0.4. 25 Stroke causes were classified according to Trial of Org 10172 in AIS Treatment criteria, 26 with the following designations: (1) large‐artery atherosclerosis, (2) cardioembolism, (3) small‐vessel occlusion, (4) stroke of other determined cause, and (5) stroke of undetermined cause. 26 Cryptogenic stroke was defined as a combination of categories 4 and 5. Patients were then dichotomized into poor and good CS based on HIR.

Study Outcomes

The primary outcome measure was dichotomized HIR, where ≥0.4 is considered poor CS.

Statistical Analysis

The data of the current evidence were collected on a secure desktop using Microsoft Office Excel 2007 (Redmond, WA) and analyzed using IBM SPSS statistics, version 22.0 (Chicago, IL), and Graph Pad Prism, version 8.0 (San Diego, CA). Continuous variables were presented as mean and SD or median and interquartile range based on the distribution of variables. Normality for all continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro‐Wilk test. Quantitative data were compared using the independent t‐test. Qualitative data were compared using χ2 or Fisher exact tests. Univariate analysis was initially applied to examine each of the baseline variables independently. Bonferroni corrections were applied for post hoc tests as multiple comparisons were made on the same dependent variables to reduce the risk of type 1 errors. When appropriate, binary logistic regression was used to examine the association between outcomes. All P values were 2 sided, and P<0.05 was statistically significant, unless multiple comparisons were made, for which Bonferroni correction was applied.

Results

Patients' Demographics of Anterior Circulation, According to HIR

A total of 470 consecutive patients were reviewed, of whom 249 (53%) were excluded because (1) CTP was nondiagnostic for patients (n=13), (2) no LVO was detected on CT angiography (n=179), or (3) no CTP was performed because either NIHSS score <6 (n=13) or NIHSS score ≥6 (n=44) at the time of admission. For NIHSS score <6, CT is not recommended; however, for NIHSS score ≥6, CTP is recommended, but in our study cases, CTP was not performed for these cases and we are investigating the rationale for not performing CTP on patients with NIHSS score ≥6. Finally, a total of 221 (47%) patients were included in the study. Of the 221 included patients, 119 (53.8%) were men, and 102 (46.2%) were women. The mean±SD age of the patients was 67.0±15.8 years (Figure 1). Ninety‐four (94/221, 42.5%) were characterized as good CS, with 124 (124/221, 57.5%) in the poor CS group. Intravenous tPA was performed on 19 cases with NIHSS score <6, of which 8 had poor CS and 11 has good CS based on HIR. No significant difference (P=0.938) was observed between the 2 groups receiving intravenous tPA.

Univariate Analysis of Patient Demographics

Univariate analysis showed that patients with poor CS presented with more severe stroke on admission (poor CS, 17.1±6.5; good CS, 12.7±7.0; P<0.001), lower diastolic blood pressure (DBP) (poor CS, 84.6±17.5 mm Hg; good CS, 90.6±22.2 mm Hg; P=0.029), and high likelihood of a history of heart disease (poor CS, 70 [55.1%]; good CS, 36 [38.3%]; P=0.013), last known well to CT time (poor CS, 443±795.9 minutes; good CS, 849±2265 minutes; P=0.025), and last known well to door time (poor CS, 410±791.6 minutes; good CS, 791.9±2216 minutes; P=0.022), as shown in Figure 2A through 2E.

In the M1 segment subgroup analysis, patients who presented with more severe stroke (poor CS, 17.9±6.5; good CS, 13.1±7.1; P<0.001), lower systolic blood pressure (poor CS, 145.0±24.1 mm Hg; good CS, 156.1±29.9 mm Hg; P=0.010), lower DBP (poor CS, 84.0±18.0 mm Hg; good CS, 92.6±22.4 mm Hg; P=0.007), and decreased last well to door time (poor CS, 380.2±539.4 minutes; good CS, 526.8±556.4 minutes; P=0.018) were associated with poor CS (Figure 2F–2I).

In the proximal M2 group, patients who presented with a more severe stroke of NIHSS score ≥12 (poor CS, 15.0±6.5; good CS, 10.5±6.3; P<0.031) were associated with poor CS. In addition, heart disease was more prevalent in the poor CS group (poor CS, 65.6%; good CS, 33.3%; P=0.038) in the proximal M2 segment group analysis (Figure 2J and 2K).

Discussion

In this study, we report that in patients with AIS secondary to anterior circulation LVO, those who present with higher admission NIHSS of ≥12, cardioembolic or cryptogenic causes, and male sex were independent predictors of poor CS, as defined by HIR.

In our study, higher stroke severity on admission (admission NIHSS score ≥12) was associated with poor CS in all anterior circulation, M1, and proximal M2 segment subgroup analyses. Increased stroke severity, as determined by admission NIHSS score, has been previously associated with poor CS. 27 The NIHSS score is commonly used to assess the severity of AIS. 28 The NIHSS score is known to correlate with CS and the severity of the perfusion deficit. 29 Fischer et al, in 2005, found significantly positive associations between NIHSS scores and both the presence and the location of vessel occlusion. 30 , 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 They reported that patients with admission NIHSS scores ≥12 had a positive predictive value of 91% to have AIS‐LVOs. The finding of our study is consistent with other studies that have reported a significant association of admission NIHSS score with poor CS. 29 , 36 , 37

Male sex has been previously reported to be associated with poor CS. According to Rexrode et al, in 2022, stroke incidence was higher in men than women during midlife. 38 The exact reason for sex bias in patients with CS is not known. However, smoking, alcohol use, large‐vessel atherosclerosis, and peripheral vascular disease are more prevalent in men with stroke than in women with AIS. 39 These factors result in premature vessel aging and impaired autoregulation, possibly accounting for men's lower CS. Several agents or mechanisms, including steroids‐ and sex hormones, sex chromosomes, cell death differences, immune pathways, epigenetic regulation, and sex‐specific microRNAs, have been suggested to contribute to sex differences in stroke. 40 , 41 Our results show that male patients have poorer CS based on HIR when compared with female patients, corroborating prior evidence.

Our study showed that stroke causes of cardioembolism and cryptogenic causes were more likely in patients with poor CS. One possible mechanism is that cardioembolic strokes develop acutely, not allowing time for the collateralization to occur. Another mechanism is that most patients with cardioembolic or cryptogenic stroke tend to have more severe symptoms, which translates to a higher admission NIHSS score. 38 On the other side, large‐artery atherosclerosis was associated with good CS. 36 It has been reported that chronic hypoperfusion is associated with better collateral circulation. Collateral circulation could maintain perfusion, and greater collateral circulation might contribute to a better baseline condition, such as a lower baseline NIHSS score and baseline infarct core volume. 42 Previously, it has been reported that large‐artery atherosclerosis is associated significantly with increased rates of good preintervention collaterals. 43 Rebello et al confirmed the pathophysiological mechanism in a study on patients with AIS with cervical atherosclerotic steno‐occlusive disease who had favorable preintervention collaterals compared with patients having embolic stroke, secondary to atrial fibrillation. 44 , 45 Other studies have reported that cardioembolic stroke is generally present with more severe deficits than a stroke of other origins. 36 , 46 This is explained by the fact that cardioembolic stroke is usually associated with relatively large thrombi, resulting more often in more proximal LVO compared with a stroke of other cause. A recent study showed atrial fibrillation was associated with symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage in patients with AIS treated with intravenous thrombolysis. This could be explained by the presence of poor collaterals in patients with atrial fibrillation, or in patients with AIS with cerebral edema cause, leading to an increased risk of symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage after reperfusion. 47

On the basis of our findings, low DBP was found to be significantly associated with poor CS; however, it was not observed to be an independent predictor of CS. In addition, hypertension showed no association with CS. Numerous studies have observed the J‐curve phenomenon, in which diastolic pressure is inversely related to the risk of coronary heart disease and adverse outcomes because coronary arteries are predominantly perfused during diastole. 48 , 49 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 According to a study, hypotension time according to the CS was not statistically predictive of poor outcomes but displayed a trend toward worse outcomes for patients with poor CS only. 54 In addition, numerous epidemiologic studies have shown hypertension as an independent risk factor for cerebrovascular diseases. In the clinical setting, hypertension is still largely interpreted as “diastolic hypertension” for unknown reasons, although systolic blood pressure is more closely related to the incidence of stroke than DBP. 55 , 56 , 57 , 58 Our findings are consistent with previous studies in which no association between DBP and hypertension was observed with CS.

CS has been reported to be a predictor of cerebral edema in ischemic stroke. 59 Jo et al reported that a collateral status score of <2 strongly predicts malignant cerebral edema. 60 Hemorrhagic transformation after endovascular thrombectomy most probably results from the blood flow restoration in the setting of blood‐brain barrier disruption and increased tissue permeability attributable to previous ischemic endothelial injury. 61 , 62 In a systematic review, it has been reported that patients presenting with higher NIHSS scores, depressed consciousness, nausea, or vomiting, and receiving ventilation are more likely to develop edema. However, there was no association of brain edema with hemispheric lateralization of infarct, large arter atherosclerosis, and cardioembolism with CS. Successful revascularization reduces the risk for malignant edema. 63

In this study, we observed that baseline characteristics were significantly different between M1 and proximal M2 subgroups. Limited data are available for the comparison of M1 and M2 branches in ischemic strokes. Previously, few studies have reported no difference in functional outcomes of M1 and M2 occlusion in patients with AIS. 64 However, the differentiation of dominant and nondominant M2 branches is important because dominant M2 branch occlusion can be responsible for perfusion of the main part of middle cerebral artery territory, resulting in similar outcomes between M1 and M2 occlusion. According to Dorn et al, favorable functional outcomes were seen more in the M2 group than in the M1 group (60% versus 43.3% for modified Rankin Scale score ≤2), and they also found that mortality was higher in the M1 group (21.2% versus 6.7%). 65 In line with previous studies, we observed that patients with M2 occlusions presented with less severe AIS compared with those with M1 occlusions. This is expected as the area of affected tissue is smaller with M2 compared with M1 occlusions.

This also explains that other baseline characteristics of patients with M2 occlusions were different from those with M1 occlusions. Our findings are also concordant with the reported studies that M2 occlusions are associated with more favorable outcomes compared with M1 occlusions, as these patients present with less severe initial AIS. 64 , 66

The study is strengthened by its sample size and multicenter approach with consecutive patients. However, our investigation is inherently limited by its retrospective design, which may introduce bias and limit generalizability. CTP is not available in many centers, as is the ability to generate HIR by automated postprocessing.

Conclusions

Higher stroke severity on admission with NIHSS score ≥12, cardioembolism or cryptogenic causes, and male sex are independently associated with poor CS in patients with AIS attributable to anterior circulation LVO, as defined by HIR. These findings are more pronounced in M1 occlusions compared with internal carotid artery and M2 occlusions.
