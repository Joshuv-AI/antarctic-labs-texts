Proteomic analysis of cerebrospinal fluid from females with multiple sclerosis highlights elevated microglial function and impaired neurogenesis
Authors: Dean Oldham, Anthony Saviola, Enrique Alvarez, Kimberley D. Bruce
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune disorder of the central nervous system (CNS) that is characterized by demyelination, inflammation, and neurological damage. MS is the most common neurological disorder of young adults, negatively impacting their quality of life. Recent population-based estimates have determined that the prevalence of MS in the United States is growing and can be up to 3 times higher in females. While the etiology of MS is complex, involving genetics, immune dysregulation, and environmental triggers, the factors elevating MS risk in women are relatively unexplored. Hence, there is a major need for studies that further our understanding of the pathophysiology of MS in women and identify potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets. To this end, we used highly sensitive and untargeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) to identify proteins in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of age-matched females who were either diagnosed with MS or headache (HA). We found that the CSF of female individuals with MS was enriched in proteins involved in macrophage and microglia function yet depleted in proteins involved in neurogenesis and neuronal function. Overall, our findings support recently identified therapeutic targets (e.g., FABP5), as well as highlighting potential targets that may predict or promote MS neuropathogenesis in females (e.g., CD99, APOC3), which should be studied in larger cohorts going forward.

Introduction

Multiple sclerosis (MS) is an autoimmune disorder of the central nervous system (CNS), characterized by demyelination, inflammation, and neurological damage (McGinley et al., 2021). Although the symptoms of MS are variable, it can clinically manifest as muscle weakness, fatigue, cognitive changes, impaired vision, bladder and bowel dysfunction, and spasticity, having a major impact on quality of life (Society, 2025). Unfortunately, MS commonly affects younger adults, impacting critical life stages, such as career development and raising a family. Recent population-based estimates have determined that 2.9 million people are living with MS worldwide, 75 % of whom are women; a prevalence that appears to be increasing (McGinley et al., 2021, Hittle et al., 2023). While there are a growing number of disease-modifying therapeutics (DMTs) for MS, which range in mechanism of action (MOA) and efficacy (McGinley et al., 2021), therapies that promote remyelination and repair are still lacking. To better tailor DMTs to the individual and identify targets with the potential to improve remyelination and MS outcomes, a deeper understanding of the mechanisms triggering MS is greatly needed.

MS risk is determined by several established and emerging factors, including genetics (Parnell and Booth, 2017), immune system dysfunction (Khan et al., 2024), exposure to Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) (Bjornevik et al., 2022), and being female (Jakimovski et al., 2024). The complicated etiology of MA presents challenges for biomarker identification, diagnosis, and individualized treatment plans. MS is equally devastating for men and women. However, in the US, women between the ages of 35–54 are 3 times more likely to be diagnosed with MS (Hittle et al., 2023), warranting studies that specifically identify factors associated with MS incidence in this age group of women. While women tend to exhibit more inflammatory markers of MS during MRI imaging studies (Nathoo et al., 2024), which may be exacerbated following menopause (Lorefice et al., 2023), the mechanisms driving the increased susceptibility of MS in women are relatively unexplored. This isin part, due to the lack of studies focusing on female subjects, highlighting the need for investigations that identify the molecular signatures of MS in women. Such analyses will not only further our understanding of the pathophysiology of MS but will also help to identify potential biomarkers, novel therapeutic targets, and appropriate treatment regimens. To this end, in the present study, we used highly sensitive and untargeted liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS) to identify proteins in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) of age-matched females that were either diagnosed with MS or headache (HA) only. Analysis of 120 differentially regulated proteins (DEPs) revealed enrichment in proteins associated with macrophage and microglia function in the CSF from MS individuals. In contrast, proteins involved in neurogenesis and neuronal function were depleted. Notably, our findings support recently identified therapeutic targets (e.g., CSFR1, FABP5) and highlight novel targets that may predict or promote MS neuropathogenesis in females (e.g., CD99, APOC3).

Methods

Patients

CSF samples were obtained from patients undergoing diagnostic lumbar puncture and deposited in the Rocky Mountain MS Center (RMMSC) biorepository. Patients enrolled in the biorepository specimen bank at the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus were identified retrospectively. Patients who were evaluated in the Autoimmune and Neuroimmunology/Multiple Sclerosis outpatient clinics or in the inpatient neurology service at the University of Colorado with a diagnosis of MS were enrolled prospectively into the biorepository. Patients who had been enrolled in the biorepository specimen bank for a primary evaluation of headaches without other significant neurologic symptoms were identified retrospectively to serve as a control group. N = 3 age-matched (MS 39 ± 7.9 and HA 39 ± 10.4) female MS and HA subjects were retrieved and processed for LC-MS analysis. All patients or legal representatives consented to enrollment in the autoimmune biorepository [approved by the Colorado Multiple Institutional Review Board (COMIRB)].

Sample digestion

CSF samples were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen following acquisition and stored at −80 °C until processed for liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis. 100 μL of CSF from each sample was subjected to trypsin digestion using S-Trap mini spin columns (Protifi #C02-mini) following the manufacturer's protocol for biofluid analysis. In brief, samples were reduced using Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP), alkylated with 2-chloroacetamide (CIASS), and digested with trypsin (1:100 enzyme:substrate ratio) for approximately 15 h at 37°C. Digested peptides were cleaned using Pierce C18 spin columns (Thermo Fisher #84850) according to the manufacturer's protocol.

MS acquisition

Desalted peptides were adjusted to a protein concentration of 100 ng/μL with 0.1 % formic acid (FA) in preparation for LC-MS analysis. Digested peptides were loaded into autosampler vials and analyzed directly using a NanoElute liquid chromatography system (Bruker, Germany) coupled with a timsTOF SCP mass spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). Peptides were separated on a 75 m i.d. × 15 cm separation column packed with 1.9-μm C18 beads (Bruker, Germany) over a 90-minute elution gradient. Buffer A was 0.1 % FA in water and buffer B was 0.1 % FA in acetonitrile. Instrument control and data acquisition were performed using Compass Hystar (version 6.0) with the tims TOF SCP operating in parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (PASEF) mode under the following settings: mass range 100–1700 m/z, 1/k/0 Start 0.7 s cm-2 End 1.3 s cm-2; ramp accumulation times were 166 ms; capillary voltage was 4500 V, dry gas 8.0 L min-1 and dry temp 200°C. The PASEF settings were: 5 MS/MS scans (total cycle time, 1.03 s); charge range 0–5; active exclusion for 0.2 min; scheduling target intensity 20,000; intensity threshold 500; collision-induced dissociation energy 10 eV.

MS/MS data analysis

Spectra were interpreted against SwissProt entries of H. sapiens (Proteome ID: UP000005640), with reverse decoys and contaminants added using MSFragger via FragPipe v 20.0. Cysteine carbamidomethylation was set as the fixed modification, and oxidation of methionine was used as the variable with an FDR set at 0.01. To determine active viral infection, we generated a new protein sequence database consisting of the human protein sequence database (proteome ID UP000005640) merged with protein sequences from Varicella-zoster virus (proteome ID UP000002602), human herpesvirus 1 (proteome ID UP000009294), and Epstein-Barr virus (proteome ID UP000007639).

Protein analysis

To determine differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), intensity values for each identified protein for each sample were processed with MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (Xia and Wishart, 2010). Proteins with missing values were removed from the analysis. Variance filtering was performed by MetaboAnalyst 6.0 using the Interquartile range (IQR) pipeline. Data was logarithmically transformed for visualization. An unpaired two-sample t-test and Wilcoxon rank-sum test were performed using a p-value of 0.05 as the threshold for significance. Significantly altered proteins were considered DEPs. Proteins that were 2-fold increased or decreased, were also considered meaningful. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to the raw p-values using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure. These adjusted p-values were used to identify proteins with statistically significant differences in expression between the MS and HA groups. To visualize the results, a volcano plot was generated with log₂ fold change on the x-axis and –log₁₀ of the adjusted p-value on the y-axis. Proteins with log₂ fold change ≤ -1 were colored blue, and those with fold change ≥ 1 were colored red. The 2D PCA and PLS-DA plots with the 95 % confidence interval were generated by MetaboAnalyst 6.0. The top 15 features of component 1 of the PLS-DA were graphed using the variable importance in projection (VIP) method. Lastly, all significantly different proteins identified by the t-test and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (n = 120) were plotted for each replicate in a heatmap.

Pathway analysis

Pathway analysis was performed using ShinyGO 0.80 (Ge et al., 2020) using the significantly upregulated and downregulated DEPs (FDR < 0.05). The top 20 KEGG and biological process pathways acquired for the upregulated proteins were selected by their FDR and sorted by their fold enrichment. Pathways with Identical protein profiles were combined, and titles were shortened to reduce space. Complete results with associated proteins in the pathway are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Results were plotted as a bubble enrichment plot using https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/en. A secondary pathway analysis using proteins with a 2-fold change greater than or less than the control and are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

WebCSEA

Web-based Cell-type Specific Enrichment Analysis of Genes (WebCSEA) was used for cell type analysis of the MS-CSF proteins (Dai et al., 2022). DEPs were processed by WebCSEA for cell type analysis. Proteins were mapped onto a background gene list revealing cell type enrichment. Graphs showing the top 20 cell types were generated with their raw p-value. Each point represents one tissue cell type and is graphed with a Bonferroni-corrected significance threshold equal to 3.69 × 10−5 (red dashed line) and a nominal significance of 0.001 (grey line). Similarly to pathway analysis, proteins that were at least 2-fold increased or decreased were also analyzed (Fig. 2; Fig. 3).

Results

CSF samples were obtained from patients undergoing diagnostic lumbar puncture and deposited in the Rocky Mountain MS Center (RMMSC) biorepository. Samples from age-matched female MS (39 ± 7.9) and HA (39 ± 10.4) subjects were retrieved and processed for LC-MS analysis, identification of differentially expressed proteins (DEPs), and Pathway enrichment (Fig. 1a). In total, 1502 unique proteins were identified. Comparing proteins identified in the MS and HA groups by Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Partial Least-Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLSDA), revealed distinct and non-overlapping protein signatures, even with the small sample size, prompting further investigation into the proteins and pathways identified (Fig. 1b-c). To determine which proteins were driving the distinct protein signatures, variable importance in projection (VIP) scores were calculated (Fig. 1d). 15 proteins were identified as driving variance of the model, most of which were upregulated in MS compared to HA. These included proteins with known roles in MS pathophysiology, such as variable domains of Immunoglobulin light chains (Immunoglobulin Lambda Variable 2–23[IGLV2–23]); proteins linking microglia and macrophage function to MS, such as Galectin-3 (LGALS3/GAL3) (Xue et al., 2023), Catalase (CAT) (Gray et al., 2014), and Arginase-1 (ARG1) (Ivan et al., 2021); proteins that have previously been associated with MS in genome-wide association study (GWAS) analyses, such as Corneodesmosin (CSDN) and Plectin (PLEC) (Orton et al., 2022, Li et al., 2021); and proteins that have been associated with MS pathology and viral replication such as the Proteosome subunit alpha type 7 (PSMA7) (Nickles et al., 2013). Interestingly, sex-hormone binding globulin (SHBG), which can slow the rate of testosterone uptake into the CSF was a main factor driving the differences between female MS and female HA protein signatures (Hobbs et al., 1992). MetaboAnalyst 6.0 revealed 120 DEPs between the MS and HA groups (Fig. 2a). Seventy-two proteins were significantly upregulated in the CSF of MS subjects compared to HA controls (Fig. 2a). Consistent with the VIP scores, several proteins linked to macrophage and microglia function were up-regulated, such as Neuraminidase 1 (NEU1) (Allendorf and Brown, 2022), Cathepsin A (CTSA) (Macrophages and cathepsin et al., 1974), Colony-stimulating factor-1 receptor (CSF1R) (Marzan et al., 2021), Fatty acid binding protein 5 (FABP5) (Hammond et al., 2019, Hamel et al., 2023), Major Histocompatibility Complex, Class I, A (HLA-A) (Martin et al., 2021), Collectin 12 (COLEC12) (van den Bosch et al., 2024), and Cluster of differentiation 99 (CD99) (Winschel et al., 2024). Notably, CSF1R and FABP5 inhibitors are currently being investigated as MS therapeutics that target microglial activation in both clinical and preclinical studies, respectively (Cheng et al., 2021, Hagan et al., 2020). Interestingly, expression of CD99 is higher in males and leads to an exacerbated inflammatory response to lipopolysaccharide T cell stimulation (Winschel et al., 2024). Whereas the expression of COLEC12 has recently been shown to be enriched in female microglia in response to demyelination (Soler-Sáez et al., 2024). Together, these proteins do not represent an existing, or pre-defined macrophage and microglial subpopulation, highlighting atypical polarization of immune cells in these subjects. Analysis of proteins upregulated in MS compared to HA, also revealed an increased abundance of proteins involved in lipid and lipoprotein metabolism such as Apolipoprotein C3 (APOC3) (Zewinger et al., 2020), Acyl coenzyme A binding protein (DBI) (Joseph et al., 2020), FABP5, and COLEC12, which likely highlights the attempts to resolve aberrant lipid processing in demyelinating MS. Pathway analysis of the upregulated DEPs indicated that response to infection, estrogen signaling, and PPAR signaling were enriched in the CSF of MS subjects (Fig. 2c, Supplemental Table 2). Since pathway analysis is challenging with a small set of proteins, analysis was repeated using all proteins that were 2.0-fold upregulated in MS compared to headache (Fig. 2d-e, and Supplemental Table 3). Importantly, 7 out of 20 pathways identified involved neurodegenerative disease pathogenesis (Parkinson's disease, ALS, AD, Huntington's disease), and 3 out of 20 pathways involved the elevation of endogenous proteins involved in the response to microbial pathogens (COVID-19, E. coli, Salmonella). To determine which cell types were represented by the DEPs, we performed a web-based cell-type-specific enrichment analysis (WebCSEA). In support of immune cell activation, proteins expressed by macrophages and other immune cells were enriched in the DEPs (Fig. 2f), and proteins with a 2.0-fold increase (Supplemental Figure 1).

Forty-six proteins were significantly downregulated in MS patients compared to HA controls. Downregulated DEPs included proteins involved in autophagy and lysosomal biology such as Lysosomal Associated Membrane Protein 2 (LAMP2) Cathepsin S (CTSS) (Haves-Zburof et al., 2011, Xu et al., 2023, Tang et al., 2024), Ganglioside GM2 activator (GM2A) (Lopshire et al., 2023), and iduronate 2-sulfatase (IDS) (Arguello et al., 2021); proteins involved in neural protection such as Neuronal pentraxin 2 (NPTX2), Netrin receptors/Deleted in colorectal carcinoma (DCC) (Jasmin et al., 2021, Zhou et al., 2023); and proteins involved in axonal transport and neuronal functions such as SLIT and NTRK like family member 1 (SLITRK1) (Hatayama and Aruga, 2022), SLIT and NTRK like family member 4 (SLITRK4) (Matsumoto et al., 2024), DCC, Semaphorin 6 A (SEMA6A) (Lettieri et al., 2023), and Neurexophilin-1 (NXPH1) (Born et al., 2014). Interestingly, factors that are involved in the immune suppression of microglia and other immune cells, such as Cluster of Differentiation 200 (CD200) (Koning et al., 2007), NPTX2, Neuropilin-1 (NRP1) (Solomon et al., 2011), and Semaphorin 3 G (SEMA3G) (Tan et al., 2019), were also downregulated, suggesting that immune activation in MS females could also result from a lack of immune suppression. The most robustly downregulated protein in MS was leucine-rich repeats and transmembrane domains 2 (LRTM2), which is predominantly expressed in astrocytes and neurons. While LRTM2 has not been previously linked to MS, recent studies have reported a role for LRTM2 in GABAergic synaptic vesicle formation, monoamine metabolism, and motor function (Ichise et al., 2022), highlighting a novel role in MS pathology. Pathway analysis of DEPs confirmed that the CSF of female patients with MS was depleted in proteins involved in neurogenesis and axonogenesis, with 18 out of 20 enriched pathways involving neuronal function and regeneration (Fig. 3a-b, and Supplemental Table 5). Negative regulation of neuronal function and neurogenesis was also highlighted when all −2.0-fold proteins were incorporated into the analysis (Fig. 3c, and Supplemental Table 6). To further confirm that the depleted proteins were of neuronal origin, we performed WebCSEA on downregulated proteins. Neurons, specifically bipolar and excitatory neurons, were enriched in the downregulated DEPs (Fig. 3d) and larger data set (-2.0-fold depleted, see supplemental Figure 2). Although DEPS were generated using validated MetaboAnalyst 6.0 software, we reanalyzed DEPs using a more conservative P-value adjustment (Fig. 4a). Proteins driving key pathways of interest, namely, hormonal regulation (SHBG, Fig. 4b), lipid metabolism, and microglia/macrophage function (APOC3, FABP5, CD99, Fig. 4c-e), remained robustly upregulated. Summary and conclusion

These data show that the CSF of female MS patients is enriched in proteins involved in macrophage and microglia activation yet depleted in proteins involved in the immune suppression of these cells, highlighting aberrant microglial phenotypes as a targetable process in females with MS, and, consistent with recent data, showing exacerbated microglial dysfunction in females with aging (Lynch, 2022). Our analysis also shows that the CSF of female MS patients contains protein signatures indicative of defective lysosomal function, and impaired neurogenesis/neuronal function, consistent with the impaired regeneration and remyelination observed in MS. While this is a small study, our findings galvanize the importance of existing therapeutic targets (e.g., CSFR1, FABP5), as well as highlight novel targets that may predict or promote MS neuropathogenesis in females (e.g., SHBG, ARG1, APOC3, LRTM2 and NXPH1), and should be the focus of future studies involving larger cohorts in both men and women.
