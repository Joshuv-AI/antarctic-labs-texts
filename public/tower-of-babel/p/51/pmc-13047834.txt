Exploration of facilitators and barriers to the implementation of the eight-contact antenatal care model in healthcare facilities in Nigeria
Authors: Oluwayomi T. Adebisi, Margaret O. Akinwaare
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study explored the facilitators and barriers influencing the implementation of the eight-contact ANC model in selected healthcare facilities in Nigeria.DesignA qualitative study using semi structured interviews. The data were analysed via thematic analysis. A qualitative descriptive design was employed, involving key informant interviews with antenatal care providers and focus group discussions with pregnant women. The interviews were audio recorded and transcribed.SettingAntenatal care clinic in selected health facilities.ParticipantsA purposive sample of 8 antenatal care providers (doctors, nurses, and allied health professionals) who are heads of their departments between November and April 2025 were eligible to participate. Four focus group discussions with pregnant women were held, each group consist of 8 pregnant women, with an age range of 16–38 years, and data saturation was reached.ResultsFour themes were identified: (1) implementation status, (2) facilitators of implementation, (3) barriers to implementation, and (4) opinions of pregnant women. Major barriers identified by antenatal care providers include inadequate awareness and training Pregnant women also reported low health literacy, long travel distances, delayed initiation of ANC, and negative provider attitudes as challenges (The way she attended to me was rude”). Providers reported a key finding, willingness to adopt innovations (Training healthcare workers on the latest guidelines in antenatal services will help in implementing any policy that is being introduced”), availability of health education materials, and If there is availability of supplies, we will be able to implement the model. It’s been a while since we got supplies from the government to facilitate ANC services”).ConclusionsThis study offers valuable insight into the experiences of some frontline health professionals working in antenatal clinics. This study concludes that the eight-contact ANC model has not been effectively implemented due to systemic challenges such as insufficient provider training, weak institutional support, and socioeconomic limitations among pregnant women. Although women value ANC services, gaps in awareness, structural constraints, and negative care experiences hinder their full utilization. A disconnect remains between WHO guidelines and real-world clinical practice in these settings.

Introduction

Antenatal care (ANC) as a crucial component of maternal health and a key factor in reducing maternal and infant mortality rates [1]. Globally, approximately 210 million pregnancies occur each year, and proper antenatal care can significantly mitigate the adverse effects associated with these pregnancies, emphasizing the importance of prioritizing antenatal care to achieve the objectives of the Global Strategy for the Health of Women, Children, and Adolescents [2]. Antenatal care refers to the treatment provided to expectant mothers by trained medical personnel, including preventive, promotive, and therapeutic services for pregnancy-related and other medical conditions. This includes risk assessment, health promotion, health education, and the prevention and treatment of illnesses related to pregnancy or that occur concurrently. ANC is vital for ensuring favourable pregnancy outcomes and protecting the health of both mothers and babies throughout pregnancy [3].

Maternal mortality remains a critical global challenge and a vital indicator of a country’s development, with women in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) facing a 1 in 36 lifetime risk, and maternal and neonatal mortality rates continue to be alarmingly high [2]. Lateef et al. [4] reported that approximately 287,000 women have died during and after pregnancy, with SSA accounting for 70% of maternal deaths (202,000 out of 287,000); these figures highlight the urgent need for action to achieve the SDG by 2030, which aims to reduce maternal mortality to less than 70 per 100,000 live births. In low- and middle-income countries, the utilization of ANC has increased over time; however, this growth has not been associated with a decrease in maternal death or mortality, highlighting the need for a paradigm shift from simply using ANC to using it in a quality manner [5].

The World Health Organisation (WHO) has issued a number of guidelines and recommendations aimed at improving the quality of healthcare services for expectant mothers. The traditional ANC model was founded in 1900 on the premise that a pregnant woman’s chances of success increased with the number of hospitals visits she made. Regardless of their risk classification, they were expected to attend up to 14 prenatal consultations before delivery, with only pregnancy-related concerns being discussed at each visit, the antenatal care model prioritised hospital visits over quality care [6] but failed to lower maternal mortality rates because of execution challenges and excessive resource usage. The 2000s Focused Antenatal Care (FANC) model, linked to more perinatal deaths due to increased stillbirths, has faced criticism for not being comprehensive enough to address all health needs, especially in low-resource settings [7].

In 2016, the World Health Organization (WHO) increased the recommended number of antenatal care (ANC) visits from four to eight, aiming to reduce maternal mortality and morbidity [5]. This change has helped detect and prevent adverse pregnancy outcomes earlier, reinforcing ANC as a crucial part of healthcare strategies to lower mortality and morbidity among mothers and newborns. Sub-Saharan Africa continues to make sluggish progress despite these efforts, with 77% of stillbirths occurring in these low-income countries, highlighting ongoing challenges in improving maternal and neonatal health in the region [8]. At least eight ANC encounters are recommended by the WHO, with one occurring in the first trimester, two during the second, and five during the third trimester). The new model recommends pregnant women to have their first contact during the first 12 weeks’ gestation, with the following contacts taking place at 20, 26, 30, 34, 36, 38, and 40 weeks’ gestation [9].

Contact 1 (Up to 12 Weeks): Confirmation of pregnancy, baseline health assessment (weight, blood pressure), initial blood tests (blood type, Hb, infections), and ultrasound to confirm gestation, Contact 2 (20 Weeks): Detailed anomaly scan, blood pressure/weight check, fetal heart rate monitoring, and fetal movement assessment. Contact 3 (26 Weeks): Blood pressure check, weight, urine tests for infection, nutritional counseling, and checking for edema. Contact 4 (30 Weeks): Fetal growth assessment (fundal height), fetal heart check, maternal counseling on pregnancy danger signs. Contact 5 (34 Weeks): Review of blood pressure, fetal growth check, education on newborn care, and discussion of birth preparedness. Contact 6 (36 Weeks): Full physical examination, fetal heart rate, checking for fetal position, and review of birth plan. Contact 7 (38 Weeks): Blood pressure and weight check, fetal monitoring, and assessing for signs of labor. Contact 8 (40 Weeks): Assessment of fetal well-being, monitoring for signs of overdue pregnancy, and planning for induction if necessary [10].

Globally, 65% of expectant mothers receive at least eight antenatal care (ANC) visits, and approximately 48% do so in South Asia; however, in Africa, only 13.0% of women meet this recommended number of visits [11]. This stark disparity is alarming, especially since sub-Saharan Africa alone accounts for 66% of all maternal deaths worldwide, highlighting a critical gap in ANC access and its contribution to the region’s high maternal mortality rate [11]. Despite the rollout of the 2016 WHO ANC model, which recommends at least eight contacts to replace the old four-visit approach, maternal and perinatal outcomes in many low- and middle-income countries remain suboptimal [5]. During antenatal healthcare, providers must be alert to identify pregnant women at risk of complications. Successful implementation depends on resource availability, healthcare infrastructure, midwife training, staffing, and supplies [12].

Antenatal care providers are essential in delivering ANC services, and their perspectives, experiences, and challenges are key to understanding the facilitators and barriers of the eight-contact model. These providers are often the most accessible, qualified maternity care providers in remote areas; similarly, pregnant women are the recipients of this care. There is little research on the facilitators and barriers influencing the implementation of the eight-contact antenatal care model across all levels of healthcare. Furthermore, few studies have explored the perspectives of key stakeholders, the private health sector, antenatal care providers, and pregnant women on the eight-contact model through qualitative research in Osun State and Nigeria. Understanding their experiences in maternity care is vital for identifying and implementing improvements in the eight-contact model. This study aims to fill that knowledge gap. The findings are expected to help develop evidence-based recommendations for individuals, educators, and policymakers. Methods

This study was informed by a phenomenological perspective, with data analysed using reflexive thematic analysis for this exploratory qualitative study. Semi structured interviews and a focus group discussion guide were used to explore both the perspectives of antenatal care providers and pregnant women on the eight-contact ANC model as well as its facilitators and barriers. The consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research guidance were followed in the reporting of the study.

Research setting

The study was conducted in selected public and private healthcare facilities across Osun State, Nigeria. Osun State is located in the southwestern region of Nigeria, with an estimated population in 2013 of 4,274,858 people [13]. Osun State has three Senatorial districts, a total of 30 local government areas, most of which are predominantly rural. A representative sample of the state’s healthcare system is provided by the 736 public health facilities (678 primary, 54 secondary, and 4 tertiary facilities) and 359 private health facilities [14]. Eight hospitals were selected. The following facilities were selected purposively on the basis of the number of pregnant women who access ANC services, and the above facilities cut across every level of health (primary, secondary, tertiary, and private facilities).

Participants and sampling

One senatorial district (Osun East) in Osun state was purposively selected in Nigeria because there was no documentation on the implementation of the ANC model, and eight health facilities, which included tertiary, secondary, primary, and private health facilities (The two private health facilities included one secondary-level and one tertiary-level facility), were purposively selected. From the eight health facilities selected, antenatal care providers who are leaders of each unit were chosen for key informant interviews on the basis of their official positions and the roles they play in policy formulation and the delivery of maternal health services in the health facilities. Focus group discussions were conducted among pregnant women from the selected health facilities, with each group of 8 pregnant women, until data saturation was reached.

Data collection

A key informant interview guide was used to collect data from the leaders of ANC providers and focus group discussions (FGDs) among pregnant women. The interviews were conducted from December 2024 to July 2025. The key informant interviews were conducted in the English language and took place in the offices of the providers. The researcher scheduled an interview time and date with the participants at their convenience. The focus of the study and its benefits were explained to the participants. The participants were also informed about voice recording for analysis and encouraged not to mention their names or the name of their institution during the interview. The participant information sheet and consent form were completed. The participants were then invited to take semistructured interviews. All interviews were conducted by independent researchers (OT and KB). The OT is a nurse and midwife with approximately 5 years of experience who holds a BNSc in nursing. The topic guide was developed based on a review of relevant literature and expert input from a senior academic with doctoral training in nursing. The topic guide included a set of topics and questions but allowed participants to articulate their thoughts and experiences clearly, (it focuses on knowledge of the model, barriers/facilitators, experiences with ANC attendance). Notes were taken by the note taker during the interview. Following the completion of the interview, the participants were sent a debrief form. The interviews ceased once data saturation had been reached. All the interviews were audio recorded, transcribed and stored on a secure online drive. Identifiable information was removed to protect participants’ confidentiality. The average duration of the interviews was 30 minutes. Focus group discussions were conducted in English and Yoruba, and the same was recorded via a recorder. It was performed on clinic days.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from selected health facilities. Ethical clearance was obtained from the Obafemi Awolowo Teaching Hospital Complex with the following protocol number: ERC/2025/04/19 dated 23rd April, 2025; Seventh Day Adventist Hospital with the protocol number SERC-2024–11–0041 dated 6th January 2025; and the Osun State Ministry of Health dated 7th March 2025.

Trustworthiness of the study

The study ensured credibility by creating a relaxed, nonauthoritative interview environment, building rapport with participants, and explaining the study objectives in clear, simple language. Credibility was further enhanced by presenting participants’ direct quotes in the findings. Transferability was achieved by selecting a sample representative of the study population. Dependability was ensured through an audit trail documenting all stages of the study, including participant selection, data collection, reduction, interpretation, and presentation, allowing replication by other researchers.

Reflexivity was maintained through reflective diaries and critical self-awareness to minimize researcher bias. A pilot focus group discussion and interview were also conducted in one district for pretest interview clarity and duration; these data were excluded from the main analysis.

Facilitators for implementing the eight-contact antenatal care model

Subtheme 1: capacity building and educational Resources

Findings under this subtheme focused specifically on the capacity of healthcare providers to implement the WHO eight-contact ANC model. Providers reported limited formal training on the eight-contact guidelines, inadequate access to updated ANC protocols, and insufficient opportunities for continuing professional development. These gaps constrained providers’ ability to plan, schedule, and document ANC visits in accordance with the structured eight-contact model. These enabling factors were the most highly ranked by the ANC providers. Capacity building focused primarily on provider training and access to updated clinical guidelines. Several providers emphasized that regular training and refresher courses were essential for understanding and correctly applying the eight-contact ANC model.

Educational materials such as ANC registers and guideline handbooks were also viewed as supportive tools rather than determinants of policy implementation. The results highlight the importance of antenatal care provider-based training that improves maternal care through enabling one-to-one support and education to facilitate compliance with the eight-contact model. Antenatal care providers expressed that these personalized elements created a more tailored healthcare experience.Training healthcare workers on the latest guidelines in antenatal services will help in implementing any policy that is being introduced. Subtheme 2: health system strengthening

Discussion of findings

Theme 1: implementation status

The World Health Organization (WHO) introduced the eight-contact antenatal care (ANC) model in 2016 to improve maternal and foetal outcomes by increasing the frequency and quality of contact between pregnant women and healthcare providers. Nevertheless, the findings of this study, which was conducted across several health facilities in Osun State, Nigeria, reveal that the implementation of the model remains inadequate, which aligns with similar patterns observed in other low- and middle-income nations (LMICs).

Findings from the present study reveal that the majority of the antenatal care providers were not aware of the WHO eight-contact antenatal care model. Providers reported continued reliance on the traditional gestational age–based scheduling of antenatal visits, indicating a disconnect between knowledge of updated guidelines and routine service delivery. This finding is consistent with evidence from Ethiopia, where Mengistie [15] reported that only 20.9% of healthcare providers implemented the eight-contact antenatal care model, despite national adoption of the WHO guideline. The low level of implementation was attributed to factors such as organizational constraints, inadequate dissemination of policy directives, limited training opportunities, and entrenched clinical practices.

A noteworthy insight from this study is that providers who are aware of the model continue to use the traditional antenatal care model across all selected facilities, with most providers reporting that visit schedules were primarily determined by gestational age rather than the standardized eight-contact framework. Although some providers noted that pregnant women often attended antenatal care more than eight times during pregnancy, these visits were largely provider-directed and lacked structured scheduling in accordance with the WHO eight-contact model. The quality and purpose of each contact remain critical and are not addressed in ad hoc visit schedules. As stated by Mengistie [15], eight antenatal care contacts are still not being exploited to their full potential. It is essential to implement strategic interventions aimed at addressing the identified barriers if service use is to improve and comply with recommended criteria.

Many providers noted that their flexible, symptom-based appointment system highlights the reactive nature of antenatal care. The strategy may seem effective for handling large client numbers and appointment no-shows; providers’ partial awareness of the model was insufficient to drive structured adoption in the absence of institutional support, continuous training, and clear implementation frameworks, however, it contradicts the structured ANC’s purpose of delivering preventive and holistic care. Research findings demonstrated major deficiencies in provider education and understanding of the eight-contact ANC model. The study participants revealed their complete ignorance of the model or their limited knowledge about it. The WHO guidelines date back nearly a decade, which makes this situation worrisome. The findings of Ekholuenetale et al. [11] confirm the reported lack of awareness among providers who reported considerable variability in ANC contact coverage across countries, with very low uptake in countries such as Uganda, Zambia, and Senegal, where awareness and provider training have also been identified as major barriers. Even in contexts where providers had heard of the model, implementation was inconsistent and lacked a clear policy framework. The discrepancy between the dissemination of policies and actual practices at facilities highlights the necessity of robust training and supervision systems.

Importantly, this study explores pregnant women’s independent decision-making regarding antenatal attendance and eight-contact model and most of them quoted that they were not informed about eight-contact antenatal care and most of them are willing to use the model if they were informed; therefore, the observed lack of orderliness in visits should be interpreted as a reflection of provider-led scheduling practices and systemic limitations, rather than behavioral noncompliance by women. This study’s findings align with the widespread trends observed throughout sub-Saharan Africa. Demissie et al. [16] reported that only 9% of women in sub-Saharan Africa receive eight ANC visits. According to national statistics, only 17.4% of pregnant women in Nigeria receive eight or more ANC check-ups [11]. However, regional data reveal variation, with Osun state showing 80.2% compliance in some areas [17].

Theme 2: facilitators of the eight-contact model

Training healthcare professionals emerged as a crucial facilitator, in line with the findings of Khatri et al. [18], who highlighted the significance of training and communication skills in providing quality ANC. The current study emphasized how crucial it is for antenatal care providers to continue professional growth and receive up-to-date training that complies with ANC criteria. This implies a proactive approach to eight-contact model implementation and an understanding that more skilled healthcare professionals can provide more reliable and individualized treatment. Additionally, calendars, handbooks, and flyers were found to facilitate educational outreach and make it easier for patients and carers to follow the recommended ANC schedule. According to Owili et al. [19], the availability of structured information is associated with the provision of higher-quality care. These resources help improve comprehension and bridge communication gaps.

Increasing ANC uptake also requires a cost reduction. Reducing the financial strain on expectant mothers would encourage regular attendance, according to ANC Provider 1. This finding is in line with research by Atuhaire et al. [20], who reported that women who earned more money were more likely to attend ANC visits. Therefore, cost reductions could encourage equitable maternal health outcomes and democratize access. The results show that the transition to the eight-contact model is greatly aided by policymakers. Healthcare professionals in private facilities, in particular, stressed the necessity of institutional approval and strategic guidance from higher authorities.

Health system strengthening emerged as another key facilitator, particularly in relation to staffing adequacy and financial support. Providers highlighted how staff shortages, high workload, and workforce attrition negatively affected their ability to schedule and deliver structured antenatal care contacts. In contrast, adequate staffing and funding were perceived as enabling factors that would allow providers to dedicate sufficient time to patient education, appointment planning, and continuity of care.These findings are consistent with earlier studies demonstrating that better-resourced health facilities are more likely to deliver higher-quality antenatal care services [18, 19]. Workforce stability and provider satisfaction have also been linked to improved patient experiences and greater adherence to clinical guidelines. The present study therefore reinforces the importance of system-level investments to support the operational demands of the eight-contact model.

Teaching expectant mothers about the benefits and structure of the eight-contact model was one significant facilitator that was frequently brought up. This is in line with studies by Alibhai et al. [21] and Wulandari and Laksono [22], who demonstrated a favourable correlation between maternal education and ANC, early booking, and more visits. The results of this study show that adherence to planned antenatal care appointments is facilitated by first-time pregnancy. Regular clinic attendance was driven by participants who were pregnant for the first time describing increased anxiety about pregnancy outcomes, heightened concern for fetal well-being, and a strong desire for reassurance.

This study identified time-conscious service delivery and reduced waiting times as important facilitators for implementing the eight-contact antenatal care (ANC) model, particularly among working pregnant women. Antenatal care providers reported deliberately organizing clinic activities to ensure timely consultations, allowing women to return to their workplaces promptly and minimizing the opportunity costs associated with repeated clinic visits. This finding aligns with Andiso et al. [23], who reported that women who waited more than 30 minutes at health facilities were five times more likely to discontinue antenatal care compared to those who experienced shorter waiting times. The consistency between these findings suggests that prolonged waiting times constitute a critical deterrent to sustained ANC attendance, especially in settings where women balance employment, schooling, and household responsibilities. Therefore, improving clinic efficiency and reducing waiting times may enhance adherence to scheduled visits and support successful implementation of the eight-contact ANC model.

Women stressed that going to antenatal clinics gave them the chance to learn about health issues, monitor their own and their unborn child’s health, and lessen their anxieties about getting pregnant. This aligns with the findings of Wulandari and Laksono [22], who found that in both nations under study, primiparous women had a considerably higher likelihood of attending at least four prenatal care appointments. This tendency, according to the authors, is a result of first-time pregnant women’s increased caution and inexperience, which makes them more dependent on structured maternal health services and expert advice. Women in FGD 1 and 4 in the current study also stated that regular attendance at antenatal clinics was encouraged by worry, a lack of knowledge about pregnancy, and concerns about laboratory results such as packed cell volume (PCV).

Theme 3: barriers to implementation of the eight-contact model

This study identified late initiation of antenatal care and poor adherence to scheduled visits as major barriers to achieving the WHO-recommended eight-contact antenatal care (ANC) model. Providers consistently reported that many pregnant women delay booking until the second or third trimester, thereby limiting the opportunity to complete the recommended number of contacts. This finding is consistent with evidence from low- and middle-income countries, where early booking has been shown to be a strong predictor of completing eight or more ANC contacts. Tegegne et al. [24] demonstrated that early initiation of ANC significantly increases the likelihood of achieving eight or more visits. Similarly, Ekholuenetale et al. [11] reported a 97% reduction in the likelihood of completing eight or more ANC contacts among women who booked after the first trimester in Benin. These findings support providers’ observations in the current study that late booking substantially undermines adherence to the eight-contact model.

Socioeconomic and logistical constraints further compounded challenges in visit adherence. Providers reported that school commitments, income-generating activities, transportation costs, and long travel distances discouraged regular attendance. These findings are consistent with studies across multiple settings that have identified maternal education, occupation, place of residence, socioeconomic status, and distance to health facilities as key determinants of ANC utilization [1, 25]. In Nigeria specifically, nearly half of pregnant women were reported not to meet the eight-contact ANC recommendation, with distance to health facilities and household characteristics cited as significant barriers [25]. These structural and economic barriers highlight that adherence challenges extend beyond individual behavior and are deeply rooted in broader social determinants of health.

A recurring theme among healthcare providers was the socio-economic issues affecting pregnant women, which significantly impacted ANC compliance. Challenges in adherence to scheduled visits was mentioned by Providers 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5. Some women were reported to miss scheduled visits due to competing school commitments, market activities, or limited perceived need for care in the absence of symptoms. These results support previous studies [22, 26], highlighting the significance of social context and personal motivation in health-seeking behaviour. ANC compliance is closely linked to women’s educational attainment, and equality. Women who have more education and financial resources are likely to adhere to the eight ANC contacts, which is in line with research from Ethiopia, Jordan, and Nigeria [11, 24].

Understaffing is a significant institutional barrier that leads to higher workloads and lower-quality care. According to Providers 5 and 6, the fact that entire facilities have only a small number of nurses is indicative of a larger human resource crisis in LMIC healthcare systems. Poor pay, a lack of professional motivation, and health worker migration all contribute to high turnover and instability, which further exacerbates this constraint [18, 27].

Another major barrier to providing high-quality, evidence-based ANC was the absence of recent training and mentorship. To effectively implement the 2016 WHO ANC guidelines, providers acknowledge that they have not participated in refresher training for more than ten years [28, 29]. The absence of educational materials and post-training support further undermines program sustainability

The present study also revealed that health staff did not receive enough training or updates. Many providers lack ANC guideline training or refresher courses. Clear policies directing ANC schedules were lacking because of the lack of standardized procedures or supervision facilities. Missed appointments and late bookings were mentioned as client-related issues. Complete implementation is hampered by heavy patient loads, staffing shortages, and poor facility-level coordination. These challenges align with international research, including that published by Hlongwane et al. [30], which highlighted that systemic support, such as respectful maternity care, intimate partner violence screening, and proper staffing, is necessary for the model to be implemented successfully.

Our analysis indicates that limited dissemination of the WHO eight-contact antenatal care guideline and weak institutional awareness constrained its implementation across facilities. Providers’ accounts suggest that policy directives related to the eight-contact model were not consistently communicated. This gap in policy dissemination restricted providers’ awareness of updated guidelines and hindered structured adoption of the model. This result is in line with the global literature that emphasizes the importance of informed leadership, system-wide coordination, and supportive governance in improving maternal health interventions [28]. Involving healthcare providers in the policy dissemination process and providing facilities with authority and resources to operationalize new guidelines are essential for effective policy translation from theory to practice.

Theme 4: opinion of pregnant women

This study explored pregnant women’s perceptions of the WHO eight-contact antenatal care (ANC) model, focusing on perceived benefits, low awareness, and factors affecting ANC attendance. These findings align with previous research and are supported by it. A persistent lack of knowledge about the WHO eight-contact model was evident in every focus group discussion. Women’s responses, such as “I have no idea” and “Not aware that such a model exists”, revealed a critical information gap. This finding matches the findings of Ali et al. [9], who noted that ANC attendance is heavily influenced by knowledge about the number of antenatal contact they are expected to make. Women are less likely to follow the minimum eight visits if they do not understand the recommendation fully. This highlights the urgent need for community-based health education and stronger communication strategies by healthcare professionals. Interestingly, women showed positive attitudes after learning about the eight-contact model.

They recognized benefits such as monitoring maternal and fetal health, identifying issues early, and promoting overall well-being: “It will enable us to check on our baby’s welfare” and “Anything can go wrong during pregnancy.” These views indicate that once the benefits are understood, women are more open to ANC. These findings support the findings of Rahman et al. [31] and Ali et al. [9] that increasing health literacy can improve ANC compliance. The demonstrated willingness suggests that effective awareness campaigns could increase uptake, even among initially unaware women.

Several systemic and socioeconomic obstacles were identified: financial restraints. According to Jitu et al. [32], women prioritize daily necessities such as food and housing over clinic visits. “Things are so expensive … if the government can reduce the cost of delivery materials, it will be appreciated,” stated one respondent. Financial strain remains a significant barrier, especially for women from low-income families.

Many mentioned lengthy wait times and full-day clinic visits: “Once you arrive at the clinic, you are required to spend an entire day there.” This finding supports Andiso et al. [23], who reported that women waiting more than 30 minutes are five times more likely to drop out of ANC care. Similarly, Tsegaye et al. [33] reported that long waiting times negatively affect ANC compliance.

Negative staff behavior was a significant deterrent. “She treated me in an impolite manner.” Mlambo & Ibeziako [34] noted similar things, stressing that women’s willingness to attend ANC is strongly influenced by the attitudes of healthcare professionals. Unhappiness and mistrust were exacerbated by the lack of qualified staff and the use of student nurses. The problem was made worse by infrastructure problems, which made it less likely that the eight-contact schedule would be completed.

Despite these obstacles, a few facilitators were frequently mentioned: Concern for the baby was the most common reason for attendance: “I come mainly for my baby’s wellbeing.” “We learn new things” was the value placed on information sessions during ANC visits. Women were encouraged to return by positive service experiences: “The way the staff here attends to me makes me come.” Women agreed that regular monitoring was essential for the early detection of complications. These findings support those of Dinagde et al. [35], who linked ANC dropout rates to provider behaviour and dissatisfaction with health education.

Interestingly, Abera et al. [36] reported that women who were satisfied with their prior care were more likely to discontinue, suggesting that a positive perception of one’s health may reduce the need for follow-up. Contrary to the current findings, which suggest that patient behaviour may vary depending on context or culture, satisfaction seemed to promote follow-up visits.

Summary of findings

This study examined the implementation of the WHO-recommended eight-contact ANC model in selected public and private healthcare facilities in Osun State, Nigeria. Despite the WHO’s 2016 guidelines aimed at enhancing maternal and fetal health through structured and frequent contact, the findings revealed limited adherence. Most facilities still follow traditional models, scheduling visits on the basis of symptoms rather than proactive planning. Many providers were unaware of or untrained in the eight-contact model, resulting in inconsistent application. Likewise, pregnant women showed limited awareness, although they responded positively when educated about its benefits.

Barriers to full implementation included a lack of training, poor infrastructure, financial constraints, negative staff attitudes, long wait times, and poor transport access. However, the key facilitators identified were client motivation driven by fetal health concerns, positive ANC experiences, and the use of educational materials and structured health talks.

Conclusion

This study concludes that Osun State has not effectively implemented the eight-contact ANC model due to systemic challenges such as insufficient provider training, weak institutional support, and socioeconomic limitations among clients. Although women value ANC services, gaps in awareness, structural constraints, and negative care experiences hinder their full utilization. A disconnect remains between WHO guidelines and real-world clinical practice in these settings.

Limitations of the study

The research focused on selected health care facilities in Osun State, which may not represent the whole state or other states in Nigeria; however, the study carefully selected health facilities to ensure good representation.While the qualitative approach offered rich insights, there were no quantitative outcome measures incorporated, such as a proportionate increase in ANC compliance over time, which might restrict capturing the true effectiveness of the implementations.

Implications for nursing

Policy: This study emphasizes the need for national nursing policies that incorporate the eight-contact ANC model into the framework, alongside resource allocation for training and SOP development.

Practice: Nurses and midwives must shift from traditional care to a structured, quality-driven ANC model, ensuring documentation and respectful, culturally sensitive care.

Research: Longitudinal and interdisciplinary nursing studies are needed to evaluate how changes in training, policy, or financial incentives impact ANC adherence and outcomes.

Education: Nursing curricula should be revised to reflect current WHO ANC guidelines, with an emphasis on clinical simulations, systems thinking, and public health leadership.
