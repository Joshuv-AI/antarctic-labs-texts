Dyssynergia phenotypes in obstructed defecation: insights from Fecobionics assessment
Authors: Wenbo Li, Mingyang Hou, Yi Li, Houde Yu, Yuqin He, Haibin Yao, Shili Xiao, Kaijun Liu, Xin Fu, Liangzhi Wen, Zhiyong Huang, Min Yang

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveObstructed defecation syndrome (ODS) is a chronic constipation subtype. Conventional tests are limited by non-physiological conditions and poor correlation with symptoms. The findings support the use of Fecobionics as a comprehensive diagnostic tool to characterise dyssynergia subtypes, which could guide individualised treatment strategies such as targeted biofeedback or surgical intervention. Introduction

Chronic constipation (CC) is a common anorectal disorder affecting up to 25% of adults, increasing to 33% in those over 60 years.1 CC impairs daily life, leading to absenteeism from work or study in 12% of patients and reduced work capacity in up to 60%.2 3 Despite substantial healthcare resources allocated to its management, many patients with anorectal functional disorders fail to achieve satisfactory outcomes. Obstructed defecation syndrome (ODS) is a major subtype of CC, clinically characterised by excessive straining, incomplete evacuation and the need for manual assistance. Its pathophysiology is heterogeneous, involving impaired anorectal coordination (dyssynergia) or structural abnormalities such as rectocele and rectal prolapse, which frequently coexist and complicate diagnosis and treatment.

The anal sphincter, rectum and surrounding pelvic floor muscle tissue are a complete functional unit. The tissue’s perception and coordinated movement are key to maintaining normal anorectal physiological function. The anorectal function and structure affect the steady-state balance of the evacuation function that has typical mechanical characteristics of a system that changes its motion state and deformation. The effective propulsion of anorectal contents does not only come from local pressure. It is completed by the coordinated efforts of the pelvic floor muscles and affected by compliance and friction.4 To capture the dynamics comprehensively, tests in a natural posture with stool-like properties are required. However, current tests are mostly limited to collecting and analysing single or limited characteristic parameters in unnatural conditions. Anorectal manometry (ARM), balloon expulsion test (BET) and defecography are widely used for evaluating anorectal function; however, testing conditions and measurement constraints may not fully reflect natural defecation. The diagnostic tests are limited by poor reproducibility, inconsistent correlation with symptoms and lack of validity.5,8 In contrast, Fecobionics is a bionic probe designed as Bristol stool type III–IV, allowing defecation testing in a seated posture while capturing rectal propulsive forces, anal resistance, sensation and evacuation dynamics. Previous studies have demonstrated its feasibility, safety and superior correlation with clinical symptoms compared with ARM or BET and even predict therapeutic response to biofeedback.9,12 Nevertheless, systematic studies characterising dyssynergia phenotypes in patients with ODS are still limited.

The aim of this study was to investigate anorectal dynamics in patients with ODS using Fecobionics in a seated posture compared with control subjects (CS). Preload–afterload diagrams were used to identify distinct dyssynergia phenotypes of paradoxical puborectalis contraction. We aimed to provide novel pathophysiological insights and potential clinical implications for the classification and management of ODS.

Methods

Study design and participants

The study was designed as a prospective observational study, conducted at Army Medical Centre (Daping Hospital), Army Medical University. Data collection spanned from September 2022 to July 2025. According to the registered study protocol, the planned sample size was 30 patients with ODS and 15 CS. Owing to recruitment challenges and strict inclusion criteria, 25 patients with ODS and 10 CS were ultimately enrolled. All measurements were performed according to a predefined protocol.

The Rome IV criteria for chronic constipation were used. ODS is characterised primarily by failure of pelvic floor and anal muscles to coordinate properly during attempted defecation, leading to symptoms such as difficulty evacuating stool, excessive straining, sensation of obstruction, incomplete evacuation and prolonged toileting, which distinguishes it from motility disorders of the colon, for example, slow colon transit constipation. Hence, patients with ODS symptoms were included.

All patients with ODS had defecography data (SHIMADZU, SON-IALVISON SAFIRE17, Japan). ARM was performed in accordance with the London Protocol using a high-resolution water-perfused system (Medical Measurement Systems bv, Solar GI, Netherlands). Rome IV symptom profiles were confirmed with abnormal ARM and defecography findings.

Abnormal ARM patterns were defined as paradoxical contraction, <20% relaxation of the anal sphincter during push manoeuvres, a negative anorectal pressure gradient or inadequate propulsive forces (rectal pressure <45 mmHg (≈61 cmH2O) during push manoeuvres). None of the patients used any medication for constipation at least 4 weeks before treatment.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: tumours, inflammatory, endocrine/metabolic disorders and drug-induced bowel dysfunction or opioid-induced constipation. Furthermore, patients with constipation resulting from organic diseases, as well as those with severe primary diseases (eg, severe cardiovascular, hepatic, renal, respiratory or end-stage organic diseases) or infectious, oncological or psychiatric disorders, were excluded, and individuals with metal or silicone allergies or severe phobias were excluded from participation (online supplemental figure 1).

CS were recruited through advertisements on social media and in publicly accessible areas. ARM was performed in CS to allow comparison of manometric findings between groups. Defecography was not performed in CS for ethical and practical reasons. CS did not exhibit any constipation-related symptoms according to comprehensive assessments and reported no significant gastrointestinal symptoms (eg, abdominal pain, diarrhoea and constipation) within the 4 weeks before enrolment. Furthermore, CS did not take any medications affecting gastrointestinal motility and had no diagnosed psychiatric or cognitive disorders.

The Wexner constipation scoring system,13 Patient Assessment of Constipation-Symptom Severity Index (PAC-SYM),14 Bristol Stool Form Scale15 and Patient Assessment of Constipation-Quality of Life (PAC-QOL)16 were used to evaluate constipation symptoms and their impact on the quality of life. Both groups were capable of understanding and completing these evaluations. The experiments were conducted in accordance with internationally accepted principles and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki as revised in 2000.

The study was registered at www.chictr.org.cn with Identifier: ChiCTR2300078807. Reporting of this study follows the STARD (Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy Studies) 2015 guidelines, with the completed checklist provided as online supplemental material.

Fecobionics and study procedures

The 10-mm outer diameter (OD), 10-cm long Fecobionics device was utilised for the defecation test. The probe was constructed from Shore A5 medical-grade silicone, mimicking the consistency and shape of normal stools, classified as type 3–4 on the Bristol Stool Form Scale.17 The balloon was 9 cm long, made of 30-µm thick polyester-urethane and spanned most of the core length. This cylindrical balloon has a maximum diameter of 4.5 cm when unstretched and can contain up to 120 mL of saline. It was connected via a thin tube extending from the front of the probe to a syringe containing saline. Additionally, a thin cable extended from the front for data recording and power supply. Enhanced graphical user interface and further processing were conducted using MATLAB (R2019a, The MathWorks, USA) (online supplemental figure 2).

The experiments were conducted in a private room within the gastroenterology ward. All participants were instructed to naturally empty their rectum as much as possible, without the use of an enema 2 h prior to the test. The participants were positioned in the left lateral position with their hips and knees bent. A digital rectal examination was performed before probe insertion to evaluate anal pressure, the length of the anal canal and the distal rectum, including the presence of faeces, as well as to facilitate relaxation of the anal sphincter. Subsequently, the probe was manually inserted into the rectum at approximately 1–2 cm proximal to the inner margin of the anal canal. The participants were then asked to cough, squeeze and push to confirm correct placement and report any discomfort. The probe relocates more distally during these procedures.3 11 Subsequently, they were instructed to transfer from the bed to the commode chair to perform the defecation test in a seated position.12 18

The protocol for the subsequent procedure included a 3-min rest period, two cough manoeuvres to confirm probe placement, two short squeezes (5–10 s each), one long squeeze (30 s) and two pushes (5–10 s each). The balloon was subsequently inflated manually at a rate of 60 mL/min with warm saline (37°C) until the urge to defecate sensation was reported, or up to a maximum of 80 mL. To ensure privacy, curtains or dividers were used, and the investigators left the room during the defecation attempt. If evacuation was not completed within 120 s, the investigator removed the probe manually.12 18 The entire experiment lasts approximately 15–20 min, which includes effective communication with the participants before the examination, explaining the precautions and obtaining their signed informed consent.

Data analysis

Multiple parameters related to demographics, lifestyle, questionnaire scores, digital rectal examination and simulation defecation were calculated. In the Fecobionics test, the rear pressure (P_oral) primarily reflected the longitudinal thrust of the rectum and served as an indicator of the expulsion force. The front channel (P_aboral) represented the relaxation and contraction of the anal sphincter and quantifies the anal pressure opposing the rectal propulsion. The balloon pressure (P_balloon) reflected intrarectal pressure. All pressure recordings were continuous. Mean, extreme and differential values were derived during rest, cough, squeeze, push and defecation. The P_delta, which represented the difference between P_oral and P_aboral, indicated the effective anorectal propulsive force.19 For sensation, the first sensation, desire-to-defecate sensation and urge sensation were recorded during balloon filling, along with detailed volume–time data. The recto-anal inhibitory reflex (RAIR) was determined from real-time pressure curves. To demonstrate the expulsion dynamic characteristics, the paradoxical contraction rate and average effective propulsive force (Avg P_delta) throughout the evacuation were calculated. Furthermore, expulsion dynamics were further analysed using preload–afterload diagrams, also now referred to as the Gregersen Nomogram for Anorectal Disorders (G-NOM), which depicts the relationship between propulsive and outlet pressures during defecation.4 12 18 This diagram presents the P_aboral plotted as a function of P_oral. The defecatory contraction resulted in an upward vertical line during the isometric contraction phase. Based on the similar paradoxical and ineffective contractions identified in the diagrams, a preliminary analysis of ODS subgroups was conducted.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted with SPSS V.22.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York, USA). Continuous variables were expressed either as mean±SEM or as medians with quartiles, depending on data distribution. Differences between the ODS and CS were analysed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Spearman correlation analysis assessed the association between constipation symptom scores and anorectal functional parameters. Data distributions were illustrated using box–whisker plots to show medians, quartiles, ranges and outliers. A significant difference was defined as p<0.05.

Results

35 individuals residing in the southwestern region of China were included in the studies, in which 25 had ODS (15 females and 10 males) and 10 were CS (4 females and 6 males). Online supplemental table 1 shows the demographic characteristics and clinical features of the two groups. No difference between the CS and ODS groups was found for age, height, weight and body mass index (p>0.2). The Wexner score was 3.0±1.0 in the CS, and 70% had score 0–3. Patients with ODS exhibited significant symptoms. The average duration of constipation was 7.8±6.5 years. The Wexner (18.0±0.5), PAC-SYM (27.4±0.9) and PAC-QOL score (63.6±3.0) were significantly higher in the ODS group than in CS (p<0.05). Stool consistency differed significantly between groups, with the majority of CS reporting type III–IV stools (90%), whereas the ODS group had type I–II stools (100%). Further physiological testing showed abnormal push procedure (70%), rectal hyposensitivity (36%) and lack of RAIR (36%) in ODS, with overlapping findings in several patients. Notably, 50% of CS also exhibited abnormal ARM findings. Defecography demonstrated two predominant abnormalities among patients with ODS, that is, paradoxical puborectalis contraction (PPC) (40%) and rectal prolapse or rectocele (56%) in patients with ODS. One had mid-perineal descent. For subsequent analyses, patients were categorised according to the predominant defecographic findings. Furthermore, defecography showed that the anorectal angle was 106° (100°–113°) and 122° (99°–130°) before and during evacuation, respectively. Impaired rectal emptying was found in the majority of patients with ODS, with an emptying rate of 49.9% (25.6%–66.8%).

Baseline anorectal function

Fecobionics

All Fecobionics studies lasted 15–20 min from device insertion to expulsion. Adverse events were not reported. None of the participants experienced pain, discomfort or rectal bleeding during or after these experiments. Most participants reached a stable resting anorectal pressure within 60–120 s after transitioning to the commode chair, whereas two CS needed a longer adaptation period before resting pressures returned to baseline. The median aboral resting pressure values were 52.5 (quartiles 45.1–62.8) and 32.9 (29.8–43.7) cmH2O in CS and patients with ODS, respectively, with a significant group difference (p<0.01).

The aboral pressure increased instantly during squeezes and coughing manoeuvre. All participants were able to contract the anal sphincter under voluntary effort. Coughing induced a sharp and reproducible rise in all pressure channels exceeding 60–80 cmH2O. The median maximum aboral squeeze pressure was 90.6 cmH2O (83.4–105.6) in CS and 71.1 cmH2O (54.1–82.4) in patients with ODS (p=0.08). The median long squeeze contract durations were 24.5 s (20.3–29.0) and 14 s (7–29) in the CS and ODS groups, respectively.

During balloon inflation, the Fecobionics pressure sometimes exhibited variable fluctuations at the beginning, which was followed by more stable pressures. The elicitation of RAIR was characterised by a transient fall in aboral pressure followed by a return to the baseline. RAIR was not elicited during the balloon inflation of Fecobionics in nine patients with ODS. Ten CS and 16 patients with ODS had RAIR. The median desire-to-defecate volume was 55 (43.8–71.5) mL in CS and 55 (45.0–80.0) mL in patients with ODS, with no significant group differences. One CS and seven patients with ODS reached 80 mL, before reporting desire-to-defecate or without any desire at 80 mL.

Anorectal manometry

Anal resting pressures measured by ARM were overall higher than those recorded by Fecobionics, with medians of 107.4 cmH2O (86.6–118.2) in CS and 65.2 cmH2O (32.6–97.8) in patients with ODS (p<0.03) (figure 1A). During voluntary squeeze manoeuvres, the median maximum anal squeeze pressure was higher in ARM recordings than in Fecobionics (p<0.01) with 203.2 cmH2O (185.5–219.5) in CS and 144.1 cmH2O (122.3–229.7) in patients with ODS (p=0.13 between groups) (figure 1A). The median long squeeze contraction durations were 9.3 s (7.5–12.8) in CS and 5.1 s (4.6–13.1) in patients with ODS (figure 1B), with no significant group differences. However, 80% of CS were unable to sustain contraction >13 s in ARM, whereas only 20% failed to do so in Fecobionics. The remaining participants sustained contractions >20 s. With ARM, RAIR was absent in the same nine patients with ODS in whom it was not elicited during Fecobionics testing. Desire-to-defecate volumes measured by ARM did not differ significantly between CS and patients with ODS (53 (48.5–60.5) vs 53 mL (43.0–76.0); p>0.05).

Dyssynergia phenotypes in defecation

14 patients with ODS (56%) were unable to evacuate the Fecobionics probe within 60 s, including eight patients with ODS who failed to evacuate within 120 s. Two CS could not evacuate within 60 s (these participants had Wexner scores close to the abnormality limit). The remaining CS (80%) completed expulsion in ≤35 s. The median expulsion time of the two groups is presented in figure 2A (participants who did not evacuate within 120 s were assigned an expulsion time of 120s). Significantly prolonged expulsion time was found in patients with ODS (76 (23–120) s) compared with CS (18.7 (10.6–34.0) s, p<0.01). During expulsion, no significant difference was found between CS and patients with ODS in the maximum rectal propulsive pressure (P_oral). Consistently, the recto-anal pressure gradient on ARM during push also did not differ significantly between the two groups. In contrast, the mean effective anorectal propulsive pressure (P_delta) in Fecobionics experiments was higher in CS (48.2 cmH₂O (32.5–88.0)) than in patients with ODS (26.2 cmH₂O (8.6–35.9)), with statistically significant group difference (p<0.01, figure 2B). Patients with ODS (7 (4–14) contractions) used more contractions to expel Fecobionics than CS did (3 (1–4), p<0.01, figure 2C). The paradoxical contraction rates were 71.4% (50–100) vs 0% (0–44; p<0.01, figure 2D) in patients with ODS and CS, respectively. Anal relaxation rates during attempted expulsion with ARM were lower in patients with ODS (16% (12–37)) than in CS (36% (27–44), p<0.03). Furthermore, correlation analyses showed that symptom severity (PAC-SYM, PAC-QOL and Wexner scores) correlated positively with Fecobionics expulsion duration, contraction number and paradoxical contraction rate and negatively with the average propulsive force (P_delta) and anal relaxation rates (online supplemental table 2).

A preload–afterload diagram was used to present the pressure tracings during Fecobionics expulsion. Most CS exhibited similar pressure tracings as tracings described in the previous reports.12 20 Three major dyssynergia phenotypes were identified in patients with ODS: (1) multiple simultaneous contractions, (2) insufficient or delayed anal relaxation and (3) persistently elevated outlet pressure. According to defecography, these phenotypes showed distinct preload–afterload tracings between patients with PPC (figure 3) and with rectal prolapse or rectocele (figure 4). Significant differences were not found on demographic characteristics between these two subgroups.

In summary, PPC cases often showed high-amplitude repetitive activity and prolonged paradoxical contractions, whereas prolapse/rectocele cases were characterised by lower pressure amplitudes (maximum anorectal pressure <100 cmH2O in most cases), unstable tracings and delayed anal relaxation. Furthermore, patients with multiple simultaneous contractions in both subgroups consistently failed to expel the probe within the normal time frame (online supplemental table 3).

Discussion

In this study, the bionics anorectal testing Fecobionics device was used to evaluate anorectal dynamics in patients with ODS, with particular attention on dyssynergia phenotypes on paradoxical puborectalis contraction and anal prolapse/rectocele. The results demonstrated abnormal metrics in patients with ODS compared with CS, including impaired expulsion performance, reduced propulsive forces and altered anorectal coordination, which were associated with symptom severity. These findings provide novel insights into the mechanisms underlying dyssynergia and the pathophysiology of ODS.

Pathophysiological insights from Fecobionics

Fecobionics is a smart tool integrating several current tests, enabling the recording of natural defecation in sitting position. The differences in testing position and displacement of pressure measurement may explain the discrepancies between ARM and Fecobionics. Several previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility and repeatability of Fecobionics testing.12 In this study, prolonged expulsion duration, reduced effective propulsive pressure, increased number of contractions required for evacuation and a higher rate of abnormal contraction patterns indicate the complex and multifactorial pathophysiology of ODS.18

Reduced effective propulsive forces (P_delta) are a key feature. Mechanistically, propulsion impairment may be caused by inadequate generation of abdominal pressure or altered rectal compliance. However, patients with ODS often do persistent straining during evacuation attempts. Long-term abnormal drive can desensitise the rectum and change pelvic floor muscle recruitment patterns.5 Outlet dysfunction is equally important. Insufficient anal relaxation and paradoxical pressure patterns during expulsion further reveal underlying dysfunction. These abnormalities reflect disturbances in neuromuscular coordination. Furthermore, behavioural and learnt factors have been suggested as a cause of dyssynergia.21 According to the results, almost all the failed expulsion cases within 120 s (n=8) were characterised by multiple simultaneous contractions. Half of them had PPC, who can generate high anorectal pressure, even higher than CS. Hence, it is clear that dyssynergia played a pivotal role in patients with ODS.

Previous studies have shown that conventional ARM yields a high rate of dyssynergia patterns in CS, with low diagnostic specificity.22,24 In contrast, bionics assessment such as Fecobionics measures pressure and coordination during simulated natural evacuation, providing more comprehensive assessment of effective propulsion and abnormal expulsion patterns. Furthermore, correlated metrics with clinical symptom scores captured in this study underscore the potential clinical relevance as objective markers for disease burden and treatment in ODS.

Characterisation of dyssynergia phenotypes

ODS presents with variable patterns of anorectal dysfunction. Many clinical and physiological studies have shown that ODS encompasses diverse disturbances of anorectal coordination and rectal function. A considerable number of CS show paradoxical contractions, impaired relaxation, but without any symptoms of obstructed defecation.25 26 A recent study combining 3D high-resolution ARM with study defecography and balloon expulsion test (BET) showed that not all patients who meet manometric criteria for dyssynergia show abnormal findings in other functional tests.27 Moreover, the cut-offs used to define ‘abnormal’ vary across studies, and factors such as patient demographics, test posture and equipment further affect results.7 These findings suggest that while dyssynergia plays a key role in many patients with ODS, reliance on single manometric metrics or subtype defined solely by attempted defecation risks misclassification. A previous Fecobionics study in patients with ODS reported a broader spectrum of phenotypes in a larger cohort.12 Differences from our findings are likely to reflect variations in patient composition and phenotype analytical focus. In the present work, we deliberately aimed for a clinically interpretable classification by linking expulsion-based phenotypes to defecography-defined PPC versus prolapse/rectocele subgroups in an independent cohort.

Two subgroups with clear defecography characteristics were found in this study. The preload–afterload diagram was used in the subtype analysis.4 Three typical dyssynergia subtypes were revealed. The preload–afterload loops of these subtypes showed distinct differences in the PPC and prolapse/rectocele subgroups, and subtypes (1) and (2) were more common. In PPC, patients exhibited high-amplitude and repetitive contraction patterns leading to failed or delayed expulsion, despite generating anorectal pressures comparable to or exceeding normal. It detailed the previous findings that patients with PPC had higher resting anal pressures, more negative anorectal pressure differentials and a greater rate of prolonged BET failures.28 Symptom severity may be due to increased puborectalis muscle thickness and ultrastructural changes.29 Although rectal prolapse and rectocele are anatomically distinct conditions, they were presented together in this analysis due to the similar preload–afterload patterns and expulsion dynamics in this cohort, reflecting a shared functional phenotype of structural outlet obstruction. The structural deformity may impair the transmission of propulsive forces, and insufficient relaxation or abnormal contraction may further compromise defecatory efficiency in both conditions.

The distinctions of dyssynergia phenotypes have therapeutic implications. Patients with coordination disorders may benefit most from biofeedback and behavioural retraining.30 Those with structural abnormalities should have further imaging and follow-up to assess severity. Conservative management is appropriate in many cases; surgery can be considered for persistent and symptomatic defects.31

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small and derived from a single centre. Notably, the planned sample size specified in the study protocol was not fully achieved, primarily due to recruitment challenges and strict inclusion criteria. The characteristics of the study population, including the modest sex imbalance between patients with ODS and CS, may limit the generalisability of the findings. Furthermore, standard balloon expulsion testing was not performed. However, the expulsion performance captured by Fecobionics provides comparable information. We recruited patients with symptoms indicating ODS rather than slow colon transit constipation. However, overlap exists between constipation subtypes, and we cannot exclude that some had mixed slow colon transit constipation and ODS. Finally, potential influences related to device characteristics or procedural factors cannot be entirely excluded, that is, the probe mimics type III–IV stool, whereas patients with ODS more often experience stool types I–II; hence, a stiffer device may have shown even more abnormality.

Conclusion

This study reveals distinct dyssynergia phenotypes in obstructed defecation using Fecobionics. Patients with ODS showed prolonged expulsion, reduced propulsive forces and frequent paradoxical contractions. Several parameters correlated with symptom severity. Preload–afterload (G-NOM) analysis differentiated phenotypes: paradoxical puborectalis contraction featured repetitive high-amplitude, prolonged paradoxical activity, whereas prolapse/rectocele showed lower pressures, unstable trajectories and delayed relaxation. These findings highlight the heterogeneous, multifactorial nature of dyssynergia. This is of clinical interest since the various subtypes need different types of therapy. Future multicentre studies integrating Fecobionics with imaging or neuromuscular assessments may refine classification and link distinct patterns to symptoms and treatment outcomes.
