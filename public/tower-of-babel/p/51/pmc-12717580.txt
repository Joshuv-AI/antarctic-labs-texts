Incorporating Implementation Science Into Frailty Interventions for Cardiovascular Disease
Authors: Kang Hui Teow, Jie Jun Wong, Yang Zhao, Laureen YT. Wang, Silvana Choo, Angela S. Koh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Frailty is common among patients with cardiovascular diseases and is linked to increased risk of adverse outcomes. This article emphasizes the importance of implementation science in tackling frailty, with a special focus on the inclusion of implementation research into cardiovascular trials to improve the uptake of frailty interventions and their eventual integration into routine practice. Through analyzing the barriers and facilitators of implementation, this discussion will explore how conceptual frameworks can guide implementation efforts and provide suggestions for trial designs that facilitate long-term implementation.

Frailty syndrome can be described as a state of heightened vulnerability to acute stressors, arising from age-related changes across multiple organ systems.1 A 2019 study found that the prevalence of frailty among adults aged 65 to 79 years was 5.4%, with a higher incidence of 13% in adults aged 80 years and above.2 Among patients with cardiovascular disease (CVD), between 10% and 60% have frailty syndrome.1 Physiological aging may be accelerated by the presence of CVD and increased risk of multimorbidity. This occurrence impacts other domains such as cognition and can lead to adverse outcomes of increased fall risk, fractures, delirium, disabilities, institutionalization, reduced quality of life, disability, and death.3,4

Based on World Health Organization reports, the global population of older adults aged 60 years or more is projected to rise to approximately 2 billion by 2050.5 With a rapidly aging global population and rising CVD prevalence, frailty creates greater health care utilization and poses a burden on patients, caregivers, clinicians, and health care systems, thus presenting a potential public health challenge.6 There are multilevel gaps in the frailty pathway in terms of recognition, assessment, and management. Frail individuals often have multimorbidity, and existing guidelines can be disease-centric, with limited unifying frameworks to guide decision-making in complex care management.7 Furthermore, the practical implementation of strategies addressing frailty among cardiology patients is scarce, with frail individuals often excluded from major cardiovascular trials due to multimorbidity.8

Key principles of geriatric care have been defined by Goyal et al through the 4M conceptual framework—Medication, Mentation, Mobility, and What Matters (ie, quality of life).9 These considerations reinforce the holistic approach targeting biopsychosocial well-being and must be incorporated into clinical care to address the complexities in caring for frail individuals with CVD.

While the evidence base for frailty interventions in CVD is currently evolving,10 several biopsychosocial factors are known to predispose patients toward frailty, including physical inactivity, malnutrition, cognitive decline, and social inadequacy.11 In contrast to single-drug interventions and treatments that the CVD field is accustomed to, the heterogeneity of factors required to tackle frailty points to a critical need to adopt methods in implementation science (IS) for the rapid translation of research findings into clinical practice, thereby improving health systems and patient outcomes.

We focus on adopting IS research methods to improve the uptake and integration of frailty interventions into routine practice. Through analyzing the barriers and facilitators of implementation, we discuss how conceptual frameworks can guide implementation efforts and offer suggestions for suitable trial designs that facilitate long-term implementation.

Methodology

The Cochrane and PubMed literature databases were used to perform a comprehensive search and literature review. Search findings focused on studies on frailty and CVD, with database-specific search terms customized to include this (eg, “implementation science AND frailty,” “type 1 hybrid study AND frailty OR cardiovascular” as well as relevant MeSH terms for Cochrane Library). Articles on nonhuman subjects or written in non-English language were automatically excluded. For this review, studies or trials were selected based on recency, specifically those occurring in the past decade (from 2015 onward). Given the nature of the discussion regarding frailty, we focused on interventions targeting participants aged 60 years and above. Information gathered included journals from the fields of cardiology (such as JACC, JAMA Internal Medicine), geriatric medicine (Geriatrics and Gerontology), and IS. Key information extracted included the type of study design, clinical intervention, primary and secondary outcomes, and any relevant details.

Apart from obtaining information on existing frailty-related interventions, relevant trials were reviewed to identify any specific hybrid designs that were adopted and how they influenced study processes to enable statistically significant outcomes. Findings were synthesized to extract key IS principles to develop recommendations for future frailty research.

Implementation science for translating frailty interventions into cardiovascular care

IS presents an approach to explore methods and frameworks that translate evidence-based research into clinical care. Although researchers increasingly recognize the value of IS, current trials fail to incorporate its concepts into their study design. The lack of consideration of barriers and facilitators that determine treatment adoption has led to subsequent failure of translation into clinical practice.12 In the field of frailty, other limiting factors include a lack of relevance to patients' quality of life, treatment preferences, insufficient resources, and training to assess and manage frailty, cost, and health care organizational barriers.13

Understanding implementation science

IS is defined as “the scientific study of methods to promote the systematic uptake of research findings and other evidence-based practices (EBPs) into routine practice, and, hence, to improve the quality and effectiveness of health services.”14 The field encompasses a scope that extends beyond traditional clinical research, focusing on various levels of health care, including the provider, organization, and policy. Transdisciplinary research teams include members such as health service researchers, economists, sociologists, organizational scientists, and operational partners, including administrators, front-line clinicians, and patients.

Unlike clinical research which typically focuses on the health effects of an EBP, IS focuses on the implementation processes, such as adoption and integration, and the sustainable delivery of the intervention outside the research context (Table 1). Implementation interventions may include efforts to change behavior at different levels. Common examples at the provider level include education and training, audit and feedback, and performance incentives. Key components of adoption, implementation, and sustainability

To study the success of translating clinical research into actionable interventions, 5 categories of IS that underpin the efficacy and effectiveness of an intervention have been defined19 (Central Illustration). Using heart failure (HF) as an example, diffusion describes the relative passive spread of ideas and practices over time. This concept may include regular updates to HF societal guidelines and practice changes advocated by institutional opinion leaders and HF champions. Dissemination is the active distribution of evidence-based interventions through specific channels to reach a broad audience swiftly. Examples include engagement among health care providers on HF topics at scientific conferences and department meetings. Implementation involves integrating new practices and organizational structures into existing health care delivery models, such as identifying deficiencies and developing strategies through engagement with multiple stakeholders. Adoption measures the extent to which new practices are embraced. Sustainability involves a feedback loop cycling through action phases, necessitating monitoring, adoption, and prolonged integration into the system.Central IllustrationKey Components of Implementation Science Using Heart Failure (HF) as an Example19Templates were made by Slidesgo and Freepik at www.slidesgo.com and www.freepik.com.

A recent multinational survey on frailty practices among cardiologists20 uncovered unique perspectives across various subspecialties. For instance, interventionists exhibited a lower uptake of multidisciplinary and rehabilitative care, while HF specialists demonstrated greater awareness of issues related to polypharmacy and preprocedural frailty. Targeted interventions addressing the specific needs of various providers and patients, compared to a generalized untargeted approach, may lead to a more sustainable approach to implementing frailty strategies that is less resource-intensive (Central Illustration).

Diffusion and dissemination focus on ensuring knowledge is transferred between researchers and clinicians, while implementation, adoption, and sustainability are key to the successful end-to-end implementation of new interventions and treatment strategies. Clinicians and researchers should consider these 5 categories when designing studies and interventions.

Current state of scientific interventions for frailty among patients with CVD

Frailty can arise through various mechanisms, including cognitive decline, physical inactivity, poor nutrition, and insufficient social support.10 Ijaz et al explored how targeting these specific functional domains create opportunity for numerous scientific interventions that aim to prevent, improve, or reverse the development of frailty syndrome.

Physical intervention

Physical intervention includes cardiac rehabilitation (CR), which consists of patient education, diet counseling, psychological support, risk factor management, and tailored physical training. Rehabilitation aims to enhance the functional capacity and quality of life, as measured by indices such as the 6-minute walk distance, muscle strength, and the Barthel Index training.21 CR may start from early mobilization in cardiac intensive care unit patients during acute hospitalization. Subsequent engagement post-transcatheter aortic valve replacement, coronary artery bypass grafting, acute HF, and acute coronary events have demonstrated improvements in frailty metrics and physical function. CR has been shown to provide cognitive and psychosocial benefits through enhancing cognitive function and alleviating anxiety and depression. Preoperative rehabilitation through home-based programs and structured exercise therapies also optimizes frailty and functional ability before coronary artery bypass graft or valve surgery.22 Other interventions include electric muscle stimulation to strengthen skeletal muscles, which may serve as a potential adjunct to CR.23

Pharmacological intervention

In terms of pharmacological interventions, emerging data have examined the role of systemic inflammatory targets; specifically, the anti-inflammatory effects of metformin in improving frailty status, albeit limited to observational studies.24 Omega-3 fatty acid supplements have shown weak associations with enhancing mobility and cognition.25 Studies into allogeneic human mesenchymal stem cell infusions are promising, with phase 1 and 2 studies showing reported improvements in 6-minute walk distance, and cognitive frailty.26 Clinical trials are still in early stages; significant research gaps remain concerning optimal treatment regimens, long-term effects, and safety in humans that must be addressed before effective clinical implementation can occur.

In addition to new pharmacological agents, safe deprescribing may be a potential beneficial strategy in reducing excessive pill burden and risk of polypharmacy for frail patients. Deprescribing antihypertensive medication was achieved in the OPTIMISE (Optimising Treatment for Mild Systolic Hypertension in the Elderly) trial,27 with no negative impacts on blood pressure control among older patients with hypertension after 3 months.

Nutritional interventions

The nutritional domain aims to address the rapid, unintentional loss of body weight and accompanying disability. Reduced dietary protein intake may predispose individuals to sarcopenia; several studies have investigated the effect of protein supplementation on frailty status. The PROVIDE study randomized older adults with mild to moderate limitations in physical function and sarcopenia to either a protein-rich oral supplement with vitamins or an isocaloric control product without protein or micronutrients.28 Participants who received the supplement had greater change in muscle mass, handgrip strength, and lower-extremity function compared to the control group. Specific nutritional supplementation alone might benefit geriatric patients, especially those unable to exercise. Other interventions include combining whey protein supplementation with or without resistance training to improve muscle strength.29

Psychosocial interventions

Social support interventions, such as volunteer-administered interventions, have also shown potential in reducing frailty.30 Participants who received social visits by designated buddies and underwent cognitive training exercises showed improvements in both nutritional score and frailty index. Notably, this occurred even without receiving targeted physical training and nutritional intervention.

Overall, frailty is a potentially reversible state that targeted interventions can positively influence. Being frail did not significantly limit treatment efficacy for cardiovascular interventions, with certain trial interventions reducing risk of major adverse cardiac events. Multicomponent interventions (diet, exercise, cognitive training, and social activities) have shown improvements in cognitive frailty and cardiovascular health. While evidence suggests that these interventions are beneficial for frail patients, particularly in reducing adverse cardiovascular outcomes and hospitalizations, standardized assessment methods and more dedicated, large-scale trials are needed to solidify these findings.21

Intervention level

Common barriers to implementing an EBP include cost and limited resources. Integrated exercise-based CR is frequently evaluated as an evidence-based strategy for addressing frailty. However, the need for regular sessions and multidisciplinary teams to carry out the interventions over prolonged periods may be economically nonviable despite clinical effectiveness.31 As illustrated in other trials of exercise, low levels of staff training and limited resources32 for the intervention resulted in limited to no observed effects, owing to low attendance and high dropouts.33,34

Patient level

The heterogeneous nature of frailty manifestation means that individual patients may require different approaches. Engaging frail individuals may be challenging due to physical limitations such as limited mobility or transportation barriers, cognitive impairments, or inadequate social support networks that hinder their ability to participate in recommended interventions or follow-up carefully. Patients' perception and understanding of their health condition, and associated stigma toward being labeled as “frail,” can perpetuate reluctance to participate in frailty-specific interventions.35 Cultural beliefs and socioeconomic factors can limit willingness to engage with lifestyle modifications or additional health care visits.

Clinician level

At the clinician level, differences in individual perceptions, knowledge and attitudes toward frailty can pose challenges in identifying frailty and implementing treatment guidelines. A qualitative research study in Australia conducted interviews with health professionals working in multidisciplinary teams across various hospitals.36 While most participants reported having knowledge about frailty, there were differing levels of understanding. Additionally, many did not see value in distinguishing frail patients among patients under their care as they perceived it would not significantly affect their management. Such preconceived notions may lead to resistance against adopting frailty screening tools in routine practice.

Apart from demographic variables and influence from cultural contexts, clinicians may be influenced by stigma or existing bias associated with frailty. In the same study, some health care professionals chose to avoid using the word ‘frail’ for fear of offending patients. Such attitudes could also limit open discourse about frailty, resulting in poorer patient outcomes.

Beyond this, there may also be limited awareness of updated evidence and inertia for change, leading to delay in adopting updated guidelines into practice. According to the 2024 European Society for Cardiology hypertension guidelines,37 the recommended target systolic blood pressure for adults on antihypertensives is between 120 and 129 mm Hg. Certain patients may be allowed a more flexible threshold if they cannot tolerate these levels, experience orthostatic symptoms, are over 85 years of age or frail, or have limited life expectancy. For these individuals, the goal is to achieve the lowest possible blood pressure that approaches the recommended target, which is often higher, to reduce the risk of orthostatic intolerance and falls. Understanding concepts of frailty and tailoring treatment to factors such as age, frailty status, lifespan, and patient tolerability will aid clinicians in holistic management.

Institution/systemic level

A common barrier to the adoption of evidence in real-world practice is the lack of standardized tools that harmonize frailty assessment across various clinical care settings.38 Frailty instruments include electronic frailty indices which can be used in settings with digitalized medical records but may not be feasible in departments without electronic medical records. Even within the same organization, medical records may be used differently for research purposes vs clinical care, with varying levels of data access.39 Having differing practices between departments has also contributed to the lack of standardized protocols from detection to management of frailty.40 These real-world issues indicate an upfront need for communication, planning, and discussion among stakeholders as well as interprofessional collaboration.41

Clinical trial level

Frail patients are less frequently recruited into trials, owing to strict exclusion criteria that may exclude frail patients with multimorbidity and polypharmacy.42 With fewer randomized trials involving frail patients, current evidence has mainly stemmed from observational studies of limited scientific relevance. The findings of FRAIL-AF (Frail Atrial Fibrillation),43 which tested the efficacy of warfarin vs non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs), showed that, contrary to concerns on bleeding risk with warfarin (over NOAC), more bleeding was observed when older frail patients were switched out of warfarin to NOAC. It is critical to achieve adequate representation of frail patients in landmark trials to address evidence gaps in frailty.

Similarly, there had been limited observational evidence on the benefits and risks of discontinuing antihypertensive drugs in frail patients. The multicenter randomized trial (RETREAT-FRAIL [Impact of the Reduction in Antihypertensive Treatment on Total Mortality in Frail Subjects With Low Systolic Blood Pressure])44 evaluated the effect of downtitrating antihypertensive treatment in nursing home residents in France. This large-scale Randomized Controlled Trial (RCT) recruited over a 1,000 nursing home patients above 80 years of age. In this study, the progressive reduction of antihypertensive therapies for individuals with a systolic blood pressure below 130 mm Hg did not result in lower all-cause mortality compared to patients receiving usual care. These 2 examples demonstrate that frail patients can be recruited into trials and is important to create generalizable findings that effect change.

Crucially, a meta-analysis by Jiang et al21 assessed the implications of frailty on cardiovascular outcomes in a range of RCTs. Frailty did not significantly reduce treatment efficacy, suggesting that interventions need not be withheld solely based on frailty status, enabling greater advocacy for more frail patients to be included into future RCTs.

Overall, the study of barriers and drivers to implementation, as well as the design of implementation strategies, is essential to ensuring the successful implementation of trial evidence in practice. Besides the challenges discussed above, other potential challenges include misalignments with policy or system priorities, associated costs of the intervention, and inadequate infrastructure or personnel to deliver the intervention.

Types of implementation study methods and best practices (Case studies)

Implementation research encompasses a diverse range of methodologies, including experimental, quasi-experimental, observational, and qualitative approaches.45 While clinical effectiveness and implementation research traditionally address distinct research questions through different study designs, their separation often impedes the translation of evidence into practice. To bridge this gap, researchers and clinicians must consider implementation strategies during the initial stages of study design, rather than as an afterthought.

The emergence of hybrid effectiveness-implementation designs is a promising solution to this challenge. These designs blend elements of both effectiveness and implementation research, creating a framework that advances clinical knowledge while facilitating real-world adoption of EBPs.

Type 1 hybrid design

A type 1 hybrid design focuses primarily on the effectiveness outcomes of an intervention while exploring its “implementability.” It explores implementation-related factors, including the barriers and facilitators to implementation. Using a hybrid type 1 design, study investigators aimed to understand both the effectiveness of their Home-based Cardiac Rehabilitation program and evaluate patients' and providers' perceptions of the feasibility and acceptability of this program.46 The feedback provided valuable insights into identifying key barriers and was used to refine the implementation strategy before its widespread dissemination. The program was subsequently implemented by the Veterans Affairs (VA) with an over 60% completion rate. Participants recorded significant improvements in exercise capacity, depression, self-efficacy, body mass index, and eating habits.

A similar hybrid type 1 effectiveness-implementation RCT was the TOP UP (Telephysiotherapy for Older People) trial, which delivered exercise interventions via telehealth to older adults receiving aged care services at home or in residential care.49 Interviews were conducted with trial participants as well as providers (physiotherapists, coaches, and aged care managers) to glean insights into their experiences during the intervention. This process identified extrinsic and intrinsic barriers to implementation, including innate technology avoidance, physical and cognitive impairments in the geriatric population, and associated hesitation by providers in service delivery. These issues were addressed and mitigated through plans to provide local support and telehealth training, aiming to improve the uptake of telerehabilitation services.

In both examples, a type 1 design was advantageous in facilitating concurrent investigation of the effects of clinical interventions and the context for implementation. Conducting a process evaluation during the trial period minimized delays in identifying barriers, provided transferable lessons that allowed for timely adjustments, and facilitated the translation of these interventions into practice. These practices should be incorporated into future frailty hybrid trials.

Moving forward, the evaluation of implementability could potentially begin before effectiveness trials commence. A study by Landes et al50 disseminated information regarding the trial's components to naive sites (sites not yet involved in the intervention) to seek feedback on the intervention's components in terms of potential and feasibility, thereby enhancing contextualization for each site.

Type 2 hybrid design

In contrast, a type 2 hybrid design holds a dual focus on clinical intervention and implementation outcomes, with simultaneous testing of implementation strategies during clinical trials. This design often involves an explicitly described implementation strategy that is perceived to be feasible in the real world; the interventions have evidence but face uncertainty in a new context, while policy demands drive momentum for their implementation.

An Australian RCT evaluated an Interdisciplinary Home-based Reablement Programme targeted at improving the functional independence of older patients with dementia in the community.51 Phase 1 involved an RCT to examine the effectiveness of the 4-month care model; phase 2 involved interviews and focus groups with participants and interventionists to evaluate the procedure. A type 2 hybrid approach enabled the generation of evidence for its effectiveness, while simultaneously investigating the processes, outcomes, and factors influencing the success of implementation. This can better inform consumers, providers, and policymakers in their decision-making and overcome challenges in moving intervention strategies from successful efficacy trials into the real-world clinical context in a swift manner.

Case study: Fortress trial

The FORTRESS (Frailty in Older people: Rehabilitation, Treatment, Research Examining Separate Settings) trial is a multisite hybrid type 2 stepped wedge, cluster-randomized trial.47 Besides employing the hybrid framework to test the success of the implementation strategy and the intervention, the study also focused on evaluating effectiveness at both the hospital and community levels. This holistic approach enables testing whether interventions are successful in various settings. A follow-up assessment was expected to conclude in December 2023, and the results of this study can inform the design of similar hybrid-type trials in the field of cardiovascular frailty research.

Type 3 hybrid design

Type 3 hybrid trial designs test an implementation strategy while secondarily collecting data on the clinical intervention and related outcomes.50 The primary focus is on implementation outcomes of adoption, fidelity, and sustainability.16 These studies are particularly valuable when concerns arise that effectiveness outcomes may be significantly influenced by the fidelity of delivery in real-world settings, or when there is a strong push for implementation, such as a formal mandate.50 They compare different implementation strategies and assess their impact on implementation outcomes. Random assignments may occur at the provider, clinic, or system level, with clinics randomized to receive different implementation strategies varying in type, target, or intensity. The effectiveness of these strategies is subsequently compared. Meanwhile, secondary clinical outcomes are examined without randomization and are correlated to the level of adoption and fidelity achieved.

The U.S. Department of VA's Safer Aging through Geriatrics-Informed Evidence-Based Practices Quality Enhancement Research Initiative program employed a randomized, type 3 hybrid effectiveness-implementation study48 to improve age-friendly aligned EBPs. The program targeted over 4 million veterans aged 65 and older.52 It compared 2 different strategies: passive implementation vs facilitation. Four main EBPs were identified: Surgical Pause,53 EMPOWER (Eliminating Medications Through Patient Ownership of End Results),54 TAP (Tailored Activities Program),55 and CAPABLE (Community Aging in Place—Advancing Better Living for Elders).56 Facilitation was selected as the primary implementation strategy due to the multifaceted nature of each EBP—none had been fully implemented within the VA before.57,58 In this stepped-wedge design, all sites received the active treatment condition after experiencing the comparison condition for a predetermined period.59 This study design is helpful when equipoise is deemed ethically insufficient to justify the use of a control condition for the entire study period.60

The primary implementation outcome is defined as “Reach”—the proportion of veterans eligible for each EBP who ultimately received that EBP during implementation. The primary effectiveness outcome across EBPs was “facility-free days”—the number of days older veterans remain alive and not institutionalized for postacute care. Secondary outcomes specific to each EBP will be collected using a convergent mixed-methods approach informed by the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance (RE-AIM) framework.61

Overall, adopting hybrid designs ensures that while we generate evidence for new interventions, new strategies are simultaneously developed for their successful diffusion, implementation, and sustainable adoption. These aspects should be incorporated into future frailty hybrid trials.

Use of implementation theories, models, and frameworks to inform study

It is helpful to discuss the heuristics involved in IS to bridge the gap between research and clinical practice. Studies regarding the upscaling of 40 public health interventions identified that nearly half of these interventions had adopted a structured refinement process,63 highlighting the importance of study design being theoretically grounded.

IS utilizes theories, models, and frameworks (TMFs) to enhance understanding of implementation processes and outcomes, and achieve insights into the influential factors of successful implementation, thereby selecting appropriate strategies. Such theories are borrowed from fields of psychology, sociology, and organizational theory.64 TMFs provide structured pathways for planning and executing implementation efforts, along with structured criteria to evaluate the success of implementation efforts.

Common frameworks used in cardiovascular-related IS studies include the RE-AIM framework;61 the Practical, Robust Implementation and Sustainability Model;65 and the Capability, Opportunity, Motivation, and Behaviour Model of the Behaviour Change Wheel.66 Other frameworks recommended for hybrid type 1 designs include Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research67 and Pragmatic Explanatory Continuum Indicator Summary 2 (PRECIS-2)68 for study focus and reporting outcomes.

Outcome assessments

In hybrid trials, it is imperative to incorporate implementation outcomes to ensure that interventions are not only effective but also feasible and sustainable within real-world settings. Implementation outcomes are essential for evaluating the adoption, execution, and long-term maintenance of interventions, particularly when addressing the complexities associated with frailty in CVD patients. Examples of implementation outcomes include acceptability, appropriateness, affordability, practicability, unintended consequences, equity, and feasibility. This dual focus on effectiveness and implementation is what makes hybrid trials so valuable in translating research into practice.

TMFs offer criteria to evaluate implementation outcomes. Weiner et al developed and psychometrically assessed 3 new implementation outcome measures for evaluating the success of implementation efforts.69 These include Acceptability of Intervention Measure, Intervention Appropriateness Measure, and Feasibility of Intervention Measure. These brief four-item scales involve Likert-based questions designed to measure implementation outcomes that are often considered to be leading indicators of implementation success. In designing frailty trials, these open-access tools may be easily administered with stakeholders to determine whether they believe an intervention is acceptable, appropriate, and feasible to evaluate an implementation and track its potential success.

Example of re-aim and other frameworks

The RE-AIM framework is commonly used in implementation research.70 It examines 5 key dimensions: reach, efficacy, effectiveness, adoption, implementation, and maintenance. These may be used to assess the efficacy of outreach or intervention, aiming to achieve greater adoption and sustainability in implementation.71 It is available on a free-use website, offering useful material for community leaders and researchers, including practical research translation tools and resources.

The CRAMS Trial (Cardiovascular Rehabilitation for transient ischaemic Attack and Mild Stroke)72 modeled a type 1 effectiveness-implementation hybrid design to explore whether an integrated exercise-based CR program for patients with transient ischemic attack or stroke can be implemented in existing health services in Australia. The RE-AIM framework was used to identify barriers and facilitators, select implementation strategies, and assess implementation outcomes. Implementation outcomes included participant characteristics (reach), change in 6-minute walk distance (effectiveness), number of referrers and characteristics of program clinicians (adoption), fidelity and cost of intervention (implementation), and dropout rates (maintenance). It also modeled constructs (domains of “Intervention Characteristics” and “Inner Settings”) from the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research to gain insight into factors that may potentially influence the intervention's success.73

Existing tools such as the Implementation Planning Assessment tool can also be utilized.74 Overall, alongside the application and adaptation of existing models and frameworks, there must be proactive collaboration with researchers, clinicians, and policymakers to address barriers at each level of the implementation process, to boost implementation efforts, and ensure interventions are adopted into routine care.

Vision for enhanced cardiovascular care

Frailty has become increasingly recognized as a common disease burden among older adults. This observation aligns with the phenomenon of rapidly aging populations and “superaging.” Countries in the Asia-Pacific region are undergoing rapid aging, with individuals aged above 60 projected to increase to more than 25%, and those over 80 years of age in the region expected to triple by 2050.75 The increased burden of frailty has profound implications for health care provision.

The field of frailty interventions in cardiology is nascent, presenting both challenges and opportunities to integrate IS from the outset. A key opportunity lies in the lack of evidence, as well as a lack of direction. Limited examples involve interventions in frail elders due to exclusion criteria. Promising studies such as the Department of VA' Safer Aging through Geriatrics-Informed Evidence-Based Practice Quality Enhancement Research Initiative program are ongoing but without available results yet to determine their success.

Nonetheless, adopting an IS lens enables a systematic adaptation of interventions through structured evaluation and iterative feedback processes. This article introduces key concepts from IS, primarily focusing on the types of design and frameworks. We analyzed existing trials, seeking ways to incorporate elements of implementation research into frailty trials. Implementation strategies can be modified based on context-specific barriers, while maintaining the core components of the intervention. Glaringly, such an approach may lower the internal validity of a study and attenuate the estimated effect of the intervention. However, there is still merit in such designs. Any limitations in internal validity can be mitigated by increased external validity and the ability to adapt to diverse settings and communities. Overall, there is a greater likelihood that the intervention will be implemented and maintained rather than be embedded in theory.

The importance of IS is increasingly recognized. In recent years, the Veterans Health Administration's Office of Research and Development has mandated that all clinical trials under the Cooperative Studies Programme incorporate an implementation plan as a requirement for funding.76 This approach encourages clinicians to strive for results that can be effectively implemented in clinical practice, enhancing real-world impact. There is immense potential for IS and research methods to expedite the translation of existing EBPs into routine care. This focus is especially critical in the field of frailty to ensure positive health outcomes in the timeliest way possible.

Study Limitations

Although implementation studies offer a shorter time frame between the start of the research and the uptake of new interventions, existing practices that face implementation challenges may require more targeted strategies. This concept may involve assembling multidisciplinary teams of professionals from different organizational sites, who cooperate within and across teams to improve the uptake of new health care processes and deliver implementation outcomes. Ultimately, every health care system is unique, with different priorities set by the governments that build the system. Access to health care varies at both the primary and tertiary levels, with some systems having a bridge between primary care and rehabilitation, a reminder that implementation requires a tailored approach across different settings. Interventions that do not address local barriers or capitalize on local resources may not succeed, and strategies that are effective in some health care systems and regions may be less so in others. Furthermore, gaps in implementation may be amplified in ethnic minority groups and those of low socioeconomic status.77

Conclusions

Frailty is nuanced and closely linked to cardiovascular morbidity—being cognizant of key geriatric principles will promote holistic care. Collaborative research with key stakeholders in research adoption (ie, clinicians and front-line health staff) and early engagement is critical for rapid knowledge translation. As existing interventions strive to target the multifaceted domains of frailty, systematic efforts to integrate frailty assessment in cardiovascular practice are needed to enhance patient benefit. IS bridges the chasm between intervention and practical application, enabling comprehensive understanding of anticipated barriers and facilitators that predict implementation outcomes. Through our proposed roadmap, we hope these methods can be better incorporated to elevate and accelerate the adoption of frailty interventions among patients with CVD.
