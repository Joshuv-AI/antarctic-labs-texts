Species-specific microbial dynamics are associated with molecular compositional restructuring of free metabolites during Light-flavor Baijiu fermentation
Authors: Tao Bo, Meiyi Ren, Jinghong Zhou, Baoqing Bai, Ying Zhang, Jinhua Zhang, Pan Zhen, Ying Han, Yukun Yang, Shuo Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrobial succession during Jiupei fermentation is closely associated with biochemical changes that contribute to Baijiu quality formation; however, the fermentation-induced restructuring of the non-volatile metabolome and its species-level microbial associations remain poorly understood. We hypothesized that species-level microbial succession would be associated with pathway-specific changes in the non-volatile metabolome, and that bacterial and fungal diversity would show contrasting association patterns with different metabolite classes during fermentation. To test these hypotheses, we integrated physicochemical analysis, PacBio SMRT sequencing, and untargeted metabolomics to characterize microbial succession and metabolite dynamics during Light-flavor Baijiu Jiupei fermentation. A total of 1392 peaks were detected, with 134 metabolites annotated, including amino acids, organic acids, carbohydrates, alcohols, and nucleotides. Fermentation was accompanied by marked temporal shifts in the non-volatile metabolite profile, with galactose metabolism and starch and sucrose metabolism identified as the main enriched pathways. Fungal diversity (Shannon index) showed predominantly positive correlations with many metabolites across multiple classes, whereas bacterial diversity showed predominantly negative correlations with most metabolites. At species level, Lactobacillus homohiochii showed the largest number of positive correlations with amino acids (14 edges), alcohols (8 edges), and organic acids (7 edges), while Saccharomyces sp. was positively correlated with 19 amino acids and derivatives. These species-specific correlation patterns provide a correlation-based view of microbe-metabolite dynamics during Jiupei fermentation and generate testable hypotheses for future culture-based and mechanistic studies.Keywords: Free metabolite dynamics, Non-volatile metabolome, Lactic acid bacteria, Yeast, Species-level correlation network, Solid-state fermentation

Introduction

Baijiu, a traditional fermented beverage in China (Liu et al., 2018; Xie et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2012), has a history spanning over 2000 years (Jin et al., 2017; Li et al., 2024). During its fermentation, microorganisms and their secreted enzymes convert grain starch into Jiupei (fermented grains) containing alcohols, organic acids, esters, and other flavor-related compounds (Marco et al., 2017; Zou et al., 2018). Baijiu can be categorized into 12 distinct aroma types based on sensory characteristics. More than 2400 compounds, including esters, aldehydes, ketones, acids, acetals, and other trace components (Fang et al., 2019; Jiang et al., 2021; Sun et al., 2015; Xu et al., 2020), have been identified, and they collectively contribute to the aroma type, style, and overall quality of the final liquor (Jin et al., 2017). Although volatile compounds have received substantial attention because of their direct contribution to aroma, the non-volatile fraction, including amino acids, organic acids, carbohydrates, sugar alcohols, and nucleic-acid-related compounds, reflects substrate conversion and changes in the biochemical state of Jiupei (Huang et al., 2020). Non-volatile organic acids can also discriminate among Baijiu types, indicating that this fraction contains chemically informative variation not captured by volatile profiles alone (Wang et al., 2022).

Microbe-driven fermentation of Jiupei represents the primary pathway for flavor compound formation in Baijiu (Li et al., 2024; Liu & Sun, 2018; Ye et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2018). Studies of sauce- and strong-flavor Baijiu have identified aroma-type-specific bacterial and fungal consortia and associated individual taxa with n-propanol, short-chain fatty acids, caproic acid, and ethyl caproate (Deng et al., 2025; Tu et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2025; Zeng et al., 2025). In Light-flavor Baijiu, microbial succession is characterized by a bacterial shift from Staphylococcus to Lactobacillus acetotolerans and a fungal transition from Lichtheimia, Pichia, and Saccharomycopsis to Saccharomyces dominance (Luo et al., 2023). In mechanized Light-flavor Baijiu production, L. casei, L. paracasei, L. guizhouensis, and L. sunkii were positively correlated with 17 major volatile flavor compounds, including ethyl lactate, ethyl acetate, isoamyl lactate, benzaldehyde, and phenylethyl alcohol, while S. cerevisiae and Kazachstania spp. were also associated with key flavor compounds (Luo et al., 2023). A recent comparison of dual-round Light-flavor Baijiu fermentation identified L. acetotolerans and Acetilactobacillus jinshanensis as dominant in Dacha and Ercha Jiupei, respectively, and linked several lactic acid bacterial species with flavonoid accumulation (Qu et al., 2025). These studies provide an increasingly detailed view of microbial succession and taxon-specific metabolite associations across Baijiu production systems.

Integrated metagenomic and metabolomic analyses have also begun to characterize polar non-volatile and volatile metabolites in Light-flavor Jiupei (Huang et al., 2020), while recent studies of strong-flavor Jiupei have revealed spatial and temporal changes in amino acids, organic acids, carbohydrates, and related microbial communities (Xu et al., 2025; Xu et al., 2026). Nevertheless, the temporal restructuring of a broad free non-volatile metabolite pool across a complete Light-flavor Dacha fermentation cycle remains insufficiently resolved. Non-volatile metabolites include nutrients and signaling-related molecules associated with microbial growth and community development (Hu et al., 2017; Liu et al., 2022; Siedler et al., 2020; Wei et al., 2021; Xia et al., 2024), yet their temporal associations with bacterial and fungal succession remain unclear. Species-level resolution is particularly important because different species within the same genus may follow distinct successional trajectories and show contrasting metabolite association patterns that can be obscured by genus-level summaries. Full-length 16S rRNA gene and ITS sequencing provide finer taxonomic resolution for evaluating such patterns (Han et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2024).

Light-flavor Baijiu is one of the three major aroma types of Chinese spirits (Luo et al., 2023) and is characterized by solid-state fermentation in ground jars with separate steaming and distillation steps (Qu et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2019). We hypothesized that (i) species-level microbial succession would be associated with pathway-specific changes in the non-volatile metabolome during Dacha Jiupei fermentation; and (ii) bacterial and fungal diversity would show contrasting association patterns with different metabolite classes during fermentation. To test these hypotheses, we integrated physicochemical monitoring, PacBio SMRT sequencing, and untargeted metabolomics to characterize microbial communities using species-level assignments and free metabolite dynamics throughout fermentation. We then used diversity analysis and Spearman correlation networks to investigate temporal microbe-metabolite associations rather than directly test functional interactions or infer metabolite production and consumption.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Experimental samples were collected from Xinghuacun Fenjiu Distillery Co., Ltd. in Fenyang City, Shanxi Province, China (37°16′ N, 111°44′ E). Sampling was performed on days 0, 3, 7, 11, 15, 21, 24, and 28 of a single representative batch of Dacha Jiupei fermentation in a ground jar (approximate capacity, 130 kg). For each time point, samples were separately collected from the upper, middle, and lower layers of the fermentation vessel and mixed thoroughly to obtain a representative composite sample. A 200 g aliquot of this homogenized composite was placed into a sterile sample bag and transported to the laboratory under low-temperature conditions. Upon arrival, the 200 g composite sample was processed as follows. For free small- molecule metabolite analysis, six subsamples (∼5 g each) were taken from the same composite material and independently extracted and analyzed as technical replicates (n = 6). For physicochemical analysis, three subsamples (∼10 g each) were taken from the same composite and independently processed as technical replicates (n = 3). For DNA extraction and PacBio SMRT sequencing, three subsamples (∼5 g each) were taken from the same composite, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, stored at −80 °C, and independently processed as technical replicates (n = 3). Each technical replicate was weighed and processed separately through extraction, cleanup, and instrument injection, as applicable.

Determination of physicochemical parameters of Jiupei

During fermentation, temperature was monitored with an electronic sensor (Shixin Instruments, Fenyang, China); moisture content was determined using a dedicated analyzer (Shenzhen Guanya Moisture Analysis Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China); and pH was measured with a calibrated benchtop pH meter (Shanghai Yuejin Medical Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Reducing sugar content was quantified by the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid (DNS) method using glucose standards (Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Ethanol and lactic acid contents were analyzed by gas chromatography (SHIMADZU GC-2010, Kyoto, Japan) (Chen et al., 2017) and high-performance liquid chromatography (Agilent 1260 Infinity II, Santa Clara, CA, USA) (Zhao et al., 2022), respectively.

DNA extraction, amplicon generation, and high-throughput sequencing

DNA extraction from the Jiupei samples were performed using a previously reported protocol (Luo et al., 2023). Bacterial full-length 16S rRNA genes were amplified with the universal primer set 27F/1492R (Johnson et al., 2019), producing amplicons of approximately 1500 bp. The complete fungal ITS region was targeted with primers ITS1F/ITS4 (Banerjee et al., 2019). All primers were tagged with unique 8-nt barcodes for multiplex identification. PCR amplification was carried out in a 25 μL reaction volume containing 50 ng of genomic DNA template, 12.5 μL PCR Master Mix (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany), 1 μL each of 10 μM forward and reverse primers, and nuclease-free water to adjust the final volume. After amplification, the PCR products were purified. Following purification, amplicons from the samples were equimolarly pooled and subjected to single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing. The pooled library was loaded onto one SMRT cell and analyzed using the PacBio Sequel II platform (Pacific Biosciences, Menlo Park, CA, USA), according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Sequencing data processing and taxonomic classification

High-quality consensus reads were generated from raw PacBio subreads using the CCS pipeline (version 6.0.0; https://ccs.how). Subsequent data processing was performed using VSEARCH (Rognes et al., 2016) and USEARCH (v11; https://drive5.com/usearch). Potential chimeric sequences were detected and removed by referencing the UNITE CHIME database (Nilsson et al., 2019) via the ‘uchime_ref’ algorithm. To generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), unique sequences were clustered at 100% nucleotide identity using the ‘unoise3’ algorithm with default parameters (i.e., minsize = 8). Taxonomic assignment of ASVs was performed by querying representative sequences against the NCBI nucleotide (nt) database (March 2024 release). Bacterial ASVs were classified using full-length 16S rRNA sequences (∼1500 bp), and fungal ASVs using full-length ITS sequences. BLASTn alignment was conducted with a sequence identity threshold of ≥97% and query coverage of ≥90%. A best-hit approach was employed: each ASV was putatively assigned to the most specific taxonomic rank supported by the single top BLAST hit meeting these criteria. When multiple equally scoring top hits were obtained (≥97% identity with overlapping bit-scores within 1% of the maximum), a lowest common ancestor (LCA) algorithm was applied to assign the ASV to the most specific taxonomic rank shared by all equally scoring top hits. For fungal classification, ITS sequences were additionally queried against the UNITE database (version 9.0) to refine genus- and species-level assignments, and non-fungal matches were excluded from downstream fungal analyses (Abarenkov et al., 2010).

Extraction and determination of non-volatile free metabolites from Jiupei

The non-volatile free metabolites in the Jiupei samples were analyzed using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS; Shimadzu GCMS-QP2010 SE, Kyoto, Japan) equipped with a DB-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm id, 0.25 μm film thickness). The detailed procedure was as follows: after being freeze-dried for 12 h, the Jiupei samples were pulverized. Subsequently, 50 mg of the resulting powder was weighed into an microcentrifuge tube, and 700 μL of 50% chromatographic methanol was added, followed by vortex mixing. The mixture was subjected to ice-bath ultrasonication at 70% power for 30 min, vortexed again, and this ultrasonication/vortex step was repeated once. After centrifugation at 13,958 ×g for 10 min, 100 μL of the supernatant was collected. Then, 3 μL of succinic acid-2,2,3,3-d4 (1.4 mg/mL; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was added as an internal standard. The solution was freeze-dried for 2 h, after which 50 μL of a 20 mg/mL methoxyamine hydrochloride (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) in pyridine solution was added, and the mixture was incubated in a 40 °C water bath for 80 min. Following this, 80 μL of N-Methyl-N-(trimethylsilyl)trifluoroacetamide (Macklin, Shanghai, China) was added. The mixture was vortexed and incubated again in a 40 °C water bath for 80 min. After centrifugation at 13,958 ×g for 10 min, 80 μL of the supernatant was transferred to a GC vial. For quality control (QC), a 5 μL aliquot was taken from each sample to create a pooled QC sample, which was used to evaluate the stability and performance of the analysis. All prepared samples were stored at −80 °C until GC–MS analysis.

The GC–MS analysis was conducted under the following conditions: Helium was used as the carrier gas at a column flow rate of 1.21 mL/min, with a total flow of 12.2 mL/min, a purge flow 3.0 mL/min, and a pressure of 78.0 kPa. The initial oven temperature was 70 °C and was held for 2 min, increased to 290 °C at 5 °C/min, and held at 290 °C for 3 min. Mass spectra were acquired in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV, scanning m/z 50–800. The ion source and interface temperatures were 250 °C and 280 °C, respectively. For metabolite annotation and relative quantification, raw GC–MS data were processed using MS-DIAL software (version 4.0). Metabolite annotation was performed by matching mass spectra against the GCMS DB-PublicFiehnRI-VS3 MSP spectral database combined with retention index (RI) matching (RI tolerance ±20). Because compound identities were not confirmed using authentic standards analyzed under identical conditions, annotations based on spectral library and RI matches were classified as putatively annotated compounds, consistent with Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) Level 2. Features were retained if present in ≥80% of QC samples with relative standard deviation (RSD) < 30% in QC samples. Missing values were imputed using the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm. Normalization was performed using the internal standard (succinic acid-2,2,3,3-d4) followed by probabilistic quotient normalization (PQN). All samples were analyzed in a single batch. Relative quantification was performed using the internal standard method.

Data visualization and statistical analysis

Data visualization and statistical processing were conducted using Origin 2022 software. β-diversity was assessed by principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray–Curtis distance matrix, with significance tested by PERMANOVA (adonis, 999 permutations). Spearman's rank correlation analysis was performed to evaluate associations between microbial diversity, dominant microorganisms (top 20 species), and metabolites, with p-values adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Correlation networks were constructed using |r| > 0.7 and FDR-adjusted p < 0.05. Network construction and visualization were performed using the igraph, ggplot2, and ggraph packages in R (version 4.2.1). Multivariate statistical analysis of metabolomics data was performed with SIMCA 14.1 software, including principal component analysis (PCA), orthogonal projections to latent structures discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA), and variable importance in projection (VIP) scores. For OPLS-DA, model quality was evaluated by R2X(cum), R2Y(cum), and Q2(cum), with overfitting assessed via 200-iteration permutation testing. Normalized abundances of all putatively annotated metabolites were compared among fermentation stages using the Kruskal–Wallis test. p-values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate method. Metabolites satisfying both VIP > 1 and FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 were retained as candidate discriminatory metabolites.

Results

Bacterial and fungal species-level community structures and α-diversity diverged during Jiupei fermentation

Within the studied Dacha Jiupei fermentation batch, the α-diversity of the microbial communities during fermentation was assessed based on the Shannon index, as shown in Fig. 1A and B. The bacterial Shannon index was lowest at the beginning of fermentation (day 0), increased significantly after fermentation commenced, and peaked on day 3 (Fig. 1A). It subsequently decreased from day 3 to day 28. The fungal Shannon index was also lowest on day 0, increased significantly at the onset of fermentation, and then increased gradually before showing limited further variation after day 15 (Fig. 1B). PCoA based on Bray–Curtis distances showed time-associated separation for both communities, and PERMANOVA testing revealed statistically significant clustering (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1C and D). For bacteria, D0, D3, and D7 occupied distinct positions, whereas profiles from D11 onward clustered relatively closely (Fig. 1C). For fungi, D0 samples were separated from the later time points, whereas profiles from D3 onward clustered relatively closely in the ordination space (Fig. 1D).

Microbial α- and β-diversity analysis in Light-flavor Jiupei. (A) Box plot of the bacterial shannon index; (B) Box plot of the fungal shannon index; (C) and (D) Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis distances for bacterial and fungal communities.

Lactobacillus species exhibit the highest diversity and dominate bacterial community succession during fermentation

Full-length 16S rRNA gene and ITS sequencing using PacBio HiFi reads enabled putative species-level assignments, although closely related taxa may not have been fully resolved (Han et al., 2024). The composition and succession of putatively assigned dominant bacterial species are shown in Fig. 2A. At the onset of fermentation (day 0), Staphylococcus gallolyticus was the most abundant species (11%). By day 3, Lactobacillus ingluviei and Staphylococcus gallinarum each rose to 12%, while Lactobacillus sanfranciscensis, L. delbrueckii and Leuconostoc citreum increased to >5%. L. brevis predominated on D7 (37%), accompanied by L. homohiochii (5%). From D11 onward, L. homohiochii and L. brevis collectively accounted for 86% of the bacterial community, with mean relative abundances of 75.4% and 10.8%, respectively. Thus, bacterial succession in the studied batch was characterized by the sequential predominance of nine putatively assigned Lactobacillus taxa. Staphylococci were the second most diverse group, comprising three putatively assigned taxa. From day 3 to the end of fermentation, Saccharomycopsis fibuligera and Rhizopus arrhizus were the two most abundant species, with mean relative abundances of 29% and 32% (Fig. 2B). Saccharomyces sp. and Issatchenkia orientalis were also dominant, with average relative abundances exceeding 4% each.

Temporal succession and composition of the dominant microbial community at the species level. (A) Top 20 bacterial community; (B)Top 20 fungal community.

Fungal α-diversity was associated with multiple physicochemical variables

Temperature, pH, moisture, residual reducing sugars, ethanol and lactic acid are key physicochemical descriptors of Jiupei associated with flavor formation (Guan et al., 2020); their temporal profiles are shown in Fig. S1. Regression analysis was used to evaluate associations between these six variables and Shannon diversity (Fig. 3). Temperature, pH, and reducing sugars were positively correlated with bacterial α-diversity, while moisture, ethanol, and lactic acid showed negative correlations. However, none of these associations reached statistical significance (Fig. 3A–F). In contrast, fungal α-diversity was positively associated with temperature, moisture, reducing sugars, ethanol, and lactic acid (p < 0.05), and negatively associated with pH (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3G–L). Within the studied batch, fungal α-diversity therefore showed significant associations with more physicochemical variables than bacterial α-diversity.

Temporal restructuring of the Jiupei metabolite profile was associated with galactose and starch/sucrose metabolism

GC–MS profiling yielded 1392 valid peaks, of which 134 metabolites were putatively annotated by spectral-library and retention-index matching. QC samples clustered tightly in PCA (Fig. S2), supporting analytical reproducibility. PCA revealed a temporal trajectory in which D0 and D3 occupied distinct positions, D7 and D11 were adjacent, D11–D24 overlapped, and D28 formed a distinct cluster (Fig. 4A). HCA produced a comparable pattern, separating D0–D3 from D7–D28. Within the latter branch, D7 and D28 formed separate subclusters, whereas D11–D24 clustered together (Fig. 4B). The separation of D28 from D24 indicated that the non-volatile metabolite profile continued to change between days 24 and 28 in the studied batch. OPLS-DA reproduced the temporal separation (R2X(cum) = 0.926, R2Y(cum) = 0.854, and Q2(cum) = 0.720. permutation-test Q2 = −0.454; Fig. 4C and Fig. S3). On the basis of VIP > 1 and FDR-adjusted p < 0.05, 28 features were retained as candidate discriminatory metabolites (Fig. 4D). KEGG enrichment analysis (p < 0.05) of these candidate features identified galactose metabolism and starch and sucrose metabolism as the primary pathways (Fig. 4E). Metabolites assigned to galactose metabolism showed overall increases during fermentation (Fig. 4F).

Bacterial and fungal diversity showed contrasting associations with metabolite classes

To elucidate associations between free metabolites and microbial diversity, the 134 annotated metabolites were categorized into six classes: amino acids and derivatives, nucleic acid-related compounds, sugars and derivatives, alcohols, organic acids, and other compounds. Correlation analyses between Shannon index and individual metabolites are summarized by class in Fig. 5. Across all classes, bacterial Shannon index was negatively correlated with most significantly associated metabolites, whereas fungal Shannon diversity was positively correlated with most significantly associated metabolites.

Correlation heatmap between microbial diversity and six categories of free small-molecule metabolites. Correlation matrices were constructed between the Shannon diversity indices of bacterial and fungal communities and six metabolite categories, including (A) amino acids and derivatives; (B) Nucleic-acid-related compounds; (C) Alcohols; (D) Organic acids; (E) Sugars and derivatives; and (F) Other metabolites (esters, amines, phenolics, ketones). The results were filtered using an adjusted p-value of 0.05 [Benjamini-Hochberg correction].

Amino acids and derivatives

Thirteen metabolites were negatively correlated with bacterial diversity, while twenty-nine were positively correlated with fungal diversity (Fig. 5A). Thus, bacterial and fungal Shannon indices showed contrasting correlation directions with amino acids and their derivatives.

Nucleic-acid-related compounds

Metabolites related to nucleic-acid metabolism commonly function as microbial growth-promoting factors or precursors of cofactors. Four metabolites (methanolphosphate, 5,6-dihydrouracil, pyrophosphoric acid, phosphate) were negatively associated with bacterial diversity; five metabolites (phosphate, adenine, urea, thymine, uracil) were positively associated with fungal diversity, whereas N-carbamoylaspartate was negatively associated (Fig. 5B).

Alcohols and organic acids

Eleven alcohols and nineteen organic acids were negatively correlated with bacterial diversity, while thirteen alcohols and nineteen organic acids were positively correlated with fungal diversity (Fig. 5C and D).

Sugars and derivatives

Six saccharides (α-lactose, tagatose, ribose, fructose, dihydroxyacetone, N-acetyl-galactosamine) were negatively correlated with bacterial diversity. For fungal diversity, seven saccharides were positively correlated and seven were negatively correlated (Fig. 5E). Positive correlations included fructose-6-phosphate, dihydroxyacetone, N-acetyl-galactosamine, N-acetylmannosamine-minor, glucosaminic acid, galactose-6-phosphate and an unidentified trisaccharide; negative correlations involved isomaltose, inulotriose, maltotriose, β-gentiobiose, galactose, lactose and trehalose. Thus, associations between fungal diversity and sugars were bidirectional and metabolite-specific.

Other metabolites (esters, amines, phenolics, ketones)

Nine compounds were negatively correlated with bacterial diversity. Six compounds, mainly amines, esters, and ketones, were positively correlated with fungal diversity, whereas two were negatively correlated (Fig. 5F).

In summary, bacterial and fungal Shannon index showed contrasting correlation patterns with multiple metabolite classes in the studied batch.

Correlation network reveals taxon-specific associations between key microorganisms and free small-molecule metabolites

To further elucidate associations between free metabolites and the top 20 dominant microorganisms, a correlation network was constructed (|r| > 0.7, FDR-adjusted p < 0.05; Fig. 6).

Correlation network between dominant microbial species (top 20) and six categories of metabolites (|r| > 0.7, p < 0.05). Nodes represent microbial species or metabolites, and edges indicate significant positive or negative correlations. The results were filtered using an adjusted p-value of 0.05 [Benjamini-Hochberg correction].

For amino acids and derivatives (Fig. 6A), L. homohiochii, Saccharomyces sp., and Thermoascus aurantiacus showed the highest numbers of significant positive edges (14, 19 and 10, respectively). Streptococcus gallolyticus subsp. macedonicus and L. delbrueckii displayed 16 and 21 negative edges, respectively. Notably, the congeneric pair L. homohiochii and L. delbrueckii showed opposite association patterns.

The network for nucleic acid-related compounds is shown in Fig. 6B. S. gallolyticus subsp. macedonicus, L. delbrueckii and Saccharomyces sp. had the largest numbers of significant correlations with these metabolites. S. gallolyticus subsp. macedonicus and L. delbrueckii exhibited four and three negative edges, respectively, whereas Saccharomyces sp. displayed three positive edges. Among the metabolites, adenine, phosphate, uracil, urea, and thymine were correlated with a broader range of microorganisms. Adenine correlated with five taxa; phosphate and uracil with four each; urea and thymine with three each. These metabolites were therefore among the most highly connected nodes in the network.

The correlation network for alcohols revealed extensive and taxon-specific interactions (Fig. 6C). L. delbrueckii, L. homohiochii, S. gallolyticus subsp. macedonicus, L. citreum, Enterococcus mundtii, Brevibacterium linens and Saccharomyces sp. were the most highly connected taxa. L. delbrueckii exhibited ten significant negative edges, whereas L. homohiochii displayed eight positive edges. S. gallolyticus subsp. macedonicus showed eight edges (one positive, seven negative), L. citreum, E. mundtii and B. linens each showed four negative edges, and Saccharomyces sp. showed four edges (three positive, one negative). Among the metabolites, threitol, mesoerythritol, sorbitol, and sorbitol 6-phosphate were correlated with a wider range of microorganisms. Specifically, threitol showed significant negative correlations with eight species; mesoerythritol was negatively correlated with seven species; and both sorbitol and sorbitol 6-phosphate were significantly correlated with five species.

The correlation network between organic acids and the dominant microorganisms is depicted in Fig. 6D. All highly connected taxa were bacteria: L. citreum (eight negative edges), L. homohiochii (seven positive edges), S. gallolyticus subsp. macedonicus (seven negative edges), L. delbrueckii, E. mundtii and B. linens (six negative edges each). Metabolites serving as network hubs were glycolic acid (seven edges), 2-hydroxyhexanoic acid (six edges), and 3-phenyllactic acid, 3,4-dihydroxybenzoate, quinic acid and isohexonic acid (five edges each).

A broader spectrum of saccharides and their derivatives exhibited significant associations with the dominant microorganisms S. gallolyticus subsp. macedonicus, Saccharomyces sp., L. homohiochii, and L. delbrueckii (Fig. 6E). Specifically, S. gallolyticus subsp. macedonicus exhibited 10 significant correlations (5 positive, 5 negative), while Saccharomyces sp. showed 8 (3 positive, 5 negative). L. homohiochii showed seven correlations (3 positive, 4 negative), and L. delbrueckii with 6 (1 positive, 5 negative). Among the metabolites, β-gentiobiose, inulotriose, isomaltose, maltotriose, and fructose-6-phosphate were markedly associated with a larger number of microbial taxa. β-Gentiobiose and inulotriose each correlated significantly with six microbial species; isomaltose and maltotriose each showed significant relationships with five species; fructose-6-phosphate was significantly associated with four species.

Other compound classes exhibited the largest numbers of significant correlations with S. gallolyticus subsp. macedonicus and L. delbrueckii (Fig. 6F). S. gallolyticus subsp. macedonicus exhibited six significant correlations, consisting of one positive and five negative relationships, whereas L. delbrueckii displayed five significant negative correlations with the annotated compounds. Among the metabolites, putrescine, xylonolactone, 4-hydroxymandelate, β-mannosylglycerate, and hydroxylamine demonstrated significant correlations with a greater number of microorganisms. Putrescine was significantly correlated with six microorganisms; Xylonolactone, 4-Hydroxymandelate, and β-Mannosylglycerate were each significantly correlated with four microorganisms; and Hydroxylamine was significantly correlated with three microorganisms. These compounds were associated with a broader set of taxa than the other metabolites in this network.

Temporal profiles were subsequently examined for 17 microorganism-metabolite pairs selected from the significant correlations (Fig. 7). L. homohiochii showed significant positive correlations with nine metabolites: tryptophan, pyroglutamic acid, isoleucine, xylonolactone, dihydroxyacetone, 2-hydroxyhexanoic acid, urea, fructose-6-phosphate, and 3-phenyllactic acid. The kinetic curves of L. homohiochii and these nine metabolites were highly synchronized during fermentation (Fig. 7A–I), consistent with the significant positive correlations. L. homohiochii was significantly negatively correlated with maltotriose. As shown in Fig. 7J, maltotriose decreased gradually during fermentation, while the abundance of L. homohiochii increased, consistent with their negative correlation. Saccharomyces sp. showed a significant positive correlation with uracil, glucosaminic acid, thymine, and quinic acid. These metabolites and Saccharomyces sp. initially increased and subsequently decreased, although the Saccharomyces sp. profile peaked later than the metabolite profiles (Fig. 7K–N). Rhizopus microsporus was positively correlated with quinic acid and xylonolactone, and its temporal profile was similarly delayed relative to those metabolites (Fig. 7O and P). B. linens was negatively correlated with glycolic acid, and their temporal profiles showed opposite trends (Fig. 7Q). These temporal covariations were consistent with the corresponding correlations.

Discussion

In this study, we characterized the microbial community during Light-flavor Baijiu fermentation at the species level. L. brevis and L. homohiochii dominated the bacterial community, while S. fibuligera, R. arrhizus, Saccharomyces sp., and I. orientalis were the core fungi. L. homohiochii showed 14 positive correlations with amino acids, suggesting it may contribute to amino acid availability through proteolytic activity (Pereira et al., 2001). This taxon was also positively correlated with alcohols and organic acids, consistent with its flavor metabolite synthesis capabilities (Zhao & Gu, 2019).

The dominant fungi putatively assigned here differed from those in sauce-flavor (Aspergillus, Pichia; Zhang et al., 2021) and strong-flavor Baijiu (Kazachstania, Issatchenkia; Liu et al., 2023), suggesting that fungal composition variation contributes to distinct aroma profiles across Baijiu types. The fungal community plays a crucial role in the fermentation process of Baijiu, particularly in the synthesis and biotransformation of alcohols, esters, and aromatic compounds (Yi et al., 2019). In this study, the dominant fungi in the Jiupei from Day 3 until the end of fermentation were S. fibuligera, R. arrhizus, and Saccharomyces sp. Among these, S. fibuligera is recognized as one of the most proficient yeast species in ester production and amylase secretion (Bo et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2017; Zhang et al., 2014). R. arrhizus is widely present in diverse fermented foods, capable of utilizing diverse carbon and nitrogen sources while producing a variety of amylases and hydrolases. This enables it to perform saccharification and acidification functions during fermentation, providing essential substrates for subsequent ethanol and flavor precursor production by yeasts (Wang et al., 2020). Saccharomyces sp. exhibits strong fermentative capacity and ethanol tolerance. During the mid to late fermentation stages, it contributes to aroma formation through amino acid metabolism and higher alcohol synthesis (Dong et al., 2024). Compared with our findings, the composition of dominant fungi differs in the fermentation systems of other Baijiu aroma types. For instance, the primary dominant fungi in sauce-flavor Baijiu fermentation include Aspergillus, Pichia, and Thermomyces (Zhang et al., 2021), whereas the strong-flavor Baijiu system is predominantly characterized by Kazachstania, Issatchenkia, and Aspergillus (Liu et al., 2023). The variation in dominant fungal composition across different Baijiu aroma types is likely one of the key factors contributing to differences in the composition of aroma substances and their distinct flavor profiles.

Within the studied batch, fungal diversity was significantly correlated with physicochemical parameters, whereas bacterial diversity showed no significant associations. Differences in functional redundancy may provide one possible explanation for this pattern (Tan et al., 2025). Fungal diversity showed predominantly positive correlations with metabolites, whereas bacterial diversity showed predominantly negative correlations. These contrasting patterns were broadly consistent with previous correlation-based studies (Du et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023; Ren et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2018). The observed associations may also partly reflect shared temporal changes in microbial diversity and metabolite abundance.

Metabolomics analysis showed that the Jiupei metabolite profile changed markedly after fermentation initiation. During the first 7 days, the profile exhibited substantial temporal variation, whereas samples from days 11 to 24 clustered relatively closely. A further shift occurred between days 24 and 28. These temporal patterns coincided with microbial succession and changes in lactic acid, ethanol, and pH. Low pH and increasing ethanol can constrain sensitive microorganisms in fermentation systems (Sangha et al., 2024; Wade, 2021), providing possible context for the concurrent community and metabolite patterns. Interestingly, from day 24 to day 28, the metabolic profile changed significantly again. This may be related to continued fluctuations in microbial community structure during this period. Although samples after day 11 generally clustered together, both bacterial and fungal community analyses showed that the day-28 sample was relatively distant from other post-day-11 samples (Fig. 1C, D). This suggests that microbial community dynamics during this final stage still play a key role in shaping flavor compounds and metabolite accumulation. In addition, metabolic pathway enrichment analysis identified galactose metabolism, as well as starch and sucrose metabolism, as the most significantly altered pathways during fermentation. Galactose metabolism, primarily via the Leloir pathway, converts galactose into glucose-6-phosphate for entry into glycolysis and energy metabolism systems. This pathway represents an important mechanism for efficient sugar utilization by microorganisms (Frey, 1996). In the fermentation system, the presence of galactose can enhance the metabolic activity of LAB and yeast, promoting the production of key flavor compounds such as lactic acid, ethanol, and organic acids. Therefore, rational regulation of galactose content in the fermentation substrate may facilitate synergistic metabolism between LAB and yeast, ultimately improving fermentation efficiency and flavor quality.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Due to the correlation-based and time-series design, the observed microbe–metabolite associations cannot be interpreted as direct causal relationships and may partly reflect shared temporal dynamics during fermentation. Therefore, future studies incorporating culture-based validation, synthetic community experiments, and viability-targeted approaches (e.g., RNA-based sequencing or PMA pretreatment) are needed for mechanistic confirmation. In addition, the present study was based on a single fermentation batch, and species-level resolution may remain limited for some closely related taxa despite the use of long-read sequencing.

Conclusion

Integrated analyses characterized temporal relationships among physicochemical variables, microbial communities, and non-volatile metabolites in the studied Dacha Jiupei fermentation batch. L. brevis and L. homohiochii as dominant bacteria and S. fibuligera, R. arrhizus, Saccharomyces sp. and I. orientalis as core fungi at species resolution. Fungal α-diversity was strongly associated with multiple physicochemical variables, while bacterial and fungal diversity showed contrasting associations with different metabolite classes. The non-volatile metabolite profile changed rapidly after fermentation initiation, remained relatively similar from days 11 to 24, and shifted further between days 24 and 28. Pathway enrichment highlighted galactose metabolism and starch and sucrose metabolism in relation to these temporal changes. At the putative species level, L. homohiochii and Saccharomyces sp. showed positive correlations with amino acid derivatives, alcohols, and organic acids. These findings provide association-based clues and testable hypotheses regarding microbial succession and non-volatile metabolite dynamics, advancing our understanding of traditional solid-state fermented foods.
