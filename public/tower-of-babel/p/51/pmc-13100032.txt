Managing salinity stress through microclimate control to enhance tomato productivity in arid regions
Authors: Farhat Abbas, Salem Al-Naemi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGiven the increasing reliance on saline groundwater in arid regions, it is essential to assess how interacting abiotic stresses affect tomato growth, physiological performance, and productivity under different production environments. This study aimed to evaluate selected abiotic factors (including light, temperature, humidity, and irrigation water salinity), typical of Middle Eastern groundwater, that interact to influence morpho-physiological traits and yield of tomato grown under greenhouse, net house, and open field systems. Tomato plants were cultivated under the three selected environments (greenhouse, net house, and open field), combined with four salinity levels (2–5 dS m⁻1). Growth, yield, and physiological traits were measured, including plant height, stem diameter, leaf number, fruit characteristics, yield per plant, and leaf chlorophyll indicators (SPAD and NDVI). Treatment effects were assessed statistically. Growth and yield traits exhibited significant interactive effects of environment and salinity, whereas SPAD and NDVI responded independently to each factor. Net house conditions under low salinity produced the highest fruit weight and yield, while higher salinity generally reduced performance, particularly under greenhouse conditions. Fruit weight showed strong positive relationships with vegetative growth parameters and fruit diameter. Increasing salinity consistently reduced SPAD and NDVI values, indicating declining photosynthetic capacity. Despite yield reductions under salinity stress, controlled environments partially mitigated physiological decline, with higher chlorophyll indices observed under low-salinity greenhouse conditions. SPAD and NDVI were positively correlated, confirming their consistency as indicators of plant physiological status. Salinity stress negatively affected tomato growth, physiology, and yield, with responses strongly modulated by environmental conditions. Optimizing both salinity management and growing environment is essential for sustaining tomato productivity in arid regions and controlled-environment agriculture systems.Keywords: Environmental stress, Fruit yield, SPAD, NDVI, Salinity levelsSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Physiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Tomatoes (Solanum lycopersicum L.) stand among the top globally consumed foods in their fresh or processed forms1. Their nutritional value2–5 and dietary nature6 stimulate a healthy body and prevent weight gain and human chronic diseases7,8. Despite record production of 70, 20, and 12 million tons of tomatoes respectively in China, India, and the United States during 20239, the soil and water salinity continues to affect their production adversely10,11 and causes abandonment of millions of hectares of arable lands12,13. However, tomato plants are considered moderately salt-tolerant within an electrical conductivity (EC) range of 1.3–6.0 dS/m as they are cultivated in arid environments exposed to salinity conditions14.

The effect of salinity stress on morpho-physiological (i.e., plant growth variables, chlorophyll content, normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), and yield traits of horticultural crops has therefore been the interest of recent work15–19. Soil Plant Analysis Development (SPAD) values have been related to NDVI to assess plant growth and health20,21. SPAD meters provide a direct, non-destructive measurement of leaf chlorophyll content, which is closely linked to nitrogen concentration in plant tissues22. In contrast, NDVI values estimate vegetation vigor by quantifying the differential reflectance of near-infrared and red light23. Despite the differences in measurement scale and method, numerous studies have demonstrated significant correlations between SPAD values and NDVI, reflecting their shared basis in chlorophyll content and photosynthetic activity.

The soil and groundwater salinity and various meteorological factors exert abiotic stress on crop growth. All types of crops cultivated across the Middle East experience these stresses, which continuously pose threats to agricultural productivity. Groundwater salinization in Middle Eastern aquifers is increasingly reported and is largely driven by intensive groundwater abstraction for agriculture. In the United Arab Emirates, groundwater salinity increased by approximately threefold in the southern and northern regions and twofold in the central region between 1985 and 199624,25. In Qatar, non-peer-reviewed sources have suggested an annual groundwater salinity increase of approximately 5% associated with aquifer over-exploitation; however, this estimate has not been validated in the scientific literature. Salinity poses a significant constraint on crop production across Qatar. The Aruma aquifer in southwestern Qatar, covering approximately 1,985 km2 (about16% of the national area), is classified as saline, with groundwater salinity of approximately 5 ppt26. Qatar’s main basins include the Northern, Central, and Southern basins, have low to high salinity levels increasing toward the South27. Agriculture is practiced in the Northern Basin with a salinity of 500–3000 pmm (~ 0.9–5.45 dS/m). It covers ~ 19% of Qatar at 10 to 40 m of water depth. Despite the recognition of groundwater and soil salinity as major and growing challenges to agriculture in Qatar, quantitative estimates of the annual extent of agricultural land affected by salinization are not currently available.

Hypothetically, it is challenging to mitigate salinity stress on crop productivity. However, controlling certain meteorological factors, under controlled environment agriculture (CEA), may result in finding optimum conditions for growing horticultural crops with minimal salinity28,29. Above 90% of greenhouse crop production in Qatar occurs under CEA30. The greenhouse ventilation can mitigate prevailing ambient air temperature and relative humidity. Under CEA, the controlled ambient air temperature and humidity can lower soil and plant temperatures, leading to favourable cultivation conditions in arid environments31–33.

Salinity affects plant growth and development through several interconnected physiological mechanisms, primarily osmotic stress, ion toxicity, and oxidative stress34. High salt concentrations in the soil lower the soil water potential, making it difficult for plants to absorb water, leading to osmotic stress and reduced cell expansion and growth35. Excessive uptake of sodium (Na⁺) and chloride (Cl⁻) ions can cause ion toxicity, disrupting nutrient balance, enzyme activity, and membrane integrity36. In addition, salinity induces oxidative stress by increasing the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which can damage proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids37. Together, these stresses impair photosynthesis, metabolism, and overall plant development, ultimately reducing crop productivity38.

Literature reports work on the effect of salinity on tomato growth. Alharbi et al.36 studied the effects of saline water and irrigation management on the growth and yield of tomato under greenhouse conditions. They reported that the total tomato yield was less affected by saline water with better water management techniques, such as the use of sub-surface drip irrigation. Segura et al.39 evaluated the effects of 0.6 and 2.2 dS/m EC-based salinity levels of irrigation water and nitrogen–phosphorous–potassium (NPK) rates-based fertigation on tomato fruit quality and yield grown under a polyethylene greenhouse. They recorded a higher yield and quality of tomatoes grown under irrigation water EC of 2.2 dS/m. The yield of greenhouse tomatoes decreased with increased salinity levels (2.2, 6.3, and 10.2 dS/m) as reported by Rodríguez-Ortega et al.40. Salinity levels of 0.9, 2.25, and 3.60 dS/m were tested with deficit irrigation water treatments for their effects on physiological responses and yield of a Middle Eastern greenhouse tomato by Alshami et al.41, who found no significant difference in the yield of tomatoes from freshwater and 80% deficit irrigation of 3.60 dS/m saline water. Overall, the literature reports a decrease in the tomato fruit yield with increases in salinity levels42–46.

Long-term and continued nature of this study

Growers in arid regions of the Middle East employ a wide spectrum of controlled environment agriculture (CEA) systems, ranging from fully controlled smart greenhouses to semi-controlled net houses. Al-Naemi and Al-Otoom31 reported the development of a CEA facility in Qatar, while Abbas et al.47 experimentally and environmentally assessed tomato performance under fully controlled greenhouses, semi-controlled net houses, and open fields combined with inorganic, organic, and mixed fertilizer regimes. Their results, supported by life cycle assessment, showed superior plant performance in semi-controlled net houses under mixed fertilization. Nevertheless, a fully controlled greenhouse was identified as potentially advantageous when equipped with appropriate supplemental lighting, particularly due to its capacity for year-round production.

These recommendations informed subsequent work by Abbas et al.48, who examined eggplant production under different CEA systems and nutrient sources. Consistent with earlier findings, semi-controlled net houses proved economically viable, energy-efficient, and environmentally favorable. To address productivity limitations, Abbas et al.49 evaluated supplemental LED lighting strategies, demonstrating that red LED lighting enhanced eggplant growth. The use of red and white LEDs is critically important in greenhouse crop cultivation experiments as they allow precise control of light quality, intensity, and photoperiod, which directly influence plant growth, physiology, and yield. Red LEDs are highly efficient in driving photosynthesis and regulating key developmental processes such as stem elongation, flowering, and fruit set through their strong absorption by chlorophyll and involvement in phytochrome-mediated responses. White LEDs, which provide a broad spectrum like natural sunlight, support balanced plant development by enhancing leaf morphology, pigment synthesis, and overall plant architecture, while also improving visual assessment of plant health. Together, these studies highlight trade-offs among CEA systems and underscore the role of optimized lighting and nutrient management in improving crop performance under arid conditions.

Although salinity and irrigation effects on crops are well documented, systematic studies integrating salinity management with controlled abiotic factors are lacking. This study aimed to evaluate the combined effects of light intensity, temperature, and humidity on morpho-yield and physiological traits of greenhouse-grown tomato under typical Middle Eastern groundwater salinity conditions. This led to the main objective of this study, which was to explicate how key abiotic factors, namely light intensity, temperature, and relative humidity, interact with prevailing salinity levels typical of Middle Eastern groundwater to influence the morpho-yield performance and physiological traits of greenhouse-grown tomatoes. Specifically, this study quantified the individual and combined effects of these environmental variables on plant growth, phenological development, photosynthetic efficiency, and yield components under CEA conditions. It was hypothesized that optimizing these abiotic factors could partially alleviate salinity-induced stress and enhance tomato performance in arid and semi-arid greenhouse production systems.

The novelty of this study lies in its integrated, multi-environment assessment of tomato responses to interacting abiotic stresses typical of arid regions, particularly groundwater salinity. Unlike previous studies that examine salinity or environment in isolation, this research systematically generates comparative data across greenhouse, net house, and open field systems under realistic Middle Eastern salinity levels. By jointly analyzing growth, physiological indicators (SPAD and NDVI), and yield relationships, the study identifies environment-specific salinity thresholds and mitigation effects, providing practical, evidence-based recommendations for optimizing tomato production under saline groundwater and arid climate conditions. This study aims to investigate the effects of salinity and meteorological stresses on tomato growth, yield, and physiological responses under controlled, semi-controlled, and uncontrolled environmental conditions in Qatar. It integrates the evaluation of morphological, yield, and physiological parameters, including SPAD, NDVI, light intensity, and soil substrate temperature and moisture measurements. By offering a comprehensive understanding of salinity stress, the study seeks to identify effective mitigation strategies tailored to Qatar’s arid environment, contributing to the development of sustainable agricultural practices for greenhouse cultivation in saline conditions.

Materials and methods

Study site and trials

The trials reported here took place at the University of Doha for Science and Technology, Qatar (approximately 25.276987° N latitude and 51.520008° E longitude). The site has arid environments and saline soil and groundwater conditions. Qatar has hot and humid summers and mild winters with annual precipitation as low as 80 mm/year. The summer temperatures exceed 40°C in June-July, winter temperatures are lower than 15 °C in January–February, and maximum humidity during summer exceeds 65%. These conditions are challenging for field agriculture, compelling for food production under CEA in most of the Middle Eastern region.

Trials for this study evaluated the effects of abiotic stress factors, including salinity levels and CEA microclimate, including temperature and relative humidity, on morpho-physiological and yield traits of tomato (Lycopersicon esculentum L). The CEA treatments included plant growing conditions of a fully controlled-environment smart greenhouse (E1), semi controlled-environment net house (E2), and an uncontrolled open field to serve as a control (E3). The E1 treatment used red-spectrum LED lights, consistent temperature and humidity levels controlled with air conditioning and exhaust fans. Literature reports that an elevation in photosynthetic efficiency proved beneficial in improving the growth of the plants. The E2 treatment was a traditional greenhouse that allowed natural light exposure while providing some protection from harsh outdoor conditions. The E3 treatment exposed plants to natural environmental conditions, including sunlight, fluctuating temperature, humidity, and winds.

The early maturing tomato variety SV4129TH Seminis (Beej Ghar, Reg. No. SW/MoA/4971/07, Kapaliya Patti, Comilla, 3500, Bangladesh), used in Abbas et al.47 and Dash et al.50, which produces intermediate-sized tomatoes51, was used in all treatments. Among common tomato genotypes cultivated in Qatar, SV4129TH was preferred over Velocity and Sigma as SV4129TH is characterized as an early-maturing, vigorous, indeterminate fresh-market cultivar with strong plant vigor, compact growth habit, and good fruit quality and disease resistance50.

Five (5) liter pots were used to grow 3-week-old tomato seedlings on 05-Oct-24. Tomato seedlings were obtained from a commercial nursery in Qatar, which grew tomato seedlings in controlled conditions using well-aerated and sterile growing media, such as peat-based substrates, perlite, and vermiculite to provide optimal water-holding capacity, drainage, and root aeration. Seeds of SV4129TH were sown in plastic seedling trays with 128 cells. Following germination, seedlings were supplied with nutrients through overhead misting to deliver a dilute, balanced nutrient solution to support early root and shoot growth.

Each experimental pot was filled with 3.5 kg of peat moss and randomly distributed within each environment-control setup (E1, E2, and E3). Four salinity treatments, i.e., S1, S2, S3, and S4 were established by adjusting the EC of the irrigation water to specific levels using sodium chloride (NaCl) solution levels of 2, 3, 4, and 5 dS/m, respectively. For example, a salinity / EC level of 1 dS/m was achieved by adding 1 g of NaCl to 1 L of irrigation water52,53. The required salinity levels for respective salinity level treatments (S1, S2, S3, and S4) were consistently maintained by regularly applying saline solutions and irrigation water based on calculated plant growth and water uptake requirements54. The S1 (2 dS/m), S2 (3 dS/m) S3 (4 dS/m), and S4 (5 dS/m) salinity level treatments corresponded to Lawler et al.27 values of ~ 0.9 – 5.45 dS/m specific for the Northern Basin of Qatar.

Altogether, there were 48 experimental pots (4 salinity levels × 3 environment controls × 4 replications) arranged under a Completely Randomized Factorial Design (CRD Factorial). A CRD Factorial Design is used in factorial experiments to test combinations of multiple factor levels. In this study, the combined effects of S and E factors (i.e., 4 salinity levels: S1, S2, S3, and S4, and 3 environment controls: E1, E2, and E3) were evaluated on morpho-physiological and yield traits of tomato plants that were completely randomized as shown in Fig. 1.

Schematic of experimental design showing four replications (R1: Replication 1, R2: Replication 2, R3: Replication 3, and R4: Replication 4) of four electrical conductivity (EC) based salinity levels (S1: salinity with an EC of 2 dS/m, S2: salinity with an EC of 3 dS/m, S3: salinity with an EC of 4 dS/m, and S4: salinity with an EC of 5 dS/m) completely randomized in three environmental controls (E1: greenhouse, E2: net house, E3: open field) under a completely randomized factorial experimental design.

The daily light integral (DLI) is pivotal to plant growth and supports plant rooting, stem, branching, and flowering. It was measured to be about 7 mol/m2/day in the greenhouse, 17 mol/m2/day in the net house, and 32 mol/m2/day in the open field.

Crop husbandry and cultural practices

Irrigation water was scheduled and applied daily using a drip irrigation system from 7:00 AM to 4:00 PM. The saline solution was applied every Sunday until the first harvest to maintain the salinity treatment levels. Solution concentration was measured using an EC meter. The experimental pots had a single main stem of the plants, of which about one-third of the bottom leaves were pruned, but suckers were not removed. At the stage of fruit setting, the plants were trained to bear fruit without bending. Cluster pruning and topping were not conducted to maintain the local practice of tomato cultivation in Qatar. Disease and insect attacks were regularly monitored and not found. Yellow sticky traps were used to monitor and manage flying insects. Light hoeing was conducted in each pot on a fortnightly basis.

Experimental variable monitoring and data collection

The WatchDog 1650 Micro Stations (Spectrum Technologies, Aurora, IL 60,504), installed in all three experiment-control treatments (E1, E2, and E3) were configured to measure ambient air temperature, relative humidity, and soil temperature. The Micro Stations were equipped with a built-in data logger to record data from external sensors. Temperature sensors were installed at a 1.5 m height and in the soil pots (to measure plant root zone temperature), and humidity sensors were built in the stations of all environment-control treatments for relative humidity facing experimental plants. The data collected by these sensors was periodically downloaded to a laptop using the SpecWare Pro software application, which was connected to the WatchDog 1650 Micro Station via a data transmission cable (3661U).

For the morphological parameters data, plant height was measured using a measuring tape. The height measurements were taken at the flowering stage from the base of the plant to the tip of its stem. A digital Vernier caliper (Altraco Inc., Sausalito, California, USA) was used to measure plant stem diameter at 5 cm from the plant base. The plant leaf development was monitored by measuring the total number of fully expanded leaves per plant. Yield parameters included fruit count (number), fruit diameter (cm), fruit weight (g/fruit), fruit yield (kg/plant), and plant biomass (kg/plant). The fruit length and diameter were measured at their longest and widest points using a digital ruler and caliper, respectively, at the harvest stage. The fruit harvesting started on 24-Jan-25, 14-Jan-25, and 12-Jan-25 and the fourth/last harvesting ended on 05-Feb-25, 01-Feb-25, and 26-Jan-25 in the greenhouse, net house, and open space, respectively. Fruit weights were recorded using a digital balance with ± 2 mg error. Plant biomass was assessed by weighing the fresh plant biomass immediately after the final harvest.

The measurements of plant physiological parameters included measuring SPAD values at the fruiting stage using a SPAD-502 Plus Chlorophyll Meter (Konica Minolta Optics, Japan). The plant leaf NDVI values were also recorded during the fruiting stage using a handheld CM 1000 NDVI Meter. Light intensity, which indicates the availability of light for photosynthesis, was recorded at the fruiting stage using a quantum light sensor at the plant canopy height. All SPAD and NDVI readings were taken from 10:00 a.m. to 11:00 a.m. on the same day. Five values of SPAD and NDVI were individually averaged for each measurement for accuracy.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics v28.01 was employed to analyze the data collected55. A two-way ANOVA examined the individual and interactive effects of experimental treatments on dependent variables. Statistical significance was defined as P ≤ 0.05 (with 95% confidence); results above that threshold were deemed non-significant. Where significant differences were found, mean values were compared using Tukey’s Least Significant Difference (LSD) test. Means sharing the same or overlapping LSD letter labels were not significantly different from each other.

The study results clearly demonstrated that plant growth, yield components, and biomass production were strongly influenced by the interaction between growing environment and salinity stress. Across all treatments, optimal growth and yield were recorded under low salinity conditions, while progressive salinity stress (5 dS m⁻1) significantly suppressed plant performance.

Plant height was maximized under the greenhouse control treatment (E1S1), whereas the shortest plants were observed under open field conditions subjected to the highest salinity stress (E3S4). The approximately 24% reduction in plant height under severe salinity stress highlights the inhibitory effects of high salt concentrations on cell elongation and division. The comparatively greater plant height observed in greenhouse conditions may be partly attributed to altered light quality and intensity, encouraging stem elongation as plants search for supplemental light. In contrast, plants grown in the open field were exposed to sufficient natural light; however, the combined effect of environmental exposure and high salinity stress restricted vertical growth, suggesting that salinity stress outweighed any potential benefits of higher light availability.

Stem diameter followed a similar trend, with thicker stems recorded under control treatments and a marked reduction under high salinity stress. The 46% decrease in stem diameter under 5 dS m⁻1 indicates substantial impairment of vascular development and assimilate transport under saline conditions. Reduced stem girth under stress is commonly associated with osmotic stress and ion toxicity, which limit cambial activity and structural growth.

Leaf production was also negatively affected by increasing salinity across all environments, reflecting the sensitivity of leaf initiation and expansion to salt stress. However, the net house environment consistently supported superior leaf development, producing approximately 12% more leaves than both greenhouse and open field conditions. This suggests that the semi-controlled microclimate of the net house—moderating temperature, radiation, and evapotranspiration—provided more favorable conditions for leaf growth under saline stress, thereby mitigating some of the adverse effects of salinity.

Fruit diameter and fruit weight declined with increasing salinity in most environment-salinity combinations, confirming that reproductive growth is particularly vulnerable to salt stress. Among the environments, the net house produced the largest fruits and the highest fruit weights, followed by the open field and greenhouse. The superior performance of the net house under low salinity conditions may be attributed to reduced environmental extremes and improved source–sink balance. Conversely, the smallest stem diameter and lowest fruit weight were recorded under the open field with the highest salinity stress, indicating compounded stress effects on assimilate allocation and fruit development.

Consistent with trends in fruit number and size, both fruit yield and total biomass decreased progressively with increasing salinity across all environments. The control treatment in the net house produced the highest fruit and biomass yields, emphasizing the advantage of a semi-controlled environment under non-saline conditions. In contrast, the lowest yields were observed under the highest salinity stress in the greenhouse. Compared with the lowest salinity treatment in the net house, high salinity in the greenhouse reduced fruit yield by approximately 51% and biomass yield by about 59%. The reduced yield observed in the fully controlled greenhouse may be partly explained by the spectral composition of the artificial lighting, particularly the dominance of red LED light56. Although red wavelengths are efficient for photosynthesis, red-enriched or monochromatic spectra can disrupt photomorphogenic regulation, leading to altered plant architecture and reduced reproductive performance. Insufficient blue and far-red light impair photoreceptor signaling, affecting stomatal function, flowering, and fruit set in tomato growth57. These effects may be further exacerbated under salinity stress, where balanced light spectra are critical for maintaining physiological stability and biomass allocation to tomato fruits58.

This substantial reduction suggests that greenhouse conditions, while favorable under optimal management, may exacerbate salinity stress due to higher temperatures and increased evaporative demand, leading to greater salt accumulation in the root zone.

Overall, the findings indicate that salinity stress significantly restricts vegetative growth, reproductive development, and yield, with the magnitude of impact strongly dependent on the growing environment. The net house emerged as the most resilient environment under both low and moderate stress conditions, while high salinity under greenhouse conditions proved most detrimental. These results highlight the importance of integrating appropriate environmental management with salinity control strategies to sustain crop productivity under saline conditions.

These findings accord the historical literature reports where salinity sourced from soil or irrigation reportedly affected tomato morph-yield traits, including root biomass decrease59, seed germination, plant growth variables, and yield factors60,61.

Abiotic stresses, including extreme temperature and salinity, were also reported to negatively affect plant growth and productivity of crops62, including tomato, whose optimal range of growing temperatures is very narrow under both low63 and high temperature ranges64. Higher tomato yield in net house treatment, where the temperature ranged from 20 to 30 °C is supported by the findings of Camejo et al.65, who recommended the tomato optimum growing temperatures from 25 to 30 °C during the daytime and as low as 20 °C at nighttime. Growing tomatoes beyond the lower and upper limits of optimum temperature can negatively affect the growth and yield of tomato fruits, as reported by literature65–68. The temperature of the environment-control treatments E1 and E3 fell beyond these optimum ranges, hence justifying a lower yield from these treatment plants than from that of E2 treatment.

Under the combined effects of environmental control and salinity levels, fruit weight directly depended on plant height, leaf count, plant stem diameter, and fruit diameter, showing strong relationships (Fig. 3). These relationships indicate correlation, not causation. Across greenhouse, net house, and open field conditions, both SPAD and NDVI values declined progressively with increasing salinity. This reduction suggests a salinity-induced decrease in chlorophyll concentration and canopy greenness, which are closely linked to photosynthetic capacity. High salinity is known to induce osmotic stress and ionic toxicity, leading to reduced chlorophyll synthesis, enhanced chlorophyll degradation, and impaired photosystem efficiency. Consequently, the observed decline in SPAD and NDVI values under higher salinity levels reflects a deterioration in photosynthetic function and overall plant health.

Among the treatments, plants grown in the open field under the lowest salinity level (S1) exhibited the highest SPAD readings (48.9 ± 1.60). This response may be attributed to exposure to natural sunlight, which likely enhanced chlorophyll accumulation and leaf photosynthetic activity under non-stress conditions. Adequate light intensity and spectral quality in the open field can stimulate chloroplast development and improve nitrogen use efficiency, thereby increasing chlorophyll content as measured by SPAD values.

NDVI values were also significantly affected by both environmental control and salinity level (P ≤ 0.05). Although NDVI declined with increasing salinity across all environments, higher NDVI values were consistently recorded in the fully controlled greenhouse environment (E1) compared with the net house and open field treatments. This suggests that controlled temperature, humidity, and radiation conditions in the greenhouse may have helped maintain greater canopy uniformity and leaf area, partially offsetting the negative effects of salinity on canopy development. In contrast, plants in the open field were exposed to fluctuating environmental conditions, which may have intensified the physiological stress imposed by salinity and further reduced NDVI values.

The observed reductions in SPAD and NDVI values under saline conditions are in agreement with earlier reports indicating that salinity stress limits leaf growth and photosynthetic activity through reductions in cellular turgor and stomatal conductance35. Decreased turgor pressure restricts cell expansion, while ionic imbalance disrupts chloroplast function, collectively resulting in reduced chlorophyll content and canopy vigor.

These results confirm that salinity stress significantly compromises plant physiological performance, as evidenced by declines in SPAD and NDVI values, and that the extent of this impact is modulated by the growing environment. While natural sunlight enhanced chlorophyll content under low salinity in the open field, the controlled greenhouse environment better sustained canopy greenness under increasing salinity. These findings highlight the importance of integrating environmental control strategies with salinity management to maintain photosynthetic efficiency and crop productivity under saline conditions.

As seen in Fig. 4, there was a strong positive relationship between SPAD chlorophyll readings and NDVI values across the tested environments and salinity treatments, with a coefficient of determination (R2 = 0.65). This finding indicates that a substantial proportion of the variation in canopy-level greenness (NDVI) can be explained by changes in leaf-level chlorophyll content (SPAD). The derived linear regression equation, NDVI = 0.008 × SPAD + 0.179, further supports the close physiological linkage between chlorophyll concentration and spectral reflectance in the red and near-infrared regions, both of which are sensitive to plant health and photosynthetic activity.

The observed correlation suggests that salinity-induced reductions in chlorophyll content were consistently reflected in decreased canopy vigor across environments. As salinity stress intensified reductions in leaf chlorophyll concentration likely due to limited light absorption and photosynthetic efficiency, resulting in lower NDVI values69. This coherence between SPAD and NDVI responses confirms that both indices are reliable indicators of plant stress status under varying environmental control and salinity conditions70.

However, despite the strong correlation observed in this study, the SPAD–NDVI relationship may not be universally linear or stable across all growth stages or environmental contexts71,72. NDVI measurements integrate signals from the entire canopy and are influenced by background reflectance from soil, variations in leaf area index (LAI), and canopy structure, which can partially decouple NDVI from leaf-level chlorophyll content73. In contrast, SPAD meters directly measure chlorophyll concentration at the leaf scale, and their measurements are less affected by canopy geometry or soil background, making them particularly sensitive to early physiological changes in plants induced by salinity stress.

Additionally, NDVI is known to exhibit saturation at high biomass or dense canopy conditions, where further increases in chlorophyll content or leaf area produce minimal changes in NDVI values74. Under such conditions, NDVI may underestimate variations in plant physiological status, whereas SPAD readings can still capture subtle changes in chlorophyll concentration. This limitation may explain deviations from perfect linearity and underscores the importance of interpreting NDVI values within the context of canopy development and growth stage.

Despite these limitations, the complementary nature of SPAD and NDVI is evident. NDVI offers rapid, non-destructive, and spatially extensive assessment of crop status, making it suitable for monitoring field-scale variability and temporal dynamics. SPAD measurements, while more localized, provide precise and sensitive indicators of leaf-level physiological condition. Their combined use enhances the robustness of plant health assessment, particularly under stress conditions such as salinity. Hammad et al.20 used NDVI measurements to evaluate various growth and yield parameters of maize and reported a positive and significant correlation of maize plant growth with the NDVI values.

The practical relevance of integrating SPAD and NDVI measurements is further supported by previous studies. For instance, Zhou and Yin75 demonstrated that SPAD and NDVI readings collected during early square to mid-bloom stages were effective predictors of cotton yield. Similarly, the strong association observed in the present study suggests that integrating proximal sensing (SPAD) with remote or proximal canopy sensing (NDVI) could improve stress detection, yield prediction, and decision-making in controlled and open-field production systems.

Canopy temperature

The responses of canopy temperature varied across growing environments and salinity levels, indicating a strong influence of environmental control and its interaction with salinity stress on plant water relations and physiological functioning. There were a significant effect of environment (P = 0.001) and a highly significant E × S interaction (P = 0.001), whereas salinity alone did not exert a statistically significant main effect (P = 0.18). This pattern suggests that the impact of salinity on canopy temperature was largely environment dependent.

As seen in Table 4, canopy temperature under greenhouse conditions was highest in the control treatment (E1S1; 28.05 ± 0.23 °C) and declined progressively with increasing salinity, reaching the lowest value under the highest salinity stress (E1S4; 21.25 ± 2.95 °C). Reduction in canopy temperature under severe salinity in the greenhouse may be associated with growth suppression and reduced canopy density, leading to greater exposure of underlying soil and altered leaf energy balance. Additionally, salinity-induced impairment of metabolic activity and reduced leaf area may have limited transpiration dynamics, thereby modifying canopy thermal characteristics. The relatively large variability observed in E1S4 also suggests heterogeneous stress responses under compounded greenhouse and salinity conditions.

In the net house environment, canopy temperature remained comparatively stable across salinity levels, ranging narrowly between 23.03 and 24.43 °C. Even under the highest salinity stress (E2S4), the canopy temperature values did not increase substantially, indicating that the semi-controlled microclimate of the net house moderated thermal stress. Reduced radiation load, moderated air temperature, and improved air circulation in the net house might have helped maintain transpiration and evaporative cooling, buffering the effects of salinity on canopy temperature. This thermal stability aligns with earlier observations of improved growth, physiological performance, and yield under net house conditions, highlighting its resilience under stress.

In contrast, the open field environment exhibited a clear increase in canopy temperature with increasing salinity. The highest canopy temperature across all treatments was recorded under E3S4 (29.75 ± 1.16 °C), while the control treatment (E3S1) maintained a lower temperature (24.83 ± 2.39 °C). Elevated canopy temperatures under high salinity in the open field can be attributed to salinity-induced stomatal closure, which restricts transpiration and reduces evaporative cooling. Under open field conditions, where plants are exposed to high solar radiation and fluctuating air temperatures, the reduction in transpiration exacerbates heat accumulation at the canopy level. This response is a well-recognized indicator of plant water stress and impaired physiological function.

Canopy temperature is a sensitive integrative indicator of plant stress, reflecting the combined effects of salinity, transpiration, and microclimate76,77. Lower and more stable canopy temperatures in the net house suggest better maintenance of plant water status and physiological activity under salinity stress, whereas elevated canopy temperatures in the open field under high salinity indicate pronounced stress. These findings reinforce the importance of environment-specific management strategies when using canopy temperature as a diagnostic tool for stress detection and irrigation or salinity management.

Canopy temperature is a well-established integrative indicator of plant water status, transpiration efficiency, and stress tolerance78. Its variation among environments and salinity levels in this study provides important physiological insights. Leaf and canopy temperature are primarily regulated by transpiration-driven evaporative cooling; thus, lower canopy generally reflects higher stomatal conductance and more efficient transpiration, whereas elevated canopy indicates stomatal closure and stress-induced limitations to water flux79.

Summary of the findings

Across all environments (greenhouse, net house, and open field) and salinity treatments, the results consistently demonstrate that increasing salinity stress negatively affected plant growth, physiological performance, and yield. These responses can be mechanistically explained by the fundamental physiological disruptions imposed by salinity stress, including osmotic stress, ion toxicity, nutrient imbalance, and oxidative damage.

At the vegetative level, reductions in plant height, stem diameter, and leaf number under high salinity (5 dS/m) reflect salinity-induced osmotic stress, which lowers soil water potential and restricts water uptake by roots. This limitation in water availability reduces cell turgor pressure, thereby constraining cell division and elongation, ultimately leading to stunted growth.

Physiological traits, including SPAD and NDVI, declined significantly with increasing salinity across all environments, indicating reduced chlorophyll content and canopy greenness. These changes are closely linked to ion toxicity and oxidative stress, as high salt concentrations promote the overproduction of ROS.

Environmental conditions moderate the magnitude of salinity effects. The net house environment supported relatively healthier vegetative growth, higher leaf numbers, and superior yield attributes under low to moderate salinity, likely due to reduced temperature extremes, moderated radiation, and lower evaporative demand. In contrast, greenhouse conditions, despite providing controlled growth parameters, exacerbated salinity effects under high stress, possibly due to higher temperatures and accelerated salt accumulation in the root zone, intensifying osmotic and ionic stress. Open field conditions benefited from natural sunlight, which enhanced chlorophyll content under low salinity, but plants were more vulnerable to combining salinity and environmental stresses under high salt levels.

Reproductive growth and yield were particularly sensitive to salinity stress. Declines in fruit size, fruit number, total yield, and biomass with increasing salinity can be attributed to reduced photosynthate availability and impaired source-sink relationships. Salinity-induced reductions in photosynthesis limit carbon assimilation, while oxidative stress and hormonal imbalances disrupt flower retention, fruit set, and fruit development. As a result, the highest yields were recorded under low salinity in the net house, whereas severe salinity in the greenhouse caused yield reductions exceeding 50%.

The strong positive relationship between SPAD and NDVI (R2 = 0.65) further confirms that salinity-induced physiological damage at the leaf level is reflected at the canopy scale. While NDVI integrates canopy structure and biomass, SPAD provides precise measurements of chlorophyll content, making them complementary tools for assessing plant health.

Under open field conditions, the progressive increase in canopy temperature with increasing salinity, culminating in the highest canopy temperature under E3S4 (29.75 °C), suggests a strong reduction in transpiration efficiency under severe salinity stress. High salt concentrations reduce root water uptake through osmotic stress and promote stomatal closure to minimize water loss. While this response conserves water, it also limits evaporative cooling, resulting in higher canopy temperatures. Elevated canopy temperature under salinity stress therefore reflects reduced transpiration efficiency and lower stress tolerance, particularly under high radiation and temperature conditions typical of the open field.

In contrast, the relatively stable and lower canopy temperatures observed in the net house across salinity levels indicate a more favorable balance between water uptake and transpiration. The moderated microclimate likely allowed plants to maintain stomatal function and transpiration even under saline conditions, enhancing evaporative cooling and sustaining lower canopy temperature. This thermal regulation is indicative of improved transpiration efficiency and greater stress tolerance, consistent with the superior growth, physiological performance, and yield observed in the net house treatments.

The greenhouse exhibited a distinct response, where the lowest canopy temperature was recorded under the highest salinity stress (E1S4). Rather than reflecting improved transpiration efficiency, this response likely resulted from severe growth suppression, reduced leaf area, and altered canopy architecture under combined greenhouse and salinity stress. A sparse or poorly developed canopy can reduce sensible heat accumulation and decouple canopy temperature from actual transpiration rates. Therefore, in such cases, lower canopy temperature does not necessarily indicate enhanced stress tolerance but may instead reflect physiological dysfunction and reduced metabolic activity.

These findings highlight that canopy temperature must be interpreted in the context of canopy structure, environmental conditions, and stress severity. In environments where canopy development is maintained, such as the net house, lower canopy temperature under salinity stress can be directly linked to efficient transpiration and greater stress tolerance. Conversely, elevated canopy temperature in the open field signals impaired transpiration and heightened stress sensitivity. Thus, integrating canopy temperature measurements with growth, yield, and physiological traits provides a powerful approach for evaluating transpiration efficiency and identifying stress-tolerant production systems for tomatoes under saline conditions.

Scalability, economic feasibility, CEA policy for commercial tomato production in arid regions

In arid regions such as Qatar and the broader Middle East, CEA (including greenhouses and net houses), offers a strategic approach to mitigating extreme heat, water scarcity, and soil salinity constraints for tomato production. Greenhouses provide precise environmental control, enabling higher yields and year-round production, while net houses offer partial protection at lower capital cost. However, the scalability of CEA is constrained by high initial investment in infrastructure, energy-intensive cooling and lighting systems, and the need for skilled labor. Operational costs, particularly for electricity and water, are significant in hot climates, affecting economic feasibility for small- to medium-scale producers. Adoption policies should therefore prioritize cost-effective technologies, such as hybrid net houses, passive cooling, LED light optimization, and efficient irrigation systems, alongside financial incentives or subsidies to lower entry barriers. Adoption of CEA can be profitable under high-value markets, while widespread adoption requires integrated support, including technical training, supply chain development, and access to affordable inputs. Government-subsidized operation could boost CEA and help achieve nations’ food security targets.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that effective management of both irrigation water salinity and production environments is critical for sustaining tomato growth, physiological performance, and yield under arid conditions. Increasing salinity consistently reduced vegetative growth, physiological health, and fruit yield; however, these negative effects were strongly moderated by the cultivation systems. Semi-controlled and controlled systems, particularly net houses and greenhouses equipped with red LED lighting, provided more favorable microclimatic conditions that enhanced plant height, biomass accumulation, fruit yield, and physiological indicators such as chlorophyll content and NDVI. From a practical standpoint, the results suggest that growers in arid regions relying on saline groundwater should prioritize net house or greenhouse production, especially when salinity levels are moderate to low. Net houses, in particular, offer a cost-effective strategy to mitigate salinity stress while maintaining high productivity, making them suitable for resource-limited growers. Routine monitoring of SPAD and NDVI can serve as a rapid, non-destructive decision-support tool to detect early stress and guide timely management interventions such as irrigation scheduling, salinity dilution, or microclimate adjustment. For open-field production, the findings highlight the need for improved salinity management practices, including blending water sources, leaching strategies, or the use of salt-tolerant cultivars. Limitations of this study include short-term experimentations and the use of only one genotype. Future research should focus on long-term field validation of these results, economic analyses of protected cultivation systems, and exploring genotype-environment-salinity interactions to further support the development of resilient tomato production systems under increasing abiotic stress. The controlled environment agriculture profitability in high-value markets requires technical support, supply chains, affordable inputs, and government-subsidized operations to enhance food security.
