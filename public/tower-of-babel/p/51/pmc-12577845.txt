Spontaneous rupture of pseudoaneurysm of testicular artery: a case report on an unusual presentation in dengue hemorrhagic syndrome
Authors: Suraj KC, Tek Nath Yogi, Shailesh Maharjan, Ashish Sapkota, Awaj Kafle, Rikesh Jung Karkee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF) is a mosquito-borne viral illness that can lead to severe bleeding complications. However, its association with the rupture of a testicular artery pseudoaneurysm due to orchitis is extremely rare. Testicular pseudoaneurysms are usually linked to trauma, infection, or inflammation. We report a unique case of spontaneous rupture of a testicular artery pseudoaneurysm in a DHF patient, leading to massive scrotal hematoma and hemorrhagic shock.Case presentation:A 69-year-old male with DHF presented with acute right scrotal pain, swelling, and extensive bruising. Doppler ultrasonography revealed a pseudoaneurysm in the right testicular artery. The patient developed hemorrhagic shock, necessitating blood transfusions. Emergency surgery revealed a necrotic testis and a large hematoma extending to the anterior abdominal wall. An orchidectomy was performed, and histopathology confirmed hemorrhagic necrosis with inflamed blood vessels.Discussion:Testicular pseudoaneurysms are rare, with spontaneous rupture being even more uncommon. While trauma and infection are recognized risk factors, dengue-associated vasculitis may have played a role in this case. The absence of prior testicular trauma or infection makes this presentation particularly unique.Conclusion:This case highlights a rare vascular complication of DHF. Clinicians should suspect testicular pseudoaneurysms in cases of acute scrotal pain and swelling, even without trauma. Early Doppler ultrasonography is crucial for diagnosis, and timely surgical intervention can be life-saving

Introduction

Dengue is a mosquito-borne arboviral disease commonly found in tropic and subtropic regions[1]. Dengue hemorrhagic fever (DHF) is characterized by high grade fever, myalgia, headache, maculopapular rash, leukopenia, and thrombocytopenia. Common hemorrhagic manifestations such as epistaxis, gum bleeding, gastrointestinal bleeding, hypermenorrhea, and hematuria have been well documented in literatures. However, its hemorrhagic association with testicular artery pseudoaneurysm has not been described in literature. Testicular pseudoaneurysm itself is rare entity, arising spontaneously or secondary to trauma, infection, or inflammation. It usually presents with acute scrotal pain, scrotal swelling, or hemorrhage precipitated by trauma. Spontaneous rupture of testicular pseudoaneurysm is extremely rare and has been first reported in 1914 with only two case reports till date[2]. Case history

A 69-year-old male, presented to emergency department with fever, myalgia, and headache for 7 days with pain and swelling of scrotum for 2 days. The patient had received levofloxacin, doxycycline, and paracetamol at local health center. Patient denied any recent history of trauma to the scrotum. Patient wasn’t on any anticoagulant. Patient did not have voiding difficulty or any history of lower urinary tract symptoms. There was no history suggestive of prior orchitis, prostatitis or STDs. The patient had history of COPD and was under MDI since last 5 years. There no significant past surgical history or any genitourinary instrumentations. On examination, patient was ill looking, pallor was present, tachycardia was present. Abdomen was soft and non-tender. Scrotum was enlarged with blackish discoloration. There was no discrete testicular mass palpable. Ecchymoses were present on bilateral flank, buttocks, and perineum as shown in Figures 1 and 2. No phenotype characteristics suggestive of testicular artery aneurysm such as Marfan syndrome or other connective tissue disorder were evident. Preoperative investigations showed Hb-5.5 g/dl, TLC-12250 per cc. The platelet count was within normal limit, and PT/INR was 23 sec/1.64. Dengue serology was positive. Tumor markers were negative. An emergency ultrasound examination revealed heterogeneous hypoechoic lesion of 2.6 cm × 1.6 cm in right inguinal region showing yin-yang type of flow on color Doppler which was in continuation of right testicular artery through a small communicating neck, which shows to and fro on spectral tracing – likely to be right testicular artery pseudoaneurysm. A well-defined heterogeneous predominantly echogenic area of ~4.8 cm × 2.8 cm with internal echoes and no vascularity was noted in right inguinal region extending into scrotal wall along with thickened and edematous scrotal skin and subcutaneous tissue. Right spermatic cord was bulky with heterogeneous echotexture and increased vascularity. Both testes were normal in shape and size. The patient’s clinical presentation and imaging findings were consistent with spontaneous rupture of testicular pseudoaneurysm. The pseudoaneurysm was located on the testicular artery extra-testicularly, later confirmed intraoperatively.

The patient was admitted and resuscitated. Four pints of PCV were transfused and Hb reached to 9.3 g/dl. Adequate blood products were arranged. Intravenous (IV) antibiotics and vitamin K were given. Patient and patient party were counselled and informed consent taken for scrotal exploration +/− orchidectomy. General anesthesia was given due to deranged PT/INR. Inguinal and scrotal incision were given. The hematoma was extending from scrotum to the space between external oblique aponeurosis and internal oblique muscle through inguinal canal. Testis was necrosed and non-viable. These findings can be visible in the operative findings as in Figure 3. About 1000 ml of hematoma was evacuated, and orchidectomy was done. Primary closure of inguinal incision was done and scrotal wound was kept open due to edema and doubt of viability. Postoperatively, the patient stayed in ICU for 2 days. Hemoglobin remained static and patients’ vitals gradually improved. Patient was discharged on 6th postoperative day. At the time of postoperative follow up after 10 days, patient was in excellent condition with resolution of his prior complains. Inguinal wound had healed, and scrotal wound was healing. Pathological examination of tissue showed blackish spermatic cord with congested and dilated blood vessels. Tunica albuginea had large areas of hemorrhages along with dilated and congested blood vessels with hemorrhagic material inside. After 3 months, the patient reported complete resolution of symptoms with no signs of recurrence or complications

Discussion

The most common vascular disease of spermatic cord is varicocele. Aneurysmal dilatation of testicular vessel is a rare entity with the first literature of testicular artery aneurysm known to be documented in 1992 from Japan in a 51-year old male with the pathological examination of extirpated specimen establishing the diagnosis[4]. A pseudoaneurysm represents a vessel leak that is surrounded and contained by a pseudocapsule[5]. Chronic orchitis has been implicated in many of the case reports as an etiology for the pseudoaneurysm formation. Intratesticular pseudoaneurysm in the absence of trauma or infection is exceedingly rare[6]. The rarity of this condition in the reported literature means there is no established pathophysiology for the formation of intratesticular pseudoaneurysms in the setting of orchitis.

Isolated orchitis is rare and is usually due to viral hematogenous spread which could lead to vasculitis. The mumps virus is most commonly involved, but others such as adenovirus and enterovirus are also seen[7]. Coxsackievirus group B type 5, a type of enterovirus that causes hand-foot-and-mouth disease, had been previously documented to be a cause of viral-induced orchitis[8]. In our patient, there was no prior history of orchalgia or testicular swelling, which are known to trigger pseudoaneurysm formation. However, the fact that he was being treated for UTI indicates subtle infection in testis that triggered the formation of pseudoaneurysm, which could not be ruled out.

In dengue hemorrhagic syndrome, several mechanisms contribute to vascular injury and pseudoaneurysm formation. The dengue virus non-structural protein 1 (NS1) disrupts endothelial integrity by activating Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4), leading to pro-inflammatory cytokine release and endothelial hyperpermeability[9]. NS1 also promotes macrophage migration inhibitory factor secretion and activates autophagy, further increasing vascular permeability[9]. Additionally, matrix metalloproteinases, especially MMP-9, are overexpressed in dengue-infected immune cells, degrading the extracellular matrix and weakening the vascular wall[10]. This environment, along with immune complex-mediated vasculitis and severe thrombocytopenia, predisposes to vascular disruption and pseudoaneurysms, even in smaller vessels like the testicular artery, These pathophysiologic insights suggest a plausible molecular basis for rare complications like testicular artery pseudoaneurysm in severe dengue infections.

The diagnosis of aneurysm and pseudoaneurysm is usually by imaging studies. Ultrasonography (USS) with Doppler study or CT angiography is the mainstay for the diagnosis. The typical USS appearance of a pseudoaneurysm demonstrates an anechoic area within the testis which is filled by a mosaic of colors (typically known as the yin-yang sign). There is usually a to-and-fro flow pattern at the neck of the pseudoaneurysm that is diagnostic of the condition[5]. In many cases, when the diagnosis is not possible preoperatively, intraoperative diagnosis is made after direct visualization of blood vessels. Clinical evaluation help very less in the diagnosis. Most common presentation is scrotal swelling and pain.

Management of aneurysm and pseudoaneurysm usually mandates operative intervention with ligation and resection of the diseased segment. Bangash had reported non-operative management for intact pseudoaneurysm, who reported complete resolution in 3 weeks with IV analgesics and antibiotics[5]. In our case, scrotal exploration + orchidectomy was done with incisions over inguinal and scrotal areas. The inguinal incision allowed proximal vascular control, while the scrotal incision enabled hematoma evacuation and testicular assessment. This approach ensured safe and effective management of the pseudoaneurysm. The scrotal incision was closed in layers after achieving hemostasis and evacuation of the hematoma

The possibility of spontaneous rupture of intratesticular pseudoaneurysm exists as reported by Mujoomdar et al[11]. Given the rarity of this condition there are at present no predictive features of likelihood of spontaneous rupture. Although sonography is the reference standard for diagnosis of intratesticular masses, no findings are pathognomonic for spontaneous intratesticular hemorrhage[12]. However, spontaneous rupture of a testicular pseudoaneurysm is a rare but serious event that almost always mandates surgical intervention with exploration and orchidectomy. Our management included radical orchiectomy – to rule out possible malignancy due to lack of trauma – and the patient recovered with complete resolution of his pain. The pseudoaneurysm likely ruptured afterward, causing significant intratesticular hemorrhage.

In a case reported in 2014 by Parker and Nangia in the USA, a patient with testicular pain is described. Ultrasound diagnosed a lesion suggestive of pseudoaneurysm of the testicular artery; It was determined that the etiology of the pseudoaneurysm was chronic orchitis, which supported this disease as a risk factor for its formation[13]. From the histopathological examination of the resected testis in our case, destruction of the native structure with mixed inflammatory infiltrates where seen suggestive of orchitis being an antecedent condition for the formation of pseudoaneurysm.

Conclusion

This case highlights the need for high suspicion of vascular complications in patients with scrotal pain, swelling, and discoloration, especially with a history of infection. Early Doppler ultrasonography is essential for diagnosis, and timely surgical or endovascular intervention can prevent complications. Clinicians should consider testicular pseudoaneurysm in cases of acute scrotal pain and swelling, even without trauma. This case confirms that orchitis may be a risk factor for pseudoaneurysm formation and emphasizes using ultrasound for diagnosis and orchiectomy for persistent pain.
