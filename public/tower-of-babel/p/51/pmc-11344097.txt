3DFFL: privacy-preserving Federated Few-Shot Learning for 3D point clouds in autonomous vehicles
Authors: Abdullah Aman Khan, Khwaja Mutahir Ahmad, Sidra Shafiq, Waqas Amin, Rajesh Kumar
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents a comprehensive study of 3D point cloud Federated Few-Shot Learning (3DFFL), focusing on addressing challenges such as limited data availability and privacy concerns in point cloud classification for applications such as autonomous vehicles. We introduce a novel approach that integrates Federated Learning with Few-Shot Learning techniques, with a special emphasis on optimizing network architectures for 3D point cloud data. Our method capitalizes on the strengths of PointNet++ for feature extraction and ProtoNet for classification, all within a federated learning framework to ensure data privacy and collaborative learning. Significantly, the approach is augmented with the use of attention and SoftMax layers, enhancing the feature extraction and classification processes. Extensive experiments on the ModelNet40, ShapeNet, and ScanOnjectNN datasets validate our method’s accuracy and adaptability in handling 3D point cloud classification, especially in privacy-sensitive and collaborative scenarios. This study not only demonstrates the potential of integrating attention mechanisms and SoftMax layers in 3DFFL but also lays a robust foundation for future advancements in this evolving field, particularly in technologies dependent on 3D data processing.Subject terms: Computer science, Software

Introduction

The field of 3D point cloud classification holds a significant place in several computer vision applications, particularly in emerging areas such as autonomous vehicles and robotics1. This process involves classifying objects in 3D point clouds into various categories. However, this area faces numerous challenges, especially when considering Federated Few-Shot (FFL) 3D point cloud classification. Additionally, the efficiency of embedding or feature extraction in 3D point clouds typically does not match the levels achieved for 2D image data. This discrepancy largely arises from the dependence on pre-trained models based on images1, such as ResNet2, which are built upon deeply trained networks using extensive image datasets. In contrast, models designed for point clouds, such as PointNet++3,4 and PointNet5,6, often rely on less complex networks trained with fewer point cloud samples, characterized by considerable intra-class variation and minimal inter-class distinctions. This variation in data richness and network complexity leads to less effective embedding for 3D point cloud7.

The introduction of deep learning techniques has been transformative in the processing of point cloud. Recently, FSL algorithms in the 2D image domain have seen significant strides by creating auxiliary training meta-tasks, employing metric learning or optimization-based methodologies to glean transferable knowledge and disseminate it across new tasks. The potential of these algorithms is particularly promising for enhancing the capabilities of deep networks in the point cloud domain. By utilizing meta-learning techniques8 and integrating a limited number of labeled examples, these algorithms aim to boost the generalization ability and mitigate the inherent limitations of traditional deep learning methods in point cloud classification9–13. The motivation behind this research lies in the relatively uncharted territory of FFSL on 3D data, especially when compared with its 2D image domain counterpart, necessitating addressing several unique challenging areas such as simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) and autonomous vehicles (AVs). These methods depart from traditional approaches that utilize manually crafted features, instead applying deep networks to extract more representative features from either shape projections or raw data points, thereby achieving improved performance. Nevertheless, they face two main challenges in the classification of point clouds: the need for a large amount of labeled training data and the difficulty in extending the model’s application to new classes or tasks not encountered during the training phase. While techniques such as data augmentation and regularization provide some mitigation against the scarcity of data14, they often fall short in scenarios lacking substantial labeled training data for specific classes or tasks. Challenges in point cloud structures: The inherent nature of point clouds, being irregular, unstructured, and unordered collections of points in Euclidean space15, presents considerable challenges. The performance and adaptability of current architectures and algorithms in 3D FSL for processing these point clouds remain a topic of ongoing exploration. It is essential to assess their effectiveness concerning the unique properties of point cloud data.

Scarcity of labeled data in 3D learning: There is a significant gap between the effectiveness of 2D FSL algorithms and their 3D counterparts, primarily because 2D algorithms benefit from deeper networks that have been pre-trained on extensive datasets. For example, tieredImageNet16 encompasses 608 classes with about 800,000 images, in stark contrast to most 3D model datasets, such as ModelNet17 and ShapeNet18, which comprise considerably fewer labeled examples. ModelNet includes only 40 classes with a total of 12,311 models. As a result, models trained on these limited datasets tend to yield poorer quality 3D feature clusters, characterized by prominent intra-class variations and minor inter-class distinctions. Developing effective strategies to address these limitations is thus of paramount importance.

To advance the field of 3D Federated FSL (3DFFL), particularly in addressing data scarcity challenges, it is imperative to explore the concept of data sharing among autonomous car manufacturers equipped with LiDar or similar point cloud generation technology. The utilization of federated learning techniques in this context not only helps mitigate data scarcity issues but also ensures the preservation of data privacy. Furthermore, the integration of FSL into this collaborative approach enhances the capacity to effectively work with limited data resources. This collective effort not only narrows the gap between FSL in the 2D image and 3D data domains but also lays the foundation for the development of precise 3DFFSL algorithms tailored specifically for point cloud data, with significant implications for autonomous vehicle applications. Through this comprehensive investigation, we seek to further the understanding and development of 3DFFL techniques, laying a solid foundation for future research and advancements in this evolving domain. Related work

Few-Shot for 3D data

Methods based on deep learning diverge from traditional techniques that rely on manually crafted feature extraction19, showcasing their ability in deriving complex and nuanced features through the use of extensive network architectures. The techniques employed in handling point cloud data are generally divided into two categories: those that use projection-based methods and those that utilize point-based methods, each differing in their approach to extracting features from either organized grids or raw point data.

Networks using projection-based techniques transform unstructured point cloud data into an organized format, such as through multi-view projections20,21, voxel grids22–24, or lattice structures25,26. Following this, conventional 2D or 3D CNNs are applied to derive features specific to views or structures. Nonetheless, projection-based techniques can face challenges, including potential loss of crucial information or increased demands on memory27.

Contrastingly, point-based methods directly engage with raw point cloud data, bypassing the need to structure them. Techniques such as PointNet, PointNet++, and DGCNN28 facilitate direct learning on these unstructured data sets, eliminating the necessity for voxelization29 or projection. Such point-based methods have been gaining traction due to their efficient handling of irregular and unstructured data in point clouds. Utilizing deep network architectures, these methods, whether projection-based or point-based, are adept at identifying and representing complex patterns within point clouds, offering advancements over traditional feature extraction techniques. Nevertheless, the choice between projection-based and point-based methods should be made with careful consideration of aspects like information loss, memory usage, and the specific characteristics of the point cloud data in question.

In recent developments, there has been an increased focus on applying deep network architectures directly to raw point cloud data. PointNet was a pioneer in this area, utilizing MultiLayer Perceptrons (MLP) for learning individual point features and integrating a symmetric function for global feature aggregation, typically through max-pooling. Following in PointNet’s footsteps, subsequent innovations such as PointNet++, PointCNN, RSCNN, DensePoint30, and DGCNN have delved into examining local relationships in specific areas, employing either convolution-based or graph-based networks. These methods have been effective in discerning significant geometric characteristics, setting new benchmarks in performance. However, the deep learning-based approaches in point cloud processing are somewhat constrained by their reliance on large volumes of annotated data, which limits their adaptability to new, unseen class types. Recent research efforts have been directed towards learning with fewer data samples.

The authors of31 have investigated feature representation learning through self-supervision, a distinct approach from few-shot learning (FSL). The study by32 focused on developing a discriminative embedding space for 3D model multi-view images, emphasizing the bias in point cloud shapes. A notable study in this area33 has been the pioneering systematic analysis of FSL classification of point clouds. In this review, the authors examine the latest networks in 3D point cloud learning and establish robust baselines for addressing this challenge. Furthermore, the authors introduce an innovative, adaptable module, termed the Cross Instance Adaption module, designed to overcome the challenges posed by significant variance within classes and minimal differences between classes in the context of 3D FSL. This module offers the flexibility to be seamlessly incorporated into existing FSL algorithms, thereby enhancing their effectiveness in point cloud scenarios.

Few-Shot for 2D data

As previously discussed, FSL emerges as a critical area in the field of deep learning, notable for its capacity to effectively learn from a limited amount of labeled training data and to generalize well to new tasks. The existing algorithms in FSL are primarily divided into two groups: metric-based and optimization-based methodologies. The metric-based approach is centered on developing an embedding space where pairs of similar samples are positioned in proximity. Notable examples of this include the Matching Network34, which employs a bidirectional LSTM module along with cosine distance for the classification of query samples. Another example is the Prototypical Network35,36, which determines class prototypes by averaging features from the support set and uses squared Euclidean distance to evaluate similarity. Furthermore, Relation Net37 introduces a module capable of learning metrics to calculate relation scores between the support and query sets. In addressing task distribution shifts in Few-Shot Learning (FSL), Luo et al.38 highlight the potential of channel-wise feature transformation to enhance the adaptability and generalization of image representations across varying tasks. Mangla et al.39 introduce the concept of Manifold Mixup, a novel approach aimed at improving the performance of few-shot learning models by encouraging smoother decision boundaries and more generalizable feature representations. Chen et al.40 propose the Meta-Baseline method, which simplifies meta-learning for few-shot learning by enhancing a base network’s ability to generalize from few examples without complex meta-learning algorithms.

On the other hand, optimization-based methods view meta-learning as a process of optimization. For instance, MAML41 focuses on learning a set of initialization parameters that are model-agnostic and, through a minimal number of gradient updates, can significantly enhance performance on new tasks. Additionally, MetaOptNet42 integrates a differentiable quadratic programming solver into its framework to learn a linear SVM predictor that is feature-relevant, thereby facilitating better generalization to new categories. In our research, we undertake a comprehensive analysis of diverse meta-learning algorithms within the scope of FSL classification for point clouds. We introduce robust baseline methods and specialized components designed to tackle the unique challenges inherent in this area. Our goal is to utilize various meta-learning strategies to improve the efficacy and expand the capabilities of FSL specifically in the context of point cloud data.

Other methods for point cloud processing

In recent times, advancements have been made in 3D shape analysis through the development of methodologies involving view-based and point-based neural networks. Tatarchenko et al.43 introduced Tangent Convolutions, which operate within the tangent space of surfaces to achieve dense prediction in 3D shape analysis. The “Walk in the Cloud” approach44 uses curve representations of geometric properties in point clouds to enhance shape analysis. Qian et al.45 utilized the PointNet++ structure and the PointNeXt framework to improve training and scaling strategies for 3D point cloud models. These innovations extend the frontier of 3D shape recognition by introducing new geometric processing techniques and optimizing training methodologies. Approaches like View-GCN and MVTN use multi-view 2D projections of 3D shapes for recognition tasks. Wei et al.46 proposed View-GCN, achieving state-of-the-art performance in 3D shape classification and retrieval. MVTN47 aims to enhance classical multi-view methods by learning the optimal viewpoint for each 3D shape, increasing recognition performance.

Advancements have also been made in point-based approaches. Thomas et al. introduced the KPConv framework, which uses deformable convolutions on point clouds to predict dense 3D data48. Wu et al. further developed this concept with PointConv, enabling deep convolutional networks directly on 3D point clouds49. FPConv50 addresses challenges in point-based methods through local flattening techniques for point convolution. Recently, there have been efforts to integrate point-based methods with view-based methods. Xiao et al. introduced RegGeoNet, a learning architecture for regular representations of large-scale 3D point clouds, extracting geometric features and learning relationships between them directly51. Another example is Flattening-Net, which efficiently analyzes equivalent 2D representations from 3D point clouds52. However, there are still gaps in these approaches. View-based methods may excel in exploiting 2D image features but often fall short in capturing the complete geometric context of 3D shapes. On the other hand, point-based approaches, while effective in capturing geometric details, can be computationally expensive due to extensive data preprocessing. Thus, integrating multi-view and point-based approaches could offer a holistic solution for the analysis of 3D shapes.

There is also growing interest in combining multi-view approaches with self-supervised learning. PointVST53 method demonstrated self-supervised pre-training can enhance the robustness and generalization of 3D point cloud models. By fine-tuning models from previous work to official implementations, significant improvements were achieved across a wide range of challenging tasks. Additionally, the re-evaluation of the PointNet++ framework using the PointNeXt architecture showcased the potential of advanced training strategies45. PointMCD employed multi-view cross-modal distillation to achieve state-of-the-art performance in point cloud encoders54. Park et al.55 presented a novel, efficient transformer-based architecture for processing 3D point cloud data, achieving faster performance compared to previous transformer based method56.

Federated learning for 3D point cloud

Gkillas et al.57 propose a federated learning framework for enhancing lidar data resolution in automotive scenes, achieving significant improvements while preserving data privacy by training models collaboratively without sharing raw data. Similarly, Zhang et al.58 introduce a reconfigurable holographic surface aided collaborative wireless SLAM system using federated learning for autonomous driving, enhancing localization and mapping accuracy while maintaining data privacy through decentralized model training. Moreover, Lan et al.59 present a decentralized federated learning framework utilizing BEV feature exchange pyramid networks for 3D object detection in small and distant situations, improving detection accuracy while preserving data privacy by avoiding centralized data aggregation. These studies explore various point cloud processing techniques; however, to the best of our knowledge, no one has addressed Federated Few-Shot Learning for 3D point cloud classification, making it a relevant area for further investigation.

FSL

In this section, we describe the methodology for our 3DFFSL, integrating FL with PointNet++ (embedding) and ProtoNet (learnable) components. The challenge lies in developing a model capable of accurately classifying new point clouds into their respective classes, despite having limited labeled examples for each class. This model, leveraging the FL framework, enables learning from various datasets while maintaining privacy and data diversity without the need for data sharing. The model aims to capture discriminative features from the input point clouds effectively. It incorporates an attention mechanism to highlight relevant information, crucial for dealing with the sparsity and irregularity of point clouds. The FL approach adds a layer of complexity and innovation to this process. By distributing the learning process across multiple nodes, the model benefits from a wider range of data variances and real-world scenarios, which is particularly advantageous in FSL settings where data scarcity is a common issue.

Additionally, the learnable component for classification, represented by ProtoNet, is adapted to work within the federated framework. This adaptation ensures that the model not only learns generalized features across distributed datasets but also retains the ability to perform robustly on unseen point clouds. The ultimate objective is to achieve accurate and robust classification performance on new point clouds, effectively overcoming the inherent challenges of FSL in the 3D domain, while also benefiting from the collaborative and privacy-preserving aspects of FL.

FL adaptation in embeddings

The PointNet++ architecture, an advanced extension of the original PointNet designed for point cloud analysis, is further enhanced by incorporating FL techniques. This integration is crucial in addressing the challenges of data scarcity while ensuring data privacy and leveraging the diversity of data sources. PointNet++ operates across multiple nodes (e.g., devices or servers), each with its own subset of data. The architecture’s hierarchical set abstraction (SA) and feature propagation (FP) layers are adapted to function in a distributed manner.

Federated SA Layer: Each node receives a subset of the input point set X and applies the SA layer locally. This process involves partitioning local point sets into smaller regions S={S1,S2,..,SM}, where M represents the number of regions formed within each node. A shared multi-layer perceptron (MLP) learns local features for these regions. The output at each node is a transformed set of points X′ and their corresponding features F.

Federated FP Layer: Similar to the SA layer, the FP layer is executed locally at each node. It refines the features of the transformed points by aggregating information across different scales. The local outputs are refined feature representations F′.

Following the local processing, the key innovation in the federated adaptation lies in the aggregation of learned features across nodes. After each training epoch, the model parameters or the learned feature representations from each node are sent to a central server. This server performs an aggregation operation, such as Federated Averaging, to update the global model. The updated global model is then redistributed to each node for further training.

This federated approach to embeddings allows PointNet++ to benefit from diverse datasets without compromising data privacy. Each node contributes to the learning process with its local data, ensuring that the global model gains a comprehensive understanding of various point cloud patterns. This method is particularly advantageous in scenarios with data that cannot be centralized due to privacy or logistical constraints.

The mathematical foundation of PointNet++ remains intact in this federated setting. However, the FL adaptation ensures that the learning process is collaborative, and privacy-preserving. The global model, enriched with diverse data insights, becomes more robust and generalizable, making it well-suited for real-world applications where data is inherently distributed and varied. Handling data heterogeneity in Federated Few-Shot Learning

In a federated learning setup, the presence of data heterogeneity among different nodes presents significant challenges to model performance and generalization. The heterogeneity at the node level can lead to instances of localized overfitting, thereby decreasing the effectiveness of global modeling due to variations in data distribution. This heterogeneity arises from differences in data collection methods, environmental conditions, and specific use cases across the nodes. Consequently, the model may perform well on data similar to that of one node but struggle with data from another node. Although exposure to diverse data can enhance model generalization, significant differences can hinder the learning process and impair the model’s capability for global tasks. The Federated Averaging (FedAvg) approach addresses these discrepancies in data distributions by aggregating model updates from all participants. However, this method may not suffice for highly heterogeneous data. Thus, we employ data normalization techniques to standardize the data across nodes, thereby reducing variability. This approach contributes to simulating various scenarios that facilitate robust feature learning by the model. Additionally, adaptive learning rates offer a means of adjusting the learning rate based on the distribution of local data, thereby preventing overfitting to localized data and promoting better generalization.

Experiments, results and discussion

Datasets

We adopted specialized splits for the ModelNet40 and ShapeNet datasets33, tailored for FSL-based 3D point cloud classification. These splits were chosen with an emphasis on maintaining similar data distributions between the training and testing sets. Specifically, the ModelNet40 dataset is split into 30 training classes with 9240 instances and 10 testing classes comprising 3104 instances. Conversely, the ShapeNet dataset is more extensive, encompassing a total of 30,073 instances. It is divided into 50 training classes with 21,722 samples and 20 testing classes holding 8351 samples. For the ScanObjectNN dataset, the splits are constructed to further challenge the model due to real-world data complexity. The ScanObjectNN60 consists of 15 categories with a total of 14,298 perturbed objects. This dataset is divided into three non-overlapping subsets, each containing five classes. The splits are as follows: Split 0 has 9509 instances for training and 4789 for testing, Split 1 has 9129 training instances and 5169 testing instances, and Split 2 includes 9958 training instances and 4340 testing instances. Testing and training

As mentioned earlier, to ensure a standardized and equitable evaluation framework, we implement the widely-recognized episode-based FSL methodology33,60 throughout our training and assessment phases. The training regimen entails developing the network from its foundational stages over 80 epochs. Within each epoch, the network undergoes 400 meta-training episodes, supplemented by 600 validation episodes randomly selected from the training dataset to track the network’s development. The episode-based FSL paradigm structures each episode to include N distinct classes, with each class represented by K labeled support instances and Q query instances. This configuration is often termed as the N-way K-shot Q-query setting, facilitating the network’s learning process from a constrained set of labeled examples and enabling it to generalize to query instances. Upon completing the meta-training phase, the network undergoes an evaluation phase using 700 meta-testing episodes, randomly chosen from the test dataset. These episodes adhere to the identical N-way K-shot Q-query format. The performance is assessed by averaging the classification outcomes across these meta-testing episodes. Additionally, we incorporate a 5-fold cross-validation technique. This approach involves partitioning the training dataset into five equal segments, with each segment serving once as a validation group while the others are utilized for training. This cycle is repeated five times, and the aggregate of these results offers a comprehensive and reliable measure of the network’s effectiveness on the test dataset. By following these standardized procedures and incorporating cross-validation, we ensure reliable and unbiased evaluation of our 3DFFL, reporting the average performance across multiple estimations.

For experimental purposes, we chose to utilize three client nodes, dividing the datasets into three equal parts. It should be noted that we used the splits provided by Ye et al.33,60. These splits are disjoint in nature, making them significantly more challenging compared to natural splits of the dataset. This partitioning ensures diversity in the data seen by each client, promoting robust model training. Each node employs a continuous training method with a patience of five epochs to ensure stability and convergence. The federated learning process involves multiple communication rounds between the clients and the central server. Specifically, we conducted 80 training rounds, with each client performing local training for 40 epochs per round. We used the FedAvg algorithm to aggregate the local models into a global model. This iterative process continued until the global model converged. By following this approach, we set a baseline for few-shot based federated 3D classification of point clouds.

Upon completing the meta-training phase, the network undergoes an evaluation phase using 700 meta-testing episodes, randomly chosen from the test dataset. These episodes adhere to the identical N-way K-shot Q-query format. The performance is assessed by averaging the classification outcomes across these meta-testing episodes. Additionally, we incorporate a 5-fold cross-validation technique. This approach involves partitioning the training dataset into five equal segments, with each segment serving once as a validation group while the others are utilized for training. This cycle is repeated five times, and the aggregate of these results offers a comprehensive and reliable measure of the network’s effectiveness on the test dataset. By following these standardized procedures and incorporating cross-validation, we ensure reliable and unbiased evaluation of our 3DFFL, reporting the average performance across multiple estimations.

Implementation details

In our implementation, we utilize PointNet++ as the feature extraction network i.e., the backbone. PointNet++ is a deep learning architecture specifically designed for processing and understanding point cloud data. Unlike traditional methods that rely on handcrafted features or grid-based representations, PointNet++ directly operates on the raw point cloud input. The key innovation of PointNet++ lies in its ability to learn point-wise features and capture global context simultaneously. It achieves this by employing shared MLP to extract local features from individual points and then using a max-pooling operation to aggregate the point features into a global feature vector. This global feature vector captures the overall characteristics of the entire point cloud. By leveraging a symmetric function, PointNet++ ensures that the learned representation remains invariant to point order permutations. This property allows the network to effectively handle unordered and unstructured point cloud data. PointNet++ has shown remarkable performance in various 3D tasks, including classification, segmentation, and object recognition. It is a pioneering work in the field of deep learning for point cloud processing and has paved the way for subsequent advancements and architectures that build upon its foundations. We employ the Adam optimizer with an initial learning rate of 0.00008 and a gamma value of 0.5. The learning rate is reduced every 5 epochs to facilitate effective training. To augment the training data, we apply random points jittering and rotation techniques, similar to the approach used in6. These augmentation strategies help introduce variability and enhance the model’s ability to generalize.

Scalability and computational requirements

Our proposed framework utilizes the PointNet++ architecture, which ensures consistent computational load by processing a fixed number of points (e.g., 512 points) regardless of the initial input size. Regardless of whether the input consists of 128, 256, 512, 1024, or 2048 points, PointNet++ abstracts the points to this fixed size. This design guarantees predictable and reliable performance, with 4.07 Giga FLOPs and 1.76M parameters. In the context of federated learning, each client node performs local training on its dataset and only transmits model updates to the central server. This decentralized approach reduces the need for centralized data storage and preserves data privacy, but it does introduce communication overhead during model aggregation. The constant computational load of the PointNet++ architecture enhances resource management efficiency, making our framework particularly suitable for federated learning environments where data privacy and optimal resource utilization are crucial.

Potential limitations of the proposed approach

Despite the promising results exhibited by our proposed 3DFFL method, it is imperative to acknowledge several potential limitations. The grid search method employed for hyperparameter tuning, while exhaustive, necessitates substantial computational resources and time, resulting in a significant investment. Concerns regarding scalability may arise as the federated learning framework could face bottlenecks when applied to larger or more intricate datasets. While the federated approach guarantees data privacy, it also introduces communication overhead and heavily relies on the quality of local data, potentially impacting the overall performance of the global model.

Conclusion

Our comprehensive study introduces a pioneering approach in 3D Federated Few-Shot Learning (3DFFL), specifically tailored for the classification of 3D point clouds. The proposed method successfully integrates Federated Learning with Few-Shot Learning techniques, utilizing PointNet++ and ProtoNet as key components. This integration addresses significant challenges in the field, particularly the scarcity of labeled data and the need for privacy-preserving collaborative training. The experimental results on benchmark datasets such as ModelNet40, ShapeNetand, and ScanObjectNN validate the effectiveness of our approach. Our method demonstrates competitive performance compared to state-of-the-art models, achieving high accuracy in both 5-way 1-shot and 5-way 5-shot settings. Notably, the federated aspect of the model ensures data privacy and collaborative learning among various entities, crucial in real-world applications. The success of this study not only advances the field of 3DFFL but also opens new avenues for future research. Future directions include exploring more sophisticated federated aggregation techniques to improve model robustness and accuracy, integrating advanced 3D point cloud processing methods such as graph-based neural networks or voxel-based approaches for additional performance gains, and addressing communication overhead to optimize the federated learning process for large-scale and diverse datasets. It highlights the potential of federated and few-shot learning techniques in handling the unique challenges of 3D point cloud data, setting a precedent for further exploration and development in this domain. The implications of this work extend to various applications, remarkably in the development of autonomous vehicles and other areas where 3D data processing is essential.
