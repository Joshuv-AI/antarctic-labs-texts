Systemic inflammation triggers local complement production in the mouse retina and RPE
Authors: Vittoria Spero, Pascal Escher, Sophie Braga-Lagache, Volker Enzmann, Martin Zinkernagel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Systemic inflammation is increasingly recognized as a potential contributor in sight threatening retinal diseases such as Age-related Macular Degeneration (AMD). Yet, its impact on local eye immune responses and further pathological processes remains poorly understood. This study aims to elucidate the effect of peripheral immune activation on the local complement system, a central component of innate immunity with a dual role in maintaining ocular homeostasis and mediating inflammation, in the mouse retina and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). Using a systemic lipopolysaccharide (LPS) challenge and a high-resolution proteomic approach, we observed a significant increase in complement factor 3 (C3) production in the RPE and retina 24 h post-injection. Further analysis confirmed the local source of complement signaling in resident cells such as astrocytes and RPE, independently from blood-retina barrier disruption. These findings reveal a direct cross-talk between systemic immune activation and ocular complement activation, and highlight the potential implications of systemic inflammation in the pathogenesis of ocular diseases, underlining the importance of future research into targeted therapies for inflammation-driven conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03737-y.

Introduction

The interplay between systemic inflammation and ocular health is poorly understood, despite growing evidence linking chronic inflammation to the progression of vision-threatening diseases such as age-related macular degeneration (AMD) [1–3]. Chronic systemic inflammation, often arising from aging, infections, or autoimmune conditions, has the potential to disrupt immune homeostasis in the retina, contributing to tissue damage and disease progression [4–7].

The complement system has drawn particular attention in common eye diseases such as AMD [8, 9], because of the now established role of innate immunity in the progression of the disease from early or intermediate stages to the advanced stage of geographic atrophy (GA). However, the exact mechanisms underlying this transformation remain unclear [10, 11], and in particular, the exact source of complement activation in the eye has not been completely clarified yet. Many evidence show the presence of complement proteins in the RPE, in the outer segments of photoreceptors, in the drusen and retina of AMD patients, however, whether they derive from peripheral activation or local production remains an open question [12, 13]. The complement cascade consists of over 30 soluble proteins and receptors, with complement component 3 (C3) playing a central role.

as it marks the convergence of the classical, lectin, and alternative activation pathways [14]. Complement activation mediates opsonization, immunomodulation, and the formation of the membrane attack complex (MAC), which induces cell lysis [15]. Because of its strong inflammatory potential, complement activity is tightly regulated by inhibitory proteins such as factor H, which controls C3 activation, and CD59, which prevents MAC assembly [16, 17].

Initially, systemic complement activation was proposed as the main driver of ocular pathology, with elevated circulating factors thought to disrupt local homeostasis. More recently, however, accumulating evidence points to a major role for local complement activation in the eye, which remains only partially understood [12, 18]. This highlights the need to better define the interplay between systemic and local complement activation, particularly in the retina and RPE, as a basis for safer and more effective therapeutic strategies.

To investigate the potential influence of systemic inflammation on local complement activity in the eye, a number of experimental tools and model are available. In particular, a well-established experimental tool to induce systemic immune activation is Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) administration, a widely used endotoxin in mouse models to induce immune activation and to mimic inflammatory responses. LPS is a component of the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria and is recognized by Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) on immune cells, leading to activation of downstream signaling pathways such as NF-κB and MAPK. This results in the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β), chemokines, and type I interferons, contributing to systemic or localized inflammation [19, 20]. Moreover, LPS is known to activate the complement cascade both in a direct and indirect manner. It can interact with mannose-binding lectin (MBL) leading to the initiation of the lectin pathway [21] or stimulate hepatocytes, the main producers of complement proteins, to synthetize key components such as C3, C4, and factor B (FB), enhancing the body’s capacity to fight infection and amplifying complement activation [22].

In the retina, cell-specific contributions to complement activation further complicate the immune landscape. RPE cells, which play a central role in retinal homeostasis, are particularly susceptible to inflammatory insults and can produce complement proteins, as well as complement inhibitors, under stress [23–25]. Glial cells, including microglia and Müller cells, are primary sources of complement proteins, although recent findings also implicate astrocytes and photoreceptors in complement activation [25, 26]. To date, only one publication addressed the impact of systemic LPS on the ocular environment, however, no differences between retina, RPE and choroid were investigated, and therefore, no analysis of specific cell types involved was possible [27]. To address this knowledge gap, we exploited a mouse model of LPS induced systemic inflammation to investigate retinal and RPE complement activation using a label-free proteomic approach.

Materials and methods

Animals

This study was approved by the local Animal Ethics Committee (Veterinärdienst des Kantons Bern: BE124/2023 and BE3/2021) and adhered to the Association for Research in Vision and Ophthalmology Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. Male and female C57BL/6J mice (Charles River Deutschland, Sulzfeld, Germany), aged 8 to 12 weeks, were used in this study. The mice were housed under controlled temperature and humidity conditions in ventilated cages with a 12-hour light–dark cycle. All mice had free access to food and water.

Systemic LPS challenge

The mice were injected intraperitoneally with 1 mg/kg LPS from Escherichia coli serotype O111:B4 (Cat #L2630; Sigma-Aldrich, Buchs, Switzerland) as previously described [28]. The animals were euthanized via CO2 inhalation followed by decapitation either 6, 24, or 72 h (h) post-injection (p.i.), to perform molecular analyses.

Label-free nLC/MS proteomic analysis

After euthanasia, eyes were enucleated and opened at the ora serrata. The neural retina was carefully removed, and the eyecup was incubated with dispase (2 U/mL, 10 min at 37 °C) to facilitate the separation of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) monolayer from the choroid. RPE sheets were then gently micro-dissected under a stereomicroscope. To perform proteomic analysis, one retina per mouse and two RPE layer preparations per mouse were pooled, snap-frozen, and lysed in a solution containing 8 M urea, 100 mM Tris pH 8, and protease inhibitors using a sonicator (Bandelin, Berlin, Germany). Following centrifugation, the supernatant underwent reduction, alkylation, and overnight precipitation. The resulting pellet was re-suspended in a solution of 8 M urea and 50 mM Tris pH 8. Protein concentration was determined using the Qubit Protein Assay (Invitrogen), and 10 µg of protein was subjected to a 6-hour digestion with trypsin at 37 °C.

The digests were analyzed by nano-liquid chromatography on a Dionex Ultimate 3000 system (ThermoFisher Scientific, Reinach, Switzerland) coupled to a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) through a CaptiveSpray source (Bruker). The parameters included an end-plate offset of 500 V, a drying temperature of 200 °C, and a fixed capillary voltage of 1.6 kV. A 2 µL (200 ng) volume of the protein digest was loaded onto a pre-column (C18 PepMap 100, 5 μm, 100 A, 300 μm i.d. x 5 mm length, ThermoFisher) at a flow rate of 10 µL/min with 0.05% TFA in water/acetonitrile (98:2). Following loading, peptides were eluted in back flush mode onto a homemade C18 CSH Waters column (1.7 μm, 130 Å, 75 μm × 20 cm) using a 70-minute gradient of 5% acetonitrile to 40% in water with 0.1% formic acid at a flow rate of 250 nl/min.

The TimsTOF Pro was operated either in DDA or DIA mode in the Parallel Acquisition SErial Fragmentation (PASEF). The mass range was set between 100 and 1700 m/z, with 10 PASEF scans between 0.7 and 1.4 V s/cm2. The accumulation time was set to 2 ms, and the ramp time was set to 100 ms. Fragmentation was triggered at 20,000 arbitrary units (au), and peptides (up to charge 5) were fragmented using collision induced dissociation with a spread between 20 and 59 eV.

The mass spectrometry data was searched and quantified with Spectronaut (Biognosys, Newton, MA, USA) version 18.4.231017.55695 in the hybrid directDIA+ (Deep) mode, whereby the DIA samples are searched in a library-free mode with the uniprot Mouse database (release March 2023, and including common contaminants). Factory settings were applied, except that groups with less than two peptides were requested to be excluded. A list of all the identified protein-coding genes can be found in the supplementary Table 1. For further analysis, protein groups flagged as potential contaminants were removed. In order to evaluate log2 fold change between conditions, protein missing values were replaced in each sample by the lowest value of the sample. Volcano plots to show the up and down regulated proteins was created using GraphPad Prism software version 9.5.1 (San Diego, CA, USA). The list of all the identified proteins, together with their log2FC of variation compared to the control group, was submitted to STRING (version 12.0) to find the significantly involved Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes and KEGG pathways (10.1093/nar/gkac1000). R package ggplot 2 (version 3.5.0) was used to create the complement relative abundance graphs, as well as the GO and KEGG pathway dotplot graphs.

Immunohistochemistry

For immunohistochemistry, one eye per mouse was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 1X PBS (pH 7.4) overnight and processed through a sucrose gradient (5–30%) prior to embedding in OCT (Sakura, Alphen aan den Rijn, NL) and storage at − 80 °C. Cryosections (12 μm) were prepared using a cryostat, blocked with 5% normal goat serum (NGS) in 0.2% Triton X-100 (in PBS), and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Primary antibodies used were anti-complement C3 (rabbit, Cat. #A006302-2, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and anti-GFAP (mouse, Cat. #NBP1-05197, Novus Biologicals, Zug, Switzerland). Secondary antibodies were goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (1:200, Cat. #A11012) and goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 594 (1:200, Cat. #A48286; both ThermoFisher Scientific), applied for 1 h at room temperature. Slides were mounted with DAPI-containing medium (VectorLabs, Newark, CA, USA) and stored at 4 °C.

RPE-choroid whole mounts

For RPE-choroid whole mounts, one eye per mouse was fixed in 4% PFA for 10 min. After removing the anterior segment and lens, eyecups were further fixed in 4% PFA for 50 min. Retinas were carefully separated from the RPE layer, and remaining RPE-choroid cups were blocked in 5% normal goat serum in PBS for 1 h. Samples were stained with anti-C3 primary antibody (1:500, Cat. #A006302-2, Agilent) at 4 °C overnight and incubated with goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h.

Fluorescence In Situ Hybridization (FISH)

FISH was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections using RNAscope technology (RNAscope™ Multiomic LS, Ref. 323430, Advanced Cell Diagnostics, Newarc, CA, USA). All steps were carried out on the Leica Bond RX automated staining platform according to the manufacturer’s instructions for automated RNAscope assays.

Briefly, sections were deparaffinized and subjected to heat-induced target retrieval using Bond Epitope Retrieval Solution 2 (AR9640), followed by protease treatment to permeabilize the tissue. Hybridization with the RNAscope target probe (RNAscope™ 2.5 LS Probe – Mm-C3-O3, 1116038-C1) was then performed under controlled conditions on the Leica Bond RX system. RNA signal amplification and detection were achieved using RNAscope amplification reagents and HRP-mediated tyramide signal amplification (TSA) with Opal 690 fluorophore (Akoya Biosciences, Marlborough, MA, USA). Both a positive and negative controls were included in the experiment. For the negative controls, we used the RNAscope™ 2.5 LS 3-plex Negative Control Probe (REF. 320878) to assess background signal. For the positive controls, we used the RNAscope™ 2.5 Probe Mm-Ppib (REF. 313918) to confirm RNA integrity and proper assay performance.

Following completion of RNA detection, nuclei were counterstained with DAPI.

Slides were mounted using ProLong Gold antifade reagent (Invitrogen).Microscopy.

C3 and GFAP-stained retinal sections were examined using a Nikon Eclipse 80i fluorescence microscope. RPE-choroid whole mounts were imaged using a Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope. Z-stacks for retinal whole mounts were captured with 0.54 μm intervals using Zen system 2011 software (Zeiss) with a 20X lens. Image analysis and quantifications were performed using ImageJ (v1.54d).

RNA Preparation and quantitative real-time PCR analysis

Total RNA was isolated using a single-step guanidinium isothiocyanate/phenol extraction with the TriZol RNA isolation reagent (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Cressier, Switzerland) according to the manufacturer’s protocol and quantified via spectrophotometry. For qPCR, retinas from two eyes and RPE layer preparations from six eyes were pooled. Samples were processed for real-time reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) to measure mRNA levels of C3 (FWD: AGCCCAACACCAGCTACATC; REV: GAATGCCCCAAGTTCTTCGC). Reactions were performed in a 96-well qPCR plates, run in duplicate, and normalized against the internal control β-actin (FWD: GCTGACAGGGAAATCGTG; REV: CCAGGGAGGAAGAGG).

A total of 120 ng of RNA was used for reverse transcription (GoScript Reverse Transcription; Promega, Madison, WI, USA), and one-fourth of the reaction volume was employed for subsequent qPCR experiments. The PCR amplification cycle consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 39 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 60 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s. A final elongation step using GOTaq qPCR Master Mix (Promega) was carried out at 72 °C for 5 min.

Relative target gene expression was calculated using the comparative cycle threshold (Ct) method. Fold change for each target gene relative to β-actin was determined using the formula 2 − Δ(ΔCT), where ΔCT = CT(target) − CT(β-actin), and Δ(ΔCT) = ΔCT(experimental group) − ΔCT(control group). For clarity, data were expressed as percentages relative to the control group set at 100%.

Measurement of C3 serum levels

Blood samples were collected and allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min. Then, the samples were centrifuged at 2000 g at 4 °C for 15 min to isolate the serum. The serum samples were stored at − 80 °C until analysis. C3 serum levels were measured using the Hycult ELISA kit (HK2002, Hycult Biotechnologies, Uden, NL) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software version 9.5.1 (San Diego, CA, USA) was used for statistical analysis. The qPCR and immunohistochemistry results were analyzed with an Unpaired T-test, the protein levels were analyzed with a one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test, while proteomic adjusted p-value was calculated with the FDR-controlled Benjamini and Hochberg multiple test correction. Results are presented as mean relative abundance ± standard error of the mean (SEM), with the control group set at 100. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Complement proteins identified via proteomics in the retina and RPE of mice treated with LPS

We then targeted the results analysis to the complement system, an essential pathway activated by LPS and relevant for the development and progression of eye diseases.

Subcomponents of complement protein 1 (C1) C1qa and C1qc were identified in both retina and RPE (Fig. 1A and B). However, their expression was modulated exclusively in the RPE, showing a significant upregulation 72 h post-LPS treatment compared to earlier time points, whereas C1q receptor (C1qR, Fig. 1C) exhibited a non-significant upregulation in the retina at both 24 and 72 h p.i.

C3 (Fig. 1D), a key mediator of the complement cascade, showed a marked upregulation in both the retina and RPE following LPS administration, with peak expression observed at 24 h p.i. This upregulation was accompanied by increased expression of complement 3 activator, namely FB (Fig. 1E), which was more prominent and faster in the RPE but reached significance in the retina 24 h after the stimulus as well.

Complement protein 4b (C4b, Fig. 1F), though present in both the RPE and retina, was upregulated exclusively in the RPE at 24 h p.i. In contrast, complement protein 5 (C5, Fig. 1G) was detected only in the RPE, where the expression was increased at 6 and 72 h post-LPS treatment.

Regarding complement cascade inhibitors, complement factor H (CFH, Fig. 1H) and CD59 (Fig. 1I) were identified in both tissue types. However, only a marginal trend toward increased CFH expression in the retina was observed.

Based on the proteomic data, we then determined the relative abundance of the identified complement proteins in response to a systemic LPS stimulus in the RPE and the retina (Fig. 1J). First, we detected a higher diversity of complement proteins in the RPE after systemic LPS injection, i.e. 20 expressed complement proteins, than in the retina, i.e. 8 proteins. Relative abundance of C3 was around 11% in the RPE, and around 20% in the retina. In both the RPE and retina the relative abundance of C1qa was around 3%. Concerning complement regulatory factors, in the RPE, the relative abundance of FH was around 18%, while only 5% in the retina. Conversely, CD59a composed only around 7% of the total complement panel in the RPE, while almost 50% in the retina. Specifically, FH (beige) and C1qb (light blue) were the most abundant proteins in the RPE at t0 (control). At 6 h p.i., but especially at 24 h post-LPS injection, the relative expression of complement proteins varied significantly, while by 72 h p.i. the expression profile partially returned to baseline. In contrast, in the retina, complement proportions remained relatively stable across the different time points, with complement inhibitor CD59 and C3 (in purple and green, respectively) being the most abundant proteins. While less pronounced, an increase in C3 and FB was observed after LPS injection, indicating a specific role for these factors in response to systemic inflammation. Interestingly, complement components downstream of C5 were either very low abundant in the RPE, or not detected at all in the retina. This indicates that in physiological conditions, as well as in response to our peripheral stimulus, the membrane attack complex (MAC) is not formed or does not play a role in site-specific response at the time points we analyzed.

Site-specific expression of complement 3 in the serum, retina and RPE

The proteomic analysis identified C3, the central factor of the complement cascade, to be the most upregulated in both RPE and retina, with the highest response at 24 h p.i. Therefore, we decided to analyze further its expression. First, we measured the systemic levels of C3 in the serum in controls (saline treated mice) and at 6, 24, and 72 h following intraperitoneal LPS injection (Fig. 2A).

Notably, a significant increase in serum C3 levels was observed only at 24 h post-LPS injection. This finding reinforces the role of the complement cascade during inflammatory processes and highlights the 24-hour time point as the critical phase for complement activation.

Second, we assessed the local expression of c3 in the eye. To do so, we performed qRT-PCR on retina and RPE samples. In both cases, a significant upregulation of c3 mRNA was observed, with a 14-fold increase in the retina (Fig. 2B), and a 78-fold increase in the RPE (Fig. 2C). This indicates not only a local presence of complement in response to systemic inflammation, but demonstrates a local production of this factor. In order to locate C3 in the eye, we performed immunohistochemistry on cryosections of saline and LPS-treated mice 24 h p.i. In the control group, C3 expression was minimal, with a faint signal detected in the RPE (Fig. 2D-E, yellow arrowhead) and almost no expression observed in the retina. However, 24 h post-LPS injection, a strong signal appeared on the basal surface of the RPE, a weaker signal on its apical surface and tight junctions, and a distinct signal was detectable in the ganglion cell layer (GCL) of the retina (Fig. 2F-G, yellow arrowheads). No signal was detected in the negative control (Supplementary Fig. 1). To better show the localization and potential source of C3 signal, an overlap of C3 staining and a brightfield image has been performed (Supplementary Fig. 2), showing that C3 fluorescence is solely located on the RPE side of the Bruch’s Membrane. Moreover, to exclude the presence of subretinal infiltrating macrophages as potential source of C3 near RPE cells, an LPS treated retina has been stained for Iba1 (Supplementary Fig. 3) showing, as expected, that in these experimental conditions, no subretinal infiltration of macrophages takes place.

Interestingly, in the GCL, C3 expression was predominantly localized in the ganglion cell bodies and, notably, in astrocytes, as confirmed by the double staining with the astrocyte marker GFAP (Fig. 3A-D, yellow arrowheads). To further confirm the source of C3 signal, retina whole mounts were stained for GFAP and C3 24 h post-LPS injection (Fig. 3E-G). Furthermore, C3 was identified in structures suggestive of blood vessels within the ganglion cell layer and inner plexiform layer of the retina (green arrowheads). Moreover, to exclude a potential expression of C3 by Müller cells, whose end feet are in close proximity with astrocytes and with whom they also share the marker GFAP in specific conditions, a double staining for C3 and Glutamine Synthetase (GS, specific Müller glia marker) was performed (Supplementary Fig. 4). A double staining for C3 and Iba1 was also performed, to exclude that the signal observed in the inner and outer plexiform layer came from microglia cells (Supplementary Fig. 4).

To corroborate the observed local upregulation of C3 during systemic inflammation, C3 signal intensity in the RPE was investigated and quantified using RPE-choroid whole mounts (Fig. 3H-J). This analysis revealed a 48% increase in C3 expression (Fig. 3D). The signal, beside the basal and apical surfaces, was localized in the tight junctions of RPE cells, as shown in Fig. 3I-J.

PCA analysis of the RPE and retina proteomic signature in response to systemic inflammation

Principal component analysis (PCA) of the RPE and retina log2-transformed datasets revealed a distinct clustering of the experimental groups in response to systemic LPS. For the RPE data PCA1 and PCA2 clearly clustered the dataset according to time-points (Fig. 5A), with the 6-hour time point exhibiting the most significant deviation from the control group. This result indicates a marked and clear longitudinal shift in the RPE phenotype in response to systemic inflammation. On the other hand, in the retina (Fig. 5B), clusters are less defined, with a high variability in the 6-hour group, and a partial overlapping between groups.

Proteomic profile of the RPE and retina in response to systemic inflammation

To visualize the general reaction of the two different samples, and to identify how complement system activation integrates with the broader proteomic response to an inflammatory stimulus, we analyzed the whole proteome of the RPE and retina at 6, 24, and 72 h post-LPS injection. Volcano plots for the RPE (Fig. 6A, D, and G) and retina (Figs. 6J, M, P) revealed striking differences in the responses of the two tissue types. The RPE exhibited significantly greater dysregulation of protein expression compared to the retina. Specifically, at 6 h post-stimulus, 1464 proteins were significantly dysregulated in the RPE versus 84 in the retina. At 24 h, 771 proteins were dysregulated in the RPE compared to 91 in the retina. By 72 h, 172 proteins were affected in the RPE, whereas only 39 were dysregulated in the retina.

Next, we explored the biological processes and pathways associated with these dysregulated proteins using the STRING database for Gene Ontology (GO) biological processes (Fig. 6B, E, H, K, N, Q) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways (Fig. 6C, F, I, L, O, R). GO analysis revealed that at 6 h and 24 h p.i., both RPE and retina samples showed activation of biological processes linked to inflammation, immune responses, and defense mechanisms. By 72 h p.i., these processes persisted in the RPE but were no longer evident in the retina. KEGG pathway analysis indicated that the complement system was activated in the RPE at both 6 h and 24 h p.i., alongside other inflammatory pathways such as “cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction” and “Staphylococcus aureus infection.” In contrast, the retina exhibited upregulation of the complement system as the sole inflammatory-specific pathway, underscoring the distinct role of complement activation in the response of this tissue to peripheral inflammation. This more confined response of the retina is also confirmed by the lower or absent activation of other typical pro-inflammatory factors, such as Icam, Vcam, and Nf-kB (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Discussion

To our best knowledge, this is the first report of the robust production of C3 specifically from the RPE and retina in response to systemic LPS-injection, at mRNA and protein level. This is in line with a known role for the RPE as a key immune modulator in ocular homeostasis [29, 30], and with its ability to produce complement factors [31–33]. This is also in agreement with previous reports of increased complement expression in the whole homogenized eyecup [27, 34]. The detection of both C3 mRNA and protein in RPE and retina suggests that the eye independently produces C3, resulting in a local complement activation even without blood-retina barrier (BRB) disruption. Indeed, reportedly, a single LPS injection does not disrupt the BRB [35, 36], and this barrier blocks the entry of complement factors and LPS in the RPE and retina [18]. The absence of MAC components, despite C3 upregulation, indicates that complement activation in this model favors immunomodulation over lytic pathways, a finding that diverges from studies emphasizing MAC-driven pathological features in eye diseases [37].

The upregulation in the RPE of complement proteins including C3, alongside other inflammatory pathways, suggests the potential of RPE cells to build up a strong inflammatory response. Despite that, this is the first time that a link between peripheral inflammation and local activation of RPE cells is shown so convincingly, supporting the hypothesis that complement involvement in AMD might arise from RPE, among other, cells [38]. More interesting is the selective and sole increased presence of complement proteins in the retina. Nonetheless, the observation that systemic inflammation triggers local complement production and expression clearly marks the need of considering systemic factors, such as chronic inflammatory stimuli as potential drivers of ocular pathologies. Indeed, the complement system might act as a bridge between systemic inflammation-driven diseases and eye pathologies, whose association has been established through the years. In particular, diseases such as psoriasis have been associated with wet AMD [3], while rheumatoid arthritis was associated with increased risk of dry AMD [2]. Moreover, oxidative stress-related activities such as smoking, proved to be linked to retinal damage partially via complement activation [39].

This study also shows the cell-specific contributions to local complement activation in the retina, particularly from the astrocytes. Indeed, while the available literature mainly focus on the role of microglia and Müller glia as main sources of complement proteins [26, 31], in our experimental conditions, astrocytes appear to play an important role too. An involvement of astrocytes is being increasingly recognized in neuroinflammatory processes. For example, their role in complement production has been explored in post-surgical brain inflammation, in which C3 production from hippocampal-derived astrocytes has been demonstrated [40]. At the same time, astrocytes have been proposed as the main source of C3 in a murine model of Parkinson’s disease [41]. In the retina, the work of Schafer and colleagues demonstrated that during acute white light-induced photoreceptor damage (a locally-induced retinal damage), C3d is mainly expressed by RPE and astrocytes [25]. The identification of astrocytes as a source of C3 in the ganglion cell layer adds complexity to the retinal immune landscape and raises questions about neuron-glia and astrocytes-microglia crosstalk during inflammation. In this regard, it was shown that in the brain, in response to systemic inflammation, C3 acts as a communication factor between astrocytes and other surrounding cells, propagating inflammation through the modulation of microglia phenotype [42, 43], or impairing neuronal development of nearby neurons [44, 45]. This amount of data gathered in the brain highlight the need to perform similar research in the eye, and underline the possibility that astrocytes-produced complement factors might initiate complement-related mechanisms in the retina.

In our work, the temporal specificity of local response—peaking at 24 h post-LPS—mirrors the systemic upregulation in serum C3. These findings challenge the traditional view of ocular immune privilege by demonstrating that systemic inflammation can indirectly disrupt local immune regulation. This may however reflect a difference between acute inflammatory models and chronic degenerative diseases. Indeed, we cannot exclude that a different type of stimulus might induce MAC’s production, justifying the abundance of CD59 detected in the retina. Moreover, the delayed upregulation of complement factor 1 subtypes rules out an early involvement of the classical pathway and of this specific factor in the ocular response to systemic inflammation, while at the same time, it indicates a possible involvement in mediating long-term or prolonged inflammatory responses.

The findings presented in this work have important implications for complement-targeted therapies. The local C3 production in the RPE supports intravitreal delivery of complement inhibitors like pegcetacoplan [46]. However, the transient efficacy and adverse effects of such therapies may arise from an only partial understanding of temporal and spatial complement dynamics. Moreover, information concerning the exact amount of complement inhibitor able to reach the RPE and retina are limited, making it difficult to identify the effective dosage and time of administration, especially when resident cells are able to produce complement factors. In addition to that, if the source of complement is clearly determined, a targeted therapy toward specific cell types might increase its effectiveness.

A few limitations of this study are worth mentioning. Multiple administration of a lower dose might more efficiently mimic conditions of chronic/systemic inflammation than the used single dose of LPS. Moreover, it might be interesting to investigate the long-term effects of complement activation on the retina, beyond the herein investigated latest time-point of 72 h p.i. Despite the focus of this study was complement 3, it would be also important to investigate the role of other complement factors in the murine retina and RPE, such as C1, that might have a role in the prolonged effects of complement activation on ocular homeostasis. Finally, the choroid, another relevant structure for AMD pathophysiology, was not analyzed. Future work will be needed to investigate how systemic inflammation modulates complement activity in the choroidal compartment.

Using a systemic LPS-induced inflammation model combined with proteomic and immunohistochemistry analyses, this study demonstrates for the first time that peripheral inflammation triggers robust local production of complement factor 3 (C3) in the RPE and retina. This result provides direct evidence of systemic-to-local complement signaling in the eye and advances our understanding of how peripheral immune challenges affect ocular health. In the last decades, the complement system has drawn attention in ophthalmology, because of many clinical observations pointing at its role in the development of sight-threatening eye diseases, such as age related macular degeneration (AMD) and glaucoma [47]. Preclinical models, as well, revealed complement components accumulation in the outer retina, validating a potential role in pathological processes [48], but without clarifying the source and the etiopathological mechanisms of complement activation.

Conclusions

Overall, our experimental approach represents a significant advance in studying systemic-to-local immune signaling in the eye. By combining systemic inflammatory challenge with high-resolution proteomics of the RPE and retina, we could identify C3 as a key mediator in this process, and create a base for future studies aimed at understanding the implications of systemic inflammation on ocular health. This perspective could pave the way for safer and more effective treatments for inflammation-driven ocular diseases.
