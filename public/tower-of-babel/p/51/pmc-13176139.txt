Scoping review of apixaban and rivaroxaban dosing for atrial fibrillation and venous thromboembolism in advanced chronic kidney disease
Authors: Sophie Gillis, Emma Phelan, Jennifer Pitman, Shanna Trenaman, Marisa Battistella, Chiranjeev Sanyal, Christopher Daley, Karthik Tennankore, Louise Gillis, Jo-Anne Wilson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIndividuals with advanced chronic kidney disease (CKD) have elevated risks for stroke, venous thromboembolism (VTE), and bleeding, yet their exclusion from major trials leaves uncertainty about dosing of apixaban and rivaroxaban.AimTo map existing evidence on apixaban and rivaroxaban dosing practices and associated clinical outcomes in patients with advanced CKD, predominantly not receiving dialysis, treated for atrial fibrillation (AF) and/or venous thromboembolism (VTE), and to identify knowledge gaps in this area.MethodA scoping review was conducted following the Arksey and O’Malley framework and reported per PRISMA-ScR guidelines. The search, developed with a medical librarian, was conducted in MEDLINE, Embase, Cochrane, and CINAHL through May 7, 2025. Duplicates were removed, and records managed in Covidence. Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts. We included studies of apixaban or rivaroxaban dosing in adults with advanced CKD (mostly non-dialysis) with AF or VTE reporting thromboembolic or major bleeding outcomes. Non-English articles and publication types (e.g., abstracts, case series, editorials) were excluded. Data was extracted using a structured form and summarized to address the study aim.ResultsThirty-four studies were identified: seven VTE, six combined AF/VTE, and 21 AF. Standard-dose apixaban and rivaroxaban were more commonly used and associated with lower, though non-significant, rates of recurrent VTE and major bleeding compared to warfarin. In apixaban groups, standard-dose 5 mg BID had fewer VTE events than the 2.5 mg BID dose but more major bleeding events which were also not statistically significant. In AF studies, both standard and reduced-dose apixaban (5 mg BID and 2.5 mg BID), and reduced-dose rivaroxaban (15 mg daily), significantly reduced major bleeding risk versus warfarin. Stroke reductions were not consistently significant but trended lower with apixaban and rivaroxaban compared to warfarin. In the only AF study comparing apixaban and rivaroxaban, both doses of rivaroxaban (20 mg or 15 mg daily) had higher bleeding rates than apixaban. One of four AF studies showed higher bleeding with standard versus reduced-dose apixaban.ConclusionThis review summarizes apixaban and rivaroxaban dosing for AF/VTE in advanced CKD, revealing gaps such as limited dose-comparison studies, heterogeneous outcomes and sparse data in non-dialysis. Robust trials are urgently needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11096-026-02096-1.

Impact statements

This review offers pharmacists a comprehensive synthesis of dosing patterns and clinical outcomes for apixaban and rivaroxaban in patients with advanced CKD, AF and/or VTE, a population often excluded from randomized clinical trials.The findings provide an overview of current apixaban and rivaroxaban dosing practices and associated outcome trends that may help pharmacists contextualize clinical dosing decisions for patients with advanced CKD.By addressing evidence gaps and clinical practice patterns, this review empowers pharmacists to support individualized anticoagulation strategies and promote evidence-based care in high-risk CKD populations.

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is an increasingly prevalent global health condition, affecting approximately 9.5% of the population worldwide, with a similar burden observed in Canada [1, 2]. CKD is a well-established independent risk factor for atrial fibrillation (AF), with both prevalence and incidence rising alongside CKD severity [3–5]. A Medicare cohort study reported a 2-year AF incidence of 12.2% in patients with category 1–2 CKD and 14.4% in those with category 3–5 CKD, compared to 7.5% in individuals without CKD [6].Category 4 and 5 CKD further increase the risk of ischemic stroke, systemic embolism (SE), and major bleeding, complicating anticoagulation decisions in this population [7]. From a non-interventional analysis, each 10 mL/min/1.73m2 decline in estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), may increase the risk of stroke or SE by 7% [8]. A meta-analysis of landmark AF trials of direct-acting oral anticoagulants (DOACs) in which included patients with moderate CKD, demonstrated reduced risks of stroke, SE and major bleeding compared to warfarin [9]. However, pivotal clinical trials of DOACs excluded patients with category 4 and 5 CKD, defined as either a creatinine clearance of less than 30 mL/min with rivaroxaban, or in the case of apixaban less than 25 mL/min, resulting in limited evidence to guide anticoagulant dosing in this high-risk group [10–12]. In addition to AF-related risks, CKD is associated with a significantly higher risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE).The Longitudinal Investigation of Thromboembolism Etiology study found VTE incidence rates of 4.5 per 1000 person-years in patients with categories 3–4 CKD, compared to 1.5 per 1000 in those with normal renal function [13, 14]. Another non-interventional analysis reported that each 10 mL/min/1.73 m2 decline in eGFR may increase VTE risk by 1.13-fold [15]. Despite these risks, patients with category 4 and 5 CKD were excluded from key apixaban or rivaroxaban randomized controlled trials (RCT) for VTE treatment [16–19], highlighting a persistent evidence gap in guiding anticoagulation dosing decisions.Reduced drug clearance in this population can lead to drug accumulation, making optimal dosing for efficacy and safety uncertain. Consequently, in clinical practice, apixaban and rivaroxaban have emerged as the most commonly used DOACs for stroke prevention in AF and treatment of VTE, largely due to their favorable pharmacokinetics and relatively low renal clearance (27% and 35%, respectively) [20–22]. These agents offer several advantages over vitamin K antagonists such as warfarin, including rapid onset of action, predictable anticoagulant effects, fixed dosing, minimal need for monitoring, fewer drug-drug interactions, and no known association with increased vascular calcification [23]. While there are clear dosing guidelines for those with mild to moderate CKD, there is a notable lack of RCT data to guide dosing of apixaban and rivaroxaban in individuals with category 4 and non-dialysis category 5 CKD. Current dosing strategies are often informed by pharmacokinetic modeling or extrapolated from RCTs involving patients with moderate kidney disease or those on hemodialysis [24–27]. Despite their growing use, evidence-based dosing recommendations for apixaban and rivaroxaban in patients with category 4 and 5 CKD not receiving dialysis are limited, leading clinicians to depend on observational evidence to inform decisions. This review focused on advanced non-dialysis CKD, including studies involving categories 4 and 5 CKD or where data for these groups were separable, and excluding those limited to eGFR > 30 or dialysis-only population.

Aim

This scoping review aims to map the existing evidence on apixaban and rivaroxaban dosing practices and associated clinical outcomes in patients with advanced CKD, predominantly not receiving dialysis, treated for AF and/or VTE, and to identify knowledge gaps in this area.

Method

This scoping review followed the methodological framework by Arksey and O’Malley, refined by Levac and colleagues [28, 29]. Key steps included defining the research question, selecting studies, charting data, and summarizing findings. The review was registered with the Open Science Framework and reported in accordance with PRISMA-ScR and updated guidelines from Peters and colleagues [30, 31]. We conducted a scoping review rather than a systematic review to characterize the breadth of existing evidence and identify knowledge gaps, rather than to address a narrowly defined question through quantitative synthesis [32].The review was guided by the following research question: What dosing regimens of apixaban and rivaroxaban have been reported in the literature for the treatment of AF and/or VTE in individuals with category 4 or 5 non-dialysis dependent CKD (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73m2), and how do these dosing strategies relate to the occurrence of thromboembolic and hemorrhagic outcomes in this population? To answer our research question, we employed a population (category 4/ 5 CKD with AF and/or VTE [deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE)]), concept (anticoagulant dosage regimens), and context (thromboembolic [ischemic stroke, SE, recurrent VTE] or major bleeding outcomes) framework. A search strategy combining controlled vocabulary and natural language was developed with a librarian, tested for performance, and peer-reviewed using the PRESS checklist [33]. Searches were conducted on May 7, 2025, in MEDLINE (Ovid), Embase (Elsevier), Cochrane (Wiley), and CINAHL (Ebsco) (Supplementary Table 1). Citation chaining and reference checks of relevant reviews were done to find additional studies. Grey literature was sought via targeted anticoagulation website searches using key words related to apixaban, rivaroxaban, CKD, AF and/or VTE to locate relevant guidelines, reports, and resources.Study selection included literature on apixaban and rivaroxaban dosing in adults with category 4 and 5 CKD, predominantly not receiving dialysis, with AF and/or VTE, focusing on thromboembolic or major bleeding outcomes. Exclusions were studies on populations outside this scope (exclusively eGFR > 30 mL/min/1.73m2, dialysis-only patients, eGFR not defined, pediatrics and animals), non-English publications, abstracts only, undefined dosing, outcomes not of interest, and publication types unlikely to provide primary data (case series, editorials, guidelines). Article selection was managed with Covidence, where duplicate records were automatically removed prior to screening [34]. After duplicate removal, two reviewers screened titles/abstracts independently, with a third resolving conflicts (J.W, S.G, S.T). Full-text articles deemed eligible were screened by the same process as title and abstract screening (J.W, S.G, S.T, J.P, M.B). Charting of data entailed utilizing a predefined Microsoft Word template, which was pilot tested on a sample of studies for clarity and completeness. Extracted data included author, date, country, study design, follow-up, kidney function and equation used (2009 or 2021 CKD-EPI, MDRD, Cockcroft Gault creatinine clearance), treatment (apixaban or rivaroxaban dose by kidney function), comparison groups, baseline characteristics (age, sex, CHA2DS2-VASc, HAS-BLED score), and clinical outcomes (thromboembolic and major bleeding). For each study, two team members extracted the data, which was then reviewed by a third team member for verification and consensus (J.W, S.G, S.T, J.P, C.S, C.D). Articles were summarized and categorized from all sources and according to whether it was sourced from a bibliographic database or through supplemental grey literature searching. The range of apixaban and rivaroxaban dosing regimens used to manage AF and/or VTE in individuals with CKD stages 4 and 5 was charted, and associated clinical outcomes, were summarized.

Risk of bias was assessed using two tools. The Cochrane Risk of Bias (ROB) tool was applied to five RCTs, evaluating seven domains including randomization, blinding, and outcome reporting, each rated as low, high, or unclear risk (Supplementary Table 2) [35]. For 29 cohort studies, Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) was used, awarding up to nine stars across selection, comparability, and outcome/exposure assessment [36]. Two reviewers.

(E.P. and J.W.) independently assessed study quality using the appropriate tool. A risk-of-bias assessment, while not routine for scoping reviews, was included to contextualize evidence quality amid study heterogeneity.

Results

Study selection

Figure 1 presents the PRIMSA-ScR flow diagram. A total of 2356 studies were retrieved through electronic database searches, with two additional records identified through citation chaining of reviews. After removal of duplicates, 1844 unique articles were included for screening. Title and abstract screening using our inclusion criteria yielded 280 studies for full text review screening. Study characteristics

Of the 34 included studies, seven focused exclusively on VTE, six reported cohorts with both VTE and AF, and 21 investigated AF [37–69]. Among the seven VTE studies (2014–2025), four were retrospective, two were prospective observational, and one was a sub-analysis of the EINSTEIN-DVT and EINSTEIN-PE RCTs (Supplementary Table 3) [37–43]. Most of these studies were conducted in the United States [37, 39, 40, 42, 43]. The six studies involving both VTE and AF were retrospective (Supplementary Table 4) [44–49]. All six studies, conducted in the United States between 2017 and 2024, included four single-center and two multicenter designs, with data collected via chart reviews or electronic medical records [44–49].

Of the 21 AF studies published between 2012 and 2025, 14 were retrospective, utilizing administrative databases or chart reviews (Supplementary Table 5) [50–69]. The remaining seven were prospective and included two randomized trials, two sub-analyses of previous RCTs, two observational studies, and one sub-analysis of a prior multicenter cohort study. These studies were conducted across multiple regions, including North America, Europe, and Asia.

Quality assessment

The ROB tool was used for three RCTs and two RCT sub-analyses, all rated as low risk of bias [35]. The NOS assessed 29 cohort studies; 27 were high quality (7–8 stars), while two received 5–6 stars due to lack of blinded outcome assessment or unclear baseline reporting of thromboembolic and bleeding events [36] (Supplementary Table 2).

VTE studies in advanced CKD: apixaban and rivaroxaban dosing and outcomes

All seven VTE studies included recurrent VTE and major bleeding as an outcome, with four reporting major bleeding as the primary outcome (Table 1) [37–43]. Four studies included participants with category 4 or 5 CKD with some on dialysis; one included people with category 3b and higher; another included those with eGFR < 50; and one included all CKD categories, though the results were extrapolatable to category 4 and 5 CKD. Most studies defined recurrent VTE based on International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes located in chart reviews or electronic medical records (EMRs), while two studies defined it as either a fatal or nonfatal DVT or PE, or as a composite of these events confirmed through diagnostic imaging. All but one study defined major bleeding according to the criteria of the ISTH. Five studies compared mostly standard-dose of apixaban 10 mg BID (twice daily) for 1 week, then 5 mg BID and/or rivaroxaban 15 mg BID for 3 weeks, then 20 mg daily compared to warfarin [37–40, 42], while 1 study compared apixaban maintenance standard dose (5 mg BID) and apixaban reduced-dose (2.5 mg BID) combined to warfarin [41]. Only one study compared apixaban standard dose (10 mg BID for 1 week then 5 mg BID, or 5 mg BID without loading dose) with reduced dose (10 mg BID for 1 week, then 2.5 mg BID, or 2.5 mg BID without No significant differences were found in incidence of recurrent VTE or major bleeding between these comparisons. A trend toward lower incidence of VTE and major bleeding was observed with standard-dose apixaban and rivaroxaban compared to comparators, mainly warfarin [37, 38, 40, 41]. Reported warfarin TTR (time in therapeutic range) ranged from 35–56% (Supplementary Table 3). A sub-analysis found no significant difference between maintenance standard-dose (5 mg BID) and reduced-dose (2.5 mg BID) apixaban versus warfarin [42]. In a large U.S. claims-based study of category 4 CKD (n = 1363), VTE rates with apixaban were 5.8% versus 7.8% with warfarin (HR 0.70; 95% CI: 0.44–1.13), and major bleeding was 12.6% versus 12.8% (HR 0.96; 95% CI: 0.67–1.36) [40]. Similar non-significant trends were seen in category 5 CKD and dialysis cohorts [40]. Comparing initial standard-dose (10 mg BID for 1 week then 5 mg BID or 5 mg BID without loading dose) to reduced-dose (10 mg BID for 1 week then 2.5 mg BID or 2.5 mg without loading dose) regimens, VTE rates were 6.4% versus 7.7% (P = 0.78), and major bleeding 16.7% versus 0% (P = 0.23), also not statistically significant [43].

Combined AF and VTE Studies in advanced CKD: apixaban and rivaroxaban dosing and outcomes

Six studies included both AF and VTE participants on apixaban, mostly with AF (Table 2) [44–49]. Five studies focused on major bleeding as the primary outcome, with stroke/SE and VTE as secondary outcomes. All involved patients with category 4 or 5 CKD, some on dialysis. Major bleeding was defined by ISTH criteria; stroke/SE and VTE were identified via ICD codes, chart reviews, or imaging confirmed by EMR data. Four studies reported mean CHADS2-VASc and HAS-BLED scores of approximately 5 and 3, respectively (Supplementary Table 4) [46–49]. Four studies compared apixaban (5 mg BID and 2.5 mg BID) to warfarin, finding no significant differences in stroke/SE or VTE, though events were more frequent with warfarin [44, 45, 47, 48]. Major bleeding was generally higher with warfarin but only significantly lower with apixaban in one study (1.5% versus 8.4%, P < 0.001) [47]. AF studies in advanced CKD: Apixaban and Rivaroxaban dosing and outcomes

Of the 21 AF studies, 20 identified both stroke/SE and major bleeding as primary outcomes, while one study listed them as secondary outcomes (Table 3) [50–70]. All studies enrolled individuals with CKD category 4 or 5, including some receiving dialysis. Seven studies also included patients with earlier categories of CKD where data could be extracted from supplementary information [50, 54, 56, 58, 59, 61, 63]. The definitions of major bleeding and stroke/SE were consistent with those described above. The TTR with warfarin, when reported, ranged between 38–63%. CHADS2-VASc and HAS-BLED scores were reported for most studies, ranging from 3.8–6.0 and 2.0–4.3, respectively (Supplementary Table 5). Regarding dosing comparisons, six studies evaluated both standard and reduced-dose apixaban (5 mg BID and 2.5 mg BID) versus warfarin [50, 54, 58, 63, 65, 68] and one compared reduced-dose apixaban (2.5 mg BID) to warfarin [53]. Two studies directly compared standard-dose apixaban (5 mg BID) with reduced-dose apixaban (2.5 mg BID) [60, 69]. Another six studies assessed standard-dose rivaroxaban (20 mg daily) and/or reduced-dose rivaroxaban (10–15 mg daily) compared to warfarin [51, 52, 56, 59, 61, 67]. One additional study examined reduced-dose apixaban (1.25–2.5 mg BID) and reduced-dose rivaroxaban (10–15 mg daily) versus warfarin [64]. Finally, four of the 21 studies included multiple cohort comparisons: standard or reduced-dose apixaban (5 mg BID or 2.5 mg BID) and rivaroxaban (20 mg daily or 15 mg daily) versus warfarin, as well as head-to-head comparisons of apixaban and rivaroxaban at both standard and reduced-doses [55, 57, 62, 66, 70]. Six studies reported a significant difference in major bleeding when standard and reduced doses of apixaban (5 mg BID and 2.5 mg BID) were compared to warfarin [50, 54, 58, 63, 65, 68]. However, only one study found a significant difference in stroke/SE outcomes (HR 0.61; 95% CI 0.39–0.94) [58]. Another study found a significant difference in stroke/SE outcomes only when standard-dose apixaban 5 mg BID was compared to warfarin (HR 0.59; 95% CI 0.37–0.93) [68]. One study comparing standard-dose apixaban (5 mg BID) to reduced-dose apixaban (2.5 mg BID) found a significantly higher risk of major bleeding with the standard dose (HR 1.63; 95% CI: 1.04–2.54) [69].

Among the studies comparing standard and reduced-dose rivaroxaban to warfarin, only one reported a significant difference in stroke/SE outcomes (HR 0.78; 95% CI: 0.62–0.99), and one reported a significant reduction in major bleeding when reduced-dose rivaroxaban (< 20 mg daily) was compared to warfarin (HR 0.68; 95% CI: 0.47–0.99) [52, 56]. From the studies with multiple cohort comparisons, one study reported significant reductions in both stroke/SE (HR 0.53; 95% CI: 0.33–0.86) and major bleeding (HR 0.57; 95% CI: 0.46–0.71) outcomes when standard and reduced-dose apixaban, rivaroxaban, and other DOACs were compared to warfarin [62]. Additionally, a significant difference in major bleeding outcomes were reported when standard and reduced-dose apixaban or rivaroxaban was compared to warfarin [70]. One study cohort comparing standard and reduced-dose rivaroxaban to apixaban reported a significant increase in major bleeding with rivaroxaban (HR 1.69; 95% CI: 1.22–2.15) [55].

Discussion

Our scoping review found that standard-dose apixaban and rivaroxaban were more commonly associated with lower reported rates of recurrent VTE and major bleeding compared to warfarin, though differences were not statistically significant. Among apixaban users, the 5 mg BID dose was linked to fewer recurrent VTE events than 2.5 mg BID, but a higher number of major bleeding events, without significant differences reported.

Most VTE studies in our scoping review used standard-dose apixaban or rivaroxaban [37–42] for eGFR < 30 mL/min, aligning with product labels for patients with eGFR 15–29 mL/min. However, use is generally not advised below 15 mL/min due to limited data (Table 4) [71–76]. A prior systematic review found standard or reduced-dose DOACs had similar efficacy and safety to warfarin for VTE in patients mostly with category 3 CKD or higher, aligning with our scoping review findings in CKD category 4 or higher [77]. A meta-analysis (doses not reported) showed DOACs significantly reduced VTE risk across all CKD categories (HR 0.14; 95% CI: 0.04–0.54), with no difference in major bleeding [78]. Our findings in multiple AF studies comparing apixaban or rivaroxaban with warfarin, found major bleeding was significantly lower with both standard and reduced-dose apixaban, as well as with reduced-dose rivaroxaban. Significant reductions in stroke/SE were seen in only a few analyses, though overall trends favored apixaban and rivaroxaban compared to warfarin. Comparisons between standard and reduced-dose of apixaban showed mixed findings, with one study reporting increased major bleeding with the apixaban standard-dose (5 mg BID). A head-to-head comparison of standard and reduced-dose rivaroxaban versus apixaban demonstrated significantly lower major bleeding rates with apixaban.

A systematic review and meta-analysis (without dosing details) in AF patients reported a 20% lower major bleeding risk with DOACs versus warfarin and a trend toward reduced stroke/SE across CKD stages, aligning with our findings [78]. Similarly, a scoping review found overall stroke incidence comparable between DOACs and warfarin, with apixaban showing improved stroke/SE prevention in advanced CKD, though dosing was not reported [79]. Another systematic review and meta-analysis also concluded that DOACs, particularly apixaban offered superior efficacy and safety compared to warfarin in patients with AF, regardless of CKD severity [80]. Our scoping review found lower major bleeding rates with standard and reduced-dose apixaban and reduced-dose rivaroxaban. A recent meta-analysis (without dosing details) similarly reported significantly reduced bleeding with both agents in patients with category 5 CKD or on renal replacement therapy (apixaban: OR 0.66; 95% CI: 0.52–0.85; rivaroxaban: OR 0.58; 95% CI: 0.35–0.94) [27]. As shown in Table 4, product labeling in Canada and Europe does not recommend the use of apixaban for AF in patients with eGFR < 15 mL/min, highlighting a significant gap in guidance. Our scoping review maps supporting the use of both standard and reduced-dose apixaban in this population, aligning with U.S. recommendations and prior studies in hemodialysis patients [24–27]. Our scoping review uniquely summarizes clinical practice dosing of apixaban and rivaroxaban in advanced CKD, whereas systematic reviews and meta-analyses often reported similar outcomes but lacked specific dosing information, underscoring the need for further trials to guide dose selection in this population.

This scoping review has several strengths. The search strategy was peer-reviewed and developed with a medical librarian. We included diverse study designs to ensure broad evidence capture. Study quality was assessed to support interpretability. Most studies used ISTH criteria for major bleeding and reported apixaban and rivaroxaban specific dosing and outcomes for category 4 and 5 CKD as well provided the equations used to estimate renal function, aiding dose evaluation.

This study has several limitations. Most included studies were observational, with substantial heterogeneity in dosing strategies, outcome definitions, and kidney function assessment, which limited comparability and synthesis. Although a formal risk-of-bias assessment was conducted, variations in study design and reporting restrict the strength of comparative conclusions. As a scoping review, this work is limited by potential publication bias, reliance on available data without quantitative synthesis (as results were summarized descriptively rather than statistically combined), and the possibility that some relevant studies were missed despite a comprehensive search. These gaps highlight the need for standardized, high-quality research in this population.

Conclusion

This scoping review maps clinical practice dosing and clinical outcomes of apixaban and rivaroxaban in category 4 or 5 CKD patients, mostly not on dialysis, treated for AF or VTE. In VTE studies, standard-dose apixaban and rivaroxaban were more commonly used and linked to lower, though non-significant, rates of recurrent events and bleeding versus warfarin. In AF studies, both standard and reduced-dose apixaban and reduced-dose rivaroxaban reduced major bleeding risk, with stroke/SE reductions less consistent but generally favoring DOACs over warfarin. Dosing should be guided by individual risk–benefit profiles. Our findings underscore gaps in current practice and highlight the urgent need for high-quality RCTs directly comparing standard and reduced-dose DOACs to better serve this high-risk population.
