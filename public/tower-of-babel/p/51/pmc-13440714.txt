Perceptions, access, and outcomes of cadaveric training among neurosurgery residents in Mexico: A nationwide survey
Authors: Elizabeth Escamilla-Chávez, Estefany Gilberty Mendoza-Verdin, José Carlos Rocha-Villegas, Mar Borrayo-Dorado Sheila María del, Ricardo Adrián Crortés-Monterrubio, Noé Pérez-Carrillo, José Carlos Hernández-González
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Cadaveric dissection is central to neurosurgical education; however, access to structured training remains uneven, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. Data on cadaveric exposure, perceived value, and barriers among neurosurgery residents in Mexico are limited.Methods: A nationwide, cross-sectional survey was distributed electronically to residents from 30 accredited training centers in Mexico, assessing cadaveric exposure, private course participation, costs, satisfaction, institutional interest, and resident-driven initiatives. Pearson’s Chi-square tests and Kruskal–Wallis analysis were performed.Results: A total of 115 residents from 26 centers completed the survey. Nearly all (97.4%) considered cadaveric practice essential, yet 57.0% reported no prior exposure during residency. Residency year was significantly associated with cadaveric exposure (χ2 [4] = 36.20, p < 0.001, Cramér’s V = 0.564): no postgraduate year (PGY)1 residents reported any exposure, compared to 88.9% of PGY5 residents. Attendance at private courses followed a similar gradient (χ2 [4] = 22.89, p < 0.001, Cramér’s V = 0.446). Among those with prior exposure, 72.2% were very satisfied. Interest in institutional access was nearly universal (99.1%), and prior exposure was not associated with having proposed institutional initiatives (p = 0.600).Conclusion: Neurosurgery residents in Mexico show strong interest in cadaveric training, and those with prior exposure report high satisfaction. Access remains limited and dependent on private initiatives, with the exposure gradient across training years reflecting personal access rather than deliberate educational design. Integrating structured cadaveric training into residency programs is necessary to close this gap and support more equitable neurosurgical education.

INTRODUCTION

Neurosurgical training is built on the gradual acquisition of precise psychomotor skills, visuospatial reasoning, and a detailed three-dimensional command of neuroanatomy; few fields in medicine demand as much from trainees over as many years. Apprenticeships in the operating room remain central to that process, yet it has grown harder to sustain. Reduced working hours, tighter patient safety requirements, and medicolegal pressures have cut into residents’ exposure to complex cases and the repetitive practice those cases provide.[25] Simulation-based education has absorbed part of that loss. Among available modalities, cadaveric dissection is the closest to operating on a real patient, offering accurate anatomy, realistic tissue handling, and the ability to rehearse surgical positioning and full operative sequences without placing anyone at risk.[2] Its educational value is well supported in the literature: systematic reviews and narrative analyses across surgical specialties consistently show that cadaveric workshops improve technical skill acquisition, anatomical understanding, and trainee confidence, especially for procedures requiring precise manipulation within tight operative corridors.[1,3,4,7]

The role of cadaveric dissection in neurosurgical training extends beyond skill practice. Rhoton’s Laboratory established microsurgical anatomy, developed through meticulous cadaveric dissection and anatomical teaching, as the foundation of safe and precise neurosurgery; its influence shaped the training of generations of neurosurgeons worldwide.[11]

Professional societies have positioned themselves clearly on this. The European Association of Neurosurgical Societies (EANS) Young Neurosurgeons’ Committee has described cadaveric dissection as a core element of neurosurgical education, necessary for three-dimensional anatomical understanding, microsurgical dexterity, and collaborative learning, while naming the practical barriers that remain: cost, logistics, and specimen availability.[23,25] Virtual reality and three-dimensional modeling are useful adjuncts, but they do not replicate the haptic feedback and tissue handling that cadaveric work provides.[6]

Access to structured cadaveric programs is uneven, and the gap is sharpest in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Financial constraints, ethical and legal regulations, and limited institutional infrastructure continue to restrict the implementation of cadaveric laboratories, even where programs in resource-limited settings have already shown that such training is both feasible and educationally effective.[5,12]

In Mexico, published data on neurosurgery residents’ cadaveric exposure, perceived educational value, financial barriers, and institutional support are scarce. Understanding what residents experience and think is necessary to build programs that are structured, equitable, and sustainable at a national level. This study uses a nationwide survey to assess residents’ interest in cadaveric training, their access to it, their satisfaction with available programs, the outcomes they perceive, and the institutional initiatives that support or hinder them.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A nationwide, cross-sectional survey was conducted to evaluate access to, interest in, and perceived outcomes of cadaveric training among neurosurgery residents in Mexico. The national neurosurgery residency system in Mexico comprises approximately 443 residents distributed across 30 accredited training centers. All postgraduate year (PGY) neurosurgery residents from these institutions were eligible to participate; there were no exclusion criteria based on residency year, institution, or prior exposure to cadaveric training. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Strengthening the reporting of observational studies in epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

Neurosurgery residents with academic interest in surgical education and microsurgical training developed a structured, anonymous questionnaire. The survey was administered electronically through Google Forms and distributed through resident chiefs across participating institutions. Questions were predominantly closed-ended to allow standardized data collection and descriptive analysis. Before distribution, faculty members from three neurosurgery programs reviewed the questionnaire for clarity, content validity, and relevance to neurosurgical education.

Not all respondents completed every survey item; analyses were therefore performed using available-case analysis, with the denominator adjusted for each question. Responses marked as “Not applicable” were excluded from specific subanalyses.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive statistical analysis was performed. Categorical variables were summarized as absolute frequencies and percentages. To examine associations between residency year and cadaveric exposure, and between residency year and private course attendance, Pearson’s Chi-square tests were performed on contingency tables; the strength of association was quantified using Cramér’s V. Given the ordinal nature of the session frequency variable, a Kruskal–Wallis test was additionally used to compare session counts across residency years. The association between prior cadaveric exposure and resident-driven institutional proposals was assessed using Pearson’s Chi-square. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Data analysis was conducted using Python (SciPy library).

Ethical considerations

The study involved an anonymous, noninterventional survey and did not collect sensitive or identifiable personal data. Participation in the survey implied informed consent. Formal ethics committee approval was not required in accordance with national regulations and institutional policies. The study was conducted in compliance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

RESULTS

A total of 115 neurosurgery residents from 26 training centers completed the survey. Respondents were distributed across all 5 years of residency, with the largest group corresponding to PGY3 residents (27.0%), followed by PGY1 and PGY2 (20.9% each), and PGY4 and PGY5 (15.7% each) [Table 1 and Figure 1].

Perceived importance of cadaveric training

Nearly all respondents (97.4%, n = 112) considered cadaveric practice essential before performing neurosurgical procedures on patients. Only three residents (2.6%) did not consider it essential. This perception was consistent across all residency years [Figure 2].

Perceived importance of cadaveric training. Residents’ perception of the necessity of cadaveric practice before performing neurosurgical procedures on patients (n=115).

Exposure to cadaveric dissection during residency

Of 114 residents who provided valid responses, 57.0% (n = 65) reported no prior cadaveric practice during residency, while 43.0% (n = 49) reported at least one session. Among those with exposure, 20.2% (n = 23) reported one session, 7.9% (n = 9) two sessions, 5.3% (n = 6) three sessions, and 9.6% (n = 11) more than three sessions [Figure 3].

Cadaveric exposure during residency by postgraduate year. Proportion of neurosurgery residents with and without cadaveric practice during residency, stratified by postgraduate year (1–5). χ2 (4) = 36.20, p < 0.001, Cramér’s V = 0.564 (n = 114).

A statistically significant association was found between residency year and cadaveric exposure (χ2 [4] = 36.20, p < 0.001, Cramér’s V = 0.564), indicating a large effect. Kruskal–Wallis analysis of ordinal session frequency confirmed this result (H = 42.27, p < 0.001). Exposure increased progressively with training level: none of the PGY1 residents reported any cadaveric practice, compared to 88.9% of PGY5 residents. The median number of sessions remained zero through PGY3, rising to a median of one session in PGY4 and three sessions in PGY5 [Table 2].

Attendance at private cadaveric training courses

Of 114 residents who provided valid responses, 36.0% (n = 41) reported attending private cadaveric courses in Mexico and 16.5% (n = 19) attended courses abroad. A total of 58.8% (n = 67) had not attended any private cadaveric course. Attendance to private courses also increased significantly with residency year (χ2 [4] = 22.89, p < 0.001, Cramér’s V = 0.446), with 8.3% of PGY1 residents having attended any course compared to 77.8% of PGY5 residents [Table 3 and Figure 4].

Objective and satisfaction of cadaveric practice

Among the 56 residents with prior cadaveric exposure who provided valid responses regarding the objective of their practice, microsurgical simulation was the most common goal (58.9%, n = 33), followed by anatomical study (41.1%, n = 23) [Figure 5]. Of these same residents, 72.2% (n = 39) reported being very satisfied with cadaveric training in relation to their neurosurgical practice objectives, while 27.8% (n = 15) reported being moderately satisfied. No resident reported low or no satisfaction.

Primary objective of cadaveric practice. Distribution of objectives reported by residents with prior cadaveric exposure, distinguishing between microsurgical simulation and anatomical study (n = 56).

Cost of cadaveric training courses

Cost data were available for 50 residents after excluding those who reported not applicable. The most common scenario was attendance through full scholarships or no-cost courses (36.0%, n = 18). Among residents who paid out-of-pocket, the most frequent cost range was $100–500 USD (28.0%, n = 14), followed by $500–1,000 USD (14.0%, n = 7). Expenditures below $100 USD were reported by 10.0% (n = 5), while higher costs of $1,000–3,000 USD and above $3,000 USD were reported by 4.0% (n = 2) and 8.0% (n = 4) of residents, respectively [Figure 6].

Cost of private cadaveric training courses. Maximum cost paid by neurosurgery residents for private cadaveric training courses, in USD (n = 50).

Institutional interest and resident-driven initiatives

Interest in institutional access to cadaveric training was nearly universal: 99.1% of respondents (n = 114) expressed interest in having access to cadaveric practice through autopsies or structured courses within their training institutions. More than half of the surveyed residents (57.9%, n = 66) reported having personally proposed the implementation of cadaveric training within their institution. No significant association was found between prior cadaveric exposure and having proposed institutional training initiatives (χ2 [1] = 0.275, p = 0.600, Cramér’s V = 0.049), with a similar proportion of residents proposing initiatives regardless of whether they had prior exposure (55.1%) or not (60.0%).

DISCUSSION

This national survey describes current patterns of access, interest, and perceived educational outcomes related to cadaveric training among neurosurgery residents in Mexico. Our results show high interest in cadaveric surgical education, strong satisfaction among residents with prior exposure, and structural and financial barriers that limit institutional access. Inferential analysis showed that cadaveric exposure increases progressively with residency year, yet this gradient reflects personal initiative and private course attendance rather than a structured institutional curriculum, a distinction with direct implications for educational policy. These findings are consistent with international evidence supporting cadaveric dissection as a core component of neurosurgical training.

Residents with previous exposure reported high satisfaction and perceived educational benefit, consistent with multiple studies showing that cadaveric dissection improves anatomical understanding, microsurgical dexterity, and surgeon confidence.[14,18] National and international surveys have similarly found that residents and program directors widely consider laboratory dissection essential in neurosurgical education, often reporting greater perceived benefit than virtual or synthetic simulators alone.[8,21] Our findings extend this pattern to a different healthcare system and economic context.

Despite its recognized value, access to cadaveric training is limited for many residents. In our study, 57.0% of respondents reported no cadaveric practice during residency, and participation frequently depended on private courses at substantial personal cost. This is consistent with reports from both high-income and LMICs, where institutional cadaveric laboratories are inconsistently available.[16,22,26] A global survey of neurosurgical trainees across 69 countries found that 70.1% of respondents had no dissection or surgical skills laboratory at their home institution; the access gap observed in Mexico reflects a wider structural deficit in neurosurgical education rather than a local anomaly.[20]

The association between residency year and cadaveric exposure (χ2 [4] = 36.20, p < 0.001, Cramér’s V = 0.564) warrants particular attention. While 88.9% of PGY5 residents reported prior exposure, none of the PGY1 residents did, and the median session count remained zero through PGY3. This pattern does not reflect a structured, longitudinal curriculum; it reflects cumulative personal access over time. Structured, PGY-based dissection curricula have been proposed and implemented in other settings, with competency milestones defined for each training year: from basic craniotomies and neurovascular anatomy in junior years to white matter dissection and skull base approaches in senior years.[2,9] The contrast with our findings suggests that residents in Mexico are effectively self-directing their cadaveric training through private courses, rather than receiving it as part of a planned educational framework.[9,20]

The association between residency year and private course attendance (χ2 [4] = 22.89, p < 0.001, Cramér’s V = 0.446) supports this interpretation. Senior residents are more likely to have attended private courses, which likely reflect greater access to professional networks, institutional support, and personal financial resources accumulated over time. The result is an inequity within residency training itself: early-year residents, precisely those who stand to benefit most from cadaveric exposure before operating independently, are the least likely to have had any.

Multiple studies have shown the feasibility of cost-effective cadaveric laboratories through institutional collaboration, shared resources, and low-cost infrastructure models.[19,22,24] Practical guidelines for setting up neurosurgical skills laboratories show that cadaveric training is achievable even in resource-constrained environments when anatomy, forensic medicine, and neurosurgical departments collaborate effectively.[24]

Although most residents expressed interest in institutional access to cadaveric training, no significant association was found between prior cadaveric exposure and having proposed institutional training initiatives (χ2 [1] = 0.275, p = 0.600, Cramér’s V = 0.049). Residents without any cadaveric experience were equally likely to advocate for such programs (60.0%) as those with prior exposure (55.1%). This result shows that motivation to advance cadaveric training does not depend on personal experience and that the barrier is structural rather than attitudinal. Addressing these barriers at an institutional and regulatory level, rather than relying on individual resident advocacy, is therefore the more productive path forward. Similar challenges have been described in program evaluations, where a lack of formal curricula and institutional support limits resident-driven initiatives.[8] Structured courses have also contributed to the formation of leading neurosurgeons and to scientific output in their respective countries.[17]

Neurosurgical education is moving toward a hybrid model that integrates operative experience, simulation technologies, objective skill assessment, and cadaveric dissection. While virtual reality and performance metrics contribute to skill acquisition, cadaveric training occupies a distinct place in bridging simulation and live surgery by combining realistic anatomy, tissue handling, and procedural flow.[10] The argument that cadaveric dissection has been made obsolete has been addressed repeatedly in the literature; current evidence supports its continued relevance within structured, ethically regulated, and longitudinal training frameworks.[13] Combining cadaveric training with simulation and early educational exposure, including laboratory-based teaching at the undergraduate level, may develop earlier technical preparedness and professional interest in the field.[15]

The combination of high resident interest, positive educational outcomes among those with access, and clearly identified access barriers provides a basis for national or regional initiatives. Models involving shared cadaveric laboratories, inter-institutional collaboration, and integration into formal curricula have been implemented in other countries and could be adapted to the Mexican context.[16,17,22,24]

A representative national initiative is the Skull Base Laboratory, which between 2021 and 2022 conducted 11 courses through an inter-institutional alliance, each comprising 11–18 stations and 12 h of combined theoretical and practical cadaveric dissection over 2–3 days. Neurosurgery residents represented up to 60% of participants per course, reflecting the substantial unmet demand for structured cadaveric training within the country [Figure 7].

Limitations

The response rate of 26% (115 of 443 eligible residents) may introduce selection bias, as residents with a greater interest in cadaveric training may be overrepresented. Retrospective questions regarding the number of sessions and course costs are subject to recall bias. As a cross-sectional study, temporal trends cannot be assessed. Finally, not all participants completed every survey item, which limits the completeness of specific subanalyses.

CONCLUSION

Neurosurgery residents in Mexico show strong interest in cadaveric surgical training, and those with prior exposure report high satisfaction. Access remains limited and largely dependent on private initiatives, with institutional training absent from the early years of residency when it would be most beneficial. The increase in exposure across PGY reflects accumulated personal access rather than deliberate educational design; individual motivation cannot close a structural gap. Cadaveric training that is affordable, ethically regulated, and built into a longitudinal residency curriculum is a practical necessity for safer surgical practice and more equitable neurosurgical education in Mexico.
