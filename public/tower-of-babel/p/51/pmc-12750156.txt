Neuroimmune circuit mediates apathy in cancer cachexia
Authors: Yongrui Hai, Renming Fan, Fangfang Qi, Minggao Zhao, Gaofei Wei
Published: 2025

Cancer cachexia is a debilitating syndrome affecting patients with advanced malignancies, characterized by profound weight loss, skeletal muscle atrophy, and neuropsychiatric symptoms such as fatigue, apathy, and depression1. Inflammatory signaling via specific neural circuits has been known to regulate energy reserves during acute illness; however, in the context of cancer, chronic activation of these circuits may underlie persistent fatigue and mood disturbances. Interleukin-6 (IL-6), a pleiotropic pro-inflammatory cytokine secreted by tumor cells, immune cells, and stromal cells, plays a critical role in shaping the tumor microenvironment2,3. While sustained elevation of IL-6 during tumor progression is associated with brain dysfunction and the onset of cachexia4, the precise mechanisms by which IL-6 affects the brain remain poorly understood.

Previous work by Sun et al.5 demonstrated that IL-6 could activate neurons in the area postrema (ArP), thereby contributing to the development of cachexia. However, the specific downstream neural circuits involved remained unclear. In a recent study published in Science, Zhu et al.6 mechanistically delineated a neuroimmune pathway through which IL-6 drives cachexia-associated behavioral changes. Specifically, IL-6 activates a brain circuit involving the ArP–parabrachial nucleus (PBN)–substantia nigra pars reticulata (SNpr)–ventral tegmental area (VTA)–nucleus accumbens (NAc), ultimately leading to motivational deficits and apathetic behavior. This discovery provides new therapeutic targets for the treatment of neuropsychiatric symptoms associated with cancer cachexia.

In this study, the authors employed a colon adenocarcinoma (C26) mouse model and showed that cachectic mice exhibited reduced effort-based motivation in behavioral assays such as patch foraging and progressive ratio tasks, indicative of decreased willingness to work for food rewards. Importantly, behavioral deficits were not attributable to anhedonia or depressive-like despair, as shown by normal performance in sucrose preference, tail suspension, and forced swim tests. Furthermore, open-field testing confirmed that cachexia-induced weight loss and muscle wasting did not impair general locomotor ability. Together, these findings suggest that cachexia-induced apathy arises from neural dysfunction rather than physical incapacity (Fig. 1A).

Given the central role of inflammation in cachexia, the researchers conducted a comprehensive screening of 44 cytokines in plasma and brain tissue. IL-6 was markedly elevated both peripherally and centrally during cachexia progression. Administration of an IL-6-neutralizing antibody (tocilizumab) 10 days after subcutaneous tumor inoculation effectively delayed cachexia onset and prolonged survival in tumor-bearing mice. Even when administered at later stages (Days 18–21), tocilizumab failed to reverse weight loss but significantly ameliorated apathy and restored motivational behavior. These findings suggest that IL-6 is a key link between cancer-induced systemic inflammation and neuropsychiatric manifestations.

Using whole-brain activity mapping at cellular resolution, combined with viral tracing approaches, the authors reconstructed a functionally relevant IL-6–responsive neural circuit. First, ArP, a sensory circumventricular organ capable of detecting blood-borne cytokines, was identified as a key hub for IL-6 signal detection. Upon sensing IL-6, ArP neurons projected to the PBN, which then activated inhibitory neurons in the SNpr. These inhibitory projections further suppressed dopaminergic neurons in the VTA, the major source of dopamine release in the NAc. As the NAc is a critical center for reward and motivation, its dopaminergic inhibition led to reduced dopamine (DA) signaling and motivational deficits. Both optogenetic stimulation of dopaminergic neurons and drug treatment to increase DA level of the body can effectively improve the motivational deficits and apathetic behaviors in cachectic mice, but they cannot reverse the weight loss. This highlights the potential of treating cachexia-related apathy independent of metabolic symptoms (Fig. 1B).

Zhu et al.6 further showed that blocking IL-6 signaling with antibodies, knocking down IL-6 receptors in ArP, or ablating the ArP–PBN projection were each sufficient to rescue motivational behavior. These findings are consistent with previous clinical observations by Chen et al.7, where tocilizumab demonstrated benefits in alleviating cachexia in small-scale patient studies. Collectively, this work bridges peripheral inflammation and central nervous system dysfunction, revealing how chronic inflammatory signals hijack neural circuits to drive maladaptive behavioral states. In various inflammatory diseases such as Alzheimer's disease8, arthritis9, psoriasis, systemic lupus erythematosus10 and depression11, IL-6 can induce the production of inflammatory mediators and transcription factors, activate multiple inflammatory signaling pathways such as HIF-1, JAK–STAT, and IL-17, trigger a cytokine storm, and subsequently lead to the loss of control of the systemic inflammatory response. It also raises important future questions: while targeting IL-6–responsive neural circuits or enhancing dopamine signaling may restore motivation and quality of life in cachectic patients, could similar circuits underlie fatigue and motivational impairment in other inflammatory diseases, such as autoimmune disorders or depression? Is IL-6 modulation a broadly applicable strategy in chronic inflammatory conditions?

Moreover, although IL-6 signaling in ArP was shown to suppress appetite in cachectic mice, the specific downstream circuits mediating this anorexic response remain unclear. Zhu et al.6 also reported that chronic neuroinflammation in cachexia significantly increased microglial cell populations across multiple brain regions. Intriguingly, work by Yoshida et al.12 demonstrated that certain T cells originating from gut and adipose tissues infiltrate the brain, carrying critical contextual information (e.g., microbial and nutritional states) that is essential for regulating feeding behavior. As antigen-presenting cells, microglia regulate brain function by mediating the accumulation, tissue residency and clonal expansion of CD4+ T and CD8+ T cells through antigen presentation13. Meanwhile, microglia secrete chemokines such as CCL2 and CCL8 to recruit T cells. The infiltrating T cells then activate the microglia further by secreting cytokines such as IFN-γ, forming a pro-inflammatory cycle. Among these cells, subsets such as Tregs maintain immune homeostasis by inhibiting microglial inflammation14,15. Does chronic inflammation alter the function, quantity or composition of brain T cells located in the subfornical organ (SFO), thereby affecting the cachexia process? Can the neuropsychiatric symptoms of cachexia be alleviated by modulating T cells directly or by blocking the interaction between microglia and T cells? (Fig. 1C).

In conclusion, the study by Zhu et al.6 identifies a critical neuroimmune circuit that connects peripheral inflammation to central motivational decline, establishing IL-6 as a key neuromodulator of cancer-associated apathy. By pinpointing specific neural nodes rather than attributing symptoms to global brain dysfunction, this work challenges the longstanding notion that cachexia is merely a metabolic disorder. Apathy is not simply a byproduct of physical decline—it is a direct consequence of inflammatory neural rewiring. Even when the underlying disease is irreversible, this study suggests that targeted intervention in IL-6–mediated brain circuits may improve volition and patient engagement. These findings pave the way for translational strategies to address inflammation-driven motivational deficits across a range of disorders, including cancer, autoimmune diseases, and depression.
