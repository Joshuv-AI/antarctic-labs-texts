Association between fatty acid profile and normal thyroid hormone with different glucose status
Authors: Xin Hu, Dan Wang, Yazhuo Liu, Yinqiong Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStudies have demonstrated that thyroid hormone is regulated by both glucose and fatty acids. However, previous studies focused on patients with thyroid dysfunction rather than with normal thyroid hormone. The purpose of research was to explore the relationship between normal thyroid hormone and fatty acid metabolism with different glucose status.MethodsThis was an observational study. In all participants, basic demographic characteristics, blood lipid profiles, glycemic status and thyroid hormone levels were assessed. Additionally, serum fatty acid profiles were determined using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry.Results919 subjects were enrolled (239 with diabetes; 680 without diabetes). Multiple linear regression analyses showed that in the diabetic group, levels of free thyroxine (FT4) showed a negative correlation with eicosatrienoic acid (β=-0.289 p < 0.01), while levels of arachidonic acid (β = 0.178 p = 0.016), eicosanoic acid (β = 0.188 p = 0.018) and alcohol consumption (β = 0.147 p = 0.026) exhibited a positive correlation with FT4. In non-diabetic group, FT4 was negatively related to levels of palmitic acid (β=-0.32 p < 0.01), linoleic acid (β=-0.15 p = 0.04), myristic acid(β=-0.304 p < 0.01), docosapentaenoic acid(β=-0.215 p = 0.04), age (β=-0.109 p < 0.01), HDL-C (β=-0.116 p < 0.01) and smoking (β = 0.11 p < 0.01); FT4 was positively correlated with levels of arachidic acid (β = 0.305 p < 0.01) and palmitoleic acid (β = 0.569 p < 0.01).ConclusionsThe relationship between normal thyroid hormone levels and fatty acid levels significantly differed among different glucose status. These findings provide further insights into the relationship between the serum fatty acid metabolism profile and in individuals with different glucose state. The study provided clinical data that can inform the prevention and treatment of lipid metabolism disorders and cardiovascular disease.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Thyroid hormone, Fatty acid, Diabetes

Introduction

Fatty acids (FAs) are integral components of life and important energy sources for humans. They are carboxylic acids with aliphatic chains. FAs are known to have many biological activities and provide numerous health-promoting benefits. Moreover, numerous studies have shown a connection between FAs and various diseases [1–4]. For example, Yazhuo Liu et al. found that serum FAs profiles were related to diabetic kidney disease [5]. In a meta-analysis, Lefkothea-Stella Kremmyda et al. suggested that different FAs have various effects on conditions such as pancreatic and liver function, cancer, cardiovascular health, insulin sensitivity and mental health [6]. Increasing evidence suggested that a link existed between FAs and thyroid function. Jing-Jia Wang et al. found that genetically increased expression of thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) was correlated with higher cholesterol levels [7]. Guowei Zhou demonstrated a significant negative relationship between levels of FAs and free thyroxine (FT4) [8]. Furthermore, studies had shown that even fluctuations in FT4 levels within the normal spectrum may also affected lipid [9, 10]. Current studies had also found an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases or fatty liver diseases in people with low and high normal FT4 levels [11, 12]. Subclinical fluctuations in thyroid hormones have been studied not only in the context of serum FAs concentrations but also in the context of body composition disturbances and their translation into subclinical changes in the cardiovascular system [13–15]. Whether this change is due to thyroid hormone affecting the FAs profile remains to be studied further.

Diabetes has become increasingly prevalent in China [16]. There are up to 92.4 million people with diabetes and over 140 million prediabetic patients [17]. Therefore, clinical research on diabetes and diabetes mellitus (DM) comorbidities is becoming increasingly important. In people with diabetes, the incidence of thyroid dysfunction was significant [18, 19]. Reports had suggested that diabetes may affect thyroid hormone levels [19]. Furthermore, some studies had shown that serum FAs profiles were related to diabetes. D T Stein et al. found that some FAs have a hypoglycemic effect, inhibiting insulin secretion [20]. However, the relationships among diabetes, thyroid function and FAs had rarely been investigated. It was unknown whether diabetes influences the fatty acid-thyroid function relationship. In diabetic or nondiabetic individuals, a consistent conclusion regarding the relationship between thyroid hormone levels and FAs metabolism was lacking. Moreover, past studies were often limited to people with abnormal thyroid function, rather than the broader population with normal thyroid function.

The purpose of research was to explore whether the relationship between thyroid function and FAs metabolism varies according to diabetes status in euthyroid subjects. We assessed serum levels of free FAs using liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry and compared these relationships in people with and without diabetes.

Materials and methods

Study population

This was a cross-sectional observational study. Subjects were recruited from the Department of Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism at the Affiliated Zhongshan Hospital of Dalian University between May 2019 and December 2021. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Aged ≥ 18 years. (2) Serum levels of free triiodothyronine (FT3), free thyroxine (FT4), and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) within the normal laboratory reference ranges. (3) Participants were categorized according to the Chinese guidelines for diabetes [21]. Diabetic Group: HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or fasting plasma glucose (FPG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, or 2-hour post-load glucose (2 h PG) ≥ 11.1 mmol/L during an oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Non-diabetic Group: HbA1c < 6.5%, FPG < 7.0 mmol/L, and 2 h PG < 11.1 mmol/L. (3) No use of lipid-lowering drugs within the past three months. The criteria for exclusion were as follows: (1) a history of thyroid or parathyroid disorders or thyroid cancer; (2) pregnancy or lactation; (3) use of lipid-lowering medication; or (4) acute or chronic kidney disease, malignancy or acute infection. The detailed selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1. The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee in the Zhongshan Hospital (ethical approval ID: 2019271).

Data collection

Measurements

Clinical information and demographic factors were collected at enrollment from patients’ medical records including age, sex, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and any history of disease. Physical examinations were performed to record height, weight, and Body mass index. Following a minimum 8-hour overnight fast, venous blood was drawn from the antecubital vein. Serum was subsequently separated and analyzed. Biochemical markers, including fasting plasma glucose (FPG), glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TGs), and both high- and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c and LDL-c), were quantified using a Roche fully automatic biochemical analyzer (Roche Holding AG, Basel, Switzerland). And thyroid hormones were measured using a Roche Cobas E602 analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland).

LC-MS: sample preparation and analysis

The serum samples were processed as follows. Initially, each sample was mixed with 50 µl of internal standard working solution, 1 ml of hydrolysis solution, and 5 µl of covalent working solution. After vortexing for 10 s, the mixture was incubated at 90 °C for 20 min for the hydrolysis process. Subsequently, 80 µl of neutralization solution was added, followed by a brief 10-s incubation. For extraction, 1 ml of extract solution was introduced, and the mixture was allowed to stand for 5 min. Thereafter, 700 µl of the supernatant was collected and evaporated to dryness under a nitrogen stream at 50 °C. The residue was then reconstituted in 400 µl of complex solution and vortexed for 5 min. Finally, a 200-µl aliquot of the processed sample was transferred for instrumental analysis.

Targeted FFA analysis was performed using an AB SCIEX Triple Quad 4500MD system (Framingham, MA, USA). Chromatographic separation was achieved on an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (1.7 μm;). The mobile phase consisted of (A) 2 mM ammonium formate in aqueous solution and (B) 2 mM ammonium formate in methanol. The temperatures for the column and sample chamber were maintained at 40 °C and 8 °C, respectively, with an injection volume of 2 µl. The details of the methodology can be found elsewhere [5].

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as percentages. For continuous variables, the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was first used to assess normality. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and differences between diabetic and non-diabetic groups were compared using the independent samples t-test. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range), and the Mann-Whitney U test was used for group comparisons. Spearman’s correlation analysis was employed to evaluate the relationship between thyroid hormone levels and serum FAs levels. Multiple linear regression analyses were performed to establish the association between FAs levels and serum levels of thyroxine (T4). In addition, factors known to be related to thyroid hormone levels and blood glucose were included. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 22 software (SPSS, USA). Graphs were constructed using GraphPad8 Prism (GraphPad Software, USA). P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates a possible link between FAs levels and thyroid function. In addition, we found that diabetes influenced this relationship. The relationships between FAs levels and thyroid hormone levels significantly differed between nondiabetic and diabetic individuals.

With changes in people’s diet and improvements in living conditions, an increasing number of people had exhibited altered blood lipid profiles [22]. The consequences of dyslipidemia were of great concern. Furthermore, increasing evidence indicated that lipid metabolism disorders were closely associated with thyroid dysfunction [23, 24]. Thyroid hormones have a range of essential functions and are secreted by the thyroid gland [25]. Thyroid hormones are necessary for growth, differentiation, and metabolic balance [26]. The thyroid gland secretes two thyroid hormones, T4 and triiodothyronine (T3) [27]. FT4 is often referred to as a prohormone, indicating that conversion to active FT3 is required for biological activity [28]. Recently, multiple studies had shown that there may be some interaction between thyroid hormones and FAs. A study of 737 older American adults by Guowei Zhou et al. reported a negative correlation between C16:0 levels and FT4 levels [8]. Xiaoqian Wang found thatC18:2 were negatively associated with FT4 levels [29]. Arachidic acid, and docosahexaenoic acid were lower in healthy pregnant females than in women with subclinical hypothyroidism [30]. Our findings revealed that levels of some FAs, such as C20:3 and C16:0, were negatively correlated with FT4 levels. Conversely, some other FAs were found to be positively associated with thyroid function. We found that in the participants without diabetes, FT4 levels exhibited a significant positive correlation with levels of C16:1, C20:0, and Monounsaturated FAs. In a study involving 626 adults, Xiaoqian Wang et al. found positive associations among levels of palmitelaidic acid, C16:1 and total T4 [29]. Some researchers had proposed a mechanism underlying this association, with lipotoxicity leading to chronic thyroid cellular dysfunction and injury. Furthermore, in a cellular experiment, Annemarie Gagnon found that TSH could increase serum FAs levels and stimulate lipolysis and phosphorylation of perilipin [31].

Moreover, we also found that this relationship differed between diabetic and the nondiabetic group. In the diabetic group, there was a negative association between FT4 levels and C20:3, while a positive correlation was observed between FT4 levels and C20:0,C20:4. In the nondiabetic group, the levels of FT4 exhibited a significant negative correlation with C16:0 levels, C18:2, C14:0, C22:5 levels, and positively correlated with C20:0, C16:1levels. After further analysis, we found that different types of FAs were correlated with FT4 levels. In the diabetic group, most FAs related to thyroid function were omega-6 FAs, whereas in nondiabetic group, omega-3 FAs and saturated FAs were associated with FT4 levels. The main difference between the two kinds of FAs was the location of the double bonds [32]. Fish oil was the primary source of omega-3 FAs, while vegetables were the major source of omega-6 FAs [33]. Jing Song et al. found that omega-3 FAs decreased thyroid autoantibodies levels through downstream derivative resolvin E1 [34]. Previous animal experiments had found that omega-6 FAs, such as C20:4, was important for regulating thyroid function [35]. It was not clear how this difference between the two FAs arose. The association of diabetes with thyroid function and FAs levels had been established, but the relationships among the three had not been established. The following reasons may explain this finding. T. Szkudelski et al. found diabetic rats exhibit higher serum FAs levels compared to controls. Following treatment with T4, significant decreases in serum FFA levels were observed in diabetic rats [36]. A possible mechanism is that T4 could enhance the breakdown and metabolism of fat in the liver [37]. Additionally, deiodinases may also play a role in this process. Deiodinases (DIO) are a group of selenium-containing redox enzymes that modulate thyroid function by regulating the production and degradation of triiodothyronine. Current animal studies suggest that DIO1 knockout in mice may impair lipid metabolism [38], while DIO2 deficiency is associated with an increased risk of insulin resistance [39, 40]. However, most studies have not clearly distinguished among the FAs. Direct experimental evidence supporting this hypothesis is lacking. Additional research is needed to determine the exact mechanism.

This study had several strengths and advantages. First, there were relatively few studies on this aspect in this region. Second, using liquid chromatography with tandem mass spectrometry (LS-MC-MC) could provide more precise and more detailed data. However, this study also had some shortcomings. First, the causality of relationships could not be established. Second, we do not have a detailed explanation for the results. Third, Subgroup analyses were not performed. Future Directions: (1)Prospective, multicenter studies are needed to establish causality. (2) Further mechanistic studies, including basic experiments, are warranted. (3) Future studies should incorporate relevant subgroup analyses.

Conclusion

In summary, our study indicated that diabetes may affect the relationship between thyroid function and FAs profile. Our findings remained statistically significant after correction for multiple comparisons. The mechanism underlying this phenomenon merits further investigation. These findings may help to reduce or prevent lipid metabolism disorders in diabetics and lower the prevalence of cardiovascular disease.
