Phenotypic Variability in Slow‐Wave Sleep in Depression: Associations With Clinical Profiles and Disorder Severity
Authors: Antoine Salmeron, Pierre A. Geoffroy, Lily Vissouze, Jeanne Leseur, Heloise Rach, Michel Lejoyeux, Justine Frija, Marie Pia d’Ortho, Julia Maruani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSlow‐wave sleep (SWS; stage N3) is often reported as reduced in depression, yet variability across depressive phenotypes remains poorly characterised. This study aimed to determine whether N3 architecture—proportion, duration, and latency—identifies clinically distinct profiles in major depressive episode (MDE). Ninety‐seven adults with MDE underwent overnight polysomnography. N3 metrics (latency, duration, % of total sleep period (TSP), % of total sleep time (TST)) were analysed both dimensionally (correlations with clinical measures) and categorically (reduced, normal, or increased N3). Backward stepwise logistic regression identified variables distinguishing N3 categories, and sensitivity analyses assessed psychotropic medication effects. Shorter N3 latency correlated with poorer subjective sleep quality and greater anxiety and depressive severity. Extremes of N3 (%TSP) carried clinical burden: increased N3% associated with earlier hospitalisation, younger age at first suicide attempt, and higher daytime sleepiness; reduced N3% related to more hospitalisations and depressive episodes. Categorical contrasts showed increased N3 duration linked to higher anxiety and depressive symptom severity, reduced N3 (%TST) predicted more hospitalisations, while increased N3 (%TST) aligned with greater seasonality and morning chronotype. Men exhibited higher %N3 TST than women; bipolar depression had lower N3 than unipolar depression. A five‐factor model (episode count, chronotype, seasonality, age, sex) distinguished increased %N3 TST from normal levels (AUC = 0.863; sensitivity = 0.957; specificity = 0.714), explaining 30.8% of variance. Medication analyses revealed no major effects. N3 sleep is not a uniform biomarker in depression. Its proportion and duration delineate distinct, clinically meaningful profiles. Sleep‐based stratification may guide precision care.

Introduction

Depressive disorders affect up to 20% of the general population and represent a critical public health concern (WHO 2017). They significantly impair psychosocial functioning and quality of life, and are closely linked to an elevated suicide risk (Chesney et al. 2014). The World Health Organization (WHO) estimated that over 300 million people were living with depression in 2015, an 18% increase since 2005, and projects that by 2030, depressive disorders will become the leading cause of disability worldwide (Mathers and Loncar 2006). Despite international efforts to identify biomarkers of depression, none have been implemented in clinical practice for diagnostic purposes or for predicting disease progression or therapeutic response. This challenge is partly due to the highly heterogeneous nature of depressive disorders, leading to highly variable responses to treatment. Indeed, only about one in three patients achieve remission after a first SSRI trial (Trivedi et al. 2006), highlighting the critical need for research into the physiological disruptions underlying depressive disorders to better define and differentiate their subtypes.

Sleep disturbances, observed in over 90% of the individuals with major depressive episode (MDE) (Geoffroy et al. 2018), hold particular promise as potential biomarkers for refining the classification of depressive subtypes. Polysomnography (PSG) has consistently shown sleep‐architecture abnormalities in depression such as shortened REM latency and increased REM density (Leitner et al. 2025) and, in meta‐analysis, reduced slow‐wave sleep (SWS) relative to controls (Palagini et al. 2013; Wichniak et al. 2017). SWS has emerged as a promising subject of investigation for biomarker discovery in mood disorders. SWS, characterised by the N3 stage, is the deepest, most restorative non‐REM stage and supports energy recovery, declarative memory consolidation, synaptic down‐selection, immune modulation, and glymphatic clearance of neuro‐metabolites (Besedovsky et al. 2019; Mander et al. 2017; Xie et al. 2013). Because SWS can be experimentally amplified, it is also viewed as a potential therapeutic target, particularly in late‐life or treatment‐resistant depression (Hejazi et al. 2024). Converging evidence shows that SWS is not uniformly reduced in depression but varies by clinical subtype. A large meta‐analysis reports an overall reduction in deep sleep (including SWS) in MDE compared with controls, but it does not specifically show that this effect is ‘driven’ by melancholic cases or that SWS is ‘preserved’ in atypical depression (Baglioni et al. 2016). Population‐based PSG data published later indicate reduced delta power specifically in current melancholic MDE, with no robust alterations in other subtypes (Solelhac et al. 2023). Another PSG meta‐analysis, focused on seasonal affective disorder, synthesised 7 studies (~183 participants) and primarily found REM abnormalities (increased REM amount, reduced REM latency). It did not demonstrate a reliable SWS difference versus controls, and it does not establish a reduced SWS in non‐seasonal MDE contrast (Bertrand et al. 2021). In bipolar depression (BD), findings for SWS activity are heterogeneous; several studies do not show an increase in SWS during the depressive phase. Comparative work tends to emphasise differences in sleep continuity and REM markers rather than a consistent SWS increase in BD (Leseur et al. 2025; Zangani et al. 2020). Recent EEG work in treatment‐resistant depression reports reduced NREM (Non–rapid eye movement stages (N1–N3), marked by slow‐wave brain activity) delta power compared with healthy controls, supporting a deficit in slow‐wave activity consistent with reduced deep‐sleep homeostasis (Hejazi et al. 2024). Together, these findings emphasise that SWS alteration could be a phenotype‐specific rather than a universal biomarker of mood disorders. Beyond its descriptive features, disturbed SWS has well‐documented clinical consequences: greater symptom severity in youth with depression (Santangeli et al. 2017), poor or delayed ketamine response (Duncan et al. 2013), higher risk of relapse/recurrence in depression (Paykel 2008), impaired declarative memory and executive control (Zhang and Gruber 2019), heightened next‐day emotional reactivity (Ben Simon et al. 2019), low‐grade systemic inflammation (Strumberger et al. 2024) and disrupted overnight metabolic clearance (Van Hattem et al. 2025), elevated long‐term cardiometabolic risk (Kianersi et al. 2023), suicidal ideation and behaviour (Bernert et al. 2015).

Given these considerations, alterations in SWS appear to be associated with more severe depression profiles and with specific depressive subtypes. However, findings regarding SWS remain heterogeneous, likely due to methodological variability (e.g., scoring criteria, sample sizes) and clinical heterogeneity (e.g., melancholic, atypical, seasonal, or treatment‐resistant depression) (Pillai et al. 2011). A focused investigation of SWS across the depressive spectrum is warranted. Determining whether reduced (or, in some phenotypes, increased) duration of N3 sleep in minutes, or proportion of N3 sleep (N3% of Total Sleep Time; TST or Total Sleep Period; TSP) are associated with symptom severity, cumulative illness burden, suicidality, pharmacological treatment class or specific depressive subtypes such as seasonality could provide clinically actionable insights.

This study aimed to quantify N3 duration, N3% TST and N3% TSP in a well‐characterised cohort drawn from routine care of adults experiencing an MDE and to compare SWS metrics across clinically relevant subgroups including male versus female patients, suffering from bipolar versus unipolar depressive disorder, with or without seasonal pattern, treatment resistance, and suicidal ideation. Additionally, this investigation sought to explore associations between SWS and key clinical outcomes. By focusing analyses on PSG‐derived SWS measures, this work intends to clarify the potential of SWS as a biomarker in MDE and to support the development of personalised, biomarker‐driven therapeutic strategies in depression.

Method

Study Design

Patients were drawn from a routine care cohort from the Psychiatry, Sleep and Chronobiology Reference Center (ChronoS Center), the Sleep Center of the Physiology‐Functional Exploration Department, or during hospitalisation for MDE at the Department of Psychiatry and Addictology, Bichat Claude Bernard Hospital in Paris, from September 2022 to July 2025. Patients' medical histories were collected (treatments, recurrences, progression) and, with questionnaires and current MDE characteristics, comorbidities following DSM‐5‐TR criteria.

Patients were 18–70 years old, had mild to severe MDE according to DSM‐5‐TR criteria (American Psychiatric Association 2013), and were affiliated to a social security scheme. Patients who did not exhibit clinical symptoms of depression according to the DSM‐5‐TR diagnostic criteria were not considered.

Patients received an information note explaining that their clinical data could be used for research purposes, with the possibility to decline (non‐opposition procedure). This clinical research project entitled ‘Étude des biomarqueurs du sommeil et des rythmes circadiens dans les troubles psychiatriques « Som‐Psy »’ was approved (N°CER‐2020–56) by the Ethical Review Committee for Biomedical Research Projects ‘Comité d'Evaluation de l'Ethique des projets de Recherche Biomédicale (CEERB) Paris Nord’ (Institutional Review Board—IRB 00006477 of HUPNVS, Paris 7 University, AP‐HP).

Sociodemographic, Sleep and Psychiatric Clinical Parameters

Sociodemographic characteristics—gender, age and body mass index (BMI)—were collected. Clinical parameters assessed in routine care included the number of depressive episodes, hospitalisations, treatments, and severity of depressive and anxiety symptoms using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) (Zigmond and Snaith 1983) (HAD‐D for depression severity and HAD‐A for anxiety severity) and the Quick Inventory of Depressive Symptomatology—Self‐Report 16 items (QIDS‐SR16) (Reilly et al. 2015). The associated cut‐offs were: HAD ≥ 10 (clinically significant anxiety and/or depressive symptoms) and QIDS‐SR16: 6 to 10: mild depression, 11 to 15: moderate depression, 16 to 20: severe depression, 21 to 27: very severe depression.

Sleep and circadian patterns were assessed using self‐report questionnaires: Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) (Buysse et al. 1989), which assesses sleep quality (PSQI; poor sleep quality > 5); the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS, excessive sleepiness ≥ 11) (Johns 1991); the Insomnia Severity Index (ISI; mild insomnia ≥ 8) (Morin et al. 2011), the Hypersomnia Severity Index (HSI; mild hypersomnia ≥ 10) (Kaplan et al. 2019) and the Morningness‐Eveningness Questionnaire (MEQ, ≥ 70 definitely morning, 59–69 moderately morning, 42–58 neither, 31–41 moderately evening, ≤ 30 definitely evening chronotype) (Taillard et al. 2004). Seasonality was measured using the Global Seasonality Score (GSS) of the Seasonal Pattern Assessment Questionnaire (SPAQ) ranging from 0 (no seasonality) to 24 (extreme seasonality) (Reynaud et al. 2021).

Objective Sleep and Circadian Rhythms Measure Using Polysomnography

In the context of routine care, patients had one night of PSG using a standard system V‐PSG; Morpheus (Micromed, Treviso, Italy); Noxturnal (ResMed, Bella Vista, NSW, Australia); Alice 6 (Philips‐Respironics, Suresnes, France). PSG assessed 28 measures of sleep architecture, including duration, latency, and percentage of each sleep stage (N1, N2, N3, REM), total sleep time (TST), time in bed, and total sleep period (TSP, TST including micro‐awakenings). Latencies were defined as follows: N1 latency—from intended sleep to sleep onset; N2 and N3 latencies—from sleep onset to the onset of N2 and N3, respectively. Additional metrics included REM latency and duration, wake after sleep onset (WASO), sleep efficiency, nocturnal awakenings, total awakening index, apnea/hypopnea index (AHI), total arousal index, and the Kupfer Index (age + REM latency), a proposed depression biomarker reflecting sleep continuity and architecture disruption. A specific focus was placed on N3 (SWS), analysing N3 duration, N3 latency, and the duration of the first N3 episode.

Categorical analyses (low, normal, high) were based on established PSG cut‐offs (AASM, Rechtschaffen and Kales), widely used in sleep research, including psychiatric populations (Allan Rechtschaffen 1968; Iber et al. 2007).

Statistics

Correlations between MDE and polysomnographic (PSG) variables were assessed using Pearson's r or Spearman's ρ. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, continuous variables as mean ± SD. Normality was checked via Q–Q plots and the Shapiro–Wilk test (p > 0.05), and homoscedasticity via Levene's test (p > 0.05). N3 sleep metrics (latency, duration, first N3 duration, %TST, %TSP) were compared across sex, depression polarity (bipolar vs. unipolar according to the DSM‐5‐TR criteria), seasonal vs. non‐seasonal depression (based on a cut‐off score of 11 on the Global Seasonality Score from the SPAQ), and treatment‐resistant vs. non‐resistant depression using Student t‐tests or Mann–Whitney tests as appropriate. Clinical, sleep, and psychiatric variables were compared between patients with decreased (< 90 min), normal (90–120 min), or increased (> 120 min) N3 duration, and between decreased (< 15%), normal (15%–20%), or increased (> 20%) N3% TST (Ohayon et al. 2004; Redline et al. 2004). Secondly, variables differing between groups at p < 0.05 were entered into backward stepwise logistic regression (BSLR) to assess the combination of these selected variables that best classified individuals with increased, normal or reduced N3 stage. In addition, age and sex were included into the model as covariates because both exert robust, well‐described effects on slow‐wave sleep: SWS declines across the adult lifespan, and systematic sex differences in N3 have been reported in large cohorts showing that men tend to have less N3/SWS than women (Ohayon et al. 2004; Redline et al. 2004).

The sensitivity, specificity, test accuracy, area under the curve (AUC), and Youden's index were calculated to evaluate the model's predictive performance. The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to visualise the model's discriminative ability. We also evaluated the significance of this global model and assessed its variance, including the R 2 value.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted to test the robustness of our findings and account for potential medication effects on N3 sleep. For the five most commonly prescribed drug classes (antidepressants, benzodiazepine anxiolytics, hypnotics, mood stabilisers, and antipsychotics), participants were grouped by exposure (yes/no) on the night of polysomnography. Key N3 metrics (latency, absolute duration, %TSP, %TST) were compared between exposed and unexposed patients using the same statistical approach as for depression subtype comparisons.

All statistical analyses were performed using JAMOVI software (version 2.4.14), where a p‐value of < 0.05 was used as the significance threshold.

Based on the article of Solelhac et al. (2023), power analysis was conducted in G*Power (v.3.1) using the Exact test for bivariate correlations, with α = 0.05 (two‐tailed) and power = 0.80 (Solelhac et al. 2023). This resulted in a required sample size of N = 46 per correlation, which is consistent with our group sizes (n = 40) and supports that our study is adequately powered to detect associations of moderate to large magnitude.

Stage N1 lasted 38.3 ± 32.8 min (7.0% ± 6.1% of TSP; 10.5% ± 8.8% of TST) with a latency of 14.6 ± 27.6 min. Stage N2 was dominant, lasting 189.0 ± 73.9 min (45.8% ± 12.4% TSP; 49.8% ± 13.1% TST) with a latency of 8.5 ± 11.3 min. Correlations Between N3 Sleep and Sleep and Psychiatric Characteristics

We conducted correlation analyses between N3 sleep metrics and socio‐demographic, sleep and psychiatric data in the 97 complete sample of patients. N3 latency negatively correlated with anxiety (HAD‐A: Rho = −0.301, p = 0.027; HADS: Rho = −0.269, p = 0.049) and depressive symptoms (QIDS‐SR16: Rho = −0.329, p = 0.024), indicating faster transition into N3 with greater symptom severity. N3 duration (min) negatively correlated with the number of hospitalisations (Rho = −0.351, p = 0.009), which suggests that the higher the number of hospitalisations, the shorter the duration of N3 sleep. Regarding N3% TSP, a significant negative correlation was observed with age at first suicide attempt (Rho = −0.784, p < 0.001), indicating that a younger age at the time of the first attempt was associated with a higher N3% TSP. In addition, N3% TSP was negatively correlated with both the age at first hospitalisation (Rho = −0.396, p = 0.045) and the number of hospitalisations (Rho = −0.378, p = 0.018). These findings suggest that earlier psychiatric hospitalisation was linked to increased N3% TSP, and that a greater number of hospitalisations was associated with shorter N3% TSP.

Finally, when examining N3% TST, a significant negative correlation was identified with number of depressive episodes (r = −0.291, p = 0.048), suggesting reduced deep sleep proportion in individuals with more recurrent depression.

Regarding clinical subjective sleep data, a significant negative correlation was found between sleep quality (PSQI) and N3 latency (Rho = −0.363, p = 0.0009), indicating that poorer subjective sleep quality was associated with a shorter latency to N3 onset. For N3% TSP, excessive daytime sleepiness (ESS) was positively correlated (Rho = 0.292, p = 0.044), indicating that higher daytime sleepiness was associated with increased N3% TSP.

No statistically significant associations were found between first N3 episode duration and any of the clinical variables. Results are shown in Table 2 and Figure 1.

Discussion

This study is the first, to our knowledge, to show that SWS delineates clinically distinct depressive profiles based on its own phenotype—its proportion (TSP, TST), duration, and latency. Across dimensional analyses, shorter N3 latency was consistently associated with poorer subjective sleep quality and greater anxiety and depressive severity. Both increased and reduced N3 proportions were linked to markers of more severe depression. Increased %N3 (TSP) was associated with earlier hospitalisation, younger age at first suicide attempt, and greater daytime sleepiness, whereas reduced %N3 was linked to more hospitalisations and more recurrent depressive episodes. Categorical analyses further highlight that both increased and reduced either N3 duration in minutes or proportions of N3 sleep (%N3 TST) were linked to clinical indicators of severe depression: increased N3 duration in minutes compared to reduced one exhibited significantly higher anxiety and depressive symptom severity, indicating a greater depressive symptom burden and overall distress. In addition, reduced %N3 TST was linked to a higher number of psychiatric hospitalisations compared to increased %N3 TST, whereas increased %N3 was associated with greater seasonal mood variation, a stronger morning chronotype but lower lifetime number of depressive episodes and hospitalisations compared to normal %N3 TST. Interestingly, a robust five‐factor model (number of depressive episodes, chronotype, seasonality, age, sex) distinguished increased from normal %N3 TST (AUC = 0.863), with excellent sensitivity and high positive predictive value. This combination of clinical and chronobiological features including a higher sensibility to season, a tendency towards morningness, younger age and being a man, strongly predicted the subgroup with increased N3% TST and effectively ruled out its absence.

All these results challenge the long‐held assumption that SWS is uniformly diminished in depression and align with our hypothesis regarding the heterogeneity of mood disorders. Notably, the originality of the present findings lies in the observation that MDE with high N3 duration or N3% index is a marker of clinical severity across the life course. Specifically, from a dimensional perspective, higher N3% TSP subgroup is characterised by an earlier age at first hospitalisation, a younger age at first suicide attempt, and greater daytime sleepiness. From a categorical perspective, subjects with increased N3% TST also show greater sensitivity to seasonal variations, and a morning‐type chronotype. This is coherent with literature describing a hypersomnolent depressive phenotype that carries higher suicidal vulnerability, larger seasonal impacts on mood, sleep, and circadian dimensions throughout the year (Maruani et al. 2023). Our study reflected the broad heterogeneity previously reported in depression (Baglioni et al. 2016). Notably, we identified—for the first time—a reduction in N3 sleep in bipolar depression, both in duration and proportion of TST (Sarzetto et al. 2022). Men also showed higher N3% TST than women, in line with prior findings (Patel et al. 2004). No differences in N3 were found between patients with and without a seasonal MDE pattern; however, dimensional analysis revealed that individuals with increased N3% TST displayed greater sensitivity to seasonal variations. Likewise, N3 metrics did not differ according to treatment resistance or suicidal ideation. These observations highlight the need for broader phenotyping. A next step would involve cluster analyses integrating N3 measures (level, latency, first‐cycle metrics), global sleep architecture, and key clinical variables (episode count, hospitalisations, suicidality, chronotype, seasonality, daytime sleepiness). Based on our results, distinct clinical clusters are expected. One profile may present reduced N3%, particularly in bipolar depression and in women with recurrent hospitalisations. This relapsing trajectory may benefit from close monitoring, proactive relapse prevention, mood‐stabilising strategies, and N3‐enhancing interventions, including chronotherapeutic (Gottlieb et al. 2019a) such as light therapy (Geoffroy et al. 2025), and cognitive behavioural therapy adapted for mood disorders (Riemann et al. 2023). Another profile, defined by increased N3%, morning chronotype, marked seasonality, daytime sleepiness, earlier onset suicidal behaviour and psychiatric hospitalisation, predominantly among individuals with unipolar depression, suggests a hypersomnolent seasonally sensitive phenotype with a morning chronotype. This group may benefit from circadian‐modulating treatments, including morning bright‐light therapy, phase‐shifting interventions, and wake‐promoting strategies aimed at reducing N3 proportion (Gottlieb et al. 2019b). This combined approach may also help address the increased vulnerability to suicidal ideation often associated with hypersomnolence. Very interestingly, our study revealed that the duration of N3 sleep and its proportion relative to total sleep time (TST) are highly distinct variables and are not associated with the same characteristics of depression. Patients with increased N3 duration showed greater severity of anxiety and depressive symptoms, an association not observed in patients with an increased N3% of TST. This suggests that N3 sleep duration, and its proportion may reflect different underlying pathophysiological mechanisms of depression. These patterns suggest that N3 sleep should not be considered as a single, universal “sleep biomarker”. Rather, its full‐night distribution—encompassing latency and duration, and cycle—represents a dynamic and informative dimension for identifying biologically and clinically distinct subtypes of depression. The physiological heterogeneity captured by N3 architecture appears to mirror key differences in illness trajectory, relapse vulnerability, and care needs, offering a foundation for precision psychiatry anchored in objective sleep parameters.

Several limitations should be noted. Our findings suggest that N3 proportion and duration may contribute to clinically relevant stratification within depressive episodes. However, this study was exploratory and had limited statistical power. Accordingly, it should be considered a first step in the development of a predictive model. A second phase, involving a larger and independent sample with alpha risk correction for multiple tests, is planned to formally evaluate model performance, generalisability, and robustness using appropriate validation procedures. More broadly, evaluation of N3 measures as potential biomarkers will require controlled, longitudinal studies with independent samples. In addition, our data were based on single‐night PSG recordings, which may be affected by first‐night effects. The absence of randomised psychotropic exposure introduces possible confounding. Nevertheless, sensitivity analyses showed that only anxiolytics increased N3 proportion, and anxiolytic use did not remain in the final regression model predicting elevated N3% TST, suggesting that this phenotype is not medication‐driven. Finally, variability in PSG systems and clinical settings may have introduced measurement variance.

Conclusion

Our results identify distinct N3 phenotypes linked to specific clinical profiles and illness severity, supporting individualised depression care guided by sleep architecture. N3 metrics may stratify relapse risk, chronicity, and healthcare use beyond symptom scales. Future studies should use multi‐night PSG, clustering, and longitudinal follow‐up to validate these phenotypes and guide targeted interventions.
