Research on corrosion performance of Q235B steel in industrial coastal atmospheric environment
Authors: Wenjuan Chen, Gang Pan, Zihan Liu, Anding Huang, Shushu Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aims to obtain the effect of SO2 on the corrosion of Q235B low carbon steel in simulated coastal industrial atmospheres.The corrosion performance of the steel in the simulated atmospheres was studied using wet/dry cyclic corrosion test (CCT), electrochemical testing analysis, scanning electron microscopy, X-ray diffraction, and COMSOL finite element simulation methods. As a result the influence of SO2, the corrosion rate of Q235B steel increases rapidly in the initial stage of corrosion, and gradually decreases in the later stages, eventually stabilizing. There is a gradual increase in ferrite content on the surface of the steel during the initial stage of corrosion. In addition, the main components of the rust layer α-FeOOH and γ-FeOOH, and high concentrations of SO2 promote α-FeOOH formation and inhibit γ-FeOOH formation.Keywords: Low carbon steel, Atmospheric corrosion, Rust, Corrosion resistance, Layer

Introduction

As one of the most important basic materials for national economic construction, steel is known as the “grain of industry”. At present, about 10 % of the world steel production is scrapped due to corrosion every year [1,2]. Nearly 100 million tons of steel rusts every year in China, and 60 % of the total steel is used in the atmospheric environment [3]. Therefore, it is of great significance to study the atmospheric corrosion of steel for promoting the high-quality development of the steel industry.

The atmospheric corrosion on steel occurs under a thin liquid film driven by the anodic dissolution of iron and the cathodic reduction of dissolved oxygen. The hydrolysis products in the rust layer are transformed into iron oxides and hydroxyl oxides with different chemical stability due to the influence of atmospheric environment [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. The main components in the rust layer that affect the atmospheric corrosion rate of steel are α-FeOOH, β-FeOOH, γ- FeOOH and Fe3O4. Among them, α-FeOOH is the most stable component with an inhibitory effect on atmospheric corrosion; γ-FeOOH, β-FeOOH are increasingly unstable component which could easily convert into Fe3O4 through cathodic reduction reactions, accelerating the corrosion of steel eventually. Therefore, the composition and distribution of corrosion products are the determining factors affecting the atmospheric corrosion evolution characteristics of steel.

The environment of atmospheric corrosion is consisted of coastal atmospheric corrosion (mainly Cl−), industrial atmospheric corrosion (mainly SO2), and industrial coastal atmospheric corrosion (mainly Cl− and SO2) according to the corrosive media in the atmosphere [11,12]. Early studies [[13], [14], [15]] on coastal atmospheric corrosion have shown that Cl− has a small volume and can penetrate the corrosion product layer to reach the surface of steel, causing depolarization of O2 and inducing corrosion, like a catalyst. With the development of corrosion detection methods, it has been found that the corrosion products of steel in coastal atmospheric environments are mainly composed of Fe3O4, α-FeOOH, γ-FeOOH and β-FeOOH, and the content of β-FeOOH in the rust layer increases with the growth of Cl− concentration and reduction of α-FeOOH which has a protective effect [16,17]. The corrosion products can convert into inner and outer layers with further corrosion process. The components of the inner layer mainly are α-FeOOH, β-FeOOH and Fe3O4, while the outer layer is γ-FeOOH. In the industrial atmosphere, SO2 continuously dissolves steel in the way of “acid regeneration cycle” [18,19]. The corrosion products of steel are mainly composed of Fe3O4, α-FeOOH and γ-FeOOH (no β-FeOOH is detected).The α-FeOOH increases with the increase of SO2 concentration, while the γ-FeOOH decreases [[20], [21], [22]]. Preliminary studies [[23], [24], [25]] have found that in the industrial coastal atmosphere where Cl− and SO2 interact, steel follows the corrosion law of coastal atmospheric environments with the lower concentration of SO2 while steel follows the corrosion law of industrial atmospheric environments with the higher concentration of SO2. However, there is still a lack of research on the corrosion mechanism of steel in the industrial coastal atmospheric environment. However, there is currently a lack of systematic research in the academic community on the corrosion mechanism of steel in the industrial coastal atmospheric environment.

This work combines laboratory simulated atmospheric wet/dry cyclic corrosion test experiment (CCT) and finite element simulation methods, using electrochemical measurements, scanning electron microscopy, and X-ray diffraction to study the corrosion rate, corrosion evolution characteristics, rust layer structure, corrosion product phase composition, and corrosion mechanism of low-carbon steel in industrial coastal atmospheric environments.

Experimental method

Preparation of test specimens

The experimental material was Q235B low carbon steel (Anshan Iron and Steel Plant, Anshan, China), with a chemical composition (mass fraction,%) of C 0.210, Si 0.210, Mn 0.580, S 0.036, P 0.017, Cu 0.020 and remainder Fe. The specimens were processed into two sizes, each with a size of 30 mm × 30 mm × 5 mm and 10 mm × 10 mm × 5 mm, respectively for wet/dry cyclic corrosion weight gain experiments and electrochemical measurements. The specimens were encapsulated in epoxy resin mounted inside a PVC pipe, mechanically ground sequentially with SiC sandpapers (MT, Guangzhou, China) down to 2000 grit, washed with alcohol, and dried with cold air before being placed in a dryer for later use.

Corrosion and performance tests

There were three types of solutions prepared for simulating the industrial coastal atmospheric environment, namely 0.05 mol/L NaCl+0.01 mol/L Na2SO3, 0.05 mol/L NaCl+0.05 mol/L Na2SO3, and 0.05 mol/L NaCl+0.1 mol/L Na2SO3; and then adjusted the three solutions to pH 6.0 with 10 % sulfuric acid by mass fraction, and recorded them as # 1 solution, # 2 solution, and # 3 solution in sequence. The operation process of the wet/dry cyclic corrosion test (CCT) was as follows: at the beginning of the first cycle, used a pipette to drop the solution onto the surface of the specimens with 40μ L/cm2, then dried the specimens in an experimental chamber with a temperature of 30 °C and a relative humidity of 60 % for 12 h. Afterwards, took the specimens out and weighed them (Only samples with a size of 30 mm × 30 mm x 5 mm need to be weighed), and cleaned the surface of the specimens with distilled water. The experimental process flowchart was shown in Fig. 1; repeated the above steps for each subsequent cycle. During this process, there are 5 parallel samples used for weighing in each corrosive environment. The corrosion product phase composition of the specimen was analyzed using a DX-2700B model X-ray diffractometer (manufactured by Dandong Haoyuan Instrument in China).

DX-2700B X-ray diffractometer (XRD) (manufactured by Dandong Haoyuan Instrument in China) was used to test the surface of corrosion specimens and analyze the phase composition. The scanning speed of diffraction measurement was 2°/min. The phase composition was semi-quantitatively analyzed by comparing the relative peak strength of each phase in the XRD results. The surface of the specimen after different cycles of corrosion was characterized using Regulus 8100 scanning electron microscopy (SEM) produced by Hitachi Inc. in Tokyo, Japan, and the morphology of the specimen surface was directly observed.

The electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and potentiodynamic polarization curves were tested using the CS310 electrochemical workstation (manufactured by Wuhan Ke Site in China). During the experiment, a three electrode system was used: the working electrode was a rusted steel specimen with different wet/dry cycles of corrosion; the auxiliary electrode was a 4 cm2 Pt sheet; the reference electrode was a saturated calomel electrode (SCE). The working temperature was room temperature (25 °C), and all potential values were relative to the potential of saturated calomel electrode. The frequency range of electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was 10 mHz–100 kHz, and the amplitude of the disturbance AC potential applied during testing was 10 mV. The scanning potential range of the potentiodynamic polarization curve was ±0.3 V vs. Ecorr (relative to the open circuit potential), and the scanning speed was 1 mV/s; the scanning potential range of the linear polarization curve was ±0.01 V vs. Ecorr (relative to the open circuit potential), and the scanning speed was 0.167 mV/s.

In order to further analyze the corrosion process on the surface of the specimen and predict its lifespan, COMSOL Multiphysics 6.1 software was used to conduct multi-physical field finite element simulation through secondary current distribution, deformation geometry, dilute material transfer, and level set. Results

Corrosion kinetics

Fig. 2 shows the corrosion weight gain (ΔW) curves of Q235B steel in the simulated industrial coastal atmosphere. The corrosion weight gain of the specimens increase continuously with the prolongation of corrosion time, indicating that the corrosion process continues. By comparing the three curves, it can be seen that as the concentration of Na2SO3 in the simulated atmosphere increases, the corrosion weight gain also increases. This indicates that the increase in SO2 concentration in the industrial coastal atmosphere will accelerate corrosion of the steel.

The corrosion and weight gain curve of Q235B steel in the simulated industrial coastal atmosphere.

Fig. 3 shows the curves of the average corrosion rate as a function of CCT number obtained through Fig. 2 and the power function formula (ΔW = ANn)for corrosion weight gain [11,14]. It can be seen that the corrosion rate of Q235B steel in the simulated industrial coastal atmospheres rapidly increased in the first two CCT, and then showed an overall downward trend. At the first two CCT, the slope of the curve # 1 is higher than that of the curves # 2 and # 3, and at 2 CCT, the corrosion rate of specimen # 1 is greater than that of specimen # 2. However, after 2 CCT, the corrosion rate of Q235B steel in the three simulated industrial coastal atmosphere decreased with the increase of Na2SO3 concentration in the simulated atmosphere. This indicates that high concentrations of SO2 will inhibit the increase of corrosion rate in the early stage of corrosion, but accelerate corrosion in the later stage of corrosion. The average corrosion rate of the three simulated atmospheres shows a maximum value around 2 CCT. The reason may be that in the later stage of corrosion, the corrosion products on the steel surface gradually accumulate, and the density of the rust layer increases, making it difficult for O2 and electrolyte solutions to pass through the rust layer and contact the metal matrix, resulting in a decrease in corrosion rate. Afterwards, there may be cracking of the rust layer, which increases the corrosion rate again.

Q235B Double log curve of average rate-period in simulated industrial coastal atmospheric environment.

Surface morphology and composition

Fig. 4 shows the SEM image of the specimen surface after 5 CCT corrosion. Comparing Fig. 4 (a), (b), and (c), it can be seen that as the concentration of Na2SO3 in simulated industrial coastal atmospheric environment gradually increases, the surface cracks also increase and the corrosion products become more loose. Further magnification of the corrosion product image is shown in Fig. 4 (d), (e), and (f), and it can be seen that the surface of specimen #1 is relatively dense, presenting a rock sugar like morphology; the surface of specimen #2 is relatively loose, presenting a spherical morphology; the surface of specimen #3 products is more loose, presenting a granular morphology, and there are many pores inside the products. The above results indicates that after 5 CCT corrosion, as the concentration of SO2 in the atmospheric environment increases, the corrosion products on the steel surface becomes looser, and the blocking effect on corrosive media becomes weaker, which is more conducive to further corrosion of the steel substrate.

Fig. 5 shows the SEM image of the specimen surface after 10 CCT corrosion. From Fig. 5 (a) and (d), it can be seen that the surface of specimen #1 is relatively dense with fewer pores; From Fig. 5 (b) and (e), it can be seen that the surface of specimen #2 is relatively flat, but there are obvious cracks and small pores in the corrosion product layer; From Fig. 5 (c) and (f), it can be seen that the surface of specimen #3 is relatively loose and has many small pores. This indicates that after 10 CCT corrosion, as the concentration of SO2 in the atmospheric environment increases, the number of pores in the product layer on the steel surface increases, and the blocking effect on corrosive media becomes weaker, which is conducive to further corrosion of the steel substrate.

Fig. 6 shows the SEM image of the specimen surface after 30 CCT corrosion. From Fig. 6 (a) and (d), it can be seen that the surface of specimen # 1 is still relatively dense with fewer pores. From Fig. 6 (b) and (e), it can be seen that the surface of specimen # 2 is still relatively flat, but there are obvious cracks in the corrosion product layer and a significant increase in pores. From Fig. 6 (c) and (f), it can be seen that the surface of specimen # 3 is relatively flat, but there are obvious cracks and many small pores in the corrosion product layer. This indicates that after 30 CCT corrosion, as the concentration of SO2 in the atmospheric environment increases, the corrosion product layer on the steel surface thickens, and the presence of pores is still more conducive to the further occurrence of corrosion.

SEM image of specimens surface after 30 CCT corrosion:(a) and (d) for specimen #1; (b) and (e) for specimen #2; (c) and (f) for specimen #3.

Fig. 7 shows X-ray diffraction patterns of specimens #1∼#3 after 30 CCT corrosion in a simulated industrial coastal atmospheric environment. It can be seen that the rust layer composition of low carbon steel is mainly composed of α-FeOOH, γ-FeOOH, Fe3O4 and a small amount β-FeOOH. It can be seen that the diffraction peak intensity of Fe3O4 in the three specimens is not significantly different, indicating that there is not much difference in Fe3O4 content. For the diffraction angle corresponding to the α-FeOOH characteristic diffraction peak at 21°, it can be seen that the α-FeOOH diffraction peak intensity of specimen # 1 is relatively low, and the peak intensity of specimens #2 and # 3 increases sequentially. However, the γ-FeOOH diffraction peak (around 27°) intensity of specimens #1∼ #3 decreases in sequence. This indicates that as the concentration of SO2 in the atmospheric environment increases, the γ-FeOOH content decreases, while the α-FeOOH content increased in the rust.

Electrochemical properties of the rusted steel

Fig. 8 shows the polarization curve results of Q235B steel under different corrosion cycles in a simulated industrial coastal atmospheric environment. It can be seen that specimens # 1 ∼ # 3 have the lowest corrosion potential at 1 CCT. In general, the corrosion potential Ecorr can reflect the stability of the rust layer on the metal surface. The higher the corrosion potential, the better the electrochemical stability of the rust layer. In the early stage of corrosion, due to the instability of the rust layer on the metal surface, it hardly plays a protective role. As the corrosion cycle prolongs, the corrosion potential gradually increases, indicating that the rust layer becomes denser and more stable, and the protective effect becomes stronger. Comparing the corrosion potential of specimens # 1 ∼ # 3 at 30 CCT, it was found that specimen # 1 had the highest corrosion potential (−0.577V), followed by specimen # 2 (−0.641V), and specimen # 3 had the lowest corrosion potential (−0.663), indicating that the higher the concentration of SO2 in the atmospheric environment, the greater the corrosion tendency.

Polarization curves of Q235B steel under different corrosion cycles in the industrial coastal atmosphere: (a) for specimen #1; (b) for specimen #2; (c) for specimen #3.

By using the Tafel line extrapolation method on the polarization curves shown in Fig. 8, the corrosion current density curves with respect to the corrosion cycle was calculated, as shown in Fig. 9. It can be clearly seen that the corrosion current density of low carbon steel in the three atmospheric environments shows a roughly similar trend with corrosion time. In the early stage of corrosion, the corrosion current density Icorr increases rapidly with the prolongation of the corrosion cycle, reaching its maximum value around 10 CCT, then slowly decreasing and eventually stabilizing. This indicates that as corrosion continues, the coating formed by the accumulation of corrosion products has a protective effect on the steel substrate. Comparing the corrosion current density curves of different corrosion environments, it can be seen that the corrosion rate increases with the increase of Na2SO3 concentration in the simulated environment. This indicates that high concentrations of SO2 in industrial coastal atmospheric environments can accelerate atmospheric corrosion of steel.

Corrosion current density curves of Q235B steel in industrial coastal atmosphere as a function of corrosion cycle.

When the overpotential is less than 10 mV, there is a linear relationship between the overpotential and the current density, and its slope corresponds to the polarization resistance Rp. It can be seen that the variation trend of Rp in the three simulated environments is roughly the same. As corrosion progresses, Rp first sharply decreases, then slightly rebounds, and finally tends to stabilize. Comparing different simulation environments, except for 1 CCT, the Rp gradually decreases as the concentration of Na2SO3 in the simulated environment increases during the remaining corrosion cycles. The higher the concentration of SO2 in the industrial coastal atmosphere, the greater the destructive effect on the rust layer. This indicates that the higher the concentration of SO2 in the industrial coastal atmosphere, the greater the destructive effect on the rust layer. This result can be confirmed by the corrosion current density result in Fig. 9. At the beginning of corrosion, Rp is larger and the rust layer has a good protective effect, so Icorr is smaller and the corrosion rate is lower.

Discussion

Initial atmospheric corrosion

From the experimental results in Fig. 6, Fig. 9, it can be observed that the corrosion rate of steel in the three industrial coastal atmospheres shows a trend of first increasing and then decreasing with the prolongation of corrosion time. Previous studies have shown that the increase in initial corrosion rate is generally related to the reduction of rust [[26], [27], [28]]. To confirm this point, this article conducted COMSOL finite element simulation, which ignored the reduction process of corrosion products. The curve of the initial corrosion rate of Q235B steel with corrosion time was obtained as shown in Fig. 13. It can be observed that in the early stage of corrosion, the corrosion rate shows a significant increasing trend with the prolongation of corrosion time, indicating that besides the reduction of rust, there may be other reasons that promote atmospheric corrosion of steel. Fig. 14 shows the corrosion morphology and ferrite content of the steel substrate surface as a function of corrosion time obtained through COMSOL finite element simulation. From 15 (a), it can be seen that the corrosion on the steel surface within 20 h is not uniform. This is because the Q235B steel matrix is composed of two phases: ferrite and cementite, with ferrite as the anodic dissolution phase and cementite as the cathodic phase. As the corrosion continues, the position of ferrite corrodes rapidly and forms a concave pit shape. The exposed cementite may become convex and may also peel off. This leads to a change in the content of ferrite on the surface of the steel, as shown in Fig. 14 (b), which gradually increases with the prolongation of corrosion time. This may be because the ferrite phase preferentially corrodes on the surface of steel, followed by the formation of corrosion pits. The surface area inside the pits is larger than that on the original plane, resulting in an increase in the ferrite content exposed to the electrolyte. This phenomenon will have two effects on the corrosion process of steel: the corrosion rate of the primary battery formed by cementite and ferrite decreases due to the reduction of cathode area; The corrosion rate of primary batteries using H + or O2 reduction as the cathode process will increase with the increase of exposed ferrite content, and this effect will dominate. This results in the corrosion rate of steel increasing with corrosion time as shown in Fig. 13. Therefore, this article believes that the increase in initial corrosion rate may not only be related to the accelerated corrosion effect of rust reduction, but also to the morphology of the steel surface and the relative content of the constituent phases.

Later stage of atmospheric corrosion

In the industrial coastal atmospheric environment, as corrosion continues, the rust layer on the surface of steel gradually thickens. Due to the blocking effect of the rust layer, the process of corrosive ions entering the steel substrate is hindered, and the corrosion rate will slow down with the extension of time. The regeneration cycle corrosion of acid will gradually play a dominant role, and the reaction process is as follows:

Hydrolysis of FeSO4 to form hydroxylated compounds and free H2SO4:

H2SO4 accelerates the corrosion of Fe:

The H2SO4 formed during this process dissolves the corrosion products and forms pores in the rust layer, providing channels for O2, Cl−, and SO42− to enter the matrix. As the concentration of SO2 increases, more H2SO4 is generated during the acid regeneration cycle, making the rust layer more easily damaged and reducing its density, thereby accelerating corrosion. In the industrial coastal atmospheric environment, an increase in SO2 concentration promotes the regeneration cycle of acid and accelerates the corrosion of steel. However, on the other hand, an increase in SO2 concentration leads to a decrease in the O2 content in the electrolyte and an increase in the production of α-FeOOH, which will be beneficial for reducing the corrosion rate. In this experimental environment, it is possible that the former has a more significant impact on the corrosion rate of steel than the latter, resulting in an overall increase in corrosion rate with increasing SO2 concentration.
