LINC02178 drives lung adenocarcinoma progression and serves as a therapeutic target for nanodelivery-based intervention
Authors: Lizhong Zeng, Haimei Wen, Tong Jiao, Qiuhong Zhang, Xin Zhao, Long Zhang, Shuanying Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGene therapy has garnered significant attention in cancer treatment. Here, we identified the long noncoding RNA LINC02178 as an oncogenic driver in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and developed a nanoparticle-based small interfering RNA delivery system (NPs/2178) targeting this gene, providing novel insights for gene therapy development. Bioinformatics analysis was used to identify LINC02178 as a candidate oncogene and evaluate its clinical value. Subcellular localization of LINC02178 was determined through cytoplasmic/nuclear RNA fractionation coupled withs Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). The transfection efficiency of the NPs was assessed using confocal microscopy and RT‒qPCR. The biological toxicity of NPs/2178 was tested via cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) and live/dead assays. Flow cytometry, 5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU), CCK-8 and colony formation assays were conducted to evaluate apoptosis and proliferation. The therapeutic efficacy and biosafety of this platform were further validated in a subcutaneous xenograft mouse model. LINC02178 expression was elevated in LUAD tissues and cell lines and correlated with advanced clinical stage and poor prognosis. Gene enrichment analysis indicated that LINC02178 was strongly related to cancer progression and apoptosis. The NPs/2178 platform achieved effective LINC02178 knockdown and demonstrated that the cytotoxicity of LUAD cells was significantly greater than that of mouse fibroblasts. The NPs/2178 gene delivery system significantly promoted LUAD apoptosis and inhibited cell proliferation in vitro. In vivo studies revealed marked tumour growth inhibition by NPs/2178 without observable systemic toxicity. LINC02178 functions as an oncogenic factor that promotes LUAD progression. Introduction

According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), lung cancer is the most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related death, accounting for 18.7% of all cancer-related mortality worldwide1. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the most prevalent pathological subtype of lung cancer, constitutes 38.5% of all lung cancer cases2. Owing to the lack of effective early diagnostic markers and typical clinical manifestations, a significant proportion of LUAD patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, which contributes to the high mortality rate associated with this disease. In recent years, the widespread adoption of low-dose spiral computed tomography (CT) screening, along with rapid advancements in targeted therapy and immunotherapy, has improved the survival rate of LUAD patients3. However, the five-year survival rate for patients with lung cancer remains below 20%, indicating a poor prognosis4. Therefore, elucidating the molecular mechanisms underlying LUAD initiation and progression, as well as identifying potential therapeutic targets, is critical for improving patient outcomes.

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a class of noncoding RNA molecules whose lengths exceed 200 nucleotides. Although they do not encode proteins, lncRNAs regulate gene expression through various mechanisms, such as the modulation of chromatin structure and gene transcription5. Studies have demonstrated that lncRNAs are involved in numerous physiological and pathological processes, such as cell proliferation, apoptosis, inflammatory responses, and immune regulation6–8. In cancer, lncRNAs play particularly critical roles, influencing tumour initiation, metastasis, and drug resistance9–11. With increasing research, the clinical importance of lncRNAs in various diseases, such as cancer, has garnered increasing attention, and their potential as diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets is being progressively explored12–14. In general, lncRNAs are key regulators of gene expression and are significantly associated with human diseases. There has been rapid progress in developing lncRNA-targeted therapies for various diseases, and some have reached the clinical trial stage15–17.

LINC02178 is a long noncoding RNA located on chromosome 16, with a length of 425 bp. Through differential gene expression analysis, we revealed that LINC02178 is significantly overexpressed in LUAD tissues and strongly correlated with poor prognosis in patients. Previous studies have revealed significant roles for LINC02178 in various cancers. In breast cancer, LINC02178 has been implicated in the regulation of gene methylation18. In endometrial cancer, LUAD, and bladder cancer, LINC02178 is associated with prognosis in patients19–21. Additionally, LINC02178 is involved in the regulation of the immune microenvironment in lung squamous cell carcinoma22. However, the functional mechanisms and therapeutic potential of LINC02178 remain poorly understood and warrant further investigation. Therefore, we conducted an in-depth study to elucidate the biological functions and therapeutic potential of LINC02178 in LUAD.

In recent years, an increasing number of gene-based therapeutics have been approved for clinical use, making gene therapy a promising approach to overcome the limitations of conventional treatments for various diseases23,24. Small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), a class of small RNA molecules, have emerged as widely used gene therapy tools because of their ability to directly mediate target gene silencing without integrating foreign genes into the genome25,26. However, naked siRNA is highly susceptible to degradation by nucleases in vivo, significantly limiting its clinical application. Therefore, the development of safe and effective siRNA delivery systems capable of transporting therapeutic siRNA to specific target sites remains a critical challenge in gene therapy. In recent years, various nanoparticle-targeted drug delivery systems have demonstrated significant progress in cancer therapy27. Our team has discovered and synthesized a lanthanide-based material that exhibits excellent biocompatibility in mammalian cells. These findings suggest that this material can be combined with LINC02178 to explore a novel therapeutic target for LUAD.

Through bioinformatics analysis, we revealed that LINC02178 is highly expressed in LUAD and is significantly correlated with poor patient prognosis, suggesting that LINC02178 may play a critical role in promoting the malignant progression of LUAD. To further investigate its biological functions, we knocked down LINC02178 expression and evaluated its effect on the function of LUAD cells. Additionally, we combined lanthanide-based materials with LINC02178 siRNA to explore its potential as a therapeutic target for LUAD. These findings provide a novel theoretical foundation for the development of LUAD treatments.

Methods and materials

Bioinformatics analysis of LINC02178

RNA sequencing data and clinical records from 535 LUAD patients and 59 nontumour controls were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. Differential expression analysis was performed using the “edgeR” R package28. Kaplan‒Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazards regression analysis were used to assess the prognostic significance of LINC02178 in LUAD. Patients with incomplete clinical records or follow-up times < 60 days were excluded from subsequent analyses. Six clinicopathological parameters were included in the correlation studies: age, sex, tumour stage (T) stage, lymph node metastasis (N) stage, and tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stage.

Gene enrichment analysis of LINC02178

To explore the functional relevance of LINC02178, we performed a Pearson correlation analysis between LINC02178 and protein-coding genes (PCGs). PCGs meeting the following criteria were defined as coexpressed genes: absolute Pearson’s correlation coefficient |r|> 0.30 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.01. A total of 464 PCGs were significantly coexpressed with LINC02178. These genes were subsequently subjected to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis using the KOBAS 3.0 online platform (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn)29.

LUAD tissues and cells

Fifteen paired LUAD tissues and matched adjacent nontumour tissues were collected to validate LINC02178 expression. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Xi’an Jiaotong University (Ethics Approval No.: 2023179), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was carried out in compliance with the Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki Declaration of 2013). Five LUAD cell lines (A549, H1299, PC-9, H1975, and SPC-A-1), one normal bronchial epithelial cell line (BEAS-2B), and one mouse fibroblast line (L929) were included. All cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 (Gibco, USA Cat. No. C22400500BT) medium supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS; VivaCell, China, Cat. No. C04001-500) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator.

Synthesis of LaN nanoparticles

First, 0.2 g of La(NO3)3 was dissolved in 30 mL of deionized water, followed by the addition of 1 g of cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB). The pH of the mixture was adjusted to 10–11 using 1 M NaOH solution, and the mixture was stirred magnetically for 30 min at room temperature. The mixture was heated at 120 °C for 12 h. The resulting precipitate was collected by centrifugation (9800 rpm, 10 min) and then freeze-dried under vacuum. High-temperature calcination was performed on the freeze-dried samples. The temperature was increased from 25 to 500 °C at a rate of 2 °C per minute. The temperature was maintained at 500 °C for 6 h, after which the samples were allowed to cool naturally to room temperature. The morphology of the obtained La2O3 was examined using Transmission Electron Microscope (TEM), and the image was shown in Fig. S2.

On the basis of the sequencing of LINC02178, we designed three siRNAs and commissioned them by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA, Cat. No.11668019) was used as a transfection reagent to evaluate the knockdown efficiency of siRNA targeting LINC02178 and as a positive control to verify the transfection efficiency of the LaN nanomaterial. Subcellular localization of LINC02178

Cytoplasmic and nuclear RNA fractions were isolated using a Cytoplasmic & Nuclear RNA Purification Kit (Norgen Biotek, Canada, Cat. No. 21000). The expression levels of LINC02178 in the cytoplasmic and nuclear fractions were quantified by RT‒qPCR, with GAPDH and U6 serving as cytoplasmic and nuclear reference genes, respectively31.

Preparation of siRNA nanodelivery systems

On the basis of the results of preliminary experiments, the optimal volume ratio of LaN to siRNA was determined to be 4:1. The results of the preliminary experiment are shown in Fig. S1. LaN and siRNA were mixed in a sterile centrifuge tube at a 3:1 mass ratio. The mixture was sonicated at 100 Hz for 60 s to facilitate the formation of LaN-siRNA complexes. NPs/2178 and NPs/NC were subsequently incubated at room temperature for 20 min to allow stabilization, followed by transfection into target cells.

Live/dead cell assay

Target cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at a density of 2,000 cells/well and cultured for 24 h prior to transfection. After 48 h of transfection, the culture medium was carefully removed, and the cells were gently washed twice with PBS. A working solution was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher, Cat. No. L3224), and the cells were incubated with the solution in the dark for 30 min. Fluorescence images were captured using a fluorescence microscope to evaluate cell viability32.

Apoptosis assay

Transfected cells were incubated with 500 μL of working solution (containing 490 μL of binding buffer, 5 μL of Annexin V-APC, and 5 μL of 7-AAD) at 25–30 °C for 15 min in the dark. Apoptosis was then analysed by flow cytometry (Becton Dickinson, USA) within 1 h. The apoptosis detection kit was purchased from Jiangsu KeyGEN BioTECH Corp., Ltd. (Nanjing, China, Cat. No. KGA1104).

5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) assay

Transfected cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with 100 μL of EdU working solution for 2 h. In accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, stained with Apollo solution to detect EdU-positive cells, and counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 to visualize DNA. Fluorescence images were captured using a Nikon microscope (Tokyo, Japan). EdU kit purchased from RiboBio (Guangzhou, China, Cat. No. C10310). The percentage of EdU-positive cells was calculated as follows: EdU-positive percentage (%) = (number of EdU-positive cells/number of Hoechst-stained cells) × 100.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

Transfected cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured for 24, 48, or 72 h. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with 100 μL of CCK-8 working solution (prepared by mixing complete medium and CCK-8 reagent at a 9:1 ratio; Seven Seas Biotech, Chongqing, China, Cat. No. C008) for 40 min. Absorbance was measured at 480 nm using a spectrophotometer (BioTek, USA).

Colony formation assay

Transfected cells were seeded in 6-well culture plates and incubated for 7 days. The cells were subsequently fixed with 4% formaldehyde for 20 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 20 min. Colony images were captured, and the number of colonies was quantified.

Subcutaneous tumour model in nude mice

Four-week-old female nude mice were purchased from the Animal Center of Xi’an Jiaotong University and housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. Approximately 4 × 10⁶ PC-9 cells were subcutaneously inoculated into the right flank of each mouse. After 4 days, the mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6 per group) and administered ddH2O, NPs/2178, or NPs/NC via tail vein injection every 2 days, which derived from our research group’s prior experience33. The experiment was terminated after 7 doses. At the experimental endpoint, the nude mice were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital (250 mg/kg). Upon confirmation of death, the tumours were harvested and weighed, and their sizes were measured. Tumour tissues and related organs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for further analysis of target gene expression. All animal procedures were performed in compliance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC Approval Number: XJTUAE2023-1114), adhering to the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining

The tumour tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and cleared in xylene. Dehydrated tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 4–5 μm thickness. The sections were blocked with 10% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies against PCNA, Ki-67, p53, and Bcl-2 overnight at 4 °C. After being washed, the sections were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. All primary and secondary antibodies were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, USA; anti-p53: Cat. No. 2524; anti-PCNA: Cat. No. 13110; anti-Ki67: Cat. No. 12202; anti-Bcl-2: Cat. No. 15071; anit-rabbit secondary antibody: Cat. No. 8114; anti-mouse secondary antibody: Cat. No. 8125). Finally, the sections were mounted and imaged under a light microscope to evaluate target gene expression.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data were analysed using Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad, USA). Kaplan‒Meier survival curves were compared by a two-sided log-rank test. The expression levels of LINC02178 in 15 paired LUAD and adjacent normal lung tissue samples were analysed using paired Student’s t tests. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to compare clinical data among multiple groups. Differences between two groups were assessed by an unpaired Student’s t test. A p value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. All cell experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates.

Results

LINC02178 is overexpressed in LUAD and is positively related to advanced clinical stage

Differential expression analysis revealed that LINC02178 expression was significantly upregulated in 535 LUAD tissues compared with that in nontumour tissues (Fig. 1A; logFC = 5.74; FDR = 7.85E-12). To further validate these findings, we examined LINC02178 expression in 15 paired LUAD tumour and adjacent nontumour tissues. Consistent with the bioinformatics results, LINC02178 was highly expressed in LUAD tumour tissues (Fig. 1B). Additionally, LINC02178 expression was significantly higher in LUAD cell lines than in normal bronchial epithelial cells (Fig. 1C). These results collectively demonstrate that LINC02178 is overexpressed in LUAD tissues and cells.

Evaluation of the cytotoxicity and siRNA transfection efficiency of LaN

To assess the potential of LaN as a siRNA delivery vector for gene therapy, we evaluated its cytotoxicity and siRNA transfection efficiency. As shown in Fig. 3A and B, live/dead assays were performed to assess the cytotoxicity of LaN in LUAD PC-9 cells and mouse fibroblast L929 cells. The results indicated that at a concentration of approximately 40 μg/mL, the number of dead cells in the L929 group significantly increased, whereas at a concentration of approximately 20 μg/mL, the number of dead cells in the PC-9 group significantly increased. To further validate these findings, CCK-8 assays were conducted to measure the cytotoxicity of LaN in L929 and PC-9 cells. The results revealed significant differences in cell viability between L929 and PC-9 cells at LaN concentrations of approximately 40 μg/mL and 30 μg/mL, respectively (Fig. 3C and D). These findings suggest that compared with mouse fibroblasts, human LUAD cells exhibit greater cytotoxicity to LaN. Next, the siRNA transfection efficiency of LaN was evaluated using confocal microscopy, with Lipofectamine 2000 serving as a positive control. As illustrated in Fig. 3E, the ability of LAN to deliver Cy3-labelled siRNA was observed using a confocal microscope. The siRNA transfection efficiency of LaN was comparable to that of Lipofectamine 2000. Collectively, these results indicate that LaN possesses the necessary properties to serve as a gene therapy vector.

Functional prediction and subcellular localization of LINC02178

To investigate the functional role of LINC02178, we first predicted its biological functions through gene enrichment analysis. Using a |correlation coefficient|> 0.3 and an FDR < 0.01 as selection criteria, 464 protein-coding genes (PCGs) were identified as highly coexpressed with LINC02178. These PCGs were analysed using the KOBAS online database to identify pathways associated with LINC02178. The results revealed that LINC02178 was significantly enriched in pathways related to apoptosis, p53 signalling, Wnt signalling, and MAPK signalling (Fig. 4A). These pathways are closely associated with LUAD progression, further supporting the oncogenic role of LINC02178 in LUAD. Next, we determined the subcellular localization of LINC02178 in LUAD cells. As shown in Fig. 4B–4D, LINC02178 was predominantly localized in the cytoplasm.

Knockdown of LINC02178 promotes LUAD apoptosis

To validate the functional role of LINC02178 in LUAD, we synthesized three siRNAs targeting LINC02178. All three siRNAs effectively knocked down LINC02178 expression (Fig. 4E). A nanodelivery system, LaN/LINC02178-siRNA (NPs/2178), was constructed using LaN nanoparticles and siRNA, and its knockdown efficiency was evaluated with Lipofectamine 2000 as a positive control. As shown in Fig. 4F, NPs/2178 successfully knocked down LINC02178 expression. On the basis of the gene enrichment analysis results, we further assessed the effect of LINC02178 knockdown on apoptosis. The results demonstrated that suppression of LINC02178 expression significantly promoted apoptosis in LUAD cells (Fig. 4G), suggesting that LINC02178 may promote LUAD progression by inhibiting apoptosis.

Knockdown of LINC02178 suppresses LUAD cell proliferation

Given that LINC02178 is closely associated with cancer-related signalling pathways and apoptosis, we hypothesized that LINC02178 may also promote cell proliferation. First, we assessed cell viability after transfection with NPs/2178. The results revealed that the number of dead cells in the NPs/2178 group was significantly greater than that in the NPs/NC group, which is consistent with the results observed in the positive control Lipo/2178 group (Fig. 5A). The results of the CCK-8 assay demonstrated that the cell growth rate in the NPs/2178 group was significantly lower than that in the NPs/NC group, with trends consistent with those in the Lipo/2178 group (Fig. 5B). Next, we evaluated the percentage of EdU-positive cells in each group. The results revealed that the percentage of EdU-positive cells in the NPs/2178 and Lipo/2178 groups was significantly lower than that in the NPs/NC and control groups (Fig. 5C). Furthermore, compared with those in the NP/NC group, the number of colonies in the NPs/2178 group was significantly lower, with a similar trend observed in the Lipo/2178 group (Fig. 5D). Collectively, these results indicate that knockdown of LINC02178 inhibits LUAD cell proliferation.

Antitumour efficiency of LaN/LINC02178-siRNA in a subcutaneous tumour model.

These results suggest that LINC02178 functions as an oncogenic factor in lung adenocarcinoma. To further explore the therapeutic potential of NPs/2178 in tumour therapy, we evaluated its antitumour efficacy in vivo. A subcutaneous tumour model was established in nude mice, and tumour growth was monitored following continuous tail vein injection of NPs/2178, NPs/NC, or ddH2O. After the treatment cycle was complete, the mice were euthanized under anaesthesia, and the subcutaneous tumours were excised, weighed, and measured (Fig. 6A). The results demonstrated that the tumour weight in the NPs/2178 group was significantly lower than that in the NPs/NC and control groups, while the tumour weight in the NPs/NC group was also significantly lower than that in the control group (Fig. 6B). Similarly, the tumour volume in the NPs/2178 group was significantly smaller than that in the NPs/NC and control groups, and the tumour volume in the NPs/NC group was significantly smaller than that in the control group (Fig. 6C). Subsequently, the tumour tissues were fixed in paraformaldehyde and subjected to immunohistochemical staining. The results revealed that the expression of tumour proliferation markers (e.g., Ki-67 and PCNA) was significantly reduced in the NPs/2178 group. In contrast, the expression of p53 was significantly greater in the NPs/2178 group than in the NPs/NC and control groups, and the expression of the antiapoptotic marker bcl-2 was significantly lower in the NPs/2178 group (Fig. 6D). These findings indicate that NPs/2178 effectively inhibits tumour growth in vivo and holds promise as a novel gene therapy target. The immunohistochemical results further confirmed that NPs/2178 suppressed lung adenocarcinoma progression by promoting apoptosis and inhibiting tumour proliferation.

Biocompatibility evaluation of NPs/2178 in vivo

Although LaN exhibits excellent biocompatibility in vitro, the safety of NPs/2178 for tumour therapy in vivo requires further validation. NPs/2178, NPs/NC and ddH2O were administered intravenously every two days. After seven cycles, major organs (e.g., lungs, liver, kidneys, and spleen) were collected for haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining to assess the in vivo toxicity of the NPs. Compared with those in the control group, no significant differences in tissue integrity or cellular morphology were observed in the experimental group (Fig. 7). These results demonstrate that NPs/2178 and NPs/NC are safe for tumour treatment in vivo.

Discussion

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide and poses a significant social burden. LUAD is the most common pathological subtype of lung cancer. Exploring novel pathogenic mechanisms and therapeutic targets is crucial for improving the survival of LUAD patients. Studies have shown that although lncRNAs do not encode proteins, they play critical roles in tumour initiation and progression, making them potential therapeutic targets for cancer treatment17. In this study, we performed differential expression analysis on gene expression data from LUAD patients. The results revealed that LINC02178 is significantly overexpressed in LUAD and strongly correlated with poor prognosis, suggesting that LINC02178 may be a driver of malignant progression in LUAD. Given the lack of research on the functional role and mechanisms of LINC02178 in LUAD, we further investigated its contribution to the malignant progression of LUAD.

To further validate the expression of LINC02178 in LUAD, we detected its expression in 15 paired LUAD tumour tissues and adjacent nontumour tissues, as well as in LUAD cell lines and normal bronchial epithelial cells. The results demonstrated that LINC02178 is significantly overexpressed in LUAD tissues and cell lines. Furthermore, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis and univariate Cox regression both demonstrated that LINC02178 was positively associated with poor prognosis and serves as a prognostic risk factor. However, multivariate Cox regression indicated that LINC02178 could not function as an independent prognostic biomarker. This non-significance may reflect limitations in sample size and source, or it may work in synergy with other molecules, which requires verification in larger cohorts. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that LINC02178 is closely associated with apoptosis and necroptosis. Previous studies have reported that LINC02178 is one of the prognostic signatures constructed by apoptosis-related lncRNAs in bladder cancer, which is consistent with the results of our bioinformatics analysis37. In addition, we found that LINC02178 is closely associated with the MAPK signalling pathway, Ras signalling pathway, and Wnt signalling pathway, all of which are highly related to tumour progression. These findings further suggest that LINC02178 may play a role in promoting the malignant progression of LUAD.

The subcellular localization of lncRNAs plays a critical role in determining their gene regulatory functions and mechanisms of action31. The subcellular localization of LINC02178 in A549, H1975, and PC-9 cells was analysed using RNA isolation and RT‒qPCR. The results demonstrated that LINC02178 is predominantly localized in the cytoplasm. To investigate the functional role of LINC02178, we constructed siRNAs to knockdown its expression. Using Lipofectamine 2000 as a positive control, we confirmed the transfection and knockdown efficiency of LINC02178 siRNA delivered by lanthanide-based materials. On the basis of the enrichment analysis results of LINC02178, we assessed the proliferation and apoptosis of LUAD cells following LINC02178 knockdown. The results demonstrated that knockdown of LINC02178 expression significantly inhibited the proliferation of LUAD cells and promoted their apoptosis, which is consistent with the results of the bioinformatics analysis. These results suggest that LINC02178 may promote the malignant progression of LUAD by enhancing cell proliferation and suppressing apoptosis.

Gene therapy has emerged as a prominent focus in tumour therapy in recent years. This approach is used to treat diseases by targeting or utilizing RNA molecules. LncRNAs are among the most promising therapeutic targets38. We confirmed that LINC02178 promotes the malignant progression of LUAD and holds significant potential as a therapeutic target for LUAD. Given that naked siRNA is highly susceptible to degradation in vivo, we combined the lanthanide-based material LaN with LINC02178 siRNA to ensure its non-degradation and integrity.

However, the potential toxicity of LaN in vivo need careful consideration. Variations in local microenvironments throughout the body may also influence material stability, further complicating their biomedical applicability39. Studies indicate that while these LaN materials can elevate mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm), they concurrently reduce intracellular ATP levels and exhibit genotoxic effects40. Additionally, lanthanide compounds may interfere with the transport pathways of essential biological ions41. Therefore, it is of vital importance to assess the biological safety of NPs/2178 as accurately as possible.

Rare earth nanomaterials have been found to exhibit selective cytotoxicity toward tumor cells while sparing normal cells. This selectivity can be attributed to a phenomenon known as the “Warburg effect”42. Tumor cells display high rates of glucose uptake and utilization, leading to increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) as a byproduct of cellular respiration. Consequently, oxidative stress increases, which can damage cellular components such as DNA, proteins, and lipids. Rare earth nanomaterials are known to exploit this vulnerability by selectively inducing high levels of oxidative stress in tumor cells. The elevated ROS levels generated by these nanomaterials are toxic to tumor cells and ultimately lead to cell death43,44. In contrast, non-tumor cells exhibit lower rates of glucose uptake and utilization and thus produce lower baseline levels of ROS. As a result, rare earth nanomaterials do not induce similarly high levels of oxidative stress in non-tumor cells, allowing them to survive.

As expected, we observed that the toxicity of LaN towards PC-9 cells was greater than that towards mouse fibroblast L929 cells in vitro (Fig. 3A–D), which may be attributed to the “Warburg effect”. Subsequently, we validated the transfection and knockdown efficiency of NPs/2178 in vitro (Fig. 3D and E). Furthermore, we established a xenograft tumour model in nude mice to evaluate the antitumour efficacy of NPs/2178 in vivo. As shown in Fig. 7, NPs/2178 significantly inhibited tumour growth. The expression of tumour proliferation markers markedly decreased and that of apoptosis markers significantly increased in the NPs/2178 group, which is consistent with the in vitro findings. The biological safety of NPs/2178 was further validated in vivo. H&E staining of major organs from nude mice revealed no significant pathological damage in any treatment group, further confirming the biological safety of NPs/2178.

Our study has several limitations. First, we validated the expression of LINC02178 in 15 paired LUAD tumour tissues and adjacent nontumour tissues. In future studies, we plan to increase the sample size and collect additional clinical data to further analyse the correlation between LINC02178 expression and clinical factors. Second, we primarily used the PC-9 cell line to investigate the biological functions and therapeutic potential of LINC02178. Subsequent experiments should include additional LUAD cell lines to further validate the functional role and therapeutic potential of LINC02178. Finally, functional enrichment analysis revealed that LINC02178 is strongly associated with apoptosis and the p53 signalling pathway. Given that p53 is a key regulator of apoptosis, future studies should explore whether LINC02178 promotes LUAD apoptosis through the p53 signalling pathway and elucidate the detailed mechanisms by which LINC02178 drives the malignant progression of LUAD. These investigations will be the primary focus of our follow-up research.

Conclusion

Our study revealed that LINC02178 is significantly overexpressed in LUAD and is associated with poor prognosis. LINC02178 significantly promotes LUAD cell proliferation and inhabits apoptosis. When combined with a lanthanide-based material, LINC02178 can inhabit tumour growth in vivo. In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that LINC02178 promotes LUAD malignant progression, and could serve as a promising therapeutic target.
