Bacterial and fungal keratitis in Northeast Mexico: demographic, predisposing, and microbiological differences among non-contact lens, optical contact lens, and bandage contact lens wearers
Authors: Karim Mohamed-Noriega, Karen Margarita Saldaña-Rodríguez, Sergio Ayala-De la Cruz, Fernando Morales-Wong, Alberto J Cavazos-Támez, Edgar E Martínez-Morales, Gerardo González-Morones, Gerardo Villareal-Méndez, Jorge Martín Llaca-Díaz, Jesús Mohamed-Hamsho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeContact lens wear is a major risk factor for infectious keratitis, this study compared demographics, predisposing factors, and microorganisms in infectious keratitis between non-contact lens (NCL), optical contact lens (OCL) and bandage contact lens (BCL) wearers.MethodsThis was a cross-sectional observational study based on retrospectively collected data from a consecutive series of patients with bacterial or fungal keratitis conducted between 2009 and 2020. Data collected included demographics, clinical characteristics, predisposing factors, and associated microorganisms.Results536 infectious keratitis were analyzed (NCL: 362, 67.5%; CL: 174, 32.5%). Of the CL-group, 31 (17.8%) used BCL and 143 (82.2%) OCL. NCL-group was older than CL-group (47 vs. 25 years; p < 0.001). Females were more common in CL than NCL-wearers (119/174, 68.4% vs. 143/342, 39.5%; p < 0.001). NCL-group, as compared to CL-wearers, showed more vegetal (34/362, 9.4% vs. 2/174, 1.1%; p < 0.001) and non-vegetal (58/362, 16.0% vs. 6/174, 3.4%; p < 0.001) trauma. The positive culture rate was 56.7% (304/536). NCL-group, as compared to CL-wearers, had more gram-positive (110/362, 54.5% vs. 27/174, 26.5%; p < 0.001), more fungal (50/362, 24.8% vs. 10/174, 9.8%; p = 0.01), and fewer gram-negative (64/362, 31.6% vs. 73/174, 71.6%; p < 0.001). BCL, as compared to OCL, showed more gram-positive (10/31, 55.6% vs. 17/143, 20.2%; p = 0.01) and fewer gram-negative (10/31, 55.6%vs. 63/143, 75.0%; p = 0.01) bacteria. Pseudomonas aeruginosa was the primary bacteria, and Fusarium spp. was the primary fungus. P. aeruginosa was more common in CL-wearers than NCL-group (67/174, 65.7% vs. 46/362, 22.8%; p = 0.02). Gram-negative was associated with OCL use (OR 1.96, CI 1.06–3.64; p = 0.03) and hypopyon (OR 3.39, CI 2.07–5.56; p < 0.001). Vegetal trauma was associated with fungal keratitis (OR 3.11, CI 1.20–8.05; p = 0.02).ConclusionsCL use and trauma were key predisposing factors for infectious keratitis. P. aeruginosa and Fusarium spp. predominated, with higher P. aeruginosa rates in CL-wearers, fungal infections in NCL-wearers, and more gram-negative isolates in OCL- than in BCL-wearers. These findings provide insight into microbiological trends in Mexico and support targeted preventive strategies.Keywords: Keratitis, Contact Lenses, Bacteria, Fungi, Bandage contact lens, Contact-lens adverse effects

Introduction

Infectious keratitis is a major cause of corneal visual impairment and blindness [1–3]. Contact lens (CL) wear is a major risk factor, followed by ocular trauma, ocular and systemic diseases and indiscriminate use of topical corticosteroids [4–7]. It can be caused by bacteria, fungi, parasites, or viruses [3]. Bacterial and fungal keratitis are growing problems; their prevalence, geographic distribution, etiological microorganisms, and risk factors vary by country and geographic region [2]. The environmental, cultural, medical history and urbanization differences in the country lead us to expect that there could be differences in the pattern of microorganisms that cause bacterial and fungal keratitis in different regions [8]

Infectious keratitis related to CL-wear is a rare but severe adverse event that affects approximately 3.4 in every 10,000 individuals who use CL [4, 6, 9]. There are many types of CL; the most widely used are soft CL, but rigid and scleral lenses are also available [10]. Based on its use, it can be divided into refractive/optical (OCL), cosmetic, and bandage contact lens (BCL) [10]. The BCL can be further categorized into BCL and drug delivery contact lenses. There are epidemiologic studies that demonstrate an increased risk of infectious keratitis in daily-wear soft CL when compared to daily-wear rigid CL [11]. Differences in predisposing risk factors for infectious keratitis between BCL and OCL-wearers can influence the distribution of causative microorganisms [12, 13]. In BCL-wearers, the natural barrier against infections, the corneal epithelium and ocular surface, are compromised either with an epithelial defect, corneal bullae, or generalized erosions, which is expected to predispose to infections caused by gram-positive bacteria from the natural ocular surface flora [12]. Furthermore, the long-term use of prophylactic antibiotics carries a risk of infections with resistant and uncommon microorganisms [13]. Among the main risk factors in CL-wearers are nighttime use, poor case hygiene, smoking, reusable CL, swimming with CL, reactions to CL solution and BCL-wear [6, 12]. In contrast, OCL-wearers are expected to have a healthy ocular surface; however, patterns of chronic CL-wear contribute to an increased risk of colonization with bacteria capable of infecting corneas with an intact ocular surface and epithelial barrier [1, 2, 12].

Overall, the main causative microorganisms of infectious keratitis are, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Staphylococcus epidermidis and Staphylococcus aureus [14–16]. Fungal microorganisms are important etiologic agents of infectious keratitis, with a global incidence of 1 million cases per year, and filamentous fungi, particularly Fusarium and Aspergillus species are the most frequently isolated pathogens in fungal keratitis [17]. Soft CL-wearers are associated with a higher likelihood of developing infectious keratitis caused by the gram-negative bacteria P. aeruginosa, fungi, or the protozoan Acanthamoeba spp.[1, 13, 18, 19]. In NCL keratitis, S. aureus, S. epidermidis and P. aeruginosa are among the most frequently identified microorganisms [20]. Ocular surface diseases have been associated with bacterial keratitis caused by gram-positive bacteria from the ocular surface microbiota [21].

There are limited studies on the differences in demographics, microorganisms and predisposing factors between OCL, BCL, and NCL-wear infectious keratitis [4, 6]. There is limited information available in Mexico on the demographics, epidemiology, risk factors, and microorganisms that cause bacterial and fungal keratitis. Furthermore, the existing studies mostly focus on the population of central Mexico [22, 23]. Consequently, there is a need for a study aimed at characterizing bacterial and fungal keratitis in the northeastern Mexico population, as well as to compare the microbiological causative agents and associated factors associated with BCL, OCL, and NCL-related infectious keratitis.

Methods

This was a cross-sectional observational study based on retrospectively collected data from a consecutive series of patients with bacterial or fungal keratitis. The study was conducted between March 2009 and February 2021 at the Ophthalmology Department of the University Hospital and Faculty of Medicine of the Autonomous University of Nuevo León (UANL), a tertiary care university hospital in Monterrey, Mexico. The included patients were adults of both genders with a clinical diagnosis of microbial keratitis, suspected to be caused by bacteria or fungal keratitis, and who underwent a corneal smear culture. Exclusion criteria included patients under 18 years of age, herpetic keratitis, peripheral ulcerative keratitis, and aseptic/inflammatory keratitis, missing culture reports, missing clinical files, and culture-confirmed Acanthamoeba spp. keratitis; patients that with prior antibiotic treatment were not excluded. Patients were categorized into NCL, CL and CL group is divided in OCL, and BCL groups according to the relationship between infectious keratitis and CL-wear.

The study received institutional ethics committee approval (ID: OF16:00001) and was conducted in accordance with Good Clinical Practices and the Declaration of Helsinki. The ethics committee waived informed consent because the risk of the study was considered less than minimal, and the screening tests were a standard screening procedure. Demographic data, initial clinical presentation of the infectious keratitis, predisposing factors, and isolated causative microorganisms were obtained from the clinical record. As a standard procedure, patients with a clinical diagnosis of microbial keratitis have a general ophthalmologic evaluation which includes elaboration of medical history, physical examination, visual acuity assessment, slit-lamp examination, anterior and posterior segment examination and a corneal scrape culture in non-selective media. Initial best corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was recorded in Snellen scale and then was converted to Logarithm of the Minimum Angle of Resolution (LogMAR) scale for analysis. Presence of diabetes, systemic hypertension, and known ophthalmic and systemic diseases were recorded. Well-known predisposing factors for bacterial or fungal keratitis were documented [24, 25]. Including predisposing factors, such as CL-wear, type, and indication for use. When the patient was on BCL-wear, the predisposing factor of ocular surface diseases treated with the BCL was recorded, such as corneal epithelial defect, filamentary keratitis, recurrent corneal erosion, bullous keratopathy, or neurotrophic keratopathy. Recent ocular trauma a well-known predisposing factor was also recorded and was divided into vegetal trauma (involving plant or organic matter) and non-vegetal trauma. The initial clinical presentation of the infectious keratitis was recorded, including infiltrate diameter (larger diameter in millimeters), descemetocele, corneal perforation, and hypopyon. The area of corneal involvement was categorized as central, paracentral, peripheral, subtotal, and total, as follows. The cornea was divided into sections to categorize the affected zone. The central zone was defined as 0–2 mm from the center of the cornea in any direction. The paracentral zone was defined as 3–4 mm from the center to the periphery of the cornea. The peripheral zone was defined as 5–6 mm from the center to the periphery of the cornea. Subtotal involvement was defined as when the infiltrate occupied the central, paracentral, and peripheral zones without affecting the entire cornea. Total involvement was defined as compromise of the entire cornea in all directions, with no area of healthy cornea free from infiltrate.

Corneal scraping was performed at the slit lamp under topical anesthesia using a Kimura spatula. Prior to corneal scraping, when circumstances allowed it, antibiotic drops were discontinued for 12–24 h, but when that was not possible, patients were instructed to discontinue antibiotics for at least 6 h, and a supplemental liquid culture media of brain–heart infusion served as a diluent for any residual antibiotic that could affect bacterial growth. Corneal scrapings were inoculated onto culture media at the Ophthalmology Department and applied to a glass slide for gram staining. The culture media and glass slide were subsequently submitted to the central clinical laboratory for further processing, including gram staining, incubation, and identification of causative bacteria or fungi, following standard microbiological protocols for ocular infections [26, 27]. The culture media were incubated under the following conditions: blood agar (35 °C, 5% CO2 atmosphere), chocolate agar (35 °C, 5% CO2 atmosphere), azide blood agar (35 °C, aerobic atmosphere), and eosin methylene blue agar (35 °C, aerobic atmosphere) for 72 h, while Sabouraud dextrose agar (30 °C, aerobic atmosphere) was incubated for 21 days. Absence of growth during the incubation period was interpreted as a negative result. Until September 2019, bacterial identification was performed using phenotypic methods (including gram staining, selective media culture, and conventional biochemical assays). From October 2019 onward, identification was conducted using MALDI-ToF MS (Bruker Microflex LT, Biotyper 3.1). Fungal identification was based on macroscopic and microscopic morphological criteria. Immediately after corneal scraping, patients were started on empirical treatment consisting of topical antibiotics and/or antifungals, as determined necessary by one of the three attending professors and cornea specialists (KMN, FMW, GVM) who were accompanied by resident ophthalmologists in training under continuous supervision. The standard initial empirical antibiotic was moxifloxacin (Vigamoxi 0.5% AlconMR) eye drops every 1 h. The standard initial empirical antifungal treatment consisted of natamycin (Miconacina 5% Grin) eye drops administered every hour. In severe cases, and at the discretion of the attending professor, additional antimicrobial therapy was added, such as fortified antimicrobial eye drops. There was no standard use of topical steroids, nor systemic antimicrobials. Follow-up data were not collected for this study.

The post-hoc statistical power of the study to detect differences in infectious keratitis prevalence between NCL-group compared to the CL-group was calculated retrospectively based on the study findings using G*Power software (Statistics for Mac OS X, version 3.1.9.6). The observed prevalence of gram-positive culture was 30.3% (110/362) in NCL-group and 15.5% (27/174) in CL-wearers. Assuming a two-tailed alpha error of 0.05, the analysis revealed the study achieved a statistical power of 0.947. Similarly, the statistical power to detect differences in the prevalence of gram-positive infectious keratitis between BCL-wearers and the OCL-wearers was calculated using the same approach. The observed prevalence of gram-positive cultures was 32.3% (10/31) in the BCL-group and 11.9% (17/143) in OCL-wearers. Assuming a two-tailed alpha error of 0.05, the analysis indicated that the study achieved a statistical power of 0.704.

Data was collected in dedicated spreadsheets (Microsoft Office Excel 2021, USA), and statistical analysis was performed with R (version 4.3.1) and RStudio (version 2023.9.1.494) using the base, tidyverse, nnet and car packages. Descriptive (percentages, central tendency, and dispersion values) and inferential statistical methods were employed. Percentages of isolated microorganisms are relative to total of positive cultures. The total of isolated microorganisms is greater than the total number of cases because some cases were polymicrobial keratitis. Normal distribution was assessed with the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q-Q plot. The Mann–Whitney U test or Welch’s t-test was used to compare continuous variables between groups, according on the distribution. The Chi-square was used for categorical variables; however Fisher's exact test was employed when more than 20% of the expected frequencies were less than 5. To analyze the relationship between clinical variables and culture results, a multinomial logistic regression was performed with the culture result as the dependent variable (grouped into negative result, gram-positive bacteria, gram-negative bacteria, and fungi) and the variables of sex, age, diabetes mellitus, CL-wear, type of CL, vegetal trauma, non-vegetal trauma, hypopyon, descemetocele/corneal perforation, and ocular surface disease[24, 28, 29]. The negative culture group was selected as the reference category. This allows for the identification of clinical characteristics that significantly increase or decrease the odds of a specific etiology group compared to the baseline of unconfirmed etiology (negative culture group). Cases with multiple microorganisms detected were excluded from the multinomial logistic regression analysis. Potential multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using Generalized Variance Inflation Factors (GVIF), adopting a conservative threshold (< 2.5) to rule out significant correlations. Regarding missing data, the final dataset analyzed contained no missing values after applying the elimination criteria described in Fig. 1; therefore, no imputation methods were required. Statistical significance was considered when p ≤ 0.05.

Results

A total of 636 patients with infectious keratitis were identified, 33 were eliminated because keratitis culture was not performed, 63 because the culture result report was missed or not available on the medical or laboratory records, 3 because acanthamoeba keratitis was culture-confirmed, and 1 because the medical record was incomplete with no information regarding microbial keratitis. Finally, a total of 536 patients with infectious keratitis (bacterial or fungal keratitis) were analyzed (Fig. 1); 67.5% (362/536) were NCL-wearers, and 32.5% (174/536) were CL-wearers. Of these, 82.2% (143/174) were OCL-wearers and 17.8% (31/174) were BCL-wearers. All OCL-wearers were using soft CL, no cases in this cohort used rigid or scleral CL. The most frequent indication for BCL-wear was corneal epithelial defect (16/31, 51.6%), followed by bullous keratopathy (10/31, 32.3%). (Table 1).

There were significant demographic differences among the study groups (Table 1). Male sex was more prevalent in patients with NCL-wear keratitis than those with CL-wear keratitis (219/362, 60.5% vs. 55/174, 31.6%, p < 0.001). Patients with CL-wear keratitis were significantly younger than those with NCL-wear keratitis (Median [IQR] age: 25 [18–36] vs. 47 [32–65] years, p < 0.001). Similarly, OCL-wearers were younger than BCL-wearers (Median [IQR] age: 24 [18–33] vs. 34 [27–69] years, p < 0.001). Diabetes appeared to be more prevalent in BCL (5/31, 16.1%) than OCL-wearers (7/143, 4.9%, p = 0.04). However, when adjusting the prevalence of diabetes for age, it becomes non-significant (p = 0.28, Cochran-Mantel–Haenszel test). As expected, BCL-wearers showed significantly higher proportions of ocular surface diseases than OCL-wearers: Corneal epithelial defect (16/31, 51.6% vs. 0%; p < 0.001). bullous keratopathy (10/31, 32.3% vs. 0%; p < 0.001). A lower median [IQR] initial BCVA was observed in patients with NCL-wear keratitis compared to those with CL-wear keratitis. (2 [0.6–3] vs. 0.9 [0.4–1.5], p < 0.001), but there was no significant difference between BCL and OCL-wearers. (Table 1). The initial clinical presentation of the infectious keratitis showed that the median [IQR] infiltrate diameter was significantly larger in patients with NCL-wear keratitis than in patients with CL-wear keratitis (4 [3–6] vs. 4 [2–5], p = 0.02), but with no significant difference between BCL and OCL-wearers (p = 0.06) (Table 2). The presence of corneal perforation, descemetocele, hypopyon, and the zone of corneal involvement were not significantly different between study groups (p > 0.05) (Table 2).

In relation to possible predisposing factors for infectious keratitis, the presence of a CL was identified in 32.4% (174/536), thereby representing the most frequently identifiable predisposing factor. OCL was observed in 27.2% (143/536), representing the single most common associated factor. The second most frequent factor was recent ocular trauma identified in 18.7% (100/536) of patients, between them 11.9% (64/536) had non-vegetal trauma, and 6.7% (36/536) had vegetal trauma. Other frequently observed conditions were ocular surface disease (OSD) in 16.6% (84/536), diabetes in 14.4% (77/536), and BCL in 5.7% (31/536) (Table 1). As expected, some patients had more than one predisposing factor, as these were not mutually exclusive.

A detailed microbiological description is shown in Table 3. Positive cultures were observed in 56.7% (304/536) of patients, with no significant differences between NCL and CL-groups (p = 0.60), nor between OCL and BCL-groups (p > 0.99), (Table 3). Polymicrobial keratitis was observed in 5.0% of cases (27/536), and no differences were observed between study groups (p > 0.05). Among these, 4.3% showed growth of 2 microorganisms and 0.7% (4/536) showed growth of 3 microorganisms, respectively. Among these 3.9% (21/536) were bacteria-bacteria, 0.7% (4/536) were bacterial-fungi, 0.4% (2/536) were fungal-fungi.

In general, considering all cases together, an equal proportion of infections with gram-positive (137/304, 45.1%) and gram-negative (137/304, 45.1%) bacteria were observed, followed by fungal keratitis that accounted for 60 (60/304, 19.7%) cases. In general, the most frequently isolated microorganism was P. aeruginosa (113/304, 37.2%), followed by S. aureus (47/304, 15.5%) and coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) (41/304, 13.5%) (Table 3). The most frequently isolated fungi were Fusarium spp. (27/304, 8.9%), followed by Aspergillus spp. (11/304, 3.6%) and unspecified filamentous fungi (11/304, 3.6%). Notably, in our population, most fungal cases were filamentous mycotic keratitis, with only one case of Candida spp. (0.3%) yeast infection (Fig. 2).

Significant differences in the distribution of microorganisms were observed between NCL and CL-wearers (Table 3); NCL-wearers had significantly more gram-positive (54.5%, 110/362), S. pneumoniae (9.4%, 19/362), Streptococcus spp. (3.3%, 10/362), and mycotic infections (24.8%, 50/362; p < 0.05). On the contrary, CL-wear keratitis showed significantly more frequent infections with gram-negative bacteria (71.6%, 73/174) and P. aeruginosa (65.7%, 67/174; p < 0.05) (Table 3). Notably, significant differences were observed between BCL and OCL infectious keratitis. The BCL-wearers showed a higher proportion of gram-positive bacteria (55.6%, 10/31) and Corynebacterium spp. (11.1%, 2/31; p < 0.05), which are very similar to those observed in the NCL-wearers. On the contrary the OCL-wearers had significantly more infections with gram-negative bacteria (75.0%, 63/143; p = 0.01), and a non-statistically significant trend towards more P. aeruginosa and fungal infections, which are comparable to the CL-wearers (Table 3).

In the multinomial logistic regression analysis (using negative culture as the reference category), distinct clinical associations were observed. Multicollinearity assessment confirmed the absence of significant correlations among independent variables (all GVIF values < 1.88). The odds of a gram-negative culture result were higher in OCL wearers (OR 1.96, p = 0.03) and in patients presenting with hypopyon (OR 3.39, p < 0.001). OCL-wearers had lower odds of gram-positive culture results (OR 0.36, p = 0.01) and vegetal trauma was associated with higher odds of fungal keratitis (OR 3.11, p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Discussion

There is limited information in Mexico about the demographics, epidemiology and microorganisms of infectious keratitis and it is mainly representing center of the country, furthermore, there have been no published reports in northeast Mexico [22, 23]. The current study analyses infectious keratitis in northeast Mexico and found that the most frequent predisposing factor was CL-wear, and the principal causative microorganism was P. aeruginosa in NCL and CL-wearers. Hernandez-Camarena et al. and Beltran et al. reported that Staphylococcus epidermidis and P. aeruginosa were the most prevalent bacteria in Mexico City [30, 31]. This agrees with the present study, were P. aeruginosa, S. aureus, and CoNS (mainly S. epidermidis) were the most frequent isolated bacteria. Hernandez-Camarena et al. also reported that the use of soft contact lenses was observed as the main associated factor which agrees with the present study [30]. Regarding fungal keratitis, the main associated factor was ocular trauma, while the most common associated fungi was Fusarium spp. This aligns with Perez-Balbuena et al. and Vanzzini-Zago et al. reported in a Mexican cohort of fungal keratitis that Fusarium spp. was the most frequent etiologic fungal agent, and surgical trauma and ocular trauma with organic matter was the main associated factor [22, 32].

The present study identified that CL-wear was the most frequent associated factor, on the contrary, in NCL-wearers, ocular trauma was the principal associated factor, regardless of whether it involved organic material. These findings are consistent with previous studies. Ting et.al. in a systematic review, described CL-wear as a widely documented predisposing factor, especially in high-income countries and frequently associated with P. aeruginosa [3]. CL-wearers were younger, had more females and a higher frequency of gram-negatives. These findings align with Green et.al. which documented a significant age difference between CL and NCL-wearers, CL-wearers were younger. Moon et al. studied infectious keratitis related to CL-wear and similar results related with age and sex were reported [24, 33]. The finding of contact lens wear as main risk factor aligns with the studies cited earlier in this paragraph and with the study by Hernández-Camarena conducted in Mexico. Highlighting that social and demographic characteristics in Mexico make contact lens wear a risk factor. Therefore, it is important to establish educational strategies for the use of contact lenses for the prevention of infectious keratitis.

Regarding the etiologic microorganism of the infectious keratitis, in the present study P. aeruginosa was the most frequently isolated microorganism (37.1%), followed by S. aureus (15.4%) and CoNS (13.4%), this aligns Wang et al. that documented that P. aeruginosa (29.6%) was the principal microorganism followed by Staphylococcus spp. (25.5%) [34]. P. aeruginosa was also the most frequent isolated microorganism in CL-wearers, corresponding to 65.7% (67/102) of culture-positive cases, which was significantly higher than the 22.8% (46/202) of P. aeruginosa observed in NCL-wearers. This aligns with previous studies reporting P. aeruginosa as main causative agent in CL-wearers and with a higher frequency than NCL-wearers, such as Ting et al., Green et al. and Hilliam et al. [3, 19, 24]. Given the high prevalence of P. aeruginosa and the global challenge of antimicrobial resistance, novel therapeutic strategies, such as bacteriophage therapy, are currently being explored to address these difficult scenarios [35]. In the current study higher proportion of gram-positives was found in NCL-wearers (54.4%) than in CL-wearers (26.4%), in culture-positive cases. This agrees with Green et al. that reported 70% of gram-positives in NCL-wearers versus 44% in CL-wearers. In addition, both studies found that S. pneumoniae was observed more frequently in NCL-wearers than in CL-wearers [36]. There is controversy regarding the first-line antibiotic to use. The American Academy of Ophthalmology (AAO) bacterial keratitis preferred practice pattern (PPP) 2024 report recommends using either fluoroquinolones or fortified antibiotics as first-line treatment options [37]. Several comparative studies have reported similar efficacy and safety profile of moxifloxacin eyedrops as compared with fortified antibiotics such as tobramycin and cefazolin [38]. A recent cross-sectional study of the US Collaborative Network on TriNetX that included 5916 bacterial keratitis cases found that the majority of respondents preferred fluoroquinolones (73%), with moxifloxacin (54.1%) the most frequent first-line option [39]. This aligns with the present study empirical antibiotic choice, where the first-line antibiotic for bacterial keratitis was moxifloxacin eyedrops in the vast majority of cases.

Regarding fungal keratitis, in the present work, Fusarium spp was the most frequently isolated fungal pathogen, followed by Aspergillus and ocular trauma with vegetal and non-vegetal matters, and NCL-wear was significantly associated with fungal keratitis. This aligns with Green et al. that reported more fungal keratitis in NCL-wearers (9%) than in CL-wearers (2.4%) [36]. Furthermore, vegetal trauma increased the odds of having fungal keratitis (OR 3.11). This is in agreement with Sharma et.al. documented in a review, that Fusarium spp and Aspergillus spp were the most frequently isolated fungus and history of recent trauma is a significant predisposing factor [40]. Also, Soleimani et.al. in a study of fungal keratitis in Iran, documented Fusarium spp as the main etiologic microorganism, followed by Aspergillus spp., which aligns with the findings of the current study [41].

Differences in infectious keratitis according to the CL type is scarce. In the present study, OCL and BCL were analyzed separately and found that BCL-wearers were older women (average age of 47 ± 13 years) compared to OCL-wearers was younger women (average age was 28 ± 14 years old). P. aeruginosa was the main microorganism isolated in BCL-wearers (50.0%) and OCL-wearers (69.0%), in contrast to other studies such as Zhu et al. in a retrospective study that included 6,188 infectious keratitis, 8 of them associated to BCL-wear and reported that the most frequently isolated microorganism was Fusarium spp. (25%) and S. pneumoniae (25%) and no cases by P. aeruginosa (0%) [12]. Tzamalis et al. studied infectious keratitis after Crosslinking (CxL) and BCL-wear, reported more cases caused by S. aureus (88%) and no cases by P. aeruginosa (0%) [42]. The present study documented OCL-wearers are associated with gram-negative bacteria (OR 1.96) and OCL-wearers have a negative association with gram-positives (OR 0.36).

Diabetes mellitus is a highly prevalent comorbidity in Mexico, as noted by the 2022 Mexican National Health and Nutrition Survey (ENSANUT) the prevalence of Diabetes is 18.3% [43]. Considering all infectious keratitis in the present study, 14.4% had diabetes, 18.0% (65/362) of them were NCL-wearers and 6.9% (12/174) CL-wearers although NCL-wearers was older. Diabetes appeared to be a predisposing factor for keratitis; however, after adjusting for age, diabetes was no longer identified as an associated factor. There are conflicting reports in the literature regarding diabetes as a risk factor. Arunga et al. reported a prevalence of diabetes of 7% in infectious keratitis cases versus 1.4% between controls and documented that having diabetes is associated with an OR of 9.38 for developing infectious keratitis [44]. Chidambaram et al. documented that the prevalence of diabetes was 8% in fungal keratitis and 0% in bacterial keratitis [45]. Lin et al. in a retrospective 10-year study in Taiwan, reported a prevalence of diabetes mellitus of 2.8% in patients with infectious keratitis [46].

Hypopyon is an inflammatory manifestation of infectious keratitis, that can be associated with the severity of the disease, although it is not pathognomonic of a particular etiologic agent. The present study, documented that hypopyon is associated with gram-negatives (OR 3.39). Xu et al. documented in a study in China, that Fusarium and Aspergillus were risk factors for hypopyon [47]. Zeng et al. compared patients with bacterial keratitis with hypopyon and bacterial keratitis with endophthalmitis, and documented similar prevalence of S. epidermidis, Streptococci, S. aureus and P. aeruginosa between these groups [48].

Clinical features of bacterial and fungal keratitis are important to assess disease severity although insufficient to distinguish between both, therefore it is standard practice to take a corneal scraping to look for the microorganism. Even though the culture growth rate varies between 50–70% across studies of infectious keratitis. The positive-culture rate obtained in the present study was 56.7% and was comparable to previous studies such as Zhu et al. that documented a 63% of positive-culture rate and Suresh et al. obtained a 46% of positive-culture rate in CL-wearers, a study carried out in Taiwan by Wang et al. reported a positive-culture rate of 45.2% [12, 29, 34]. However, other studies reported higher positive culture rates, such as Tzamalis et al. and Moramarco et al., who reported 88% and 75% of positive cultures, respectively [42, 49]. There are multiple reasons for the differences in positive culture rates between studies, including standardized procedures for processing cultures, techniques for analyzing microbial growth, the technique for performing corneal scraping, and the number of hours the patient is left without antibiotic drops before performing corneal scraping. In the present study, although most patients had no prior antibiotic use, for those who had been using antibiotics, we waited a minimum of 6 h, and when circumstances allowed, 12–24 h of waiting, with 24 h being the ideal recommended interval to increase the chance of growth in the culture. To compensate for this, in these cases we supplemented with the liquid heart-brain medium. Having said that, the present study achieved growth percentages comparable to previous studies, as already mentioned [50, 51].

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design, resulting in missing data for some patients, including incomplete follow-up information. Cosmetic contact lenses were not differentiated from optical contact lenses; therefore, a specific analysis of cosmetic contact lens wearers cannot be performed. In addition, cases of Acanthamoeba spp. keratitis were excluded. Cultures of contact lenses and contact lens cleaning solutions were not obtained, limiting the ability to identify potential sources of infection. Finally, in certain cases, the ideal antibiotic-free interval of 24 h prior to corneal scraping could not be achieved; however, all included patients had discontinued topical antibiotics for a minimum of 6 h, and an additional liquid media culture of brain–heart was used.

Strengths of this study include being the first large-scale study reporting bacterial and fungal keratitis in northeast Mexico. Cases were prospectively recorded over an extended period and then analyzed retrospectively using a standardized protocol for culture and microbial identification. The comparison between optical and bandage contact lens–associated keratitis further strengthens the study. There are limited studies evaluating differences between optical and bandage contact lenses related to infectious keratitis. This study adds valuable information regarding the differences in microbiologic agents associated with bandage contact lens–related infectious keratitis. Finally, this study analyzed the odds of developing gram-positive, gram-negative, or fungal infections based on demographic data.

In conclusion, the present study on infectious keratitis in northeast Mexico found that P. aeruginosa was the most frequently isolated microorganism overall, and the main fungi was Fusarium spp. OCL-wear was the main predisposing factor, and it was associated with a higher frequency of gram-negative bacteria, mainly P. aeruginosa, whereas BCL-wearers had similar frequencies of gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria. BCL-wearers had more often gram-positive bacterial keratitis than OCL wearers. NCL wearers had more often gram-positive bacterial, fungal microorganisms, and vegetal trauma than CL wearers. Vegetal trauma was associated with developing fungal keratitis. Future research should focus on antimicrobial resistance and keratitis prevention, aiming to improve clinical outcomes and prevent vision-threatening complications. Investigating antimicrobial resistance in keratitis will help tailor effective antibiotic therapy. Developing strategies, such as education, to prevent ocular trauma and CL-related infections will help reduce the incidence of infectious keratitis.
