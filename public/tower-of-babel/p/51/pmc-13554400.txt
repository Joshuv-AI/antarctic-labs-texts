Association of left ventricular global longitudinal strain with outcomes of severe mitral regurgitation due to severe mitral annular calcification: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mohammad Al Zein, Yuichiro Okushi, Shinya Unai, Gösta B. Pettersson, A. Marc Gillinov, Richard A. Grimm, Brian P. Griffin, Bo Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMitral annular calcification (MAC) is associated with increased mortality and a heightened risk of complications such as stroke, myocardial infarction, and arrhythmias. In advanced stages, MAC can cause severe mitral regurgitation (MR), further worsening outcomes. Prognostic markers to guide therapy in this population remain unclear. Left ventricular global longitudinal strain (LVGLS) has been linked to outcomes in MR, but its role in severe MR due to severe MAC is not well established. This study evaluated the prognostic value of LVGLS in this setting.MethodsWe conducted a single-center, retrospective study of patients with severe MAC identified from the Cleveland Clinic echocardiographic database between January 2010 and August 2023. From these, patients with mild or moderate MR and those with concomitant significant mitral stenosis (MS) were excluded. Subsequently, patients with isolated severe MR due to severe MAC were identified. Clinical and echocardiographic parameters, including LVGLS, were recorded at baseline. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. Patients were stratified by the median LVGLS value for analysis. Survival was assessed using Kaplan-Meier analysis and Cox proportional hazards models. Restricted cubic spline was used to evaluate potential non-linear associations between LVGLS and mortality.ResultsDuring the study period, 10,061 patients were diagnosed with severe MAC on echocardiography at our institution. Of these, 8,912 with mild or moderate MR and 1,021 with concomitant significant MS were excluded, and 128 patients with isolated severe MR due to severe MAC were included in our study. The median follow-up was 134 days [interquartile range (IQR), 33–1,812 days]. The median age was 81 years (IQR, 71–88 years), and 35 (27.3%) were male. The median left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was 63% (IQR, 56–69%), mean MR regurgitant volume was 60±20.8 mL, and median right ventricular systolic pressure was 54 mmHg (IQR, 42–71 mmHg). The median LVGLS was −14.8%±4.5%. Of the 128 patients, 45 (35.2%) underwent mitral valve intervention, 42 (93.3%) surgery and 3 (6.7%) mitral clip implantation, while 83 (64.8%) received conservative management. Among surgical cases, 37 underwent valve replacements (34 bioprosthetic) and 5 valve repairs. During follow-up, 63 patients (49.2%) died. LVGLS worse than −14.8% was associated with higher 12-month mortality (log-rank P=0.043) but not over the entire follow-up period (P=0.11). On multivariable cox regression, LVGLS was not a predictor of mortality after adjusting for other covariates [hazard ratio (HR) 1.046, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.983–1.112].ConclusionsIn severe MR due to MAC, impaired LVGLS was associated with increased short-term but not long-term mortality. The limited long-term prognostic value may reflect the low myocardial reserve in patients with severe valvular dysfunction due to MAC, the progressive and systemic nature of the disease, and the influence of non-cardiac comorbidities.

Introduction

Mitral annular calcification (MAC) is a chronic degenerative process characterized by calcium deposition within the fibrous support of the mitral valve (1). Calcification most commonly affects the posterior annulus but may extend to the anterior annulus in advanced cases. In more severe forms, MAC may involve extra-annular structures, including the fibrous trigones, left ventricular outflow tract, and even the adjacent ventricular myocardium (2). MAC is increasingly recognized as an active metabolic process involving complex interactions among inflammation, atherosclerosis, and abnormalities in calcium-phosphate metabolism (3). The prevalence of MAC varies across populations, ranging from approximately 5% to 15%, and increases with advancing age as well as in patients with chronic kidney disease, hypertension, and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (4-6).

Echocardiography remains the primary imaging modality for detecting and grading MAC, typically based on the circumferential extent of annular involvement (2). More recently, cardiac computed tomography has emerged as an important complementary modality for quantifying the extent and severity of MAC (7). Increasing MAC burden has been associated with worse clinical outcomes (8). In advanced stages, extensive annular calcification can impair mitral valve dynamics and lead to significant mitral valve dysfunction (9). In MAC-related mitral regurgitation (MR), disruption of the sphincter-like function of the mitral annulus during systole may result in incomplete leaflet coaptation. Less commonly, MAC may contribute to MR through elongation or rupture of the chordae tendineae secondary to leaflet restriction and increased traction on the subvalvular apparatus (10).

Prior studies have demonstrated that MAC-related MR is associated with adverse clinical outcomes (11,12). Despite this growing recognition, reliable markers for risk stratification in patients with severe MR due to severe MAC remain limited. Recently, our group demonstrated that left atrial strain and frailty are associated with outcomes in patients with severe MR due to severe MAC (13,14). Left ventricular global longitudinal strain (LVGLS) is a sensitive marker of subclinical left ventricular dysfunction and has demonstrated prognostic value in several valvular heart diseases (15). However, the prognostic significance of LVGLS in patients with severe MR due to severe MAC has not been well established. Therefore, the aim of the present study was to evaluate the prognostic value of LVGLS in patients with severe MR due to severe MAC. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://cdt.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/cdt-2025-1-620/rc).

Methods

Study population

The Cleveland Clinic echocardiography database was retrospectively queried to identify patients with severe MAC between January 2010 and August 2023. Patients with mild or moderate MR and those with concomitant significant mitral stenosis (MS) were excluded. The final cohort consisted of patients with isolated severe MR due to severe MAC. The date of the echocardiogram at which severe MAC was first diagnosed was defined as the index date. Clinical data at the initial encounter and subsequent outcomes were obtained from electronic health records. Decisions regarding MV intervention were made in accordance with contemporary expert recommendations for the management of severe MAC with significant MV disease (16,17). The decision-making process was made on a case-by-case basis by a multidisciplinary heart team, incorporating symptom burden, comorbidities, life expectancy, as well as anatomic feasibility and procedural risk. In general, MV intervention was pursued when a meaningful symptomatic or survival benefit was anticipated, whereas guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) was favored in asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic patients or in those deemed to have prohibitive procedural risk. For patients undergoing MV intervention, procedural characteristics were recorded, including surgical versus transcatheter approach, valve repair or replacement, and associated procedures such as coronary artery bypass grafting, aortic valve replacement, left atrial appendage ligation, or MAZE procedure. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality, while secondary outcomes included heart failure hospitalization (HFH) and stroke during follow-up. Follow-up data were obtained through review of electronic medical records, and patients were followed up until the occurrence of primary outcome or last clinical encounter. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the institutional review board of the Cleveland Clinic (IRB: 19-1055). Informed consent was waived in this retrospective study.

Conventional and speckle-tracking echocardiography

Transthoracic echocardiographic examinations were performed by experienced sonographers using Vivid E9 (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA), EPIQ 7C, or EPIQ CVx (Philips Medical Systems, Boston, MA, USA). All echocardiographic parameters were assessed according to current American College of Cardiology (ACC) and American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) guidelines (18,19). Significant MS was defined as a mitral valve area ≤1.5 cm2, a mean transmitral pressure gradient >10 mmHg, with an estimated peak pulmonary artery systolic pressure >50 mmHg, and evidence of left atrial enlargement (>34 mL/m2) (20). MR severity was assessed using both qualitative and quantitative parameters. The proximal isovelocity surface area (PISA) method was used to derive the effective regurgitant orifice area (EROA) and regurgitant volume (21). Severe MR was defined by an EROA ≥0.40 cm2, regurgitant volume ≥60 mL, regurgitant fraction ≥50%, vena contracta width ≥0.7 cm, and/or a central jet occupying >40% of the left atrial area. Severe MR was considered related to MAC when it occurred in the presence of severe MAC and demonstrated echocardiographic features of leaflet restriction related to annular calcification. Accordingly, all included patients exhibited Carpentier type III dysfunction, supporting MAC as the dominant mechanism of MR (21). Other MR etiologies, including prolapse or flail leaflet, endocarditis, rheumatic disease, ischemic MR, or functional MR, were excluded from our cohort.

MAC was evaluated using parasternal short-axis and apical views. MAC severity was graded according to the multiparametric classification proposed by Xu et al., which is based on the circumferential extent of calcification involving the mitral annulus (2). Severe MAC on echocardiography was defined as grades III or IV MAC, corresponding to dense echogenic thickening involving more than 50% of the mitral annular circumference, as determined by experienced imaging cardiologists. In patients who underwent mitral valve surgery, echocardiographic findings were confirmed intraoperatively by cardiac surgeons, with assessment focused on the extent and density of calcification and its proximity to the left circumflex artery.

LVGLS was measured offline using vendor-independent three-dimensional speckle-tracking software (4D LV-Analysis, version 3.1; TomTec Imaging Systems, Unterschleissheim, Germany). LV endocardial borders were automatically detected and manually adjusted when necessary in apical two-, three-, and four-chamber views. LVGLS was calculated as the average peak systolic longitudinal strain from all segments across the apical views and expressed as a negative value, with more negative values indicating better systolic function.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when normally distributed and as median with interquartile range (IQR) when non-normally distributed. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was applied to evaluate normality, with P<0.005 indicating departure from a normal distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages. Comparisons of baseline parameters between LVGLS subgroups were performed using the Student’s t-test or the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables, and the Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Survival analyses for the all-cause mortality were conducted using Kaplan-Meier curves, and differences between groups were examined with the log-rank test. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to examine associations of baseline characteristics with survival. Clinical and echocardiographic covariates were selected based on clinical relevance. LVGLS was used as a continuous variable in Cox analyses, and hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. To further evaluate the relationship between LVGLS and mortality risk, restricted cubic spline modeling was performed after adjustment for age and mitral valve intervention. Knot placement followed Harrell’s recommendations, and the number of knots (three, located at the 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles) was selected using Akaike information criterion after testing models with three to seven knots. Patients with missing LVGLS measurements were excluded from the analyses. Since the missing data were due to software availability rather than clinical factors, the risk of bias related to missing data is expected to be minimal. All tests were two-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Measurement reproducibility of LVGLS was evaluated in a randomly selected subset of 20 patients. Two independent investigators, blinded to all clinical information, performed strain measurements. Each observer repeated the analysis while remaining blinded to their prior measurements and to the measurements obtained by the other investigator. Interobserver and intraobserver variability were quantified using the standard error of measurement (SEM), denoted as SEMinter and SEMintra, respectively. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 3.6.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Between January 2010 and August 2023, 10,061 patients were diagnosed with severe MAC on transthoracic echocardiography at our center. Patients with mild or moderate MR, concurrent moderate or severe MS, or inadequate image quality for LVGLS assessment were excluded. After exclusion, 128 patients with isolated severe MR due to severe MAC were identified (Figure 1). The cohort was stratified using the median LVGLS value (−14.8%) to allow comparison between two similarly sized groups. Although the restricted cubic spline curve crossed an HR of 1 at −10.6%, only 24 patients had LVGLS values worse than this threshold, limiting statistical power for analyses using this cutoff. During a median follow-up of 134 days (IQR, 33–1,812 days), 63 patients (49.2%) died. Table 1 shows the baseline clinical characteristics of the entire cohort and subgroups stratified by median LVGLS. The median age was 81 years (IQR, 71–88 years), and 72.7% were female. A total of 45 patients (35.2%) underwent mitral valve intervention, while 83 (64.8%) received conservative management. Surgical intervention was performed in 42 patients, including 37 mitral valve replacements and 5 surgical repairs, whereas 3 patients underwent percutaneous mitral valve intervention (MitraClip). Concomitant procedures included coronary artery bypass grafting in 14 patients, aortic valve replacement in 22, MAZE procedure in 12, and left atrial appendage closure in 8. Among those who underwent intervention, 12 of 45 patients (26.7%) died during follow-up, with a median time to death of 553 days (IQR, 103–1,969 days). Patients with impaired LVGLS (worse than −14.8%) had similar age, sex distribution, and comorbidity burden compared with those with LVGLS equal to or better than −14.8%.

Inter-observer and intra-observer variability

LVGLS was analyzed in 20 randomly selected subjects by two independent observers. The interobserver intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was 0.969 (95% CI: 0.923–0.988) with an SEMinter of 4.84%. The intraobserver ICC was 0.988 (95% CI: 0.969–0.995) with an SEMintra of 3.06%. These high ICC values and low SEM values indicate excellent measurement reproducibility.

Survival outcomes based on LVGLS

To determine the prognostic value of LVGLS, the cohort was stratified by the median LVGLS value (−14.8%) (Figure 2). Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated that LVGLS was not significantly associated with overall mortality in the entire follow-up period (log-rank P=0.11). During the first 12 months of follow-up, patients with LVGLS values worse than the median experienced significantly higher mortality compared to patients with better LVGLS values (log-rank P=0.04).

Cox regression analysis

Results of the univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses are presented in Table 3. In univariable analysis, older age (HR 1.038, 95% CI: 1.012–1.065, P=0.004), higher Charlson Comorbidity Index (HR 1.216, 95% CI: 1.061–1.395, P=0.005), and worse LVGLS (HR 1.071, 95% CI: 1.004–1.142, P=0.04), were each associated with reduced survival, whereas MV intervention was significantly associated with improved outcomes (HR 0.128, 95% CI: 0.058–0.281, P<0.001). In the multivariable Cox regression model, LVGLS was not independently associated with survival (HR 1.046, 95% CI: 0.983–1.112). MV intervention remained a strong predictor of improved survival (HR 0.164, 95% CI: 0.073–0.370). When an LVGLS threshold of −10.6% was applied in Cox regression analyses, it was associated with all-cause mortality in univariable analysis (HR 2.121, 95% CI: 1.132–3.974). However, this association was no longer statistically significant after multivariable adjustment for age, sex, Charlson comorbidity index, and MV intervention (HR 1.664, 95% CI: 0.866–3.210, Table S1).

Discussion

Prognostic value of LVGLS in severe MR

Previous studies have been reported the prognostic value of LVGLS in patients with MR. In a study of 593 patients with severe primary MR undergoing MV surgery, LVGLS worse than −20.6% was significantly associated with increased all-cause mortality over a median follow-up of 6.4 years (P<0.001) (22). Similarly, in a cohort of 750 patients with MR and preserved EF undergoing MV surgery, LVGLS worse than −14% was associated with a higher risk of cardiovascular mortality or HFH (P<0.001) (23). Notably, the event rates in these studies were substantially lower compared to our cohort, likely reflecting patients with better functional status and fewer co-morbidities compared to patients with severe MR due to severe MAC. Furthermore, all patients in these cohorts underwent surgical intervention, which may have contributed to the reported long-term prognostic value of LVGLS. In patients undergoing surgical MV repair, impaired LVGLS may reflect reduced myocardial reserve or a limited capacity for reverse remodeling, thereby contributing to adverse outcomes.

In our cohort, LVGLS may be associated with all-cause mortality only during the first year of follow-up. This suggests that while LVGLS may serve as a useful short-term prognostic marker, its intermediate-term predictive value appears limited in patients with severe MR due to severe MAC. Several factors may contribute to this observation. First, MAC is a progressive and systemic disease, often associated with advanced age, frailty, and multiple comorbidities (24). Furthermore, increasing MAC severity is independently associated with worse outcomes. In 331 patients with MAC who underwent MV surgery, those with severe MAC on computed tomography had significantly higher mortality rate compared to those with non-severe MAC (P=0.026) (8). Similarly, in patients with aortic stenosis and subsequent aortic valve replacement, severe MAC was an independent predictor of mortality (P<0.05) (25). As such, non-cardiac factors and extra-valvular pathologies likely play a major role in mortality in this population, potentially attenuating the prognostic influence of LV-specific parameters like LVGLS over time. Second, patients with severe MR due to MAC often present at an advanced stage of disease, with pre-existing myocardial remodeling and impaired contractile reserve. This chronic myocardial dysfunction may reduce the potential for reverse remodeling following intervention, unlike in populations with isolated valvular disease. Finally, surgical intervention in this population is highly complex and typically reserved for carefully selected individuals. It frequently involves mitral valve replacement rather than repair, and outcomes may be influenced more by surgical candidacy and perioperative risk than by intrinsic myocardial function.

Interestingly, a similar temporal pattern was observed in a randomized study of 565 patients with moderate-to-severe or severe secondary MR and symptomatic heart failure receiving either GDMT or GDMT plus transcatheter MV repair (26). In that study, LVGLS was not predictive of outcomes at two years but was significantly associated with the composite endpoint of death and HFH during the intermediate follow-up period (10 to 24 months) (26).

Clinical implications

MAC is a progressive systemic entity independently associated with increased mortality, and the presence of concomitant mitral valve dysfunction further worsens outcomes (1,27). The associated systemic, inflammatory, metabolic, and frailty factors make the management of MR due to MAC in this population distinct from isolated primary MR. Although the hemodynamic burden of MR is similar to that observed in other etiologies of chronic MR, the broader disease context substantially influences prognosis and therapeutic decision-making.

Our study demonstrates that LVGLS may be associated with outcomes during the first year of follow-up, consistent with prior observations in severe MR populations. However, the absence of a sustained association over longer follow-up underscores the dominant role of systemic disease burden and competing risks in determining outcomes in patients with severe MR due to MAC. These findings suggest that LVGLS should be interpreted as part of a broader, integrated risk assessment rather than as a standalone prognostic marker in this high-risk population.

Our results also extend the findings of our previous work in this population. In prior studies from our group, higher frailty index was independently associated with worse outcomes, whereas mitral valve intervention was associated with improved survival irrespective of frailty status (13). Additionally, left atrial reservoir strain was shown to predict adverse outcomes in patients with severe MR due to MAC (14). Taken together, these findings emphasize the importance of a multidimensional approach to risk stratification in this population, incorporating myocardial function, atrial remodeling, and systemic vulnerability. Such an integrated assessment may help identify patients who could benefit from earlier intervention despite the complexity of their underlying disease.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center retrospective study and is therefore subject to inherent selection bias. Patients with worse LVGLS tended to be older, and this age imbalance may have partially contributed to the higher early mortality observed in this group during the first year. Additionally, patients undergoing mitral valve intervention were selectively chosen based on an anticipated clinical benefit and were therefore more likely to survive, which may have contributed, at least in part, to the more favorable outcomes observed in the intervention group. Although we adjusted for MV intervention in multivariable analyses, residual confounding and indication bias cannot be fully excluded, and therefore the independent prognostic effect of LVGLS cannot be completely isolated. Furthermore, propensity score matching was not performed, which may limit the ability to fully account for differences between groups. Second, despite being the largest study to date on the unique population of severe MR due to severe MAC, the sample size remains relatively small, which limits the statistical power for subgroup analyses. In addition, the duration of follow-up was relatively short, likely reflecting the high event rate in this high-risk population. This limited follow-up may have reduced the statistical power to detect the prognostic effect of LVGLS beyond 1 year. Third, echocardiographic assessment of MR in severe MAC is technically challenging. Grading of MAC on echocardiography is subject to observer variability; therefore, we adopted the multiparametric approach proposed by Xu et al. to minimize inter- and intra-observer variability. Additionally, quantification of MR using the PISA method may be limited by annular distortion and acoustic shadowing, while LVGLS measurements may be influenced by annular immobility, myocardial tethering, and suboptimal endocardial tracking in the setting of extensive MAC. Although ICC was high, the SEM for interobserver analysis was suboptimal. Fourth, as this study was conducted at a quaternary referral center with highly experienced cardiologists and cardiac surgeons, the generalizability of the findings to other institutions may be limited. Finally, the primary endpoint was all-cause mortality. Cardiovascular-specific mortality or heart failure-related outcomes may be more closely linked to myocardial mechanics and would likely provide a more sensitive assessment of the prognostic value of LVGLS. The lack of cardiovascular outcomes may therefore have attenuated observed associations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our findings highlight the unique pathophysiological and prognostic characteristics of severe MR due to severe MAC. LVGLS appears to serve as an imaging marker of short-term prognosis in patients with severe MR due to severe MAC; however, its longer term prognostic value appears limited in the current study. Further studies are warranted to elucidate the temporal and mechanistic role of LVGLS in this unique population.

Overall, the present study provides novel insights into the time-dependent prognostic value of LVGLS in patients with severe MR due to severe MAC and highlights the need for a comprehensive, systemic approach to risk stratification and management in this unique patient population.
