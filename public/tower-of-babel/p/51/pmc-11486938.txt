Machine learning-based analysis of nutrient and water uptake in hydroponically grown soybeans
Authors: Sambandh Bhusan Dhal, Shikhadri Mahanta, Janie McClurkin Moore, Stavros Kalafatis
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecent advancements in sustainable agriculture have spurred interest in hydroponics as an alternative to conventional farming methods. However, the lack of data-driven approaches in hydroponic growth presents a significant challenge. This study addresses this gap by varying nitrogen, magnesium, and potassium concentrations in hydroponically grown soybeans and conducting essential nutrient profiling across the growth cycle. Statistical techniques like Linear Interpolation are employed to interpolate nutrient data and a feature selection pipeline consisting of chi-squared testing methods, Linear Regression with Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) and ExtraTreesClassifier have been used to select important nutrients for predicting water uptake using non-parametric regression methods. For different nutrient growth media, i.e. for soybeans grown in Hoagland + Nitrogen and Hoagland + Magnesium media, the Random Forest regressor outperformed other methods in predicting water uptake, achieving testing Mean Squared Error (MSE) scores of 24.55 (R2 score 0.63) and 8.23 (R2 score 0.81), respectively. Similarly, for soybeans grown in Hoagland + Potassium media, Support Vector Regression demonstrated superior performance with a testing MSE of 4.37 and R2 score of 0.85. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values are examined in each case to elucidate the contributions of individual nutrients to water uptake predictions. This research aims to provide data-driven insights to optimize hydroponic practices for sustainable food production.Keywords: Sustainable agriculture, Hydroponics, Non-parametric regression, Machine learning, Shapley additive explanationsSubject terms: Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Soybean plays a pivotal role in global agriculture due to their high nutrition value and extensive use. Global soybean production has been estimated to an increase of 7% during 2023–2024, owing mostly to soybean production in South America. Sustainable agricultural methods like hydroponics are crucial in enhancing soybean production and ensuring food safety. Soybeans contain 40–42% protein and 18–22% oil, making them the second-largest source of vegetable oil. Additionally, they are used to produce soy milk, soy nuts, sprouts, edamame, soy flour, soy meals, and tofu1. Given their importance in food production, optimizing soybean yields is critical to meeting the dietary needs of the growing global population. Current projections indicate that the annual growth rate of soybean production will be only 1.3% by 2050, which is insufficient to meet future demands. To address this shortfall, certain post-harvest food processing techniques, such as ozone treatment2and electric pulse field treatments3,4, have been implemented to improve the quality and safety of soybean products. Additionally, hydroponic media growth offers a promising solution for enhancing soybean yields due to its efficient nutrient delivery and water-saving capabilities, making it a sustainable alternative to traditional farming methods.

Hydroponics has gained prominence as a sustainable alternative to traditional farming due to its significant water-saving capabilities, reducing water usage by 50 to 70% through recyclability5–7. These systems also decrease the need for pest management and exhibit resilience to adverse climatic conditions, boosting agricultural yields. As agriculture becomes increasingly digitalized, efforts are underway to integrate intelligent Internet of Things (IoT) systems and machine learning to optimize nutrient and water uptake, promoting broader commercial adoption8,9. The economic and social benefits of hydroponically produced food are evident, with research showing potential ten-fold production increases without harmful chemicals and using only 10–50% of the water needed by traditional methods10,11. Despite numerous studies over the past thirty years on aquaponic plant growth, the specific regulation of essential nutrients according to seasonal variations remains unexplored.

The integration of machine learning and data-driven methodologies into hydroponic growth is crucial for optimizing nutrient uptake and maximizing crop yields. Traditional hydroponic studies often lack data-driven approaches, leading to suboptimal nutrient regulation and growth conditions. Machine learning algorithms can analyze and predict nutrient requirements, water uptake, and growth parameters with high precision, enabling better control and enhancement of crop yields. Recent advancements in predictive methods have shown the potential of these technologies to sustainably increase crop yield. For example, convolutional neural networks (CNNs)12, various ML-based regression algorithms13–16and AutoML models17 have been successfully used to predict growth periods, regulate nutrients, and optimize environmental parameters in agricultural settings.

A significant challenge in hydroponic crop datasets is the limited number of data points available to formulate data-driven models. To address this, various data interpolation methods have been developed, proving highly beneficial. For instance, fractal interpolation18techniques generate detailed time series from small datasets by incorporating time-varying complexity features, effectively predicting wheat and maize yields in Austria using an average of only 50 training data points per yield. Other techniques include using farm field polygons with Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) data19and boosted decision tree classifiers for generating pixel-based datasets, non-linear spatial interpolation technique for transferring daily flow data20, and Decomposition and Analysis of Time Series (DATimeS) software for producing cloud-free composite maps and capturing vegetation patterns with Gaussian Process Regression21. All these methods were used in cases to help overcome data limitations and improve predictions in agricultural contexts.

In the context of data modeling, an ideal scenario occurs when there are few predictors relative to a wealth of data points (p ≪ n). This setup facilitates effective pattern recognition and relationship modeling without the risk of overfitting, ensuring robust generalization to new data. However, in hydroponic systems, a challenge arises due to the vast number of predictors compared to the limited number of observations, often leading to the "Curse of Dimensionality."22Addressing this challenge involves careful selection of essential features to retain relevant information. In agricultural research, feature selection techniques like Random Forest, Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), and SelectKBest algorithms are commonly used to prioritize significant predictors from diverse datasets23. Hybrid methods combining PCA, entropy, and skewness-based covariance have been applied successfully in disease prediction for citric fruits24. Additionally, approaches such as genetic algorithms25, fuzzy feature selection26, and boosting algorithms like BORUTA27have been utilized to predict crop damage. Despite these advances, overfitting28remains a concern on smaller datasets post-feature selection, necessitating the use of regression techniques compatible with limited data, including deep learning models29, hybrid approaches30, Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) Regression31,32, and Partial Least Squares Regression33. Further research is needed to explore growth parameter prediction for hydroponically grown crops using sparse datasets, highlighting an area for future investigation in agricultural science.

In summary, this study aims to leverage data-driven approaches to optimize nutrient uptake for soybeans grown in hydroponic environments. By employing statistical techniques and machine learning algorithms, the research seeks to enhance the efficiency and sustainability of soybean growth. The findings can significantly impact U.S. agriculture by providing insights into effective nutrient management, leading to increased yield and reduced resource consumption. Additionally, the study’s focus on hydroponics aligns with the broader goals of sustainable agriculture, potentially reducing the environmental footprint of farming practices. The successful implementation of these methodologies could pave the way for the widespread adoption of hydroponic systems, contributing to food security and sustainability amid growing global demand.

Construction of the dataset

Soybean seeds were cultivated in ten distinct hydroponic environments at Texas A&M University’s Post-Harvest Engineering and Education (PHEED)9,12,34 Research Laboratory. Initially, seeds were germinated in petri plates using deionized water. Each nutrient media was tested with six germinated seeds placed in plastic tubes, ensuring robust replication. Hoagland solution was chosen for its balanced nutrient composition, encompassing nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium and essential micronutrients crucial for soybean health. This versatility allowed us to adjust nutrient concentrations to support various growth stages, from vegetative to reproductive phases, promoting optimal development. Precise control over nutrient availability in hydroponic setups minimized deficiencies or toxicities, enhancing overall nutrient uptake efficiency. The standardized use of Hoagland solution in this research study facilitated consistent experimentation and reliable results, which emphasizes the significance of different nutrient solutions in soybean growth. Prior to conducting the analysis, several studies have examined various nutrient concentrations in hydroponics that could contribute to the development of a comprehensive dataset for cultivating soybeans35–38. In one of the previous studies conducted, various concentrations of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium were employed to ascertain the most favorable growth medium for soybean/sorghum intercropping systems35. The nutrient, acid, and water budget of hydroponically grown crops were investigated in another study, with the biomass of the crops as the desired outcome36. A similar study investigated the impact of silicon as a nutritional supplement on enhancing biomass and chlorophyll levels in soybeans37. These studies were used as a foundation for developing our experimental configurations used in the analysis. The varied concentrations of nitrogen, magnesium, and potassium in the Hoagland solution for hydroponic soybean growth were designed to optimize plant development and productivity. Nitrogen, was tested at 100 ppm, 175 ppm, and 250 ppm to determine the effects of moderate to high nitrogen availability on growth, leaf development and yield. Magnesium, crucial for chlorophyll production and photosynthesis, was evaluated at 30 ppm, 50 ppm, and 70 ppm to understand its impact on basic metabolic processes, leaf greenness, and overall plant health. Potassium, important for enzyme activation, photosynthesis, and stress resistance, was assessed at 100 ppm, 200 ppm, and 300 ppm. This approach allowed researchers to identify the optimal nutrient levels for maximizing soybean growth and productivity in hydroponic systems.

For each nutrient growth media (nitrogen, potassium and magnesium), the nutrient profiling of the samples was carried out five times in the entire growth cycle.

Since six germination seeds were used for each nutrient concentration, each plastic tube was filled with 50 mL every three days and the values of water uptake by the soybean seeds were recorded once in every three-day interval. Water uptake by the seeds was considered a good measure of the plant growth, a data-driven approach has been formulated to find out the important nutrients in each hydroponic medium of plant growth using feature selection techniques, and it was regressed against the water uptake throughout the growth period of soybeans.

Data interpolation

For each of the nutrient media used for growing soybeans, the nutrient profiling of the hydroponic solutions was carried out on the 1st, 4th, 8th, 18th and 20th day after germination of the soybean seeds. The data was interpolated for the rest of the growth period.

As the nutrient profiling was carried out at irregular intervals, we carried out different non-linear interpolation techniques on the data like polynomial interpolation48, spline interpolation49, Radial Basis Function (RBF) interpolation50and Gaussian process interpolation51. These non-linear interpolation techniques were carried out on the time-series data as it provided ways to estimate the values between data points and helped to capture more complex patterns in the data. However, the results that were obtained using these methods were not optimal as some values of the nutrient concentrations showed up to be negative for attributes which were very small i.e. the value of Sodium Absorption Ratio, and the concentration of copper. Therefore, it was decided to proceed with a linear interpolation method to obtain the nutrient values for the entire growth period as the nutrient values showed a non-negative increase. Similar linear interpolation method was used for values of water intake from the different nutrient media.

Feature selection

After conducting linear interpolation to generate time-series data for the entire growth period, feature selection techniques were conducted as the dimensionality was much larger than the number of samples in the dataset. A series of steps were followed to generate the important nutrients in each growth media. These nutrients were then used to predict the water uptake which was used as a parameter to gauge plant growth in our analysis. The data was interpolated till the maturation period and there was a total of 20 data points collected for each distinct concentration of magnesium, potassium and nitrogen. A total of 23 different chemical properties were recorded for each date, and the dimension of the dataset recorded for each concentration of magnesium, potassium and nitrogen was 20 observations X 23 predictors, which were used for analysis.

The first step in the analysis before carrying out feature selection was formulation of datasets to be used. In this analysis, three datasets were formulated. The first dataset comprised of potassium concentrations (100 ppm, 200 ppm and 300 ppm), followed by nitrogen concentrations (100 ppm, 175 ppm and 250 ppm), and magnesium concentrations (30 ppm, 50 ppm and 70 ppm). Every dataset had a total of 60 observations X 23 predictors which were further analyzed.

On the dataset which was formulated for different concentrations of potassium combined, an unsupervised learning algorithm named as Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM)52clustering was carried out to bin the observations into a binary classification problem. It was binned into two categories to carry out supervised feature space reduction using a pipeline of methods such as chi-square testing method53, Linear Regression54with RFE55and ExtraTreesClassifier56 to generate the feature importances (%) with respect to predicting each class, which in this case was a GMM, thereby optimizing model performance and reducing overfitting by focusing on the most relevant features.

In this case, a GMM was used instead of traditional clustering techniques like K-Means57and Hierarchical clustering58, as the size of the dataset in our case was very small and the data was high-dimensional. As GMMs work on the principle of assigning probabilities to each cluster rather than hard labels, they were less sensitive to noisy and outlier data points. It might be one of the hindrances in the analysis where the size of the dataset was small.

A pipeline of steps were used to reduce the feature space in a sequential manner. Firstly, a Chi-Squared test was performed on the dataset to determine the predictors ranked in order of their significance level (p-value). The top 15 predictors which had the lowest significance level (p-value), were fed on to the Linear Regression model where RFE was used to find out the top 10 predictors.

Linear Regression was used as a feature selection algorithm as it handles multicollinearity. As the correlated predictors were not removed before proceeding with this analysis, linear regression was helpful to avoid redundancy and improves the stability of the model. RFE was used as a measure to calculate the feature importances as it evaluates the importance of each feature by considering its contribution to the performance of the linear regression model and gives a more comprehensive understanding of feature importance in the context of their interactions. The other important reason for using it was that it is model agnostic.

Thirdly, a correlation matrix was generated between the reduced feature space. The advantage of using a correlation matrix was that it helps to preserve information by providing features which are less informative or redundant. This helps to remove the highly correlated features, making the model more manageable and less prone to overfitting.

The final step of the analysis was the application of ExtraTreesClassifier59 on the dataset after removing the highly correlated features. As ExtraTreesClassifier builds an ensemble of decision trees, each tree was trained on a randomly selected subset of features and a bootstrap sample of the training data. This helped to reduce the bias towards certain features and improved the model’s robustness to noise and overfitting. Additionally, ExtraTreesClassifier can easily capture non-linear relationships between predictors and the label generated using the Gaussian Mixture Models, which helpful to generate feature importances. Therefore, due to the robustness and feature ranking capabilities of the ExtraTreesClassifier, it was used as a valuable tool for selecting the most important features before carrying out the regression analysis.

Regression analysis

Small datasets pose significant challenges in accurately capturing underlying patterns and relationships due to their limited observations. This limitation often leads to less reliable estimates and increased uncertainty in predictions, exacerbated by the risk of overfitting. In such cases, models may end up capturing noise rather than meaningful patterns, which becomes particularly evident when data is sparse.

Moreover, statistical tests for significance may lose reliability with small datasets because of insufficient sample sizes, which reduce the statistical power needed to detect genuine effects. Linear regression models, commonly used but less robust in small datasets, are particularly sensitive to outliers and influential points that can distort both coefficients and overall model performance.

To mitigate challenges with small datasets, employing more flexible modeling approaches becomes crucial. Decision trees60 and non-linear regression methods were recommended as they can better capture complex relationships that linear models may overlook. Decision trees, for instance, offer interpretability and handle small datasets efficiently, making them suitable for understanding intricate data dynamics.

Support Vector Regression (SVR)61 stands out as another effective method for small datasets due to its robustness against overfitting. By employing regularization techniques and utilizing kernel transformations such as Radial Basis Function (RBF), SVR can enhance its ability to capture intricate patterns in the data. The epsilon-insensitive loss function in SVR further aids in handling outliers by ignoring errors within a specified range (epsilon), which was crucial for maintaining model performance integrity in the presence of outliers.

Additionally, K-Nearest Neighbor (KNN)62algorithm proves advantageous for small datasets by focusing on local data patterns which enhances prediction accuracy, improves model transparency, facilitates easier interpretation of predictions based on neighboring data points. Techniques like Synthetic Minority Over-Sampling Technique (SMOTE)63 was integrated with KNN to augment dataset effectiveness, particularly in scenarios with class imbalance or sparse data regions.

In a practical application example, a comparative study of regression algorithms was conducted to predict water uptake in soybean hydroponic growth mediums with varying nutrient concentrations. SVR, KNN, and Random Forest were evaluated, with each growth medium having its best-performing regression model selected based on predictive accuracy. SHAP analysis64,65 was employed to quantify the impact of different features on predictions, providing valuable insights into how varying hydroponic conditions influence water uptake predictions.

As the SHAP value for a feature represents the average contribution of that feature to the predicted outcome across all potential feature combinations, it quantified how much each feature deviates the prediction from the model’s expected baseline (average prediction). The magnitude of SHAP values indicate the magnitude of a feature’s influence on a prediction. Greater absolute SHAP values indicate that the corresponding characteristic had a greater impact on the prediction. If a feature had a positive SHAP value for a particular instance, it indicated that the presence or higher value of that feature was associated with an improvement in the predicted outcome relative to the baseline prediction. In other terms, this characteristic positively influences prediction. Negative SHAP values indicate that the absence or reduced value of a feature is associated with a decrease in the predicted outcome relative to the baseline prediction. This characteristic negatively affects prediction.

Discussion

Using a Random Forest model with 100 estimators, the water uptake for soybean seeds grown in Hoagland + Nitrogen solution was predicted with a testing Mean Squared Error (MSE) of 24.55% and a R2score of 0.63, indicating a moderately strong prediction capability. SHAP values were utilized to interpret the model’s predictions, revealing that the concentrations of calcium, potassium, and phosphorus were the most important nutrients influencing water uptake.The findings from the SHAP analysis align with previous research, highlighting the critical role of these nutrients. Elevated calcium levels were positively correlated with higher water uptake and nitrogen concentration, enhancing cell wall robustness and structural integrity66,67. Lower calcium levels, however, could disrupt nitrogen uptake, leading to nutrient imbalances68. Potassium’s importance in the model underscores its role in nitrogen utilization efficiency and its influence on stomatal regulation, affecting water loss and drought resistance69. Phosphorus, the third most significant nutrient in the SHAP analysis, is vital for nitrogen metabolism and photosynthesis, impacting Adenosine Triphosphate (ATP) and Nicotinamide Adenine Dinucleotide Phosphate (NADPH) synthesis70,71, which are crucial for energy production and carbon assimilation.

Similarly, using a Random Forest model with 100 estimators, the water uptake for soybean seeds grown in a Hoagland + Magnesium solution was effectively predicted with a testing MSE of 8.23% and a R2 score of 0.81. The SHAP analysis, as shown in Fig. 7, revealed a linear relationship between nitrate concentrations and water uptake, with higher nitrate values corresponding to higher water uptake. Conversely, lower concentrations of bicarbonates positively influenced water uptake whereas the hardness values had a mixed impact on the prediction. This analysis highlights the complex interactions between different nutrients and water uptake in soybean plants grown in a Hoagland + Magnesium solution.

Magnesium plays a crucial role in plant physiology, activating enzymes and regulating membrane transporters, which optimizes nutrient absorption72. Adequate magnesium levels enhance nitrate absorption through the plant’s root system73, where magnesium acts as a component of enzymes involved in nitrate assimilation pathways through higher water uptake, such as nitrate reductase. This enzyme facilitates the conversion of nitrates into ammonium ions and amino acids, essential for protein synthesis and overall plant growth.

The SHAP analysis also emphasized the importance of bicarbonates and water hardness. Bicarbonates can be used by plants as an alternative carbon source for photosynthesis, especially in conditions with high pH or low CO2 availability. Hardness, primarily due to calcium and magnesium ions, serves as a buffering agent, maintaining pH stability in the nutrient solution74. This stability is crucial for nutrient uptake and overall plant health. The presence of calcium ions contributes to robust cell wall formation, enhancing plant architecture and resistance to pathogens and mechanical stress75.

Finally, using a Radial Support Vector Machine Regression (penalty parameter = 10), the water uptake for soybean seeds grown in a Hoagland + Potassium solution was predicted with a testing Mean Squared Error (MSE) of 4.37% and an R2 score of 0.85. The SHAP analysis, depicted in Fig. 8, shows that higher sulfate, nitrate and manganese concentrations corresponded to higher SHAP values, indicating a positive impact on water uptake predictions. Firstly, potassium through higher water uptake facilitates the utilization of sulfate ions in plants, crucial for synthesizing amino acids, proteins, and vitamins. It supports the active transport of sulfates into roots and their distribution across plant tissues, enhancing overall metabolic functions and nutrient assimilation76,77. Secondly, potassium optimizes the absorption and translocation of nitrates within soybeans, crucial for nitrogen uptake essential in protein synthesis, chlorophyll formation, and vegetative growth. Maintaining a balanced potassium-to-nitrogen ratio is emphasized to prevent nutrient deficiencies or toxicities that could hinder soybean development.

Additionally, potassium influences manganese dynamics in soybeans, essential for enzyme activation, photosynthesis, and nitrogen metabolism. While adequate potassium levels promote manganese utilization and stress resilience, excessive potassium can hinder manganese uptake, potentially leading to deficiencies characterized by chlorosis and stunted growth78–80. The passage underscores the significance of these nutrient interactions in soybean agriculture, highlighting how predictive models and SHAP values contribute to understanding nutrient contributions and optimizing growth conditions in agricultural research. This holistic approach underscores the importance of balanced nutrient management to maximize soybean yield and quality in diverse environmental conditions while growing soybeans in hydroponics.

This research is significant in light of previous studies demonstrating that soy derivatives, such as soy milk and okara, derived from hydroponically grown soybeans exhibit high nutritional value, with a 40% increase in fat and dietary fiber accumulation compared to soybeans grown using traditional soil-based methods81. Additionally, as stated before, hydroponically grown soybeans reduce water usage by 50 to 70% due to the inherent recyclability of the system and it eliminates the need for harmful pesticides and fertilizers. Other studies have also highlighted substantial improvements in the anatomical features of soybean plants, such as a 29.9% increase in leaf area and a 36.9% increase in seed yield compared to traditional soil-based agriculture82. These findings emphasize the advantages of cultivating soybeans using hydroponic techniques.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study employed a rigorous systematic approach to optimize nutrient management during soybean growth in hydroponics across varying nitrogen, magnesium, and potassium concentrations. By employing linear interpolation for nutrient concentration calculations and employing robust feature selection techniques such as the chi-squared test, Linear Regression with Recursive Feature Elimination, and correlation matrix analysis, the key nutrients critical for soybean growth in different media compositions were identified. The ExtraTreesClassifier further pinpointed pivotal nutrients specific to each growth medium.

Subsequently, predictive modeling using Random Forest, Support Vector Regression, and K-Nearest Neighbors allowed to forecast water uptake, with model selection based on rigorous testing of MSE and R2 scores. In the Hoagland + Nitrogen medium, the Random Forest regressor (100 estimators) proved most effective, achieving a testing MSE of 24.55% and a R2 score of 0.63. Calcium, potassium, and phosphorus emerged as key contributors to water uptake, as indicated by their substantial SHAP values. Similarly, in the Hoagland + Magnesium medium, the Random Forest regressor (100 estimators) excelled with a testing MSE of 8.23% and a R2 score of 0.81, highlighting the significant roles of nitrates, bicarbonates, and hardness parameters.

In the Hoagland + Potassium solution, Support Vector Regression (Penalty parameter = 10, radial kernel) emerged as the optimal predictor, achieving a testing MSE of 4.37% and a R2 score of 0.85. Here, SHAP values underscored the critical contributions of sulfates, nitrates, and manganese in shaping water uptake dynamics. These findings enhance our understanding of nutrient interactions and their impact on soybean growth and underscore the efficacy of integrating advanced modeling techniques with interpretable SHAP analyses in agricultural research.

Furthermore, growing soybeans in hydroponics offers potential advantages by reducing costs associated with nutrient inputs compared to conventional farming techniques. This approach also minimizes nutrient runoff and soil degradation, contributing to environmental sustainability. Future research could explore optimizing nutrient formulations tailored to specific growth stages of soybeans in hydroponic systems, investigating broader environmental impacts, and expanding predictive modeling approaches to include additional variables influencing crop performance and yield. By advancing these methodologies, we aim to continually enhance soybean production practices, contributing to sustainable agriculture and global food security.
