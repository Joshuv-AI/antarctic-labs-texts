The association of multiple whole blood metals with hypertension prevalence and blood pressure among general Chinese adults
Authors: Yurong Xiong, Jiajing Xu, Jingyi Wang, Ruikun He, Mengyan Hu, An Pan, Limiao Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpidemiological associations between multiple metals exposure and hypertension or blood pressure remain limited and inconclusive, especially for essential metals. This investigation intended to examine the individual and collective effects of multiple metals on hypertension and blood pressure in general Chinese adults.MethodsWhole blood levels of 8 essential metals and 3 nonessential metals were determined via inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry in 867 adults from Shiyan, China. Multivariable logistic/linear regression, restricted cubic spline regression, weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression and Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) analyses were employed for exploring associations of individual metals or metal mixtures with hypertension prevalence and blood pressures.ResultsIn total, 239 participants (29.5%) were identified as being hypertensive. Multivariable logistic regression models indicated that magnesium and iron were positively, while calcium was inversely, associated with hypertension prevalence, with odds ratios (95% CI) of 2.19 (1.36, 3.54), 2.70 (1.62, 4.50), and 0.58 (0.35, 0.94) respectively for extreme-tertile comparisons. Multivariable linear regression analysis demonstrated a positive association of iron with mean arterial pressure, showing a β (95% CI) of 2.70 (0.88, 4.51) for extreme-tertile comparison. Both WQS regression and BKMR models demonstrated a positive collective effect of eleven metals on hypertension, with magnesium and iron being the top contributors. The WQS regression model also showed a positive combined effect of metal mixtures on systolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure. BKMR analyses further indicated that the eight essential metals presented a stronger association with hypertension or blood pressure than the three nonessential metals.ConclusionThe present study demonstrated positive associations between multiple metals (individually or collectively) and hypertension or blood pressure among general Chinese adults, with essential metals magnesium and/or iron as major contributors. It may present useful epidemiological evidence on the pathogenesis and prevention of hypertension.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-025-01271-w.

Introduction

Hypertension is the primary contributor to cardiovascular diseases and mortality globally [1]. In China, hypertension currently affects around 25% of the adults, presenting a substantial increase in its prevalence during the past three decades [2]. Complex interactions between hereditary and environmental components are accountable for hypertension occurrence and progression [3]. Apart from traditional risk factors, metal and metalloid elements (henceforth referred to as metals) were also considered to be linked to hypertension and blood pressure [4].

Although epidemiological investigations have demonstrated associations of some toxic heavy metal exposure (e.g., arsenic, cadmium, mercury, lead) with hypertension or blood pressure [5–8], the associations of essential metals were less reported and with inconsistent findings. Based on conventional multivariable regression models, some studies demonstrated significant associations of essential metals from biofluids with hypertension or blood pressure, e.g., positive associations of urinary vanadium, iron, zinc and selenium with hypertension prevalence in a Chinese general population from Wuhan China [9], positive associations of urinary iron, cobalt, nickel, copper, and zinc, but a negative association of vanadium in the urine with hypertension in another Chinese population from Wuhan China [10], positive associations of magnesium, calcium and copper in blood samples with hypertension prevalence and positive associations of magnesium and iron in the blood with blood pressures in participants from an unpolluted area of rural southwest China [11].

However, in daily life, human may be concurrently exposed to various metals. Traditional multivariable parametric regression models would be subjected to multicollinearity and model misspecification when metals presented as mixtures [12]. Several relatively new statistical methods for the analysis of mixtures, including weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression and Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) [13], were developed to deal with such issue and have recently been used for assessing the collective effects of multiple metals on hypertension or blood pressure [14]. In a cross-sectional investigation on the basis of 2017–2018 China National Human Biomonitoring, WQS regression suggested that the exposure to 13 metals in the blood was positively associated with diastolic and systolic blood pressures, with lead being the top contributor [15]. In a cohort study performed in an area alongside the Yangtze River, BKMR models demonstrated a positive joint effect of five urinary metals (i.e., cadmium, copper, magnesium, molybdenum, zinc) on hypertension risk [16]. BKMR models indicated a significant collective effect of whole blood manganese, arsenic, cadmium and lead on hypertension prevalence, in adults from Guangdong, China [17]. But, to date, for studies exploring the combined effects of metal mixtures on hypertension or blood pressure, limited studies focused on essential metals, limited studies employed several methods on mixture analysis, and few studies simultaneously investigated the effect on both hypertension and blood pressure [14].

This study intended to examine the individual and collective effects of multiple metals exposure, including 8 essential and 3 nonessential metals, on the prevalence of hypertension and the levels of blood pressure among general Chinese adults, using a combination of conventional multivariable logistic/linear regression and two joint analysis methods (i.e., WQS regression and BKMR).

Materials and methods

Study design and population

The present study was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Wuhan, China). From August to November in 2019, 867 adults were enrolled from the health management center of Sinopharm Dongfeng General Hospital (Shiyan city, Hubei province, China). Participants were not enrolled if they met one of the following criteria: (1) pregnant and lactating women, (2) with major chronic diseases (e.g., cancer, cardiovascular diseases, endocrine diseases, mental diseases), (3) long term usage of antibiotics, traditional Chinese medicine, and dietary supplements. All participants presented their signed informed consent.

At enrollment, the data on sociodemographics (including age, sex, education), lifestyles (including the status on smoking and drinking, night sleep duration, regular exercise), medical and medication histories (e.g., hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes), and dietary supplementation (e.g. minerals) of each participant was obtained via a semi-structured standardized questionnaire by trained interviewers. After finishing the questionnaire, each participant also completed a physical examination. Height, weight, and blood pressures was determined by trained staff. For each subject, the blood pressure was recorded as the average of two measurements with a 5-minute interval. Mean arterial pressure was approximated as 1/3 (systolic blood pressure) + 2/3 (diastolic blood pressure) [18]. For each volunteer, 5 mL fasting venous blood was collected into a sterile K2EDTA tube. After gentle mix, aliquots of whole blood sample were stored at -80 °C till analysis. After excluding those with missing questionnaires (n = 10) and regular usage of mineral supplements (n = 47), 810 subjects were subjected to further data analysis. Study participants were selected according to the flow diagram shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Metal measurements

Blood samples were prepared and detected according to previously published methods with modifications [19]. In brief, for each volunteer, 40 µL whole blood sample was mixed with 4.96 mL extraction solution (0.5% HNO3 and 0.01% Triton X-100 in ultrapure water, V/V) before being kept in dark at room temperature for 1 h. After sonication in water bath (40 °C, 10 min) and centrifugation (9900 g, 5 min), the supernatant was used for the measurement of metals. Eight essential metals (i.e., calcium, Ca; chromium, Cr; copper, Cu; iron, Fe; magnesium, Mg; manganese, Mn; selenium, Se; zinc, Zn) and three nonessential metals (i.e., arsenic, As; lead, Pb; mercury, Hg) were measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) (Agilent 7800, Agilent Technologies, USA). The samples were randomly divided into batches and detected in a random order. In each batch, blank samples, quality control samples and samples used for the construction of calibration curves were also measured. Metals were quantified by external calibration with the use of internal standards (Part Number 5188–6525, Agilent Technologies, USA). For all the metals measured, the coefficients of variation were within 10% for both intra-day and inter-day assessment, the spiked recovery rates were between 86.5% and 102.1%, and the limits of detection (LODs) were 0.001 to 21.71 ng/mL (Table S1). For each metal, over 98% of the measurement was above the LOD, and half of the LOD was used to substitute the values below its LOD (Table S1).

Outcome ascertainment

In this study, participants were considered to be hypertensive if one of the following criteria were met: (1) diagnosed hypertension by a physician (self-report), (2) current usage of antihypertensive medications (self-report), (3) systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, (4) diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg [20, 21].

Covariates definition

Variables collected by questionnaire and physical examination at enrollment were used as covariates for subsequent statistical analysis, including age (continuous), sex (female, male), education level (junior college/college or above, senior high school/specialized secondary school, junior high school or below), status of smoking (former/never, current), status of drinking (former/never, current), night sleep duration (≥ 9, 7–9, < 7 h), regular exercise (yes, no), hyperlipidemia (yes, no), usage of antihypertensive medications (yes, no), and body mass index (continuous). Participants who had smoked no less than 1 cigarette a day for 6 months or longer were considered to be ever smokers. Participants who consumed alcohol no less than once a week for at least 6 months were classified as ever drinkers. Regular exercise was defined as participating in leisure-time exercise with no less than 30 min a time for no less than five times a week, lasting for no less than six months. Subjects with self-reported hyperlipidemia or usage of hypolipidemic drugs were grouped as hyperlipidemia. Those with self-reported diabetes mellitus or use of hypoglycemics were considered to have diabetes mellitus. Subjects with body mass index of 24.0–27.9 kg/m2 were classified as overweight, and those with body mass index ≥ 28.0 kg/m2 were defined as obesity [22].

Statistical analyses

The median (interquartile range) was employed to express continuous variables, and the frequency (percentage) was utilized to represent categorical variables. For the comparison of characteristics between hypertensive and non-hypertensive participants, continuous and categorical variables were compared using Mann-Whitney U test and Chi-squared test respectively. Spearman rank correlation analysis was used to check the correlation among metal levels. Due to the skewed distribution, metal levels were natural logarithm transformed prior to subsequent analysis.

The association of each metal with hypertension was examined by multivariable logistic regression. For each metal, its level was modeled as tertiles based on the level distribution in non-hypertensive participants. The odds ratio (OR) with the corresponding 95% confidence interval (CI) was computed after adjusting of age, sex, education, body mass index, status on smoking and drinking, night sleep duration, regular exercise and hyperlipidemia. For each metal, the median of every tertile was modelled continuously to assess the linear trend. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was used to correct multiple testing [23], with false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P < 0.05 deemed as statistically significant. Each metal level was also modelled as continuous variable to calculate the OR (95% CI) per SD increment. Multivariable linear regression was employed for assessing associations of individual metals with mean arterial pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and systolic blood pressure. The level of each metal was modeled as tertiles according to its level distribution across all participants, and β coefficients (95% CIs) were calculated after adjusting for covariates used in multivariable logistic regression models and the usage of antihypertensive medications. For each metal, the median of individual tertile was modelled continuously to calculate the linear trend, where statistical significance was defined as FDR-adjusted P < 0.05. Each metal level was also modelled as continuous variable to calculate β (95% CI) per SD increment. In addition, potential non-linearity of the associations was assessed by restricted cubic spline (RCS) regressions, where three knots were selected as the 10th (reference), the 50th, and the 90th percentiles of metal levels after excluding extreme values (i.e., < 1% and >99% percentiles).

Combined effects of multiple metals on hypertension prevalence or blood pressure were evaluated by WQS regression and BKMR analyses, with covariates adjusted as previously described. WQS regression analysis [24] was constructed by including all 11 metals in positive and negative directions. Combined effects of each tertile of metals on the prevalence of hypertension or blood pressure were assessed. The importance of metals was evaluated by randomly splitting the dataset into two parts, a training part (40%) and a validation part (60%) [24, 25]. The weights were estimated by employing 1000 bootstrapping and were summed to one [24, 25]. For BKMR models, the overall effect of metal mixtures was evaluated by calculating the difference in hypertension prevalence or blood pressure every five percentiles of their levels between the 25th and the 75th percentile, relative to their medians [26]. The effect of one metal was evaluated by removing the effect at its 25th percentile from that at its 75th percentile, while the concentrations of other metals were placed at their 25th, 50th, or 75th percentiles, respectively [26]. Univariate exposure-response functions were further utilized for estimating the association between each metal and hypertension or blood pressure while other metals were set at their medians [26]. Moreover, bivariate exposure-response functions were employed for examining interactions between two metals, where one of the two metals was placed at the 25th, the 50th, and the 75th percentile, and the remaining metals other than the two were placed at their medians [27]. Additionally, the relative importance of a metal was assessed using the posterior inclusion probability (PIP) [26]. The 11 metals were further divided to the essential metals group and the nonessential metals group, and the intergroup importance was evaluated by hierarchical variable selection, with the group PIP (groupPIP) and the conditional PIP (condPIP) representing the importance of either group and the importance of a particular metal within a group respectively [27]. Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) iterations were used for the fitting of BKMR models, by employing a total of 50,000 iterations [27]. For each BKMR model, two independent chains were run, with the first 20,000 iterations discarded as burn-in to exclude non-stationary samples. A thinning interval of 5 was applied to reduce autocorrelation, resulting in 6,000 effective samples per chain (12,000 combined samples across two chains). Convergence of MCMC chains was assessed by (1) Gelman-Rubin R̂ statistics (with values < 1.1 indicating convergence), (2) trace plots for visualizing chain mixing, and (3) kernel density plots for evaluating the stability of posterior parameter distributions.

To examine the robustness for the association of whole blood metals with hypertension or blood pressure, sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding those with hyperlipidemia or diabetes (for the association with hypertension) or by excluding those with usage of antihypertensive medications (for the association with blood pressure). Stratified analyses were performed for metals presenting significant associations with hypertension or blood pressure, by sex (female, male), age (< 45, ≥ 45 years), overweight/obesity (yes, no), the status of smoking (current, former/never), the status of drinking (current, former/never), and regular exercise (yes, no).

Stata/SE 15.1 (StataCorp LLC) and R 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) were utilized for statistical analyses. WQS regression, BKMR, and RCS regression models were constructed via the R packages gWQS, bkmr, and rms respectively. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P < 0.05.

Results

Population characteristics

Characteristics and metal levels of the study population were summarized in Table 1. Among the 810 subjects, the median age (interquartile range) was 44.9 (33.7, 51.9) years, 57.0% was male, and 29.5% was hypertensive. Compared with non-hypertensive participants, hypertensive subjects tended to be older, male, current drinkers and current smokers. The hypertension group showed higher levels of regular exercise, hyperlipidemia, body mass index, blood pressures, but a lower level of education (all P < 0.05). In addition, hypertensive subjects presented elevated concentrations of whole blood magnesium, iron, zinc, and lead, but decreased concentrations of calcium and manganese (all P < 0.05). Among all participants, weak to moderate correlations were observed among levels of the 11 whole blood metals, presenting Spearman correlation coefficients (rs) between − 0.43 and 0.52 (Fig S2). Notably, moderate positive correlations were observed for Mg-Fe (rs = 0.52), Zn-Fe (rs = 0.47), Mg-Zn (rs = 0.46), Se-Hg (rs = 0.43), and Ca-Cu (rs = 0.41), but moderate negative correlations were observed for Ca-Fe (rs = -0.43), and Ca-Mg (rs = -0.35).

Associations between metal levels and hypertension or blood pressure

After adjusting covariates, whole blood calcium presented a negative association with hypertension prevalence, while magnesium and iron showed positive associations (Table 2). For the comparison between tertile 3 and tertile 1 of metal levels, the OR (95% CI) of hypertension was 0.58 (0.35, 0.94) for calcium, 2.19 (1.36, 3.54) for magnesium, and 2.70 (1.62, 4.50) for iron (all Ptrend < 0.05) (Table 2). After FDR correction, the associations of calcium, magnesium, and iron with hypertension remained statistically significant (Table 2). In sensitivity analyses by excluding those with hyperlipidemia or diabetes, the significant associations of calcium, magnesium, and iron with hypertension persisted (Table S2). Restricted cubic spline-based models revealed a negative linear association of whole blood calcium, but positive linear associations of magnesium and iron with hypertension (P-overall < 0.05, P-nonlinear > 0.05) (Fig S3).

Multivariable linear regression models revealed a negative association of whole blood calcium, but positive associations of iron with blood pressures (Table 3). Compared to the first tertile group of calcium level, participants in the third tertile group had 2.22 (95% CI: 0.37, 4.06) mmHg and 2.27 (95% CI: 0.48, 4.06) mmHg lower levels of mean arterial pressure and diastolic blood pressure respectively (Ptrend < 0.05) (Table 3). Participants in the tertile 3 group of iron level presented 2.70 (95% CI: 0.88, 4.51) mmHg, 2.36 (95% CI: 0.59, 4.13) mmHg, and 3.36 (95% CI: 0.97, 5.75) mmHg higher levels of mean arterial pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and systolic blood pressure respectively than the tertile 1 group (all Ptrend < 0.05) (Table 3). However, only the association of iron with mean arterial pressure remained significant after FDR correction (FDR Ptrend < 0.05) (Table 3). In sensitivity analyses by excluding participants with usage of antihypertensive medications, whole blood iron presented significant positive associations with both mean arterial pressure and systolic blood pressure after FDR correction (Table S3). Restricted cubic spline-based analyses demonstrated a negative linear association between calcium and mean arterial pressure or diastolic blood pressure, and a positive linear association of iron with mean arterial pressure or diastolic blood pressure or systolic blood pressure (Fig S4).

Joint effects of multiple metals on hypertension and blood pressure

To examine the combined effects of metals on hypertension and blood pressure, all the 11 metals were incorporated into WQS regression and BKMR models respectively. In WQS regression models, a tertile increase of WQS index showed positive association with the prevalence of hypertension (OR = 1.98; 95% CI: 1.23, 3.19), and associated with 2.53 (95% CI: 0.42, 4.65) mmHg increase of mean arterial pressure and 4.38 (95% CI: 1.46, 7.31) mmHg increase of systolic blood pressure after adjusting for covariates (all P < 0.05, Table 4). For hypertension, magnesium served as the primary contributor to the positive association, with an estimated weight of 0.452 (Fig. 1A). For systolic blood pressure, zinc was the top contributor to the positive association, with an estimated weight of 0.220 (Fig. 1B). For mean arterial pressure, iron was the top contributor to the positive association, with an estimated weight of 0.279 (Fig. 1D). The BKMR model showed significant combined effects of metal mixtures on hypertension prevalence (Fig. 2A). Magnesium was shown to be positively associated with hypertension, when its exposure was elevated from the 25th to the 75th percentile, and the exposure for the remaining metals were fixed at the 25th, the 50th, or the 75th percentiles respectively (Fig. 3A). Iron was shown to be positively associated with hypertension, when its exposure was elevated from the 25th to the 75th percentile, and the exposure for the remaining metals were fixed at the 50th percentiles (Fig. 3A). It indicated that magnesium or iron was independently associated with hypertension. Although no significant combined effects of metal mixtures on blood pressures were shown by BKMR models (Fig. 2B-D), it may indicate that iron was positively associated with blood pressures, when the exposure for the remaining metals were fixed at the 25th, the 50th, or the 75th percentiles respectively (Fig. 3B-D). It may suggest that iron was independently associated with blood pressures. No potential nonlinear associations between single metal levels and hypertension or blood pressure were shown by the univariate exposure-response curves, while the concentrations for remaining metals were held at the medians (Fig S5). No potential interactions among the 11 metals for hypertension prevalence or blood pressure were suggested by bivariate exposure-response functions (Fig S6). In addition, the BKMR model indicated that the eight essential metals presented a stronger association with hypertension or blood pressure than the three nonessential metals. For the eight essential metals, the group PIP was 0.962 for the association with hypertension, with magnesium showing the highest conditional PIP (0.762) (Table S4). The reliability of BKMR model estimates was confirmed by MCMC convergence diagnostics, including Gelman-Rubin R̂ values (all < 1.1, Table S5), stable trace plots (Fig S7), and unimodal kernel density plots (Fig S8).

Stratified analyses

Stratified analyses showed no significant interactions between whole blood calcium, magnesium or iron and the stratified variables (i.e., sex, age, smoking status, drinking status, overweight/obesity, and regular exercise) on hypertension prevalence (all Pinteraction >0.05) (Table S6). No significant interactions were shown between calcium, magnesium, or iron and some stratified variables (i.e., sex, age, drinking status, overweight/obesity, and regular exercise) on blood pressure (Table S7). However, smoking status significantly modified the association of calcium with diastolic blood pressure and the association between iron and systolic blood pressure (both Pinteraction < 0.05) (Table S7). The significant negative association of calcium with diastolic blood pressure only existed in current smokers, showing a β (95% CI) of -2.14 (-3.57, -0.72). Current smokers showed a stronger positive association between iron and systolic blood pressure, with a β (95% CI) of 3.21 (1.18, 5.24).

Discussion

The present study examined the associations of eleven whole blood metals exposure (8 essential and 3 nonessential metals) with hypertension and blood pressure among general Chinese adults, using a combination of conventional multivariable regression models and joint analysis methods. Multivariable logistic regression models showed positive associations of magnesium and iron levels, but an inverse association of calcium level with the prevalence of hypertension. Multivariable linear regression models indicated a positive association of iron level with mean arterial pressure. Both WQS regression and BKMR models revealed a significant positive joint effect of 11 metals mixture on the prevalence of hypertension, with magnesium and iron being the top contributors. The WQS regression model also showed a positive combined effect of metal mixtures on systolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure. BKMR models further showed that the eight essential metals presented a stronger association with hypertension prevalence or blood pressure than the three nonessential metals.

Magnesium

Magnesium serves as one of the most abundant ions within our cells, and plays vital physiological roles, e.g., participating in more than 600 enzymatic reactions [28]. Its deficiency is related to many diseased conditions, including hypertension [28]. Magnesium might alter blood pressure via the induction of vasodilation [28]. A systematic review and meta-analysis revealed an inverse association of dietary magnesium intake, but a marginally negative association of serum magnesium level with hypertension risk [29]. In contrast, several recent epidemiological studies based on ICP-MS measurement suggested positive associations between the level of magnesium and hypertension or blood pressure. For example, a positive association of urinary magnesium with hypertension risk was observed in single-metal models, but not in models adjusted for other metals, according to a prospective cohort study on Chinese adults [16]. As shown by a cross-sectional investigation in an unpolluted location from rural southwest China, there was positive associations of circulating magnesium with hypertension prevalence, and diastolic and systolic blood pressures [11]. Our study also showed a positive association of whole blood magnesium with hypertension prevalence, which remained robust after FDR correction. However, no significant associations were observed between whole blood magnesium and blood pressures. Although the exact mechanism remains unclear and needs to be explored further, it provided population evidence regarding effects of magnesium exposure on hypertension and blood pressures, based on a general adult population in central China. The following factors might be responsible for the inconsistent conclusions concerning associations between magnesium exposure and hypertension or blood pressures: (1) the way in which magnesium level was derived, via food frequency questionnaire or from different biofluids measured by a variety of detection methods (e.g., ICP-MS or others [30, 31]), (2) the types of biofluids used for measurement (e.g., blood and urine may have difference in reflecting magnesium status [32, 33]), (3) the range of magnesium level in the population, (4) the design of the studies, prospective cohort studies or cross-sectional studies, (5) the locations where the study participants were from, (6) the bias caused by potential confounding factors, e.g., the usage of antihypertensive medication may cause alteration in blood pressure and may disturb the observed associations.

Iron

Iron acts as one of the top metals presenting high abundance in our body and plays many important metabolic roles [34]. Its level disturbance, either deficiency or overload, will lead to significant clinical consequences [34]. Iron metabolism was indicated to be crucial for hypertension development [35]. Some epidemiological investigations based on ICP-MS analysis have also explored the associations between the level of iron and hypertension or blood pressure, but demonstrated inconsistent results. For example, based on an unpolluted area of rural southwest China, a cross-sectional investigation indicated a positive association of circulating iron with diastolic blood pressure [11]. As suggested by a cross-sectional investigation from Wuhan, China, urinary iron showed positive associations with the prevalence of hypertension and systolic blood pressure, where participants in the top quartiles of iron presented a 4.9-fold increased odds of having hypertension and a 5.9 mmHg higher level of systolic blood pressure than those in the lowest quartiles [9]. However, according to a community-based cohort of the elderly in Yinchuan, China, urinary iron level showed negative association with hypertension prevalence [36]. In our study, whole blood iron showed positive association with hypertension prevalence, mean arterial pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and systolic blood pressure. But the association of iron with diastolic or systolic blood pressure became insignificant after FDR correction. The discrepancy in the findings among these studies may be related to the regions where the participants were from or difference in the iron level or the types of biofluids used for iron detection. The blood and urine sample may show difference in indicating the status of iron exposure [16], which is related to its metabolism (e.g., homeostasis and renal excretion properties). The increased iron level may result in oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, thereby contributing to the elevated blood pressure and occurrence of hypertension [37]. Nevertheless, further in-depth investigations are required to explore the underlying mechanism. In addition, our stratified analysis indicated that the positive association between iron and systolic blood pressure was significantly modified by smoking status, where current smokers showed a stronger association than former/never smokers. As shown by a cross-sectional investigation in Wuhan, urinary iron presented positive associations with hypertension prevalence in smokers but not in non-smokers [9]. Such modification effect of smoking status may be attributed to the following reasons: (1) Smokers may have higher iron level through the absorption of iron in the mainstream smoke [38], (2) Smoking is a potential risk factor of hypertension. In the present study, current smokers showed significantly higher whole blood iron level than former or never smokers (P < 0.001), presenting a median (interquartile range) value of 301.4 (276.4, 322.2) and 284.2 (261.0, 310.1) µg/mL in the two groups respectively, which supported the above deduction.

Calcium

Calcium ranks as the fifth most plentiful element in human beings and plays many instrumental biologic roles [39]. It was shown to be associated with hypertension, evaluated by dietary intake or the circulating level. As indicated by a meta-analysis on prospective observational investigations, higher calcium intake from diet is connected with a reduced risk for developing hypertension [40]. Based on results from randomized controlled trials, a meta-analysis reported that increased calcium intake in normotensive people can slightly reduce diastolic and systolic blood pressures [41]. However, some investigations using circulating calcium showed contradictory findings, e.g., the positive association of serum total calcium with hypertension prevalence in adults from the 2007–2018 NHANES [42], and the positive association of serum calcium with hypertension risk among adults from the Harbin Cohort Study on Diet, Nutrition and Chronic Noncommunicable Diseases [43]. By using ICP-MS measurement, several epidemiological studies demonstrated inconsistent associations of blood calcium with hypertension. For example, as shown by a cross-sectional investigation in rural southwest China, there was a positive association of circulating calcium with hypertension prevalence [11]. While another cross-sectional investigation on adults from Shenzhen China observed lower plasma calcium level in participants with hypertension and an inverse V-shaped dose-response curve of plasma calcium with hypertension [44]. In our study, whole blood calcium showed negative association with hypertension prevalence, mean arterial pressure and diastolic blood pressure. However, only the association with hypertension prevalence remained significant after FDR correction. The findings presented population evidence on effects of calcium exposure on hypertension and blood pressures, based on a general Chinese adult population from central China. Low circulating level of calcium would facilitate parathyroid hormone and parathyroid hypertensive factor to be released, calcitriol synthesis to be stimulated, and the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system to be activated [45]. These processes may raise intracellular calcium levels, cause vasoconstriction, promote peripheral vascular resistance, and eventually elevate blood pressure [45]. The discrepancies in findings from different investigations may be related to the types of biofluids or detection methods employed for calcium measurement or calcium concentration distribution in the specified population. Due to calcium balance and homeostasis [39], blood and urine sample may present difference in representing calcium status. Future investigations are needed for verification of the results and unravelling of the underlying mechanisms. Furthermore, our stratified analysis showed that the status of cigarette smoking significantly modified the negative association of calcium with diastolic blood pressure, where such negative association only presented in current smokers. Lower calcium level in smokers may partly explain such modification effect. In our study, current smokers presented significantly lower whole blood calcium level than former or never smokers (P < 0.001), showing a median (interquartile range) value of 49.7 (47.3, 53.4) and 53.2 (49.8, 57.0) µg/mL in the two groups respectively, which supported the above inference.

Metal mixtures

Since human beings can be simultaneously exposed to multiple pollutants, potential multicollinearity and interactions among the pollutants may exist, which necessitate sophisticated statistical methods for addressing the effects of mixtures [13]. This principle also applies to investigations on relationships between mixtures of multiple metals and hypertension or blood pressure. Several investigations have examined the joint effects of multiple metal exposure on hypertension or blood pressure by employing statistical methods on mixture analysis, e.g., WQS regression and BKMR analysis [14]. WQS regression models demonstrated positive associations of 13 blood metals with blood pressures among adults in China National Human Biomonitoring [15], and suggested a positive relationship between three metal mixtures (i.e., lead, mercury, cadmium) and the prevalence of hypertension among adults in the Korea National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey [46]. BKMR models demonstrated a positive combined effect of five urinary metals exposure (i.e., cadmium, copper, magnesium, molybdenum, zinc) on hypertension risk in Chinese adults from the area along the Yangtze River [16], and indicated a positive combined effect of four blood metals exposure (i.e., manganese, arsenic, cadmium, lead) on hypertension prevalence in adults from Guangdong, China [17]. However, currently essential metals were less frequently investigated than nonessential metals. Few studies simultaneously explored the joint effects both on hypertension and blood pressure. The combined applications of multiple methods on joint effect assessment are rare [25]. In this study, WQS regression and BKMR were used for exploring the combined effect of eleven metals exposure (including 8 essential metals) on prevalence of hypertension and blood pressures among adults from central China. Both WQS regression and BKMR models indicated a positive joint effect of eleven metals on hypertension, with magnesium and iron being the top contributors. The WQS regression model also showed a positive collective effect of metal mixtures on systolic blood pressure and mean arterial pressure, with zinc and iron being the primary contributor respectively. BKMR analyses further demonstrated that the eight essential metals presented a stronger association with hypertension or blood pressure than the three nonessential metals, with magnesium or iron playing a major role. Single metal effects in BKMR models may indicate that blood magnesium or iron were independently associated with hypertension and may suggest that blood iron was independently associated with blood pressures. However, the magnitude of the overall associations in BKMR models appeared relatively modest, which may reflect that the associations with opposite trends from different metals were partially offset. These findings provided useful reference for associations between metal mixtures and hypertension or blood pressure, especially for essential metals.

Strengths and limitations

This study possessed a few advantages. First, the exposure of multiple metals were measured using whole blood sample, which may be a more suitable biomatrix for certain metals (e.g., iron and chromium) than other biofluids (e.g., plasma) [47]. Second, since the majority of included metals were essential metals, the study provided useful information on the less reported associations between essential metals and hypertension prevalence or blood pressure. Third, a variety of statistical methods were utilized for characterizing separate and combined effects of multiple metals exposure with the adjustment of many confounders, which will ensure the robustness of the findings. Potential multicollinearity and interactions among multiple metals would be overcome by the employment of mixture analysis methods, i.e., WQS regression and BKMR. This study also had several limitations. First of all, the causal relationship of multiple metals exposure with hypertension or blood pressure can’t be addressed in a cross-sectional investigation. Secondly, since the participants were enrolled from one health management center, external validations in independent populations are needed to generalize the findings. Third, metal levels in whole blood samples were measured by a previously established method, which included 11 metals. Other metals potentially associated with hypertension or blood pressure were not included and their effect may have been overlooked. Future studies with a more comprehensive coverage of metals are needed to fully elucidate the effect of metals on hypertension and blood pressure. Fourth, as the number of sample size is not big enough, the chance of false positive interactions will increase when stratification analysis is performed across many stratification factors, metals and outcomes. Finally, although a variety of covariates were adjusted in each model, potential confounding factors may not be fully considered.

Conclusions

In summary, the present study demonstrated positive associations between multiple metals (individually or collectively) and hypertension or blood pressure among general Chinese adults, with essential metals magnesium and/or iron as major contributors. Although external validations and prospective investigations are needed, it may provide useful population investigation evidence for associations between multiple metals and hypertension or blood pressures, especially for essential metals.
