Deep learning-based tooth axis estimation from 3D tooth crowns using quaternion representation and multi-loss optimization
Authors: Geunhye Kim, Sena Lee, Junghun Han, Eun-Hack Andrew Choi, Yongkyu Jin, Chooryung Judi Chung, Sejung Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTooth axis estimation plays a key role in various digital dental workflows, including orthodontic diagnosis and prosthetic design. In clinical practice, tooth axes are typically determined by jointly considering crown morphology and radiographic information; however, this process is subjective and time-consuming. In particular, anatomical tooth axes are defined based on root information, and cone-beam computed tomography is commonly used for this purpose. However, its routine use is limited by radiation exposure, cost, and workflow complexity. As an alternative, crown-based references such as the facial axis of the clinical crown have been used as auxiliary indicators of tooth orientation in clinical settings. In this study, we propose a deep learning–based framework for automatically estimating crown-based tooth axes using only geometric information from 3D tooth crown point clouds. The proposed method first translates each tooth to its center and applies absolute orientation alignment to normalize all samples into a common coordinate system. Rotation-based data augmentation is then applied to incorporate diverse pose variations. During inference, medial–distal orientation alignment is performed to ensure consistent directional alignment of each tooth. A quaternion-based rotation regression model is used to estimate the tooth axis by rotating a predefined initial axis, and a composite loss function is employed to jointly enforce numerical, geometric, and directional consistency. Experimental results on a controlled patient-level split show that the proposed method achieves an average angular error of 3.23 (standard deviation: 2.06), along with relatively consistent Chamfer distance measures. In addition, qualitative analysis indicates that the predicted tooth axes exhibit relatively consistent directional patterns at the full-arch level. These findings suggest that crown surface geometry, combined with alignment-based preprocessing, may be useful for approximating crown-based tooth axes. Rather than replacing anatomical tooth axes, the proposed framework aims to explore the feasibility of estimating clinically meaningful reference axes under limited information settings, and may serve as a supportive geometric reference for crown-based digital dentistry workflows.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Health care, Mathematics and computing, Medical research

Introduction

Tooth axis estimation plays a critical role in various digital dental workflows, including orthodontic diagnosis, prosthetic design, and occlusal analysis1,2. Accurate and consistent definition of the tooth axis directly influences treatment planning, appliance placement, and the reproducibility of treatment outcomes3. Clinically, the tooth axis is typically defined as the anatomical long axis connecting the center of the crown to the root apex, encompassing both crown and root information4,5. This anatomical tooth axis is commonly determined using root information obtained from cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT)6,7. Previous studies have focused on automatically estimating this anatomical tooth axis by reconstructing the full tooth structure, including the root, from CBCT data8–12. While such approaches can achieve high accuracy by directly incorporating root information, their practical use is often limited by factors such as radiation exposure, cost, and the complexity of data acquisition and processing13–15.

In contrast, 3D intraoral scans primarily capture crown geometry. From these scans, individual teeth are segmented, resulting in 3D tooth crown data that represent the geometry of single tooth crowns without root information8–10. In such settings, crown-based reference axes, such as the facial axis of the clinical crown (FACC), have been widely used to represent tooth orientation in clinical practice1,3. However, 3D tooth crown data obtained from scans often contain noise, artifacts, missing teeth, and irregular or misaligned tooth arrangements, which make tooth axis estimation more challenging2,7. In particular, manual definition of the tooth axis may lead to variability and inconsistency across operators1,3.

Despite these challenges, existing studies on crown-based tooth axes have primarily focused on defining or analyzing such axes, while the problem of automatic estimation has received relatively limited attention1,3. Moreover, even in studies addressing automatic tooth axis estimation, most approaches rely on CBCT data to reconstruct full tooth anatomy and estimate the anatomical axis8–12,16–18. As a result, prior work has mainly focused on either (1) manual axis definition or analysis of crown-based reference axes1,3 or (2) CBCT-based anatomical axis estimation and reconstruction8,10,11,16–18, and the problem of automatically estimating crown-based tooth axes using only crown data remains relatively underexplored18–20.

Motivated by this gap, this study addresses the problem of automatically estimating a crown-based tooth axis defined by clinical experts based on FACC, using 3D tooth crown data obtained from intraoral scans. Importantly, the tooth axis considered in this work is not intended to replace the anatomical tooth axis, but rather to serve as a clinically defined reference derived from crown morphology, which can be used as a supportive guide in tooth orientation tasks.

From a computational perspective, tooth axis estimation involves several technical challenges, including rotation representation, pose variability, and optimization stability21,22. In particular, rotation representation in 3D tooth crown data is a critical factor in model training. Conventional representations such as Euler angles suffer from discontinuities, rotation-order dependency, and gimbal lock issues, which can hinder stable learning21,23. To address these limitations, we adopt a quaternion-based representation that enables continuous and stable rotation estimation22,24. In addition, 3D tooth crown data obtained from scans exhibit significant pose variability depending on scanning conditions and alignment states25,26. To address this, we apply absolute orientation alignment to normalize all teeth into a common coordinate system27. Rotation-based data augmentation is then applied to the aligned data to incorporate diverse pose variations during training28. Furthermore, medial–distal orientation alignment is applied during inference and evaluation to ensure consistent directional alignment across samples.

Moreover, minimizing only numerical rotation error is insufficient for accurate tooth axis estimation21,23. Therefore, we introduce a composite loss function that jointly considers numerical rotation consistency, geometric correspondence, and directional agreement29,30. Based on these components, we propose a deep learning framework that directly estimates crown-based tooth axes from 3D tooth crown point clouds25,31. The proposed method integrates quaternion-based rotation regression, alignment-based preprocessing, rotation data augmentation, and a composite loss formulation22,27,28.

In summary, this study explicitly formulates the problem of automatic crown-based tooth axis estimation from crown data and presents a learning-based framework to address it. Unlike prior approaches that rely on manual definition or CBCT-based anatomical reconstruction, this work provides a method for estimating crown-based tooth axes directly from 3D tooth crown data, thereby exploring the feasibility of crown-based tooth axis estimation from crown-only data.

Results

In this section, we present the quantitative and qualitative evaluation results of the proposed tooth axis estimation framework. First, the directional precision of the estimated tooth axis is evaluated for each tooth type using the average Angle Precision (aAP) metric. In addition, the angular discrepancy between the estimated tooth axis and the ground-truth tooth axis is quantitatively analyzed using Angular Difference (AD), while the Chamfer Distance (CD) is used to evaluate the crown surface alignment error induced by the estimated tooth axis.

Next, an ablation study is conducted to analyze the impact of the main components of the proposed framework on tooth axis estimation performance. Specifically, we investigate performance variations with respect to different loss function combinations, the effect of rotation-based data augmentation factors, and performance comparisons among various deep learning models for tooth axis estimation. The results of the ablation study are quantitatively evaluated using AD and CD.

Finally, qualitative visualization results are presented to compare the estimated tooth axis with the ground-truth tooth axes on the input crown geometry. Specifically, tooth axis obtained by applying the ground-truth quaternion and the predicted quaternion to a predefined initial axis , respectively, are visualized on the crown surface mesh. This visualization enables an intuitive comparison between the estimated and ground-truth tooth axes and provides insights into the geometric consistency of the predicted tooth axis with respect to the input crown shape.

Discussion

In this study, we addressed the problem of estimating a crown-based tooth axis using only 3D tooth crown. This study is grounded in the clinically established concept of the FACC and aims to explore whether meaningful tooth axis estimation can be achieved solely from crown morphology.

The experimental results showed that the proposed method achieved an average angular error of approximately 3, suggesting that crown surface geometry contains sufficient structural information to infer tooth axis orientation. However, these results were obtained under a controlled experimental setting based on a fixed patient-level split. Therefore, the reported performance should be interpreted as an indication of methodological feasibility rather than an absolute performance benchmark.

One of the key components of this study is the alignment strategy applied to the crown data. In the proposed pipeline, each tooth is first translated to its center point, followed by absolute-orientation alignment to the global coordinate system. This process normalizes all samples into a consistent reference frame, providing a stable basis for learning. Subsequently, rotation-based data augmentation is applied in this aligned space, allowing the model to learn diverse orientation variations of the same crown geometry. In contrast, during inference and evaluation, medial–distal alignment is applied to normalize the in-plane orientation based on the anatomical mesial–distal direction of the crown. This provides a consistent reference for comparing tooth axes while preserving anatomical interpretability. The separation of alignment strategies between training and inference plays an important role in ensuring both learning stability and evaluation consistency. Furthermore, the distribution of rotation differences between medial–distal alignment and absolute alignment was analyzed, revealing that most rotations fall within a limited angular range. This observation was used to define the range for rotation-based data augmentation. This strategy is meaningful in that it reflects the actual distribution of orientation variations observed in the data, rather than relying on arbitrary augmentation settings. The augmentation experiments also highlight the importance of accounting for pose variability in crown data. Since intraoral scans may exhibit diverse orientations depending on acquisition conditions, applying random rotations improves robustness to such variations. Notably, this augmentation strategy does not introduce new geometric structures but instead reorients existing ones; therefore, the observed performance improvement should be interpreted as enhanced robustness to pose variation rather than increased generalization in terms of shape diversity.

From a modeling perspective, DGCNN was adopted as the backbone to learn geometric representations of the crown. The experimental results obtained using this model showed stable performance, suggesting that dynamic graph-based local neighborhood learning may be effective in capturing the geometric characteristics of crown surfaces. In particular, the ability to model local geometric features such as curvature, cusps, and boundary structures aligns well with the nature of tooth crown data. However, it should be noted that DGCNN represents only one possible architectural choice, and the performance and generalization behavior may vary when alternative models are employed.

Methodologically, the combination of quaternion-based rotation regression and composite loss functions plays a central role in the proposed framework. The quaternion representation mitigates discontinuity issues inherent in Euler angle representations, enabling more stable learning of rotations. The integration of , , and allows the model to simultaneously consider numerical accuracy, geometric consistency, and directional alignment. While each individual loss term provides limited improvement, their combined use results in overall stable performance, indicating complementary contributions.

For qualitative analysis, Teeth3DS+ was used to examine the model’s behavior on external data. Since this dataset provides ground-truth crown segmentation but does not include tooth axis annotations, quantitative evaluation is not feasible. Instead, we visually analyzed how the predicted tooth axes are arranged at the full-arch level. The results showed that the predicted axes tend to form a relatively consistent directional pattern across the dental arch, suggesting that the model captures meaningful orientation cues from crown geometry.

In addition, to evaluate whether the results obtained from the fixed patient split are dependent on a specific data configuration, five-fold cross-validation was conducted at the patient level. The results showed no substantial variation in performance across folds, and no statistically significant differences were observed. This indicates that similar performance trends are maintained across different patient partitions, suggesting limited sensitivity to data splitting.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the quantitative evaluation in this study was conducted using a dataset collected from a single institution, and additional validation using multi-institutional datasets with annotated tooth axes was not performed. Second, the external dataset lacks tooth axis annotations, which inherently limits the ability to assess quantitative generalization performance on external data. Moreover, since this study primarily focused on automatic crown-based tooth axis estimation, tooth segmentation and mesial–distal landmark annotation were manually performed by experienced clinicians. Future work will aim to automate these processes and develop a fully automated framework that directly processes raw 3D dental scan data and automatically estimates tooth axes without manual intervention. Furthermore, while DGCNN was used as the backbone in this study, the choice of network architecture may influence performance, and alternative models could yield different results. In addition, although five-fold cross-validation was conducted to evaluate robustness with respect to data partitioning, repeated experiments with different random initialization seeds were not performed. Therefore, a more detailed analysis of variability arising from model initialization and data sampling may be required in future work.

Future work should include comparative studies using diverse model architectures, as well as extended validation using multi-institutional datasets with annotated tooth axes. Incorporating patient-level metadata may further improve axis estimation performance. In addition, the proposed framework could be extended toward a more comprehensive system that enables automatic tooth axis alignment at the full-arch level.

Finally, it is important to clarify that the tooth axis considered in this study is a crown-based reference axis rather than an anatomical axis. The primary objective of this study is to investigate the feasibility of automatically estimating clinically meaningful reference axes under constrained information settings.

Methods

Tooth axis estimation process

We estimate a crown-based tooth axis from crown-only three-dimensional data using a pipeline that combines quaternion-based rotation prediction. First, to clearly separate individual tooth crowns from surrounding structures such as the gingiva, tooth segmentation was manually performed by clinicians. For each crown, mesial and distal reference points are manually annotated by clinical experts based on crown morphology, and these two points are used to perform rigid alignment, thereby establishing a consistent local coordinate system.

The aligned crown mesh is uniformly sampled into 2,048 points and used as input to a deep learning model. In this study, a Dynamic Graph Convolutional Neural Network (DGCNN)29 is adopted as the backbone network for learning crown-based tooth axis representations. The model extracts features based on the intrinsic geometric characteristics of the crown and predicts a quaternion representing the rotational transformation required for tooth axis estimation. The point encoder is implemented using a DGCNN backbone consisting of four EdgeConv blocks with nearest neighbors. The output channel dimensions of the four blocks are 64, 64, 128, and 256, respectively. These features are concatenated and projected to a 1024-dimensional global feature vector, which is then fed into a fully connected quaternion decoder with a 1024–512–256–4 architecture. The predicted quaternion is applied to a fixed unit initial axis , and the final tooth axis vector is obtained through rotation38. Each crown is processed, and the tooth axis is estimated by reflecting the intrinsic geometric characteristics of each tooth based solely on crown surface geometry. To improve robustness to pose variability, rotation-based data augmentation is applied during training.

Specifically, based on annotated clinical data, the distribution of rotational differences between mesial–distal orientation alignment and absolute orientation alignment was analyzed. Rotation angles are sampled from this distribution and applied to the absolutely aligned tooth crowns in the order of . This data-driven augmentation strategy preserves geometric consistency while effectively reflecting variations in crown orientation observed in real clinical data.

Loss functions

To improve the accuracy and stability of crown-based tooth axis estimation, this study employs a combination of complementary loss functions that capture different aspects of tooth axis consistency. One loss term minimizes the numerical discrepancy between the predicted and ground-truth rotational representations, thereby reducing errors in quaternion space associated with tooth axis estimation. Another loss term enforces geometric consistency by minimizing the spatial distance between crown point sets transformed using the predicted tooth axis and the ground-truth tooth axis. In addition, an angular similarity loss evaluates the directional agreement between the predicted and ground-truth tooth axis using cosine similarity, encouraging consistent and stable tooth axis estimation across samples.

Chamfer distance

In this study, we employ the bidirectional CD to quantitatively evaluate the geometric discrepancies arising from tooth axis estimation errors. While AD measures directional consistency of the estimated tooth axis in vector space, CD directly reflects how errors in the predicted tooth axis propagate to geometric misalignment on the crown surface40.

To isolate the effect of tooth axis estimation errors, each pair of crown point clouds is aligned to a common canonical frame by applying inverse rotations derived from the predicted quaternion and the ground-truth quaternion , respectively. After this alignment, any remaining point-wise spatial discrepancies represent geometric inconsistencies induced by tooth axis prediction errors, rather than differences in translation or initial pose.

To report distances in physically interpretable units, we apply per-sample isotropic scale normalization following common practices in prior studies on 3D shape analysis and point-cloud processing46. This normalization removes the influence of inter-tooth size variability, allowing CD to focus on geometric discrepancies induced by tooth axis estimation rather than absolute scale differences. By multiplying all point coordinates by , the crown point clouds are expressed in millimeter units while preserving their shape and relative geometry.

Although both point clouds are aligned to the same canonical frame, inaccuracies in the predicted tooth axis lead to point-wise correspondence errors on the crown surface, which are effectively captured by CD. We compute CD using both the L1 norm (Manhattan distance) and the L2 norm (Euclidean distance)47. For CD-L2, squared distances are used only during nearest-neighbor search, and the square root is taken before averaging so that all reported values correspond to true Euclidean distances in millimeters rather than squared units. Accordingly, we report CD-L1 (mm) and CD-L2 (mm) as complementary metrics that characterize crown surface geometric misalignment caused by tooth axis estimation errors.

Dataset

In this study, two 3D tooth crown datasets were used. First, a clinical dataset was collected at Yonsei University Gangnam Severance Hospital (Seoul, Republic of Korea), consisting of three-dimensional scans from 100 patients. Second, the 3DTeethLand Challenge dataset (Teeth3DS+)48, released as part of MICCAI 2024, was utilized. This public dataset provides ground-truth tooth crown segmentation, but does not include annotations for tooth axes.

The clinical dataset was randomly split at the patient level, ensuring that data from the same patient were not included across different subsets to prevent data leakage. The dataset was divided into training, validation, and test sets with a ratio of 8:1:1. A total of 2,740 teeth were included, comprising 2,195 teeth for training, 273 for validation, and 272 for testing. For the training set, rotation-based data augmentation was applied with an augmentation, resulting in 39,510 training samples. This augmentation was applied only to the training set, while the validation and test sets remained unchanged. Unless otherwise specified, all quantitative evaluations were conducted on the test set of the clinical dataset.

To reflect a wide range of clinical scenarios, both natural teeth and teeth with crown prostheses were included. In other words, various crown geometries observed in real clinical environments were incorporated into the training data. However, cases containing non-crown structures, such as implant screws that could distort crown geometry during the scanning process, were excluded. Except for these limited cases, most teeth were included in the analysis.

Ground-truth tooth axes, mesial–distal landmarks, and tooth center points were manually annotated by experienced dental clinicians. The tooth axis was defined based on crown morphology following clinically established guidelines, and consistent criteria were applied across all teeth. Mesial–distal landmarks were defined to reflect the anatomical mesial and distal directions of each crown and were assigned based on visual inspection of the 3D scan data. The tooth center point was defined as a representative point selected by clinicians, indicating where the tooth axis should pass, and was used as the origin (0, 0, 0) of the local coordinate system for each tooth. All annotations were performed according to clinically defined guidelines, ensuring that consistent criteria were applied across the entire dataset. In this study, these guideline-based annotations were used as the ground-truth. Although quantitative inter-observer variability was not evaluated, annotation quality was controlled through consistent guidelines and repeated verification. Tooth segmentation was performed by clinicians to extract only the crown region, excluding gingival tissues. Tooth boundaries were defined based on crown morphology.

In addition, five-fold cross-validation was conducted at the patient level to further evaluate the stability and generalization of the proposed method with respect to data partitioning. The primary quantitative results of this study were obtained based on a fixed dataset split constructed via random patient-level partitioning. However, results based on a single split may be influenced by a particular data configuration; therefore, additional analysis under different data partitions is required to assess performance consistency. Accordingly, the full set of 100 patients was randomly partitioned at the patient level into five folds, ensuring that no patient appeared in more than one fold. Within each fold, the dataset was further divided into training, validation, and test sets with a ratio of 8:1:1. The same 18-fold rotation-based data augmentation was applied to the training data in each fold. This setup allowed us to analyze performance trends and variability under different data partitioning conditions. The cross-validation experiments were not intended to replace the fixed split evaluation but were conducted as a supplementary analysis to verify that the proposed method is not excessively dependent on a specific data split and demonstrates stable performance across different partitions.

The Teeth3DS+ dataset was not used for quantitative training or evaluation due to the absence of ground-truth tooth axis annotations. Instead, it was used to assess whether the proposed model can infer consistent and anatomically plausible tooth axes based solely on crown geometry in the absence of ground-truth axis information.

All experiments were conducted in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and informed consent was obtained from all participants and/or their legal guardians. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Yonsei University Gangnam Severance Hospital (IRB No. 3-2024-0442).

Implementation and training details

All the experiments were conducted in a Docker-based Linux environment using Python and PyTorch. Training was performed on an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU, with Automatic Mixed Precision49 enabled to improve efficiency.

The Adam optimizer50 was used with a fixed learning rate of . The training was performed for up to 500 epochs, with early stopping (patience = 200 epochs) to prevent overfitting.

Each tooth crown was treated as an individual sample and processed independently by the model. Mini-batches were constructed by randomly sampling individual tooth crowns, with a batch size of 80. The average training time was approximately 2–3 days.

Data alignment

Absolute orientation alignment

Each tooth is represented by three mutually orthogonal intrinsic tooth axis vectors, , , and , which together define a local tooth axis coordinate system. In this study, these intrinsic tooth axis vectors are derived from clinically annotated tooth axes provided by experienced dental experts. Specifically, the expert-defined tooth axis are converted into a quaternion representation, which serves as the ground-truth quaternion . The intrinsic tooth axis coordinate system is then obtained by applying to the standard orthonormal basis vectors of the global reference coordinate system.

Prior to orientation alignment, each tooth crown surface mesh is translated such that its geometric center is located at the tooth center (0, 0, 0). This centering operation removes inter-tooth positional variability and ensures that all subsequent orientation alignment procedures are applied consistently with respect to the crown-centered coordinate system.

The global reference coordinate system is defined by the standard orthonormal basis vectors , , and . In this study, the global vector represents the mesial–distal direction, the global vector represents the buccal direction, and the global vector represents the crown–root direction.

After crown centering, a rigid rotation is computed such that the intrinsic tooth orientation coordinate system , derived from the ground-truth quaternion, is aligned with the global reference vector set 27. This rotation is applied to the single-tooth mesh, and this procedure is referred to as absolute-orientation alignment.
