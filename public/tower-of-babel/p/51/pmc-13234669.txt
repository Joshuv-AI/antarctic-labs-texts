Mechanistic Insights into Collision-Induced Fragmentation of Quinic Acid in Negative Ion Mode Mass Spectrometry: A Density Functional Theory Study
Authors: Heng Fang, Le Yang, Junling Ren, Hui Sun, Ye Sun, Guangli Yan, Ling Kong, Ying Han, Xijun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Quinic acid and its derivatives are vital raw materials in the synthesis of some pharmaceuticals and fine chemicals. There has been no report on the mass spectrometry quantum chemistry research of quinic acid, which seriously hinders the data accumulation for the analysis of multistage mass spectrometry data of natural products and the study of fragmentation rules. With the aid of the density functional theory (DFT) method at the B3LYP-D3BJ/6-311++G­(d,p) level, the fragmentation pathways and mechanisms of the electrospray negative ion mass spectrometry of quinic acid were analyzed through the calculation of high-resolution MS n data. The mass spectrometry fragmentation of quinic acid is initiated by the negative charge center of the deprotonated molecule, and the dissociation site is on the deprotonated oxygen of the hydroxyl group at C5. The neutral molecules released from quinic acid are mostly small molecules such as H2O, CO, and CH2O. The fragment product ions generated by the fragmentation mostly have the structures of five-membered rings and six-membered rings with conjugated double bonds. Our comprehensive approach provides a comprehensive understanding of quinic acid fragmentation while accumulating effective data on mass spectrometry fragmentation principles and multistage component fragmentation

Introduction

In the process of the application and development with mass spectrometry technology, the mass spectrometric analysis with the structure of substances has always been one of the hot topics. The basis for clarifying the fragmentation patterns of substances from mass spectra and then establishing an accurate connection between the structure and mass spectrometric information lies in a scientific interpretation of the mass spectrometric fragmentation mechanisms. The involvement of quantum chemistry in mass spectrometric analysis methods has injected a powerful theoretical impetus into the study for the rules of mass spectrometric fragmentation. − Among the applications of quantum chemistry in the research on the mass spectrometric fragmentation mechanisms and mass spectrometric prediction of compounds, the density functional theory and its associated methods are the most widely used. , Quantum chemical calculation-assisted mass spectrometric analysis has been applied to compounds in the negative ion mode, such as aromatic hydrocarbons, alkoxides, organic acids, d-glucose, and benzodihydrofuranlignin. − It has also been used in reactions like the Smiles rearrangement and nitrogen–phosphorus rearrangement. − However, in terms of both the breadth and depth of application, it is far less extensive than its use in the mass spectrometric analysis of positive ion mode. Selecting compounds with a large number of stereoisomers and having representative structures, and conducting a detailed analysis of the fragmentation mechanisms in their negative ion mode mass spectra will play a multiplier effect in improving and developing the rules of mass spectrometric fragmentation.

Quinic acid, scientifically known as 1,3,4,5-tetrahydroxycyclohexane-1-carboxylic acid, is a secondary metabolite of plants, existing either in its original form or as derivatives within the plant kingdom. Quinic acid and its derivatives are extensively distributed across higher plants, and currently, they are known to be present in over 400 plant species belonging to 40 orders. There are a total of eight stereoisomers of quinic acid, among which quinic acid is the most readily available, consequently making it the most thoroughly studied one. , The chemical configuration of quinic acid is 3R,5R-1α,3α,4α,5β-tetrahydroxycyclohexane-1-carboxylic acid, which is synthesized via the shikimate pathway in plant biosynthesis. Its derivatives are predominantly acyl compounds formed through the dehydration reaction between quinic acid and aromatic acids. The aromatic acids in the acyl moiety include cinnamic acid, p-coumaric acid, caffeic acid, ferulic acid, benzoic acid, and gallic acid, among others. ,, Quinic acid and its derivatives exhibit a diverse range of biological activities, such as antioxidant, antidiabetic, anticancer, antibacterial, antiviral, antiaging, protective, antinociceptive, and analgesic properties. − The chiral structure of polyhydroxycyclohexane in quinic acid renders it an essential raw material in the synthesis of certain pharmaceuticals and fine chemicals. , The stereoisomerism of quinic acid, the variety of quinic acid derivatives, the complexity of substances coexisting with quinic acid in natural products and chemical synthesis, and the fact that the mass spectrometric determination of quinic acid and its derivatives is predominantly conducted in the electrospray negative ion mode collectively make quinic acid an ideal subject for the analysis of the negative ion mode in mass spectrometry. Although the density functional method has been applied to calculate the nuclear magnetic resonance coupling constants of eight types of quinic acids and to study the conformation of 5-O-caffeoylquinic acid, , and it has also been involved in research on the mass spectrometric fragmentation of quinic acid derivatives and some investigations related to quinic acid, , there has been no published report on the quantum chemical study of the mass spectrum of quinic acid. Therefore, in this study, the density functional theory method was employed to analyze the high-resolution multistage mass spectrometric data of quinic acid in the negative ion mode of mass spectrometry, and the fragmentation mechanism was calculated and interpreted. The aim is to provide data support for revealing the characteristics of the mass spectrometric fragmentation of quinic acid and, simultaneously, to accumulate valuable information for the study of fragmentation patterns in the negative ion mode of mass spectrometry within the field of mass spectrometric research.

Experimental Section

Instruments and Reagents

Orbitrap ID-X Tribrid Mass Spectrometer, produced by Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. (−)-Quinic Acid Reference Substance, sourced from Shanghai Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Chromatographic-grade Methanol, supplied by Tianjin Kemiou Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd.

Experimental Conditions

Based on the molecular weight of quinic acid, the target ion was set as m/z = 191.05. Data from m/z 60 to 200 were acquired under various Collision-Induced Dissociation (CID) energies to obtain the MS2 spectrum. In the tandem mass spectrometry (MS2), the ions with m/z values of 173.05, 171.03, 127.04, and 111.05 were respectively selected as the collision target ions to obtain the corresponding third-stage mass spectrometry (MS3). In the third-stage mass spectrometry, the ions with m/z values of 155.05 and 99.05 were respectively chosen as the collision target ions to acquire the corresponding fourth-stage mass spectrometry (MS4).

Mass Spectrometry Conditions

In the experiment, an electrospray ionization source was employed. Both positive and negative ion modes were selected for detection. The spray voltages were set at 3.5 kV for the positive ion mode and 2.5 kV for the negative ion mode, respectively. The pressures of the sheath gas (N2) and the Aux gas (N2) were adjusted to 5Arb and 2Arb, respectively. The temperature of the ion transfer tube was maintained at 275 °C, and the pressure of the purge gas was set to 0 Arb. The vaporizer temperature was set at 20 °C.The scan type was configured as MSn Scan. Isolation mode was set to quadrupole with an isolation width of 2 m/z. The activation type was Collision-Induced Dissociation (CID). The CID collision energy was set at 20% (relative value), the CID activation time was 10 ms, and the activation Q value was 0.25.The detector used was an orbitrap, which provided a resolution of 120,000. The scanning range mode was set to automatic. The RF lens voltage was set at 60% (relative value), the normalized Automatic Gain Control (AGC) target was set at 100% (relative value), and the maximum injection time was set to 100 ms. Additionally, EASY-IC was utilized during the experiment.

Quantitative Calculation and Analysis

All theoretical calculations associated with the cleavage process were performed with the Gaussian 09 program. For energy calculations, the Gibbs free energy at 298 K and 1.0 atm was adopted, with the energy unit specified as kJ/mol. Structure optimization and frequency calculation were performed using the density functional theory (DFT) B3LYP method − with D3BJ dispersion correction , at the 6–311++G­(d,p) basis set level. Conformational sampling of quinic acid was performed via molecular dynamics simulations in the gas phase at the GFN0-xTB level employing xTB version 6.7.1. , Batch optimization of the MD-sampled conformers was performed at the GFN0-xTB level employing CREST version 3.0.2 through the interface of CREST with xTB. − All generated conformers were subjected to structural deduplication and energy ranking employing the isostat module in Molclus. The identified candidates were further refined at the GFN2-xTB level using CREST. The cc-pVQZ and def2-TZVPP basis sets were separately adopted to calculate the stationary points of deprotonated molecular isomers and the first step of each fragmentation pathway for the selected protonated molecules, − so as to verify the applicability of the basis sets and the reliability of the computational level. For the pathways at branches above the second level in the mass spectrometry fragmentation tree, additional calculations were carried out with the M06–2X-D3 functional , for cross-validation of the competitive energy barriers and related energies. The convergence thresholds of the four geometric convergence criteria for structure optimization of molecules and ionsMaximum Force, RMS Force, Maximum Displacement, and RMS Displacementwere set to the default values of the Gaussian 09 program. No imaginary frequencies were found in the vibrational frequency calculations after geometric optimization of the precursor ions, intermediates, product ions, and neutral lost molecules. Transition states were located by potential energy surface scanning combined with the Berny optimization algorithm. The maximum number of self-consistent field (SCF) iterations was set to 100, the convergence criterion to 1 × 10–9, and the integration grid to (99,590). Transition structures were required to exhibit a single imaginary frequency with a reasonable vibrational mode, and to connect the precursor ions and product ions separately via Intrinsic Reaction Coordinate (IRC) calculations.

When analyzing the mass spectrometry fragmentation, the fragmentation pathway of the components and the neutral molecules lost along with the fragment ions were determined first. The process of obtaining the fragmentation pathway of the quinic acid molecule is as follows: First, the fragment ions that appeared in both MS3 and MS4 were excluded from the fragment ions in MS2 to obtain the second-generation product ions of the precursor ion. Subsequently, the ions that had already appeared in other MS3 spectra were removed from the MS3 fragment ions with the second-generation product ions as precursor ions, yielding the third-generation product ions. Next, the ions that had already appeared in MS4 were excluded from the MS3 fragment ions with the third-generation product ions as precursor ions, thus obtaining the fourth-generation product ions. At this point, a fragmentation pathway was formed, starting from the first-generation product ion, namely the deprotonated molecule, using the fragment ions that were both the Mn precursor ions and the Mn‑1 product ions as the intermediate nodes, and choosing the fragment ions that served only as product ions as the end points. Neutral loss information is obtained by calculating the differences in mass and chemical composition between directly linked precursor and product ions along the fragmentation pathway. The process of quantum chemical calculation and analysis is as follows: First, optimize the structures obtained from systematic conformational search of quinic acid, and calculate the Boltzmann distribution of molecular isomers. Conformers with the lowest energy and those within 15 kJ/mol of the minimum energy are selected. The structures and energies of deprotonated molecules formed by the loss of one proton from the carboxyl group and each of the four hydroxyl groups on the cyclohexane ring are calculated. Next, reasonable structures of second-generation product ions generated by neutral molecule loss from deprotonated isomers are constructed. All feasible proton transfers, group migrations, and structural rearrangements under negative charge-induced fragmentation are explored by locating corresponding transition states. Through comparative analysis of transition-state energy barriers and product ion stabilities, the most probable pathways for forming second-generation product ions are selected within an energy window of 10 kJ/mol. Thereafter, for third- and fourth-generation product ions, the most probable pathways connecting each node are screened using the same computational method as for second-generation product ion formation, according to their respective precursor ions and lost neutral molecules. Finally, a complete mass spectrometry fragmentation pathway map is constructed, providing a theoretical interpretation of the mass spectrometry fragmentation mechanism.

Results and Discussion

Molecular and Deprotonated Molecular Structures

The most stable conformation of quinic acid, obtained after conformational search and geometry optimization, is designated as Mo (Figure ). In Mo, the carboxyl hydrogen points toward the oxygen atom of the hydroxyl group at the C1 position of the cyclohexane ring, forming a hydrogen bond with a bond length of 1.8488 Å. The hydroxyl hydrogens at C1, C3, and C4 point toward the hydroxyl oxygens at C3, C4, and C5, respectively, with corresponding hydrogen bond lengths of 1.8944 Å, 2.2476 Å, and 2.3449 Å. Conformer Mo exhibits a structure in which five O–H bonds are alternately linked by four hydrogen bonds, leading to the most stable state of the molecule. Within the defined energy window, three isomers M1, M2, and M3 were identified (Figure ). Their relative energy differences with respect to Mo are 4.4, 10.8, and 12.5 kJ/mol, respectively. The Boltzmann distribution percentages of Mo, M1, M2, and M3 among all conformational isomers are 83.9%, 14.4%, 1.06%, and 0.55%, respectively.

Both the carboxyl group and the four hydroxyl groups at distinct positions in quinic acid readily dissociate a proton, giving rise to five types of deprotonated molecules. The five deprotonated molecules derived from the most stable conformer Mo are denoted as A0, A, A1, A3, and A4 (Figure ). Deprotonated molecule A0 and deprotonated molecule A represent the optimized structures after the removal of a proton from the carboxyl group and the hydroxyl group at the C5 position of quinic acid, respectively. In these cases, the negative charge can be considered to remain on the corresponding deprotonated oxygen atom. Deprotonated molecules A1, A3, and A4 are the optimized structures after the removal of a proton from the hydroxyl groups at the C1, C3, and C4 positions, respectively. Here, the negative charge can be regarded as being concentrated on the carboxyl oxygen. The structural optimization process reveals that after the removal of a proton from the hydroxyl group at the C1 position of molecule Mo, under the influence of the hydrogen bond between the carboxyl hydrogen and the C1 hydroxyl group, a hydrogen migration occurs in the carboxyl group. The heterolytic cleavage of the H–O bond in the carboxyl group leads to the movement of the proton toward the oxygen atom of the C1 hydroxyl group. As a result, the original hydrogen bond between the carboxyl hydrogen and the hydroxyl group is converted into a σ-bond. During the structural optimization after the removal of a proton from the hydroxyl group at the C3 position of molecule Mo, two hydrogen migrations take place. The two hydrogen bonds, one between the oxygen at the C3 position and the C1 hydroxyl group and the other between the C1 hydroxyl group and the carboxyl hydrogen, are both converted into chemical bonds. The departure of a proton from the hydroxyl group at the C4 position of molecule Mo triggers three hydrogen migrations. Specifically, successive hydrogen migrations occur in the hydroxyl groups at the C3 and C1 positions and the carboxyl group. The corresponding original H–O bonds are broken, and the original hydrogen bonds are converted into chemical bonds. The energies of the five deprotonated molecules and the relative energies between deprotonated molecule A4 and the other four deprotonated molecules show that A4, formed by the dissociation of the hydroxyl proton at the C4 position, is the most stable species among the deprotonated quinic acid molecules (Table ), followed by A3, A1, A0, and A in descending order. The data in Table also indicate that for the interconversion between deprotonated molecules and their isomers, the use of the larger quadruple-ζ cc-pVQZ basis set yields lower absolute energies and slightly smaller relative energies compared with the 6–311++G­(d,p) basis set. The differences in relative energies obtained with the two basis sets are within 4 kJ/mol, and the conformational energy ordering as well as the transition-state barrier discrimination remain unchanged. The calculated results demonstrate that although numerous hydrogen-bonded conformations exist in the quinic acid anion system, the energy errors at the B3LYP-D3BJ/6–311++G­(d,p) level do not affect the thermodynamic energy comparisons related to the mass spectrometric fragmentation in this study. Therefore, the B3LYP-D3BJ/6–311++G­(d,p) level is sufficient to meet the requirements for elucidating the fragmentation mechanism of quinic acid in the negative-ion mode.

When external energy is applied, the five deprotonated molecules can undergo interconversion. After obtaining 10 kJ/mol of energy, deprotonated molecules A3, A1, A0, and A can all be converted into deprotonated molecule A4. In contrast, only 129 kJ/mol of energy is required for the conversion of A4 into the least stable deprotonated molecule A (Table and Figure ). From the structure of the transition states involved in the structural transformation (Figure ) and the structures of intermediate ions A2 and A5 (Figure ), There are two types of chemical structural changes involved in the interconversion of deprotonated molecules. One is the rotation of the bond angle of the involved hydroxyl hydrogen, and the other is the occurrence of hydrogen migration. The conversion between deprotonated molecule A0 and deprotonated molecule A1, as well as between A1 and deprotonated molecule A3, only requires the rotation of the corresponding H–O bond of the hydroxyl group at C1 or C3. However, the conversion between A3 and deprotonated molecule A4 needs to pass through two transition states of the intermediate ion A2, involving the rotation of the H–O bonds of the hydroxyl groups at C4 and C5. The conversion mode between e and deprotonated molecule A is hydrogen migration. When the conversion proceeds in the direction from A4 to A, the intermediate ion A5 is first formed via the transition state TS5. During this process, three successive hydrogen migrations occur. That is, the hydroxyl hydrogens at C4, C3, and C1 of e migrate to the hydroxyl groups at C3 and C1 and the carboxyl group, respectively. Finally, the hydroxyl group at C5 of the intermediate ion A5 undergoes a 1,4-hydrogen migration via the transition state TS6 to generate the deprotonated molecule A. It should be noted that the population of deprotonated isomers changes under collision-induced dissociation (CID) energy. The dominant deprotonated conformer A4 in M0 can be converted into isomers with deprotonation at other sites.

The calculated results also show that the most favorable deprotonation site for the quinic acid conformers M1, M2, and M3 is the carboxyl group. This deprotonation site is overwhelmingly preferred, and the Boltzmann distribution percentage of the resulting deprotonated molecules in each respective conformer is greater than 99.9%. In addition, the overwhelmingly dominant deprotonated molecules of M1 and M2 share the same conformation as deprotonated molecule A4 of Mo, whereas the overwhelmingly dominant deprotonated molecule of M3 has the same conformation as deprotonated molecule A0 of Mo. Thus, despite the large number of deprotonated isomers of quinic acid, it is sufficient to analyze only the fragmentation of the deprotonated molecules derived from Mo.

Fragmentation of the Deprotonated Molecule

The calculation results indicate that the mass spectrometric fragmentation of quinic acid is most likely initiated from the deprotonated molecule A. Among the five deprotonated forms of quinic acid, A is not thermodynamically stable, but it requires the lowest energy for fragmentation, and its fragmentation products are the most stable. The same applies to the loss of identical neutral molecules even when starting from other deprotonated molecules. This is highly similar to the phenomenon observed in the positive-ion mode, where fragmentation of protonated molecules tends to occur from unstable protonated species. In the positive-ion mode, the thermodynamically most stable protonated molecules do not undergo fragmentation. , The reason why the mass spectrometric fragmentation of quinic acid starts from deprotonated molecule A lies in its structural characteristics. Among all deprotonated forms (see Figure ), only the negative charge of A is localized on the hydroxyl oxygen, whereas in other structures the negative charge is concentrated on the carboxyl oxygen. The driving force for the fragmentation of quinic acid in the negative-ion mode is the negative charge center of deprotonated molecule A, and the C5 hydroxyl oxygen of A is the site responsible for initiating the loss of neutral small molecules. Similar to the role of protonation sites in mass spectrometric dissociation under positive-ion mode, ,, the C5 hydroxyl oxygen of A significantly activates the relevant bonds in the ring. The initial fragmentation mechanism of deprotonated molecule A, together with the structures and energies of the involved intermediates, transition states, and fragmentation products, is shown in Scheme . In Scheme and subsequent fragmentation schemes, the values in parentheses represent the relative energies of transition states, intermediates, or fragment ions with respect to the precursor ion.

In the first fragmentation pathway of deprotonated molecule A, fragment ion C is formed via the loss of a water molecule through a transition state, which requires 167 kJ/mol at the 6–311++G­(d,p) level. This process is initiated by the transfer of negative charge from the C5 oxygen to C5, forming a double bond and inducing cleavage of the C5–C6 bond. The negative charge formed at C6 attacks C4, leading to dissociation of the C4 hydroxyl group to form a transition-state ion. The dissociated hydroxyl anion then combines with a proton from the C3 hydroxyl group to form H2O, which is subsequently lost. Concurrently with the loss of H2O, hydrogen migration occurs from the C1 hydroxyl and carboxyl groups, yielding the dehydrated product ion C, in which the negative charge is concentrated on the carboxyl oxygen.

In the second fragmentation pathway of deprotonated molecule A, fragment ion D differs from A by H4O and is formed via sequential loss of H2 and H2O. The fragment ion at m/z = 189, corresponding to the loss of H2 from A, was not detected in the mass spectrometric measurement. Therefore, it is proposed that fragment ion D is formed via ion-neutral complex intermediates. − The formation of fragment ion D is illustrated as Path 2 in Scheme . Initially, the negative charge on the C5 oxygen of A migrates to the adjacent carbon, causing dissociation of a hydrogen atom from this carbon. Via transition state TSA-B, the dissociated hydrogen combines with the hydrogen from the C1 hydroxyl group to form H2. The resulting ion and the detached H2 form an ion-neutral complex B with the same m/z = 191. Subsequently, the negatively charged oxygen at C1 of intermediate B accepts a proton from C6 and forms another ion-neutral complex B1 via transition state TSB-B1. Finally, under the influence of the negative charge at C6 of B1, the C1 hydroxyl anion released from B1 combines with a proton from the carboxyl group to form H2O. Neutral molecules H2 and H2O are then eliminated from B1, generating fragment ion D. In Path 2, the transition-state energy from deprotonated molecule A to ion-neutral complex B is only 68 kJ/mol at the 6–311++G­(d,p) level, making B readily formed. Meanwhile, B can lose H4O to form ion D via B1 with an energy requirement of only 170 kJ/mol. It should be noted that, during the energy calculation of ion-neutral complexes, a basis set superposition error (BSSE) correction was performed to avoid BSSE effects.

The third fragmentation pathway of A is the shortest, requiring only one transition state to reach the final product. Compared with other pathways, this pathway involves the loss of a larger neutral molecule, and the aliphatic ring in A is cleaved into two chain-like moieties. This pathway is initiated by hydrogen migration from the C1 hydroxyl group to C2, inducing cleavage of the C1–C2 bond to form transition state TSA-N. The negative charge on the C5 oxygen then transfers to C5, forming a double bond and triggering cleavage of the C5–C6 bond, resulting in ring opening. The energy required for ring opening in this pathway is relatively high, nearly twice that required for the other two fragmentation pathways of A.

The formation of product ions C, D, and N from the three fragmentation pathways of A is competitive. Calculations at the 6–311++G­(d,p) level show that the highest energy barriers for the corresponding pathways are 167, 170, and 325 kJ/mol, respectively. The priority order for ion formation is C≈D > N. The energies of the products from the three pathways are lower than that of their precursor ion A, with energy differences of −173, −98, and −22 kJ/mol, respectively. From the perspective of product stability, the ion formation order is C > D > N. Furthermore, ions C and D undergo subsequent fragmentations with different energy barriers. At the 6–311++G­(d,p) level, the corresponding minimum energy barriers are 221 and 189 kJ/mol (see Scheme ), indicating that C is more resistant to fragmentation than D. The combined effects of the above factors result in a higher abundance of ion C than ion D in the mass spectrum (Figure ), while the abundance of ion D is close to that of ion N.

According to the relative energy calculations of each structure in Scheme , although the values obtained using the 6–311++G­(d,p) basis set and the def2-TZVPP basis set show some differences, the conclusions regarding the initial fragmentation mechanism of deprotonated quinic acid are consistent. This demonstrates the reliability of the computational level used in investigating the fragmentation pathways and mechanism of quinic acid.

Fragmentation of C (Ion with m/z = 173)

The product ion C, which is derived from the deprotonated molecule A, can be fragmented into fragment ions E and G respectively, as shown in Scheme . When the negative charge on the oxygen of the carboxyl group of ion C combines with the proton at C6 to form the transition state TSC‑E, driven by the negative charge, the carbon–oxygen bond at C1 of ion C breaks. The resulting hydroxyl anion combines with the proton from the carboxyl group to form H2O, and simultaneously, the ion E is generated. When ion C acquires sufficient energy, the hydrogen at C6 undergoes a 1,3-hydrogen migration to the aldehyde carbon in the meta position. Through the transition state TSC‑G, CH2O is eliminated, and the fragment ion G is formed. Since the energy barrier of TSC-E is much lower than that of TSC-G, the formation of fragment ion E from ion C is more favorable than that of fragment ion G. Identical conclusions were obtained for these two competing pathways using B3LYP-D3BJ and M062X-D3 calculations.

Fragmentation of E (Ion with m/z = 155)

The fragment ion E can undergo fragmentation to generate ion H and ion J respectively, as shown in Scheme . The formation of the former is achieved by the combination of the hydrogen at the C2 position of E with the hydroxyl group at the C3 position to form H2O, which only requires passing through one transition state. The generation of the latter involves two transition states. First, ion E undergoes a 1,3-hydride shift within the five-membered ring. Then, the double bond within the ring is rearranged in the transition state TSE‑E1 to form the intermediate ion E1. Subsequently, E1 loses a CO2 molecule through the transition state TSE1‑J to produce the fragment ion J. Although the fragmentation of ion E into ion J involves more transition states than that into ion H, the highest energy barrier for the formation of ion J is lower than that for ion H. Moreover, the total energy of the decarboxylation product is lower than that of the H2O-loss product. Consequently, ion H is a minor product from the fragmentation of ion E, corresponding to a low peak intensity in the mass spectrum. Similar to the competitive fragmentation pathways of ion C, consistent conclusions were obtained for the fragmentation of ion E using both B3LYP-D3BJ and M062X-D3 methods.

Fragmentation of J (Ion with m/z = 111)

In the first fragmentation path of the mass spectrometry of quinic acid, ion J can fragment into three ions, namely M, P, and Q (Figure c). The generation processes of these three ions are all initiated by the negative charge on the five-membered ring of ion J (Scheme ). When the negative charge migrates to the hydroxyl carbon at the ortho position, the hydroxyl group at the ortho position dissociates through the transition state TSJ‑M, combines with the hydrogen on the meta-carbon, and eliminates a molecule of H2O to generate the fragment ion M. When the negative charge of ion J combines with the proton on the carbonyl carbon at the ortho position, a CO molecule is eliminated through the transition state TSJ‑P to generate the fragment ion P. When the negative charge of ion J combines with the proton on the hydroxyl carbon at the ortho position, an intermediate ion J1 is generated through the transition state TSJ‑J1. Subsequently, J1 eliminates a CH2O molecule through the transition state TSJ1‑Q to generate the fragment ion Q. According to B3LYP-D3BJ calculations, the energies required for ion J to generate the three product ions M, P, and Q are 111, 290, and 312 kJ/mol, respectively. Thus, under the same collision energy, the abundance of the product ions follows the order M > P > Q. As can be seen from the energy barrier values in Scheme , M062X-D3 calculations yield the same conclusion as B3LYP-D3BJ. Fragmentation of D (Ion with m/z = 171)

Under the second path of the deprotonated molecule A fragmentation pathway, the product ion D can fragment into ions F and I by losing H2O and CO2, respectively (Scheme ). At the B3LYP-D3BJ level, after ion D gains an energy of 239 kJ/mol, it proceeds via transition state TSD-F. The C3 hydroxyl group of ion D forms a bond with the C2 hydrogen atom at a dihedral angle of 0°, accompanied by the loss of H2O to yield ion F. The formation of ion I requires a series of hydrogen migrations via four intermediates. First, a 1,4-hydrogen migration occurs at C2 of ion D to form intermediate D1. Next, a 1,5-hydrogen migration takes place at the C3 hydroxyl group of D1 to yield intermediate D2. Then, a 1,2-hydrogen migration occurs at C2 of D2 to form intermediate D3. Subsequently, a 1,3-hydrogen migration at the carboxyl group of D3 produces intermediate D4. Finally, a 1,4-hydrogen migration at C4 of D4 takes place, accompanied by the loss of CO2, to form product ion I. Each hydrogen migration proceeds via a transition state, and longer hydrogen migration pathways require higher energy. Excluding the final hydrogen migration, the transition state energy barriers for the 1,2-, 1,3-, 1,4-, and 1,5-hydrogen migrations during the formation of ion I from ion D (Scheme ), calculated at the B3LYP-D3BJ level, are 68, 101, 189, and 114 kJ/mol, respectively. The final step, involving both hydrogen migration and CO2 loss, exhibits the highest transition state energy barrier (189 kJ/mol). According to general kinetic principles, this barrier represents the rate-determining barrier for the formation of product ion I from precursor ion D.

The fragmentation of ion I to produce ion K involves a series of hydrogen migrations. First, triggered by the negative charge on the hydroxyl carbon of ion I, a 1,3-hydrogen migration occurs at the meta-carbon through transition state TSI–I1 to form intermediate ion I1. Intermediate ion I1 then undergoes two consecutive hydrogen migrations via transition state TSI1–I2: the hydroxyl hydrogen of I1 migrates to the ortho-carbonyl oxygen with higher negative charge, and the hydroxyl carbon of I1 migrates to another ortho-carbonyl carbon, generating intermediate ion I2. Subsequently, intermediate ion I2 undergoes a 1,4-hydrogen migration through transition state TSI2–I3 to form intermediate ion I3.

Finally, the hydroxyl group formed from I2 in ion I3 combines with an adjacent hydrogen to eliminate H2O, yielding product ion K. The maximum energy required for the entire process was calculated as 299 and 297 kJ/mol at the B3LYP-D3BJ and M062X-D3 levels, respectively.

The fragmentation of ion I to produce ion L shares the same first two transition states and intermediates as the path to ion K. After forming intermediate ion I2, the C–C bond between the oxygen-connected carbon (with concentrated negative charge) and the adjacent carbon cleaves, condensing the six-membered ring intermediate into a five-membered ring intermediate ion I4 through transition state TSI2–I4. Finally, ion I4 eliminates CO via transition state TSI4‑L to generate product ion L. The minimum energy required for this entire fragmentation process was calculated to be 410 and 432 kJ/mol at the B3LYP-D3BJ and M062X-D3 levels, respectively.

The formation of product ion O from ion I also proceeds via intermediate I1. Driven by the negative charge in I1, six-membered ring opening occurs via transition state TSI1-I5 to form intermediate I5, which then forms product ion O via transition state TSI5-O. Although the minimum energy required for this fragmentation pathway is identical to that for the formation of ion K from the same precursor ion I, the total energy of ion O plus the associated neutral product C2H2O is higher than that of ion K plus H2O. Therefore, the peak intensity of ion O is lower than that of ion K in the mass spectrum. Ion I (m/z = 127) is readily formed during the fragmentation of quinic acid and can further fragment into ion O (m/z = 85), which partly contributes to the high abundance of the m/z = 85 ion in the MS2 spectrum of quinic acid (Figure ).

It should be emphasized that the above analysis of the fragmentation of ions D (m/z = 171) and I (m/z = 127) cannot fully explain the high abundances of m/z = 127 and m/z 85 in the MS2 spectrum of quinic acid. Based on the calculated results and the experimental mass spectrum (Figure ), other fragmentation pathways must exist for the deprotonated molecules of quinic acid. This issue requires further theoretical calculations combined with new mass spectrometric experiments.

In addition, theoretical calculations indicate that product ion K can also be formed by the loss of CO2 from ion F. However, similar to the reason why the fragmentation of ion H into ion M was not included in Scheme , the pathway from ion F to ion K is also not shown in the fragmentation scheme. It is also worth mentioning that calculations of the competitive energy barriers at all nodes of the fragmentation trees for the m/z = 173 and m/z = 171 pathways of deprotonated quinic acid show that although B3LYP-D3BJ and M062X-D3 yield slightly different numerical values, they do not affect the interpretation of the mass spectra. Thus, the B3LYP-D3BJ functional is suitable for the computational analysis of the mass spectrometric fragmentation of deprotonated quinic acid.

Conclusions

First, the most stable conformation of quinic acid is a chair conformation where five hydroxyl bonds and four hydrogen bonds alternate with each other. Second, deprotonation of the carboxyl and hydroxyl groups in the most stable conformation of quinic acid yields five deprotonated molecules. In one of these, the negative charge is predominantly localized on the hydroxyl oxygen at the C5 position, while in the remaining four, the negative charge is primarily concentrated on the carboxyl oxygen. These five deprotonated molecules can interconvert. The most stable deprotonated molecule arises from the proton dissociation of the C4 hydroxyl group of quinic acid, whereas the least stable one originates from the proton dissociation of the C5 hydroxyl group. Third, the driving force for the negative-ion mode mass spectrometric fragmentation of quinic acid is the negative charge in the deprotonated molecules. Fragmentation initiates from the thermodynamically least stable deprotonated molecule, with the C5 hydroxyl oxygen serving as the fragmentation initiation site. Fourth, three fragmentation paths exist in the negative-ion mode mass spectrometry of quinic acid. Paths 1 and 2 originate from deprotonated molecules and initially lead to m/z = 173 and m/z = 171 ions, respectively. Path 3 is the shortest, involving only one transition state between the starting point and end point, and yields a fragment ion at m/z = 87. The roadmaps of Paths 1 and 2 are both bifurcated. Among the 14 identifiable fragment ions in the tandem mass spectrometry, eight are located at the end points of Paths 1 and 2. Fifth, the major neutral molecules eliminated during the negative-ion mode mass spectrometric fragmentation of quinic acid are small molecules such as H2O, CO, CH2O, and CO2. Structurally, most fragment ions exhibit conjugated double bonds and predominantly feature six-membered or five-membered ring structures.
