Extracellular vesicles in senescence-associated chronic lung diseases
Authors: Ruiying Wang, Yahong Chen, Xiansheng Liu, Peter J. Barnes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic lung diseases, such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, obstructive sleep apnea, asthma, bronchiectasis, and lung cancer, are intricately linked to the aging process. These diseases are characterized by a high prevalence rate and a paucity of effective treatment options. Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of extracellular vesicles (EVs) in the pathogenesis and progression of these diseases. EVs, released by senescent cells, mediate intercellular communication and modulate immune responses through their cargo of microRNAs, proteins, and other molecules. These vesicles contribute to disease progression by promoting inflammation, fibrosis, tissue remodeling, and cellular senescence. Specifically, certain microRNAs, such as miR-21, miR-34a, and miR-570-3p, along with several proteins in EVs, have been identified as key factors influencing these processes. Additionally, EVs play significant roles in immune regulation and have potential anti-inflammatory effects, making them promising candidates for therapeutic applications. Recent advances in the use of EVs as therapeutic agents, including their application in nanotechnology for targeted drug delivery, have demonstrated potential in reducing inflammation, modulating immune responses, and enhancing tissue repair. Understanding the role of EVs in these diseases offers insights into potential therapeutic targets to mitigate disease progression and improve patient outcomes. Future research should focus on standardizing EV isolation and characterization methods, verifying the safety and efficacy of EV-based therapies in clinical trials, and elucidating the complex biological mechanisms of EVs in aging and disease.

Introduction

As populations age, cellular senescence, a key marker of aging, has attracted considerable attention.[1] The accumulation of senescent cells shifts tissue and organ function from healthy homeostasis toward pathological decline, ultimately restricting the lifespan.[2] Cellular senescence is involved in the pathogenesis of many age-related diseases, including respiratory diseases such as chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)[3] and idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF).[4] Moreover, cellular senescence may play a role in a variety of other chronic respiratory conditions, such as obstructive sleep apnea (OSA),[5] asthma,[6] bronchiectasis,[7] and lung cancer.[8] Among chronic lung diseases, COPD is considered the most closely associated with senescence. COPD is characterized as a disease of accelerated lung aging.[3] It has been demonstrated that the pharmacological clearance of accumulated senescent cells can delay natural and premature aging and preserve organ function.[9] Therefore, therapeutically targeting cellular senescence presents an appealing alternative for chronic lung diseases.

Cellular senescence is a multidimensional biological process involving complex mechanisms and signaling pathways. It is driven by mechanisms such as the deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) damage response, which leads to cell cycle arrest; telomere shortening, which triggers senescence once critically short; oxidative stress from reactive oxygen species (ROS), which causes cellular damage; and epigenetic modifications, which influence gene expression.[2,10] Metabolic disruptions, particularly mitochondrial dysfunction, contribute to dysfunctional energy metabolism issues.[11] Changes in the extracellular matrix (ECM) impact cell shape and function and influence signaling pathways.[12] Cellular senescence is a key factor in chronic lung diseases, involving pathways such as tumor protein 53/cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 1A (p53/p21) and p16INK4a/Rb in cell cycle arrest, mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) in metabolism, and nuclear factor (NF)-κB and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK)/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) in inflammation.[9,13,14] The senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) influences the environment through cytokine secretion, and pathways such as Wnt/β-catenin and transforming growth factor (TGF)-β are linked to fibrosis.[1] Intracellular TGF-β1 signaling promotes the apoptotic-resistant phenotype of senescent fibroblasts in pulmonary fibrosis via Janus kinase 2 (JAK2)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3)-mediated BCL-2 upregulation.[4] Additionally, extracellular vesicles (EVs) derived from diverse cellular sources play crucial roles in modulating cellular senescence by transmitting senescent signals, modulating immune-inflammatory responses, regulating gene expression and cell function, altering intracellular environments, and serving as potential therapeutic targets.[14] The various contents in EVs, particularly microRNAs (miRNAs), regulate aging and influence disease progression through several mechanisms such as targeting key signaling pathways and modulating cellular processes.[14]

Emerging evidence underscores EVs as critical pathophysiological mediators in senescence-associated lung diseases, revealing several core mechanistic commonalities. First, EVs function as key vectors in aberrant intercellular communication, facilitating the transfer of pathogenic cargo, including proteins, miRNAs, and other regulatory molecules, between senescent cells, structural cells (epithelial cells, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts), and immune cells within the aging lung microenvironment, propagating aging signals and exacerbating chronic lung diseases. This transfer disrupts cellular homeostasis and propagates damaging signals.[151617] Second, EVs contribute to chronic inflammation, a hallmark of both aging and diseases such as COPD and IPF. EVs derived from stressed or senescent lung cells can package and deliver proinflammatory mediators, exacerbating local and systemic inflammation and amplifying disease progression.[15,16] Crucially, EVs propagate cellular senescence—a key driver of aging—through the transfer of SASP factors and senescence-inducing molecules (e.g., specific miRNAs).[151617] This transfer can induce senescence in recipient cells, establishing a detrimental feedback loop that accelerates tissue dysfunction and fibrosis in conditions such as IPF. Third, EVs regulate the immune response during the pathological process of lung tissue senescence. This regulation promotes or inhibits the inflammatory response, influencing T-cell activity and participating in immune cell recruitment and differentiation.[18192021] Dendritic cell (DC)-derived EVs present antigens and stimulate T cells. In contrast, EVs derived from regulatory T cells suppress immune responses.[22] Furthermore, plasma EVs derived from immune cells, including lymphocytes, have been shown to decline in quantity and functional mitochondrial content with advancing age, a change that reflects alterations in EV dynamics during immunosenescence.[23] Macrophage-derived EVs in patients with COPD or asthma have the potential to activate neutrophils and other immune cells, which subsequently leads to airway inflammation and tissue damage.[24,25] Fourth, EVs drive oxidative stress pathways, which are heightened in aging and heavily implicated in diseases such as COPD. EVs carry molecules that amplify ROS production or diminish antioxidant defenses in recipient cells, contributing to tissue damage.[15,26] Furthermore, EVs play crucial roles in tissue remodeling and fibrosis in chronic lung diseases. EVs facilitate the excessive deposition of ECM and induce structural alterations in the lungs by mediating pathogenic interactions between epithelial cells and fibroblasts. This process encompasses the transfer of molecules that govern tissue repair and remodeling, thus actively driving fibrosis progression.[272829] Collectively, these mechanisms, including enhanced intercellular transfer of damaging cargo, propagation of inflammation and senescence, regulation of the immune response, amplification of oxidative stress, and promotion of tissue remodeling and fibrotic processes, establish EVs as fundamental, shared players in the pathogenesis of diverse senescence-associated lung diseases. Understanding these core mechanisms is vital for developing treatments that target cellular senescence, potentially improving outcomes in chronic lung diseases.

EVs

EVs refer to particles that are released from cells, are delimited by a lipid bilayer, and cannot replicate on their own (i.e., do not contain a functional nucleus). EVs are a collective term encompassing various subtypes of cell-released membranous structures, including exosomes, microvesicles, microparticles, ectosomes, oncosomes, and apoptotic bodies. However, specific issues arise when studying these entities, as their size and abundance often make them difficult to obtain as relatively pure preparations, making proper characterization difficult.[30,31] EVs can be released by cells under both normal physiological conditions and pathological conditions. To help practitioners in all areas of EV research and application to follow best practices for each specific question and indication, the “Minimum Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles” (MISEV) was produced by the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles. The MISEV document, first published in 2014 and subsequently in 2018 and 2023 as MISEV2014, MISEV2018, and MISEV2023, respectively, provides recommendations and guidance for EV-related studies to encourage enhanced research design and reporting of experimental details. The MISEV2023 classifies EVs into “large EVs” (≥200 nm) or “small EVs” (<200 nm), while in the early editions, EVs were categorized into exosomes, microvesicles, and apoptotic bodies.[303132] Molecular markers are important for characterizing EVs, confirming their presence, and indicating their origin. These markers include transmembrane or glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored proteins, such as cluster of differentiation 63 (CD63), cluster of differentiation 81 (CD81), cluster of differentiation 9 (CD9), ALG-2-interacting protein X (ALIX), tumor susceptibility gene 101 protein (TSG101), and integrins, which serve as signatures of the lipid bilayer structure of EVs. Owing to the high heterogeneity of EVs, different subtypes may share common markers.[33] Exploring the composition and function of EV content and cargo is important for understanding their functions. EV cargo sorting is mediated by the Endosomal Sorting Complex Required for Transport (ESCRT) machinery and accessory proteins, and the content comprises miRNAs, messenger RNAs (mRNAs), lipids, proteins, and cytokines.[34] The content of EVs is actively selected and is different from the cytoplasmic content of cells [Table 1 and Figure 1]. EVs have diverse biological functions in both health and disease. They mediate intercellular communication and signal transduction by transporting cargo to recipient cells when released into the extracellular space.[34,35] In health, EVs maintain tissue homeostasis, regulate immune responses, and promote cell proliferation and repair. However, in disease states, EVs may contribute to pathological processes, such as tumor growth, inflammation, and neurodegenerative diseases.[363738] Additionally, EVs are implicated in aging and cellular senescence by facilitating altered intercellular signaling.[39,40] Compared with nonsenescent cells, EVs derived from senescent cells contain distinct cargo, potentially altering immune cell function and promoting inflammation.[41] These dual roles are particularly consequential in senescence-associated lung diseases, where EVs transform from homeostasis maintainers into drivers of shared pathological cascades. Investigating the functions of various senescent cell types in senescence-associated lung diseases and understanding their interactions with EVs may provide new insights into pathogenesis and may lead to the identification of novel therapeutic strategies.[42]

EV Cargo Involved in Senescence

Notably, the quantity and cargo composition of EVs are dynamically regulated during cellular senescence–a process closely linked to the pathogenesis of age-related diseases, including senescence-associated lung diseases. Senescent cells release more EVs, and EV cargo is altered during cellular senescence.[14,43] The increased quantity of EVs and their specific cargo have been linked to senescence progression.[43] Moreover, senescent cells secrete a SASP that includes small and large EVs, which can induce chronic inflammation and contribute to various age-related pathologies.[16,40] Senescent cell-derived EVs disseminate the senescent phenotype to normal cells and possess the ability to transfer this phenotype to target cells, thereby enhancing the response within the aging environment.[44] For example, proteins such as interferon-induced transmembrane 3 (IFITM3) in EVs and satellite II RNA in senescent cells may play a role in EV-mediated paracrine senescence.[45,46] Additionally, cigarette smoke induces the release of mitochondrial DNA into the extracellular environment via vesicles and promotes cellu­lar senescence through oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, as well as through intercellular communication via EV-containing miRNAs.[47,48] In summary, EVs contribute to senescence propagation via both direct and indirect mechanisms.

The database of EV contents reports that 566,911 proteins, 27,692 mRNAs, 22,858 miRNAs, and 3839 lipids are contained in EVs (Vesiclepedia [http://www.microvesicles.org/]). With respect to senescence-related pulmonary diseases, research on EV content is relatively more extensive in COPD, with studies focusing on their role as biomarkers in peripheral blood, bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF), and sputum, as well as their molecular profiles in plasma-derived EV RNAs.[49,50] Most studies have investigated EV-mediated miRNA involvement in intercellular cross-talk and COPD pathogenesis.

MiRNAs

EV-associated miRNAs play pivotal roles in regulating cellular senescence and aging-related processes. Some miRNAs mimic the youth-reviving effects of young blood transfusions by upregulating the expression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator 1 alpha, thereby alleviating age-related mitochondrial deficits. Conversely, other miRNAs, including miR-29a-3p, miR-29c-3p, and miR-34a-5p, exacerbate mitochondrial insufficiency by suppressing proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator 1 alpha expression. The impact of these miRNAs varies according to their abundance in the circulation.[51] An increasing amount of attention has been directed toward investigating the role of EV-associated miRNAs in the aging process. These miRNAs are involved in regulating cellular senescence through multiple pathways, including modulation of mitochondrial function, intercellular communication, and metabolic reprogramming. In the context of COPD, a disease characterized by accelerated lung aging, EV miRNAs have been shown to be dysregulated. When compared with healthy nonsmokers, patients with COPD exhibited differential expression of EV miRNAs. Specifically, miR-423-5p, miR-320b, and miR-22-3p were upregulated in BALF-derived exosomes, whereas miR-122-5p was downregulated in lung-derived exosomes.[52] Additionally, plasma exosome miRNAs in patients with COPD included five miRNAs (miR-23a, miR-1, miR-574, miR-152, and miR-221) with upregulated expression and four miRNAs (miR-3158, miR-7706, miR-685, and miR-144) with downregulated expression.[53] Different cell types preferentially utilize specific sorting sequences, leading to the formation of cell-type-specific small EVs (sEV) miRNA profiles.[54] Among these miRNAs, miR-22,[55] miR-144,[56] miR-152/sirtuin 7 (SIRT7),[57] miR-122-5p, miR-221, miR-23a, miR-574,[58] and miR-1-3p[59] are associated with senescence in different diseases and various cell types, including Parkinson’s disease, human dental pulp stem cells, lung fibroblasts (LFs), and human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs). However, the functions of EV miRNAs are not fully understood.

In addition to these context-specific findings, a broader set of EV miRNAs has been robustly associated with senescence. For instance, miR-126-3p, miR-146a-5p, miR-21-5p, and miR-181a-5p, are widely recognized as senescence-associated miRNAs and regulate the expression of genes involved in aging and longevity.[60,61] Elevated miR-34a, miR-570-3p, miR-125a-5p, and miR-494-3p are thought to be involved in lung epithelial cell senescence.[6263646566] EV miR-34a in epithelial cells may regulate COPD senescence by targeting sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) and sirtuin 6 (SIRT6),[13] and it is also involved in common lung disease comorbidities, such as skeletal muscle dysfunction.[67,68] Additionally, miR-377-3p has been shown to promote LF senescence in COPD via different signaling pathways.[69] Furthermore, miR-125a-5p has been found to be enriched in EVs in COPD,[70] while miR-146a-5p has been shown to be enriched in EVs in both COPD and asthma,[71] suggesting potential roles for these inflammation-associated miRNAs in airway diseases. Elevated miR-125a-5p participates in senescence through the specificity protein 1 (Sp1)/SIRT1/hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-1α pathway,[62] and the role of miR-125a-5p has been increasingly acknowledged.[70] MiR-146a-5p is involved in the inflammatory response and epithelial–fibroblast communication.[727374] However, further investigations are needed to ascertain its involvement in senescence. In addition, miR-21-5p carried by small EVs spreads pro-senescence signals, affecting DNA methylation and cell replication. Exosomal miR-21 plays a crucial role in the impaired communication between epithelial cells and fibroblasts, and it exhibits an anti-proliferative effect on endothelial cells.[75,76] MiR-126 from endothelial microparticles targets high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) to mediate inflammation.[77] These findings suggest that miR-146a-5p, miR-21, and miR-126 are associated not only with lung disease, but also with age-related multimorbidity, such as cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) and type 2 diabetes mellitus.[61,78] Additionally, in diseases such as acute respiratory distress syndrome, miR-181a-5p, miR-709, and miR-494-3p have been shown to induce EV-mediated phenotypic changes.[798081] These findings suggest that these miRNAs may also be released via EVs from cells in senescence-associated lung diseases and propagate senescence signals. In particular, further studies on EV miR-34a, miR-570-3p, miR-125a-5p, miR-21, and miR-146a-5p are warranted [Table 2].

Proteins

Growing evidence indicates that proteins carried by EVs contribute to diverse cellular functions, including senescence regulation. Koba et al[82] validated 45 proteins via targeted proteomics of the serum EV protein signatures from patients with COPD and mouse models. Elevated fibulin-3, tripeptidyl-peptidase 2, fibulin-1, and soluble scavenger receptor cysteine-rich domain-containing protein are observed in lung disease. Fibulin-3 has been linked to spontaneous emphysema development associated with aging, featuring alveolar enlargement and elastin degradation.[82] In addition to their roles as circulating biomarkers, EV proteins also mediate intercellular communication within the airway epithelium. Gupta et al[83] demonstrated that exosomal transfer between airway epithelial cells significantly alters the secretory profile of recipient cells, including the induction of mucin hypersecretion. Specifically, approximately 20% of proteins (including MUC5AC and MUC5B) were significantly altered in airway secretions following exosomal transfer, suggesting that exosomal cargo transfer between airway epithelial cells may play an important role in airway biology and epithelial remodeling.

The SASP Atlas (www.SASPAtlas.com) catalogs EV contents from LFs and lists matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-1 (MMP-1), growth/differentiation factor 15 (GDF15), stanniocalcin 1 (STC1), and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1 encoded by SERPINE1) as potential senescence biomarkers.[84] Among these, GDF15 is upregulated during aging and plays a key role in the stress response. It promotes airway epithelial senescence via the activin receptor-like kinase 1/Smad1 pathway, as evidenced by increased p21 and p16.[85] However, whether GDF15 is secreted via EVs in chronic lung disease and contributes to EV-mediated senescence remains unclear. STC1 is another senescence‑associated biomarker in pulmonary fibrosis, cancer, and Alzheimer’s disease,[868788] but its presence in EVs from aging lungs has not been explored. MMP‑1 is implicated in COPD pathogenesis and cellular senescence,[89] yet whether EVs contain MMP‑1 and how EV‑associated MMP‑1 regulates senescence are unknown. To date, several EV proteins have been reported in COPD, mostly in exosomes. For instance, exosome‑associated AU‑rich‑element factor‑1 (AUF‑1) preserves bound RNA targets and sustain their function, participating in the spread of inflammation and senescence to neighboring cells.[90] These findings indicate that EVs act as carriers of pro‑senescence signals. Nevertheless, major research gaps remain regarding EV proteins such as GDF15 and MMP‑1 in the context of lung disease‑associated senescence.

Lipids

Lipids play crucial roles in regulating aging and longevity through multiple genetic pathways, many of which are linked to lipid metabolism and lipid signaling.[91] Given that EVs are emerging as important carriers of lipids and lipid-associated signals between cells, it has been reported that EVs enriched in fatty acids are involved in the pathogenesis and progression of cancer and liver diseases.[92,93] However, research on the lipid content of EVs and its association with senescence is largely lacking, presenting an area for future investigation.

EVs and Senescence-Associated Lung Diseases

Cell senescence is characterized by permanent cell cycle arrest in response to various cell stressors, such as telomere shortening, oxidative stress, ionizing radiation, oncogenes, and persistent DNA damage.[949596] EVs drive the progression of senescence-associated lung diseases through multiple mechanisms. COPD has been extensively studied in this context. IPF has also received substantial attention, whereas other diseases (e.g., OSA, asthma, bronchiectasis, and lung cancer) remain understudied.

COPD

COPD is a disease in which cigarette smoke and particulate matter (PM) are significant risk factors, as they impede lung injury repair and accelerate cellular senescence.[97] Cellular senescence plays a critical role in COPD development, contributing to both disease progression and associated comorbidities.[13] The accumulation of senescent cells with age leads to increased release of the SASP, which is composed of cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and EVs, forming a vicious cycle that drives further senescence.[13,98] Chronic inflammation and oxidative stress in COPD trigger cell senescence, leading to the release of SASP factors as well as EVs. These EVs contain specific miRNAs, proteins, and other molecules; they can be taken up by surrounding healthy cells, inducing their senescence and further exacerbating the inflammatory response and tissue damage in COPD. Consequently, a bidirectional mechanism is established in which COPD and aging perpetuate and intensify each other. The characteristics of senescence in various cell types and the specific EV-mediated mechanisms involved in COPD development are described below.

COPD LFs exhibit increased cellular senescence characterized by elevated p16INK4a expression, senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity, and SASP release.[100] Small airway fibroblasts from COPD patients display senescent characteristics, and it is plausible that they release EVs that propagate senescence to neighboring cells.[101] Additionally, miR-377-3p is significantly upregulated in COPD fibroblasts and directly induces cellular senescence by targeting zinc finger protein 36, C3H type-like 1 (ZFP36L1), however, whether this miRNA is packaged into fibroblast-derived EVs remains an open question.[67] Similarly, cigarette smoke-induced miR-570-3p targets SIRT1 to enhance cellular senescence in COPD, and given that miRNAs are often enriched in EVs, it is possible that EV-mediated transfer contributes to senescence spread.[65] Beyond these plausible mechanisms, forkhead box protein O1 (FOXO1) has been shown to contribute to cigarette smoke condensate-induced cellular senescence and fibrosis by directly promoting TGF-β1 transcription and activating the TGF-β1/Smad2/3 signaling pathway in COPD.[102] Furthermore, Wnt5A, transported by fibroblast-derived EVs in COPD, is upregulated in senescent cells and may contribute to pro-senescent signaling in neighboring cells.[103]

In contrast to the indirect evidence for fibroblast-derived EVs, direct evidence supports EV-mediated pro-senescent signaling from epithelial cells in COPD. Small EVs from COPD small airway epithelial cells have been shown to induce cellular senescence in healthy recipient cells via miR-34a-mediated suppression of SIRT1. When taken up by healthy cells, these EVs reduce SIRT1 expression and increase p21CIP1 and SA-β-gal positivity. These effects are blocked by a miR-34a antagomir, confirming that EV-mediated senescence depends on miR-34a transfer.[104] Together, these findings indicate that EV-mediated transfer of proinflammatory and pro-senescence factors links chronic inflammation to cellular aging in COPD.[105]

Unlike these pro-senescent EVs, mesenchymal stem cell (MSC)-derived EVs have been shown to counteract senescence and promote lung tissue repair in COPD. MSC-derived EVs delay or reverse senescence-associated tissue damage by transferring bioactive miRNAs, a property that has been extensively reviewed in the context of COPD.[106] MSC-derived EVs have been reported to suppress cellular senescence and the SASP via delivery of anti-senescent miRNAs, while also modulating key signaling pathways involved in aging and age-related diseases.[107]

Overall, EVs in COPD play a dual role in regulating cellular aging. EVs from airway epithelial cells and fibroblasts can propagate pro-senescent signals, amplifying tissue damage. Conversely, therapeutic administration of MSC-derived EVs have been shown to counteract senescence and alleviate lung injury [Figure 4].

IPF

IPF primarily arises from aberrant activation of fibroblasts, characterized by their differentiation into myofibroblasts and excessive secretion of type I collagen (collagen type I alpha 1 chain [COL1A1] or alpha 2 chain [COL1A2]), α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), and fibronectin, which collectively drive fibrotic lesion formation.[108,109] This process involves key signaling pathways, including the TGF-β, Wnt/β-catenin, and phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)-protein kinase B (Akt) cascades,[110] all of which are closely linked to cellular senescence.[111]

Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of EVs from diverse cellular origins in IPF progression, particularly in propagating senescence signals. Quantitatively, EV release from IPF-diseased human bronchial epithelial cells (DHBEs) is significantly elevated compared to normal human bronchial epithelial cells (NHBECs).[112] Coculture experiments have demonstrated that exposure to DHBE-derived EVs increases senescence markers (SA-β-gal, phosphorylated histone H2AX (γH2AX), and p16/p21) and interleukin (IL)-6/IL-8 secretion in healthy recipient bronchial epithelial cells. In contrast, lung fibroblast-derived EVs (LF-EVs) trigger mitochondrial dysfunction, characterized by elevated mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) and reduced adenosine triphosphate (ATP).[17,112] Mechanistically, LF-EVs have been shown to directly induce epithelial cell senescence via miR-23b-3p and miR-494-3p, which suppress SIRT3 (a key mitochondrial antioxidant regulator), thereby exacerbating oxidative stress and DNA damage responses in IPF.[17] Small RNA sequencing further revealed that DHBE-derived small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) contain a distinct miRNA profile compared to NHBE-derived sEVs, with differentially expressed miRNA linked to cell cycle regulation and the p53 pathway.[112] These findings collectively indicate that EVs from both epithelial cells and fibroblasts contribute to senescence propagation in IPF.

Proteomic profiling revealed fibrogenic mediators in IPF-EVs, including HMGB1, insulin-like growth factor binding protein 7 (IGFBP7), and collagens (COL1A1/COL1A2). Strikingly, many of these proteins are not merely pro-fibrotic but have been identified as key functional components of the SASP or have been directly implicated in the induction and propagation of cellular senescence. For example, IGFBP7, a core SASP factor capable of inducing secondary senescence in healthy cells,[113] and HMGB1, which drives paracrine senescence via the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE)-NF-κB-JAK/STAT axis, have been reported to be carried by or upregulated in response to pathological EVs in fibrotic conditions.[113,114] Collagen, as a core ECM component carried by IPF-EVs, acts not simply as a structural product of fibrosis but as an active inducer of cellular senescence and the SASP microenvironment.[115,116] These observations underscore a paradigm wherein pathogenic IPF-EVs, by shuttling a cargo that includes SASP-related proteins and senescence-associated collagens, may actively reinforce a pro-senescent, pro-fibrotic microenvironment.

Given the prominent role of pathogenic EVs in propagating senescence and driving IPF progression, therapeutic EVs that counteract these senescence-associated processes hold significant promise. As cellular senescence is a key driver of IPF pathogenesis, the antifibrotic effects of therapeutic EVs may be partly attributed to their ability to mitigate senescence-associated processes. Human bronchial epithelial cell-derived EVs carrying miR-16, miR-26a/b, miR-141, miR-148a, and miR-200a have been shown to disrupt TGF-β–Wnt cross-talk by targeting Wnt3A, Wnt5A, and WNT10B. Importantly, these EVs directly inhibit TGF-β-mediated lung epithelial cellular senescence in a bleomycin-induced IPF mouse model, accompanied by reduced β-catenin and senescence markers.[117] Similarly, menstrual blood-derived endometrial stem cell exosomes are enriched in let-7, which targets the lectin-like oxidized low-density lipoprotein receptor (LOX-1) 3′-untranslated region (3′-UTR-1) to suppress NOD-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation, alleviate mitochondrial dysfunction-associated cellular senescence (marked by SIRT3/aconitase 2 downregulation), and reduce alveolar epithelial apoptosis, thereby mitigating mitochondrial DNA damage and fibrosis.[118] Given that mitochondrial dysfunction and DNA damage are established hallmarks of cellular senescence,[119,120] the protective effects of therapeutic EVs likely involve anti-senescence mechanisms.

In parallel, engineered MSC-derived EVs have been developed to target senescent type 2 alveolar epithelial cells (AEC2s) via CD38 antigen receptor modification, effectively restoring nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+) levels, reversing epithelial–mesenchymal transition, and rejuvenating senescent cells in vitro, thereby alleviating pulmonary fibrosis in aged mice.[121] This rejuvenation strategy may further support the concept that combating cellular senescence is a key therapeutic mechanism of EV-based therapies in IPF.

OSA

OSA is characterized by intermittent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation, conditions that promote systemic oxidative stress and inflammation. These pathological states alter the secretion and cargo composition of EVs, particularly exosomes. Accumulating evidence indicates that OSA-derived EVs contribute to accelerated aging processes in multiple organs, positioning exosomes as key mediators linking OSA to senescence-related comorbidities.[122,123]

Direct evidence for EV-mediated senescence comes from studies on endothelial aging. Plasma exosomes from untreated OSA patients significantly increase SA-β-gal activity and p16 expression, while downregulating SIRT1 and SIRT6 in naïve endothelial cells, demonstrating that OSA-EVs directly transmit pro-senescent signals.[124] Mechanistically, intermittent hypoxia-induced oxidative stress is involved in this process.[124] Given that vascular dysfunction is a well-established contributor to cognitive decline and brain aging, the pro-senescent effects of OSA-EVs on endothelial cells may also have implications for central nervous system pathology.

Consistent with this notion, OSA-derived exosomes promote brain aging through mechanisms involving complement-mediated neuroinflammation and the accumulation of senescence-associated proteins.[125,126] In OSA patients with mild cognitive impairment, astrocyte- and neuron-derived exosomes carry elevated complement proteins (C3b, C5b-9) and Alzheimer’s disease-related biomarkers (Aβ42, P-T181-tau), which correlate with cognitive decline.[125,126] Complement activation is recognized as a key driver of inflammaging, and these factors may contribute to neuroinflammation.[126] Intermittent hypoxia, a hallmark of OSA, has been shown to promote β-secretase activity and impair Aβ clearance, leading to the accumulation of these senescence-associated proteins.[125,126] Furthermore, exosomal miR-20a-5p from intermittent hypoxia-exposed cells induces hippocampal neuronal pyroptosis—a gasdermin-mediated pro-inflammatory form of programmed cell death—thereby amplifying neuroinflammation and contributing to cognitive impairment,[127] which may represent a mechanism linking OSA to accelerated brain aging.

Given the pro-senescent role of OSA-EVs, therapeutic interventions that target these EVs or their effects are of great interest. Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), the gold-standard therapy for OSA, partially reverses EV-mediated senescence. Compared to exosomes from untreated OSA patients, exosomes from patients after long-term adherent CPAP treatment significantly improve the senescent phenotype of recipient endothelial cells, as evidenced by reduced SA-β-gal-positive cells, decreased p16 expression, and increased SIRT1 and SIRT6 expression.[124] In line with these findings, multi-omics profiling of circulating exosomes derived from OSA patients before and after 1 year of adherent CPAP therapy revealed that CPAP treatment modulates the miRNA, protein, and lipid cargo of OSA-derived EVs. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis further demonstrated that these molecules are implicated in multiple signaling pathways, including the MAPK and PI3K-Akt cascades.[128] This reflects the complex and interconnected nature of senescence-associated signaling networks.

Collectively, these findings establish OSA-derived EVs as critical transmitters of pro-senescent signals that promote both vascular and brain aging, while demonstrating that effective CPAP therapy can partially reverse these aging-related effects. Future studies are warranted to explore whether EVs can serve as non-invasive biomarkers for monitoring CPAP efficacy and as therapeutic targets by modulating their cargo to counteract senescence in OSA patients.

Asthma

Aging is a critical risk factor for asthma development and severity, as age-related immune dysregulation and lung structural changes promote airway inflammation, remodeling, and therapeutic resistance.[129] EVs play a pivotal role in asthma pathogenesis and have emerged as potential mediators of this age-related susceptibility. Although multiple asthma-associated EV biomarkers have been identified (e.g., miRNAs and proteins in serum, BALF, and nasal lavages),[130131132] their relationship with aging remains largely underexplored.

Recent studies have begun to uncover specific cellular senescence mechanisms that may drive asthma pathogenesis. Airway smooth muscle cells (ASMCs) from elderly asthmatic patients show increased levels of phosphorylated p53, p21, and p16 proteins, along with activation of the p53/p21 and p16/Rb pathways. These senescent cells release SASP factors, which correlate with airway fibrosis and treatment resistance.[133] EVs are hypothesized to serve as transmitters of these senescence signals. In an IL-13-treated bronchial epithelial cell model mimicking asthma, airway epithelial EVs exhibit altered miR-34a levels, a miRNA predicted to promote Th2 immune bias through effects on dendritic cell function.[132] Notably, miR-34a is also recognized as a senescence-associated miRNA that targets SIRT1[64] and SIRT6[63] in epithelial cells. Collectively, these findings suggest that EV-mediated alterations in miR-34a may concurrently influence both immune polarization and cellular aging pathways in the asthmatic airway. Similarly, environmental stressors may exploit this EV-mediated communication: cigarette smoke extract induces airway epithelial senescence via mTOR signaling, resulting in elevated SASP markers (S100A8/9 and HMGB1) that can be propagated through EVs to prime dendritic cells for allergen responses.[134] This process renders mice susceptible to developing asthma upon subsequent low-dose allergen challenge.[134] Thus, whether triggered by allergic inflammation or environmental insult, convergent EV-SASP mechanisms may propagate senescence-associated signals that underlie asthma pathogenesis. Bioinformatics analysis of Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets has identified two distinct asthma senescence subtypes: subtype 1 characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction and oxidative phosphorylation enrichment (with regulatory T cell [Treg] and immature dendritic cell infiltration), and subtype 2 characterized by activation of immune response and NF-κB/tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-mediated inflammation (with eosinophil and neutrophil infiltration).[135] These senescence subtypes may be reflected in distinct EV cargo profiles, although direct evidence for this association is currently lacking.

Given the potential detrimental role of EV-mediated senescence signals in asthma, therapeutic targeting of these pathways is an area of active investigation. MSC-derived EVs have been shown to exert senomorphic effects in experimental models. They reduce the expression of senescence markers (p16INK4a, p21CIP1), decrease the number of SA-β-gal-positive cells, and lower the secretion of SASP factors such as IL-1β and IL-6. Mechanistically, these EVs deliver a specific miRNA cocktail (miR-181a-5p, miR-92a-3p, miR-21-5p, miR-186-5p) that targets p300/CREB-binding protein (CBP)-associated factor (PCAF) and homeodomain interacting protein kinase 2 (HIPK2) within the p53 signaling pathway.[136] Together, these findings suggest that EV-mediated regulation of cellular senescence represents a promising therapeutic avenue for asthma. Future studies are warranted to explore whether EVs can be harnessed to deliver anti-senescence factors or neutralize pro-senescence signals in asthmatic patients.

Bronchiectasis

Bronchiectasis is a chronic airway inflammatory disease characterized by irreversible bronchial dilatation and progressive lung function decline, in which aging acts as an important risk factor driving its development and progression. Senescent cells accumulate in the aging lung and remain metabolically active, secreting cytokines, chemokines, matrix metalloproteinases, and EVs, forming a SASP that profoundly alters the tissue microenvironment.[137] In bronchiectasis, the neutrophilic and dysbiotic airway environment links dysregulated cellular senescence to disease persistence and progression.[137]

Building on this aging-related background, recent studies have begun to characterize the aging airway in bronchiectasis, revealing increased expression of senescence markers (p16, p21, γH2AX) and decreased expression of the anti-aging molecule SIRT1 in bronchial epithelial cells, particularly in the large airways.[138] This senescent phenotype may be driven by telomere dysfunction, as telomere attrition in the bronchiectatic lung activates p21 signaling without necessarily engaging p16.[7] These findings collectively establish that lung tissue from bronchiectasis patients exhibits characteristic features of cellular senescence. Notably, in the context of bronchiectasis where Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa) is a common pathogen, sputum exosomal miR-92b-5p and miR-223-3p, which positively correlate with local IL-1β and IL-8 levels, have been shown to accurately discriminate P. aeruginosa colonization. Furthermore, these miRNAs are predominantly enriched in longevity regulatory pathways and NF-κB signaling cascades, suggesting a potential mechanistic link between EV cargo and accelerated airway aging driven by the SASP.[139]

EVs have emerged as key transmitters of senescence signals. In bronchiectasis airways, neutrophil elastase (NE)—a major inflammatory protease—directly disrupts epithelial repair by promoting both cellular apoptosis and senescence, and indirectly amplifies airway inflammation by triggering extracellular trap release and exosome release, which magnify protease activity and inflammation in the airway.[140] Consequently, NE-driven exosomal communication perpetuates a pro-senescent and pro-inflammatory microenvironment that overwhelms airway homeostasis.

Emerging evidence supports the therapeutic potential of EVs in bronchiectasis, although studies directly investigating their anti-senescence effects are currently lacking. A case report documented successful treatment of severe bronchiectasis with inhaled human umbilical cord MSC-derived exosomes (HucMSC-Exos), showing reversal of hypoxemia, improved clinical outcomes, and enhanced quality of life.[141] However, whether these therapeutic benefits involve the reversal of cellular senescence remains to be determined.

Lung cancer

Lung cancer is an age- related disease, with both its incidence and mortality increasing markedly with advancing age.[129] EVs play significant roles in lung cancer; however, direct evidence mechanistically linking EV-mediated signaling to cellular senescence in lung cancer is still emerging.[142,143] For instance, in small cell lung cancer (SCLC), non-canonical integrin signaling activates the RAS-MAPK/ERK pathway,[142] and this pathway may also be involved in senescence-related cellular responses. The PI3K/Akt/mTOR pathway, commonly dysregulated in lung cancer, can be activated by EV-carried phosphorylated proteins, contributing to therapy resistance-pathways that may also drive cellular senescence in a context-dependent manner.[143]

Senescent cells and their EVs play a dual role in lung cancer. Within the tumor microenvironment, senescent stromal cells (such as cancer-associated fibroblasts [CAFs]) release EVs that carry pro-inflammatory and pro-tumorigenic cargo. These EVs, upon uptake by recipient cells, can activate signaling pathways such as TGF-β and NF-κB, thereby promoting the acquisition of a SASP and driving tumor progression.[144] Moreover, senescent LF-derived exosomes enriched in matrix metalloproteinase 1 (MMP1) have been shown to promote non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cell proliferation, invasion, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition.[145] Beyond classical signaling molecules, emerging evidence has expanded the repertoire of EV-mediated signals. Tumor-derived exosomes carrying 3′ transfer RNA-derived stress-induced RNA-AlaCGC (3′tiRNA-AlaCGC) target fibroblasts to induce a SASP by inhibiting forkhead box protein O3 (FOXO3) and activating the TGF-β/Smad3 pathway, thereby increasing galectin-9 secretion; both SASP and galectin-9 induce synthetic dysfunction of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells, leading to anti-PD-L1 therapy resistance in lung adenocarcinoma.[144]

Radiotherapy has been shown to modulate senescence-related therapeutic resistance in NSCLC, partly through its effects on CAFs. Specifically, radiotherapy-induced senescent cells modulate CAF function and behavior through the secretion of soluble and EV-associated SASP factors, whereas the reciprocal interplay between CAFs and these factors potently enhances tumor cell invasion, promotes therapy resistance, and accelerates metastatic dissemination.[146] Extending the role of EVs to the direct effects of radiation on cancer cells, irradiated NSCLC cells with wild-type tumor protein 53 (TP53) undergo senescence and secrete cluster of differentiation 63 (CD63)-positive EVs carrying DNA:RNA hybrids and long interspersed nuclear element-1 (LINE-1) elements. These EVs induce senescence in recipient cells via a TP53-dependent mechanism, contributing to the abscopal effect, with upregulation of SASP factors (IL-6, interferon-β [IFN-β]) and senescence markers (β-galactosidase).[147] Beyond its role in radiotherapy, CAF-derived EVs have also been implicated in chemotherapy resistance. CAF-derived exosomal miR-103a-3p targets BCL2 antagonist/killer 1 (Bak1) to suppress mitochondrial apoptosis and promote cisplatin resistance.[148] Given that anti-apoptotic pathways are often intertwined with cellular senescence in the tumor microenvironment, such EV-mediated signaling may contribute to therapy resistance through senescence-associated mechanisms.

Furthermore, EV-mediated senescence signals also target immune cells. VEGFR2 knockdown elevates exosomal miR-3200-3p, inducing regulatory T cell (Treg) senescence. In humanized mouse models, treatment with miR-3200-3p-enriched exosomes combined with anlotinib synergistically suppressed tumor growth, increased Treg senescence markers (p16 and MMP-3), and reduced Ki67+ cell numbers.[149] These findings demonstrate that EV-delivered miRNAs can specifically drive senescence in immunosuppressive Tregs, thereby unleashing anti-tumor immunity. Moreover, EVs derived from senescent cells themselves have emerged as important mediators of anti-tumor immune surveillance. Chemotherapy-treated senescent tumor cells release EVs that trigger an immune response against remaining tumor cells; senescent cell-derived EVs (senEVs) were both necessary and sufficient to trigger immune-mediated clearance of senescent cells, thereby suppressing tumor growth in wild-type mice. These findings suggest that senEVs complement the activity of secreted inflammatory mediators by recruiting and activating distinct immune cell subsets, thereby enhancing the efficient clearance of senescent cells to inhibit cancer recurrence.[150]

Finally, emerging EV-based therapeutic strategies are being developed to directly target therapy-induced senescence. An antibody-decorated, curcuma-derived nanovesicle platform loaded with doxorubicin (DR5-CNV/DOX) selectively binds to and clears senescent tumor cells while suppressing their SASP output.[151] In animal models, this intervention blocked the onset of epithelial–mesenchymal transition, curtailed aberrant vascularization, and reshaped the tumor immune milieu, ultimately boosting chemotherapeutic response.[151] This study underscores the promise of plant-derived EVs as an innovative delivery vehicle for eradicating therapy-induced senescent cells and neutralizing their pro-tumorigenic secretome.

In summary, EVs in the lung tumor microenvironment act as versatile communicators, capable of either propagating pro-tumorigenic senescence signals to stromal cells or, conversely, delivering anti-tumorigenic senescence signals to immunosuppressive cells, depending on their cellular origin and molecular cargo. Targeting these EV-mediated senescence pathways, by blocking harmful signals or boosting beneficial ones, holds promise for future therapy.

EV‑based Therapeutic Strategies

EVs offer a novel therapeutic modality for a broad spectrum of pulmonary disorders (e.g. chronic inflammatory lung diseases, fibrotic conditions, and lung cancer), by modulating cellular signaling, suppressing inflammatory responses, and promoting tissue repair mechanisms.[152] Analyzing the composition and function of EVs allows for personalized treatment, thereby enhancing treatment efficacy and prognosis. Furthermore, EVs hold promise as tools for senotherapy, and the development of optimal EVs for senotherapies is becoming an important research focus. Consequently, EVs may offer a multifaceted approach to address the complex pathophysiology of age-related pulmonary diseases. Several strategies can enhance the therapeutic potential of EVs. These include drug loading methods, such as coprecipitation, electroporation, and membrane fusion, which enable efficient drug encapsulation.[153] Surface modification of EVs with specific targeting molecules or ligands allows for targeted delivery to specific cells or tissues, enhancing their cellular uptake and immune modulation.[154] Additionally, EVs can protect internal drugs from degradation or immune attack, thereby increasing drug stability and bioavailability.[155] Moreover, EVs can efficiently deliver drugs to target cells and enhance drug efficacy while minimizing toxic side effects on surrounding normal tissues.[153,156,157]

MSC-EVs have emerged as a highly promising therapeutic strategy. These EVs, especially exosomes, encapsulate biologically active molecules and have exhibited therapeutic effects in a diverse range of diseases. Specifically, they have demonstrated therapeutic benefits in neurological diseases, CVDs, kidney disorders, IPF, and other autoimmune and inflammatory conditions.[158159160161162163] Recent studies have suggested that MSC-EVs effectively mitigate chronic inflammation and promote tissue regeneration in COPD models.[163,164] They counteract lung remodeling induced by cigarette smoke and other factors. In the case of IPF, MSC-EVs have demonstrated safety and efficacy in reversing abnormal ECM deposition and enhancing lung function, as evidenced by both murine models and clinical evaluations. Moreover, EVs derived from immature sources, such as urinary stem cells or hypoxia-induced pluripotent stem cells (hypoxia-iPSCs), can inhibit cellular senescence, enhance the regenerative capacity, and extend the lifespan in scenarios of aging-related lung injury. This highlights their crucial role in alleviating senescence-associated deteriorations in lung homeostasis.

Multiple delivery modalities, with aerosol-mediated delivery among them, present promising pathways for EV-based therapies in the realm of treatment. Aerosol-mediated delivery facilitates the efficient delivery of small EVs to lung tissues, thereby demonstrating substantial therapeutic potential for senescence and improving bioavailability in respiratory diseases.[165166167] Despite the existing challenges related to the compatibility of drug delivery carriers with host tissue cells, exosomes are emerging as next-generation drug delivery platforms, attributable to their outstanding biocompatibility, target specificity, and extended half-life within the body.[168] As a result, harnessing the potential of EV-based therapies may revolutionize the treatment of senescence-associated lung diseases and enhance the quality of life of patients.

Despite the promising results achieved, limitations still remain, such as EV storage and the necessity for optimized targeting strategies. This underscores the ongoing research endeavors into the mechanisms of EVs, which are crucial for the advancement of personalized therapies in senescence-associated lung diseases.

Clinical Translation of EVs

Beyond preclinical evidence, clinical trials have validated the translational potential of EVs in lung diseases, particularly COPD and lung cancer [Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C957]. Although most of these trials were not designed to investigate senescence, their findings may indirectly inform EV-mediated aging mechanisms.

In COPD, one trial (NCT07131020) is investigating plasma exosomal miR-125b, a miRNA known to regulate cellular senescence[62]; other trials (NCT04183530, NCT03049202) are profiling EV molecular signatures in COPD patients, which may reflect senescence-related pathways given that COPD is characterized as accelerated lung aging.[3,13] In lung cancer, EV-based liquid biopsy trials focus on mutation detection to guide therapy, where therapy-induced senescence is a recognized determinant of treatment outcome.[147] For SCLC, a trial (NCT05191849) is exploring lncRNA profiles in circulating EVs as predictive biomarkers for treatment response. Conceptually, such EV cargo could potentially reflect chemotherapy-induced senescent cell burden, though this specific application remains untested in this trial.

Collectively, these trials were not designed to study senescence directly, but their findings may offer indirect insights into EV-mediated aging mechanisms in lung diseases, highlighting the need for future studies with senescence-specific endpoints.

Conclusion

The association between EVs and cellular senescence has emerged as an increasingly prominent area of investigation, with growing interest in translating these findings into clinical applications. To date, research efforts have predominantly centered on exosomes and small EVs, while the understanding of other EV subtypes remains relatively limited. In COPD, studies have commenced to explore the roles of EVs released from various cell types, including bronchial epithelial cells, AECs, macrophages, neutrophils, and fibroblasts, all of which are intricately linked to cellular senescence. EVs secreted by senescent cells are capable of transmitting signals to adjacent cells through miRNAs or other cargo molecules, thereby exacerbating the inflammatory milieu and tissue damage in age-related pulmonary diseases and their comorbidities. Among these, miR-21, miR-34a, and miR-125a-5p within EVs play pivotal roles in the process of senescence, with the underlying mechanisms primarily involving the sirtuin and MAPK pathways. Moreover, EV-associated miRNAs may exert a dual function, acting as a double-edged sword in cellular senescence. The beneficial or detrimental effects of these miRNAs depend on their identity, target cell type, and the microenvironmental context.

EVs possess significant potential for therapeutic application. Through the engineering of EVs to encapsulate specific therapeutic agents, targeted delivery to senescent cells or damaged lung tissue can be accomplished, thereby augmenting therapeutic efficacy and minimizing side effects. At present, strategies including drug loading, surface modification, and the utilization of MSC-EVs are being investigated to optimize EV-based therapeutic modalities. Notably, MSC-EVs have demonstrated potential in alleviating inflammation and reducing senescence markers in COPD and IPF models, rendering them promising candidates for next-generation treatments. Additionally, neutrophil-derived EVs may confer substantial advantages as drug-delivery carriers. Neutrophils generate abundant EVs in response to external stimuli, and their short lifespan facilitates easy manipulation.

The research on EVs in age-related pulmonary diseases presents numerous challenges and opportunities. The establishment of standardized methodologies for EV isolation, characterization, and engineering is of paramount importance for the translation of EV-based therapies from the laboratory bench to the clinical setting. Additionally, large-scale clinical trials are essential to validate the safety and efficacy of these therapies in patients with COPD, IPF and other senescence-associated lung diseases. Comprehending the intricate biological mechanisms of EVs in the context of aging also necessitates advanced technologies and interdisciplinary approaches.
