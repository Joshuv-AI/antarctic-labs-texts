Efficacy and safety of one-stop Angiojet mechanical thrombectomy combined with iliac vein stenting in May-Thurner syndrome complicated with acute iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis
Authors: Tianpeng Shao, Yingwen Zhou, Jinsheng Wang, Zhaowei Song
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
May-Thurner Syndrome (MTS) with acute iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis (IFDVT) carries high risks of limb ischemia and post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS). Traditional treatments like catheter-directed thrombolysis have limitations, including high major bleeding risk and prolonged hospital stays. A retrospective cohort study included 52 patients with MTS-related acute IFDVT (one-stop group: 27, Angiojet mechanical thrombectomy + simultaneous iliac vein stenting; traditional group: 25, catheter-directed thrombolysis alone or with delayed stenting). Main outcomes were 24-hour limb circumference edema reduction rate, 6-month iliac vein patency (stenosis <30%), 6-month PTS (Villalta score ≥5), and severe bleeding. Baseline limb swelling was comparable between the 2 groups (all P > .05). The one-stop group had better 24-hour limb circumference edema reduction rate (15 cm above knee: 82.2 ± 4.9% vs 73.6 ± 2.4%; 15 cm below knee: 70.0 ± 8.2% vs 58.5 ± 7.4% in the traditional group, both P < .01), higher 6-month patency (81.48% vs 32.00%, P < .001), lower PTS incidence (11.11% vs 76.00%, P < .001), and less severe bleeding (3.70% vs 24.00%, P < .05). The one-stop strategy offers superior early swelling relief, significantly higher 6-month iliac vein patency (81.48% vs 32.00%, P < .001), lower PTS risk, and better safety for MTS-related IFDVT, supporting it as a preferred clinical option.

1. Introduction

May-Thurner Syndrome (MTS) is a well-recognized vascular disorder stemming from the compression of the left common iliac vein by the overlying right common iliac artery.[1,2] This anatomical compression, occurring in the pelvic region, disrupts normal laminar venous flow, leading to stasis within the left iliac vein and its tributaries.[3] Recent advances in computational fluid dynamics have deepened understanding of MTS pathophysiology, revealing that compression not only reduces venous cross-sectional area but also induces abnormal shear stress patterns: key drivers of thrombus formation.[4,5] When complicated by acute iliofemoral deep vein thrombosis (IFDVT), MTS follows an aggressive clinical course: the thrombus, amplified by preexisting stasis, raises risks of phlegmasia cerulea dolens (limb ischemia) and long-term post-thrombotic syndrome (PTS).[6] PTS, characterized by chronic swelling, pain, skin changes, and venous ulcers, severely impairs quality of life (QoL);[7,8] epidemiological data suggest up to 50% of DVT patients develop PTS within 5 years, with MTS likely increasing this risk.[9,10]

Traditional management of MTS-related IFDVT has critical limitations. Catheter-directed thrombolysis (CDT) is a longstanding approach that involves direct thrombolytic infusion. It is associated with major bleeding risks, which is a particular concern for elderly or comorbid patients.[11] Additionally, this approach requires prolonged hospital stays, which in turn elevates healthcare costs and the risk of infections.[12] Surgical thrombectomy, while physically removing thrombus, fails to address underlying venous compression, leading to high recurrence rates; surgical trauma also exacerbates venous inflammation, promoting re-thrombosis.[13] Standalone mechanical thrombectomy using devices like Angiojet provides acute thrombus clearance but neglects anatomical stenosis, limiting long-term efficacy due to recurrent stasis.[14,15]

The one-stop strategy: combining Angiojet mechanical thrombectomy with iliac vein stenting, addresses these gaps. Angiojet high-velocity saline jets disrupt and aspirate thrombus via drug-mechanical thrombolysis, reducing reliance on high-dose thrombolytics and lowering bleeding risks.[16] Simultaneous iliac vein stenting corrects the MTS root cause by expanding the compressed vein, restoring normal outflow, and mitigating abnormal hemodynamics to prevent re-thrombosis.[17,18] This integrated approach streamlines care, combining acute symptom relief with long-term patency benefits, and may enhance patient compliance.

This study evaluates the efficacy and safety of one-stop Angiojet thrombectomy plus iliac vein stenting in MTS-related acute IFDVT. Efficacy endpoints include early limb swelling resolution (assessed by 24-hour limb circumference edema reduction rate), 6-month iliac vein patency, and 6-month PTS incidence. Safety was assessed via perioperative bleeding complications. By comparing these outcomes with traditional treatments, we aim to provide evidence-based guidance for optimizing MTS-related IFDVT management and improving patient outcomes.

2. Materials and methods

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Cangzhou Central Hospital between September 2018 and October 2024, following the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (Approval No. 2022-049-02(z)), and all patients provided written informed consent prior to treatment.

2.1. Study design and patient selection

Eligible patients were identified from the institutional vascular intervention database. And demographic data, including age and gender, comorbidities including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, obesity, and smoking history, clinical presentation including symptom duration and affected limb, and preprocedural imaging findings including thrombus extent and degree of iliac vein stenosis were collected from electronic medical records.

2.2. Treatment protocols

All procedures were performed by attending vascular interventionists with at least 5 years of experience in venous interventions.

2.2.1. One-stop group

Patients underwent combined Angiojet mechanical thrombectomy and iliac vein stenting in a single session. First, an inferior vena cava filter was placed via the contralateral common femoral vein under fluoroscopic guidance to prevent pulmonary embolism. Angiojet thrombectomy was then performed using a 6F or 8F catheter introduced through the ipsilateral popliteal vein. The catheter was positioned at the distal end of the thrombus, and the drug-spraying mode was activated first: 300,000 IU of urokinase (dissolved in 150 ml of normal saline) was slowly sprayed from the distal to the proximal end of the thrombus. After approximately 30 minutes, the mode was switched to aspiration mode, and thrombi were removed via antegrade aspiration to clear residual thrombus. After successful thrombectomy, iliac vein angioplasty was performed with a 12 to 14 mm balloon, and a self-expanding stent (14–16 mm diameter, 90–120 mm length) was deployed to cover the stenotic segment more completely. Stent positioning was confirmed with fluoroscopy, ensuring <5 mm protrusion into the inferior vena cava to avoid caval obstruction. Postoperatively, patients received rivaroxaban 15 mg twice daily for 3 weeks, followed by 20 mg once daily for 6 months. The inferior vena cava filter was removed between 1 and 3 months postoperatively via percutaneous retrieval, depending on thrombus resolution assessed by duplex ultrasound.

2.2.2. Traditional group

Patients underwent CDT as initial treatment. A multi-sidehole infusion catheter was placed into the thrombus via the ipsilateral popliteal vein (left for most cases, consistent with MTS-related IFDVT distribution), and urokinase was administered at a dose of 400,000 IU per day for 3 to 7 days. Systemic anticoagulation with unfractionated heparin (adjusted to maintain activated partial thromboplastin time 1.5–2.0 times baseline) was initiated during thrombolysis, transitioning to rivaroxaban 20 mg once daily after catheter removal. Iliac vein stenting was performed only if follow-up angiography at 1 month demonstrated residual stenosis >50%, using the same stent specifications as the one-stop group.

2.3. Outcome measures

All outcome measures were defined in accordance with established standards from prior venous intervention research and clinical practice guidelines, ensuring consistency with existing literature. Assessments were conducted by independent reviewers blinded to treatment allocation, with data documented in standardized case report forms and verified by a clinical events committee to ensure accuracy.

2.3.1. Primary efficacy outcomes

The 24-hour limb circumference edema reduction rate was adopted for quantifying the extent of acute limb swelling relief by the following formula:

Preoperative Limb Circumference Difference: Calculated as (circumference of the ipsilateral/affected limb - circumference of the contralateral/unaffected limb) measured preoperatively at 2 standardized sites: 15 cm above the knee joint and 15 cm below the knee joint.

24-Hour Postoperative Limb Circumference Difference: Calculated using the same method as the preoperative measurement at the identical 2 sites (15 cm above and below the knee) at 24 hours after the procedure.

2.3.2. Secondary efficacy outcomes

Secondary efficacy outcomes included the 6-month incidence of PTS and the total effective rate at discharge. PTS was diagnosed using the validated Villalta scoring system, which evaluates clinical signs (pain, swelling, erythema, pigmentation, induration, and ulceration) and symptom severity.[19] A score of 5 or higher was considered indicative of PTS, aligning with definitions used in the Adjunctive Catheter-Directed Thrombolysis (ATTRACT) trial and other large-scale studies of thrombotic complications.

The total effective rate at discharge was a composite endpoint, defined as the combination of complete resolution of clinical symptoms (limb pain and swelling) and angiographic confirmation of venous patency (stenosis <30%). This composite measure, previously employed in comparative studies of mechanical thrombectomy, provides a comprehensive assessment of early treatment success.

2.3.3. Safety outcomes

In this study, the primary safety endpoint was defined as severe bleeding complications, consistent with criteria established by the International Society on Thrombosis and Haemostasis.[20] Safety was evaluated by monitoring severe bleeding complications. Severe bleeding was defined according to the above criteria, including intracranial hemorrhage, retroperitoneal bleeding, or bleeding requiring transfusion of 2 or more units of packed red blood cells within 30 days of the procedure.

2.4. Follow-up

Posttreatment follow-up was conducted in accordance with established protocols for venous intervention studies, with structured assessments at 1, 3, and 6 months after the procedure.

At 1 month, all patients attended an in-person outpatient visit. A physical examination was performed to evaluate limb swelling, pain, and signs of infection or recurrence. Duplex ultrasound was used to assess early stent patency, detect thrombus recurrence, and evaluate venous flow patterns. Serum creatinine levels were measured to screen for contrast-induced nephropathy.

The 3-month follow-up was conducted either in-person or via telemedicine, based on patient preference and clinical stability. During this visit, the Villalta score was calculated to identify early signs of PTS. Patients were questioned about their adherence to anticoagulation therapy, and any adverse effects related to rivaroxaban, such as bleeding or gastrointestinal discomfort, were recorded. Duplex ultrasound was repeated to monitor stent integrity and venous patency.

At 6 months, patients returned for an in-person visit. A physical examination was performed, and the Villalta score was reassessed. Digital subtraction angiography was conducted to confirm iliac vein patency and detect late complications, including in-stent restenosis or stent fracture.

To ensure high follow-up adherence, patients received written instructions about follow-up schedules before discharge, along with contact information for the study coordinator. Automated text message reminders were sent 3 days before each scheduled visit. For patients who missed appointments, study personnel contacted them by phone within 1 week to reschedule. If phone contact was unsuccessful, a letter was mailed to inform them of the importance of follow-up and provide options for rescheduling. All follow-up data were recorded in a secure electronic case report form, with regular audits to ensure completeness and accuracy.

2.5. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 and R software version 4.2.1. A two-tailed P-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range, depending on the results of normality testing via the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were compared between groups using independent samples t-tests. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. They were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test, with the latter applied when cell sizes warranted it. Intragroup changes in continuous outcomes such as limb circumference reduction over time were assessed using repeated-measures analysis of variance with post hoc Bonferroni correction.

All statistical methods were prespecified in the study protocol and adhered to the consolidated standards of reporting trials guidelines for reporting observational studies.

3.1.1. Demographic characteristics

In terms of gender, there were 25 male and 27 female patients, with no significant difference in gender distribution between the 2 groups. Regarding age, the patients’ ages ranged from 52 to 76 years, with a mean age of 63.69 years. The age distribution was balanced between the 2 groups (t-test, P > .05).

3.1.2. Underlying diseases

Sixteen patients had underlying diseases (hypertension/diabetes), among whom 7 were in the one-stop treatment group, and 9 were in the traditional treatment group. There was no statistically significant difference in the prevalence of underlying diseases between the 2 groups (χ2 test, P > .05).

3.1.3. Disease-related characteristics

For the affected limb, 50 patients (96.2%) had left-sided involvement (consistent with the pathophysiology of MTS) and 2 (3.8%) had right-sided involvement (due to anatomical variation). There was no significant difference in the distribution of affected limbs between the 2 groups (χ2 test, P > .05). Preoperative limb circumference differences (15 cm above and below the knee between affected and contralateral limbs) were comparable between the 2 groups (both P > .05), indicating similar baseline swelling severity.

The duration of symptoms ranged from 1 to 14 days, with a mean duration of 5.98 ± 3.35 days. The symptom durations were comparable between the 2 groups (one-stop group: 5.74 ± 3.51 days; traditional group: 6.24 ± 3.22 days; t-test, P > .05), indicating balanced baseline symptom severity. In terms of thrombus type, 14 patients had central-type thrombi, and 38 had mixed-type thrombi; there was no significant difference in thrombus type distribution between the 2 groups (χ2 test, P > .05). Regarding the preoperative degree of iliac vein stenosis (angiographic results), the mean stenosis degree was 85.52 ± 9.8%. There was no significant difference in the preoperative iliac vein stenosis degree between the 2 groups (t-test, P > .05).

3.2. Early efficacy

Early efficacy was primarily assessed using the 24-hour limb circumference edema reduction rate (relative percentage, %), with the calculation formula of the latter detailed in the Materials and Methods section (2.3.1 Primary Efficacy Outcomes), to quantify acute limb swelling relief by comparing the preoperative and postoperative discrepancies in circumference between the affected limb and contralateral healthy limb.

At baseline, the 2 groups showed no significant difference in initial swelling severity. Postoperatively (Table 2), the one-stop group achieved a significantly superior 24-hour limb circumference edema reduction rate relative to the traditional group. At 15 cm above the knee, the one-stop group edema reduction rate was 82.2 ± 4.9%, which was significantly higher than the traditional group 73.6 ± 2.4% (P < .01). Similarly, at 15 cm below the knee, the one-stop group had a higher edema reduction rate (70.0 ± 8.2% vs 58.5 ± 7.4%, P < .01).

3.3.1. Iliac vein patency

Iliac vein patency is the primary anatomical indicator of long-term treatment success, defined as stenosis <30% in the iliac vein with assessment conducted via imaging reexamination at 6 months; a higher patency rate indicates better maintenance of venous outflow and lower risk of re-thrombosis. At the 6-month follow-up, in the traditional group (n = 25), 8 cases achieved iliac vein patency (effective cases), resulting in a patency rate of 32.00%, while the remaining 17 cases showed ineffective cases, including stenosis ≥30% or re-thrombosis. In the one-stop group (n = 27), 22 cases achieved iliac vein patency (effective cases), leading to a patency rate of 81.48%, and only 5 cases showed ineffective cases. Statistical analysis revealed a highly significant difference in patency rates between the 2 groups (χ2 = 10.83, P < .001), indicating that the one-stop treatment strategy is more effective in maintaining long-term iliac vein patency: an advantage that may stem from its integrated optimization of lesion assessment, intervention timing, and post-procedural management.

3.3.2. PTS incidence

PTS is a common long-term complication of venous thrombosis, characterized by chronic lower limb pain, swelling, and skin changes; diagnosis was based on the Villalta score, with a score ≥5 indicating PTS, and an “effective case” for this indicator refers to no PTS occurrence (Villalta score <5). The incidence of PTS directly reflects the long-term QoL of patients after treatment. At the 6-month follow-up, in the traditional group (n = 25), only 6 cases had no PTS (effective case), corresponding to a non-PTS rate of 24.00%, and 19 cases developed PTS (ineffective case). In the one-stop group (n = 27), 24 cases had no PTS (effective case), resulting in a non-PTS rate of 88.89%, and merely 3 cases developed PTS (ineffective case). Chi-square test showed an extremely significant difference in PTS incidence between the 2 groups (χ2 = 20.59, P < .001), and the remarkably lower PTS rate in the one-stop group suggests this treatment strategy not only improves vascular patency but also effectively reduces the risk of long-term complications: a benefit that may be associated with its more precise control of thrombus clearance and better protection of venous valve function.

3.3.3. Total effective rate

The total effective rate is a comprehensive efficacy indicator that integrates anatomical patency (iliac vein stenosis <30%) and clinical symptom improvement (including relief of lower limb swelling, pain, and discomfort); an effective case is defined as the simultaneous achievement of both anatomical patency and clinical symptom remission at discharge, while any case failing to meet both criteria is considered an ineffective case. This indicator comprehensively reflects the overall therapeutic value of the treatment strategy. At the 6-month follow-up, in the traditional group (n = 25), 3 cases were evaluated as effective, yielding a total effective rate of 12.00%, and the remaining 22 cases were ineffective due to either insufficient patency, persistent symptoms, or both. In the one-stop group (n = 27), 21 cases were evaluated as effective, resulting in a total effective rate of 77.78%, and only 6 cases were ineffective. Statistical analysis confirmed a significant difference in total effective rates between the 2 groups (χ2 = 9.03, P < .001), further verifying that the one-stop treatment strategy has superior comprehensive efficacy compared to traditional treatment, as it synergistically improves both vascular patency and clinical symptom control.

3.4. Safety outcomes at 6-month follow-up

As prespecified in the study protocol, only severe bleeding complications were analyzed as the core safety endpoint; other procedure-related adverse events were excluded due to low incidence. This section presents the safety outcomes of the traditional treatment group and the one-stop treatment group at the 6-month follow-up (Table 4), with the only recorded safety indicator being severe bleeding complications including intracranial bleeding and gastrointestinal bleeding; intergroup comparison was conducted using the chi-square test, and statistical significance was defined as P < .05. In the traditional treatment group with a total of 25 cases, 6 cases developed severe bleeding complications, 19 cases had no severe bleeding complications, and the incidence rate of severe bleeding complications was 24.00%. In the one-stop treatment group with a total of 27 cases, 1 case developed severe bleeding complications, 26 cases had no severe bleeding complications, and the incidence rate of severe bleeding complications was 3.70%. Statistical analysis showed that the incidence rate of severe bleeding complications in the traditional treatment group was significantly higher than that in the one-stop treatment group, with a chi-square value of 6.15 and a statistically significant difference (P < .05).

4. Discussion

The findings of this study provide critical insights into the efficacy and safety of one-stop Angiojet mechanical thrombectomy combined with iliac vein stenting for MTS complicated with IFDVT, offering valuable context for interpreting its clinical value and addressing key considerations in venous intervention practice.

The one-stop strategy delivers favorable early efficacy, particularly in alleviating acute limb swelling: a key priority for patients with IFDVT. Despite no significant statistical difference in preoperative baseline swelling between the 2 groups, the one-stop strategy achieves superior outcomes with better control of postoperative residual swelling, leveraging Angiojet unique mechanism: high-velocity saline jets rapidly disrupt and aspirate thrombi for targeted clearance.[21,22] By contrast, traditional CDT relies on 3 to 7 days of continuous urokinase infusion for gradual thrombus dissolution, delaying venous outflow restoration.[23] Compounding this advantage, the one-stop strategy simultaneously addresses iliac vein compression as the anatomical root cause of MTS, via immediate stenting,[24] while the traditional group only performs stenting if residual stenosis >50% is detected at the 1-month follow-up.[25] This delayed anatomical correction prolongs venous stasis in the acute phase, hindering early symptom resolution. The integration of thrombus clearance and anatomical correction in a single session not only shortens time to symptom relief but also reduces risks of thrombus extension during the CDT-to-stenting interval, aligning with prior clinical insights that simultaneous therapeutic goals optimize early venous intervention outcomes. Additionally, faster symptom relief allows earlier ambulation, lowering bed rest-related complications and shortening hospital stays, addressing the limitation of long hospitalization in traditional CDT.

Regarding long-term outcomes, the one-stop strategy delivers notable benefits in PTS prevention, even as it maintains strong iliac vein patency.[24] This difference in iliac vein patency likely stems from the traditional group delayed stenting: only a subset of CDT patients requires subsequent stenting, leaving underlying iliac compression untreated in the acute phase and increasing recurrent stasis and re-thrombosis risk.[26] The one-stop strategy of immediate stenting expands the compressed vein, restores laminar flow, and eliminates shear stress abnormalities that drive MTS-related thrombus formation, ensuring sustained patency.[24] More critically, the one-stop group had a drastically lower 6-month PTS incidence: 3 cases (11.11%) versus 19 cases (76.00%) in the traditional group, with a non-PTS rate of 88.89% versus 24.00% (χ2 = 20.59, P < .001). This is clinically meaningful given that PTS affects up to 50% of DVT patients within 5 years and severely impairs QoL. The reduced PTS risk arises from 2 key factors: Angiojet immediate thrombus clearance minimizes venous valve damage, unlike prolonged CDT, which exposes valve endothelial cells to high-dose thrombolytics and increases reflux, and maintained iliac patency prevents chronic venous hypertension, a driver of PTS-related skin changes and pain. These findings extend existing literature linking PTS risk to venous obstruction duration and thrombus clearance completeness, showing that combining mechanical thrombectomy with immediate stenting creates a dual barrier against long-term complications, especially critical for MTS patients inherently at higher PTS risk.

The one-stop strategy also boasts a favorable safety profile, with significantly lower severe bleeding complications than traditional treatment. At 6 months, only 1 case (3.70%) in the one-stop group experienced severe bleeding (intracranial or gastrointestinal), compared to 6 cases (24.00%) in the traditional group (χ2 = 6.15, P < .05). This difference stems from contrasting thrombus management approaches: traditional CDT requires prolonged urokinase infusion, disrupting the coagulation cascade and carrying a known 11% major bleeding risk, while the one-stop group uses Angiojet drug-mechanical thrombolysis mode, reducing reliance on high-dose thrombolytics and minimizing systemic anticoagulation escalation. The one-stop strategy single-session design further enhances safety: it completes thrombectomy and stenting in under 2 hours, reducing percutaneous accesses and catheter dwell time, key contributors to access-site bleeding and infection in traditional CDT. Notably, the one-stop group safety holds even for high-risk patients: 7 patients (25.93%) with underlying diseases (hypertension/diabetes) experienced no severe bleeding, aligning with prior data on Angiojet lower bleeding risk in comorbid populations and supporting its broad clinical applicability.

Nonetheless, the interpretation of the results should consider the study limitations. First, our study is a single-center retrospective cohort study with a limited sample (n = 52), which may lead to selection bias. This restricts generalizability, because single-center cohorts may not represent the wider MTS-related IFDVT population: validation of the results in large-scale multicenter prospective studies is warranted. Second, as the length of follow-up is only 6 months, we cannot evaluate longer-term outcomes, such as in-stent restenosis or evolving PTS or late PTS progression; longer-term follow-up >12 months will be necessary to determine whether this strategy is durable. Third, PROs, including QoL assessed using the venous insufficiency epidemiological and economic study-QoL/symptoms Scale, were not evaluated in this study; PROs should be incorporated into future studies to assess the effect of treatment on daily life. Fourth, the traditional group regime was not standardized, introducing heterogeneity that might distort the findings; a standardized control group protocol is advised in future trials. Fifth, we excluded patients with recurrent IFDVT, and whether the one-stop strategy is effective and safe in that high-risk group is unknown, representing an area for future exploration.

These findings carry important clinical implications for MTS-related acute IFDVT management. The one-stop strategy emerges as a viable first-line option, particularly for patients with high bleeding risk or those needing rapid symptom relief. Its ability to integrate acute thrombus clearance and anatomical correction addresses traditional treatment limitations, offering a single-session solution that improves short- and long-term outcomes. The study also highlights the need to prioritize immediate anatomical correction in MTS management: prior guidelines focus on thrombus clearance with stenting as a secondary intervention, but our data show delayed stenting correlates with lower patency and higher PTS risk, supporting immediate stenting as a standard component. Practically, the one-stop strategy enhances healthcare resource utilization: shorter hospital stays and reduced long-term complications lower overall costs, a valuable benefit in resource-constrained settings. Finally, the study paves the way for future research: prospective randomized controlled trials with larger samples and longer follow-up are needed to validate efficacy and safety, while comparisons to emerging therapies and integration of PROs and long-term stent patency data will further strengthen the evidence base for this strategy.

5. Conclusions

This study compared the one-stop Angiojet thrombectomy + iliac stenting with traditional CDT-based therapy in 52 patients with MTS-related acute IFDVT. The one-stop strategy showed clear advantages: it achieved better early limb swelling relief, maintained a significantly higher 6-month iliac vein patency rate, reduced PTS incidence significantly, and had a lower rate of severe bleeding.

The one-stop strategy addresses both acute thrombus and underlying venous compression, overcoming traditional therapy limitations. It can be recommended as a first-line treatment for MTS-related acute IFDVT, especially for patients with high bleeding risk or urgent symptom relief needs. Future multi-center trials with longer follow-up should further validate its long-term durability.
