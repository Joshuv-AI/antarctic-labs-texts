Improving complementary medicine literacy: a systematic review of educational interventions for consumers and caregivers
Authors: Kaveh Naseri, Wejdan Shahin, Ayman Allahham, Sarah Hassan, Gabriela Irrazabal, Barbora de Courten, Thilini R Thrimawithana
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComplementary medicines (CMs), including herbal products, nutritional supplements, vitamins, minerals, probiotics and prebiotics, are increasingly integrated into standard medical treatments. Despite their growing popularity, concerns about safety, efficacy, and potential interactions with conventional medications persist. This systematic review evaluates the effectiveness of various educational interventions (EIs) designed to enhance the knowledge, informed decision-making capabilities, awareness of side effects, and consumer perceptions of safety and efficacy of CMs. This study aims to identify the most effective strategies for improving the awareness of potential risks and benefits, and overall health literacy among CMs users.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted across multiple databases including PubMed/ MEDLINE, Embase, ISI Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library to identify relevant studies published from inception to May 2024. Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and non-MeSH terms, along with various logic operators, were used to identify studies evaluating EIs aimed at improving CMs knowledge and decision-making. We included the studies if they evaluated EIs about CMs for consumers, patients, and caregivers, with eligible designs including randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and other types of interventional studies.ResultsFifteen eligible studies were included. Interventions ranged from interactive multimedia programs and online platforms to personalized face-to-face counselling. The interventions demonstrated significant improvements in CMs knowledge, self-efficacy, and informed decision-making among participants. High levels of participant satisfaction were consistently reported, particularly with interactive and engaging tools.ConclusionThis systematic review underscores the critical role of education in promoting the safe and informed use of CMs. Effective educational interventions enhance knowledge, informed decision-making abilities, and awareness of side effects. However, challenges such as high attrition rates and variability in study designs indicate the need for further research to optimize these interventions. Future efforts should focus on standardizing educational strategies to improve CMs related health literacy and ensure the safe use of CMs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-04931-w.Keywords: Complementary medicine, Educational intervention, Health literacy, Decision-making, Patient education

Introduction

Complementary medicines (CMs), which include herbal remedies, nutritional supplements, vitamins, minerals, probiotics, and prebiotics, are gaining widespread popularity and are increasingly being incorporated into standard medical treatments [1, 2]. The use of CMs is prevalent in both high- and low-income countries [3]. In Europe, the prevalence of CMs use ranges from 10% in Hungary to almost 40% in Germany [4]. A study from 2019 in Australia found that 50.3% of people used CMs, with the majority (47.8%) using vitamin and mineral supplements [5]. However, this growing trend brings with it significant concerns regarding their safety, efficacy, and potential for adverse interactions with conventional medications [6, 7].

The popularity of CMs is attributed to various factors. Many people perceive these products as having fewer risks compared to modern pharmaceutical drugs [8]. This perception is partly due to the view that CMs are more natural, leading to the belief that they are safer [9]. Additionally, some patients feel that the conventional treatments prescribed by physicians are not effective, leading them to seek other options such as CMs as potential solutions [10]. Recommendations and influences from friends, family, and social media also play a significant role in the decision to use CMs [9, 11].

As the utilization of CMs continues to grow, the need to educate consumers about their safe use becomes more critical, especially for patients with chronic diseases who are at higher risk for potential side effects, drug interactions, and contraindications, due to the common use of CMs with prescribed medications [12]. Providing accurate, evidence-based information is essential to prevent misuse and ensure the effective and safe utilization of CMs.

Few studies have systematically reviewed educational interventions (EIs) aimed at improving knowledge about CMs usage. Soliman and Bilszta [13] focused on undergraduate medical students, revealing inconsistencies in CMs teaching and assessment methods and emphasizing the need for standardized educational strategies. Quartey et al. [14] reviewed CMs education for biomedical doctors and medical students, highlighting the lack of standardized methods and emphasizing the need for adopting comprehensive, evidence-based strategies.

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first systematic review to specifically evaluate EIs targeting the safe use of CMs among patients, and caregivers. By assessing interventions that enhance knowledge and informed decision-making, this review aims to identify effective educational strategies for consumers and highlight gaps in current practices. This will provide the foundation for developing future educational programs and research, ultimately improving public health by enhancing the understanding and safe use of CMs. In this review, CMs refer specifically to products, such as herbal medicines, nutritional supplements, vitamins, minerals, probiotics, and prebiotics. The scope of this study does not include practitioner-led therapies (e.g., acupuncture, chiropractic care, or massage) or broader integrative health practices.

Methods

The updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) checklist was followed to conduct this systematic review [15]. The protocol for this review was prepared and registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024546205).

Data sources and strategies

The systematic search strategy was developed by 4 of the authors (K.N., T.T., A.A., W.S.). A comprehensive literature search was performed by K.N., using five electronic databases (PubMed/MEDLINE, ISI Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar) from inception to May 2024.

A combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and non-MeSH terms were used along with various logic operators in our search strategy to identify potentially relevant studies (see Supplementary Material 1 for search terms used in different databases).

Study selection and eligibility criteria

Articles identified from the search strategy were considered eligible if they met the selection criteria outlined in a predetermined PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcomes, and Study design) framework shown in Table 1. The titles and abstracts of all records identified in the searches were imported into an online systematic review management platform (Covidence; Veritas Health Innovation Ltd). Two independent reviewers (K.N. and S.H.) screened the titles and abstracts, and eligible studies were retrieved for full-text review. Where there were disagreements on full text eligibility, these were resolved by conversation or by consulting a third reviewer (T.T.). Unpublished or non-peer reviewed studies, non-English studies, as well as grey literature, were excluded. In addition, the bibliographies and citations of the included articles were reviewed to identify additional relevant publications.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Each article was read by at least two authors, and each author independently extracted the data. If there were any inconsistencies, all researchers were involved in resolving the conflict by reviewing the paper. Data extracted included details of the study: first author, year of publication, country, study design, sample sizes, attrition rate, participants’ age, level of education, health status, intervention type, duration and frequency of the intervention, form of delivery of the intervention, and measurement tools, results mean or median follow-up value with standard deviations or standard errors, main findings, main objective, participants satisfaction, and limitations and strengths highlighted in the studies. This review aimed to assess the impact of EIs on CMs health literacy without restricting the analysis to predefined health literacy domains. While general health literacy has validated frameworks [16], there is no standardized measure for CMs health literacy that aligns with the definition and scope of CMs in this study. However, the Montana State University (MSU) CAM Health Literacy Model, which categorizes CMs literacy into four domains: dose, effect, safety, and availability [17], is the most applicable model despite its restriction to herbal products. To ensure a structured interpretation of the extracted data, the health literacy-related factors assessed in the included studies were aligned to these domains. Dose-related factors encompassed knowledge acquisition and self-efficacy in CMs use, including frequency, quantity, and administration methods. Effect-related factors included perceptions of safety and efficacy, as well as adherence to recommended use. Safety-related factors covered awareness of side effects, potential risks, and interactions with conventional medicine. Availability-related factors addressed decision-making regarding CMs accessibility and affordability. This comprehensive approach allowed us to capture the full range of CMs health literacy components reported in the literature, ensuring a thorough evaluation of how EIs influence consumers’ understanding and safe use of CMs.

To ensure consistency, health literacy-related outcomes were categorized and analysed based on their definitions within each study, allowing for a broad yet structured synthesis of the available evidence. All extracted data were cross-checked for accuracy by multiple authors (K.N., T.T., A.A, and W.S.).

The same independent reviewers assessed the risk of bias using the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool 2.0 (RoB 2.0) for RCT studies [18] and the Risk Of Bias In Non-randomized Studies tool of Interventions (ROBINS-I) for non-RCT studies [19]. For randomized trials, the following domains were evaluated: bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in the measurement of the outcome, and bias in the selection of the reported result. Based on these assessments, each study was assigned an overall risk of bias rating categorized as low risk, some concerns, or high risk. For non-randomized studies, the assessment included domains such as bias due to confounding, bias in selection of participants into the study, bias in the classification of interventions, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing data, bias in the measurement of outcomes, and bias in the selection of the reported result. Each study was then assigned an overall risk of bias rating categorized as low risk, moderate risk, serious risk, or critical risk as per ROBINS-I. Disagreements were resolved through discussion. Traffic light plots and summary plots for both RCT and non-RCT studies were generated using Robvis [20]. Additional information, including subdomains within each domain, can be found in Supplementary Material S2.

Evaluation of findings

Descriptive and narrative analyses approaches were utilized to evaluate the EIs, their contents, and their impact on the knowledge and perceptions of patients and caregivers. This involved comparing data on the impact of various EIs, and analysing participant demographics, intervention types, delivery methods, and measured outcomes. Key findings, including primary and secondary outcomes, participant satisfaction, and limitations of each study, were systematically reviewed. Overall, this comprehensive evaluation aimed to provide a detailed understanding of the effectiveness of EIs on CMs for both consumers and caregivers, highlighting the best practices and areas needing further investigation.

Results

Study selection

Through the initial search of databases, a total of 13,585 articles were identified. Additional 9 articles were identified through hand searching (bibliography and citation search). After removing 1,933 duplicate records (67 identified manually and 1,866 by Covidence), 11,661 unique records remained. Following the screening of titles and abstracts, 11,608 records were excluded as irrelevant to the research question. The remaining 53 records were sought for retrieval and assessed for eligibility. Of these, 38 studies were excluded for various reasons, including irrelevant/ineligible intervention (n = 23), not an intervention study (n = 2), irrelevant patient population (n = 2), trial registration details only (n = 4), and other reasons such as abstracts (n = 1), protocols (n = 3), irrelevant outcomes (n = 1), full text not in English (n = 1), and preprints not peer-reviewed (n = 1). Ultimately, 15 studies were included in this review (Fig. 1).

Risk of bias assessments

RoB 2.0 tool assessment

The RoB 2 tool was utilized to assess the risk of bias in five RCTs included in our review. Neafsey et al. [21], Smith et al. [22], and Stie et al. [23] exhibited a low risk of bias across most domains, including the randomization process (D1), missing outcome data (D3), and the selection of reported results (D5). All studies showed some concerns in the domain related to deviations from the intended intervention (D2). Goodman et al. [24] showed additional concerns about missing outcome data (D3). Notably, the publication by Rahman et al. [25] was assessed to have a high risk of bias in the measurement of outcomes (D4), leading to an overall high-risk judgment. The overall assessments for most studies indicated some concerns, highlighting the need for cautious interpretation, particularly in areas related to deviations from the intended interventions and outcome measurements. ROBINS-I tool assessment

The ten non-RCT studies were assessed using the ROBINS-I tool and the results indicated an overall moderate risk of bias. Most studies demonstrated a low risk of bias in most domains, including bias due to selection of participants (D2), classification of interventions (D3), deviation from intended interventions (D4), missing data (D5), and selection of reported results (D7). However, all studies showed a moderate risk of bias in the measurement of outcomes (D6). Thakerar et al. [26], Chiba et al. [27], and Stie et al. [28] exhibited moderate concerns in domain 1 (bias due to confounding). Simpson et al. [29] presented a serious overall risk of bias, due to significant issues in confounding (D1). Overall, while the majority of non-randomized studies exhibited a low risk of bias in several domains, the consistent moderate risk in the measurement of outcomes (D6) and the serious risk of bias in Simpson et al. [29] highlight the need for careful interpretation of these study findings. The detailed results of these assessments are illustrated in Fig. 3.

The study populations varied significantly, with participant numbers ranging from 15 [28] to 5,269 [35] with a median size of 112. Demographic details, including age and gender distribution, showed wide variation across the studies. For example, Rahman et al. [25] focused exclusively on female participants, while studies like Neafsey et al. [21] and Shreffler-Grant et al. [32] included both genders. Age-wise, Goodman et al. [24] targeted young adults aged 18–25, while Smith et al. [22] focused on older adults aged 65 and above.

The studies explored various health statuses, including generally healthy individuals [24, 27, 34], cancer patients [23, 26, 28, 29, 33, 35], pregnant women with anaemia [25], and older adults with various chronic conditions and other health concerns [21, 22, 30–32].

Types of interventions and delivery methods

The reviewed studies employed a diverse range of intervention types and delivery methods to educate participants on CMs use, ranging from paper-based materials and printed brochures to interactive multimedia programs and online platforms. Despite the diversity in their approaches, the interventions shared the common objective of enhancing CMs-related knowledge and decision-making. They achieved this by combining educational content, practical guidance, and interactive formats to meet the diverse needs of their target population.

Online and multimedia interventions

Several studies utilized online platforms incorporating videos, educational slides, mobile apps, and web/DVD content, which were found to be engaging for both young adults and older adults. For example, Goodman et al. [24] utilized an online intervention that included videos, educational slides, newsletters, and a mobile app targeting young adults. Rahman et al. [25] employed the MyPinkMom educational intervention, delivering messages via WhatsApp to pregnant women with anaemia. Additionally, Neafsey et al. [21] used an interactive multimedia computer software program delivered via notebook computers to educate older adults. Smith et al. [22] used a combination of approaches, using both web/DVD and traditional printed booklet, to maximize accessibility and engagement of older adults.

Face-to-face and personalized counselling

Personalized and interactive methods were also prevalent. Thakerar et al. [26] provided personalized verbal information on CMs safety and potential drug interactions through face-to-face communication by a pharmacist. Horneber et al. [35] offered a telephone consultation service about CMs for cancer patients and their caregivers. Shreffler-Grant et al. [32] combined face-to-face sessions and webinars to enhance CMs health literacy among older adults. Stie et al. [23, 28] conducted open dialogues about CMs through in-person sessions with a nurse specialist, focusing on cancer patients undergoing treatment.

Printed and lecture-based

Several studies employed printed materials and educational messages. Smith et al. [33] distributed brochures on CMs safety among adult cancer patients. Dodge et al. [31] provided comprehensive educational messages delivered both online and on paper to participants dealing with various health concerns. Chiba et al. [27] used an in-person educational lecture format to deliver CMs information to healthy young adults.

Adherence to intervention and participant satisfaction

High levels of satisfaction were reported in most of the included studies, reflecting positive reception of the interventions, especially with interactive and engaging tools. For instance, Neafsey et al. [21] reported a mean satisfaction score of 4.35 ± 0.61 out of five for the intervention group. Similarly, Goodman et al. [24] reported high satisfaction with digital tools, indicating that these methods are user-friendly and engaging. Moreover, Smith et al. [22] found that over 80% of the participants rated the content of the intervention as excellent or good. Additionally, Thakerar et al. [26] and Smith et al. [33] found that participants valued the information provided, appreciating its clarity and usefulness. Horneber et al. [35] observed that 85% of patients and 90% of caregivers felt their questions were adequately answered, contributing to their overall satisfaction. Furthermore, Shreffler-Grant et al. [32] reported high satisfaction with face-to-face sessions, although webinars were less favoured due to technical issues. Simpson et al. [29] highlighted a preference for animated videos over written materials, with participants finding them more engaging and helpful. While studies such as Searson et al. [34], Rahman et al. [25], and Stie et al. [23, 28] did not directly report satisfaction metrics, positive feedback was implied through improved outcomes.

Outcomes measured

The studies aimed to evaluate a wide range of outcomes, including knowledge acquisition, awareness of side effects, perceived safety and efficacy, self-efficacy, behavioural changes, decision-making, adherence to interventions, quality of life, and patient satisfaction.

Knowledge acquisition

Several studies measured improvements in participants’ knowledge about their respective health topics [21–25, 27, 29, 32, 34].

The analysis of knowledge acquisition across the reviewed studies reveals that different types of EIs, enhanced knowledge among diverse populations. For instance, Neafsey et al. [21] showed a significant increase in knowledge with their interactive multimedia program, achieving a mean knowledge score of 71.7% (SD ± 19.1) compared to 36.2% (SD ± 16.5) in the control group (p ≤ 0.001). Similarly, Goodman et al. [24] found that the educational intervention they conducted significantly improved knowledge on Vitamin D at three different time points. The authors reported that participants who use the app more frequently maintained higher knowledge levels with a smaller decrease over time compared to infrequent app users. Moreover, Chiba et al. [27] reported a significant improvement in participants’ understanding of dietary supplements through a one-time lecture. Similarly, Searson et al. [34] demonstrated that an educational seminar significantly enhanced knowledge about complementary therapy. Finally, Rahman et al. [25] achieved significant knowledge gains (a mean knowledge score of 26.41% (SD ± 2.17) compared to 19.1% (SD ± 3.44) in the control group (p ≤ 0.001)) with the MyPinkMom intervention delivered via WhatsApp to pregnant women with anaemia. In contrast, four other studies reported no significant differences in knowledge acquisition following the implementation of the EIs [22, 23, 29, 32].

Awareness of side effects and perceived safety and efficacy

Some studies explored the effects of EIs on participants’ awareness of side effects, perceived efficacy, and perceived safety of CMs. Dodge et al. [31] conducted an educational message intervention and found significant improvements in perceived efficacy and safety among participants for two different products (X and Y). For product X, perceived efficacy scores (a 10-point scale) increased from 3.37 (SE ± 0.27) to 4.11 (SE ± 0.24) (p < 0.06), and perceived safety scores rose from 2.94 (SE ± 0.25) to 3.82 (SE ± 0.27) (p < 0.05). For product Y, perceived efficacy scores increased from 3.29 (SE ± 0.28) to 4.25 (SE ± 0.28) (p < 0.05), and perceived safety scores rose from 2.92 (SE ± 0.24) to 4.07 (SE ± 0.24) (p < 0.01). These results highlight the effectiveness of the intervention in enhancing participants’ perceptions of both the efficacy and safety of CMs products. Rahman et al. [25] utilized the MyPinkMom educational intervention delivered via WhatsApp and observed significant improvements in attitude scores among pregnant women with anaemia. This intervention effectively influenced participants’ attitudes, indicating a positive shift in their perceptions of the educational content and its relevance to their health conditions.

Self-efficacy and informed decision-making

The reviewed studies investigated the impacts of EIs on participants’ confidence in managing their health and their ability to make informed decisions. These outcomes are crucial for understanding how such interventions can influence health behaviours and improve decision-making processes.

Neafsey et al. [21] reported a significant difference in self-efficacy scores between the intervention and control groups, measured on a five-point scale. The intervention group had a final score of 3.14 (SD ± 0.9), while the control group scored 1.76 (SD ± 0.99) (p ≤ 0.001). Searson et al. [34] also observed significant improvements in decision self-efficacy among participants who attended an educational seminar. The intervention was particularly effective in increasing participants’ understanding of CMs options.

Additionally, six studies highlighted behavioural changes and informed decision-making following EIs, demonstrating their effectiveness in influencing health behaviours and enabling participants to make informed decisions. For example, Neafsey et al. [21] reported that 59% of participants took antacids and calcium supplements simultaneously with other medications, and 22% took them within an hour. Post-intervention, 42% and 32% of participants mentioned they would change the timing of taking calcium supplements and medications, respectively.

Chaffin et al. [30] found that all patients (100%) indicated they would contact the pharmacist again when seeking information about CMs, reflecting positive behavioural changes in health management post-intervention. Goodman et al. [24] similarly observed significant behavioural changes among participants who received an online intervention with videos, educational slides, newsletters, and a mobile app. This suggests that digital interventions can effectively promote health-related behavioural changes.

Furthermore, Simpson et al. [29] noted a 14% decrease in the intent to use CMs among cancer patients post-intervention. Rahman et al. [25] reported significant improvements in perceived behavioural control scores among pregnant women with anaemia after receiving the MyPinkMom educational intervention via WhatsApp. Lastly, Searson et al. [34] observed a significant decrease in decisional conflict among support persons following the intervention, although there was no significant change in distress related to CMs decision-making.

Discussion

The current systematic review aimed to evaluate the efficacy of EIs aimed to enhance safe use of CM products (e.g., herbal medicines, vitamins, minerals, probiotics, and prebiotics). Our review aligns with the increasing recognition of the importance of health literacy and informed decision-making in healthcare [36–38]. This study fills a significant gap in the literature, since previous reviews focused on the effects of CMs education on attitude, knowledge, and behaviour towards CMs usage among healthcare professionals and medical students [13, 14, 39] investigated diverse teaching approaches on CMs, without assessing the impact on consumers, patients and caregivers. Therefore, this systematic review focused on exploring how these interventions influence knowledge, self-efficacy, and informed decision-making on CMs among consumers and caregivers.

The reviewed studies highlight the successful implementation of diverse educational strategies tailored to different demographic groups, underscoring their effectiveness in enhancing health literacy. Among these, a notable trend is the use of digital and interactive platforms in EIs, which aligns with broader trends in healthcare towards integrating technology to enhance patient education and engagement [40, 41]. Specifically, digital interventions, such as mobile apps and online platforms, were found to be effective in increasing knowledge and influencing behaviours. This was highlighted in the studies conducted by Goodman et al. [24] and Rahman et al. [25], which demonstrated the advantages of these methods, including accessibility, convenience, and the ability to engage participants at their own pace. Furthermore, interactive multimedia programs, as seen in studies by Neafsey et al. [21] and Smith et al. [22], were particularly effective among older adults, showcasing significant improvements in knowledge and decision-making. These programs leverage the engagement and interactivity of digital tools while providing comprehensive educational content, making them suitable for populations that may require more engaging and accessible formats. Additionally, personalized counselling and face-to-face interactions were highly effective in addressing specific health concerns and improving patient outcomes, particularly in vulnerable populations such as patients with cancer. Studies by Thakerar et al. [26] and Stie et al. [23] illustrate that these methods facilitate a more tailored approach to education, addressing individual needs and concerns, which can enhance the overall effectiveness of the intervention. In addition to the effectiveness of diverse modes for delivering EIs, the cost-effectiveness of personalized approaches is equally important. Although these methods, particularly in precision medicine, may involve higher initial costs, existing evidence suggest they often yield long-term savings by reducing unnecessary treatments, hospitalizations, and adverse drug reactions [42]. Similarly, while digital platforms are generally more scalable and cost-effective, personalized EIs, such as counselling remains essential for individuals with complex needs or low digital literacy, ensuring that interventions are both accessible and effective.

This study highlights the significant role of digital tools in improving the knowledge of CMs. Neafsey et al. [21] demonstrated that an interactive multimedia program significantly increased older adults’ knowledge about the interactions between prescription medications and CMs, highlighting the potential of multimedia tools to enhance understanding among this population. Similarly, Goodman et al. [24] reported notable improvements in vitamin D knowledge among young adults following an online intervention, illustrating the effectiveness of digital educational tools in improving health literacy among young, tech-savvy populations. These findings underscore the crucial role of educational programs in bridging knowledge gap and promoting the informed use of CMs. Research indicate that the comparative advantage of digital tools lies in their accessibility and engagement, particularly for younger populations, while interactive multimedia programs offer structured and comprehensive content beneficial for consumers with poor literacy and older adults [43]. This suggests that tailoring educational methods to specific demographics can enhance their effectiveness.

Previous research emphasizes the role of EIs in empowering individuals with enhanced decision-making abilities and improved self-efficacy, beyond mere knowledge acquisition, ultimately contributing to safer use of CMs [44]. A key discussion point in our assessment was the role of behavioural change within CMs health literacy, which remains less defined. While knowledge acquisition, self-efficacy, and decision-making align with CMs literacy domains, behavioural change may be a secondary outcome shaped by self-efficacy and adherence rather than a standalone literacy component. Future research should further conceptualize behavioural change within CMs literacy frameworks. Studies by Neafsey et al. [21] and Searson et al. [34] reported significant improvements in self-efficacy, particularly among intervention group [21] and caregivers of cancer patients [34]. Participants in Neafsey et al.‘s [19] study, for example, adjusted calcium supplement timings to avoid medication interactions post-intervention, and Simpson et al. [29] noted reduced intent to use CMs among cancer patients, highlighting more cautious decision-making.

The varied outcomes observed across these studies suggests that the effectiveness of EIs may significantly depend on demographic and health context, pointing to the need for more tailored and context-specific educational strategies. For example, Smith et al. [22] and Shreffler-Grant et al. [32] found no significant improvement in participants’ self-efficacy and decision-making abilities post-intervention. These conflicting results may be due to differences in participants’ health statuses. Both studies included participants with chronic diseases, potentially limiting the capacity for increased self-efficacy through EIs alone. In contrast, Neafsey et al. [21] and Searson et al. [34], who involved generally healthier participants, found them more receptive to EIs. Differences in intervention content, including the types of information presented and the activities employed, could also account for varying levels of effectiveness in enhancing self-efficacy and decision-making skills. However, it is difficult to pinpoint reasons for these differences due to high heterogeneity between studies in terms of study design, participant demographics, and intervention methods. Therefore, it is crucial to consider these factors when developing educational programs to ensure they effectively enhance both knowledge and self-efficacy. This underscores the need for EIs that not only increase knowledge but also build confidence and decision-making skills among diverse populations. This underscores the need for EIs that not only increase knowledge but also build confidence and decision-making skills among diverse populations. Furthermore, a comparison between RCT and non-RCT studies did not reveal substantial differences overall; however, even where differences appeared, they cannot be solely attributed to the study design. Other factors, such as differences in participant demographics and the fact that not all studies assessed all outcome measures, likely influenced the findings. As such, it is not possible to directly compare the outcomes based solely on the type of study design.

Positive health outcomes were also reported, particularly among cancer patients and older adults. Thakerar et al. [26] found high adherence to pharmacist recommendations regarding CMs use, suggesting that informed patients are more likely to follow professional guidance. Additionally, Stie et al. [23] observed that EIs led to improved quality of life and reduced anxiety among cancer patients, highlighting the substantial therapeutic benefits these programs can offer. These findings emphasize the therapeutic potential of well-designed EIs in not only enhancing knowledge and decision-making but also in significantly improving patient outcomes and the overall quality of care. This underscores the necessity of incorporating such educational strategies into standard healthcare practices to achieve comprehensive improvements in patient well-being.

It is also essential to consider participant satisfaction, which was consistently high across various EIs. This highlights the positive reception and overall effectiveness of these programs. The use of interactive and engaging tools, such as digital platforms and face-to-face counselling, received particularly high satisfaction rates. For instance, high satisfaction with digital interventions, such as those reported by Goodman et al. [24] and Neafsey et al. [21], suggests that digital tools should not only enhance knowledge but also actively engage participants through user-friendly and tailored interfaces. This engagement is particularly relevant for younger, digitally adept populations, but also suggests promising opportunities to support older adults, provided interventions are appropriately adapted to their digital literacy and comfort level. Similarly, Thakerar et al. [26] and Smith et al. [33] emphasized that participants valued the clarity and usefulness of information provided in verbal face-to-face communication and printed brochures. Taken together, these findings underscore that successful EIs should focus less on a one-size-fits-all approach and instead consider a diverse understanding of diverse patient experiences, technology access, and individual preferences. By doing so, future programs can better facilitate informed decision-making and self-efficacy, ultimately enhancing consumers empowerment and improving healthcare outcomes.

Overall, the findings from this systematic review underscore the critical importance of tailored, engaging and accessible EIs for enhancing knowledge, self-efficacy, and decision-making regarding CMs. Given the demonstrated benefits and positive reception by participants, integrating well-designed EIs into healthcare practices is essential. Future research should further explore strategies to effectively tailor interventions to diverse populations, ensuring broad applicability and optimal impact on consumer health outcomes.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this review is its focus on evaluating EIs aimed at improving CMs knowledge among consumers, patients, and caregivers. Additionally, the inclusion of diverse intervention types and delivery methods provides a comprehensive understanding of effective educational strategies. Although a relatively small number of studies were included, this was due to the strict selection criteria, which aimed to maintain the rigor and reliability of the review. Furthermore, this review has screened EIs using updated risk of bias assessment tools, which helps better understand the gaps and provides deeper insight into the limitations of the included studies. This ultimately could help in designing improved EIs for future studies.

However, the review has several limitations. Some studies reported attrition rates, which affected the robustness of the findings. For example, Goodman et al. [24] experienced a 17% loss to follow-up, potentially introducing bias and affecting the generalizability of the results. Additionally, many studies relied on self-reported data, which may introduce response bias and affect the accuracy of the findings. Another limitation is the focus on specific populations, such as cancer patients [26] and pregnant women [25], limiting the generalizability of the findings to broader populations, although they do provide valuable insights into these specific groups. Similarly, a lack of detailed reporting on the specific types of CMs used in some studies limits the interpretation and generalizability of the findings. Moreover, restricting inclusion criteria to studies published in English potentially excludes significant evidence from non-English-speaking regions, where complementary medicine use is prevalent. Furthermore, this review did not evaluate the accuracy of the information within the EIs themselves. Ensuring educational content accuracy is critical, as effectively delivered but inaccurate information might lead to harmful health outcomes. Lastly, we acknowledge that this review did not address all dimensions of health literacy related to CMs, primarily due to the absence of a standardized measure for assessing CM product-specific health literacy and reliance on factors previously identified in existing literature.

Gaps and future directions

This review identifies several gaps in the existing literature and suggests directions for future research. There is a need for long-term studies to assess the sustained effects of EIs on CMs use and health behaviours, as most current studies focus on short-term outcomes. More research involving diverse socio-economic and ethnic groups is crucial to enhance the generalizability of the findings, as the majority of studies have been conducted in high-income countries with relatively homogeneous populations. Future research should also incorporate robust behavioural outcome measures and develop comprehensive evaluation tools to accurately assess the effectiveness of EIs. Moving beyond self-reported data to include more objective measures of behaviour and health-related outcomes will significantly enhance the reliability and validity of future research findings.

Given the positive results associated with digital and interactive interventions, future studies should continue to explore and innovate in this area. Mobile apps, online platforms, and other digital tools should be tailored to specific populations and evaluated for their effectiveness in various settings. Integrating EIs into routine healthcare practices can maximize their accessibility and impact. Collaborations between healthcare providers and educational institutions can facilitate the development of comprehensive programs that address the informational needs of consumers, patients and caregivers [45]. Developing standardized evaluation frameworks to assess multiple dimensions of EIs, such as knowledge, self-efficacy, behavioural changes, and satisfaction, is essential. Adopting these frameworks across studies will enable more consistent and comparable assessments of intervention effectiveness.

Conclusion

This systematic review demonstrates that EIs can enhance knowledge, decision-making abilities, and self-efficacy among CMs users. The positive impacts on health behaviours and outcomes, along with high participant satisfaction, underscore the importance of these interventions in healthcare. Future research should focus on addressing current limitations and further exploring the long-term benefits of educational programs tailored to diverse populations. By doing so, more effective and inclusive educational programs can be developed that promote safer and more informed use of CMs, which can ultimately improve the consumer well-being and the overall quality of care.
