Exosomes serve as natural nanocarriers targeting cancer stem cells to advance precision oncology
Authors: Tareq Nayef AlRamadneh, Fitra Ari Aditya, Waleed K Abdulsahib, Ihsan Khudhair Jasim, H Malathi, Priya Priyadarshini Nayak, D Alex Anand, Gunjan Mukherjee, Aashna Sinha, Mehrigul Hayitova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer stem cells (CSCs) represent a formidable challenge in oncology, serving as the primary drivers of tumor initiation, progression, metastasis, and, critically, therapeutic resistance and recurrence. Their inherent self-renewal capacity and phenotypic plasticity enable them to evade conventional treatments, necessitating novel therapeutic strategies. Extracellular vesicles (EVs), particularly exosomes, have emerged as pivotal mediators of intercellular communication within the tumor microenvironment (TME), carrying a diverse cargo of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids that profoundly influence cellular behavior. This review synthesizes the current understanding of exosome biogenesis and cargo loading, highlighting their natural advantages as nanocarriers due to their low immunogenicity, high biocompatibility, and intrinsic ability to traverse biological barriers. It further explores the intricate roles of tumor-derived exosomes in fostering cancer progression and maintaining CSC properties by modulating the TME and promoting immune evasion. Crucially, this report delves into the burgeoning field of exosome- and EV-based CSC targeting, detailing strategies that leverage both naturally occurring and engineered exosomes for precise drug delivery, gene therapy, and immunomodulation. While significant challenges persist in large-scale production, isolation, cargo loading efficiency, and regulatory standardization, the transformative potential of engineered exosomes for targeted CSC eradication is immense. Future directions emphasize advanced bioengineering, multi-modal therapeutic combinations, and rigorous clinical translation to unlock the full promise of exosome-based therapies in achieving durable remissions and improving patient outcomes.Keywords: Exosomes, Cancer stem cells, Nanocarriers, Targeted therapy, Precision oncology

Introduction

Cancer remains a pervasive global health crisis, characterized by its remarkable heterogeneity and intrinsic adaptability, which frequently undermine the efficacy of conventional and targeted therapies alike [1, 2]. Within the complex cellular landscape of tumors, a distinct and highly resilient subpopulation, known as cancer stem cells (CSCs) [3], has garnered significant attention. These rare cells possess properties analogous to normal stem cells, including robust self-renewal capacity and the ability to differentiate into the diverse phenotypes observed within a tumor, thereby maintaining intratumoral heterogeneity [3, 4] CSCs are widely recognized as the fundamental drivers of tumor initiation, sustained growth, progression, invasion, and distant metastasis [4, 5] Critically, CSCs exhibit an enhanced intrinsic resistance to conventional anti-cancer therapies, including chemotherapy and radiotherapy, which frequently leads to treatment failure, disease recurrence, and poor patient prognosis [4, 6]. The quiescent (G0) state adopted by many CSCs further protects them from treatments primarily targeting rapidly proliferating cells, creating a formidable therapeutic dilemma where eliminating the bulk tumor can inadvertently stimulate quiescent CSCs to activate and drive further tumorigenesis [7, 8]. This inherent resistance and the dynamic plasticity of CSCs—their ability to interconvert between stem-like and differentiated states—underscore the urgent need for CSC-specific targeting strategies to achieve durable clinical responses [9].

In parallel, the field of intercellular communication has been revolutionized by the discovery and characterization of extracellular vesicles (EVs), particularly exosomes [10]. These nanoscale, lipid bilayer-bound vesicles, ranging from 30 to 150 nm in diameter, are naturally secreted by virtually all living cells and are found in all bodily fluids [11]. Exosomes serve as crucial mediators of cell-to-cell communication by encapsulating and transporting a diverse cargo of proteins, lipids, DNA, and various RNA species, including microRNAs (miRNAs) and long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) [12–15]. This unique ability to transfer biological information between donor and recipient cells allows exosomes to influence a wide array of physiological and pathological processes, including immune responses, tissue repair, and, significantly, cancer progression [16, 17]. Their inherent biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and remarkable capacity to traverse biological barriers, such as the blood-brain barrier, position exosomes as highly attractive natural nanocarriers for targeted drug delivery [18, 19]. Foundational mechanistic frameworks and biomedical applications of exosomes/EVs are comprehensively summarized in landmark reviews that define the field’s conceptual baseline [17].

While the individual roles of exosomes and CSCs are well-documented, a critical gap remains in understanding the bi-directional feedback loops that sustain the CSC niche. This review moves beyond a descriptive summary of exosome biology; it critically examines the paradigm shift from viewing exosomes as ‘cellular waste’ to identifying them as ‘precision-guided signaling hubs.’ We construct a narrative loop that progresses from the fundamental mechanisms of CSC-exosome crosstalk to the bioengineering logic required to hijack these pathways, concluding with a critical analysis of the clinical translation ‘bottlenecks’ and the next-generation solutions required to overcome them.

Exosomes and extracellular vesicles: biogenesis, cargo, and intercellular communication

Exosomes are a distinct subset of extracellular vesicles (EVs) that play a fundamental role in mediating intercellular communication. Their unique characteristics, including their nanoscale size and specific cargo, are a direct consequence of their biogenesis and the sophisticated mechanisms governing their content.

Biogenesis and release

Exosomes originate from the inward budding of the plasma membrane, forming early endosomes. These early endosomes then mature into late endosomes and multivesicular bodies (MVBs), which contain intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) [20, 21]. The formation of these ILVs within MVBs is primarily driven by the endosomal sorting complexes required for transport (ESCRT) machinery, a family of proteins that orchestrate cargo sorting and vesicle budding [21, 22]. However, ESCRT-independent pathways involving ceramides or RAB31 have also been identified [21]. Once formed, MVBs can either fuse with lysosomes for degradation or, crucially, fuse with the plasma membrane to release their encapsulated ILVs (exosomes) into the extracellular environment through exocytosis [21, 23]. This process allows cells to actively secrete exosomes, enabling them to transmit biological messages to neighboring or distant cells [21].

Diverse cargo and functional significance

Exosomes are rich in a diverse array of biomolecules that reflect the composition and physiological status of their parent cells[24, 25]. Their cargo includes:

Proteins: Exosomal membranes are embedded with various cell surface proteins, including tetraspanins (CD63, CD81, CD9) and integrins, which are crucial for exosome navigation, targeting, and attachment to recipient cells [25–27]. The intraluminal compartment contains proteins involved in signaling pathways (e.g., Wnt proteins, Notch ligand DLL4), cell-cell communication (e.g., interleukins), and enzymatic activities [28, 29].

The specific cargo loaded into exosomes is not random, indicating the existence of sophisticated sorting mechanisms [35, 36]. Mechanistically, several well-characterized examples illustrate how “selective sorting” occurs. First, RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) can recognize sequence or structural “zip codes” on RNAs and actively enrich them into exosomes; for instance, heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins (hnRNPs) and related RBPs have been reported to bind specific miRNA motifs and promote their packaging into small EVs [37, 38]. Second, ESCRT-associated adaptors contribute to protein sorting: ubiquitinated membrane proteins can be recognized by ESCRT components (e.g., via TSG101/ALIX-associated pathways), facilitating their inclusion into intraluminal vesicles during multivesicular body maturation [39, 40]. These representative mechanisms support the concept that exosome cargo reflects regulated selection rather than passive cytosolic sampling. This selective packaging allows exosomes to deliver precise biological messages, making them potent mediators of intercellular communication [15, 35].

These mechanisms allow exosomes to signal in both autocrine and paracrine modes and to influence cells locally and at a distance [46]. These uptake and signaling routes are not functionally equivalent and may dominate under different biological scenarios. For long-distance signaling—such as systemic immune modulation or pre-metastatic niche conditioning—circulating exosomes are more commonly internalized by recipient cells through endocytic routes (e.g., clathrin-mediated endocytosis, macropinocytosis, or phagocytosis), which enables efficient cargo delivery after vascular transport. In contrast, communication within spatially confined niches (e.g., CSC–CAF or CSC–immune cell contacts) can be mediated by close-range mechanisms such as direct membrane fusion and juxtacrine receptor engagement, where surface ligands trigger signaling in adjacent cells even without full internalization [47, 48]. Thus, mechanism selection can be influenced by distance, recipient cell type, and microenvironmental context.

The paradigm shift: from debris to determinants

Historically, the study of EVs has evolved through three distinct research paradigms. Initially regarded as a mechanism for disposing of unwanted cellular components, the field shifted in the late 2000 s to recognizing exosomes as mediators of local paracrine signaling. The current paradigm, however, identifies exosomes as systemic ‘architects’ of the pre-metastatic niche. A core controversy remains regarding the heterogeneity of EV populations; the inability to perfectly isolate ‘exosomes’ from ‘microvesicles’ has contributed to variability in isolation methods, nomenclature, and functional readouts across studies, complicating cross-study comparison and mechanistic attribution. This manuscript adopts the term ‘exosomes’ to refer specifically to endosomally-derived vesicles, while acknowledging that their functional synergy with other EV subsets is a key turning point in current oncology research. To reduce terminology-driven irreproducibility, we align our nomenclature and reporting expectations with the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) Minimal Information for Studies of Extracellular Vesicles (MISEV2023), which emphasizes operational definitions, transparent isolation workflows, and multi-parameter characterization rather than assuming vesicle subtype purity [49]. In this review, we use ‘EVs’ as the umbrella term and ‘exosomes’ when referring to small EVs of endosomal origin, consistent with current community guidelines (Fig. 1).

Biogenesis, cargo, communication, and evolving paradigms. Exosomes are small extracellular vesicles of endosomal origin that arise through the inward budding of early endosomes to form ILVs within late endosomes or MVBs, a process regulated by ESCRT-dependent and ESCRT-independent mechanisms involving proteins, lipids, and small GTPases. MVBs can either fuse with lysosomes for degradation or fuse with the plasma membrane to release ILVs as exosomes into the extracellular environment. Exosomes carry a diverse and selectively packaged cargo reflective of their parent cells, including tetraspanins (e.g., CD63, CD81, CD9), integrins, cell-surface proteins, signaling and enzymatic proteins, nucleic acids (DNA, mRNA, microRNAs, and other non-coding RNAs), and bioactive lipids such as cholesterol and sphingomyelins. Once released, exosomes mediate intercellular communication through multiple mechanisms, including direct membrane fusion with recipient cells, uptake via clathrin- or caveolin-mediated endocytosis and phagocytosis, receptor–ligand interactions at the cell surface, and juxtacrine signaling, thereby enabling autocrine and paracrine effects over short and long distances. Historically viewed as cellular waste disposal vehicles, exosomes are now recognized as important mediators of local and systemic signaling, contributing to physiological regulation and disease processes, including their emerging role as key determinants shaping tissue microenvironments and pre-metastatic niches, while ongoing challenges remain in defining their heterogeneity, terminology, and functional overlap with other extracellular vesicle populations

The role of exosomes in cancer progression and CSC maintenance

Exosomes secreted by tumor cells, particularly cancer stem cells (CSCs), are not merely waste disposal units but active participants in shaping the tumor microenvironment (TME) and promoting various hallmarks of cancer, including CSC maintenance, metastasis, and therapeutic resistance.

The bi-directional loop: exosomes as architects of the CSC niche

Tumor-derived exosomes (TDEs) actively contribute to tumorigenesis through multiple coordinated mechanisms. One major function is the reprogramming of stromal cells within the tumor microenvironment. By transferring specific microRNAs, such as miR-146a, and other bioactive molecules, TDEs can convert normal fibroblasts into cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), which subsequently promote extracellular matrix remodeling, tumor growth, and immunosuppression [50–53]. In parallel, TDEs play a crucial role in angiogenesis by delivering pro-angiogenic factors, including vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), and angiogenesis-related microRNAs to endothelial cells, thereby activating angiogenic signaling pathways and stimulating neovascularization [54, 55]. Furthermore, TDEs facilitate tumor invasion and metastasis by inducing epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), remodeling the extracellular matrix, and promoting tumor cell intravasation. CSC-derived exosomes are particularly enriched in stemness- and metastasis-associated transcripts, enabling recipient cells to acquire enhanced tumorigenic and invasive properties [56–58]. Importantly, the EMT program induced by tumor-derived exosomes is tightly linked to CSC stemness rather than being a purely migratory phenotype. EMT activation can promote CSC-like properties by converging on self-renewal-associated signaling networks, including Wnt/β-catenin, Notch, NF-κB, and TGF-β axes. Exosomal cargo can contribute to this coupling in two ways: (i) delivery of regulatory RNAs (miRNAs/lncRNAs) that suppress epithelial differentiation programs and derepress stemness pathways, and (ii) transfer of pro-EMT signaling factors that activate EMT transcriptional circuits in recipient cells [59, 60]. Through these mechanisms, exosome-driven EMT can increase phenotypic plasticity, enabling non-CSCs to acquire stem-like traits and thereby expanding therapy-resistant tumor subpopulations. This EMT–stemness coupling provides a mechanistic rationale for targeting exosome cargo and uptake routes as an upstream strategy to prevent CSC enrichment during progression and therapy. Beyond direct effects on tumor and stromal cells, tumor-derived extracellular vesicles also participate in complex multicellular feedback networks involving immune cells and the microbiota. Recent work has highlighted how tumor cells exploit EV-mediated communication to recruit and functionally reprogram macrophages toward tumor-supportive phenotypes, while simultaneously shaping microbial communities within the tumor microenvironment. These “domesticated” macrophages and microbiota engage in reciprocal interactions that reinforce chronic inflammation, immune tolerance, and metabolic adaptation, collectively promoting tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. This EV-centered macrophage–microbiota–tumor axis underscores the systemic and ecological dimensions of exosome-mediated tumor regulation [61].

Exosomes in CSC maintenance and plasticity

Exosome-mediated communication is essential for maintaining CSC populations and driving their phenotypic plasticity within the tumor microenvironment. CSCs reside in specialized niches that provide biochemical and mechanical cues supporting self-renewal and survival, and exosomes secreted by CSCs and stromal cells actively shape this niche [62]. For example, cancer-associated fibroblast–derived exosomes transfer regulatory microRNAs and long non-coding RNAs that promote CSC proliferation and stemness [7, 63, 64]. Exosomes also induce epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), a process closely linked to the acquisition of stem-like traits, enabling differentiated tumor cells to revert to a CSC-like state and reinforcing tumor heterogeneity [64]. In addition, exosomes contribute significantly to therapeutic resistance by transporting RNAs and proteins that enhance drug efflux, DNA repair, and anti-apoptotic signaling pathways [57, 65]. Finally, CSC-derived exosomes participate in immune evasion by suppressing cytotoxic T-cell and natural killer cell activity, inhibiting dendritic cell maturation, and delivering immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1, thereby fostering an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [66–68].

A pivotal turning point in this field is the discovery of ‘phenotypic plasticity’—the ability of differentiated non-CSCs to revert to a stem-like state via exosomal induction [69]. This challenges the classical hierarchical model of cancer. The controversy lies in whether exosomal cargo (such as miR-146a or Wnt ligands) provides the instruction for stemness or merely the environment that allows it to manifest. Understanding this distinction is vital: if exosomes only provide the environment, targeting the TME is sufficient; if they provide the instruction, we must neutralize the cargo itself. This debate persists because cargo-function relationships are often context-dependent, influenced by EV subtype mixtures, recipient cell state, and microenvironmental selection pressures [49].

Exosomal cargo determinants and their functional consequences in tumor progression

Beyond vesicle abundance, the biological impact of tumor-derived exosomes (TDEs) is largely defined by their molecular cargo [70, 71]. Functionally relevant cargo classes include (i) surface proteins that control biodistribution and immune interactions, (ii) soluble proteins that activate oncogenic pathways, and (iii) nucleic acids that reprogram recipient-cell transcriptional states [72, 73]. For example, integrins and tetraspanins on exosomal membranes can influence organotropic uptake and metastatic niche conditioning, while immune checkpoint cargo such as exosomal PD-L1 supports systemic immune suppression and resistance to immunotherapy. Recent work emphasizes that exosomal PD-L1 is not merely a correlate of disease burden but can actively suppress anti-tumor T cell responses at distant sites, thereby facilitating progression and treatment resistance [74].

At the signaling level, exosomal proteins and RNAs converge on stemness- and invasion-associated pathways. Exosomal Wnt ligands, Notch-related components, TGF-β–linked regulators, and EMT-associated factors promote epithelial–mesenchymal plasticity and enhance migratory phenotypes, while exosomal microRNAs and long non-coding RNAs modulate drug efflux, apoptosis resistance, and DNA repair programs [75, 76]. These cargo-driven effects are amplified by microenvironmental feedback loops: exosomes reprogram fibroblasts and immune cells, which in turn secrete vesicles and soluble factors that reinforce CSC maintenance and metastatic competence. Collectively, a “cargo-to-phenotype” framework is essential for identifying which exosomal components are mechanistic drivers versus passive biomarkers and for prioritizing therapeutic targets.

These cargo-defined mechanisms identify actionable intervention points, which directly inform the engineering logic and targeting designs discussed next.

A unified framework: exosome-driven regulation of CSCs within the tumor ecosystem

Rather than acting as isolated signaling entities, exosomes function as integrative regulators that coordinate CSC-intrinsic programs with tumor microenvironmental remodeling. CSCs secrete exosomes enriched in selective proteins, lipids, and regulatory RNAs that reinforce stemness, metabolic flexibility, immune evasion, and phenotypic plasticity. These vesicles reprogram stromal cells, immune populations, and endothelial compartments, which in turn generate reciprocal exosomal and soluble signals that stabilize the CSC niche [77, 78].

At the molecular level, exosome-mediated regulation converges on four interconnected axes: (i) activation of stemness and survival signaling pathways, (ii) metabolic reprogramming favoring glycolytic and oxidative adaptability, (iii) epigenetic remodeling that sustains transcriptional plasticity, and (iv) immune suppression through checkpoint signaling and macrophage polarization [79, 80]. This bidirectional exosome-driven circuitry creates a self-reinforcing ecosystem that promotes tumor progression and therapeutic resistance. Importantly, these same communication routes represent actionable vulnerabilities that can be intercepted through engineered exosome-based therapeutic strategies.

Mechanistic axes of exosome-mediated CSC regulation

Exosome-mediated regulation of CSC behavior extends beyond classical signaling pathways and encompasses metabolic, epigenetic, and immune-modulatory mechanisms. Metabolically, exosomal cargo can promote glycolytic reprogramming, mitochondrial adaptability, and redox balance, enabling CSCs to survive fluctuating nutrient and oxygen conditions. Exosomes derived from stromal and tumor cells have been shown to transfer metabolites, enzymes, and regulatory RNAs that support metabolic plasticity and therapy resistance [81, 82].

Epigenetically, exosomes deliver non-coding RNAs and chromatin-modifying regulators that reshape transcriptional landscapes in recipient cells, sustaining stemness-associated gene expression programs and suppressing differentiation. This exosome-driven epigenetic flexibility underlies CSC phenotypic plasticity and contributes to reversible drug tolerance states [83, 84].

Immune evasion represents a further critical axis, with exosomal PD-L1, immunosuppressive cytokines, and macrophage-polarizing signals collectively suppressing cytotoxic immune responses. By coordinating these metabolic, epigenetic, and immune-modulatory mechanisms, exosomes act as central orchestrators of CSC persistence within hostile therapeutic environments (Fig. 2) [85, 86].

Hijacking the communication axis: translating mechanism into targeted therapy

The multifaceted roles of exosomes in cancer progression and CSC maintenance highlight their immense potential as therapeutic tools. Strategies for exosome-based CSC targeting involve leveraging both naturally occurring and engineered exosomes. A major translational turning point was the demonstration that engineered exosomes can target a traditionally ‘undruggable’ oncogene driver. In pancreatic cancer models, exosome-mediated delivery of siRNA/shRNA against mutant KRAS showed improved in vivo retention and anti-tumor efficacy compared with conventional nanoparticles, partly attributed to CD47-mediated resistance to phagocytic clearance [87].

Naturally occurring exosomes as drug carriers

Exosomes possess several intrinsic properties that make them attractive drug carriers, including high biocompatibility and low immunogenicity, which enable repeated administration with minimal immune activation [88, 89]. Their stable lipid bilayer protects therapeutic cargo from enzymatic degradation, prolonging circulation time [90]. Moreover, their nanoscale size allows efficient tissue penetration and, in some cases, traversal of biological barriers such as the blood–brain barrier [18, 91]. Exosomes exhibit very high cellular uptake efficiency through direct interaction with extracellular proteins, membrane fusion, or endocytosis [92]. They also can be isolated from various sources, including mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), dendritic cells (DCs), and even bodily fluids like blood and urine [93]. MSC-derived exosomes, for instance, are produced in high quantities, are immunologically less reactive, and can serve as carriers for diverse small molecules or chemotherapy drugs [18, 94]. Furthermore, Exosomes can encapsulate a wide range of therapeutic agents, including chemotherapeutic drugs (e.g., doxorubicin, gemcitabine), nucleic acids (miRNAs, siRNAs), and proteins [95]. For example, exosome-based delivery of doxorubicin has shown higher intracellular accumulation and cytotoxicity in tumor cells with minimal toxicity to normal cells [28, 95].

Engineered exosomes for enhanced CSC targeting

The logic of engineered exosomes stems directly from the ‘homing’ mechanisms discussed in Section II. Because exosome biodistribution and uptake are influenced by membrane proteins (e.g., tetraspanins/integrins) and the recipient cell state, engineering strategies increasingly aim to exploit these natural ‘address labels’ to enrich delivery to CSC-like populations and their niches. Seminal in vivo work demonstrated that surface-engineered exosomes can deliver siRNA systemically to achieve tissue-specific knockdown, validating the concept of programmable exosome tropism [96]. While naturally occurring exosomes offer many advantages, their weak targeting capabilities and susceptibility to lysosomal engulfment upon entry into the body often limit their therapeutic application [18, 97]. To overcome these limitations, exosomes are increasingly subjected to bioengineering modifications for functional enhancement and improved stability [18, 98]. Emerging preclinical and early translational studies provide concrete examples supporting the therapeutic potential of engineered exosomes. A landmark study demonstrated that engineered exosomes loaded with siRNA targeting mutant KRAS significantly suppressed tumor growth in pancreatic cancer models, highlighting their ability to address traditionally “undruggable” oncogenic drivers [87]. More recently, combinatorial strategies have been explored in which exosome-mediated delivery of nucleic acids or small-molecule inhibitors is paired with immune checkpoint blockade, resulting in enhanced anti-tumor immune responses and improved therapeutic efficacy compared with monotherapy alone. These examples underscore the feasibility of integrating exosome-based nanotherapies into multi-modal cancer treatment regimens.

Exosomes can be engineered with surface decorations, such as specific ligands or antibodies, to improve their targeting specificity to CSCs [18, 99]. This allows for precise delivery to CSCs expressing specific surface markers. Moreover, CSC-specific markers like CD44, CD133, and EpCAM can be utilized as targeting candidates [100]. Since CSC markers can vary between tumors, future strategies may involve multiple-antibody coated exosomes to improve targeting efficiency and reduce off-target effects [99, 101]. Exosomes can be loaded with various therapeutic compounds, including chemotherapeutics, inhibitors of CSC signaling, siRNAs/miRNAs targeting oncogenes or CSC pathways, and pro-apoptotic proteins [101, 102] Methods for cargo loading include electroporation, sonication, and incubation [103]. Exosomes loaded with inhibitors, miRNAs, or siRNAs can target crucial CSC signaling pathways such as Wnt, Notch, Hippo, Hedgehog, NF-κB, and TGF-β, which are vital for maintaining CSC properties like self-renewal, differentiation, tumor initiation, and drug resistance [104, 105]. Engineered exosomes can carry immunomodulatory cargo to regulate the tumor immune microenvironment [106]. Among emerging payloads, mRNA-based therapeutics delivered via exosomes have attracted increasing interest for cancer treatment and vaccination. Exosomes can encapsulate therapeutic mRNAs either through endogenous loading during donor-cell engineering or via exogenous approaches such as electroporation and lipid-assisted complexation. Compared with synthetic lipid nanoparticles, exosome-mediated mRNA delivery offers enhanced stability, reduced immunogenicity, and improved cellular uptake in certain tumor contexts. Recent advances in EV-based mRNA vaccines and gene-replacement strategies further highlight the versatility of exosomes as platforms for transient but potent therapeutic protein expression in cancer cells and immune modulatory cells [107]. DC-derived exosomes, for example, can present antigens and immune-related proteins, triggering anti-tumor immune responses and contributing to tumor regression [18] Exosomes can also be used as anti-CSC vaccines by presenting CSC-specific antigens to T cells, activating anti-CSC immunization [101, 108]. Actionable next-step pathways include: (i) CRISPR editing of donor/producer cells to stably express targeting ligands (e.g., peptide/antibody fragments fused to exosomal membrane proteins) and/or to enrich loading of therapeutic RNAs; (ii) engineering immune-evasive pharmacokinetics by increasing anti-phagocytic signals (e.g., CD47 display) to reduce reticuloendothelial clearance; and (iii) rational combination regimens, such as exosome-delivered siRNA/miRNA targeting CSC pathways paired with immune checkpoint blockade to convert niche disruption into durable immune control.

Targeting strategies for exosome-based biomimetic nanodelivery systems

Targeting strategies for exosome-based biomimetic nanodelivery systems can be broadly classified into passive, active, and hybrid approaches. Passive targeting exploits the intrinsic biodistribution of exosomes and their preferential accumulation in tumors through enhanced permeability and retention effects. Active targeting strategies involve surface engineering of exosomes with ligands, peptides, antibodies, or aptamers that recognize CSC-associated markers such as CD44, CD133, and EpCAM, thereby enhancing selective uptake by target cell populations.

In addition, genetic engineering of donor cells enables the stable expression of targeting moieties fused to exosomal membrane proteins, allowing scalable and reproducible production of targeted exosomes. Biomimetic hybrid strategies further combine exosomal membranes with synthetic nanoparticle cores, leveraging the immune-evasive properties of exosomes while retaining the structural tunability of synthetic systems. Finally, homotypic targeting—where exosomes derived from tumor or CSCs preferentially home back to their cells of origin—offers a unique mechanism for niche-specific drug delivery, although careful control is required to avoid pro-tumorigenic signaling.

Exosomes versus synthetic nanocarriers: comparative advantages and limitations

Exosomes offer several distinct advantages over conventional synthetic nanocarriers, including liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, and lipid nanoparticles (LNPs). As endogenous vesicles, exosomes exhibit high biocompatibility and low immunogenicity, enabling repeated administration with reduced risk of immune activation. Their native membrane proteins facilitate efficient cellular uptake and, in some cases, organ- or cell-type–specific homing, which is difficult to replicate using fully synthetic systems [91, 114]. Moreover, exosomes demonstrate an intrinsic ability to traverse biological barriers, such as the blood–brain barrier, and protect sensitive cargo from enzymatic degradation [115].

However, these advantages are counterbalanced by notable limitations. Exosome production yields remain relatively low compared with scalable synthetic platforms such as LNPs, and batch-to-batch heterogeneity complicates standardization and regulatory approval [116, 117]. In contrast, synthetic nanocarriers offer superior control over size, composition, and payload encapsulation efficiency, as well as well-established large-scale manufacturing pipelines. Nonetheless, synthetic systems often require extensive surface modification to achieve targeting specificity and may trigger complement activation or off-target immune responses [118, 119].

Therefore, exosomes and synthetic nanocarriers should be viewed as complementary rather than competing technologies. Hybrid and biomimetic strategies that integrate the biological advantages of exosomes with the scalability and tunability of synthetic nanomaterials represent a promising direction for next-generation drug delivery systems. Collectively, these strategies align with recent comprehensive reviews of EV-based RNA therapeutics, which emphasize targeted delivery, RNA stability, and safety optimization as central design principles for next-generation cancer nanomedicines [120].

The clinical dilemma: bridging the gap between lab promise and patient bedside

Despite the immense promise of exosome- and EV-based CSC targeting, their widespread clinical translation faces several significant challenges. Addressing these hurdles is crucial for realizing their full therapeutic potential. A critical counterpoint is that exosomes are not inherently therapeutic: tumor-derived exosomes can promote immune suppression, angiogenesis, and metastasis, raising the risk that insufficiently defined or contaminated preparations could amplify oncogenic signaling. Therefore, therapeutic development must explicitly separate ‘carrier exosomes’ (well-defined source and cargo) from disease-propagating EVs [121].

Current clinical status: CSC-derived exosomes in diagnosis and therapy

At present, direct clinical use of CSC-derived exosomes as therapeutics is limited by safety and standardization concerns, because CSC/tumor-derived vesicles may carry pro-metastatic and immune-evasive cargo. Accordingly, most clinical development focuses on (i) exosomes/EVs as biomarkers for noninvasive diagnosis and monitoring, and (ii) engineered exosomes derived from controlled producer cells (e.g., MSCs or HEK-derived systems) as delivery vehicles rather than using tumor/CSC exosomes themselves [122].

For diagnosis, exosome-based liquid biopsy approaches are actively investigated for early detection, prognosis, and therapy-response monitoring. Exosomal nucleic acids and proteins can reflect tumor genetic and phenotypic states, and exosomal immune checkpoint cargo such as PD-L1 has been proposed as a predictive biomarker for response to immune checkpoint blockade in several cancers [122].

Beyond manufacturing considerations, exosome biodistribution, systemic clearance, and stability remain major translational barriers. Circulating exosomes are rapidly sequestered by the liver, spleen, and reticuloendothelial system, limiting tumor accumulation. In addition, subtle changes in exosome composition can alter immunogenicity and clearance kinetics, complicating dose standardization [131, 132]. From a regulatory perspective, the absence of consensus classification—whether exosomes should be regulated as biologics, cell-derived products, or drug delivery systems—continues to delay clinical approval pathways. Current clinical trials therefore prioritize safety and feasibility over efficacy, underscoring the early translational stage of the field [127, 133].

Future directions

Overcoming these challenges will require concerted efforts leveraging advanced technologies and interdisciplinary collaboration. Future research must focus on developing standardized, scalable, and cost-effective methods for exosome production, potentially through advanced bioreactor designs and genetic engineering of parent cells to boost yield. This can be achieved via the implementation of GMP-aligned, closed-system bioreactor platforms coupled with standardized release criteria and quality control parameters, harmonized with International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) reporting and characterization guidelines.

Innovations in cargo loading strategies are needed to improve loading efficiency while preserving exosome integrity and biological function. Further advancements in surface modification techniques will enhance targeting specificity toward CSCs and their unique niches, potentially incorporating AI-guided ligand selection. This pathway involves the integration of producer-cell genetic engineering (e.g., stable expression of targeting ligands or RNA-sorting motifs) with post-isolation chemical conjugation strategies, such as click-chemistry–based surface functionalization, to achieve scalable, reproducible, and modular exosome engineering.

Despite promising preclinical data, the number of exosome-based clinical trials in oncology remains limited. Well-designed clinical studies are required to rigorously evaluate safety, biodistribution, efficacy, and long-term outcomes of exosome-based therapeutics. This can be advanced through the adoption of basket-style early-phase clinical trial designs incorporating biodistribution, pharmacokinetic (PK), and functional potency assays as co-primary translational endpoints to accelerate clinical decision-making and de-risk late-stage development.

A deeper understanding of CSC-specific biomarkers and their dynamic expression patterns will enable the design of more precise exosome-based delivery systems that can effectively discriminate between malignant and healthy cells. Exosome-based therapies are likely to be most effective when combined with conventional treatments (chemotherapy, radiotherapy) or other advanced modalities (immunotherapy, targeted small molecules) to achieve synergistic anti-tumor effects and overcome resistance. In addition to therapeutic development, emerging three-dimensional (3D) tumor organoid–extracellular vesicle platforms offer powerful tools for translational discovery. Tumor organoids recapitulate key architectural, genetic, and phenotypic features of patient tumors, and recent studies have shown that organoid-derived extracellular vesicles retain tumor-specific molecular signatures, including oncogenic RNAs and proteins reflective of the parental tumor. These organoid-secreted EVs have been proposed as scalable platforms for biomarker discovery, drug screening, and evaluation of nanosystem uptake and efficacy in a patient-relevant context. Integrating organoid–EV systems with engineered exosome therapeutics may therefore accelerate personalized nanomedicine development and improve the predictive power of preclinical testing.

Conclusion

Cancer stem cells, with their inherent self-renewal, differentiation potential, and remarkable plasticity, represent the persistent root of tumor malignancy and therapeutic resistance. The intricate crosstalk between CSCs and their dynamic tumor microenvironment, mediated significantly by extracellular vesicles like exosomes, dictates tumor progression, metastasis, and immune evasion. Exosomes, as natural nanocarriers, offer unparalleled advantages due to their biocompatibility, low immunogenicity, and intrinsic ability to traverse biological barriers, making them ideal vehicles for targeted drug delivery to CSCs.

The burgeoning field of exosome engineering has further amplified this potential, enabling precise surface modification for enhanced CSC targeting and sophisticated cargo loading to deliver therapeutic agents, gene-editing tools, or immunomodulatory molecules. These engineered exosomes hold immense promise for disrupting CSC-intrinsic pathways, reprogramming the supportive TME, and sensitizing resistant tumor populations to conventional and advanced therapies.

However, the journey from preclinical promise to widespread clinical application is fraught with challenges, particularly concerning the scalability of exosome production, the complexities of isolation and purification, the efficiency of cargo loading, and the establishment of robust regulatory frameworks. Addressing these hurdles through continued innovation in bioengineering, standardization of manufacturing processes, and rigorous clinical validation is paramount.

The future of cancer therapy demands a multi-modal approach that integrates the power of exosome-based CSC targeting with other cutting-edge strategies. By leveraging the unique biological properties of exosomes to precisely deliver therapeutic payloads and modulate the intricate tumor ecosystem, we can aspire to overcome the inherent resistance of CSCs, achieve more durable remissions, and ultimately transform the therapeutic landscape for cancer patients.
