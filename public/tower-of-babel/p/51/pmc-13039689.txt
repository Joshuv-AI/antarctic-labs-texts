Narrative Dialogue Dataset: Speaker and Emotion Annotated Conversational Corpus
Authors: Uzma Patil, Sejal Hanmante, Shivani Patil, Aniket K. Shahade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This paper presents a novel synthetic, automatically annotated dialogue-style dataset derived from narrative short stories, formatted as Speaker(Emotion): Dialogue. The corpus comprises 2,588 stories containing 27,074 dialogue utterances attributed to 2,582 unique characters, each annotated with one of six emotion categories (Joy, Sadness, Anger, Fear, Disgust, Surprise). Built from a Hugging Face story corpus, the dataset captures speaker attribution and emotional context across dialogues. To accurately identify speakers and their genders, we employed FastCoref with masked dialogue processing, followed by BookNLP for enhanced entity resolution. Emotional labels were assigned using a Bi-GRU-based emotion classifier, while representative story-level keywords were extracted using Mistral-7B via prompt engineering. The resulting dataset pairs keywords with fully-annotated emotional dialogues, enabling keyword-conditioned narrative generation. This dataset serves as a foundation for fine-tuning generative language models and was further used to generate narrations with emotional TTS using ElevenLabs, showcasing the practical utility of our annotations for emotionally expressive storytelling.

Background & Summary

Despite the growing number of dialogue and storytelling datasets, most existing resources lack fine-grained speaker attribution and emotion annotations when derived from long-form narratives. This gap limits their utility in tasks requiring character-specific emotional understanding or coherent multi-speaker generation. While previous work has explored speaker attribution from transcripts of spoken dialogue, often in the context of audio-based conversations1, such approaches rely heavily on structured dialogue formats or access to high-quality ASR outputs. In contrast, our work focuses on narrative text, where speaker turns are implicit, emotional tone is embedded in prose, and dialogue boundaries are ambiguous. By leveraging tools like FastCoref and BookNLP, and integrating emotion classification and keyword-conditioning, we present a dataset that transforms raw story text into a structured Speaker(Emotion): Dialogue format, bridging a crucial gap between unstructured storytelling and structured emotional dialogue generation.

Recent studies have explored different modeling and evaluation approaches for speaker attribution and authorship verification, shedding light on both domain-specific challenges and cross-domain generalizability. For instance2, systematically examine how varying the composition of training data, ranging from annotator-specific models to aggregated and leave-one-out configurations, impacts predictive performance in authorship attribution tasks. Their approach emphasizes the importance of evaluating individual and generalized modeling in controlled speaker-specific settings.

Complementing this3, provides an in-depth benchmark of authorship models on transcribed speech data, using the Fisher English corpus. They compare content-sensitive and content-independent models such as SBERT, CISR, LUAR, and AdHominem, while also considering the effect of transcription style (BBN vs. LDC encodings) and task difficulty levels. Their findings highlight how surface-level transcription artifacts and topic control can significantly influence performance, especially in forensic and conversational settings.

Building on this4, reframe speaker attribution as a dialogue state tracking problem, presenting a full pipeline capable of generalizing across time periods and literary styles. Their approach shows how modern pre-trained language models can replace rule-based systems and improve robustness in dialogue-heavy literary domains5. propose a quote-to-character attribution framework using neural classification models with GloVe embeddings, experimenting with both n-way and binary speaker classification. They also explore the benefit of contextual windows and evaluate cross-novel generalization across corpora like CQSA and Pride & Prejudice6.

Tackles the challenge of evaluating quote attribution at the level of coreference chains, rather than exact entity spans. Their rule-based baseline, while simple, effectively handles many canonical patterns and demonstrates that correct attribution often resides in broader entity linkage rather than strict span prediction. Meanwhile7, address implicit quote attribution in Chinese novels using a novel context selection algorithm (NUCS) and a Role Knowledge Graph Correction mechanism. Their model effectively disambiguates speakers in long-form dialogue by capturing character relationships and contextual dependencies, achieving near-SOTA performance while improving contextual reasoning in multi-character narratives.

The Project Dialogism Novel Corpus (PDNC)8 provides one of the most comprehensive resources for literary quote attribution to date, with over 35,000 quotations annotated for speaker, addressee, quotation type, referring expressions, and character metadata across 22 novels. The follow-up study benchmarks core NLP tasks such as coreference, quotation identification, and character linking on this dataset revealing ongoing challenges for current models. Expanding on PDNC, the SIG model9 introduces a prompt-based generative approach that handles attribution for explicit, implicit, and anaphoric quotations using language model prompts, offering a flexible alternative to standard classification pipelines. Earlier efforts like10, introduced sequence labeling techniques for speaker and addressee tagging, laying the groundwork for social network extraction and narrative discourse analysis in fiction.

Together, these works demonstrate the evolving landscape of speaker attribution, from audio transcripts to complex literary texts. They inform our own efforts to bridge structured and unstructured dialogue representations, particularly in emotionally rich, ambiguous narrative contexts.

Emotion detection in dialogue and narrative

Numerous emotion-labeled datasets exist, such as EmoBank11 for valence-arousal annotation, GoEmotions12 for fine-grained emotional dialogue, and the CrowdFlower Emotion Dataset for social media text. These resources typically provide utterance-level or document-level emotion labels but do not link emotions to specific characters in multi-speaker narrative contexts. Furthermore, emotion annotations in existing storytelling datasets (e.g., Story Commonsense, ROCStories) are often coarse-grained (positive/negative) or missing entirely. Our dataset bridges this gap by providing fine-grained, speaker-specific emotion labels (Joy, Sadness, Anger, Fear, Disgust, Surprise) for each dialogue utterance, enabling character-aware emotional modeling in narrative generation and expressive speech synthesis.

Keyword-conditioned story representation

Keyword extraction and conditioning have been explored in story generation13,14 and retrieval-augmented models-, -but rarely in conjunction with emotionally annotated-, -speaker-attributed dialogue. Existing keyword-story datasets (e.g., CMU Book Summary Dataset) provide plot keywords at the document level without dialogue granularity. Our work integrates keyword extraction via LLM prompting to produce story-level semantic descriptors that can condition dialogue generation, offering a multi-level annotation scheme (keywords → speaker → emotion → utterance) not found in prior resources.

Together, these works demonstrate the evolving landscape of speaker attribution, from audio transcripts to complex literary texts. Our dataset, formatted as Speaker(Emotion): Dialogue and paired with story-level keywords, uniquely supports multi-task narrative modelling enabling emotionally grounded, character-aware generation and expressive TTS applications that prior isolated resources cannot facilitate.

While speaker attribution, emotional annotation, or keyword-conditioned generation has been explored in isolation, few have integrated these components into a unified dataset framework. Our work addresses these gaps by introducing a fully synthetic dialogue corpus, where all annotations speaker identity, emotion, and keywords are automatically generated using NLP tools and models, without human intervention.

Method

The process begins with raw short stories from the Hugging Face repository, as shown in Fig. 1. The pipeline branches into two main components: (1) Script Creation, which includes speaker attribution and emotion detection, resulting in structured outputs in the [Speaker](Emotion): Dialogue format; and (2) Keyword Extraction, where a large language model identifies five representative keywords summarizing each story. This end-to-end annotation process supports emotionally grounded dialogue generation and character-driven applications.Fig. 1Overview of the dataset creation pipeline.

Overview of the dataset creation pipeline.

In this work, we define “dialogue” as direct quoted speech extracted from narrative text. We do not include indirect speech, character thoughts, or narrative actions unless they are presented within quotation marks in the original story. Our extraction pipeline identifies utterances enclosed in double quotation marks (“) and attributes them to speakers.

Each story is accompanied by a list of explicitly identified character names, provided as a separate field. This dataset focuses solely on character presence, making it suitable for downstream tasks such as speaker attribution, narrative structure analysis, and character-grounded story generation.

Handling of narration and non-dialogue text

Sentences not enclosed in quotation marks are treated as narration and attributed to a Narrator speaker. This ensures complete story coverage in the structured script. The Narrator’s utterances are assigned a neutral emotion label, as they typically lack explicit emotional tone. This approach preserves narrative context while maintaining consistent formatting across all story segments.

Emotion detection

A deep learning model was implemented using the Keras Sequential API to classify emotional content in dialogue lines. The architecture was designed to capture both local linguistic patterns and long-range dependencies within the text. The model was trained on preprocessed and padded sequences from the Kaggle Twitter Emotion Dataset, using an 80/10/10 split for training, validation, and testing. Performance metrics (precision, recall, F1) reported in Table 4 are derived from the held-out test portion of the Kaggle dataset, which provides ground-truth emotion labels. Application to story dialogues

After training, the model was applied to dialogue lines extracted from the narrative stories. Since no ground-truth emotion labels exist for these story dialogues, the predictions serve as automated annotations for downstream tasks. The reported Kaggle-based performance metrics indicate the model’s reliability for emotion classification in similar textual domains.

Preservation of original dialogue text

Emotion classifier does not modify the wording, structure, or content of the original dialogue. It only assigns an emotion label based on the textual content. The narrative tone and linguistic style of each utterance remain unchanged from the source story.

Training data imbalance and its impact

The Kaggle Twitter Emotion Dataset exhibits significant class imbalance as shown in Fig. 2, with Joy and Sadness being overrepresented relative to Surprise, Love, and Disgust. This imbalance influences the classifier’s predictions and, consequently, the emotion distribution in the final annotated corpus. As a result, the frequency of emotions in our dataset reflects both the narrative content and the biases of the training data. Researchers should account for this skew when using the dataset for modeling or evaluation.

Mitigating training data imbalance

To counteract the class imbalance present in the Kaggle Twitter Emotion Dataset shown in Fig. 2, we employed a class-weighted loss function during model training. Class weights were computed as inversely proportional to the frequency of each emotion category in the training set, ensuring that underrepresented classes (e.g., Surprise, Love) contributed more to the gradient updates. This weighting scheme reduces the classifier’s bias toward majority classes and promotes more equitable prediction across all six emotion categories, thereby mitigating the propagation of training-data imbalance into the final annotated corpus.

Postprocessing and output formatting

After classification, each dialogue line was annotated with both the predicted emotion and the corresponding speaker name. During processing, dialogue utterances were extracted and annotated independently, which could disrupt their original narrative sequence. To ensure temporal and conversational coherence, dialogues were reordered to match their original sequence in the story. This step preserves narrative flow and turn structure, which is essential for downstream applications such as emotionally expressive storytelling and coherent dialogue generation. Dialogues with low prediction confidence (below 0.5) or non-dialogue narration were assigned a neutral emotion label to maintain structural consistency while acknowledging uncertain or absent emotional content. The final format for each entry followed the structure:

Speaker (Emotion): Dialogue

This enriched representation supports downstream tasks such as emotional storytelling, multimodal narration, and speaker-aware generation.

Keyword extraction

The process, as shown in Fig. 4, begins with an input story, which provides the raw narrative text. In the Build Prompt stage, the story is inserted into a predefined prompt template that instructs the model to return exactly five keywords present in the text, formatted as a comma-separated list. This prompt also explicitly constrains the output format to avoid any additional commentary. Next, the prompt is passed to the Run Model step, where the Mistral 7B model (available at https://huggingface.co/mistralai/Mistral-7B-v0.1) processes the input and generates the keywords. Finally, the Output stage captures and displays the extracted keywords, which serve as concise semantic descriptors of the original story content.Fig. 4Architecture for extracting keywords from a story.

Emotional text-to-speech demonstration

To showcase the practical utility of the emotion-annotated dialogues, we generated expressive narrations using the ElevenLabs TTS API (specifically, the eleven_monolingual_v1 model). Each dialogue utterance was synthesized with a prosody profile mapped from its emotion label (e.g., Joy → higher pitch and faster rate, Sadness → slower tempo, lower pitch). We conducted a small-scale perceptual evaluation where 10 participants rated the naturalness and emotional appropriateness of 50 synthesized dialogue samples on a 5-point Likert scale.

Data Records

The dataset generated in this study is available on Zenodo17 and is stored in.jsonl (JSON Lines) format, where each line represents a single story record in JSON format. The dataset presented here is synthetically constructed; all annotations are generated automatically through the described pipeline. No human validation or correction was applied to the final speaker, emotion, or keyword labels.

The “script” field may contain utterances that are paraphrased or syntactically adjusted from the original quoted text to improve fluency and consistency in the Speaker: Dialogue format. This occurs when quoted speech is grammatically embedded in narrative prose (e.g., “She explained how understanding the path…”) and is converted to a direct utterance (e.g., “Alexa: Understanding the path helps me…”). These adjustments are minimal and preserve the semantic content of the original dialogue. Researchers should be aware that the script field is therefore not a verbatim transcript of the original story, but a structured, speaker-attributed representation optimized for dialogue-based modeling and expressive synthesis.

Metadata

In addition to core narrative and dialogue elements, each story includes a list of keywords, which can support topic modeling, retrieval, or summarization. Additional character metadata (e.g., age, role) may be annotated in future extensions of this work.

Dataset scale and coverage

Although the dataset is moderate in size (2,588 stories, 27,074 utterances), it provides high-density annotations per story, averaging ~10.5 speaker-attributed, emotion-labeled utterances per narrative. This annotation density supports fine-grained modeling of speaker-specific emotional arcs within stories, a feature not available in larger but sparsely annotated corpora. Resource offers a unique combination of speaker identity, fine-grained emotion, and keyword conditioning in a unified format, making it a valuable benchmark for emotionally-grounded narrative generation despite its limited absolute size.

Emotion detection model

As shown in section 3, the model achieved the highest performance on Sadness, Joy, and Anger, each with precision and recall above 0.93. In contrast, Surprise showed the lowest precision (0.74) but exceptionally high recall (0.98), indicating a tendency to overpredict this category.
