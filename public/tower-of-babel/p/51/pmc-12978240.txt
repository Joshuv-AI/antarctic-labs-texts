Multiannual environmental forcing shapes breeding phenology and success in a sub-Antarctic seabird
Authors: Gaël Bardon, Téo Barracho, Joël M. Durant, Nicolas Lecomte, Yvon Le Maho, Nils Chr. Stenseth, Robin Cristofari, Céline Le Bohec
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Climate-driven phenological mismatches threaten avian reproduction by disrupting food availability during critical breeding stages. In marine ecosystems, time lags between environmental changes and their effects on food webs are challenging to model, yet they can have a profound impact on reproduction at higher trophic levels. We disentangle how oceanic and climatic variability influence the breeding phenology and success of a major predator species in the Southern Ocean, drawing on 24 years of data from 17,000 marked king penguins. We document a 19-day advancement in breeding phenology, alongside increased breeding success (44% in 2000 and 62% in 2023). A sliding-window analysis reveals that sea temperature and primary production in key foraging zones predict both phenology and breeding success, with lags up to several weeks and 2 years, respectively. The amplitude of their recent response, as well as their dependence on multiyear environmental conditions, underscores a potential vulnerability in a context of decreasing environment predictability. However, so far, king penguins appear to be keeping pace with environmental changes and demonstrate a remarkable resilience and important flexibility in the face of variable oceanographic conditions.

INTRODUCTION

The timing of life-history events like reproduction is central in optimizing fitness, even more so under changing environmental conditions, as it effectively modulates the biotic and abiotic conditions and interactions encountered by both parents and offspring (1, 2). However, the phenology of high trophic level species is often less responsive to changing conditions than that of low trophic level species such as primary producers (3, 4). This means that, while their prey might follow the changes in climate, upper-level predators could be unable to keep pace by adjusting their breeding, growth cycles, and distribution (5), leading to a well-documented mismatch between peaks in resource availability and breeding phenology (6–8).

At high latitudes, ecological processes such as thermal regimes, photosynthesis, and behavior of visual organisms are strongly seasonally constrained by light (9). Seasonal light availability and associated temperature seasonality shape a narrower annual peak of productivity compared to tropical and subtropical zones (10, 11), leading to a high risk of trophic mismatch in the current context of rapid global changes (9, 12). Many high-latitude species are already in mismatch situations [e.g., (13, 14)]. Combined with the fact that polar environments are experiencing some of the fastest warming on Earth (15), this represents a critical threat to these ecosystems.

In marine food webs, extreme abiotic forcing and high capacity for the displacement of primary producer blooms make decoupling of trophic levels prevalent (12, 16), leading to bottom-up impacts on higher trophic levels (17, 18). Seabirds in high latitudes, as upper predators, are particularly sensitive to changes in environmental conditions because their breeding success depends on synchronizing their limited seasonal reproductive window with prey availability (19–21). However, despite observed strong changes in abiotic forcing in polar oceans and phenological shifts in the trophic network (15, 18), many seabirds have not altered their breeding phenology (22) or with little consistency with prey phenology (5). In Arctic-breeding seabird populations heavily affected by climate change, thick-billed murres Uria lomvia have advanced their laying dates in response to environmental shifts. However, this adjustment has lagged behind changes in prey phenology, leading to a mismatch with the peak of high-quality prey availability (23). Rapid changes in the Southern Ocean (15) may also result in observable mismatch situations for seabirds breeding there. By examining the relationship between phenology, breeding success, and the impact of environmental changes, our aim is to gain a deeper understanding of the mechanisms that allow species to avoid mismatches, and of their limitations.

This study focuses on a central-place forager of the Southern Ocean, the king penguin Aptenodytes patagonicus. King penguins establish breeding colonies of a few hundred to several hundred thousand individuals on the ice-free coastal lands of the few sub-Antarctic islands (45°S to 55°S). The species displays notable plasticity in its phenology, behavior, and life-history traits (24). In response to fluctuations in prey availability, king penguins can adjust key aspects of their foraging strategy, such as shifting their foraging latitude in relation to the Antarctic Polar Front (25, 26), modifying trip duration (27), or increasing foraging effort (28). They also show considerable individual phenological flexibility, with the ability to initiate successful breeding anytime between November and February. However, the extent to which king penguins adjust their breeding phenology in response to environmental variability and the consequences of such adjustments for breeding outcomes remain poorly understood. Phenological plasticity likely depends on the ability to detect and respond to environmental cues, a capacity increasingly challenged by the rapid and complex environmental changes occurring in the Southern Ocean. These changes encompass both large-scale trends, such as the positive shift in the Southern Annular Mode (SAM) (29), and localized, often contrasting, shifts in biogeochemical and climatic conditions (30, 31), with cascading effects on trophic dynamics (32, 33). These rapid and complex environmental changes increase the risk of phenological mismatches in sub-Antarctic species. To understand how oceanic and climatic conditions influence the breeding activities of king penguins, we used data from a 24-year-long electronic monitoring program in the sub-Antarctic archipelago of Crozet in the Southern Ocean (Fig. 1), home for 20 to 25% of the king penguins’ world population (24, 34). After determining the annual breeding phenology and the annual breeding success of the study population, we examined which of a wide range of indices best explained the variability in the breeding timing and outcome, and over which time window.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Phenological and breeding success trends

Phenological and breeding success time series from 2000 to 2023 are shown in Fig. 2 (A and B), respectively. We observed a temporal trend in annual phenology toward an earlier onset of breeding (defined here as the arrival at the colony for the courtship and mating period). The breeding season shifted 8 days earlier per decade over the 24-year study period (27 November in 2000 to 8 November in 2023; slope = 0.81 ± 0.23 days per year, P = 0.0016) (Fig. 2A). Over the same period, breeding success increased slowly at an annual rate of 0.76% (44.2% in 2000 to 61.7% in 2023; slope = 0.76 ± 0.31% per year, P = 0.0232) (Fig. 2B), which was strongly correlated with the median breeding date (slope = −0.97 ± 0.16% per day, P << 0.001; Fig. 2C). No temporal trend in breeding success was observed when the median breeding date was accounted for in the model (P = 0.885). However, substantial interannual variation remained (residual standard error = 7.4%), indicating that the median breeding date explains only part of the annual variation in breeding success.

This 19-day advancement in phenology over the past 24 years, corresponding to an 8.1-day decadal advance, is remarkable compared to other high-latitude species (35). For example, the decadal advance in breeding initiation reached only 1.25 days [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.925 to 1.375 days] for 73 bird species in Finland (36), on average 1.2 days (ranging from 0.3 to 2.3 days) for Swedish Lapland passerines (37), or 4 days for Arctic squirrels Urocitellus parryii (38). To our knowledge, it is also the fastest shift observed in a penguin species. The clutch initiation date of the Adélie penguin Pygoscelis adeliae from the Antarctic Peninsula has shown no long-term trend over the last three decades despite substantial interannual variability and major changes in environmental conditions (39, 40). For royal penguins Eudyptes schlegeli, an average advance <1 day per decade has been recorded over 35 years (41).

King penguins appear to succeed in tracking favorable environmental conditions for reproduction. Our results suggest that recent environmental changes have been beneficial to the breeding success of the Crozet king penguin population, enabling earlier breeding by reducing constraints on the breeding season, albeit with notable interannual variability. The magnitude of this effect is substantial: Each day’s advance in breeding timing corresponds to ~1% point increase in breeding success, with consequences in terms of population productivity.

Studies have shown that climate-driven advances in breeding timing can have contrasting effects on the reproductive success of migratory and sedentary bird species (42). In some cases, earlier breeding may be detrimental, for example, black grouse Lyrurus tetrix in Finland have shifted their hatching dates in response to warmer springs, but early summer temperatures have not increased accordingly. This mismatch exposes chicks to colder conditions, leading to higher mortality rates and reduced reproductive output (43). Conversely, breeding success may remain unaffected by an advance in phenology, suggesting that some species are able to maintain synchrony with optimal environmental conditions during key breeding stages (44–46). However, many species may benefit from phenological advances, for instance, multibrooded species that can extend their breeding season (47). This is further supported by a translocation experiment of pied flycatchers Ficedula hypoleuca to the north: translocated females, which bred earlier than the local females, exhibited a 2.5 times higher fitness, suggesting that the local population had not yet fully optimized its breeding phenology (48). In our study, we found no indication of a trophic mismatch, as suggested by the absence of long-term declines in breeding success despite the exceptional advance of breeding phenology. Although direct measurements of prey availability are unavailable, increased breeding performance suggests that king penguins may currently be able to synchronize the most sensitive phases of chick provisioning with favorable foraging conditions. King penguins have a prolonged breeding cycle lasting 12 to 14 months, which includes a 3-month winter fasting period for the chicks (49). During this time, chicks are exposed to harsh environmental conditions, making it a critical phase for survival with starvation as the main cause of mortality. An advance in breeding phenology may allow chicks to attain better body condition before winter, a factor known to enhance overwinter survival (50), and, consequently, breeding success, as observed in Wandering albatrosses Diomedea exulans (51).

However, in the context of rapid and projected changes in Southern Ocean ecosystems, the substantial advancement already observed in breeding phenology of king penguins (≈19 days) suggests limited scope for further adjustment, as local environmental constraints at the colony may become prohibitive. Continued environmental change may therefore lead to seasonal mismatches between predators and prey across multiple trophic levels (18), potentially threatening penguins (39), their prey, and the ecosystem as a whole (12).

Environmental predictors of breeding phenology

Environmental drivers of clutch initiation have been well documented for several Antarctic penguin species, with temperature, snow cover, and sea-ice dynamics strongly influencing the timing of breeding [e.g., (52)]. In contrast, little is known about the cues used by king penguins, whose sub-Antarctic breeding environment and foraging ecology differ substantially from those of Antarctic species. King penguins undertake extensive at-sea movements during the winter period, ranging from areas near the Crozet archipelago to regions farther south, through the Polar Front Area, and to the edge of the sea-ice zone (53). This raises the possibility that remote and/or local conditions encountered in wintering and foraging habitats could influence breeding initiation, as, for example, suggested in black-browed albatross Thalassarche melanophris (54). Thus, we investigated how environmental conditions (i) around the king penguin breeding habitat, (ii) in their main foraging areas, and (iii) at a global scale impact the date of their annual breeding onset while accounting for short- and long-term lags (see the panel of tested variables in table S1). For that purpose, we used a thoroughly cross-validated sliding window analysis [(55, 56); see Materials and Methods], which identifies the specific time periods during which environmental variation best explains changes in breeding phenology. This approach is well suited to species with poorly known cueing mechanisms, as it avoids a priori assumptions about the biologically relevant window and instead allows the data to reveal when environmental signals are most influential. We tested both linear and quadratic effects: The inclusion of quadratic terms allowed us to assess the possibility of nonlinear responses, particularly the existence of optimal environmental conditions. Such optima may reflect thresholds beyond which environmental changes become detrimental to prey abundance or accessibility.

Sliding window analysis combined with model selection (see Supplementary Text and tables S2 and S3) allowed us to identify two environmental variables associated with the timing of breeding initiation in king penguins: (i) chlorophyll a concentration (Chla; Fig. 3B) in late winter (i.e., mid-August to late September preceding the breeding onset) with a linear effect, and (ii) the sea surface temperature (SST; Fig. 3A) in autumn (i.e., the first 2 weeks of May preceding the breeding onset) with a quadratic effect, both in the Polar Front Area (i.e., the main foraging area for the species, particularly in summer; see Fig. 1). These two environmental variables, between themselves, explained 82% of the variance in annual breeding phenology (figs. S1 and S2 and table S4), although model uncertainty increases when projecting beyond the observed range of environmental conditions. Neither of these two variables taken in isolation showed a significant temporal trend over the study period (fig. S3). However, when year was added as an additional predictor in the model, the year coefficient was nonsignificant (slope = −0.25 ± 0.14 days per year, P = 0.087), unlike in the year-only model (slope = 0.81 ± 0.23 days per year, P = 0.0016). This observation clearly indicates that a substantial fraction of the observed trend in breeding phenology is explained by the combination of these two variables, rather than by another unobserved, strongly time-trended variable.

Lower Chla and an optimal SST (i.e., the minimum of the quadratic relationship at ~4.5°C; see Fig. 3A) in the Polar Front Area were associated with an earlier onset of breeding. SST is a well-documented driver of fish productivity across numerous species [see review in (57)]. The seasonal growth peak of several species of Myctophidae, which are an important food source for king penguins (58), has been observed to occur in autumn (59). The optimal SST identified in our study likely reflects oceanographic conditions near the Polar Front before winter, and such conditions could enhance myctophid accessibility to foraging penguins, although this requires further investigation. The negative relationship between Chla and myctophid abundance, on the other hand, may first seem counterintuitive but makes sense within the complex trophic dynamics of the Southern Ocean. High surface chlorophyll concentrations can indicate either highly productive but unstable conditions, or rather a mismatched timing between phytoplankton blooms and zooplankton grazing (60): In other words, high Chla can be interpreted as a “leftover” that indicates that herbivorous zooplankton are in low abundance, with in turn a bottom-up effect on myctophid abundance. Abundance of myctophid such as Electrona antarctica, a key prey species for king penguins in winter and spring, has already been found to be higher at moderate measured Chla levels, suggesting that optimal rather than maximum primary productivity benefits their populations (61). In addition, it is also possible that high Chla occurs in areas with poor physical oceanographic conditions for fish aggregation, such as areas with weak frontal structures.

As recently suggested for black-legged kittiwakes Rissa tridactyla (23), the phenology of king penguins is likely influenced by bottom-up effects of environmental conditions rather than direct environmental cues. When conditions favor an increased abundance of their primary prey, king penguins may attain adequate body condition earlier in the spring, which in turn could facilitate an earlier onset of molt and breeding, a mechanism already proposed by Gauthier-Clerc et al. (62). However, surface ocean Chla has already been identified as a likely direct phenological cue for two auk species (63), a mechanism that could potentially apply to king penguins as well. Nevertheless, it remains unclear what information this species could derive from Chla during winter and how this might influence its reproduction.

Environmental predictors of breeding success

Using the same sliding window analysis method (see the Supplementary Text and tables S5 and S6), we found that, while breeding phenology explained a substantial part of the variance in breeding success (Fig. 2), the remaining variance was mainly explained by three environmental parameters (Fig. 3, D to F) with a time lag of a year and a half to 2 years prior to the onset of the breeding season. Our model selection indicates (i) a linear effect of the distance between the isotherms 4°C and 5°C (mid-December to mid-April, ~2 years prior), (ii) a linear effect of the SAM index (February to May, ~1.5 years prior), and (iii) a quadratic effect of the mean Chla concentration (in milligrams per cubic meter) in the Crozet Area in late winter (mid-August to early September, ~1 year prior). These three variables together with the phenology explained 94% of breeding success variance (figs. S4 and S5 and table S7). None of these three variables showed a significant temporal trend over the study period (fig. S6).

A short distance between the 4°C and 5°C isotherms (i.e., a strong latitudinal SST gradient) and a positive SAM index appear to benefit king penguin breeding success. Both the strong SST gradient and the positive SAM are associated with positive temperature anomalies in the subtropical zone and negative anomalies south of the Antarctic Polar Front (30), a pattern associated with increased primary productivity in Antarctic waters (64). King penguin breeding success showed a quadratic relationship with Chla in the Crozet Area (Fig. 3E), with highest success at intermediate levels (0.26 mg/m3) slightly over 1 year before the start of the breeding cycle, although the CI around this optimum spans 0.20 to 0.32 mg/m3. Derived from satellite imagery, Chla reflects only surface-layer phytoplankton density and does not linearly correlate with total primary productivity (65). We thus suggest that Chla optimum is linked to optimal conditions that favor the growth and abundance of available prey, and especially squids (66), spawned near the breeding colony. King penguins undertake two types of foraging trips: long-distance trips to the Polar Front Area, located ~300 to 500 km south of the colony (26), and shorter, local trips around the colony, where prey distribution is typically more variable and occurs at greater depths (67). Increased prey availability in the vicinity of the colony may enhance foraging efficiency and reduce the duration of foraging trips, ultimately supporting greater breeding success.

The 1- to 2-year temporal lag between these three environmental conditions and breeding success likely reflects two complementary mechanisms. First, given that king penguins feed primarily on subadult and adult myctophids and squids (68, 69), environmental conditions during prey spawning and recruitment phases (1 to 2 years prior) determine the abundance of fish cohorts that become available to penguins during subsequent breeding seasons. Second, the king penguin’s extended breeding cycle (12 to 14 months) makes them particularly sensitive to environmental variation over long periods. As a result, favorable conditions during one breeding season may improve adult body condition and potentially enhance breeding performance in the following season. These mechanisms may act synergistically, with favorable environmental conditions enhancing both prey availability and parental condition over multiyear timescales.

It has been reported that warm events [depicted by negative Southern Oscillation Index (SOI)] in any given year were linked to reduced king penguin breeding success (70) and that positive anomaly of SST in the Polar Front Area could also negatively affect breeding success with penguins traveling further to reach the high productivity located at the oceanic fronts (25, 26). Unlike these previous studies on the same penguin population, we found no relationship between breeding success and warm environmental conditions during the ongoing breeding season. This suggests that the abundance and/or quality of prey, driven by oceanographic conditions up to one to several years prior to a breeding season, may play a more decisive role than prey spatial distribution. Except in extreme years, such as 1997 when the Polar Front shifted too far south to support successful breeding (26), our findings underscore the capacity of king penguins to adjust their foraging strategies and breeding phenology in response to environmental variability.

To conclude, our study shows a remarkable advance of the breeding season, by more than 2 weeks, over the last two decades in a species that is usually considered as an indicator of environmental changes in the Southern Ocean (70–73). This result points to profound and rapid changes making their way up to the higher levels of the marine food chain and increasing the breeding success of the king penguin populations of the Indian Ocean sector. King penguins from the Kerguelen archipelago, located 1500 km east of Crozet, also appear to benefit from the current rise in temperature through a positive effect on prey abundance (50). For now, king penguins thus appear to be doing well under the current climate conditions, and unlike several species, this is happening without range shift or range expansion. Then, organisms seem to keep pace, matching their needs with available resources and responding to changing environmental conditions, but for how long? The plasticity of populations and their ability to cope with future changes are still challenging to gauge [e.g., (74)]. Even when populations respond rapidly to environmental change, as observed in king penguins, it is difficult to predict their responses if environmental conditions continue to shift. Nonlinear responses to environmental change may in fact be common across species. For example, increased shrub cover and density, favored by climate change, may potentially be beneficial for some Arctic tundra-breeding bird species; as shrub height increases further, however, a considerable number of these birds will likely find habitat increasingly unsuitable [e.g., (75)]. The quadratic effects found in our study may also provide a first glimpse of the future limits of the Southern Ocean ecosystem for king penguins: We predict negative impacts from excessively warm temperatures (>4.6°C in the Polar Front Area in May; Fig. 3A) and from chlorophyll concentrations much higher than the current average (>0.50 mg/m3 in the Crozet Area at the end of August; Fig. 3E), probably indicating a change in basic ecosystem structure. Southern Ocean climate projections (e.g., 1°C to 3°C warming, front displacement, and increased SAM intensity) (15) suggest that environmental conditions may exceed the optimal ranges identified here within two to three decades.

We have also highlighted that the initiation and success of the king penguin breeding cycle result from the integration of environmental conditions around their breeding and foraging grounds, spanning a period of up to 2 years (Fig. 4). Short-term conditions just prior to the breeding season might drive its onset, with favorable foraging conditions facilitating earlier, generally less time-constrained and more successful breeding. These conditions may act as direct phenological cues informing penguins when to start breeding, which generally guarantees them to remain synchronized with their prey’s phenology. Notably, our findings contrast with those of (22), who reported that seabirds globally do not exhibit phenological shifts in response to sea temperature variability; such a conclusion may overlook the role of temporal lags in species’ responses to environmental change. Nevertheless, at current rates of change, king penguins may not be able to integrate all the information from 2 years of oceanographic data at the right pace, making the prediction of resource availability virtually impossible. Growing evidence supports the existence of such long-term environmental response lags in various species, including murres, kittiwakes, and cormorants in the North Pacific and Atlantic Oceans (23, 76). Our study extends these observations to the Southern Ocean and provides a clear illustration of the bottom-up trophic chain consequences of climate change, demonstrating how environmental variability at the base of the food web can propagate through trophic levels to influence upper predators such as king penguins. With the more frequent occurrence of extreme and unprecedented events resulting from climate change (15), we have yet to understand the consequences of species inertia in their response to fast-changing and more extreme weather patterns. So far, the 2-year lag in king penguin response in breeding output is still allowing reproductive benefits, but whether this translates into juvenile survival and ultimately population dynamics is still unclear. Future work linking the length of lagged responses to population trajectories and ecosystem stability is a welcome challenge for deciphering biodiversity changes with current and future climatic conditions. An important take-home message from this study is that king penguins appear to display a remarkable degree of resilience in the face of ongoing environmental change. This resilience likely stems from substantial behavioral flexibility, rooted in their exceptionally long breeding cycle and flexible foraging strategies, which may buffer populations against short-term environmental variability. Consistent with this interpretation, several studies have shown that large penguin species have persisted through periods of pronounced environmental change, including major climatic and oceanographic shifts [e.g., (77)]. However, our results also suggest that this resilience may not be unlimited: The pronounced advancement already observed in breeding phenology indicates that adaptive responses may be approaching biological or environmental constraints. Together, these findings highlight a tension between short-term resilience and potential long-term vulnerability under continued and accelerating environmental change.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Long-term monitored population and datasets

The study was carried out on the king penguin A. patagonicus colony of “Baie du Marin” settled on the Possession Island in the Crozet archipelago (46°25S, 51°45E). This study was approved by the French Ethics Committee (latest: APAFIS#29338-2020070210516365) and the French Polar Environmental Committee, and permits handling animals and access breeding sites were delivered by the Terres Australes et Antarctiques Françaises. Health, safety, security, and other risks for participating researchers were assessed and managed by the French Polar Institute Paul-Emile Victor (IPEV) that provided logistic support at the research stations involved. Ethics questions concerning local and regional researchers, partners, or governments do not apply because the sub-Antarctic islands are uninhabited.

A subarea of the colony of ~10,000 breeding pairs has been continuously electronically monitored since 1998 using radio-frequency identification (RFID) technology (78, 79). As of 2024, the database contains information on ~17,000 birds, including ~12,000 of known age and ~5500 currently active in the colony.

Using a new application of deep learning (78) on our RFID-detection dataset, we estimated the status and the timing of breeding for each microtagged individual and each breeding season. Briefly, we used a convolutional neural network workflow to classify each season based on a 10-year training of direct field observations and performed two testing steps to ensure the quality of the classification [see (78) for precision on the system and on classification methods]. For each individual and each year, we thus obtained the breeding status (nonbreeding, failed breeding, or successful breeding) and the most probable date for the onset of breeding (defined here as the mating event). King penguins breed in an asynchronous cycle that lasts from 12 to 14 months (49). As a result, every year, the successful individuals of the previous year either take a “sabbatical” or start breeding later in the season (80). To avoid confounding our results with the variation in asynchrony parameters, we restricted our dataset to birds that (i) did not breed successfully in the previous season and (ii) were at least 9 years old at the start of the breeding season, since young inexperienced breeders have a low overall contribution to the breeding success of the population (see figs. S7 and S8). We included in this dataset unknown-age adults; as these birds were systematically RFID-tagged at an advanced stage of breeding, they were at least 5 years old (the mean age at first breeding of sexual maturity), and we then used data of these birds with a 3-year delay after their marking. Out of the 28,000 breeding events available in our dataset, 8800 from around 2600 individuals were thus selected to compute the annual median breeding initiation date of the colony and the annual breeding success rate, obtained by dividing the number of successful cycles by the number of breeding attempts (fig. S9).

Environmental datasets

We selected climate variables likely to have an impact on the phenology and reproductive success of king penguins from the Crozet population (see table S1). Two main areas were considered: (i) an area centered on Crozet archipelago (43°S-47°S to 46°E-56°E) extensively used by birds throughout the year during their circular foraging trips [sensu (67)] that we refer to as “Crozet Area”, and (ii) an area south of the archipelago (47°S-53°S to 49°E-55°E) corresponding to their main foraging area during summer, tightly linked to the polar front (26, 53), which we refer to as “Polar Front Area.”

SST is widely reported as one of the main drivers of marine productivity, trophic interactions, and distribution of species (81). Although birds may not respond to SST per se, SST often reflects broad environmental conditions influencing prey accessibility and has therefore been repeatedly associated with breeding phenology and reproductive performance in seabirds (82), including several penguin species (70, 83, 84). We computed daily mean OI (optimal interpolation) SST in the areas around and south of the archipelago. We computed 2°C, 4°C, and 5°C isotherm latitude south of Crozet as they were also previously linked to the positions of the Antarctic Polar Fronts and the main foraging area of king penguins (26, 85). We considered the distance between isotherms 4°C and 5°C as an index of SST latitudinal gradient between the breeding colony and the main foraging area. The latitude of the Antarctic Polar Front has been defined as the northernmost extent of the 200-m surface layer temperature minimum of 2°C (86) and computed at the latitude of Crozet from the GODAS model (National Centers for Environmental Prediction Global Ocean Data Assimilation System). We considered the latitude of the northern edge of the marginal ice zone (MIZ), defined as the area covered by a sea-ice concentration between 15 and 80%, as previous studies showed the presence of king penguins at these latitudes, especially during the winter season (53, 87). While SST is often correlated with sea-ice extent, the relationship is not strictly linear, and we included the MIZ latitude as a complementary indicator of environmental conditions relevant to penguin distribution. In addition to the MIZ latitude, we computed the part of the total ice area (with sea-ice concentration of >15%) that is covered by the MIZ (between 15 and 80% of sea-ice concentration) in the defined area south of Crozet (50°S-70°S to 46°E-56°E), as an index of the quality of the sea ice for foraging.

As a proxy of marine productivity, we considered the mean concentration of Chla both in the Crozet Area and in the Polar Front Area, which could act as a phenological cue (63) and as an index of prey availability. We computed the center of mass of the Chla concentrations at the longitude of Crozet and between 40°S and 56°S as an indicator of the location of the maximum productivity.

Wind force and storm probability were used to reflect direct climatic constraints on land and at sea that can affect breeding birds and their chicks. A wind force index was computed from the daily maximum wind force by taking the monthly mean of the 25% windiest days. The storm track probability index was computed as the mean latitude of the maximum gradient in the isobaric surface 500 millibars (88).

Last, two global climatic indices were considered to detect indirect effects of climate that can be left unexplained by more specific climate variables. The SAM, defined as 700-millibar height anomalies poleward of 20°S onto the loading pattern of the Antarctic Oscillation (the leading mode of empirical orthogonal function analysis of monthly mean 700-hPa height during the 1979 to 2000 period), was considered as the main mode of this region (89). In addition, the SOI, which reflects large-scale fluctuations in air pressure between Tahiti and Darwin and serves as a proxy for El Niño–Southern Oscillation phases, was used to account for potential remote climatic influences from the tropical Pacific region.

General trends

Phenological annual trend, given by the annual median breeding date, was tested using a linear and a quadratic regression of the phenology against the year using the lm() function in R (version 4.3.0, R Core Team, 2023). We considered that the quadratic regression best supported the correlation if the Akaike information criterion corrected for small sample size (AICc) was at least inferior to the linear regression by 2 units (90, 91).

The trend of the mean annual breeding success rate was tested using a linear regression of the mean annual breeding success against the year, treated as a quantitative variable. As breeding success is likely linked to the breeding starting date, we also tested whether the temporal trend of the annual breeding success rate is explained by the annual median breeding date. Although reproductive success is a proportion theoretically bounded between 0 and 1, the observed values in our dataset ranged between 0.3 and 0.7. Within this restricted interval, we considered linear and quadratic regression models to be appropriate approximations for investigating the relationships between population reproductive success and environmental conditions.

Time window identification

Despite previous studies relating environmental predictors with annual breeding traits of penguins, it is still unclear when these climate variables can affect breeding. To cover all possible temporal windows and explore possible long-term effects, we used a systematic method to find which time window has the strongest association with the response variable, while avoiding false-positive correlations when testing many variables. We therefore used the sliding window approach implemented in R (version 4.3.0, R Core Team, 2013) in the Climwin package (55, 56).

We first defined baseline models for phenology and breeding success. To avoid spurious correlation due to temporal trends (92), we included year as a continuous variable in both baseline models. For breeding success, we included the median breeding date in the baseline model, as it greatly explained the annual breeding success (R2 = 0.60). For both phenology and breeding success, we compared the AICc given by the linear and quadratic model with different time windows of each climate variable with the respective baseline models. All combinations of time windows in the temporal extent that we considered were tested. For phenology, we considered a period of 30 weeks before the breeding season (1st of November), a period long enough to catch short-lagged effects of climate conditions while avoiding autocorrelation effects between seasons.

For breeding success, because we designed our dataset to remove the breeding birds that were successful the previous season, there was no correlation between the current and the past breeding success rate. We then considered a longer period of 2 years preceding the beginning of the breeding season and until 1 year after (corresponding to the end of a successful breeding season).

The best model was chosen for each response variable and each environmental explanatory variable based on the highest difference in AICc (ΔAICc) with the baseline models (90, 91). For each environmental variable, the quadratic model was selected only when the ΔAICc was at least 2 units higher than the best linear model; otherwise, the linear model was selected.

To control for multiple testing across numerous time windows, we used Climwin’s randomization procedure (56). This approach shuffles the response variable 10 times while maintaining the temporal structure of environmental predictors, generating a null distribution to estimate the probability (Pc) that observed correlations could arise by chance alone. Only variables with Pc < 0.5 were retained, reducing the risk of false positives inherent in sliding window analyses.

To reduce the risk of overfitting arising from a high number of candidate variables relative to the number of data points, we first assessed multicollinearity among variables using variance inflation factors (VIFs). The variables with the highest VIF were removed iteratively until no variables left had a VIF > 3. Then, we performed a model selection with all the candidate variables using the package MuMin and the Dredge function (93). Among all equally best performing models (with a ΔAICc < 2 from the lowest AICc model), we selected the one with the lowest number of parameters (91).

Partial regression plots are given for the best models (Fig. 3). They are constructed by plotting the residuals of the dependent variable (after removing the effects of all other predictors) against the residuals of the independent variable of interest (after similarly controlling for other predictors). We then added the mean value of the dependent variable and of the variable of interest.

Contribution of environmental conditions in explaining observed trends

We estimated how much of the observed trends could be explained by the environmental variables obtained from our model selection. We added the year variable in the final selected model and extracted its remaining associated linear coefficient. We compared this coefficient to the slope of the temporal trend-only model to obtain the part of the observed temporal trend explained in the final model including environmental variables (94).
