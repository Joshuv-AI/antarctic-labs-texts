Haptic-visual evaluation of the J-Sign revealed a significant correlation with patella height, tibial tubercle – trochlear groove distance, trochlear bump height and the total number of risk factors for lateral patellar instability
Authors: Felix Zimmermann, Eric Mandelka, Jula Gierse, Sven Y Vetter, Paul Alfred Grützner, Peter Balcarek
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundJ-sign assessment is considered a helpful clinical parameter in the treatment of lateral patellar instability (LPI). However, it is unclear whether there is a correlation between the degree of the J-sign and anatomic risk factors. The aim of this study was to investigate whether there is a correlation between the degree of the J-sign assessed by haptic-visual assessment and anatomic risk factors of LPI.MethodsJ-sign grading was prospectively assessed by two experienced observers in 51 knee joints in 46 patients (male/female 19/32; age 25.1 ± 10 years; ≥ 1 patellar dislocation) according to the quadrant method of Zhang et al. Assessment was done with haptic-visual assessment, including palpation of the medial and lateral patellar facets with the thumb and index finger. The J-Sign grade was correlated with known anatomic risk factors for patellar instability, including the Caton‒Deschamps index, the Dejour type of trochlear dysplasia with additional measurements of trochlear bump height and lateral trochlear inclination angle, the tibial tuberosity–trochlear groove (TT–TG) distance, the tibial tuberosity–posterior cruciate ligament (TT–PCL) distance, the varus/valgus deformity and the total number of anatomic risk factors.ResultsThere was a significant correlation between the J-sign and the Caton–Deschamps index (r1 = 0.51; p < 0.001 and r2 = 0.41; p = 0.005), the total number of anatomic risk factors (r1 = 0.34; p = 0.015 and r2 = 0.30; p = 0.032) and the trochlear bump height (r1 = 0.36; p = 0.021 and r2 = 0.35; p = 0.027) for both raters. Moreover, the results revealed a significant correlation between the J-sign grade assessed by one rater and the TT–TG distance (r1 = 0.31; p = 0.044).ConclusionHaptic-visual assessment of the J-sign revealed a significant correlation with patella height, trochlear bump height, TT–TG distance and the total number of anatomic risk factors for patellar instability.Keywords: Patellar instability, J-sign, Anatomic risk factors, Patella alta, Trochlear bump height, Correlation

Background

In the treatment of patients with lateral patellar instability (LPI), it is still questionable when bony corrections of the anatomic risk factors (e.g., trochlear dysplasia, patellar height, TT‒TG distance, etc.) are beneficial in addition to isolated reconstructions of the medial patellofemoral ligament (MPFL-R) [5, 9, 15, 30], which is injured in more than 90% after first-time patellar dislocation [16]. Basing this decision solely on recommended thresholds of these anatomic risk factors has been put into question, recent studies have emphasized more clinically derived decision protocol [17, 25, 26, 28, 30]. In this context, increasing importance has been given to clinical tests of the J-sign to assess the movement of the patella in the trochlear groove during active knee joint motion [17, 25].

‘Classically’, J-sign assessment is performed via plain visual evaluation, which involves observing the patient’s knee moving from flexion to full extension and grading the lateral displacement of the patella in full extension [2, 10, 11, 14, 21–23]. While Walla et al. reported a significant correlation between visually assessed J-Signs and severe trochlear dysplasia, a tight lateral retinaculum and an elevated Beighton score [23], Tan et al. reported a significant correlation between the visually assessed J-Sign and the patellar tilt, congruence angle and tibial tubercle‒trochlear groove distance [22]. However, plain visual assessment of the J-sign has shown low interrater reliability [2, 10, 11, 14, 21].

Haptic-visual evaluation of the J-Sign has recently been shown to be associated with high interrater reliability [31] but it is unclear whether there is also a correlation between the haptic-visual assessment of the J-sign and the anatomical risk factors for LPD. Thus, the aim of this study was to investigate whether there is a correlation between the degree of the J-sign assessed by haptic-visual method and the anatomic risk factor profile for patellar instability. The hypothesis of this study was that the grade of the J-sign significantly correlates with the anatomic risk factor profile for LPI.

Methods

This study obtained approval from the local ethics committee of Rheinland-Pfalz and Baden-Württemberg (Reference no. 2023-16952-andere Forschung erstvotierend and B-F-2023-076). In total, 51 knee joints in 46 consecutive patients (male/female 19/32; aged 25.1 ± 10 years) with LPI (≥ 1 patellar dislocation) were prospectively assessed by two raters (two consultant surgeons) between October 2023 and January 2024. Only patients aged ≥ 14 years without any previous knee joint surgery and without any other knee joint disorders except LPI were included. In all patients, the J-sign was assessed via the modified quadrant method according to Zhang et al. [25, 26]. The grading was as follows: grade 0: < 1 quadrant of lateral patellar tracking; grade 1: ≥ 1 and < 2 quadrants of lateral patellar tracking; grade 2: ≥ 2 and < 3 quadrants of lateral patellar tracking; and grade 3: >3 quadrants of lateral patellar tracking or complete patellar dislocation.

The J-sign was assessed using a previously described haptic-visual evaluation method [31]. For this purpose, patients were seated on the edge of a table with their legs hanging over the side at 90° of knee joint flexion. Patients were then asked to extend their knee from flexion to full extension while the medial and lateral patellar facets were palpated with the thumb and index finger by the examiner. The degree of lateral patellar tracking was recorded according to Zhang et al. [25, 26] as described above.

In addition, the demographics of all patients were recorded via an internal clinic database. For the assessment of pathoanatomic risk factors, routine radiographs, including standing long leg axis and true-lateral views of the knee joint (≤ 3 mm of overlap between the femoral condyles), and native magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans were evaluated. The pathoanatomic risk factors were evaluated by reaching a consensus among the authors of the study. Radiological data were rated using predetermined thresholds and grading scales [29]: the severity of trochlear dysplasia (absent, low-grade [Dejour type A], or high-grade [Dejour type B-D]) using the true-lateral views of knee joint and the MRI scans [7], with only high-grade trochlear dysplasia considered relevant; patellar height, with a Caton‒Deschamps index ≥ 1.3 recorded as elevated [4]; tibial tuberosity‒trochlear groove (TT‒TG) distance, with ≥ 16 mm considered elevated using the MRI scans [30]; tibial tuberosity‒posterior cruciate ligament (TT‒PCL) distance, with ≥ 24 mm recorded as relevant using the MRI scans [20]; and ≥ 4° valgus malalignment considered elevated [12] Risk factors were weighted according to a previously described protocol [28] (see Table 1). In addition, the trochlear bump height [1] and the lateral trochlear inclination angle [13] were assessed using the MRI scans as quantitative measures of trochlear dysplasia. For trochlear bump height, the sagittal MRI scan was used by measuring the maximal distance between a tangent of the anterior cortex of the distal femur and the tip of the bony trochlear bump [1]. LTI was assessed using the two-image-method described by Joseph et al. and LTI was calculated by subtracting the angle of the posterior femoral condyle orientation from the lateral trochlear angle [13].

Discussion

The most important findings of the present study were that the results of the haptic visual assessment of the J-sign were significantly correlated with patella height, the TT–TG distance, trochlear bump height and the total number of anatomic risk factors for LPI.

In the currently available literature, multiple reasons for positive J-sign findings have been defined. The triggering factors are trochlear dysplasia [24], patella alta [19], increased TT–TG distance [24], increased femur and tibia torsion [24], and dysbalance between the vastus medialis and lateralis muscles [18]. Our results also revealed a significant correlation between the degree of the J-sign and the patella height, the bump height and the TT–TG distance. We could not find any significant correlation between the assessed degree of the J-sign and the degree of Dejour classification of trochlear dysplasia. However, the results showed a significant correlation between the degree of the J-Sign and trochlear bump height as a quantitative characteristic of trochlear dysplasia. These findings are in line with the results of Dandu et al., who showed that the presence of a J-sign is associated with a high external tibiofemoral rotation, increased TT-TG distance, increased patellar height and that the severity of the J-sign is particularly related to the height of the trochlear bump [6].

The assessment of the J-sign as a clinical sign of patellar maltracking in patients with patellar instability is becoming increasingly important. Milinkovic et al. evaluated 182 patients with recurrent LPI and showed that a high-grade J-sign negatively impacts the disease-specific quality of life [17]. In addition, various studies have shown that the presence of a J-sign leads to a poorer clinical outcome after MPFL-R [25–27].

Zhang et al. included 130 knee joints from 118 patients in their study [26]. MPFL-R was performed in all 130 affected knees, and tibial tuberosity medialization was performed in patients with a TT–TG distance ≥ 20 mm (n = 72). The degree of the J-sign was retrospectively determined on the basis of video recordings via the modified grading method of Franciozi et al. [8]. The grading method was as follows: grade 1: >1 quadrant of motion; grade 2: >2 quadrants of motion; and grade 3: complete patellofemoral dislocation [8]. The results showed that patients with a preoperative grade 3 J-sign had lower Kujala scores and increased MPFL graft laxity after surgical treatment at the 1-year follow-up. Furthermore, the authors were able to show that the included patients with a grade 3 J-sign had a significantly increased femoral anteversion angle, external tibial torsion angle and patella height [26].

In their retrospective analysis, Zhao et al. included a total of 237 patients with a significantly longer follow-up of 70.67 ± 18.05 months (range, 36–108 months) after isolated MPFL reconstruction, revealing a J-sign in 60 knee joints. In 20 knee joints (8.4%), the authors reported postoperative failure in terms of recurrent dislocation/patellar stability. Their results also found that the preoperative presence of a J-sign can be seen as a prognostic factor for treatment failure. This study found a significant correlation between the degree of the J-sign and patella height as well as the degree of trochlear dysplasia [27].

Zimmermann et al. included 122 patients with recurrent LPI and divided them into two groups according to the degree of the J-sign and the results of the reversed dynamic patellar apprehension test (ReDPAT). Patients with a positive ReDPAT result > 40° of knee flexion and/or a high-grade J-sign (grade 2 or 3) received MPFL-R and concomitant bony procedures depending on the established risk factor assessment. Patients with a positive ReDPAT < 40 to 50° of knee flexion and an absent or low-grade J-sign (grade 1) received isolated MPFL-R. The results of this study showed that patients with a high-grade J-sign and/or a positive ReDPAT score who underwent MPFL-R and concomitant bony procedures had similarly good outcomes to patients who underwent isolated MPFL-R [30]. On the basis of these findings, among others, recommendations for bony correction of anatomical risk factors in addition to MPFL-R in the presence of a preoperative J-sign have been derived in the current literature.

Previous studies have shown that a plain visual assessment of the J-sign, which has been used in the studies of Zhang et al. [26] and Zhao et al. [27], was associated with low interrater reliability [2, 10, 11, 14, 21]. To overcome this drawback in J-sign grading, the J-sign was assessed in this study via a previously described haptic-visual evaluation method, which appears superior to plain visual assessment in terms of interrater reliability [31]. The results of this study demonstrated that the haptic visual-assessed J-sign was significantly correlated with patella height, the bump height, the TT–TG distance and the total number of anatomic risk factors for patellar instability. Accordingly, it can be concluded that patients with a positively assessed J-sign should undergo further investigations for potential pathological anatomic risk factors that might be considered for clinical-decision making. However, the results of this study must be interpreted in light of several limitations: First, the study group was small, and a control group was not recorded but a priori power analysis proved sufficient power in a group of 49 participants. Second, the J-sign assessment is an observer-dependent clinical investigation tool and no objective quantification of the J-sign, such as dynamic MRI or dynamic kinematic computed tomography, was available. However, the interrater reliability of the haptic-visual method is greater than that of the typically used plain visual method. Third, the assessment of anatomic risk factors for patellar instability is influenced by the nuances of imaging modalities and by observer-dependent reliability (e.g., grading of trochlear dysplasia). To minimize potential bias, the assessment of the risk factors was performed with agreement reached by consensus among the authors of the study.

Conclusion

Haptic-visual assessment of the J-sign revealed a significant correlation with patella height, trochlear bump height, TT–TG distance and the total number of anatomic risk factors for patellar instability.
