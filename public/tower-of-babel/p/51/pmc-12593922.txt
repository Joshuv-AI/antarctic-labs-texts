Predicting early postoperative hypotension after lung resection: a multiparameter model integrating non-invasive hemodynamic profiling and inferior vena cava collapsibility index (observational study)
Authors: Yanzi Yi, Jing Huang, Xiaoxiao Li, Xiaotian Zhang, Zhouquan Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo analyze the influencing factors of postoperative hypotension (POH) following video-assisted thoracoscopic lung resection (VATS) and evaluate a predictive model combining non-invasive hemodynamic parameters with the inferior vena cava collapsibility index (IVCCI).MethodsA prospective study enrolled 100 VATS patients (September 2024–March 2025). Patients were stratified into POH (n = 36) and Non-POH (n = 64) groups based on mean arterial pressure (MAP ≤ 65 mmHg or ≥ 30% reduction from baseline) within 24 hours postoperatively. Hemodynamic parameters (cardiac output [CO], systemic vascular resistance [SVR], stroke volume [SV], stroke volume variation [SVV], left ventricular stroke work [LVSW]) were monitored using the Non-invasive Continuous Arterial Blood Pressure And Cardiac Output Monitoring System. IVCCI was ultrasonographically measured post-extubation. Linear regression analyzed correlations between post-anesthetic emergence period hemodynamic parameters and POH, while multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed to identify predictive factors, leading to the development and validation of a clinical prediction model.ResultsThe incidence of early POH was 36%. Multivariate analysis demonstrated that a model combining post-anesthesia emergence period left ventricular stroke work (PA_LVSW, OR = 0.880, P < 0.01), IVCCI (OR = 1.095, P = 0.01), baseline MAP, and ASA achieved an AUC of 0.940 (95% CI: 0.895–0.985), with 83.3% sensitivity and 89.1% specificity, outperforming individual predictors (IVCCI: AUC = 0.65; PA_LVSW༚AUC = 0.84).ConclusionEarly POH after VATS is closely associated with cardiac function suppression and volume status imbalance. The multiparameter model integrating PA_LVSW, IVCCI, ASA physical status, and baseline MAP effectively predicts POH.Trial registrationChinese Clinical Trial Register, ChiCTR2500100275. Background

Postoperative hypotension (POH) is a common perioperative adverse event, characterized by high incidence, severe manifestations, prolonged duration, and underdetection rates of up to 47% [1, 2]. For patients undergoing VATS, intraoperative one-lung ventilation (OLV) and ventilation/perfusion mismatch impose higher demands on fluid and hemodynamic management during anesthesia [3]. Due to the elevated postoperative respiratory risks associated with thoracic surgery, cardiovascular complications associated with thoracic surgery are often overlooked in routine clinical practice [4]. In reality, thoracic surgery is linked to a high incidence of POH, which correlates with multiple adverse cardiac and cerebral outcomes [5]. However, most existing studies focus solely on intraoperative hypotension, and the common practice of reporting combined cardiovascular and respiratory complications makes it challenging to define the true incidence of POH [6].

Multiple factors contribute to perioperative hemodynamic changes. The specific etiology of perioperative hypotension often remains unclear, rendering POH unpredictable [7]. In recent years, bedside ultrasound and non-invasive cardiac output monitoring technologies have advanced significantly. Traditional predictive methods rely on isolated volumetric parameters such as the inferior vena cava collapsibility index (IVCCI), while ignoring other hemodynamic parameters like cardiac function (stroke volume [SV], cardiac output [CO]) and systemic vascular resistance (SVR), resulting in limited sensitivity and specificity of predictions. Variations in hemodynamic parameters may also indicate distinct underlying causes of hypotension [8].

This study aims to investigate whether multiparameter model—including volume status (IVCCI), vascular resistance (SVR), and cardiac function (stroke volume [SV], cardiac output [CO])—can predict the occurrence and potential causes of early POH in patients undergoing VATS.

Methods

Patients and study design

This study enrolled 110 patients who underwent VATS at The Third Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University (Changzhou Second People’s Hospital) between September 1, 2024, and March 1, 2025.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification I–III; (2) patients undergoing VATS under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation; (3) New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class I–III.

Exclusion criteria included: (1) patients undergoing thoracic cardiac, esophageal, or mediastinal surgery; (2) comorbid severe cardiovascular or cerebrovascular diseases; (3) history of hypotension; (4) unstable vital signs or emergency surgery; (5) abdominal obesity or intestinal distortion interfering with ultrasound measurement of inferior vena cava diameter; (6) inability to detect arterial pulse signals at sensor placement sites; (7) requiring reoperation for postoperative hemorrhage.

Upon entering the operating room, all patients underwent standard monitoring, including electrocardiography (ECG), heart rate (HR), and pulse oximetry (SpO₂). Non-invasive continuous blood pressure and cardiac output monitoring (NICAP-T20A system) were employed to measure continuous hemodynamic parameters.

Patients were stratified into POH and Non-POH groups based on hypotension occurrence within 24 h postoperatively. Hypotension was defined as Mean Arterial Pressure (MAP) ≤ 65 mmHg [9] or ≥ 30% decrease from baseline. To avoid pseudohypertension caused by preoperative anxiety, baseline blood pressure values were obtained from admission records, reflecting patients’ routine blood pressure control.

Sample size estimation followed the Events Per Variable rule for multivariate logistic regression analysis [10]. Based on preliminary data (assumed hypotension incidence: 40%) and planned inclusion of 4 independent variables (k = 4), the minimum sample size was calculated as:

N = 10×k/incidence = 10 × 4/0.40 = 100 patients. Adjusting for an 8% data loss rate, the final target sample size was: Nadjusted = 100/(1 − 0.08) ≈ 109 patients. Ultrasonographic measurement of IVCCI

After the operation, patients were transferred to the Post-Anesthesia Care Unit (PACU). Twenty minutes after extubation of the endobronchial tube and stabilization of spontaneous respiration, patients were placed in the supine position for IVCCI measurement using a portable phased-array cardiac ultrasound probe (Fig. 2). All measurements were performed by a single sonographer. The probe was positioned perpendicularly at the subxiphoid region and rotated with the indicator oriented cephalad [11]. At the junction of the IVC and right atrial ostium, M-mode imaging was applied to measure the maximum IVC diameter during expiration (IVC-dmax) and minimum IVC diameter during inspiration (IVC-dmin) within a single respiratory cycle [12], and calculate IVCCI. Three consecutive measurements were averaged for analysis.

Data collection

Demographic characteristics and perioperative laboratory parameters were collected, including age, gender, height, weight, baseline blood pressure, NYHA class, ASA physical status, echocardiographic ejection fraction (EF), preoperative comorbidities, as well as preoperative and postoperative hemoglobin, leukocyte count, platelet count, and protein levels. Intraoperative records included surgical site, procedure type, operative duration, fluid balance, and vasoactive drug requirements. Baseline hemodynamic parameters—including baseline cardiac output (BL_CO), baseline systemic vascular resistance (BL_SVR), baseline stroke volume (BL_SV), baseline left ventricular baseline stroke work (BL_LVSW), and baseline stroke volume variation (BL_SVV)—were measured using the Non-invasive Continuous Blood Pressure and Cardiac Output Monitoring System (NICAP-T20A) prior to anesthesia induction. At 20 min after extubation, IVCCI was ultrasonographically assessed, while post-anesthetic emergence period hemodynamic parameters—including post-anesthetic emergence period cardiac output (PA_CO), post-anesthetic emergence period systemic vascular resistance (PA_SVR), post-anesthetic emergence period stroke volume (PA_SV), post-anesthetic emergence period left ventricular stroke work (PA_LVSW), and post-anesthetic emergence period stroke volume variation (PA_SVV)—were simultaneously recorded. Postoperative blood pressure monitoring during the initial 24 h was performed by ward nursing staff using hourly non-invasive measurements, with researchers blinded to continuous monitoring data. The 24-hour postoperative lowest mean arterial pressure (L_MAP) value was documented [13]. Additional recorded parameters included 24-hour postoperative fluid balance, pain scores, and complications.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0, and Python 3.13.2 and R 4.4.3. Data distribution was assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed quantitative variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD), non-normally distributed variables as median (interquartile range, IQR), and categorical variables as frequency (percentage).Intergroup comparisons utilized independent samples t-test for normally distributed variables and Mann-Whitney U test for non-parametric data, while χ² test or Fisher’s exact test (for expected counts < 5) evaluated categorical variable associations. Linear regression analysis examined correlations between post-anesthetic emergence period hemodynamic parameters and L_MAP. Variables with statistical significance (P < 0.05) from the comparative analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression model. Stepwise regression (forward selection) was employed to identify influencing factors for early POH following VATS. Ultimately, a predictive model incorporating statistically significant variables (P < 0.05) was established. Model performance was evaluated through ROC analysis with area under the curve (AUC) quantification. Internal validation employed bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations), while calibration was assessed via Hosmer-Lemeshow test. A nomogram translated regression coefficients into a visual scoring system. Clinical utility was determined through decision curve analysis (DCA). All statistical tests were two-tailed, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Correlation between Post-Anesthetic emergence period hemodynamic parameters and the L_MAP

This study investigated the correlation between post-anesthetic emergence period hemodynamic parameters (PA_CO, PA_SV, PA_LVSW, PA_SVR) and the L_MAP. Model validation and performance evaluation

The prediction model for early POH within 24 h after VATS was constructed using baseline MAP, ASA physical status, IVCCI, and PA_LVSW. A risk nomogram was developed based on regression coefficients (Figure S 1). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test confirmed good model calibration (P > 0.05; Figure S 2). Discriminatory performance was assessed via the ROC curve, yielding an AUC of 0.940 (95% CI: 0.895–0.985; P < 0.001; Fig. 4). At the optimal cutoff probability of 0.452 (maximizing Youden’s index), the model achieved a sensitivity of 83.30% and specificity of 89.10%. The persistently significant volumetric parameter IVCCI and the hemodynamic parameter PA_LVSW (which showed significant association with POH in Linear Regression) were subjected to individual parameters diagnostic analysis. The integrated model (AUC = 0.94) demonstrated significantly higher diagnostic accuracy compared to individual parameters: IVCCI (AUC = 0.65) and PA_LVSW (AUC = 0.84) (DeLong test, P < 0.001) (Table S3). Discussion

POH is a common complication following thoracic surgery, closely associated with cardiac function suppression, dysregulated vascular tone, and fluid status imbalance.

The incidence of early POH after VATS was 36% in our cohort. Elevated IVCCI, reduced PA_LVSW, higher ASA, and elevated baseline MAP were identified as critical risk factors of POH. Although hypertension prevalence did not differ between groups, the POH group exhibited higher baseline MAP and ASA, suggesting compromised vascular compliance and impaired vasomotor regulation, which may explain their lower PA_SVR.

Univariate linear regression demonstrated correlations between PA_CO, PA_SV, PA_LVSW, and the lowest MAP. However, only PA_LVSW retained independent predictive value in multivariate analysis, indicating its role as a composite metric integrating ventricular contractility and afterload [14–16]. To validate PA_LVSW’s mediating effects, two logistic regression models were constructed: one excluding PA_LVSW (Model 1) and one including it (Model 2). As anticipated, PA_SV, PA_CO, and PA_SVR lost significance in Model 2, while baseline MAP, PA_LVSW, IVCCI, and ASA emerged as independent predictors. The multiparameter model combining baseline MAP, ASA, IVCCI, and PA_LVSW demonstrated exceptional discrimination.

Turan et al. [2] conducted a study demonstrating that 24% of patients experienced hypotensive episodes. In contrast, our study revealed a higher incidence of early POH (36%), a discrepancy potentially attributable to differences in surgical type and perioperative management strategies. Thoracic surgery patients typically receive restrictive fluid regimens during the perioperative period to prevent pulmonary edema [17, 18], whereas abdominal surgery prioritizes maintaining effective circulatory volume.

Kouz et al. [8] identified six hypotension “endotypes” with myocardial suppression as the main subtype (40%). Our findings show reduced PA_LVSW is linked to higher POH risk, in line with Jentzer et al. [19], who found low LVSWI correlated with increased mortality in CICU patients. The strong correlation between PA_LVSW and POH in this study further confirms its predictive value for prognosis.

In anesthesia, timely detection of hypotension-induced organ hypoperfusion remains critical [20]. While 75.7% of clinicians recognize CO as a key hemodynamic parameter [21], only 13.3% routinely monitor it. Despite > 90% acknowledging the need to improve perioperative hemodynamic management, approximately 33% of hospitals lack related devices, and patients perceive current methods invasive. This study employed the non-invasive continuous arterial pressure and cardiac output monitor, which uses dual near-infrared sensors on the retroauricular and dorsal pedal arteries. It calculates hemodynamic parameters via integrated NICAP™ (pulse wave velocity) and NICCO™ (waveform analysis) algorithms, with clinical calibration for precision. The non-invasive design avoids vascular complications, provides real-time continuous BP trends, and may improve access to advanced hemodynamic monitoring.

Furthermore, both SVV and IVCCI are established indicators for assessing volume status. In this study, IVCCI was significantly elevated in the POH group and retained independent predictive value in the multiparameter model, suggesting hypovolemia plays a critical role in POH. In contrast, SVV showed no intergroup difference. This discrepancy may stem from the specific applicability of SVV. Extensive studies [22–24] suggest that SVV outperforms central venous pressure (CVP) in predicting fluid responsiveness, with some advocating its use as a surrogate for CVP [25]. For instance, Xu et al. [26] demonstrated that an SVV and cardiac index guided restrictive fluid strategy improved oxygenation in patients undergoing OLV. Additional evidence [27] supports comparable predictive performance of SVV for fluid responsiveness between one- and two-lung ventilation. However, critics [28] emphasize SVV’s dependence on cardiopulmonary interactions, thus limiting its utility to controlled mechanical ventilation, with cautious interpretation required in spontaneous breathing or lung-protective ventilation. Additionally, many SVV’s studies [29] exclude spontaneously breathing or arrhythmic patients, potentially compromising generalizability. In this study, PA_SVV measurements were obtained after stable restoration of spontaneous respiration—a plausible explanation for its inability to reflect volume status. During spontaneous breathing, mixed thoracic and abdominal respiratory patterns complicate intrathoracic pressure fluctuations, potentially masking true SVV signals. In contrast, literature [30] indicates minimal differences in IVC diameter changes between spontaneous and mechanically ventilated patients. IVCCI reliably predicts fluid responsiveness during spontaneous breathing [31]. Our findings further corroborate these observations.

During VATS, factors such as OLV, hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction, reduced pulmonary vascular bed area from surgical resection, and transient pulmonary artery occlusion during the procedure can directly alter pulmonary circulatory hemodynamics. The presence of intrapulmonary shunting leads to a 25%−35% increase in pulmonary artery pressure and a 20%−50% elevation in pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), where the latter serves as the core pathophysiological driver for right ventricular remodeling. However, numerous studies indicate that the rapidly increased PAP and PVR during surgery often return to baseline levels within hours postoperatively [32]. In this study, patients in the POH group showed significant decreases in PA_CO and PA_SV, consistent with Glass et al.‘s findings [33] that increased pulsatile afterload following pulmonary resection impairs right ventricular ejection efficiency, resulting in decreased right ventricular ejection fraction and worsening right ventricular free-wall strain. This right ventricular dysfunction may partially explain the reduced PA_CO and PA_SV observed in the POH group. However, the predominance of wedge resections (57%) in this study’s surgical types may attenuate the impact of PVR changes. Therefore, the roles of PVR elevation and right ventricular dysfunction in early POH remain unclear.

POH research remains an underexplored frontier. Anesthesiologists predominantly focus on intraoperative hemodynamic management. In contrast, POH in general wards, devoid of continuous anesthesiologist monitoring, often manifests more insidiously and persists longer. This study pioneers the acquisition of post-anesthetic emergence period hemodynamic and volumetric parameters (20 min after extubation during stable spontaneous breathing) to predict early POH within 24 h, focusing on the “critical transition phase” of early postoperative hemodynamics. The 20-minute post-extubation window represents early recovery from anesthetic suppression. Parameters at this juncture may reflect two pathophysiological states: exhausted compensatory reserve, where patients at their compensatory limits (elevated IVCCI indicating preload deficit; reduced SVR suggesting vasodilation) fail to sustain adequate compensation within 24 h; and occult decompensation, wherein sympathetic activation transiently masks hypotension, but delayed decompensation emerges when compensatory mechanisms (tachycardia, vasoconstriction) falter according to the “pressure-flow-resistance triangle” theory [34]. IVCCI reflects real-time preload status; however, restrictive postoperative fluid strategies often perpetuate hypovolemia. Elevated IVCCI at 20 min post-extubation indicates persistent hypotension risk even with subsequent fluid resuscitation due to delayed intravascular volume restoration. Similarly, reduced LVSW at this critical window indicates impaired myocardial contractility, limiting compensatory CO elevation in response to escalating metabolic demands (e.g., pain, fever), ultimately culminating in hypotension. Notably, the time interval from surgery completion to the lowest MAP was significantly shorter in the hypotension group, suggesting that these patients were already at the tipping point between compensation and decompensation upon leaving the operating room.

Finally, this study has several limitations. First, although the temporal sequence between the acquisition of hemodynamic parameters and hypotension onset suggests potential causality, it must be emphasized that the observational design inherently limits causal inference. Second, the study cohort exclusively comprised patients undergoing VATS, and the single-center design with limited sample size may affect the clinical generalizability of the predictive model. Additionally, the model was developed and validated on the same dataset, which may lead to overfitting and overestimation of predictive performance. Although we conducted internal validation through bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) and the results demonstrated strong stability (average AUC 0.932 ± 0.022) (Figure S 3), this approach cannot fully replace external validation. Subsequent studies should adopt multicenter designs, large sample cohorts, and independent external data for external validation to refine model parameters and verify clinical applicability across different centers. Third, the current study did not monitor PVR or right ventricular functional parameters, which may have limited our comprehensive analysis of POH mechanisms. Elevated PVR increases right ventricular afterload and ejection resistance, while also impacting left ventricular preload and IVCCI through ventricular interdependence. However, since we lacked data on PVR and other right heart indicators, it remains unclear whether elevated PVR or right heart dysfunction constitutes independent risk factors for POH, nor can we elucidate their interactions with PA_LVSW and IVCCI. Future studies should incorporate noninvasive PVR monitoring and comprehensive right heart functional assessment to establish a more comprehensive prediction model for POH following VATS. Fourth, although the non-invasive monitor allows real-time dynamic tracking, this analysis used single-timepoint data instead of hemodynamic trajectories. Future studies should incorporate longitudinal measurements to clarify perioperative hemodynamic patterns.

Conclusions

In summary, early POH following VATS demonstrates a high incidence, primarily associated with cardiac function suppression and hypovolemia. The integrated predictive model incorporating baseline MAP, ASA, IVCCI, and PA_LVSW accurately identifies patients at risk for hypotension within 24 h postoperatively in the current single-center cohort, though external validation is essential before clinical implementation.
