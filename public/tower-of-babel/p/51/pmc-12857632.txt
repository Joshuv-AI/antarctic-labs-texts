Real-time quantification of soluble tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase as a measure of osteoclastogenesis
Authors: Justin A Roosma, Hammed Gafar, Maxwell T Boruff, Colton R Quinn, Jason W Ashley
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Differentiation of osteoclasts from their macrophage precursors can be assessed via multiple methods including microscopy, gene expression analysis, and protein immunoblotting, but these methods can be expensive or labor intensive and subject to variation in approach and interpretation. We have developed a low-cost kinetic assay for the quantification of Tartrate Resistant Acid Phosphatase (TRAP), which is secreted by osteoclasts in increasing amounts as they differentiate. This assay demonstrates reliable reproducibility and provides sensitive, quantified data that accurately represents altered levels of osteoclastogenesis due to variations in Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor κB (RANK) signaling and the presence of known osteoclast inhibitors. The assay is cost-effective, scalable and readily adoptable by labs conducting osteoclast biology research.

Introduction

Osteoclasts are large, multinucleated cells derived from the fusion of macrophages that are responsible for erosion of bone matrix as part of the physiological process of bone remodeling [1]. While they are necessary components of skeletal health, they can cause perturbations in skeletal mass and organization should their activity become dysregulated. One manifestation of osteoclast dysfunction is osteoporosis, a condition in which excessive osteoclastic resorption results in progressive bone loss and increased risk of fracture [2]. Bone loss through over-active osteoclasts can also arise from instances of inflammation, such as that of periodontal disease or osteomyelitis, or bone metastatic cancers [3–5]. These disorders of mis-regulated resorption are a significant source of morbidity, and, as such, there is a strong impetus to better understand molecular mechanisms of osteoclastogenesis and osteoclast function as a path to new bone-preserving therapies.

With respect to antiresorptive therapies, which target osteoclasts, there are two broad druggable categories: osteoclast number and osteoclast function. Osteoclast number-targeting therapies seek to reduce the overall number of osteoclasts within the bone and either block differentiation, shorten osteoclast lifespan, or both. Osteoclasts differentiate from their precursors under the control of two cytokines: Colony-Stimulating Factor1/CSF1 (also known as Macrophage Colony-Stimulating Factor/M-CSF), which promotes survival and proliferation of osteoclast precursors, and Receptor Activator of Nuclear Factor κB Ligand (RANKL), which signals through multiple transcriptional regulators including NFκB and Nuclear Factor of Activated T Cells (NFAT) to drive expression of osteoclast functional genes and precursor fusion [6,7]. Once fused, the mature osteoclasts form tight attachments to the bone surface which create a sealed off zone into which the cells secrete acid and proteolytic enzymes to dissolve mineral and digest matrix proteins. Current approved therapies that target osteoclast number include bisphosphonates, a class of small molecules that embed within bone matrix and poison osteoclasts shortly after they begin resorbing, and denosumab, a humanized anti-RANKL antibody that reduces osteoclastogenesis and shortens osteoclast lifespan via inhibition of RANK signaling [8,9]. There are currently no approved therapies targeting osteoclast function, though odanacatib, an inhibitor of the osteoclast protease cathepsin K, was investigated as a potential therapy until adverse events led to its abandonment [10,11]. Because therapies that target osteoclast number have side effects related to their suppression of bone remodeling, targeting osteoclast activity remains an attractive goal [12–15].

Whether to study fundamental mechanisms or to screen for new therapies, evaluation of osteoclast formation and function requires reliable and reproducible methods. Currently, osteoclastogenesis is measured ex vivo primarily through gene expression analysis and microscopy [16,17]. Reliable markers of osteoclastogenesis include matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9), cathepsin K (CTSK), calcitonin receptor (CALCR), NFAT, and tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP/ACP5). These markers can be quantified at the transcript level by RT-qPCR and protein level by Western blot, but TRAP can also be assessed histochemically [18]. TRAP staining is a useful tool for specifically identifying osteoclasts in histological sections of bone and is also frequently applied to osteoclasts differentiated in culture to enhance contrast and facilitate quantification. As osteoclast precursors fuse, they form a growing syncytium that can reach diameters up to 200 microns, and the resultant size of and number of nuclei within cultured osteoclasts are accepted quantified measures of differentiation[19]. Nevertheless, measurements of osteoclast size and nuclear number are largely performed manually and are sensitive to the subjective interpretations of individual evaluators, and, while computer-automated quantification methods have been described, they are not yet widely applicable [19,20].

Gene expression analyses and histochemical staining are standard methods of assessing osteoclastogenesis, but they allow only inference of resultant osteoclast function. Direct measures of osteoclast function involve culture of osteoclasts on either inorganic mineral surfaces or cut bone slices (typically bovine cortical bone) followed by staining and quantification of eroded areas, a process that can be as tedious as measurement of stained osteoclasts [21]. Level of erosion is a function of degree of differentiation, but osteoclast function can be segregated from differentiation by either the reseeding of equal numbers of differentiated osteoclasts onto erodible surfaces or normalizing eroded areas to number of osteoclasts present at the time of eroded area measurement. These osteoclastic resorption assays are an important component of molecular mechanism validation and ex vivo assessment of potential antiresorptives, but additional approaches to higher throughput osteoclast assessment can supplement resorption assays.

As an enzyme with multiple synthetic substrates, measurement of TRAP activity in conditioned medium, cell lysates, and serum has long been recognized as a viable approach to quantifying osteoclast activity in vivo and ex vivo [22,23]. The two most frequently applied substrates for determining TRAP activity are p-nitrophenyl phosphate (pNPP) and naphthol AS-BI phosphate. pNPP, when dephosphorylated by a wide variety of alkaline and acid phosphatases, produces an intense yellow color with an absorbance wavelength of 405 nm at alkaline pH where naphthol AS-BI phosphate is more specifically dephosphorylated by TRAP [24]. Dephosphorylated naphthol AS-BI is fluorescent at alkaline pH with a excitation/emission maxima at 405 nm/515 nm and this fluorogenesis has been exploited in assays to detect and measure phosphatase activity [25]. While they differ in their enzymatic activity specificities, both substrates produce a measurable signal at alkaline pH; in practice, this requires that TRAP activity assays, which are run at acidic pH, be quenched via the addition of strong bases prior to absorbance or fluorescent quantification. Because the reactions are not measured in real time, care must be taken to ensure samples are properly diluted and reactions run at fixed and defined intervals for intra- and inter-experimental comparisons.

To facilitate rapid and reproducible quantification of osteoclast differentiation and function, we have developed a novel kinetic TRAP activity assay that repurposes reagents used for histochemical detection of TRAP [26]. This assay employs naphthol AS-MX phosphate and fast red in a biochemical reaction that produces a bright red pigment that is quantifiable via absorbance measurement at 520 nm at acidic pH. This assay builds on phosphatase activity assays that have been used for over 40 years and offers two primary improvements: (1) because the chromogenic character of the resulting naphthol AS-MX-fast red azo dye is detectible at acidic pH, the reaction does not have to be stopped via strong base addition prior to measurement, which allows absorbance to be measured continuously and reduces reaction timing as a source of experimental variation and (2) because the TRAP activity assay reagent is identical to the naphthol AS-MX phosphate-fast red stain, labs that already TRAP stain osteoclasts can adopt the assay immediately at virtually no additional cost. Here we describe this inexpensive method for measuring activity of TRAP enzyme that can serve as a useful initial screen of potential modulators of osteoclast function. A protocol of this method is available at protocols.io [27].

Materials and methods

Reagents

Osteoclast culture medium (α-10) was formulated from Minimum Essential Medium Eagle powder (Millipore-Sigma, M0894) reconstituted with deionized water, 1.98 g/L sodium bicarbonate, 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Cytiva, SH30910.03), and 2 mM L-alanyl-L-glutamine (US Biological, 235242-100ml). Medium was sterile filtered using a 0.1-micron PES Stericup filter (Millipore-Sigma, S2VPU11RE) and stored at 4 °C. Other cell culture materials include Dulbecco’s phosphate buffered saline (PBS) without calcium and magnesium (Cytiva, SH30028.FS), Red blood cell lysing buffer (MilliporeSigma, R7757-100ML), and Accutase cell detachment solution (MilliporeSigma, SCR005). Recombinant mouse RANKL (Biolegend, 769404) and CSF1 (Biolegend, 576404) were diluted to 25 μg/mL with PBS and stored at −20 °C in 20 μL aliquots. Cyclosporine A (Cayman Chemical, 12088) was reconstituted at 10 mM with ethanol and stored at −20 °C and bafilomycin A1 (Cayman Chemical, 11038) was reconstituted at 1 mM with dimethylsulfoxide and stored at −20 °C. Hoechst 33342 (Cayman Chemical, 15547) was dissolved in DMSO at a concentration of 10 mg/mL (10,000X concentrate).

TRAP basic incubation medium was prepared according to a protocol provided by the University of Rochester Center for Musculoskeletal Research [26]. 9.2 anhydrous sodium acetate (Millipore-Sigma, S2889), 11.4 g L-(+) Tartaric Acid (Millipore-Sigma, T6521), 2.8 mL glacial acetic acid (Millipore-Sigma, A6283) were dissolved in deionized water to a final volume of 1 L, and pH was adjusted to a final pH between 4.7 and 5.0 using 5 M sodium hydroxide. TRAP staining solution mix was prepared by creating a solution of TRAP basic incubation medium with 0.6 mg/mL Fast Red Violet LB Salt (Millipore-Sigma, F3381) and 5 μL/mL Napthol AS-MX Phosphate Substrate mix (Millipore-Sigma, N4875) dissolved at 20 mg/mL in 2-ethoxyethanol (Milipore-Sigma, 128082).

Isolation of mouse bone marrow derived macrophages

All animal procedures were performed according to an approved EWU Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee protocol. Mouse femurs and tibias were dissected from euthanized C57BL/6 mice. Bone ends were cut off and 2.5 mL alpha-MEM was flushed through each bone and pooled in 15 mL polypropylene centrifuge tubes. Debris were allowed to settle for 3 minutes and supernatants were transferred to new 15 mL tubes which were centrifuged at 300RCF for 5 minutes to pellet bone marrow cells. Medium was aspirated and cell pellet was resuspended in 0.5 mL red cell lysing buffer and incubated at room temperature for 2 minutes prior to addition of 10 mL of PBS and centrifugation at 300RCF for 5 minutes. Pellets were resuspended in 10 mL alpha-MEM and cultured in 100 mm tissue culture treated dishes at 37 °C and 5% CO2 overnight. The following morning, non-adherent cells were transferred to a 100 mm non-treated petri dish and cultured with 25 ng/mL CSF1 for 72 hours to differentiate and expand macrophages; medium was refreshed 24 hours after initial transfer.

Osteoclastogenesis assay

Differentiated macrophages were washed with 10 mL PBS and lifted with 1 mL Accutase, incubation at room temperature for 5–10 minutes, addition of 4 mL of alpha-MEM, and pipetting. 10 μL of cell suspension was mixed with 10 μL trypan blue and viable cell density was determined using a hemocytometer. 10,000 cells were seeded into each well of a 96-well tissue culture-treated plate in 0.1 mL alpha-MEM. In RANKL dose experiments, seeded cells received an additional 0.1 mL of alpha-MEM containing 50 ng/mL CSF1 and 2X RANKL (e.g., the 25 ng/mL RANKL dose received 0.1 mL alpha-MEM with 50 ng/mL CSF1 and 50 ng/mL RANKL). In cyclosporine A dose experiments, seeded cells received an additional 0.1 mL of alpha-MEM with 50 ng/mL CSF1, 50 ng/mL RANKL, and 2X cyclosporin A dose. In bafilomycin A1 experiments, cells were seeded in 0.2 mL medium containing 25 ng/mL CSF1 and 25 ng/mL RANKL, allowed to differentiate for 24 hours before the addition of 25 ng/mL CSF1, 25 ng/mL RANKL, and a dose of bafilomycin A1. Untreated cells in the cyclosporine A experiments were cultured with 0.005% ethanol (equivalent to the ethanol concentration in the highest cyclosporine A dose) and untreated cells in the bafilomycin experiments were cultured with 0.05% DMSO (equivalent to DMSO concentration in the highest bafilomycin A1 dose). All differentiations were carried out for a total of 72 hours with a medium change at 48 hours.

TRAP assay execution

The microplate reader was pre-heated to 37 °C and programmed to take measurements at an absorbance of 520 nm for 180 min at 2 min intervals. 0.1 mL medium was transferred from cultured cells to matched wells in a new 96-well plate, 100 μL of TRAP stain solution was added to each well, and the plate was loaded and allowed to run. Results were then exported to Excel for analysis. Figure 1 depicts the assay workflow.

Data analysis

Plate reader data was first normalized by dividing measurements in each well by the absorbance value at time 0. Normalized absorbance data was then restructured to create a time versus condition (RANKL dose, bafilomycin dose, cyclosporine A dose) matrix. Linear regressions of the absorbance vs time values from the linear portion of the curve (10 minutes to 120 minutes) were calculated and the slopes were used as TRAP activity. Within each experiment, relative TRAP activities were calculated by dividing each reaction’s slope by the mean of control group (3.125ng/mL RANKL-treated, DMSO-treated). Osteoclasts were imaged using the Leica Thunder Live Cell microscopy system in Texas Red and Hoechst channels and stained areas and nuclear numbers were quantified using Fiji. Linear regression and statistical significance between groups were then calculated using the linear model, one-way ANOVA, and Tukey HSD functions in R.

Results and discussion

RANKL treatment increases soluble TRAP activity in a dose-dependent manner

To determine whether secreted TRAP activity correlates with level of RANK signaling, we cultured male osteoclast precursors with increasing doses of RANKL (0–25 ng/mL) for the duration of the differentiation process. Where doses from 3.125 ng/mL to 25 ng/mL demonstrated increasing rates of absorbance increase, untreated (0 ng/mL) macrophages produced flat, slope-less curves suggesting that the assay is selective for TRAP over tartrate-sensitive acid phosphatases (Figure 2A,B). To assess intra- and inter-experimental variability, we performed four independent experiments with macrophages isolated from four different mice. Where combined values from experiments demonstrated a high level of variance (Figure 2A), variance within an individual experiment was low (Figure 2B). Potential sources of variance could include natural variation in osteoclastogenic potential in macrophages isolated from different mice, relative potency of recombinant RANKL stock, overall time in culture, and relative timing of medium changes. To account for these and potentially unidentified sources of variance, we calculated relative TRAP activity within each experiment by dividing calculated slopes of each sample by the mean slope of 3.125 ng/mL RANKL samples (Figure 2C). Following conversion, the mean relative activity of the 3.125 ng/mL RANKL group was 1 with group variation maintained (Figure 2D). Statistical analysis of combined data via ANOVA with Tukey HSD revealed a statistically significant increase in relative TRAP activity at every increase in RANKL dose (Figure 2E).

Relative TRAP activity better normalizes inter-experimental variability than TRAP activity per nucleus

To assess whether calculated TRAP activities can be better normalized by dividing the number of nuclei present, we conducted an addition three osteoclastogenesis and TRAP activity assays with a RANKL dose of 25 ng/mL with three samples per experiment. At the conclusion of differentiation, TRAP activity was measured and nuclei in resultant osteoclasts were stained with Hoechst 33342 and counted using Fiji’s analyze particles function. To calculate activity per nucleus, the slopes of each sample were divided by the number of nuclei, and, because the nucleus adjusted activity values are several orders of magnitude smaller than the unadjusted activities, we transformed activity and activity per nucleus values into relative values by dividing each value by the mean of all values to better visualize the variance before and after normalization (Figure 3A). Dividing activity by number of nuclei did not appreciably reduce variance between measured activity values and, at least within the range of nuclei tested, there was no significant correlation between nuclear count and TRAP activity (Figure 3B,C), which might be due to limitations in accuracy in threshold-based automated nuclei quantification in osteoclasts which tend to have smaller and less brightly stained nuclei compared to unfused precursors (Supplemental Figure S1). In contrast, our prior method of determining relative activity greatly reduced variance (Figure 3D).

TRAP activity correlates with TRAP-stained area in cultured osteoclasts

To evaluate whether the TRAP activity assay correlates with TRAP positive culture area, which is another measure of osteoclastogenesis, we measured wells of TRAP-stained cultures matched to measurements of TRAP activity in conditioned media from the same wells. Increasing concentrations of RANKL resulted larger, more mature osteoclasts that occupied a greater percentage of the well area (Figure 4A). After converting to relative TRAP-stained area by dividing areas in each experimental replicate by the mean area of the 3.125 ng/mL group, we found a statistically significant correlation between RANKL dose and stained area (Figure 4B). Additionally, regression analysis revealed a significant correlation between relative TRAP activity and relative TRAP area (Figure 4C). Similar relationships were observed in female derived osteoclasts (Supplemental Figure S2A–C).

TRAP activity is reduced by pharmacologic inhibition of osteoclastogenesis

To determine whether our assay can be used to identify inhibitors of osteoclastogenesis, we cultured differentiating osteoclasts with increasing doses of the calcineurin/NFAT inhibitor cyclosporine A (0–500 nM). All doses significantly reduced soluble TRAP activity (Figure 5A, B). Similar results were observed for the V-ATPase inhibitor, bafilomycin A1, which also significantly reduced soluble TRAP activity at all doses used (0–500 nM) (Figure 5A, C). These results confirm that the TRAP assay can accurately detect inhibition of osteoclastogenesis.

Conclusion

We have demonstrated a novel kinetic assay of soluble TRAP activity that is a reliable measure of osteoclastogenesis and offers several advantages. Firstly, this assay accurately detects bidirectional changes in osteoclastogenesis in response to both positive and negative signals with high sensitivity. Importantly, while this method correlated in accuracy to quantification of TRAP-stained images, a commonly utilized measure of osteoclastogenesis, its execution based on specific plate reader settings is more readily standardized as compared to variable methods in image processing. The TRAP assay also appeared to outperform quantification of TRAP-stained area as it detected significant changes at every incremental RANKL dose where TRAP stain area detected significant differences only at wider dose differences. This assay can be readily adopted by laboratories studying osteoclasts as it requires no additional reagents beyond what are used for TRAP staining. These minimal requirements of the TRAP assay make it attractive due to its rapid implementation as only conditioned medium is collected and no lysis or fixation of samples is immediately required. Although not directly highlighted within this report, the non-terminal nature of the assay easily allows for longitudinal measurements of osteoclastogenesis if time-dependent experiments are desired. Additionally, the ease of access to this assay readily scales for high throughput experimentation.

Variation in differentiation and maturation is a challenge in osteoclast research as multiple variables including idiosyncrasies in osteoclast precursor source animals, variations in potency in different production lots of M-CSF and RANKL, and culture medium pH [28,29]. pH is a particularly challenging variable as the bicarbonate buffering of culture media is controlled by CO2 concentrations, which means that the frequency of incubator door opening could contribute to the size and function of resultant osteoclasts. Because of this, management of inter-experimental variability was a major focus of our development of this assay. We found that while inter-experimental variability was high, intra-experimental variability was low, so we investigated normalizing activity to the mean of control group activity. This relative activity showed a high level of consistency between experiments and reliably controls for non-experimental variables between experimental iterations. We considered cell number as a source of variation, but when we attempted to normalize TRAP activity to nuclear number, there was no improvement in experimental consistency. This may be due to challenges in obtaining accurate nuclear counts from well scan images that result from variations in nuclear size and brightness. We have found that nuclei in osteoclasts are fainter and smaller than those in unfused precursors, which makes accurate automated threshold-based counting a challenge. Manual counts of nuclei or cells might be a viable approach, but this would compensate only for cell number and no other variables. In addition, the time, effort, and quantifier-level variability in such an approach is contrary to our goal of a simplified, scalable quantification of osteoclast differentiation and activity.

While we anticipate that this assay can be adapted to measurement of TRAP activity in other sources, such as serum, or in other species such as humans, our development focused on measurement of TRAP activity in mouse osteoclast conditioned medium. Additional development will be necessary to generalize this method. There could also be circumstances wherein increased TRAP activity could be the result of events other than enhanced differentiation. For example, rapid death of osteoclast could lead to release of intracellular TRAP, and, while we didn’t observe a correlation between nuclear number and TRAP activity in our experiments, large differences in osteoclast precursor numbers could lead to difference in TRAP activity unrelated to degree of differentiation. Both scenarios could be recognized however with microscopic examination of TRAP-stained cells, which, because the activity assay and staining use the same reagent, can be easily performed contemporaneously. With proper contextualization via direct observation of resultant osteoclasts, bone resorption assays, and other procedures, relative TRAP activity is a valuable addition to the osteoclast researcher’s experimental repertoire.

Future perspective

One of the most exciting recent developments in scientific research is the rapid maturation of machine learning approaches to analysis of experimental data. Our original inspiration for developing this assay was our on-going struggle with analysis and quantification of osteoclast cultures. Osteoclasts demonstrate highly variable morphologies that limit generalizability of automated measures of osteoclast size, number, and nuclear content. There have been multiple published algorithms for AI-driven osteoclast identification and quantification, but we have found that these models require extensive retraining before they can be applied to datasets from our lab, and often must be trained again once they are applied to a new dataset generated from a separate experiment [19,20,30,31]. Despite their variability, human eyes can identify osteoclasts after only brief training; as the technology continues to mature, we are optimistic that a generalizable model of osteoclast quantification will be developed within the next five to ten years, and its integration with existing approaches will greatly accelerate understanding of osteoclast biology and development of new therapies.
