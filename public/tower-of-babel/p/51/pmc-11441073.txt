Regarding: “Psychotherapeutic and pharmacological agents for post-traumatic stress disorder with sleep disorder: network meta-analysis”
Authors: Kun Xu, Yuan Liu, Hua Zhao, Yuejun Zhou
Published: 2024

Dear Editor

We read with great interest the paper published in Annals of Medicine entitled ‘Psychotherapeutic and pharmacological agents for post-traumatic stress disorder with sleep disorder: network meta-analysis’ by Huang et al. [1]. In this study, the network meta-analysis method was used to compare numerous interventions through direct and indirect evidence, and finally the most effective countermeasures for psychotherapy and drug treatment of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) with sleep disorders were obtained. For example, CBT-I plus IRT and CBT-I may be the most appropriate countermeasures for tackling PTSD symptom severity, CBT-I may be the most appropriate solution to improve sleep quality, and prazosin and hydroxyzine are more dominant in reducing the severity of nightmares. We highly support and appreciate the researchers’ work, and thank them for their contributions in the treatment of PTSD with sleep disorders. However, we propose the following considerations.

Under the subheading of ‘Study characteristics and quality assessment’, Huang et al. mentioned that ‘The search yield 5,095 results. After the initial evaluation, 1,048 duplicates were removed and 4,026 studies were regarded as irrelevant (as determined by reading the titles and abstracts). 21 articles with 24 RCTs were determined by reading the full text.’ We have three doubts about this description. First, the initial literature search results (5095 articles) are inconsistent with the data in the supplementary materials. According to the Supplementary Method 1-Search strategy in Huang et al.’ s article, we found that the total number of search results in the four databases were 6768 articles, of which PubMed search results were 2426 articles, EMBASE search results were 3764 articles, the Cochrane Library search results were 459 articles and American Psychological Association PsycNet search results were 119 articles. Second, the data involved in the literature screening process were inconsistent with the flowchart in the supplementary materials. According to Supplementary Figure 1 in Huang et al.’s article, we couldn’t see them removing 4026 irrelevant articles by reading titles and abstracts. Therefore, we consider that either this sentence (4026 studies were regarded as irrelevant) was incorrectly formulated, or the flowchart in the supplementary material was incorrect. Third, the previous and later expressions of the number of articles finally included were inconsistent. Huang et al. first described that ‘21 articles with 24 RCTs were determined by reading the full text’. Then, in the next paragraph, it is mentioned that ‘A total of 1,647 participants from 20 articles with 24 RCTs were included in this NMA.’ We believe that the correct expression of ‘21 articles with 24 RCTs were determined by reading the full text’ should be ‘20 articles with 24 RCTs were determined by reading the full text’.

Finally, we reiterate our appreciation for the excellent work of Huang et al. in the treatment of PTSD with sleep disorders, which not only identifies the most effective treatment for PTST with sleep disorders, but also provides a reference for the relevant clinical guidelines for PTST with sleep disorders. However, it is worth noting that the data of the publication will influence the results of the study. Therefore, for the sake of the rigor and scientificity of the article, the author needs more rigorous expression.
