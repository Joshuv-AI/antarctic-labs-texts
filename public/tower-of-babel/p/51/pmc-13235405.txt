EQ-5D vs SF-6D: Discrepancies in measuring quality of life for patients with vertebral compression fractures
Authors: Søren W. Sørensen, Andreas K. Andresen, Mikkel Ø. Andersen, Leah Y. Carreon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionEuroQoL-5D (EQ-5D) and SF-6D are both designed to measure health states or utility but differ in questionnaire structure and scoring algorithm. This yields divergent results in several populations. The magnitude of this discrepancy in patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures (OVCF) is unknown.Research questionTo compare EQ-5D and SF-6D in determining health states and utility in patients with OVCF.Materials and methodsRegistry EQ-5D and SF-6D from patients treated for OVCF with vertebroplasty at a single center were obtained for a preoperative cross-sectional analysis. Response distributions were evaluated for boundary effects. Discrepancies between utility scores were assessed by Wilcoxon signed rank tests, intraclass correlation coefficients and Bland-Altman plots.ResultsThe analysis included 736 patients. The most frequent response was the floor level in a single EQ-5D dimension and in half of SF-6D dimensions. SF-6D utility median was 0.50 (range 0.30 to 0.91) and EQ-5D utility scores were bimodal with median 0.16 (range −0.594 to 1). Median difference (SF – EQ) was 0.34 (range −0.51 to 1.08). Inter-rater agreement was poor: ICC = 0.158 (CI -0.019; 0.314).Discussion and conclusionEQ-5D and SF-6D are not interchangeable for patients with OVCF undergoing vertebroplasty. Median differences are large, and SF-6D produces systematically higher utility scores than EQ-5D in patients with poor health while the reverse is true for good health. Differing scoring algorithms and more floor effects in SF-6D than EQ-5D may contribute to utility discrepancy.Researchers are urged to be mindful of this discrepancy when interpreting health states and utility.

Introduction

EuroQoL (Rabin et al., 2001) (EQ-5D) and Short Form 36 (Ware and Sherbourne, 1992) (SF-36) are the most extensively utilized instruments to measure health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in surgical spine research (Guzman et al., 2016). These instruments facilitate comparisons of health states across different diseases and provide estimates of preference-based utility scores for use in cost-effectiveness analyses. To obtain utility scores, SF-36 must first be converted to SF-6D (Brazier et al., 2002a) whereas EQ-5D can be utilized directly. EQ-5D and SF-6D measure the same concepts, HRQoL and utility, and should in principle be interchangeable – exchange of instrument should not alter results materially. Yet they differ in questionnaire structure and scoring algorithms, and accordingly yield different results (Brazier et al., 2004; Whitehurst et al., 2011; Søgaard et al., 2009; McDonough et al., 2005)–(Brazier et al., 2004; Whitehurst et al., 2011; Søgaard et al., 2009; McDonough et al., 2005).

Numerous studies have compared EQ-5D and SF-6D utility (Whitehurst et al., 2011), and several properties underlying the differences have been characterized. Overall, the magnitude and direction of utility score difference seems dependent on disease (Brazier et al., 2004) and health state: SF-6D has been found to exceed EQ-5D in poor health while the opposite occurs in healthy respondents (Whitehurst et al., 2011). For patients with spine-related diseases in particular, the between-instrument utility difference is pronounced (Søgaard et al., 2009; McDonough et al., 2005). Floor and ceiling effects may contribute to the difference. In a heterogenous patient population, ceiling effects in EQ-5D and floor effects of SF-6D have been reported, and SF-6D items concentrate on milder health problems (Brazier et al., 2004). Specifically in back pain, EQ-5D seems to align better with poor health states (Johnsen et al., 2013). Patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture (OVCF) are often older and frail, with high levels of acute back pain and very poor quality of life, which could challenge interchangeability of the instruments even further.

However, no direct comparison has been performed between the instruments for patients with OVCF treated by vertebroplasty, and the relative impact of utilizing EQ-5D as opposed to SF-6D for this population remains unknown.

The aim of this study was to characterize the degree of interchangeability of EQ-5D and SF-6D for patients with OVCF and examine potential sources of disagreement in response distributions and dimension correlations.

Methods

Data source

Study data was obtained from the Danish national spine registry, DaneSpine, where data on surgical spine patients in Denmark is systematically and prospectively collected. Patients enter their demographic data and self-reported health status using standardized questionnaires at regular intervals, while surgeons enter information regarding diagnosis and any occurring complications. Gender is assigned based on civil registration number. Participants of DaneSpine are informed of the purpose of data collection before providing consent. For the present study, data was retrieved for all patients with osteoporotic vertebral fractures scheduled for percutaneous vertebroplasty at a single tertiary spine surgery unit between June 2010 and November 2024. Patients with malignant, infectious or high-energy fractures are not included in the cohort. Institutional guidelines indicate that surgery can be performed only where the fracture is acute, consistent with severe focal pain and vertebral body edema is present on short tau inversion recovery magnetic resonance imaging. Pincer-type fracture or complete collapse precludes vertebroplasty.

During the course of the study the indication for vertebroplasty did not change. All surgeries have been performed by the same team of surgeons who each perform more than 40 vertebroplasties per year. The administration of questionnaires was digitized in 2022, however patients who prefer paper versions are continually offered these. Surgical candidates generally have more severe symptoms, accordingly this is a highly selected population of osteoporotic vertebral fracture patients.

Questionnaires

EuroQoL EQ-5D-3L (Rabin et al., 2001) is by design a non-disease-specific questionnaire for the description and valuation of HRQoL. The questionnaire contains 5 single-question ‘dimensions’: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort and anxiety/depression. Each dimension is rated on three severity levels: 1 (no problems), 2 (some problems), and 3 (extreme problems). Additionally, a visual analog scale of overall health is included in the questionnaire. Reponses to the five questions yield a number combination that describes a specific ‘health state’, e.g. 11213 if the respondent has no problems with mobility, self-care and pain/discomfort but some problems performing usual activities and extreme problems with anxiety/depression. Through valuation studies eliciting the general population preference for these health states, a utility value can be obtained, ranging between −0.594 (worst imaginable) to 1 (perfect health) with 0 representing death (Dolan, 1997). UK preference weights (Dolan, 1997) were selected to align with the SF-6D UK-based preference weights.

Short Form Six-Dimension (SF-6D) is also designed for description and valuation of HRQoL across different diseases (Brazier et al., 2002a). The developers of SF-6D derived 6 dimensions from the comprehensive Short Form 36 (SF-36) (Ware and Sherbourne, 1992) in an attempt to reduce the health states to a number feasible for valuation studies. The dimensions of SF-6D are: Physical Functioning (6 response levels), Role Limitations (4 levels), Social Functioning (5 levels), Pain (6 levels), Mental Health (5 levels) and Vitality (5 levels). As for EQ-5D, higher level responses indicate a worse state. Through valuation studies (Brazier et al., 2002b), health states comprising a response combination of the 6 dimensions can be converted to a single utility score representing population preference for the health state. The utility score for SF-6D ranges between 0.296 (worst state) and 1 (full health).

Patients responded to the Danish translation of EQ-5D and SF-36 (Bjorner et al., 1998) at a single instance with EQ-5D presented first, and SF-36 responses were assigned to the SF-6D as intended by the developer (Brazier et al., 2002b). Though more recent versions of EQ-5D and SF-6D exist, the original versions are continually used in large surgical spine registries (Pascucci et al., 2023), as changes in instrument may decrease comparability between past and future data. To fully utilize EQ-5D-3L and SF-6D in retrospective analyses, and to interpret utility outcomes in already published literature, it is important to characterize the degree of interchangeability between the two instruments.

Statistics and analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (R Core Team, 2025) version 4.5.0 and the package Tidyverse (Wickham, 2023).

Characteristics of the study cohort are described using means, standard deviations and percentages. Risk of selection bias was assessed by testing assumptions of no difference in baseline characteristics between included and excluded patients. Distributions and assumptions of normality were assessed visually by histograms and quantile-quantile plots.

Interchangeable instruments are defined here as exchangeable without importantly changing the results at a group or individual level. Paired comparison was used to assess whether the instruments differed systematically at the group level. Agreement at the individual level was assessed by intraclass correlation (ICC 3,1) in a two-way mixed effects model for absolute agreement (Shrout and Fleiss, 1979; Koo and Li, 2016). Intra class correlation quantifies the extent to which the two instruments arrive at the exact same utility value for a patient. An ICC of 1 indicates perfect agreement; all the scores are pairwise identical. An ICC of 0 indicates the difference between instruments completely obscure the differences between patients. Descriptive labels were used to interpret the ICC: Poor <0.5, moderate≥0,5,≥0.75 good and excellent≥0.9 (Koo and Li, 2016). In addition, Bland Altman analysis was used to quantify the magnitude of disagreement on the same scale as the instruments, in units of utility.

Distribution of dimension responses were inspected visually and are presented as proportions endorsing each ordinal response level. Responses signifying good health (the numerically lowest levels) were considered ceiling values and numerically large responses (signifying poor health) were considered floor values. A floor or ceiling effect was considered when the mode was at the floor or ceiling value. This approach was chosen, because responses are ordinal with few levels (3-6) and a conventional 15% threshold would overstate the risk, even in a balanced distribution. Limited scale utilization was considered when most participants concentrated on a narrow range of response levels. Rank correlation between dimensions of EQ-5D and SF-6D were calculated as Spearman's Rho. Correlations were considered strong ≥ 0.6, 0.6 > moderate≥0.3 and weak <0.3. Spearman's rho is a measure of rank correlation; a high correlation implies that individuals maintain a similar relative ordering on the two instruments — that is, people who score high on one dimension tend to score high on the comparator. Moderate to strong correlations may be expected for conceptually similar dimensions.

Distributions and differences of utility scores are summarized as mean, SD, median and range and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Median was the preferred measure of central tendency for paired comparisons of non-parametric distributions, and all median CIs were calculated by bootstrapping. Chi-square test was used for paired comparisons of binary variables, and t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test for comparison of means for normal and non-normal continuous variables, respectively.

Results

A total of 967 patients in DaneSpine with OVCF underwent vertebroplasty and 76% (n = 736) had complete preoperative EQ-5D and SF-36 data and were included in the analysis. For excluded patients (n = 231), incompleteness in SF-6D (n = 219) was more frequent than in EQ-5D (n = 86) and this difference was significant (p < 0.001). Both scores were unobserved for some of these patients (n = 74).

Discussion

In this study, the interchangeability of the HRQoL-instruments EQ-5D and SF-6D was assessed preoperatively in patients with OVCF assigned to vertebroplasty. A large and statistically significant median difference of 0.34 between utility scores was observed, which was strongly dependent on health status and ranging from −0.51 to 1.08. Absolute agreement was poor, meaning the two instruments do not arrive at near identical values. Accordingly, EQ-5D and SF-6D can not be used interchangeably at an individual or group level for assessment of health utility in patients with OVCF. This study examined cross-sectional differences. Large preoperative differences raise concern for cost-effectiveness analyses, and longitudinal studies are needed to assess the degree to which cost-effectiveness for the same treatment depends on instrument choice.

Other studies have compared concurrent utility scores of EQ-5D and SF-6D. Across seven diverse groups of patients, Brazier (Brazier et al., 2004) found only small differences between instruments overall (SF-6D larger by a mean 0.045) and better agreement between instruments (ICC of 0.51). Larger between-instrument differences have been found in patients with spinal disease. Søgaard (Søgaard et al., 2009) recruited patients from trials of spinal fusion techniques for chronic low back pain and found a mean difference of 0.085 at long-term follow-up. McDonough (McDonough et al., 2005) investigated baseline differences between several HRQoL measures in patients with disc herniation, spinal stenosis or degenerative spondylolisthesis and found the mean difference between SF-6D and EQ-5D to be 0.18. Johnsen (Johnsen et al., 2013) examined psychometric properties of EQ-5D and SF-6D in patients from a trial of disc prosthesis or rehabilitation for chronic back pain and found a mean difference of 0.23 at baseline. Overall, the difference between the two instruments seem to depend on type of disease, and large differences are observed in many types of spine disease. Whitehurst (Whitehurst et al., 2011) summarized mean differences between EQ-5D and SF-6D across numerous studies and determined the SF-6D to be largest in poor health while the opposite was true for good health. This pattern of mean score differences was also observed for individual participants in the present study and others (Brazier et al., 2004; Søgaard et al., 2009; Johnsen et al., 2013).

In principle, the difference between EQ-5D and SF-6D utility scores can be caused by either questionnaire structure or the algorithm for deriving utility scores. Differences in questionnaire structure were presently supported by only low or moderate correlation between dimensions of the two instruments seen in this study. There were more widespread floor effects in SF-6D compared to EQ-5D. The floor value was the mode in half of the SF-6D dimensions, but only so in the Pain/Discomfort domain of the EQ-5D. These widespread floor effects in the SF-6D might be part of the reason for the large utility score discrepancies observed in patients with poorer health. For patients in the present study, who generally have poor HRQoL, the ability to provide fitting response options at especially the lower end of the range is important. Otherwise, instrument unresponsiveness occurs and differences between patients in different poor health states are not reflected in the response options. Additionally, in a longitudinal study where a person at the floor level deteriorates, this person cannot express their changed state.

Similar to the findings in the current study, Johnsen (Johnsen et al., 2013) determined EQ-5D to align with worse health problems more appropriately than SF-6D, and Brazier found the SF-6D response options to be concentrated at the milder end of health problems (Brazier et al., 2004). In Brazier's study population, this lead to concerns of ceiling effects in EQ-5D and “very little use of level 3 in four of the five dimensions of EQ-5D” (Brazier et al., 2004). These findings of poor low range utilization, however, are contrasted in the present population, where response distributions favored EQ-5D slightly. The worst health state was highly relevant with more than 45% of responder opting for the worst health state in at least 1 dimension. McDonough (McDonough et al., 2005) also did not observe an EQ-5D ceiling effect, but substantial endorsement of the worst level of Pain. The overall weak to moderate correlation between dimensions of EQ-5D and SF-6D support the notion that dimension content is not strongly overlapping between instruments for the present cohort. SF-6D Physical Functioning might be expected to correlate strongly with EQ-5D Mobility but was observed to correlate with EQ-5D Self Care (Rho 0.58). This was in fact the strongest correlation of any dimension combination. As SF-6D Physical Function worsens, responses inquire about personal hygiene, which is also the focus of EQ-5D Self Care, and this aspect may provide a speculative explanation for the correlation in patients with very high disability such as the present cohort.

The second source of discrepancy between scores is the algorithms used to calculate utility. These algorithms result in unequal utility ranges: EQ-5D allows for negative scores of −0.594 that imply a utility worse than death, whereas SF-6D has a theoretical minimum value of 0.296. This difference in range might explain a large part of the observed discrepancy in very poor health states such as the present cohort with 31% negative EQ-5D scores. Additionally, the algorithms are designed in such a way that a response in the worst level of any dimension will reduce the score more for EQ-5D than for SF-6D. In EQ-5D, responses in the worst level introduces the N3 term, whereby a substantial 0.269 is subtracted from the utility scores. In SF-6D, responses in the worst level introduces the MOST term, which subtracts only 0.032. In this way, EQ-5D penalizes worst responses substantially more than SF-6D does. For example, a patient with extreme pain would probably respond level 3 EQ-5D Pain/discomfort and level 6 SF-6D Pain. This response reduces utility scores by 0.269 for EQ-5D and 0.032 for SF-6D, introducing a large utility difference of 0.237. When a large proportion of patients have very poor health, the impact of N3 og MOST terms on the difference in median utility may be large. Lastly, the bimodal EQ-5D distribution observed presently and in other studies (Brazier et al., 2004; Søgaard et al., 2009; McDonough et al., 2005) may be introduced by the large impact of the N3 term (Brazier et al., 2004; Søgaard et al., 2009).

The sample size of this study was relatively large and had a sizable completeness of 76% for both EQ-5D and SF-36. Patients excluded for missing data were generally similar to complete cases, although statistically significant yet small differences in age and ODI were observed. A complete case analysis allowed for comparisons between instruments at the individual level, and baseline differences between included and excluded patients did not indicate substantial selection bias.

Completion rates were better for EQ-5D than SF-36, perhaps reflecting the greater length and respondent burden of SF-36 as well as the ordering of questionnaires with EQ-5D in front.

DaneSpine includes only patients assigned to surgery. As these patients generally have severe symptoms and instrument discrepancy appears dependent on severity, the study findings may not be generalizable to cases of OVCF with mild symptoms.

This study highlights the difference in outcome of EQ-5D and SF-6D and examines the underlying differences in questionnaire structure and utility algorithm. Other important measurement properties, such as reliability or responsiveness, are beyond the scope of this study, but are necessary components for guiding the choice of instrument. Importantly, the large difference in baseline scores between instruments shown here raises concerns for the comparability of cost-effectiveness studies using EQ-5D and SF-6D. The findings of this study alone cannot provide an actionable recommendation for choice of instrument, and sensitivity-analyses are therefore recommended to improve interpretability (Søgaard et al., 2009).

Conclusion

This study is the first comparison of EQ-5D and SF-6D to estimate HRQoL in patients scheduled for sugergy due to OVCFs. For this population, EQ-5D and SF-6D should not be considered interchangeable, as discrepancies between utility scores are large. Limited content overlap between instruments and broader floor effects of SF-6D potentially contribute to the difference. This study indicates that the choice between EQ-5D or SF-6D is likely to influence health state utility estimates, and sensitivity analyses are therefore recommended. Further studies with a longitudinal design are needed to evaluate the impact of instrument choice on cost-effectiveness.
