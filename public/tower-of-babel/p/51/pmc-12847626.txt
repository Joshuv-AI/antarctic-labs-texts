Rinaldo Bellomo's contribution to trauma and traumatic brain injury research
Authors: Craig French
Published: 2025

Introduction

The Austin Hospital, where Rinaldo was the Director of Intensive Care Research from 1994, did not routinely manage critically ill trauma patients apart from those with isolated spinal cord injury. Rinaldo, however, recognised the global burden of traumatic injury, particularly traumatic brain injury (TBI): here, as in so many areas of critical illness, he led and inspired others. His work addressed three broad themes: understanding the epidemiology of traumatic injury (especially in Australia and New Zealand); fluid and osmotherapy in traumatic brain injury; and the evaluation of potentially neuroprotective adjuvant therapies for moderate and severe TBI. This tribute focuses on Rinaldo's contribution to our knowledge of fluid, osmotherapy, and adjuvant therapies in TBI.

Albumin and traumatic brain injury

The story of the Saline versus Albumin Fluid Evaluation (SAFE) study1 is elsewhere in the Journal's tribute to Rinaldo. The use of human albumin solutions in Australian Critical Care Medicine practice, particularly for the expansion of intravascular volume is commonplace. The unique funding in Australia of these solutions means clinicians and indeed hospital administrators consider them to be free. The reality, of course, is that they are not; another federal government agency pays the expense.

SAFE demonstrated that in intensive care patients, the use of 4% albumin (the preparation available in Australia and New Zealand at that time) or normal (0.9%) saline for fluid resuscitation results in similar outcomes. However, in participants with trauma, a differential effect was demonstrated. This difference in the relative risk of death was due to the greater number of patients with trauma and an associated brain injury: 24.5% of such patients in the albumin group died, as compared with 15.1% in the saline group (relative risk, 1.62; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.12 to 2.34; P = 0.009). Among patients who had trauma without brain injury, there was no difference between the groups in terms of mortality: 6.2% in the albumin group and 6.1% in the saline group died (relative risk, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.56 to 1.79; P = 1.00).1

SAFE was the Australian and New Zealand Intensive Care Society Clinical Trials Group's (ANZICS CTG) foundation pragmatic large randomised controlled trial (RCT). The case report form was consciously and deliberately brief (apart from data related to fluid administration). While participants with trauma were a prespecified subgroup, those with TBI were not. Furthermore, no additional information regarding the nature of a participant’s TBI was available. You can imagine Rinaldo's response: a P-value of 0.009? What is the chance of that being a type one error? We must investigate this further. Together with John Myburgh, Jamie Cooper, and Simon Finfer, Rinaldo steered the ANZICS CTG community to conduct a post hoc follow-up study, with emphasis on follow up: 24-month functional outcome. The SAFE study primary outcome was 28-day mortality as was the outcome for most critical care RCTs in the twentieth century and the early two-thousands. Now Rinaldo, John Jamie, and Simon proposed a two-year follow-up with functional assessment, prescient. And all sites would need to go back and perform detailed data extraction for every patient with TBI for love, not a lot of money. So, we did–with Rinaldo cheering us on.

New partnerships were formed with those conducting long-term follow-up of trauma patients, new funding sources were identified; the Saline or Albumin for Fluid Resuscitation in Patients with Traumatic Brain Injury (SAFE-TBI) study was conducted and published in the New England Journal of Medicine (NEJM) in 2007(2). A total of 515 participants with TBI were identified in the SAFE study dataset, patients were stratified into severe (Glasgow Coma Score [GCS] 3-8) or moderate (GCS 9–12) TBI. The original findings were confirmed at 24 months, 33.2% of those participants randomised to albumin had died compared with 20.4% of those randomised to 0.9% saline (relative risk, 1.63; 95% CI, 1.17 to 2.26; P = 0.003). Further, the association was greatest in those with severe TBI (GCS 3–8): 41.6% versus 22.2% (relative risk, 1.88; 95% CI, 1.31 to 2.70; P < 0.0012). And the excess deaths were all observed in the first 28 days, strengthening the association between albumin administration and mortality.

Previous research suggested that colloid solutions, by maintaining or augmenting plasma oncotic pressure, may minimise the extravasation of intravascular fluid into the brain interstitium. This does not appear to be the case. While the precise mechanism remains uncertain, it is likely that albumin (and other colloid solutions) worsens vasogenic and cytotoxic cerebral oedema. This may be related to damage to the blood brain–barrier is severe TBI. Nevertheless, the magnitude of the effect observed, biological plausibility, temporality, specificity to severe TBI, and consistency with other observational data led to the findings of SAFE-TBI changing practice. It is recommended that clinicians do not administer colloid solutions as resuscitation fluids in patients with TBI.3

Osmotherapy

Consequent to the strong association demonstrated between adverse outcome and albumin administration, the choice of agent for the management of intracranial hypertension, together with its effectiveness, intrigued Rinaldo; it was an area with limited high-level evidence ripe for exploration. In the last decade of his career, Rinaldo accepted an appointment at the Royal Melbourne Hospital, one of Melbourne's two major trauma centres. To understand the “random practice” that was no doubt occurring, he facilitated a large international multicentre study of severe TBI that focused on the epidemiology of osmotherapy: the timing, choice, and volume of agent.4 The investigators hypothesised that the choice of hypertonic saline (HTS) or mannitol might influence patient outcome.

Under Rinaldo's stewardship 14 tertiary intensive care units across 11 countries, all high-volume neurotrauma centres, agreed to participate. While a retrospective study, the findings were provocative–the adjusted hazard ratio for in-hospital mortality in patients receiving only mannitol for the treatment of raised intracranial pressure (ICP) was 2.64 (95% CI, 0.96–7.30; P = 0.06).4 Did the choice of osmotherapy impact survival? Perhaps, further investigation was required. It was suggested that the erythropoietin-TBI (EPO-TBI) investigators (more on EPO will follow) dive into their dataset and conduct a post hoc analysis of osmotherapy in the study population. This demonstrated an independent association between mannitol and acute kidney injury (hazard ratio 2.3; 95% CI, 1.2-0.3; P = 0.01). The use of mannitol was independently associated with time to death (hazard ratio, 2.1; 95% CI, 1.1–4.1; P = 0.03) as was HTS (hazard ratio, 1.8; 95% CI, 1.02–3.2; P = 0.04).5

The findings of these studies challenge existing beliefs and dogma. The uncertainty regarding osmotherapy remains, and further research to confirm or refute these findings and resolve the uncertainty is required.

Neuroprotective therapies

In the early 2000s, Rinaldo observed the emerging data that demonstrated an association between the administration of EPO and improved outcome in critically ill trauma patients,[6], [7], [8] with ongoing trials evaluating potential neuroprotective effects of EPO in patients with acute ischaemic stroke.9 He hypothesised that the administration of EPO to critically ill patients with TBI would improve functional outcomes. He presented this to colleagues at the ANZIC RC including myself, Alistair Nichol, and Jamie Cooper, and so began a multidecade journey with EPO that continues.

There is strong preclinical and clinical evidence to support EPO as tissue protective. It is known that EPO is synthesised and released locally by tissues in response to injury. It then binds to a specific receptor, that differs substantially differs from the receptors activated to increase red blood cell (RBC) production.10 Following the binding of EPO, apoptosis is inhibited; EPO prevents the death of injured cells and facilitates tissue recovery. The laboratory findings were supported by clinical evidence from research evaluating the role of EPO as an RBC-sparing agent in the critically ill. Two studies made the observation that EPO, while not impacting the need for RBC transfusion was associated with a marked reduction in mortality in trauma patients.6,7 The EPO in ICU patients with TBI trial (EPO-TBI) was developed.

EPO-TBI secured funding from the National Health and Medical Research Council of Australia and the Transport Accident Commission of Victoria. The trial needed to be international, it was not possible to conduct it in Australia and New Zealand alone. So just get 29 participating hospitals across seven countries? Not a problem. Rinaldo's influence was essential to achieve this. In Finland, two ex-ANZIC RC fellows, Ville Petilla and Markus Skrifvars were, “roped in”; in Ireland, Alistair Nicho had returned from the antipodes. In Paris, Jacques Duranteau finds multiple sites in France–voila. A trusted colleague in the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia, Yaseen Arabi, also joined. The team was assembled, and the journey began in May 2010 with the first patient recruited.

Over the next four years, 606 participants were recruited. The primary manuscript was published in the Lancet:11 while no statistical difference in the primary outcome (dichotomised Extended Glasgow Outcome Score at six months) was demonstrated, mortality was lower. As Rinaldo noted, this was now the third RCT to suggest that EPO reduces mortality in trauma patients. What is going on here?

Under Rinaldo's stewardship, a series of post hoc analyses of the EPO-TBI trial were conducted. These demonstrated that EPO administration to this population was safe with no increase in venous thrombosis or thromboembolism that the third dose of EPO was not required and most significantly, the effect was greatest in patients with coexisting multitrauma.[12], [13], [14], [15] A subsequent systematic review and meta-analysis reinforced the mortality benefit. The EPO-TRAUMA trial was born.16

Over the next three years, multiple grant applications to national and international bodies were made, each one was rejected. Regularly deflated, I remain forever grateful to the regular motivational chats I had with Rinaldo, who encouraged me to continue the journey: “We must do this study”, “EPO has the best biological rationale and preclinical data of anything we have studied”. Ultimately, in 2019, the study was funded by theAustralian Government Medical Research Future Fund Rare Diseases and Unmet Needs Program. A classic example of Rinaldo encouraging people to be innovative and creative. Like many non-Covid studies, EPO-TRAUMA was adversely impacted by the pandemic, and recruitment effectively did not start until 2022. The study has thrown the management committee numerous curve balls: pharmaceutical companies ending agreements to supply EPO, a two-year delay in adding additional sites due to difficulties transitioning to a new European clinical trials platform. Recruitment remains slow. Rinaldo's answer: “You need more sites”. Rinaldo made the introductions and new relationships were forged. Soon Belgium (Fabio Taccone) was on board, with additional funding obtained via the Belgian Health Care Knowledge Centre. Our German friends recently recruited their first participant ,and hopefully France, Spain, and Italy will follow in the near future. Rinaldo remained passionate about this study even during difficult periods: the EPO-TRAUMA team will do their utmost to steer this challenging clinical trial to completion.

Rinaldo influenced and inspired clinicians across generations, across cultures, and across the globe. While relatively modest compared to his research outputs in other areas of critical care medicine, his contribution to the conduct of clinical trials of critically ill patients is substantial and practice-changing. His enduring impact, in an area where clinical trials are challenging to conduct, is the facilitation of worldwide research collaborations to address questions of public health importance. Researchers on this field are forever grateful to Rinaldo for his guidance, mentorship, wisdom, humour, and friendship.
