Accelerometer-derived “weekend warrior” physical activity pattern and cardiovascular disease in individuals with hypertension: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Yanan Qiao, Hui Yang, Ruilang Lin, Yongfu Yu, Min Zhao, Bo Xi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExtensive evidence has demonstrated that physical activity (PA), particularly moderate-to-vigorous intensity PA (MVPA), has protective effects on cardiovascular disease (CVD) in individuals with hypertension. However, the relative effects of different PA patterns in this high-risk population remain unclear. We aimed to evaluate the associations of three PA patterns (“weekend warrior” (WW), regularly active, and inactive) with incident CVD in individuals with hypertension.MethodsThis large prospective cohort study included 40,283 adults with hypertension from the UK Biobank who had accelerometer-measured PA data between June 2013 and December 2015. Participants were categorized into three groups: the regularly active pattern, the WW pattern, and the inactive group. Outcomes included overall CVD, as well as its four major subtypes: myocardial infarction (MI), atrial fibrillation (AF), heart failure (HF), and stroke. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between PA patterns and outcomes.ResultsOver a median follow-up of 7.59 years (interquartile range, 7.33–8.47), 3789 new cases of overall CVD were identified. Compared with the inactive group, both the WW (HR = 0.76, 95% CI = 0.71–0.82) and regularly active (0.78, 0.71–0.85) patterns were associated with a significantly and similarly reduced risk of developing overall CVD. Moreover, both active groups showed reduced risks of incident MI (WW, 0.75, 0.65–0.87; regularly active, 0.72, 0.61–0.86), AF (WW, 0.77, 0.70–0.85; regularly active, 0.80, 0.71–0.90), HF (WW, 0.58, 0.50–0.68; regularly active, 0.67, 0.56–0.80), and stroke (WW, 0.78, 0.65–0.93; regularly active, 0.85, 0.70–1.05). Additional subgroup and sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of main findings.ConclusionsThe WW pattern was associated with a similarly lower risk of CVD as more evenly distributed activity in individuals with hypertension, offering an effective and flexible strategy for improving cardiovascular health.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04344-z.

Background

Hypertension is a modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular disease (CVD) and mortality, affecting over 1.3 billion adults globally [1]. Despite significant advancements in the prevention and treatment of hypertension, CVD-related complications remain the primary contributors to morbidity and mortality in individuals with hypertension [2, 3]. In 2021, an estimated 10.4 million CVD deaths were attributed to hypertension [4], leading to a substantial social and economic burden for patients, their families, and healthcare systems [1, 5]. Consequently, identifying feasible and practical interventions to alleviate the health burden in the population with hypertension should be an urgent public health priority.

Evidence from observational and experimental studies [6–9], aligning with contemporary physical activity (PA) and medical guidelines [10, 11], underscores the beneficial effects of PA, particularly the moderate-to-vigorous intensity PA (MVPA), in controlling blood pressure and reducing the risk of CVD and related mortality in adults with hypertension. A systematic review of 27 randomized controlled trials demonstrated that engaging in sufficient MVPA can reduce the systolic/diastolic blood pressure (SBP/DBP) by approximately 11/5 mmHg in patients with hypertension [12]. Recently, two cohort studies from the UK Biobank further revealed an inverse relationship between device-measured MVPA duration and CVD risk in individuals with hypertension [8, 9]. However, these studies above and current guidelines predominantly emphasize the total duration of MVPA, leaving knowledge gaps regarding the optimal frequency of MVPA in adults with hypertension [11]. Given that time constraints represent a major barrier to engage in PA, optimizing the balance between frequency and duration of PA is essential for maximizing health benefits.

Previous studies have shown that performing equivalent amounts of MVPA within 1–2 days/week (termed as the “weekend warrior” (WW) pattern) could yield similar health benefits to spreading it across several days throughout a week (termed as the “regularly active” pattern) [13–16]. A phenome-wide association study reported a strong association between the WW pattern and a lower risk of hypertension, with no significant effect differences observed between the WW and regularly active patterns [17]. Moreover, irrespective of using self-reported or device-derived PA data, the WW pattern has consistently been associated with a similar decreased risk of CVD as the regularly active pattern in the general population [14, 16, 18]. Although these studies generally adjusted for hypertension status as a covariate, such an analytical approach cannot directly determine whether the cardiovascular benefits of WW pattern could extend to individuals with hypertension, and whether there are effect differences between the WW and regularly active patterns in this high-risk population. To our knowledge, only one prior study has explored the associations between the different active patterns with all-cause mortality in individuals with hypertension as a subgroup analysis [18]. However, this study relied exclusively on self-reported PA data and included a narrow range of outcomes (only all-cause mortality), limiting its reliability and generalizability. Furthermore, other studies suggested that cardiovascular health status may influence the impacts of total PA volume [19, 20]. Therefore, the associations between different PA patterns and CVD risk specifically in individuals with hypertension remain uncertain. Addressing these uncertainties is essential for translating research evidence into public health and clinical interventions, especially given the challenges of incorporating consistent exercises into busy lifestyles.

To fill these knowledge gaps, we utilized the largest cohort to date with accelerometer-measured PA data to examine the associations between three distinct PA patterns (i.e., the WW, regularly active, and inactive patterns) and the risk of incident CVD outcomes in participants with hypertension.

Methods

Data source and study population

The UK Biobank study recruited about half of a million adults aged 37–73 years in 22 study centers across England, Scotland, and Wales between 2006 and 2010. At baseline, comprehensive information covering sociodemographic variables, lifestyle factors, anthropometric indices, and health status were collected through touchscreen electronic questionnaires, face-to-face verbal interviews, as well as physical and biological measurements. Further information on the study design and procedures has been published previously [21]. The UK Biobank study was approved by the North West Multi-Center Research Ethics Committee (R21/NW/0157). All participants provided written informed consent.

This study was restricted to a subsample of participations in the accelerometry sub-study (n = 103,661). Prevalent hypertension at baseline was identified based on self-reported history of hypertension, current use of antihypertensive medications, SBP/DBP ≥ 140/90 mmHg, or diagnostic records from primary care and hospital admission [22]. Participants with insufficient accelerometer wear time (data for fewer than 3 days or missing data for any 1-h period of the 24-h cycle, n = 6992), poorly calibrated accelerometer data (n = 4), implausibly high accelerometer values (average vector magnitude scores > 100 g, n = 13), and missing data on daily MVPA (n = 1194) were excluded. Additionally, we further excluded participants without hypertension at baseline (n = 47,256), who were diagnosed with CVD prior to the accelerometry measurements (n = 5328), and who had missing data on covariates (n = 841). Moreover, participants with less than a full week of acceleration data (n = 1750) were excluded to ensure accurate classification of PA patterns based on the actual distribution of daily MVPA. Ultimately, 40,283 adults with hypertension were included in the final data analyses. PA patterns

Between June 2013 and December 2015, participants with a valid email address were invited to wear an Axivity AX3 accelerometer (Newcastle upon Tyne, UK) on their dominant wrist for 7 consecutive days to objectively measure PA [23]. Accelerometers were configured to capture triaxial accelerometer data with a sampling frequency of 100 Hz and a dynamic range between ± 8 gravity units. After the devices were returned, all acceleration signals were calibrated to local gravity. Non-wear time was identified using standard procedures, defined as consecutive episodes of at least 60 min during which standard error of each axis was less than 13.0 g. Details on data collection and procedure can be found at the related website (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/refer.cgi?id=131600). Proportions of time spent in various movement behaviors (i.e., light intensity PA, MVPA, sedentary behavior, and sleep) were derived using a machine learning algorithm trained on an external independent cohort of 152 individuals in free-living conditions [24]. This algorithm demonstrated a good performance in a previous study, with a mean accuracy of 0.88 and a Cohen’s kappa of 0.80 [24].

Participants were divided into three groups as follows [16, 25]: the inactive group (MVPA < 150 min/week), the regularly active group (MVPA ≥ 150 min/week with ≥ 50% distributed over > 2 days/week), and the WW group (MVPA ≥ 150 min/week with ≥ 50% concentrated on 1–2 days/week). For example, if a participant accumulated 200 min of MVPA during the week, and 120 min (≥ 50%) occurred on Friday and Sunday combined, the participant was classified into the WW group. In contrast, if the total MVPA volume was more evenly distributed across the week (e.g., 30–40 min per day) and the combination of MVPA over any 2 days was less than 100 min, the participant was classified as having the regularly active pattern. As the optimal MVPA duration was unclear in individuals with hypertension, we also performed three sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of our findings using additional MVPA thresholds to define PA patterns [16], including ≥ 25th percentile (100.8 min/week), ≥ 50th percentile (216.0 min/week), and ≥ 75th percentile (388.8 min/week) of the total MVPA volume in our study population.

Outcomes

The primary outcome in this study was overall CVD, encompassing fatal and non-fatal myocardial infarction (MI), atrial fibrillation (AF), heart failure (HF), and stroke, which was ascertained through self-reported medical history and linkage to hospital inpatient records and death registrations. Hospital inpatient records were provided by the Hospital Episode Statistics for England, the Patient Episode Databases for Wales, or the Scottish Morbidity Record for Scotland. Death registration data were obtained from the UK National Health Service (NHS) Digital of England and Wales or the NHS Central Register and National Records of Scotland. Incident cases were identified based on the 10th vision of International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10). Detailed disease definitions and their corresponding ICD-10 codes are available in Additional file 1: Table S1. The follow-up period spanned from the date of accelerometry data collection to the first occurrence of a CVD event, death, or the end of follow-up (30 November 2022), whichever came first.

Covariates

Selected covariates in this study were based on prior knowledge and previous analyses of WW pattern using the UK Biobank data [16, 17, 26]. Covariate definitions are provided in Additional file 1: Table S2. Briefly, the following covariates were included: age at the end of accelerometer wear (years, calculated from the date of birth and the completeness of accelerometer wear), sex (women and men), ethnicity (white and non-white), Townsend deprivation index (TDI, a comprehensive surrogate of socioeconomic status) [27], education (college or university degree, A levels/AS levels or equivalent, O levels/GCSE or equivalent, CSE or equivalent, NVQ or HND or HNC or equivalent, and others), employment status (employed and unemployed), smoking status (never, ever, and current), frequency of alcohol drinking (never, ≤ 1–2 times/week, and ≥ 3 times/week), sleep duration (< 7, 7–8, and > 8 h/day), healthy diet score (0–7 points, comprising fruits, vegetables, fish, processed meats, unprocessed red meats, whole grains, and refined grains) [26], body mass index (BMI, kg/m2), SBP/DBP (mmHg), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c, mmol/mol), triglycerides (TG, mmol/L), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C, mmol/L), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C, mmol/L), and the use of antihypertensive, antihyperglycemic, and lipid-lowering medications (yes and no). Given the potential mediating roles of cardiometabolic factors (including BMI, SBP, DBP, HbA1c, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, and use of antihypertensive, antihyperglycemic, and lipid-lowering medications) in the association between PA patterns and CVD outcome, these covariates were not adjusted for in the primary analyses but were included in the sensitivity analyses.

Statistical analyses

Baseline population characteristics were presented according to different PA patterns. Continuous variables were expressed as mean (standard deviation, SD) and categorical variables were showed as number (%). Group differences were tested by analysis of variance (ANOVA) or chi-square tests, as appropriate.

Kaplan–Meier survival curves were applied to intuitively visualize the cumulative risks of incident CVD stratified by PA patterns, with log-rank tests performed to examine differences between three groups. Multivariable-adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression models were performed to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between PA patterns and the risk of incident CVD and its four subtypes. To account for different effects of confounding factors, three models were applied. Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, and ethnicity. Model 2 was additionally adjusted for socioeconomic status (including TDI, education, and employment status). Model 3 was further adjusted for four key lifestyle factors (including smoking status, alcohol drinking, healthy diet score, and sleep duration). The proportional hazard assumption for Cox regression models was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, with no evidence of violation observed (all P values > 0.05).

Stratified analyses were performed by age (< 65 and ≥ 65 years), sex (women and men), ethnicity (White and non-white), BMI (< 25 kg/m2 and ≥ 25 kg/m2), smoking status (never and ever/current), alcoholic drinking (< 1 time/week and ≥ 1 time/week), diet quality (healthy diet score < 4 and ≥ 4 points), and daily sleep duration (7–8 h/day and < 7/> 8 h/day) to examine whether associations varied across different subgroups of potential covariates. Effect modification by subgroup of included covariates was examined via the Z-test [28].

To assess the robustness of our primary results, we further conducted a series of sensitivity analyses. First, three additional thresholds of total MVPA, including the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentile of total MVPA volume in the study population, were used to re-define PA patterns. Second, the WW pattern was re-classified based on alternative definitions, including ≥ 75% of total MVPA concentrated on 1–2 days, ≥ 50% of total MVPA concentrated on 1–2 consecutive days, and ≥ 50% of total MVPA concentrated on 1–2 weekend days. Third, to minimize potential reverse causation, landmarks analyses were conducted after excluding participants who developed new CVD events within the first 2 years of follow-up period. Fourth, we additionally adjusted for several potential mediators including BMI, SBP, DBP, HAb1c, TG, LDL-C, HDL-C, and the use of antihypertensive, antihyperglycemic, and lipid-lowering medications. Fifth, to reduce the potential influence of baseline comorbidities, we performed a sensitivity analysis with additional adjustment for the history of diabetes, hyperlipidemia, chronic lung disease, gastrointestinal disorders, chronic kidney disease, and liver disease. Sixth, covariates with missing values were imputed using the multiple imputation method to examine the potential influence of missing data of covariates. Seventh, we additionally adjusted for wear time, the season of accelerometer wear, and total MVPA volume to reduce the potential impacts of measured variance and PA volume. Finally, participants without full-week accelerometer data were classified as physically inactive.

All statistical analyses were performed with R (software version 4.4.0, R Development Core Team, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

The study population comprised 40,283 participants with hypertension, with a mean age of 64.19 years (SD, 7.22). Of those, 20,529 (50.96%) were women, and 39,106 (97.08%) were Whites. Based on the guideline-based threshold of MVPA ≥ 150 min/week, 16,660 (41.36%) participants were classified into the WW pattern, 9031 (22.42%) participants into the regularly active pattern, and 14,592 (36.22%) participants into the inactive group. Baseline characteristics stratified by PA patterns are summarized in Table 1. Compared with the inactive group, individuals in the WW and regularly active groups were more likely to be younger, male, Whites, employed, less socioeconomically deprived, never smokers, and frequent alcohol drinkers, and university-educated. They also tended to sleep 7–8 h per day, have a higher healthy diet score, use fewer antihypertensive, antihyperglycemic, and lipid-lowering medications, and have higher HDL-C levels and lower levels of BMI, SBP, DBP, HbA1c, TG, and LDL-C (Table 1). Association between PA pattern and risk of CVD

During a median follow-up period of 7.59 years (interquartile range, 7.33–8.47), 3789 new cases of CVD were identified, including 986 cases with MI, 2181 with AF, 933 with HF, and 680 with stroke. As shown in Fig. 2, Kaplan–Meier survival curves indicated that participants in the inactive group consistently had the highest cumulative risks for overall CVD and its four subtypes, while the risks were comparably lower for those in the WW and regularly active groups (all P values for log-lank test < 0.001). After adjustment for all potential covariates, both the WW group (HR = 0.76, 95% CI = 0.71–0.82) and the regularly active group (HR = 0.78, 95% CI = 0.71–0.85) demonstrated a significantly reduced risk of developing overall CVD compared with the inactive group (Table 2). Similarly, the reduced risks of incident MI, AF, HF, and stroke were observed in both the WW and regularly active groups. Discussion

Leveraging a unique resource of 40,283 adults with hypertension and accelerometer-derived data, our study demonstrated that the WW pattern was associated with a 22–42% reduction in the risks of developing overall CVD and its four subtypes in individuals with hypertension. More importantly, the WW pattern conferred cardioprotective benefits similar to those of the regularly active pattern. These associations were consistent and robust across various thresholds for definitions. Our findings suggest that the WW pattern may offer a practical and accessible option for achieving adequate PA duration in individuals with hypertension, thereby significantly contributing to the CVD prevention.

Association of “WW” pattern with CVD in individuals with hypertension

Previous studies have established an inverse association between total MVPA duration and the risk of CVD-related complications in adults with hypertension, which is consistent with our findings [7–9]. However, these studies primarily focused on the health benefits of total MVPA duration, while overlooking the frequency of MVPA. In contrast, our present study extends prior research by stratifying individuals meeting the guideline-recommended MVPA duration (i.e., ≥ 150 min/week) into two distinct patterns based on frequency: the regularly active pattern (MVPA evenly distributed throughout the week) and the WW pattern (MVPA concentrated on 1–2 days per week). We found that achieving the guideline-recommended MVPA duration, regardless of frequency, significantly reduced the risk of incident CVD in individuals with hypertension. Importantly, the magnitude of risk reduction was comparable and similar between the two PA patterns. To date, several studies have investigated the associations of different PA patterns with future risks of cardiometabolic diseases in the general population [13–17]. Compared with inactive adults, those in the WW and regularly active groups had similarly decreased risks in CVD [16], metabolic dysfunctions [29], nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [30], and arterial stiffness [31]. However, these studies were conducted specifically in the general population, and no evidence currently exists regarding the protective effect of the WW pattern in the population with hypertension. To our knowledge, our study is the first to examine the protective effects of different PA patterns on cardiovascular health in individuals with hypertension. Our results confirm that the cardioprotective effect of the WW pattern in individuals with hypertension align with that observed in the general population, highlighting its potential as a feasible and effective strategy for improving cardiovascular health. Moreover, we further examined whether an interaction exists between hypertension status and PA patterns in the whole cohort. As shown in Additional file 1: Table S17, the WW pattern was generally associated with a reduced risk of all study outcomes across both hypertensive and normotensive subgroups. Although interactions between the PA patterns and hypertension status for most outcomes were not statistically significant, a significant interaction between the regularly active pattern and hypertension was observed for MI (P = 0.01), with a more pronounced risk reduction observed in normotensive individuals (Additional file 1: Table S17). Notably, we found that point estimates of risk reduction associated with the WW active pattern tended to be larger in individuals with hypertension than in the normotensive subgroup. These results provide further insight into the applicability and potential health benefits of the WW pattern in individuals with hypertension.

Given the lack of well-established optimal MVPA thresholds for individuals with hypertension and the challenges this high-risk population faced in adhering to recommended MVPA durations, we re-analyzed these associations using alternative thresholds to define two active groups to confirm our main findings. The results from sensitivity analyses indicated that two active groups (the WW and regularly active groups), which achieved the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentile of total MVPA in the study population, still showed a significantly decreased risk of developing CVD. This was further supported by the linear relationship of MVPA duration with CVD risk in the population with hypertension [8]. Taken together, these findings suggest that both PA patterns (the WW and regularly active patterns) have similar benefits in reducing CVD risk among individuals with hypertension. Moreover, brief episodes of MVPA have been increasingly reported to be associated with a substantially lower risk of adverse cardiovascular events [32, 33]. This activity pattern may offer a promising strategy to further enhance the health benefits of PA, as individuals can make full use of fragmented times in their busy lifestyles. However, it remains poorly understood how PA timing and different modalities (e.g., aerobic or muscle strengthen activity) influence the health benefits, warranting evaluation in the future randomized controlled studies.

Clinical and public health implications

Our findings have important implications for public health and clinical practice. First, our results revealed that the WW pattern was common in the population with hypertension (41.36%), which was similar to the proportion in the general population (42.28%) [17]. This indicates that time constraints are a major barrier to sustained PA engagement in modern lifestyles, even among patients with hypertension. Second, our study highlighted that achieving the guideline-recommended MVPA duration, irrespective of PA pattern, could serve as an effective lifestyle therapy to reduce subsequent risk of incident CVD in individuals with hypertension [11, 13]. The WW pattern may offer a more feasible and convenient option for patients with time limitations, thereby warranting targeted interventions to promote PA adoption. Furthermore, our findings may encourage healthcare providers to implement targeted interventions aimed at increasing PA levels among patients with hypertension for CVD prevention. This is because concentrating targeted interventions into 1–2 days per week may be more accessible and facilitate long-term adherence.

Strengths and limitations

The major strengths of this study include its large sample size, prospective cohort design, and objective quantification of the frequency and duration of MVPA, which minimize recall bias and ensure the ability to capture a wide range of movement behaviors. Nevertheless, several limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings. First, the 7-day accelerometer measurement may not accurately represent longer-term movement behaviors, as participants may have changed their PAs during the observation week. Additionally, PA was measured at a single time-point, which precludes the assessment of potential changes in activities over time during the follow-up period. Second, while the wrist-worn triaxial accelerometer device has been widely applied to measure objective PA, it has limitations in distinguishing between different activity types, such as leisure-time and occupational activities, potentially leading to misclassification [15, 16]. Moreover, wrist-worn accelerometers may overestimate PA levels compared to hip-worn devices, due to that they are more sensitive to non-ambulatory upper-limb movements [34]. Third, the predominantly white ethnicity of UK Biobank participants limits the generalizability of our findings to populations with other ethnicities. Fourth, it should be noted that accelerometer data were collected after the baseline assessment, in which covariates were evaluated. However, prior studies have demonstrated the stability of these covariates over time, thereby unlikely influencing the reliability of our results [35]. Finally, due to the nature of observational epidemiological design, residual or unmeasured confounding cannot be entirely ruled out, despite adjustments for a series of related covariates in this study.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that both the WW and regularly active patterns confer comparable benefits in reducing CVD risk in individuals with hypertension. Our findings emphasize the WW pattern as a flexible strategy for achieving sufficient MVPA in accordance with current guidelines, particularly for patients with hypertension. Future randomized control trials are encouraged to evaluate the efficacy of concentrated interventions in achieving guideline-recommended PA for improving cardiovascular health in those with hypertension.
