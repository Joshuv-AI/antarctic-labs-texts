Impact of cyst maturity on drug resistance assays in Acanthamoeba Genotype T4
Authors: Chun-Hsien Chen, Ya-Na Wu, Chih Ming Tsai, Shang-Rung Wu, Wei-Chen Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Therapeutic failure in Acanthamoeba Keratitis (AK) is largely driven by the high resistance of cysts to antimicrobial agents. However, the development of effective cysticidal drugs is hindered by the lack of standardized in vitro encystation protocols. Inconsistencies in induction methods can produce immature cells that lack the structural defenses of mature clinical walls, potentially leading to false-positive results in drug screening. This study aims to evaluate the impact of cyst maturity on drug resistance assays by systematically comparing two common liquid media—glucose-supplemented PBS (PBSEB) and alkaline encystation buffer (EB2)—within Acanthamoeba Genotype T4 backgrounds. We assessed morphological transitions, transcriptional profiles (EMSP, ATG8, and CS-I), ultrastructure (TEM), and functional resistance to sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) and chlorhexidine gluconate (CHG) using environmental reference and clinical isolates, including the stress-tolerant NCKU_D strain.Although both media induced morphological rounding and upregulated the autophagy marker ATG8, functional and molecular assays revealed distinct developmental outcomes. PBSEB-treated cells failed to significantly upregulate the critical early serine proteinase EMSP and exhibited insufficient CS-I expression, remaining highly susceptible to SDS lysis and lacking intact walls under TEM. Conversely, EB2 triggered a robust 11.8-fold increase in EMSP, yielding structurally mature, SDS-resistant cysts. Importantly, functional assays demonstrated that EB2-induced cysts exhibited significantly higher chemical resistance to CHG than the PBSEB group at 48 h, with this resistance advantage further widening by 72 h of induction. Based on these distinct profiles, we suggest that while PBSEB serves as a useful model for investigating cell detachment kinetics and early autophagic pathways, the EB2 protocol is more suitable for evaluating downstream therapeutic efficacy and product safety where functional, double-walled resistance is required to avoid false-positive outcomes in Genotype T4 drug screening.

Introduction

Acanthamoeba spp. are free-living amoebae ubiquitous in diverse natural environments, including soil, freshwater, seawater, and air (Visvesvara et al., 2007). These organisms typically feed on bacteria and other microorganisms. While typically free-living, they can cause severe human infections, most notably Acanthamoeba Keratitis, a sight-threatening condition predominantly affecting contact lens wearers (Lorenzo-Morales et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2021). Delayed treatment for AK can lead to severe visual impairment or blindness, often necessitating corneal grafting surgery.

Acanthamoeba exhibits a life cycle with two distinct stages: the active, feeding, and potentially pathogenic trophozoite and the dormant, resistant cyst (Siddiqui and Khan, 2012). The cyst is characterized by a double-walled structure, composed of an outer ectocyst and an inner endocyst containing cellulose and proteins. This structure confers remarkable resistance to extreme physical and chemical stresses, including disinfectants, antimicrobial agents, temperature variations, and desiccation (Chen et al., 2020, 2025). Furthermore, cyst formation presents a significant challenge in clinical treatment (Chen et al., 2023). The cyst's impermeability to most currently available contact lens solutions and anti-amoebic drugs is the primary driver of treatment failure and infection recurrence (Chen et al., 2020, 2025). Collectively, the cyst stage plays a pivotal role in Acanthamoeba survival and pathogenesis. Therefore, developing effective therapeutic strategies relies heavily on our ability to reproduce this specific stage in vitro. Optimizing encystation conditions is not merely a biological exercise; it is a critical prerequisite for producing the high-quality, structurally mature cysts necessary for reliable drug screening and resistance profiling (Johnston et al., 2009; Chen et al., 2023).

To induce encystation for research, the traditional method involves inoculating amoebae onto non-nutrient agar (NNA) to trigger starvation (Sohn et al., 2017). However, this method often requires considerable time, with encystation on NNA typically exceeding one week. Furthermore, quantitative and robust studies on the effects of compounds or environmental conditions are challenging to conduct using the NNA method (Sohn et al., 2017). To address these limitations, researchers have developed various liquid encystation media. These formulations typically mimic environmental stressors, such as nutrient starvation and/or osmotic shock, to trigger the encystation pathway (Aqeel et al., 2013; Kilvington and Lam, 2013). Common basal media include non-nutrient buffers such as phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) or Neff's saline (Aqeel et al., 2013; Kilvington and Lam, 2013). These media vary considerably in their composition, pH, and osmolarity, leading to potential differences in encystation efficiency and the characteristics of the resulting cysts. Two commonly utilized approaches involve either a dedicated Neff's saline-based encystation buffer or modified PBS (Aqeel et al., 2013; Sohn et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2024). For instance, a widely used encystation buffer typically contains a balanced salt solution with specific ion concentrations buffered at an alkaline pH (e.g., pH 9.0), aiming to induce encystation primarily through nutrient deprivation and pH stress (Sohn et al., 2017; Chuprom et al., 2025). Alternatively, PBS supplemented with high concentrations of an osmotic agent like glucose and additional magnesium chloride at a physiological pH is also used, suggesting that osmotic stress and specific divalent cations can effectively trigger encystation (Aqeel et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2024; Chuprom et al., 2025).

Nevertheless, different laboratories may employ distinct formulations and induction protocols, potentially leading to discrepancies in encystation efficiency (Johnston et al., 2009; Aqeel et al., 2013; Sohn et al., 2017; Chen et al., 2020; Chuprom et al., 2025). Crucially, this lack of a standardized encystation model represents a major bottleneck in the development of novel therapeutic agents against Acanthamoeba Keratitis (AK). Inconsistencies in cyst quality—specifically regarding cell wall maturity and drug impermeability—compromise the reliability of in vitro drug efficacy screenings, often yielding false-positive results for compounds that fail in clinical settings. Consequently, establishing an efficient and reproducible in vitro encystation system is crucial. A reproducible system would facilitate the comparison of results between studies, aid in dissecting the molecular events of encystation, and enable the screening for potential cysticidal or encystation-inhibiting compounds. While various encystation buffers have been developed in encystation-related assays, the optimal concentrations, required treatment times, and the resulting transformation efficacy for many of these identified buffers remain poorly characterized for Acanthamoeba, particularly regarding the impact of initial cell density on induction efficiency (Sohn et al., 2017; Joo et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2024; Chuprom et al., 2025).

Therefore, this study specifically aims to compare the encystation efficacy induced by two widely used buffers with distinct formulations: Encystation Buffer 2 (an amine-free, alkaline salt solution) and PBSEB (a glucose-supplemented, high-magnesium solution). (Sohn et al., 2017; Shih et al., 2024; Chuprom et al., 2025). This comparative analysis is expected to provide insights into developing more optimized and standardized platforms for Acanthamoeba encystation research.

Methods

Acanthamoeba culture

Acanthamoeba castellanii ATCC_30010 (environmental source), ATCC_50492 and ATCC_50370 (clinical source) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. Cultivation of A. castellanii utilized peptone-yeast-glucose extract (PYG) medium, which was adjusted to pH 6.5. The clinical isolate NCKU_D was isolated from the corneal ulcer of patients diagnosed with AK at the National Cheng Kung University Hospital, Tainan, Taiwan (Huang et al., 2017). The medium consisted of proteose peptone, yeast extract, glucose, sodium citrate dehydrate, MgSO4·7H2O, Na2HPO4·7H2O, KH2PO4, and Fe(NH4)2(SO4)2·6H2O. Routine maintenance and expansion of Acanthamoeba trophozoites were performed by axenic culture at 28°C in T-75 tissue culture flasks. Trophozoites were harvested during the logarithmic phase of growth.

Induction of encystation in Acanthamoeba

To induce cyst formation, Acanthamoeba ATCC_30010 trophozoites underwent two washing steps using Page's modified Neff's amoeba saline (PAS). PAS contains NaCl, MgSO4, CaCl2, Na2HPO4, and KH2PO4. Encystation was initiated by transferring the trophozoites into an appropriate encystment medium, followed by incubation at 28°C. Two distinct media served as encystment media in this study: Encystation buffer 2 (EB2)(composition: 95 mM NaCl, 5 mM KCl, 8 mM MgSO4, 0.4 mM CaCl2, 1 mM NaHCO3, 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 9.0) and PBSEB (PBS supplemented with 50 mM MgCl2 and 10% glucose, pH 7.0) (Sohn et al., 2017; Chuprom et al., 2025). Following the transfer, amoeba cells were cultured in 24-well plates. Each well received 1 mL of PBSEB or EB2 for 24, 48, or 72 h. Initial cell densities were set at 2 × 106, 1 × 106, or 5 × 105 cells/mL per well. After encystation medium treatment, cysts were stained with CFW stain. For the staining procedure, the CFW solution was added to each well at a final working concentration of 2.5% (v/v). An individual cell was categorized as CFW-positive if it exhibited a continuous, bright blue fluorescent peripheral ring defining an intact spherical boundary with an excitation at 400–500 nm under a microscopy. The morphology of cysts was observed and counted using a haemocytometer under a microscope at 24, 48, or 72 h after the cysts were stained. For SDS-resistance experiments, after 48 and 72 h of cyst induction, sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) was added to each well for 10 min to lyse remaining trophozoites. After 0.5% SDS treatment, cysts were stained with CFW stain. The morphology of cysts was observed and counted using a haemocytometer under a microscope after the cysts were stained. The percentage of SDS-resistant cysts was determined by calculating the proportion of cysts that survived the detergent treatment relative to the total number of cysts formed. The formula used was: (Number of CFW-positive cysts post-SDS treatment/Number of CFW-positive cysts pre-SDS treatment) × 100 = %

For drug resistance experiments, after 48 and 72 h of cyst induction, chlorhexidine gluconate (CHG) was added to each well for 30 min to lyse remaining trophozoites. After 0.02% CHG treatment, cysts were stained with CFW stain. The morphology of cysts was observed and counted using a haemocytometer under a microscope after the cysts were stained.

For the excystation experiments, after 72 h of cyst induction, the induction buffers were replaced with PYG medium. After 7 days of excystation, the morphology of the cysts and trophozoites was observed under a microscope.

RNA extraction and real-time PCR

Acanthamoeba ATCC_30010 incubated with PBSEB or EB2 at 28°C for 48 h were lysed by adding TRI Reagent®. Acanthamoeba ATCC_50370 and Acanthamoeba ATCC_50492 and Acanthamoeba NCKU_D clinical isolated strain incubated with EB2 at 28°C for 48 h were lysed by adding TRI Reagent®. The Direct-zol TM RNA Mini Prep Kit (Zymo Research) was used for RNA extraction. The total RNA was then stored at −80°C. The concentration and purity (A 260/A 280 ratio) of the total RNA were quantified using a NanoDrop TM 1000 instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). To quantify the gene expression of treated cells, reverse transcription (RT)-PCR was used by the RevertAid™ First Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To quantify the mRNA levels of Acanthamoeba strains, the Acanthamoeba actin-related protein 2 (ARP2) forward primer sequence was 5′- GCT GTC TTG ACC CTC TAC GC -3′, paired with a reverse primer sequence of 5′- AGC GAG AAG CCC TCG TAC AC -3′, autophagy-related protein 8 (ATG8) forward primer was 5′-AAG GAA GCA CAT GAA GCT GAG C -3′, paired with a reverse primer sequence of 5′- CCA TCC TCG TCC TTG TAC TTG G -3′, encystation-mediating serine proteinase (EMSP) forward primer was 5′- AA CTA CAC CCA GGA CAC CC -3′, paired with a reverse primer sequence of 5′- GGT CTA CAA AGC GGG AGA GG -3′,and CSI forward primer sequence was 5′- GGC GAA GAA CAC CTG GTT AC -3′, paired with a reverse primer sequence of 5′- TGC TCT ACA ACA CGG AGG TG -3′ (Ng et al., 2017; Huang et al., 2021). ARP2, a highly conserved component of the actin cytoskeleton, exhibits stable expression and was therefore used as a housekeeping gene for internal normalization (Huang et al., 2021).

Transmission electron microscopy

For microscopic examination, Acanthamoeba castellanii cysts were collected and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer overnight at 4°C. The fixed cysts were subsequently embedded in 2% agar and sectioned into 1–2 mm cubes. Post-fixation was performed using a solution of 1% osmium tetroxide and 1.5% potassium ferricyanide in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer for 120 min at 4°C. After brief washing with distilled water, the samples underwent dehydration through a graded ethanol series (70%, 80%, 90%, and 99%, 15 min each), followed by two rounds of 100% ethanol for 20 min each.

Dehydrated specimens were infiltrated stepwise with Spur resin diluted in ethanol at concentrations of 25%, 50%, and 75% for 1 h each, and finally with 100% overnight. The resin was then replaced with fresh 100% Spur resin and inverted for an additional hour. The fully infiltrated samples were embedded in the models with fresh resin and polymerized at 70°C for 48 h.

Ultrathin sections (70 nm) were obtained using an ultramicrotome (Ultracut S, Leica Reichart) equipped with a diamond knife and collected onto formvar-coated grids. Sections were subsequently post-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Imaging was performed using a JEOL JEM-1400 transmission electron microscope operated at 120 keV and equipped with CCD Camera System (Ultrascan, Gatan).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in triplicate, and data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A two-tailed Student's t-test was used to compare differences between two groups. One-way ANOVA was employed for multiple group comparisons involving a single factor. To evaluate the effects of varying concentrations and time points, a two-way ANOVA was performed. Tukey's or Sidak's multiple comparison test was used for post-hoc analysis.

Results

Distinct morphological profiles and encystation kinetics induced by PBSEB and EB2

Preliminary tests using PAS alone as a control showed significantly lower encystation efficiency compared to EB2 and PBSEB (data not shown); therefore, this study focused on comparing the efficacy of these two distinct representative buffers. Previous studies utilized various concentrations of amoeba cells to induce cyst formation. To evaluate cyst induction efficacy, we incubated Acanthamoeba in PBSEB and EB2 at high (2 × 106 cells/mL), medium (1 × 106 cells/mL), and low (0.5 × 106 cells/mL) densities for 24, 48, and 72 h. Following treatment with PBSEB, Acanthamoeba cells lost their typical amoeboid morphology and adherence to the well within 24 h of incubation (Fig. 1A). The cells maintained this rounded morphology for up to 72 h at all concentrations tested. In contrast, cells treated with EB2 remained adherent after 24 h. With prolonged EB2 incubation, the proportion of cells exhibiting amoeboid morphology progressively decreased (Fig. 1B). Acanthamoeba trophozoites cultured with EB2 showed rounding by 24 h, and pre-cyst-like and cyst-like amoebae were clearly observed by 48 h. After cultivation for 72 h, a large proportion of Acanthamoeba trophozoites were encysted at low amoeba cell density. White light microscopy is insufficient to reliably distinguish between rounded amoebae and cysts based on their morphology (Marciano-Cabral and Cabral, 2003). Based on previous studies, the cyst wall is composed of cellulose and other components, which can be stained with fluorescent dyes like Calcofluor white (CFW). CFW staining is a simple, rapid, and highly reliable method for diagnosing Acanthamoeba keratitis through cyst detection (Marciano-Cabral and Cabral, 2003). We therefore used CFW staining to quantify cyst formation under PBSEB and EB2 conditions.

The results showed that PBSEB induced 80% to 100% cyst formation after 72 h of cultivation. Statistical analysis revealed significant main effects for both time (F(2, 18) = 159.9, p < 0.0001) and cell concentration (F(2, 18) = 741.0, p < 0.0001), as well as a significant interaction (F(4, 18) = 21.59, p < 0.0001). Sidak's multiple comparisons test confirmed that while cyst formation plateaued after 48 h at lower cell densities (0.5 and 1 × 106 cells/mL), it continued to significantly increase until 72 h at the highest density (2 × 106 cells/mL, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 2A). Similarly, EB2 demonstrated a time-dependent increase in cyst formation (Fig. 2B). Two-way ANOVA indicated significant effects for time (F(2, 18) = 208.2, p < 0.0001), concentration (F(2, 18) = 23.50, p < 0.0001), and their interaction (F(4, 18) = 18.88, p < 0.0001). In contrast to PBSEB, post-hoc analysis for EB2 revealed significant increases at each time interval for the lower concentrations. However, the highest concentration showed no significant difference between 24 and 48 h, followed by a significant increase at 72 h. Despite this, EB2 could not maintain its cyst-inductive activity with increasing amoeba cell concentrations. CFW stained counting showed that at low cell densities, PBSEB and EB2 had similar cyst induction efficacy. Evaluation of functional cyst maturity and structural integrity through resistance assays

SDS resistance followed by hemocytometer quantification is a well-established method for evaluating the encystation process, where SDS-resistant entities are functionally defined as mature cysts (Dudley et al., 2005; Joo et al., 2020). In these studies, SDS-resistant cysts are considered mature cysts (Khan, 2006). Building on this common approach, we further investigated the efficacy of two induction buffers, PBSEB and EB2, in producing these SDS-resistant cysts. Our previous staining results indicated similar effectiveness between PBSEB and EB2 at low initial density. Consequently, we prioritized the 48-h time point for ultrastructural and resistance analysis. Therefore, we employed the SDS-treated assay to compare their effectiveness more deeply. We tested their ability to induce SDS-resistant cysts in Acanthamoeba cells, cultivated at a low initial density, after 48 and 72 h. Following SDS treatment, CFW staining revealed pronounced morphological differences between the induction buffers. In the PBSEB-treated group at 48 h, we observed heterogeneously sized fluorescent aggregates (Fig. 3A). In contrast, the EB2-induced group exhibited uniformly sized, bright spherical structures. Notably, the number of fluorescent entities in the PBSEB group decreased following SDS exposure, whereas the population of EB2-induced cysts remained stable. Quantitative analysis confirmed that EB2 induced a significantly higher yield of SDS-resistant cysts than PBSEB after both 48 (p < 0.0001) and 72 h (p < 0.0001) of induction (Fig. 3B). To further evaluate whether these structural differences translate into clinical relevance, we challenged the induced cysts with CHG, a first-line cationic biguanide used in the treatment of AK (Chen et al., 2023). In this study, we used CHG to examine whether the induced cysts could withstand clinical biocide treatment. A two-way ANOVA revealed a highly significant main effect of buffer type (F(1, 8) = 152.49, p < 0.0001), while the main effect of induction time did not reach statistical significance (F(1, 8) = 3.80, p = 0.087). Importantly, a significant interaction between buffer type and induction time was detected (F(1, 8) = 5.39, p = 0.0488). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons demonstrated that the EB2-induced group retained a significantly higher number of surviving cysts compared to the PBSEB group at both 48 h (p = 0.0005) and 72 h (p < 0.0001) of induction (Fig. 3C). To evaluate the transcriptional profiles under different induction conditions, we analyzed the RNA expression levels of EMSP, ATG8, and CS-I at 48 h (Huang et al., 2021; Shih et al., 2024). Initial assessment of EMSP expression demonstrated significant overall variance across the treatment groups (F(2, 6) = 105.7, p < 0.0001). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons indicated that while PBSEB treatment did not significantly alter EMSP levels relative to the non-treated control (p = 0.7589), EB2 induction triggered an 11.8-fold increase (p < 0.0001). Consequently, EMSP expression was significantly higher in the EB2 group than in the PBSEB group (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4A). Regarding ATG8 expression, significant group-level differences were also detected (F(2, 6) = 27.85, p = 0.0009). Both PBSEB and EB2 treatments significantly upregulated ATG8 relative to the control (p = 0.0037 and p = 0.0009, respectively). Finally, analysis of CS-I expression confirmed highly significant variations among conditions (F(2, 6) = 523.5, p < 0.0001). Post-hoc testing revealed that while both buffers significantly elevated CS-I transcription compared to the control (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4C). This gene expression data may support our previous findings, indicating that while both buffers initiate the encystation process, EB2 more effectively promotes the formation of mature, SDS-resistant cysts. Based on the distinct morphologies and differential SDS sensitivity observed, we further used Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) to compare the ultrastructure of cysts generated by EB2 and PBSEB. After 48 h of incubation, TEM observations revealed that EB2-induced cultures contained well-formed, mature cysts, identifiable by a distinct outer wall and an inner wall separated by a space (Fig. 5). In contrast, cells from the PBSEB-induced group only appeared to be in the preliminary stages of exocyst secretion at the same time point. Trophozoites were also present in both cultures. The differences became even more pronounced following SDS treatment. In the EB2-induced group, mature cysts remained fully intact, demonstrating their structural resistance to the detergent. The cysts from the PBSEB-induced group, however, exhibited irregular and wrinkled surfaces post-treatment. Efficacy of EB2 in inducing SDS-resistant cysts across pathogenic and clinical isolates

The previous assays indicate that EB2 may be a stable choice to induce the double cell wall cysts, which could be fluorescent and resistant to SDS treatment. Our investigation has so far utilized Acanthamoeba ATCC_30010. Acanthamoeba ATCC_30010, a soil isolate, is widely recognized as an environmental amoeba in various research studies (Bernard et al., 2022; Kanakapura Sundararaj et al., 2024). To broaden the applicability of this induction medium, we next evaluated the effect of EB2 on cyst formation in pathogenic strains and clinical isolates, maintaining an identical amoeba cell concentration. Building on our previous findings, we further assessed the efficacy of EB2 against other ATCC clinical isolated strains and clinical isolates. Our results revealed that EB2 successfully induced cyst structure in ATCC_50492, ATCC_50370, and NCKU_D within 24 h, transforming most trophozoites into cysts after 72 h (Fig. 6A). Quantification showed that a portion of these strains successfully encysted after 48 h of induction: ATCC_50492 (53%), ATCC_50370 (50%), and NCKU_D (38%) (Fig. 6B). To further assess the resistance of these EB2-induced cysts, we applied SDS treatment to evaluate the percentage of SDS-resistant cysts in the ATCC strains and the clinical isolate. The results demonstrated that EB2 could induce 80% to 100% SDS-resistant cysts after 48 h of induction (Fig. 6C). Statistical analysis revealed a significant difference in cyst resistance among the strains (F(2, 6) = 7.326, p = 0.0245). Tukey's multiple comparisons test indicated that the percentage of SDS-resistant cysts in ATCC 50370 was significantly higher than that in ATCC 50492 (p = 0.0204). The clinical isolate NCKU_D showed intermediate resistance, with no significant difference observed compared to the other strains. Collectively, these findings strongly indicate the effectiveness of EB2 in promoting both general cyst formation and the production of SDS-resistant cysts across various ATCC clinical isolated strains and a clinically relevant isolate.

Discussion

The ability of Acanthamoeba to form dormant, double-walled cysts presents a clinical challenge, often associated with drug resistance and treatment failure (Huang et al., 2021). While various laboratory media are used to induce encystation for research, the characteristics and maturity of the resulting cysts are not consistently defined. A common method used to induce encystation is to inoculate the organism onto non-nutrient agar (NNA) plates, eliminating the food source to provide a stimulus. However, this method is time-consuming and difficult to standardize for quantitative assays (Sohn et al., 2017). Consequently, various liquid inducers have been reported for in vitro use (Aqeel et al., 2013; Kilvington and Lam, 2013). Previous studies have compared the liquid buffer with NNA. Two commonly utilized approaches involve either a dedicated Neff's saline-based encystation buffer or modified PBS (Aqeel et al., 2013; Sohn et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2024). This study aims to compare the morphological ultrastructural characteristics characteristics of cysts induced by these two representative buffers, specifically to evaluate their suitability for drug resistance testing.

Our results for EB2 are consistent with previous studies, showing that it successfully induces double-walled, SDS-resistant cysts. However, we observed that the percentage of mature cysts decreased as the initial cell concentration increased. We suggest that this density-dependent reduction is likely due to the rapid accumulation of metabolic byproducts, which may neutralize the specific alkaline stress signal (pH 9.0) essential for EB2-mediated induction. Previous studies have utilized PBSEB as an alternative buffer to induce cyst formation, reporting that it upregulates cyst-associated gene expression and leads to cell detachment (Shih et al., 2024). Interestingly, our results (at low cell density) are consistent with this. We also found that PBSEB failed to induce mature, double-walled cysts; although the resulting structures were fluorescently stained, they were not true cysts, as most were destroyed by SDS treatment. The reliance on such immature forms in drug discovery may lead to an overestimation of therapeutic efficacy, as candidate agents could penetrate these permeable pre-cysts more readily than the impermeable double-walled cysts found in clinical settings. Our transcriptional analysis provides a critical molecular basis for the observed failure of PBSEB to induce mature encystation. While PBSEB stimulated the expression of ATG8—a marker associated with autophagic cellular remodeling—it failed to significantly upregulate EMSP, a pivotal early signaling protease required to initiate the encystation cascade (Moon et al., 2008, 2013). In contrast, EB2 triggered a 11.8-fold increase in EMSP expression, suggesting that the alkaline stress in EB2 effectively activates the necessary proteolytic signaling that remains dormant or uninitiated under the osmotic stress of PBSEB. Furthermore, although PBSEB induced CS-I expression, the transcript levels were significantly lower than those in the EB2 group. This deficiency in cellulose synthase activity likely results in a cell wall that is either too thin or structurally disorganized to withstand chemical and mechanical challenges.

We suggest that this failure is due to the distinct stress mechanisms. PBSEB's high glucose content likely induces rapid osmotic stress, forcing cells into a pre-cyst-like stage rather than completing structural encystation (Cordingley et al., 1996). This explains why our cells rapidly detached but remained as immature, SDS-sensitive fluorescent spots. While one study reported about 40% induction of mature cysts using PBSEB, this was achieved at significantly higher cell concentrations. (Aqeel et al., 2013). We suggest that cell density is the critical variable: at high densities, cells may collectively share or buffer the acute osmotic stress, allowing a fraction to mature. However, at the low densities required for precise drug screening, this stress appears too overwhelming for individual cells to overcome, resulting only in abortive, pre-cyst formation.

To systematically evaluate the impact of these media on cyst quality, we utilized CFW staining. While white light microscopy alone is insufficient to differentiate between rounded amoebae and true cysts, CFW is widely used to detect polysaccharide polymers (e.g., cellulose) in the cyst wall (Wilhelmus et al., 1986; Dudley et al., 2005). Interestingly, we observed morphological differences in the fluorescent spots between the PBSEB and EB2 groups. To further interrogate the maturity of these structures, we applied SDS treatment, a method used to distinguish resistant cysts from sensitive trophozoites (Aqeel et al., 2013; Anwar et al., 2019; Joo et al., 2020). This appears to be the first study to evaluate the percentage of SDS-resistant cysts within the total CFW-stained population. This structural deficiency is further corroborated by functional dynamics over time. Specifically, the significant interaction between buffer type and induction time (p = 0.0488) in the chlorhexidine gluconate (CHG) resistance assay highlights the critical functional differences between the two media. Functional assays demonstrated that EB2-induced cysts exhibited significantly higher chemical resistance to CHG than the PBSEB group at 48 h. Driven by the significant interaction effect, the EB2 group sustained a progressive, time-dependent accumulation of biocide resistance by 72 h, whereas the resistance levels in the PBSEB group remained stagnant and limited. This temporal divergence suggests that EB2-induced cysts undergo a progressive maturation process that continuously solidifies the chemical defenses of the cyst wall, a kinetic profile that is absent in the aborted pre-cysts induced by PBSEB.

To understand the ultrastructural basis for this difference, we performed TEM analysis. TEM revealed that EB2-induced cultures contained well-formed, mature cysts with distinct outer and inner walls. In contrast, cells from the PBSEB-induced group appeared to be only in the preliminary stages of exocyst secretion. Since processed cyst wall precursors also contain polysaccharides stainable by CFW, we suggest that the CFW-stained population in PBSEB cultures is heterogeneous, likely dominated by immature pre-cysts (Anwar et al., 2018; Magistrado-Coxen et al., 2019). Collectively, we propose that future studies requiring functionally mature, double-walled cysts should adopt SDS treatment combined with CFW staining as a standardized method for cyst quantification. This rigorous protocol is especially pertinent for the evaluation of multipurpose contact lens solutions. Current discrepancies in the reported cysticidal efficacy of commercial solutions may stem from the lack of a standardized baseline, where fragile 'pseudo-cysts' are inadvertently utilized in testing models. By applying the EB2 induction coupled with the SDS-resistance criterion, researchers can establish a more standardized and reproducible framework for commercial product safety testing.

Finally, the applicability of the EB2 protocol was evaluated across different Acanthamoeba strains to ensure clinical relevance, which is paramount for therapeutic testing (Dudley et al., 2005; Chen et al., 2020). Our results showed that EB2 successfully induced SDS-resistant cysts not only in the environmental isolate (ATCC 30010) but also in clinical isolates (ATCC 50370, ATCC 50492, and NCKU_D). Among these, ATCC 30010, ATCC 50370, and ATCC 50492 exhibited comparable encystation percentages, whereas the NCKU_D isolate showed a lower percentage. The NCKU_D isolate was obtained from a patient with severe Acanthamoeba keratitis (AK) who failed PHMB therapy and ultimately required a corneal transplant. Previous data indicated that NCKU_D can tolerate PHMB treatment and maintain its trophozoite form, unlike laboratory-adapted strains such as ATCC 30010 (Huang et al., 2017). This indicates that NCKU_D possesses a higher intrinsic tolerance to stress conditions. Because clinical isolates often exhibit variable or attenuated responses to induction cues compared to laboratory-adapted strains, the induction of functionally mature, SDS-resistant cysts in this stress-tolerant clinical strain by EB2 is informative. The capacity of EB2 to generate mature cysts across these diverse backgrounds demonstrates its consistency and suggests its suitability for testing therapeutics against challenging, treatment-refractory infections encountered in clinical practice.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the encystation buffer composition fundamentally dictates the physiological and structural properties of Acanthamoeba cysts. Therefore, the choice of induction protocol should align with the specific research goal. Our findings suggest a functional differentiation between the two media: PBSEB serves as an effective tool for initiating early transcriptional responses, cell detachment kinetics, and autophagic signaling pathways, especially in higher-density cultures. In contrast, for downstream applications requiring functionally mature, double-walled resistance such as therapeutic efficacy screenings or product safety evaluations the EB2 protocol provides a more suitable and phenotypically relevant targeted baseline to minimize false-positive outcomes. We also recommend incorporating the functional SDS-resistance assay alongside molecular markers to validate cyst quality. Ensuring that induced cysts possess both transcriptomic signatures and functional cell wall integrity will significantly improve the translational reliability of future therapeutic studies against Genotype T4 infections.
