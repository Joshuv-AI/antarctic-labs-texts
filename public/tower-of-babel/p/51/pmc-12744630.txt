Impact of extended eligibility criteria on survival after veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation for refractory cardiac arrest: a 8-year single-center study
Authors: Bjarke Risgaard, Sebastian Wiberg, Jesper Kjærgaard, André Martin Korshin, Lene Holmvang, Jacob Eifer Møller, Peter Hasse Møller-Sørensen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimVeno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) may improve survival in refractory in- or out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA/OHCA), yet optimal selection criteria remain uncertain. We aimed to report contemporary survival rates after VA-ECMO for cardiac arrest (E-CPR) and to explore prognostic significance of extended criteria in improving patient selection.MethodsWe conducted an observational single-center study of patients treated with E-CPR from 2017 to 2024. Patients were stratified according to four extended inclusion criteria in favor of E-CPR (arterial pH > 6.8, lactate < 15 mmol/L, low-flow time < 100 min, signs of life during CPR). Primary outcome was 180-day survival.ResultsOf 159 patients included, 59 (37 %) were alive at 180 days, and 82 % of survivors had favorable neurological outcomes. Transient ROSC (OR 0.25, 95 % CI 0.13–0.49) and signs of life during CPR (OR 0.37, 95 % CI 0.19–0.75) were associated with lower mortality in univariate analysis. In multivariate analysis, only age (OR 1.03, 95 % CI 1.00–1.06) and transient ROSC (OR 0.25, 95 % CI 0.11–0.56) remained independently associated with 180-day mortality. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed significant differences between groups (log-rank p < 0.05), with survival ranging from 21 % in patients meeting none or one criterion in favor of E-CPR to 56 % in those meeting all four.ConclusionIn this single-center cohort, we observed a 180-day survival rate of 38 % among patients treated with E-CPR for refractory cardiac arrest. Notably, our study suggests acceptable survival rates of 21 % even in patients with an adverse risk profile.Keywords: Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (E-CPR), Veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO), Refractory cardiac arrest, Patient selection criteria, Survival outcome

Introduction

In- and out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA/OHCA) remains among the most critical medical emergencies, with survival rates remaining low despite advancements in resuscitation techniques.1 Survival to hospital discharge in general OHCA populations is achieved in only a minority of cases, and even fewer patients survive with favorable neurological outcomes at longer-term follow-up.2, 3 When conventional resuscitation efforts fail, Veno-Arterial Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation (VA-ECMO) has emerged as a potential life-saving intervention, offering circulatory support to patients with refractory cardiac arrest (E-CPR).4 Data supporting usage of E-CPR come from observational studies5, 6, 7 and three recent randomized clinical trials (RCTs) with conflicting result.8, 9, 10 Although some studies have shown that E-CPR can restore circulatory stability11, 12, 13, 14, 15; the prognosis remains poor for a significant proportion of patients.4 Despite these challenges, the implementation of E-CPR in carefully selected patients holds promise, particularly when initiated promptly and within specific clinical contexts.9 Significant variability in patient outcomes has led to ongoing debates about the optimal patient selection criteria, timing of ECMO initiation, and the overall benefit of E-CPR compared to standard resuscitative efforts.16 As for now, robust data to identify those who may benefit from E-CPR are lacking.17.

In Denmark, a national consensus on the use of E-CPR in patients with refractory OHCA was implemented in February 2018.18 A subsequent retrospective multicenter study identified regional differences in application and in outcomes.19 At our institution, E-CPR candidacy is based on national and European recommendations, considering factors such as witnessed arrest, no-flow time <10 min, initial shockable rhythm, age < 65 years, and limited comorbidities. However, additional extended selection criteria—arterial pH > 6.8, lactate < 15 mmol/L, low-flow duration < 100 min, and signs of life during CPR—have been suggested to further refine patient selection, though their clinical impact remains uncertain.

In this study, we aimed to report 180-day survival rates among patients treated with E-CPR for refractory IHCA/OHCA at our institution and to explore the prognostic significance of extended criteria in improving patient selection.

Material and methods

Study design and setting

We included consecutive patients from the prospectively recorded ECMO database of our 24-bed cardiothoracic intensive care unit (ICU) at Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen, Denmark. The study was approved by the institutional board of health [25033352]. Patient data were anonymized and handled in accordance with applicable data protection regulations. Rigshospitalet is a tertiary care university hospital, in the eastern part of Denmark with an established E-CPR program covering roughly 1.89 million residents.20 In case of refractory cardiac arrest the attending cardiologist are point of first contact that will activate the E-CPR team for possible cannulation and subsequent primary percutaneous intervention. Patients are treated and transferred by emergency medical services to the cardiac catheterization laboratory where cannulation is performed as described in detail previously.21, 22 In this study, we enrolled consecutive patients treated with E-CPR for refractory IHCA/OHCA unresponsive to advanced cardiac life support between January 1st 2017 to December 31st 2024 excluding cases <15 years of age.

E-CPR indications

Upon arrival to the catheterization lab, the decision to cannulate was made jointly by the on call cardiothoracic intensivist, the invasive cardiologist, and the cardiac surgeons. Criteria in favor of E-CPR include: witnessed cardiac arrest, no-flow <10 min, initial shockable rhythm, age < 65 years and limited comorbidity. In- and exclusion criteria’s follow our institution's protocol (available in Appendix) which are in line with Danish and European recommendations previously published.17, 18

Risk factor stratification

Additional criteria in favor of E-CPR have been described and implemented with regional variance in Denmark and include (1) arterial pH > 6.8, (2) lactate < 15 mmol/L, (3) low-flow duration < 100 min, and (4) signs of life during CPR.19 For the purpose of this study, signs of life were defined as eye opening, purposeful movement of any extremity, or spontaneous motor response suggestive of preserved neurological activity. This assessment was performed at the discretion of the treating physician during CPR and documented upon arrival at the catheterization laboratory. Patients were a priori classified into four groups based on fulfillment of the additional pre-defined ECMO criteria. Patients meeting all four criteria were assigned to Group 4, those meeting three criteria to Group 3, those meeting two criteria to Group 2, those meeting one or none of the criteria to Group 1.

Patient management

The management of patients on VA-ECMO support has been described in the literature and largely follows recommendations from the Extracorporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO).23, 24 According to our protocol, patients were cannulated percutaneously via the femoral vessels, with routine placement of a distal arterial perfusion cannula prior to leaving the catheterization laboratory to ensure adequate limb perfusion. Pump speeds were adjusted to maintain a blood flow of >50 ml/kg/min. To facilitate left ventricular unloading and prevent further deterioration of left ventricular function, an arterial pulse amplitude of >10 mmHg was maintained using epinephrine and/or milrinone. In case of progressive LV dilatation and no aortic valve opening, patients were evaluated for LV unloading with microaxial flow pump. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was adjusted to maintain a pulse pressure amplitude >10 mmHg, and ECMO flow was titrated to achieve a central venous oxygen saturation (ScvO2) > 60 %.

Sedation was tapered as soon as the intensivist assessed the patient to be circulatory stabilized. Anticoagulation with heparin infusion was initiated 12–24 h post-cannulation, aiming for an activated clotting time (ACT) of 160–180. Heparin infusions were temporarily halted during bleeding episodes and resumed once bleeding was controlled. Perfusionists conducted daily assessments of the membrane oxygenator and VA-ECMO circuit, replacing components as necessary in cases of malfunction.

Weaning from VA-ECMO was evaluated daily according to established protocols.20 Patients who could not be weaned due to persistent cardiac failure underwent comprehensive neurological assessments, including physical exams and EEGs. Those with a favorable neurological prognosis were considered for bridging to long-term ventricular support or cardiac transplantation. In cases where poor neurological outcomes were anticipated, VA-ECMO was gradually withdrawn, and life-sustaining treatments were discontinued.

Data collection and outcome measures

Data were extracted from the electronic health records system (Epic Systems Corporation, Verona, WI, USA) and included baseline demographic information, clinical characteristics, details of the IHCA/OHCA event (e.g., initial rhythm, time to ROSC), and VA-ECMO-related parameters (e.g., duration of VA-ECMO, complications, etc.). The primary outcome was survival at 180 days following event and were obtained through follow-up. Secondary outcomes included in-hospital complications (e.g., bleeding, infection, surgery, neurological impairment), duration of hospital stay, causes of death and neurological outcomes at discharge as described by the Modified Rankin Scale (MRS). Study data were managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at Rigshospitalet.25, 26

Statistical Analysis: Continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges [IQR, P25–P75] and categorical data as number and percentages. Comparisons between survivors and non-survivors at 180 days were made using the Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the Chi-square or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Logistic regression was performed to assess the association of risk factors on 180-day morality. Results are expressed as odds ratio (OR) and 95 % confidence interval (CI). Survival analysis was performed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves, and differences between groups were assessed using the Log-rank test. The time-to-event was defined as the number of days from E-CPR to death or the end of the follow-up period (180 days). Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p-value of <0.05. All analyses were performed using SAS Enterprise Guide Version 8.

Results

Between January 1st 2017 and December 31st 2024, a total of 159 patients with refractory IHCA (41 %) or OHCA (59 %) were treated with E-CPR. Overall, 87 (55 %) patients were successfully weaned from VA-ECMO with 66 (42 %) surviving to ICU discharge. Sixty-two patients (39 %) survived to hospital discharge whereas 59 (37 %) survived 180 days (Fig. 1).

Baseline and in-hospital characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the clinical characteristics of the study population, stratified by survival status at 180 days. No significant differences were observed in the distribution of age, sex and body mass index between survivors and non-survivors. However, hypertension was more prevalent among non-survivors (24 % vs. 6 %, p < 0.05). Other baseline characteristics did not differ significantly between the groups (Table 1). Table 2 present the clinical course, in-hospital interventions, and causes of cardiac arrest. As shown in Table 2, no significant difference between survivors and non-survivors regarding the proportion of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, witnessed arrest, primary rhythm, or the frequency of bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR). However, survivors were more likely to exhibit signs of life during CPR (46 % vs. 24 %, p < 0.01), and to experience intermittent return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) (64 % vs. 31 %, p < 0.01) compared with non-survivors. The median low-flow time was 60 min (IQR 40–80 min) not statistically significant between survivors and non-survivors (median 53 min [ IQR; 39–76] vs. 64 min [IQR; 43–80], p = 0.46, but s-lactate levels at 6 h and 24 h were significantly lower in survivors compared to non-survivors (6 h: 4.4 vs. 7.5 mmol/L, p < 0.01; 24 h: 2.0 vs. 3.4 mmol/L, p < 0.01). Coronary angiography and percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) were performed at similar rates in survivors and non-survivors. The most common cause of cardiac arrest was coronary artery disease (65 %), with no statistically significant difference between the groups (Table 2).

Table 3 summarizes the length of stay, outcomes, and complications among the study population stratified by survival status at 180 days. Survivors had a significantly longer median ICU stay (12 days vs. 1 day, p < 0.01) and hospital stay (31 days vs. 2 days, p < 0.01) compared to non-survivors. Favorable neurological outcomes, defined as a modified Rankin Scale score of less than 4, were observed in 81 % of survivors. Complications were frequent in both groups, with 84 % of patients experiencing at least one defined complication. The most common was continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) which was required in 46 % of patients, with no significant difference between survivors and non-survivors. Only one patient required hemodialysis at end of follow-up. Although there was a trend toward higher rates of ischemic extremities, ischemic gut and device failure in non-survivors, these differences were not statistically significant. Surgery related complications were lower and found in 35 % of patients also with no differences between survivors and non-survivor (Table 3).

Predictors of 180-day mortality and survival outcomes

Table 4 shows the results from the logistic regression. A significantly lower 180-day mortality was observed in patients exhibiting signs-of-life during CPR (OR 0.37, 95 % CI 0.19–0.75) and those achieving transient ROSC (OR 0.25, 95 % CI 0.13–0.49). In the multivariate analysis, adjusted for age, sex, shock rhythm, signs-of-life, Transient ROSC, low-flow time, pH, and Lactate only age (OR 1.03, 95 % CI 1.00–1.06) and transient ROSC (OR 0.25, 95 % CI 0.11–0.55) remained independently associated with 180-day mortality. Kaplan-Meier curves and the log rank test yielded similar findings (supplemental material, Figs. S1–S4) and the final model demonstrated good discrimination, with a c-statistic of 0.735. Although signs-of-life and transient ROSC may intuitively appear related, we found no significant interaction between the two variables in a logistic regression model (p = 0.39), supporting their inclusion as independent covariates.

Discussion

Building on national E-CPR consensus criteria implemented in Denmark, this observational single-center study explored the prognostic value of four additional extended selection parameters: (1) arterial pH > 6.8, (2) lactate < 15 mmol/L, (3) low-flow duration < 100 min, and (4) signs of life during CPR. By stratifying patients according to these criteria, we aimed to report 180-day survival rates and to refine patient selection for VA-ECMO in refractory cardiac arrest.

We report a 180-day survival rate of 37 % among patients treated with E-CPR, with 83 % of survivors achieving favorable neurological outcomes. Our findings demonstrate that fulfillment of more extended criteria correlated with improved survival. Importantly, even among patients in the lowest eligibility group meeting none or only one of the predefined criteria – 180-day survival was still 21 %. While this was the poorest survival observed across groups, it still represents a clinically meaningful outcome compared to conventional cardiac arrest studies,27 especially considering the prolonged low-flow times in this cohort. For reference, refractory OHCA patients treated conservatively at our institution have previously been shown to have survival rates of 20 %, but with virtually no survivors following resuscitation attempts beyond 60 min.21

Previous observational studies comparing E-CPR to historical controls and case matched controls have reported survival ranges from 15 % to 50 % depending on patient selection, pre-ECMO conditions and the experience of the ECMO center.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 28, 29, 30, 31 Overall, these data support the notion that E-CPR can offer not only improved survival but also meaningful recovery in well selected patients, as recently demonstrated in a systematic review and meta-analysis4 The broader implications of these findings are particularly relevant in light of the conflicting results from recent randomized trials on E-CPR.32 The ARREST trial demonstrated a survival benefit with E-CPR under controlled conditions;9 while the Prague OHCA8 and INCEPTION10 trials failed to reproduce this effect in more pragmatic, multicenter settings. The INCEPTION trial further highlighted the challenges of implementing ECMO in a real-world setting, emphasizing that survival depends not only on patient selection but also on systematic factors such as pre-hospital logistics, transport times, and post-cannulation management.

Among the evaluated parameters, signs of life during CPR and transient ROSC appeared most predictive of favorable outcomes. However, in the multivariate logistic regression, only transient ROSC and age remained independently associated with 180-day mortality whereas low-flow time and metabolic parameters such as pH and lactate on arrival in the catheterization lab were not predictive of E-CPR success. To further evaluate the discriminatory value of these variables, we performed ROC curve analyses. While pH showed modest prognostic ability (AUC 0.61) lactate and low-flow time had poor performance (AUC 0.54 and 0.46, respectively). The findings challenge the assumption that rigid adherence to specific cut-offs should be used to exclude patients from E-CPR candidacy. The results are, however, in contrast with previous studies that have found that lactate, pH, and low-flow times are associated with mortality, although few have defined clear thresholds that reliably predict mortality.33, 34, 35, 36

Our results support a more nuanced approach, viewing such criteria as risk indicators rather than strict exclusion parameters. A flexible, physiology-driven model may help refining selection without excluding potential for meaningful recovery. Lactate and pH dynamics following cannulation may for instance be more prognostically relevant than a single baseline measurement, further supporting a dynamic assessment model. The timing of death in most non-survivors, often occurring within the first few days, suggests that treatment was frequently withdrawn rather than continued unsuccessfully. These patients likely exceeded a physiological threshold beyond which recovery is no longer achievable. Identifying such patients may be equally important to ensure appropriate patient selection and avoid disproportionate use of healthcare resources.

Limitations and future directions

This study is limited by its single-center design and relatively small sample size, particularly in the lowest eligibility group. While our findings are hypothesis-generating, they require prospective validation in larger cohorts. Future studies should focus on combining real-time physiologic markers with evolving metabolic trends to develop more responsive and inclusive selection frameworks.

Conclusion

In this single-center cohort, 180-day survival after E-CPR for refractory cardiac arrest was 37 %, with most survivors achieving favorable neurological outcomes. Stratification based on extended eligibility criteria revealed higher survival among patients meeting more criteria, but acceptable outcomes even among those with adverse profiles. These findings support a flexible, physiology-driven selection strategy rather than rigid exclusion based on metabolic cut-offs.
