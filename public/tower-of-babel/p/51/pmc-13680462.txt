De novo transcriptome assembly of a pigmentation-variable population of cave snail Physella sp
Authors: Magdalena Grgić, Robert G Weck, Gaj Keresteš, Lara Kalogjera, Helena Bilandžija
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSubterranean ecosystems are among the most widespread yet least explored environments on Earth. Species that are not obligate cave dwellers are especially understudied, due to the predominant research focus on obligate subterranean organisms. Although aquatic snails represent one of the most species-rich groups in groundwater, no genomic resources have been available for these taxa until now. To fill this gap, we generated a reference transcriptome for a cave-dwelling snail of the genus Physella. The Physella population from Fogelpole Cave, IL, USA, exhibits heteromorphic phenotypes, with individuals ranging from fully pigmented to completely lacking pigment. The reference transcriptome generated in this study shows high BUSCO scores, high mapping rates and robust overall quality metrics. These datasets provide a valuable resource for investigating molecular changes associated with the early stages of cave adaptation and the evolution of specialized cave traits, especially the loss of pigmentation.Subject terms: Molecular evolution, Evolutionary genetics, Transcriptomics, Ecological genetics, Evolutionary ecology

Background & Summary

Mollusks, the second most biodiverse group of animals on Earth, are an ecologically diverse phylum. Mollusks have successfully colonized nearly every habitat, from the deep sea to terrestrial environments, and even caves. Caves are among the most extreme and least explored habitats on Earth. They are characterized by strong selective pressures, including permanent darkness, limited food resources, and disrupted daily and annual rhythms, which lead to the evolution of specialized traits in cave-dwelling species1,2. The most famous is the loss of eyes and pigmentation, but notable changes in metabolism, circadian rhythms, reproduction, nervous system and behaviors also accompany life in caves1,2. The presence of similar selection pressures in different caves makes cave animals a prime example of convergent evolution in nature and excellent models for evolutionary studies.

Gastropods are the most abundant subterranean mollusks, as only a few bivalve species are found in caves3,4. Obligate cave snails, with 450 described species, represent the second most abundant group in groundwater ecosystems, after crustaceans5. However, snails remain one of the most understudied groups in caves5–7. They are also highly threatened, as freshwater snails have the highest documented extinction rate of any taxonomic group7. Given the general lack of knowledge on subterranean fauna8, the importance of snails in groundwater communities, and their elevated risks of extinction, they are one of the highest-priority groups for research.

While obligate cave fauna, species that complete their life cycle in caves, are understudied and largely neglected in conservation and management policies8, facultative cave species (troglophiles) attract even less attention. Although troglophiles are frequently encountered in caves, they lack prominent cave adaptations that attract researchers and the general public, so the knowledge gap regarding their biology and ecology is even greater. One such example are the cave-dwelling populations of snails in the genus Physella, which inhabit several caves in southwestern Illinois, USA (Fig. 1a)9.

The taxonomy of Physella (family Physidae) is well known to be problematic, largely due to the reliance on shell morphology, which is highly plastic and environmentally influenced10. Molecular studies have shown limited genetic divergence among morphologically distinct taxa, alongside cases of cryptic diversity, complicating species-level identification11. These challenges are particularly pronounced in cave populations, and genomic data are essential for resolving taxonomic placement and understanding the evolutionary status of these populations.

Fogelpole Cave in Monroe County, Illinois is home to a highly polymorphic population of Physella snails, with a spectrum of phenotypes ranging from individuals lacking any obvious pigment to fully dark and pigmented snails12. The polymorphic nature of the Fogelpole Cave Physella, coupled with the relative ease with which laboratory colonies of these snails can be established13, creates a unique opportunity to study the genetic and evolutionary mechanisms underlying the early stages of cave adaptation, making it an ideal system for investigating colonization of subterranean habitats.

To gain a mechanistic understanding of the evolutionary processes driving cave adaptations, the availability of genomic data is essential. While genomic resources exist for cave-adapted fish, salamanders, and crustaceans14–23, mollusks remain a major gap in subterranean genomics. To address this gap, we have generated the first genomic data for any cave snail. The transcriptomic datasets of Physella sp. from Fogelpole Cave will provide a crucial foundation for understanding the evolution of cave-dwelling species and the genetic basis of specialized cave traits in a new phylum. These datasets are particularly valuable for understanding the colonization of caves and the early stages of adaptation to life underground. In particular, they can be used to study the genetic basis of pigmentation loss, one of the most common cave adaptations, whose molecular mechanisms remain largely elusive23–25, with the exception of cavefish Astyanax mexicanus26. In addition to this vertebrate model, the crustacean Asellus aquaticus has emerged as a powerful invertebrate system for studying cave evolution, with recent work providing important insights into the genetic architecture and evolutionary pathways underlying eye and pigmentation loss27–30. Despite these advances, comparable genomic resources are lacking for mollusks.

Moreover, melanogenesis is implicated in a range of biological functions beyond body pigmentation across diverse taxa31,32 and numerous pleiotropic effects associated with melanin loss have been discovered in Astyanax33–35. Physella therefore represents an alternative model system for investigating the evolution of pigmentation loss and the potential role of natural selection favoring albinism through pleiotropy. Finally, the availability of genomic datasets for different morphs of this species also enables investigation of the genetic basis of other cave-related changes in Physella, including reduced growth, delayed sexual maturity and decreased fecundity12.

Methods

Snail collection and hybrid crosses

Adult snails of different morphotypes were collected from Fogelpole Cave, Illinois, USA, and brought to the laboratory to establish breeding cultures. In the laboratory, snails were maintained at room temperature (20–22 °C) in containers with regular water changes and were fed ad libitum to ensure stable growth and reproduction. Detailed protocols for cultivating snails in the laboratory are described elsewhere13.

Parental crosses between pigmented and depigmented individuals were initiated by pairing immature snails (ca. 2 mm shell length) in small containers. After reaching reproductive maturity, parent snails laid eggs, and F1 embryos were isolated. Physids are hermaphrodites and capable of self-fertilization, though they typically outcross when mates are available36. To ensure that F1 offspring were the result of outcrossing, the depigmented parents from each cross were periodically isolated and allowed to deposit eggs. Developing embryos were examined prior to hatching, and eye pigmentation was used as an indicator of successful outcrossing. Given that parental individuals exhibited stable pigmentation phenotypes and that previous studies have demonstrated a simple Mendelian basis of this trait12, the appearance of pigmentation in offspring derived from depigmented individuals was interpreted as evidence of hybrid origin. However, low levels of residual heterozygosity or rare deviations from expected genotype–phenotype relationships cannot be entirely excluded. To produce the F2 generation, juvenile F1 snails were paired in separate containers. Following maturation, egg masses produced by these crosses were collected, and resulting F2 offspring were reared until they reached a size suitable for phenotypic assessment and downstream tissue sampling.

Sampling and sample groups

Two sample groups were used for transcriptome sequencing. The first group consisted of 10 adult F2 individuals (5 lightly pigmented snails and 5 snails with dark pigmentation) derived from a cross between an albino and a pigmented snail, as described above. Snails were starved for a week before sampling to empty their guts and reduce contamination with non-target taxa. After a week they were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and kept at −80 °C until RNA isolation.

The second sampling group consisted of the foot tissue of albino and pigmented individuals from laboratory culture. Animals were anesthetized with MS222 (3 g/L) which caused foot extension. The posterior part of the foot was quickly severed and immediately stored in DNA/RNA Shield (cat. # R1100-250, Zymo Research, USA) until RNA isolation. Since the mass of tissue from each individual was minute, the foot from 3 individuals were pooled in one sample. In total, 4 such samples were prepared from albino individuals and 4 from pigmented ones.

Species identification

To identify the species in Fogelpole Cave, we sequenced 16S and CO1 phylogenetic markers37–42 and reconstructed the phylogeny of Physidae (Fig. 1b). We used the following primers: ColFol-rev 5′-TAAACTTCNGGRTGNCCAAAAAATCA-3′ and ColFol-for 5′-TTTCAACAAATCATAARGAYATYGG-3′ for CO1 and 16Sar-L 5′-CGCCTGTTTATCAAAAACAT-3′ and 16SbR_Isopoda 5′-CCGGTCTGAACTCAGATCACG-3′ for 16S. Physid DNA from several localities including Fogelpole Cave and Losing Pond, Monroe County, IL and Jarun Lake, Zagreb, Croatia was isolated using DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (cat. # 69504, Qiagen, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Thermal cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 45 seconds, annealing at 45 °C for COI and 55.3 °C for 16S for 45 seconds, and extension at 72 °C for 1 minute, concluding with a 5-minute final extension at 72 °C. The PCR mixture consisted of 20 uL 2x PCR mix (ReadyMix Taq PCR Reaction Mix, cat. # P4600, Sigma Aldrich, USA), 1.2 uL of forward and reverse primers, 12.4 uL H2O, 1.2 uL Mg2+ and 4 uL of DNA (in total 80 ng). For purification, 10 uL of PCR product was mixed with 0.2 uL Exonuclease I and 1 uL FastAP Thermosensitive Alkaline Phosphatase (cat. # EN0582 and EF0651, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and incubated at 37 °C for 45 min followed by 80 °C for 15 min. PCR products were sequenced using the Sanger method.

16S and CO1 sequences from different physid species and outgroup were retrieved from the NCBI43–76 and processed using Geneious Prime software 2024.0.7. Sequences were aligned with MUSCLE 5.177 using the Super5 algorithm. Alignment ends were trimmed, and sites with 50% gaps were masked. Resulting alignments were used to construct a maximum-likelihood (ML) tree with IQtree 2.2.678–80 using ModelFinder and a partition model for each marker. Branch support values were estimated using 1000 ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot) replicates, and the resulting tree was visualized in iTOL (online version v7; accessed 10/3/2025) and rooted with Biomphalaria obstructa as an outgroup.

Since snails within Physidae have inconsistent use of species names in the literature and several taxonomic revisions were conducted thus far10,11,81,82, here we used current species names from MolluscaBase (https://www.molluscabase.org/, accessed on 10/3/2025) for species designations in the phylogenetic tree. RNA extraction, library preparation and sequencing

Whole RNA from 18 samples was extracted using SPLIT RNA Extraction Kit (cat. # SKU: 008.48, Lexogen, Austria) following the suggested protocol. RNA concentration was measured using spectrophotometry, and the quality and integrity of the isolated RNA was assessed using gel electrophoresis. During protocol optimization for RNA isolation, gel electrophoresis as a qualitative assessment method for RNA integrity was confirmed with Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer traces by evaluating RNA profiles. The success of DNase treatment (TURBO DNase, cat. # AM2239, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in removing potential DNA contamination was confirmed by the absence of CO1 amplification using primers HCO2198coi (5′-TANACYTCNGGRTGNCCRAARAATCA-3′) and LepF1 (5′-ATTCAACCATCATAAAGATATTGG-3′). The thermal cycling protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 seconds, annealing at 45 °C for 1 minute, and extension at 72 °C for 90 seconds, concluding with a 10-minute final extension at 72 °C. Final RNA concentrations were measured using a fluorometric assay (QFX Fluorometer and DeNovix RNA Quantification Kit, cat. # KIT-RNA-2-NS, DeNovix, USA).

Libraries were prepared in-house using CORALL mRNA-Seq V2 (cat. # 177.96, Lexogen, Austria) using the Long Insert Size (RTL) protocol to produce strand-specific paired-end libraries. All libraries were inspected on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and quantified using a fluorometric approach (QFX Fluorometer and DeNovix dsDNA Ultra High Sensitivity Assay, cat. # DSDNA-ULTRA-2, DeNovix). Prepared libraries were multiplexed and sequenced in PE-150 mode on Illumina NovaSeq X plus by Novogene Europe, Cambridge, UK.

Bioinformatics pipeline

If not stated otherwise all analyses were conducted on the computing clusters Supek and Padobran of University Computing Center, University of Zagreb, Croatia. A summary of the workflow is shown in Fig. 2.

Schematic representation of experimental and computational workflow.

Read processing and de novo assembly

Raw reads were quality assessed using FastQC 0.12.183 and MultiQC 1.1984, and trimmed for adapters, UMIs, polyX, and low-quality bases using fastp 0.23.485,86. Clean reads were rechecked for quality before continuing the analyses. A reference transcriptome was generated from all samples with Trinity software 2.15.187. Quality of the assembled transcriptome was assessed by Transrate 1.0.388 and completeness of the assembly was determined with BUSCO 5.8.089 using the metazoan gene set (metazoa_odb10) on the European Galaxy server90.

Removing redundancy and mapping

Two approaches were used to reduce the redundancy of the transcriptome. The first was transcript clustering based on homology, which was done using the cd-hit-est option of the CD-HIT 4.8.191,92, with the homology threshold set to 90%. The second was a clustering step performed with Corset 1.0793 with default parameters, which included filtering out transcripts with low read support (less than 10 reads per transcript). To assess whether the filtered transcriptome was over-filtered, reads were mapped back to the transcriptome using Salmon 1.10.294. Read-count data produced with Corset93 were used for principal component analysis (PCA) with the plotPCA function of DESeq 2 1.44.095 in R 4.4.196. The quality and completeness of the final reference transcriptome was re-assessed with Transrate88 and BUSCO89 scores in the same way as earlier.

Protein prediction and functional annotation

Open reading frames (ORF) and candidate coding regions were predicted with TransDecoder 5.7.1 (Haas, BJ. https://github.com/TransDecoder/TransDecoder) and only the longest ORF per each transcript was retained. Predicted peptide sequences for the candidate ORFs were used for functional annotation using multiple databases. Ultra-sensitive mode of DIAMOND v2.1.8.16297 was used to obtain annotations from UniProt’s SwissProt and NCBI’s NR (both databases downloaded in February 2024) using the BLASTP method. hmmscan from HMMER 3.4 (http://hmmer.org/) was used to detect protein domains from the PFAM database (downloaded in September 2023). EggNOG Mapper 5.0.298,99 was used for functional annotation of proteins in the Gene Ontology database and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway database. TMHMM 2.0100–102 was used to detect proteins with transmembrane helices. Both eggNOG Mapper98,99 and TMHMM100–102 were run on the European Galaxy server90.

Technical Validation

Species identification

In total, previously published 16S and CO1 sequences from 16 physid species and one outgroup species43–76, and snails from three localities in the US and Croatia37–42, including Fogelpole Cave were used for phylogeny reconstruction. The resulting ML phylogenetic tree successfully placed snails from Fogelpole Cave into Physidae (Fig. 1b). More specifically, Fogelpole Cave snails nested within Physella gyrina complex, which was separated from Physella acuta complex. Basic tree topography is consistent with the previous phylogenetic analyses on Physidae11,106. Because more data is needed to resolve Physella gyrina complex and this is not within the scope of our study, we decided to use the name Physella sp. for the Fogelpole Cave population. Species Physella gyrina and Physella acuta appear several times in the tree because many species were synonymized with either of the two. Original identifiers are added to the names in the tree to distinguish between different samples.

RNA extraction, library preparation and sequencing

RNA samples with high integrity and no detected DNA with PCR were further used for library preparation and sequencing. For in-house prepared libraries, instructions from the library preparation kit were followed. Quality controls of the prepared libraries included meeting library concentration criteria and the expected library profile on the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer trace results.

Read processing and de novo assembly

Detailed information on sequencing output and quality parameters for the samples used in the de novo transcriptome assembly is given in Table 2. In short, approximately 345 million paired-end reads were sequenced from 18 samples, ranging from 9 to 15 million raw reads per sample for foot tissue, and 18–26 million raw reads for whole-body samples. More than 325 million reads (94.2%) passed quality control and were used for de novo assembly with Trinity. The initial Trinity assembly consisted of 1,031,759 transcripts with an average mapping rate of 91.87% across all samples. The N50 value of the Trinity transcriptome was 1239, indicating good contiguity of the assembly. BUSCO scores (Fig. 3a) (metazoa; n:954) showed a high level of completeness (99.5% of genes being complete, 0.3% being fragmented and 0.2% missing).

Removing redundancy and mapping

CD-HIT reduced the number of initial transcripts to 662,467 and Corset further clustered them into 360,796 final clusters containing 411,434 transcripts in the final, non-redundant, assembly (Table 3). This is similar to the reference transcriptome of closely related Physella acuta which contains 325,563 transcripts and has a BUSCO score of 98%107. Both of these are within the range of published transcriptome assemblies for other gastropod species108–112 and bivalves113. BUSCO scores for the non-redundant transcriptome show a high level of completeness (98.8% complete, 0.6% fragmented and 0.5% missing genes) (Table 3), indicating that redundancy removal did not significantly affect assembly quality in terms of completeness, while it did reduce the number of duplicated BUSCO genes from 85.4% to 54.7% (Fig. 3a). Although mapping rates to the non-redundant assembly were on average 86.77% for whole-body samples, and 87% for foot samples, which is slightly lower than the initial mapping rates, these percentages are still well above the generally accepted and suggested percentage of mapping rates for de novo transcriptomes of 80%114. PCA based on the top 500 most variable genes distinguished two sample groups based on the tissue type, as expected, with the first component accounting for 93% of the variance (Fig. 3b). Additionally, whole-body samples show higher variability compared to foot samples.

Protein prediction and functional annotation

TransDecoder predicted proteins from 60,505 transcripts in the final, non-redundant, transcriptome (Table 4). Of these, 48% were complete containing both start and stop codons, 17.39% were internal, lacking both 5′ and 3′ ends, 20.19% were 5′ partial and 14.4% were 3′ partial. Predicted protein length ranged from 86 to 9,438 aa (amino acids), with a mean length of 317.7 aa. Among predicted proteins, 26,858 were annotated in the UniProt/SwissProt database, and 41,846 were annotated in NCBI’s NR database. EggNOG Mapper detected 31,792 proteins, of which 20,610 were annotated in Gene Ontology and 21,181 were assigned KEGG orthologues. A HMMER search with a cutoff E-value of 1e-5 detected 47,160 protein domains in the PFAM database. TMHMM identified 11,955 possible transmembrane proteins, defined as those containing at least one transmembrane helix of at least 18 amino acids.
