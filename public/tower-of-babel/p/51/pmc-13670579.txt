Influence of organic and inorganic particles on the properties of composite resins: a scoping review
Authors: Carlos Junio Campos Pita, Valder Ferreira da Silva Filho, Adriana Gonçalves da Silva, Rodrigo Fernandes da Cunha Pereira, Thiago Peixoto da Motta, Brender Leonan-Silva, Vitor César Dumont
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe improvement of resin-based composites is closely linked to the evolution of their organic monomers and inorganic filler particles, which directly affect their mechanical, optical, and biological performance. Understanding how these components influence clinically relevant properties is essential to guide material development and clinical decision-making. This scoping review aimed to map and synthesize the available evidence on the influence of organic and inorganic particles on the physical, mechanical, optical, and biological properties of resin-based composites.MethodsA scoping review was conducted following the PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Electronic searches were performed in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Google Scholar. The literature search was initially conducted and subsequently updated in April 2026 to ensure the inclusion of the most recent evidence. In vitro studies evaluating commercially available resin-based composites intended for direct restorations were included. Data extraction focused on resin composition, organic monomers, inorganic filler characteristics, evaluated properties, and reported outcomes.ResultsA total of 13 in vitro studies met the eligibility criteria, indicating a limited evidence base. The findings suggest that both organic monomers and inorganic fillers influence composite performance. Monomers such as Bis-GMA, Bis-EMA, and UDMA tended to be associated with improved mechanical properties and higher microhardness, whereas more hydrophilic monomers were associated with increased water sorption and solubility. Inorganic filler characteristics—including type, size, morphology, and distribution—appeared to influence degree of conversion, light transmittance, surface roughness, and hydrolytic stability. Composites containing smaller, spherical fillers based on silica or zirconia generally showed more stable mechanical behavior and improved surface properties.ConclusionsThe interaction between organic and inorganic components appears to play an important role in the performance of resin-based composites. However, the limited number of studies, their methodological heterogeneity, and the exclusive reliance on in vitro evidence restrict the strength of the conclusions. Further well-designed, long-term clinical studies are required to validate these findings under real clinical conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08571-5.Keywords: Resin-based composites, Organic monomers, Inorganic fillers, Mechanical properties, Scoping review

Background

The structure of resin-based dental composites has undergone significant improvements since their introduction into dentistry. Until recently, the most relevant modifications have focused on changes in filler agents, whose particle size was deliberately reduced, resulting in materials with improved polishability and greater resistance to wear [1]. The use of resin composites is considered one of the major advances in restorative procedures, as they restore esthetics and function through less invasive preparations, with reduced removal of dental tissues and without the need to create mechanical retention for material adhesion [2].

The selection of an appropriate restorative material requires balancing various requirements. For enhanced restoration longevity, it is imperative to have improved mechanical properties, such as high strength, fracture toughness, surface hardness, optimized elastic modulus, low wear, water sorption and solubility, minimal polymerization shrinkage, low fatigue and degradation, and high radiopacity [3]. At the same time, good biocompatibility (both systemic and local) is essential, with no postoperative pain or hypersensitivity, preservation of tooth integrity by preventing fractures or cracks, and caries inhibition capabilities [4]. Additionally, aesthetic considerations, such as color matching and stability (translucency, shades), ideal polishability, long-term surface gloss, absence of marginal or surface discoloration, and long-term anatomical form are crucial [3, 5].

Different types of inorganic components, with particle sizes ranging from macro- to nanoscale, have been particularly necessary to enable the use of these materials in posterior teeth applications [1]. Current developments have also focused on the polymer matrix, mainly aiming to develop systems with reduced polymerization shrinkage and, perhaps more importantly, reduced shrinkage stress, as well as to render these materials self-adhesive to dental structures [6–8]. The proportion between organic and inorganic components also plays an important role in controlling the mechanical properties of composite resins, as reducing the amount of the organic matrix may decrease polymerization shrinkage, thereby lowering the stress generated at the adhesive interface during the photoactivation process and improving adhesion to the dental structure [9, 10].

In the current literature, several review studies have addressed the effects of filler particles on the clinical properties of composite resins [11–14]. Across these studies, it is well recognized that the performance of composite resins is largely governed by the interactions among their constituent particles, and that achieving a simultaneous balance among multiple properties remains a challenge in restorative dentistry. Therefore, the present review is relevant to dental practitioners, as it provides an updated and comprehensive overview of how composite composition directly influences the clinical performance of resin-based materials, supporting the selection of restorative materials with greater durability, esthetics, and predictability in direct restorations. Accordingly, this study aims to conduct a scoping review on the influence of organic and inorganic components on the mechanical, optical, and biological properties of composite resins.

Although previous reviews have addressed specific aspects of resin-based composites, such as filler characteristics or monomer composition, most studies have focused on isolated components or narrative summaries. In contrast, the present scoping review provides a structured and comprehensive mapping of the combined influence of organic and inorganic particles on multiple clinically relevant properties.

Additionally, this study incorporates a comparative qualitative synthesis across different outcome domains, enabling a broader understanding of how these components interact to influence composite performance. By systematically identifying methodological patterns and gaps in the literature, this review contributes to advancing both research design and material development in restorative dentistry.

Methodology

A scoping review was conducted following a multistep approach, which included: definition of the topic and the guiding research question; development of inclusion and exclusion criteria; identification of relevant publications in the selected databases; categorization and analysis of the studies; critical appraisal of the selected evidence; and presentation and synthesis of the results. Accordingly, the guiding research question was: What is known about the impact of organic and inorganic particles on the clinical properties of composite resins?

Registration and protocol

The methodology of the present study followed the PRISMA-ScR guidelines, given their recommended items for conducting systematic reviews with extensions to scoping reviews and meta-analyses [15]. Subsequently, after review and approval by all authors, the protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF; Center for Open Science, Charlottesville, United States of America) on April 13, 2025, under the protocol registration number https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/6QPZ9 [16]. The PRISMA-ScR checklist is provided as a supplementary file. The literature search was initially conducted and subsequently updated in April 2026 to ensure the inclusion of the most recent evidence.

Eligibility criteria

The research question was formulated based on the PCC acronym (Population, Concept, and Context): P (Population): Composite resins; C (Concept): Influence of inorganic and organic particles on clinical properties; C (Context): Current evidence on the influence of inorganic and organic particles on the performance of composite resins.

Accordingly, this review exclusively included in vitro laboratory studies that investigated composite resins with established clinical applications, whose formulations are available for use in direct dental restorations. To be eligible, these studies examined the clinical properties of inorganic and organic particles in composite resins intended for direct restorations.

Outcomes considered were related to the influence of these particles on clinical properties, including mechanical, optical, esthetic, biological, or handling characteristics of the composite resins. Additionally, the studies were required to clearly describe the methods used to assess these outcomes.

The exclusion criteria applied were as follows:

Clinical studies, animal studies, ex vivo studies, narrative or integrative reviews, letters to the editor, book chapters, conference abstracts, clinical guidelines, panels, technique description articles, and opinion papers;

The decision to exclude composite resins modified with experimental materials or formulations not yet available in the clinical setting was made to ensure greater methodological homogeneity and to preserve the practical relevance of the findings. The restriction to studies published predominantly from 2015 onwards was applied to ensure that the review reflects contemporary resin-based composite formulations and recent advances in material science, including developments in filler technology and monomer systems. Older studies were considered less representative of current commercially available materials and clinical practice. Finally, no restrictions were applied regarding the language or year of publication.

The exclusion of experimental or prototype composite formulations was intentionally applied to ensure clinical relevance and to focus on materials currently available for clinical use. This approach aimed to enhance the applicability of the findings to real-world dental practice.

The search strategy applied in PubMed included indexed terms (MeSH) and their synonyms. Minor adjustments were made to adapt the strategy to the specific requirements of each database. Search strategy

Additionally, the reference lists of the included studies and relevant systematic reviews were screened to identify additional eligible studies. The included studies were also checked for possible retractions due to error or fraud, ensuring the reliability and accuracy of the evidence base.

All references retrieved from the searches were imported into the online Rayyan software (Qatar Computing Research Institute, Doha, Qatar) [18] for duplicate removal, and all stages of the study selection process were conducted using the same platform.

Study selection

Two reviewers participated in the selection process to minimize potential selection bias. In the first phase, two review authors (C.J.C.P. and V.F.S.F.) independently and blindly screened titles and abstracts. Studies were coded as “included” if they met the inclusion criteria, “maybe” for potentially eligible or uncertain records, and “excluded” otherwise. To ensure consistency between reviewers, inter-rater reliability was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient based on a random sample of twenty studies selected from the full dataset; the resulting kappa value was 0.901, indicating excellent agreement. Disagreements were resolved through discussion between the reviewers. When consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer (V.C.D.) was consulted to achieve a final decision through discussion.

In the second phase, the same two authors applied the eligibility criteria through full-text screening. Disagreements were resolved by discussion and, when necessary, consultation with the third reviewer (V.C.D.), with final decisions reached by consensus among the reviewers. The study selection process was documented in sufficient detail to complete the PRISMA flow diagram, in accordance with PRISMA 2021 reporting standards.

Data extraction

For the included studies, one author (C.J.C.P.) was responsible for data collection, while two additional reviewers (V.C.D. and V.F.S.F.) independently verified the extracted information. Data extraction was performed independently from the full-text articles using a customized data extraction spreadsheet. The following data were extracted from each included study: study characteristics (authors, year, country of publication, and study design); characteristics of the investigated item (sample size and age range) or (type of particles: organic or inorganic, particle size, and particle composition); evaluated properties, assessment methods, and reported effects.

In cases of disagreement between reviewers, the data were discussed until consensus was reached. When important data were missing or unclear, the corresponding authors were contacted by email to request additional or unpublished information. If no response was obtained, the unretrieved data were recorded in the table as missing or not reported.

The direction of the effect was determined based on whether the reported outcomes indicated a beneficial, unfavorable, or inconclusive influence of the evaluated organic or inorganic components within each study. The magnitude of the effect was interpreted considering the consistency and extent of the reported findings across the assessed properties.

Accordingly, the classification was defined as follows: Ø indicated inconclusive findings or outcomes not reported; 1 represented a low or unfavorable effect, including small, inconsistent, or negative changes; 2 indicated a moderate positive effect, characterized by consistent improvement of limited magnitude; and 3 represented a strong positive effect, defined by substantial and consistently reported improvements across multiple outcomes within the same study.

The scoring framework was conceptually based on principles of qualitative evidence synthesis commonly applied in scoping reviews, aiming to identify patterns and trends rather than to quantify effect size or establish causality. No quantitative weighting or statistical aggregation was performed due to the heterogeneity of study designs, materials, and outcome measures.

To enhance consistency in the application of the scoring system, two reviewers underwent a calibration exercise prior to data extraction using a subset of five included studies. Inter-reviewer agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, yielding a value of 0.899, indicating excellent agreement.

During the data extraction and classification process, disagreements between reviewers were initially resolved through discussion. When consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted to ensure a final decision, thereby minimizing subjective bias.

Critical appraisal of individual sources of evidence

A formal methodological quality or risk of bias assessment was not performed. In accordance with the Joanna Briggs Institute [19] and the PRISMA-ScR [15] scoping reviews aim to map the extent, range, and characteristics of available evidence, rather than to critically appraise study quality. Therefore, the omission of this step is consistent with the methodological framework adopted. Nevertheless, potential limitations and heterogeneity of the included studies are addressed narratively.

Results

Selection of sources of evidence

The initial search strategy yielded 3.041 records. After the removal of duplicate records, 1.999 articles were screened based on titles and abstracts (phase 1). Following the initial screening process, 38 articles advanced to phase 2 (full-text assessment). After the selection process, 13 articles were included in the review. Figure 1 presents the flow diagram summarizing the search and selection strategies.

PRISMA flowchart

During the full-text assessment stage, detailed reasons for exclusion were recorded and categorized to enhance transparency and reproducibility. The most common reasons for exclusion included: (i) evaluation of experimental or prototype materials without clinical availability; (ii) absence of outcomes related to clinically relevant properties; (iii) focus on materials other than resin-based composites for direct restorations; and (iv) insufficient methodological description. A summary of the exclusion reasons is provided in the PRISMA flow diagram and supplementary material.

The included studies were conducted across different geographic regions, including South America, Europe, and Asia. However, due to the limited number of studies, no formal bibliometric or geographic trend analysis was performed.

Individual results of the sources of evidence

The included studies evaluated different brands and types of composite resins, considering the characteristics of organic and inorganic particles, physicomechanical and optical properties, as well as polymerization effects and hydrolytic stability. The analyses included assessments of degree of conversion, microhardness, light transmission, flexural strength, and surface roughness, using methods such as mechanical testing, scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and spectroscopy (SEM–EDX). Results synthesis

The included studies demonstrate that both organic and inorganic particles significantly influence the physicomechanical properties of resin composites. Materials containing larger inorganic particles, irregular morphology, or pre-polymerized fillers, such as Cention N and Beautifil II, showed a lower degree of conversion, higher water sorption and solubility, and increased surface roughness after simulated aging, indicating reduced resistance to abrasion and hydrolytic degradation. In contrast, resins with smaller, spherical particles based on silica or zirconia, such as Z100, Filtek Supreme Ultra, and Estelite Σ Quick, maintained better mechanical stability and surface smoothness. Monomer composition and photoinitiator systems affected the degree and kinetics of polymerization, while increased filler content and the presence of opaque particles reduced light transmittance and depth of cure.

Table 4 illustrates the effects of inorganic and organic particles on the evaluated outcomes, including degree of conversion, mechanical properties, translucency, surface roughness, sorption/solubility, and microhardness/nanohardness. The magnitude of the effect was classified into four levels: Ø (inconclusive or not reported); 1 (low or unfavorable effect, including small, inconsistent, or negative changes); 2 (moderate positive effect, indicating consistent improvement of limited magnitude); and 3 (strong positive effect, representing substantial and consistently reported improvements within the study).

The classification presented in Table 4 was based on a structured qualitative synthesis of the outcomes reported in each included study. For each outcome domain, the direction and magnitude of the reported effects were interpreted in relation to the specific organic or inorganic components evaluated within the same study. Given the heterogeneity of materials, formulations, and testing protocols, no quantitative weighting or causal inference was performed. Therefore, the classification reflects a descriptive mapping of trends rather than a comparative effectiveness assessment.

To enhance methodological rigor and reproducibility, the reviewers underwent a calibration exercise prior to data extraction, using five studies to standardize the interpretation of the scoring criteria. Inter-reviewer agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient, yielding a value of 0.899, which indicates excellent agreement.

It is important to note that classifications indicating strong positive effects (score 3) were based on the consistency of findings within individual studies and do not necessarily reflect a large body of evidence. Therefore, these ratings should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

This scoping review aimed to evaluate the current evidence regarding the effects of organic and inorganic particles on the clinically relevant properties of resin-based composites. Previous literature reviews addressing similar topics have been published, and their findings are largely consistent with those observed in the present review [11–13]. Despite the availability of these reviews, a scoping review approach was deliberately chosen due to the heterogeneity of materials, formulations, outcome measures, and experimental protocols across in vitro studies, which precludes quantitative synthesis and justifies a comprehensive mapping of the existing evidence. An important contribution of this review lies in its structured synthesis approach, which integrates multiple outcome domains and highlights the interaction between organic and inorganic components, an aspect that has been insufficiently explored in previous reviews.

Although the final number of included studies was relatively limited, a scoping review approach remained appropriate due to the substantial heterogeneity observed across studies in terms of material formulations, experimental protocols, outcome measures, and evaluation methods. This variability precludes meaningful quantitative synthesis and supports the use of a scoping framework to systematically map the available evidence.

Moreover, the limited number of eligible studies highlights an important gap in the literature, reinforcing the relevance of this review in identifying areas that require further investigation rather than providing definitive conclusions.

It is important to emphasize that, consistent with the scoping review methodology, the present findings represent a descriptive mapping of the available evidence rather than an assessment of effectiveness or causal relationships. Therefore, the interpretations presented should be understood as indicative of trends and associations within the literature.

The composition of resin-based composites, based on the interaction between organic monomers and inorganic filler particles, appears to play an important role in their clinical performance. This interaction is associated with mechanical, optical, and chemical properties, influencing strength, hardness, surface roughness, translucency, and hydrolytic stability [33]. The findings of this review suggest that filler type, size, morphology, and content are associated with variability in composite performance reported in the literature, highlighting the relevance of optimizing the matrix–filler interface to potentially improve durability and clinical longevity [22].

Analysis of the included studies indicated that the organic matrix is associated with the physicomechanical behavior of resin-based composites. Monomers such as Bis-GMA, Bis-EMA, and UDMA were frequently associated with improved mechanical properties and higher microhardness values [20, 22]. Conversely, a higher content of hydrophilic monomers, as observed in materials such as Beautifil II (Shofu, Inc.) and Vertise Flow (Kerr Corporation), was associated with reduced mechanical resistance and increased susceptibility to degradation [23]. These findings are in agreement with previous reports suggesting that both the quality and proportion of organic components influence the mechanical performance of resin composites [22].

Water sorption and solubility are clinically relevant properties, as they may compromise both esthetic stability and flexural strength over time [34]. Monomers such as Bis-GMA and TEGDMA contain polar functional groups that increase hydrophilicity, thereby promoting higher water uptake and solubility [23]. In agreement with this mechanism, Filtek Supreme Ultra (3 M Oral Care, St. Paul, MN, USA) was associated with lower hydrolytic stability compared with materials formulated without Bis-GMA and TEGDMA, emphasizing the role of monomer composition in long-term material behavior [21].

Photopolymerization of resin composites begins with light activation, allowing photons to propagate through the material and activate the photoinitiator system. Photoinitiators generate free radicals that initiate chain reactions between carbon–carbon double bonds in monomers. When polymer network formation occurs in a heterogeneous manner, it may negatively influence the degree of conversion and the resulting mechanical properties [35]. In this context, Araújo-Neto et al. [21] reported higher maximum polymerization rates (Rp_max) in composites containing alternative photoinitiator systems more reactive than camphorquinone, such as bis(2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl)-phenylphosphine oxide (BAPO) and diphenyl(2,4,6-trimethylbenzoyl)phosphine oxide (TPO).

The degree of conversion reflects the efficiency of polymerization of the organic matrix and is related to the formation of a cross-linked polymer network, influencing hardness, strength, and overall stability [22]. In the present review, UDMA-containing materials were associated with favorable performance in terms of degree of conversion, particularly in Filtek Z250 (3 M ESPE, USA) [24]. However, despite also containing UDMA, Filtek Bulk Fill (3 M ESPE, USA) was associated with a lower degree of conversion, which may be related to its higher filler content, potentially affecting light transmission and polymerization efficiency [24].

Light transmittance has received increasing attention because it allows the placement of thicker increments, potentially reducing clinical chair time [36]. The evidence synthesized in this review suggests that both the proportion and morphology of inorganic fillers are associated with light transmittance [22]. Notably, despite its high filler content, EverX Posterior (GC Corporation, Tokyo, Japan) was reported to present higher light transmittance than the conventional control material, which may be related to the presence of glass fibers facilitating light propagation within the composite structure [20].

Vickers hardness testing is widely used to assess the resistance of a material surface to indentation [37]. Bilge and Ipek [24] reported that conventional resin composites with higher filler content were associated with greater microhardness than bulk-fill materials, regardless of the light-curing unit used. Furthermore, Fronza et al. [20] demonstrated that filler characteristics, including particle size and morphology, may influence light transmittance, indirectly affecting polymerization efficiency and the mechanical properties of resin composites.

Surface roughness is a critical parameter for predicting clinical failure, as increased roughness is associated with greater plaque accumulation and esthetic deterioration. This property is commonly evaluated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) [38]. The studies included in this review indicated that variations in filler content, particle size, morphology, and composition are associated with differences in surface roughness outcomes. Lower roughness values were reported in zirconia-filled composites, which are recognized for their enhanced mechanical resistance and surface stability [23].

Although some materials were classified as having strong positive effects, these findings were often based on a limited number of studies. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting these results, as they may not represent consistent evidence across different experimental conditions.

A critical appraisal of the included studies revealed important methodological limitations. Most were inherent to the in vitro design, including small sample sizes, the use of different commercial brands with specific clinical indications, and substantial variability in experimental protocols, light-curing methods, and material formulations.

The relatively small number of included studies should be considered when interpreting the findings of this review. Although the search strategy was comprehensive, the strict eligibility criteria—particularly the focus on commercially available materials, clinically relevant outcomes, and the exclusion of experimental formulations—were intentionally applied to enhance the clinical applicability of the findings. However, this approach may have reduced the number of eligible studies and limited the breadth of the evidence base.

In addition, the possibility of publication and selection bias cannot be excluded. Studies with non-significant or unfavorable results may be underrepresented in the literature, and the exclusion of non-indexed or inaccessible studies may have further influenced the evidence mapping.

Consequently, the limited number of included studies reduces the confidence in the observed trends, and the findings should be interpreted as indicative rather than conclusive. This reinforces the exploratory nature of this scoping review and highlights the need for further research to strengthen the available evidence. The restriction to studies published from 2015 onwards may have excluded earlier evidence; however, this decision was made to focus on contemporary materials and formulations that better reflect current clinical practice.

These factors limit direct extrapolation to clinical conditions. Consequently, well-designed long-term clinical trials are required to further investigate the behavior of different combinations of organic monomers and inorganic fillers under oral conditions. Future studies should also explore novel polymer matrices with reduced water sorption and polymerization shrinkage stress, as well as inorganic fillers that may enhance light transmittance, microhardness, and surface smoothness.

Despite these limitations, this scoping review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the influence of organic and inorganic particles on the clinically relevant properties of resin-based composites. Strengths of this review include an extensive literature search across multiple databases, inclusion of gray literature, transparent and rigorous implementation of the scoping review methodology, clearly defined eligibility criteria, and a critical appraisal of the included studies. Together, these aspects provide a structured overview of the current evidence and support the relevance of this review for both researchers and clinicians.

Although the findings of this review are primarily derived from in vitro studies, they offer potentially relevant insights into the behavior of resin-based composites. However, caution is required when extrapolating these findings to clinical performance. In vitro conditions do not fully replicate the complexity of the oral environment, which involves dynamic factors such as saliva, temperature fluctuations, masticatory forces, biofilm activity, and patient-related variables.

In addition, commonly used laboratory aging protocols, such as water storage and thermocycling, only partially simulate intraoral degradation processes and may not accurately reflect long-term clinical behavior. As a result, the outcomes observed under controlled experimental conditions may differ from those observed in clinical practice.

Therefore, the clinical implications discussed in this review should be interpreted as indicative rather than predictive. These findings may support material selection and hypothesis generation but should not be considered definitive evidence of clinical performance. Further well-designed, long-term clinical studies are necessary to validate these observations under real-world conditions.

Conclusion

Based on the available evidence, the interaction between the organic and inorganic phases appears to play an important role in the clinical performance of resin-based composites, as it is associated with their physicomechanical and optical properties. Monomers such as Bis-GMA, Bis-EMA, and UDMA were frequently associated with improved mechanical behavior, with UDMA also showing favorable trends in terms of degree of conversion.

Additionally, the type, size, morphology, and distribution of inorganic filler particles were associated with variations in strength, surface roughness, and light transmittance. Composites containing smaller, spherical fillers tended to present higher microhardness and improved hydrolytic stability.

However, these findings should be interpreted as a descriptive synthesis of the available evidence rather than definitive conclusions regarding material performance. The limited number of studies, methodological heterogeneity, and exclusive reliance on in vitro data highlight the need for further well-designed clinical investigations. These findings should be interpreted with caution, particularly due to the exclusive reliance on in vitro data, which may not fully reflect clinical conditions.
