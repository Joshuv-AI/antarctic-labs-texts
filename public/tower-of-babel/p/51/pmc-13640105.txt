From hype to reality: the feasibility, dilemmas, and solutions of Gen AI in medical education from students’ perspectives
Authors: Uzma Sarwar, Jameel Ahmed Bhutto, Kiran Fazal, Tong Sanhong, Amir Mahmood, Nadia Rehman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rapid evolution of generative artificial intelligence (AI) has sparked a pedagogical debate over whether AI can replace human teachers in medical education. What was once a theoretical inquiry has now become an urgent empirical question as AI technologies increasingly enter the classroom, challenging traditional notions of teaching, learning, and mentorship.ObjectivesThis study aims to investigate the medical students’ perceptions of generative AI as a potential replacement for traditional educators, focusing on the interrelationships among Feasibility, Dilemmas, Perception, and Replacement Intention.MethodologyData were collected from 579 medical students using a structured questionnaire and analysed using Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM) in SmartPLS. The measurement model demonstrated strong reliability and validity across all constructs.ResultsStructural analysis revealed that feasibility significantly influenced both perception (β = 0.295, p < 0.001) and replacement (β = 0.137, p < 0.001). At the same time, perception strongly predicted Replacement Intention (β = 0.314, p < 0.001) and mediated the feasibility-replacement relationship (β = 0.093, supported). However, Dilemmas did not moderate the feasibility-perception link (β = 0.045, p = 0.250), indicating that ethical or professional concerns had limited influence on students’ acceptance of AI teaching. The Importance-Performance Map Analysis (IPMA) further identified perception as the most influential construct driving replacement intention.ConclusionThe findings, grounded in the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and Expectation-Confirmation Theory (ECT), suggest that medical students’ acceptance of AI in education is shaped more by pragmatic feasibility and positive perception than by moral apprehension. The study concludes that while AI cannot yet replace the human teacher, its perceived feasibility and usefulness position it as a powerful complementary tool in reshaping the future of medical education.

Background

Generative artificial intelligence (GAI) tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek are becoming increasingly visible in higher education and have intensified debate in medical education regarding the role of human instruction, the future of classroom learning, and the boundaries of responsible innovation [24]. As these tools gain attention for their capacity to provide personalised feedback, simulate clinical reasoning, and support independent learning, educators and institutions face increasing pressure to incorporate them into mainstream pedagogy [6]. Yet an important pedagogical question remains underexplored: under what conditions do medical students perceive GAI as a viable instructional agent, and how do these evaluations influence their willingness to accept AI as a substitute for selected teaching functions? This study addresses that question by examining feasibility, perception, replacement intention, and the dilemmas associated with GAI use in medical education. Feasibility refers to students’ assessments of the accessibility, usability, and practical integration of GAI in their learning environment [46]. Perception captures their evaluative judgments of its pedagogical value and credibility [7], Because medical education extends beyond information delivery and is grounded in relational learning, professional identity formation, ethical responsibility, and mentorship, students’ evaluations of GAI are also shaped by pedagogical and ethical dilemmas associated with its use [12, 29, 30, 51].

Although research on GAI in education has expanded rapidly, much of the literature continues to emphasise technological capability, institutional readiness, or faculty perspectives, often overlooking students’ central role in evaluating instructional credibility, trust, and pedagogical effectiveness [10, 14, 17, 18, 24, 34] . Focusing on international undergraduate medical students enrolled in a transnational MBBS program in China, this study offers a cross-cultural perspective on how learners from diverse educational backgrounds assess the instructional role of GAI. Generative AI in medical education: context and debate

Generative artificial intelligence (GAI) has become an increasingly prominent innovation in medical education because of its potential to support learning, assessment, and clinical reasoning. Tools such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude can generate human-like text, provide explanations, and respond interactively to user queries, enabling forms of dialogic support and simulated expert feedback that distinguish them from earlier educational technologies [52]. Empirical research indicates that medical and health profession students are generally familiar with these tools and often view them as useful for academic support tasks such as assignments, self-directed learning, and formative feedback [1]. For example, Shimizu et al. [47] found that health profession students reported high awareness and generally positive evaluations of ChatGPT. However, trust remained moderate, and students emphasised the need for clearer usage guidelines. Beyond text-based assistance, GAI has also been used to support virtual patient simulations, scaffold clinical reasoning, and provide real-time learning support [33]. At the same time, students’ evaluations are shaped not only by technical capability but also by concerns about credibility, oversight, and alignment with established educational practices [23]. Accordingly, the literature presents both promise and controversy. On one hand, GAI is praised for expanding access to knowledge, reducing workload, and supporting independent learning [1]. On the other hand, critics emphasise its limitations in relational teaching, the development of critical thinking, and ethical oversight. This tension has fueled an ongoing debate in medical education over whether GAI should be understood primarily as a complement to human teachers or, under some conditions, as a substitute for selected instructional functions. This broader debate provides the context for examining the specific constructs that shape students’ engagement with generative AI in educational settings.

Feasibility, perception, dilemmas, and replacement intention

In this study, feasibility refers to the extent to which students perceive GAI as accessible, usable, and practically convenient for academic learning. This understanding aligns with technology adoption research on ease of use and facilitating conditions, especially in medical education, where intensive coursework and clinical demands increase the value of tools that streamline access to information and support self-directed learning [44]. Prior studies suggest that medical students often view GAI as highly feasible for everyday academic use, citing ease of access, usability, and convenience as important advantages. At the same time, contextual barriers such as subscription costs, internet restrictions, and uneven institutional support may affect the consistency of use, particularly in international and transnational medical programs [39]. Concerns such as inaccuracy, hallucinated content, and ethical uncertainty are important but are better treated as trust-related or ethical concerns rather than as core components of feasibility itself [49]. Perception refers to students’ cognitive-evaluative judgments of GAI’s usefulness, credibility, and reliability for learning. Consistent with technology acceptance research, this study treats perception as an evaluative assessment of instructional value rather than an affective attitude or moral judgment [19, 50]. Existing studies suggest that medical students often hold cautiously positive perceptions of GAI: they value its speed, accessibility, and usefulness for learning support, while also expressing concerns about accuracy, trust, and the need for faculty oversight [1, 38]. Students, therefore, appear to distinguish between recognising GAI’s utility and fully endorsing its instructional legitimacy. This distinction is particularly evident in medical education, where learners may find GAI helpful for self-directed learning and clinical reasoning, yet remain reluctant to treat it as a substitute for the pedagogical judgment and relational guidance of human teachers [39, 49].

Although recent research has also explored metaphorical and symbolic representations of GAI, such work similarly suggests that students’ perceptions are multidimensional and sometimes ambivalent, extending beyond simple instrumental utility [12, 29]. Students’ evaluations are further shaped by ethical, pedagogical, and emotional dilemmas associated with AI integration. Ethical concerns often centre on academic integrity, plagiarism, and uncertainty about appropriate use, particularly in contexts where institutional guidance remains underdeveloped [35]. Pedagogically, students may question whether AI can replicate the mentorship, empathy, professional role modelling, and clinical judgment embedded in medical teaching [39, 49]. Emotional dilemmas include anxiety about over-reliance, reduced independent reasoning, and diminished academic confidence [1, 38]. Collectively, these dilemmas suggest that students’ engagement with GAI is shaped not only by practical access and perceived usefulness but also by broader concerns about the appropriateness of AI in a high-stakes professional field.

These tensions are closely related to the replacement debate. Although advanced GAI systems can now provide explanations, feedback, and adaptive learning support, existing research consistently suggests that students view them primarily as supplementary tools rather than as comprehensive replacements for human teachers [16]. Medical students may accept GAI for selected instructional functions, such as explanations, preparatory guidance, or formative feedback, while still rejecting the idea that AI can replace the relational, supervisory, and ethical roles of educators [1, 49]. Even when students rely on GAI as a convenient source of explanation or feedback, concerns about contextual understanding, emotional intelligence, and accountability limit their willingness to view it as a full substitute for instructors [38, 39]. Because teaching in medical education involves not only knowledge transmission but also the modelling of professional identity, empathy, accountability, and ethical responsibility, full replacement remains difficult to justify [5]. For this reason, the present study defines replacement intention narrowly as students’ willingness to accept generative AI as a substitute for selected instructional tasks, rather than as a replacement for human teachers in general.

Hypothesis development

Feasibility provides the practical conditions that enable students to engage with generative AI in meaningful ways. When GAI tools are accessible, easy to use, and convenient to integrate into academic tasks, students are better positioned to form evaluative judgments about their usefulness, credibility, and instructional relevance. In this sense, feasibility functions as an antecedent to evaluative perception rather than as a direct expression of behavioural endorsement or instructional substitution [19, 21, 22]. Prior research in medical and health professions education similarly indicates that access and usability support engagement with GAI, but students’ judgments of educational value depend on how they evaluate the technology after use. Accordingly, this study proposes that greater feasibility will be associated with more favourable perceptions of GAI’s educational value.

H1

The feasibility of generative AI positively influences students’ perceptions of its educational value.

Feasibility may also contribute directly to students’ willingness to accept GAI for selected instructional functions. Even without strong, deeper endorsement, students may pragmatically accept technologies that are accessible, efficient, and easy to incorporate into learning tasks. To capture this more instrument-driven form of acceptance, the present study retains a direct path from feasibility to replacement intention.

H2

The feasibility of generative AI positively influences students’ intention to accept generative AI as a substitute for selected instructional functions in medical education.

At the same time, feasibility alone is unlikely to fully explain replacement intention. Students may find GAI easy to use without necessarily viewing it as instructionally appropriate or credible. The key mechanism is therefore expected to be students’ perceptions of usefulness, credibility, and reliability. In line with belief-based perspectives in technology acceptance research, feasibility supports engagement, which shapes evaluative perceptions, and these perceptions, in turn, influence students’ willingness to accept GAI for selected instructional tasks [11, 19]. Prior studies similarly suggest that the effects of access and facilitating conditions often operate indirectly through evaluative judgments of usefulness and value [4, 12, 49]. Accordingly, perception is expected to mediate the relationship between feasibility and replacement intention.

H3

Students’ perceptions of generative AI mediate the relationship between feasibility and their intention to accept generative AI as a substitute for specific instructional functions in medical education.

Beyond this mediating role, perception may also directly influence replacement intention. When students judge GAI as instructionally useful, credible, and reliable, they may be more willing to accept it for selected teaching functions, while still recognising the broader relational and ethical roles of human instructors. This expectation is consistent with research showing that evaluative beliefs about usefulness and credibility are important predictors of students’ willingness to rely on AI for instructional support [13, 19].

H4

Students’ perceptions of generative AI positively influence their intention to accept generative AI as a substitute for selected instructional functions in medical education.

Finally, students’ ethical, pedagogical, and emotional dilemmas may constrain the extent to which feasibility translates into favourable perception. Even when GAI is accessible and practically useful, concerns about academic integrity, accountability, patient safety, and the erosion of teacher–student relationships may weaken students’ confidence in its educational appropriateness [28, 54]. This moderating role is particularly relevant in medical education, where professional responsibility and ethical judgment are central. Prior studies suggest that perceived ethical risk and pedagogical concern can temper positive evaluations of AI, even among students who recognise its practical benefits [10, 17, 18]. Accordingly, this study treats dilemmas as a contextual constraint on the relationship between feasibility and perception.

H5

Students’ perceived dilemmas associated with generative AI weaken the positive relationship between feasibility and their perceptions of its educational value.

Methods

This study employed a quantitative, cross-sectional survey design to examine medical students’ perceptions of generative artificial intelligence (GAI) and their acceptance of AI for selected instructional functions. Given the emergent nature of generative AI in medical education and the limited availability of established theoretical models addressing instructional substitution, the study adopted an exploratory model-testing approach [26]. Data were collected at a single point in time using a structured questionnaire, and hypothesised relationships were examined using partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM), which is suitable for exploratory research involving complex models and emerging constructs [45].

Participants

The sample comprised 579 medical students from 13 universities, ensuring representation across diverse medical education contexts. As shown in Table 1, 70% of the participants were male (n = 405) and 30% were female (n = 174). The distribution across academic years ranged from Year 1 (14.4%, n = 83) to Year 5 (22.0%, n = 128), with intermediate representation in Year 2 (17.0%, n = 98), Year 3 (21.0%, n = 122), and Year 4 (25.6%, n = 148). Senior students (Years 4–5) comprised nearly half of the sample, reflecting their greater exposure to clinical training environments. Data collection procedure

Participants were recruited using a non-probability, voluntary sampling approach. Medical students enrolled in MBBS programs at 13 universities were recruited via social media platforms, including WeChat groups. The survey link was distributed electronically, allowing students to complete the questionnaire remotely at their convenience. Inclusion criteria required participants to be (a) currently enrolled medical students, (b) actively engaged in undergraduate medical education during the data collection period, and (c) familiar with or exposed to generative AI tools for academic learning purposes. Students who did not provide informed consent or submitted incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the final analysis.

This recruitment strategy enabled access to a diverse, multi-institutional sample while maintaining voluntary participation and ethical compliance. Although the sampling approach does not permit strict population-level generalisation, it is appropriate for exploratory, model-testing research aimed at examining relationships among psychological and evaluative constructs in emerging educational contexts. Data were collected during the academic year 2025 using a structured, self-administered questionnaire. Participation was voluntary, and respondents were assured of anonymity and confidentiality. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before data collection.

Sample Size and Power Considerations: Sample size adequacy was evaluated according to established guidelines for structural equation modelling rather than regression-based power analysis. In PLS-SEM, sample size requirements depend primarily on model complexity, including the number of latent constructs, structural paths, and indicators, rather than on the number of predictors alone [25]. Methodological guidelines suggest that samples exceeding 200 observations are generally sufficient for stable estimation in models of moderate complexity, while larger samples enhance parameter stability and generalizability [9, 31]. Hair et al. [26] further recommend larger samples when models include mediation and moderation effects, as interaction effects typically require greater statistical power. The present study includes four latent constructs with multiple indicators and incorporates both mediation and moderation paths. With a final sample of 579 participants, the study substantially exceeds the recommended thresholds for PLS-SEM analysis, ensuring robust parameter estimation and sufficient power to detect both direct and interaction effects.

Measurement instruments

All constructs were measured using multi-item scales adapted from the established literature and contextualised for use with generative AI in medical education. Items were phrased to reflect students’ academic learning experiences with generative AI tools (e.g., ChatGPT). Responses were recorded on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).

Feasibility: Measured using six items that capture students’ perceptions of accessibility, ease of use, resource availability, and the integration of generative AI into their academic routines. Item wording and construct operationalisation were informed by established measurement approaches in technology acceptance research, particularly studies drawing on the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), which conceptualise feasibility as an enabling condition for engagement rather than an attitudinal or ethical judgment [19, 37]. Similar operationalisations of feasibility and facilitating conditions have been widely applied in studies of educational technologies and AI-supported learning [21, 22, 37]. Sample items include: “I find generative AI tools (e.g., ChatGPT) easy to use for my academic tasks” and “I can easily integrate generative AI tools into my regular study routine.”

The construct demonstrated strong internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.912; CR = 0.931) and satisfactory convergent validity (AVE = 0.693).

Perception of generative AI

Perception was operationalised as a cognitive–evaluative construct reflecting students’ judgments of the instructional usefulness, credibility, and trustworthiness of generative AI. Item wording and measurement approaches were informed by prior empirical studies examining evaluative beliefs in AI-supported learning, including work drawing on TAM-based evaluative constructs and expectation–confirmation research that emphasises perceived usefulness, credibility, and trust as key dimensions of evaluation [8, 36]. Importantly, these sources were used to guide measurement development rather than to define the study’s theoretical framework. Sample items include: “I trust the information and explanations generated by AI tools” and “I find AI tools useful for improving my understanding of medical subjects.”

The scale exhibited good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.880; CR = 0.909) and adequate convergent validity (AVE = 0.625).

Ethical dilemmas

Ethical dilemmas were measured using 6 items that capture students’ ethical, pedagogical, and professional concerns related to the use of generative AI in medical education. Item development was informed by prior empirical research on ethical risk, academic integrity, and professional identity in AI-supported learning environments, including studies that draw on risk-oriented perspectives without positioning them as explanatory theories of the present model [18, 42, 48]. These studies consistently indicate that ethical and integrity-related concerns serve as constraints on the acceptance of AI in high-stakes educational contexts. Sample items include: “I am concerned that using generative AI might lead to academic dishonesty or plagiarism,” and “I believe that excessive dependence on AI could weaken the teacher–student relationship.”

This construct showed high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.909; CR = 0.929) and strong convergent validity (AVE = 0.687).

Acceptance of AI Instructional Substitution (Replacement Intention): Acceptance of AI instructional substitution was measured using six items assessing students’ willingness to accept generative AI performing selected instructional functions traditionally undertaken by human instructors, such as explanations, feedback, and preparatory guidance. Consistent with the study’s theoretical framing, this construct was explicitly conceptualised as function-specific acceptance rather than a general intention to eliminate or replace teachers. Item wording and operationalisation were informed by prior empirical work on AI-based task substitution and human–AI role delegation in educational and professional contexts (Meske & Bunde, 2020); [21, 22]. Sample items include: “I think AI can provide feedback and guidance similar to that of a human teacher” and “In the future, AI could substitute for teachers in some aspects of medical education.”

The scale demonstrated acceptable reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.851; CR = 0.889) and convergent validity (AVE = 0.572).

All indicator loadings exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.70, and variance inflation factor (VIF) values were below critical levels, indicating no concerns regarding multicollinearity. Collectively, these results support the reliability and convergent validity of the measurement model.

Data analysis strategy

Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and inter-construct correlations, were computed using SPSS 20.0 to provide an initial overview of the data. Hypothesis testing was conducted exclusively using partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) implemented in SmartPLS 4.0. A single coherent analytic strategy was adopted to ensure conceptual and statistical consistency across all analyses [26]. PLS-SEM was selected for its suitability for exploratory model testing of latent constructs, mediation and moderation effects, and emerging research contexts such as generative AI in medical education [27]. The method allows simultaneous estimation of the measurement and structural models while explicitly accounting for measurement error and making minimal distributional assumptions.

Model estimation was performed using the PLS algorithm, and hypothesis testing was conducted via nonparametric bootstrapping with 5,000 resamples to assess the significance of direct, indirect, and interaction effects. Mediation effects were evaluated through bootstrapped confidence intervals of indirect paths, while moderation effects were tested using interaction terms specified within the PLS-SEM framework. This integrated approach avoided reliance on regression-based tools that are not aligned with SEM assumptions. To address potential common method variance (CMV), several procedural remedies were implemented during data collection, including voluntary participation, respondent anonymity, and careful item wording to reduce evaluation apprehension. Statistically, CMV was assessed using the full collinearity variance inflation factor (VIF) approach, which is appropriate for PLS-SEM and allows detection of common method bias at both the construct and model levels. All VIF values were below recommended thresholds, indicating that CMV was unlikely to pose a serious concern for the study’s findings [32].

Results

This study investigated whether the perceived feasibility of generative AI shapes medical students’ perceptions of its educational value and their intention to accept AI for selected instructional functions, whether perception mediates this relationship and whether dilemmas moderate it. Overall, the findings supported the proposed model. Feasibility positively influenced perception and replacement intention, perception positively influenced replacement intention and mediated the relationship between feasibility and replacement intention, and perceived dilemmas weakened the relationship between feasibility and perception. Fornell-Larcker criterion

The Fornell–Larcker criterion results, presented in Table 4, provide additional confirmation of discriminant validity. The square roots of the AVE values for each construct, feasibility (0.833), Dilemmas (0.829), perception (0.791), and replacement (0.756), exceeded their corresponding inter-construct correlations. This indicates that each latent construct shows greater variance with its own measures than with those of other constructs in the model, satisfying the Fornell–Larcker criterion. Furthermore, the inter-construct correlations were moderate to low (ranging from 0.019 to 0.385), further supporting the distinctiveness of the latent variables. All constructs exhibited satisfactory internal consistency reliability, convergent validity, and discriminant validity, confirming that the measurement items appropriately capture the theoretical dimensions of Feasibility, Dilemmas, Perception, and Replacement. This robust measurement foundation enables a reliable evaluation of the structural model, in which the hypothesised relationships among these constructs are examined.

The construct of Replacement Intention warrants particular clarification. In this study, replacement intention does not represent a general intention to eliminate teachers, endorse institutional policy change, or broadly adopt AI technologies. Instead, it reflects students’ acceptance of generative AI performing selected instructional functions, such as explanations, feedback, and preparatory guidance, that are traditionally associated with teaching roles. The inclusion of multiple instructional scenarios was intended to capture this function-specific acceptance rather than heterogeneous behavioural intentions. Thus, the construct should be interpreted as an instructional role substitution appraisal, situated within the learning context of medical education, rather than as a generalised technology adoption or policy attitude.

These findings highlight perception as a key cognitive mechanism rather than a general attitude. In contrast, ethical dilemmas did not significantly moderate the feasibility–perception relationship, indicating that ethical concerns function more as direct constraints on acceptance rather than as modifiers of feasibility-based evaluations.

Discussion

This study examined how medical students evaluate generative AI as a potential substitute for selected instructional functions. The findings indicate that feasibility influences acceptance primarily through students’ evaluative perceptions, while ethical dilemmas constrain acceptance directly rather than altering feasibility-based evaluations. Together, these results highlight that acceptance of AI in medical education is shaped less by technical access alone and more by how students cognitively appraise AI’s instructional credibility within a professionally sensitive context. These findings contribute to the growing body of literature exploring AI adoption in medical education [40, 43] and extend understanding of how feasibility and ethical considerations shape acceptance of emerging technologies within professional learning environments.

The role of feasibility in shaping perceptions and acceptance

The significant effect of Feasibility on Perception (β = 0.295, p < 0.001) indicates that when medical students perceive generative AI as accessible, reliable, and easy to integrate into their learning routines, they are more likely to form favourable evaluative perceptions regarding its instructional value. This finding aligns with belief-based perspectives in technology acceptance research, which conceptualise feasibility-related beliefs as enabling conditions that support downstream evaluative judgments rather than as direct drivers of behavioural substitution [19]. Prior empirical work supports this interpretation. Dwivedi et al. [21, 22] reported that ease of use and facilitating conditions enhanced students’ reliance on generative AI primarily by shaping perceptions of usefulness and credibility. Similarly. [49] found that among medical students, feasibility-related factors influenced AI acceptance indirectly through evaluative perceptions, rather than through direct endorsement of instructional substitution. Studies in comparable educational contexts have likewise shown that usability and facilitating conditions support acceptance by enabling students to assess whether AI aligns with their learning needs and academic standards [2, 3, 55]. Although feasibility also demonstrated a statistically significant direct association with Replacement Intention in the present study (β = 0.137, p < 0.001), this effect should be interpreted as secondary and supportive, rather than as evidence that technical accessibility alone drives acceptance of AI as a teaching substitute. In medical education, where instruction is closely tied to professional judgment, mentorship, and responsibility, feasibility appears to create the conditions for evaluation rather than independently legitimising instructional substitution. Consistent with this view, prior research cautions that even highly usable AI systems may be viewed as insufficient substitutes for the relational and contextual dimensions of teaching [41]. Taken together, these findings suggest that in medical education, feasibility primarily serves as an enabling foundation that allows students to meaningfully evaluate the instructional credibility of generative AI. Enhancing digital infrastructure, institutional readiness, and instructional support may therefore strengthen students’ evaluative perceptions of AI. In contrast, acceptance of AI performing instructional functions remains contingent on broader pedagogical and professional considerations.

Mediation effect of perception

The mediation analysis (H3) indicates that perception significantly mediates the relationship between Feasibility and Replacement Intention (β = 0.093), demonstrating that feasibility alone does not directly translate into acceptance of generative AI performing instructional roles. Instead, feasibility enables students to engage with AI systems, which, in turn, allows them to form evaluative judgments about AI’s instructional usefulness, credibility, and reliability, judgments that ultimately shape their willingness to accept AI as a substitute for selected teaching functions. This mediated pathway aligns with the belief–evaluation logic underlying the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), which distinguishes between enabling beliefs (e.g., ease of use, facilitating conditions) and evaluative beliefs (e.g., perceived usefulness) when explaining downstream behavioural judgments [19].

In the present study, feasibility creates the conditions for meaningful engagement with generative AI. At the same time, perception reflects students’ cognitive evaluation of whether AI is capable of fulfilling instructional responsibilities traditionally associated with teachers. Empirical evidence supports this interpretation. Tangadulrat et al. [49] found that among medical students, accessibility and usability did not directly predict reliance on AI for learning support,rather, their effects operated through perceptions of usefulness and trust. Similarly, Wu et al. [53] reported that students’ perceptions of credibility and usefulness fully mediated the relationship between feasibility and AI adoption in higher education. Almalki et al. [4] also confirmed that facilitating conditions and usability predicted satisfaction and behavioural intention, indirectly through perceived usefulness, in a sample of Saudi medical students. Together, these findings suggest that feasibility enhances adoption primarily by forming positive perceptions regarding AI’s educational value.

Within medical education, this mediation is particularly salient. Acceptance of AI performing instructional functions, such as explanations, formative feedback, or preparatory guidance, requires students first to judge whether AI outputs meet professional standards of accuracy, contextual relevance, and pedagogical reliability. Given the ethical responsibilities and high-stakes nature of medical training, students are unlikely to accept instructional substitution based solely on technical accessibility. Instead, feasibility supports engagement, perception determines instructional legitimacy, and only then does replacement intention emerge.

Taken together, these findings highlight perception as the central cognitive mechanism translating feasibility into acceptance of AI-based instructional substitution. This underscores that technical feasibility is a necessary but insufficient condition for instructional replacement. Evaluative judgments regarding AI’s instructional credibility play a decisive role in shaping students’ acceptance in medical education.

The moderation effect of dilemmas

The moderation analysis (H5) indicated that perceived dilemmas did not significantly moderate the relationship between feasibility and perception (β = 0.045, p = 0.250). This finding suggests that ethical, pedagogical, and professional concerns did not alter how feasibility-related conditions shaped students’ evaluative perceptions of generative AI.

Importantly, this non-significant moderation does not imply that dilemmas are irrelevant in medical education. Rather, it indicates that ethical concerns operate at a different evaluative stage than feasibility-based judgments. Feasibility refers to whether AI tools are accessible, usable, and effectively integrated into learning activities. In contrast, dilemmas concern downstream concerns about professional responsibility, academic integrity, and the preservation of the human dimensions of teaching. As such, students appear to first form evaluative perceptions of AI’s instructional utility based on practical engagement before ethical considerations influence their willingness to accept AI in instructional roles.

This interpretation aligns with prior research in medical and health professions education. Consorti and D’Oria [17] reported that medical students often distinguish between recognising AI’s technical and instructional capabilities and endorsing its broader professional legitimacy. Similarly, Olohunfunmi and Khairuddin [42] reported that ethical risk perceptions had a weak moderating effect on technology acceptance in global higher education samples. In medical contexts, Consorti and D'Oria [17] observed that while students acknowledged dilemmas related to responsibility and authenticity, these concerns did not significantly reduce their openness to AI-assisted learning. These findings suggest that ethical dilemmas function as contextual constraints on acceptance, rather than as moderators of feasibility-based evaluative processes.

Within the context of medical education, this pattern is theoretically coherent. Medical training emphasises accountability, patient safety, and professional judgment, values that are more closely tied to decisions about whether AI should perform instructional functions, rather than to initial evaluations of whether AI is usable or effective. Consequently, dilemmas may shape replacement intention directly, without interfering with the earlier formation of evaluative perceptions based on feasibility.

Taken together, the absence of a moderating effect indicates that ethical dilemmas do not distort feasibility-driven evaluative judgments but instead operate as independent considerations that influence students’ acceptance of AI performing selected instructional functions. This finding reinforces the importance of addressing ethical concerns at the level of instructional role definition and professional boundaries, rather than at the level of technical feasibility.

Theoretical and practical implications

This study contributes to the emerging literature on generative AI in medical education by clarifying how students evaluate AI’s potential instructional role through a staged cognitive process. Rather than extending established technology acceptance models to domains they were not designed to explain, the study reconceptualises feasibility as an enabling condition that supports evaluative perception, which in turn shapes acceptance of AI performing selected instructional functions. The significant mediating role of perception demonstrates that technical feasibility alone is insufficient to explain acceptance of instructional substitution. Instead, medical students first translate feasibility into evaluative judgments concerning instructional usefulness, credibility, and reliability before considering whether AI can assume specific teaching-related functions. This finding extends technology acceptance research by distinguishing instructional role acceptance from general technology-use intention, an important distinction in professional education contexts where teaching involves ethical responsibility and professional identity formation. The non-significant moderating effect of dilemmas further refines theoretical understanding by showing that ethical and professional concerns do not interfere with feasibility-based evaluative judgments. Rather, dilemmas operate as contextual constraints on instructional role acceptance, not on cognitive evaluation. This supports a layered view of AI acceptance in medical education, in which practical engagement precedes ethical boundary-setting, rather than both processes occurring simultaneously.

Practical implications

From a practical perspective, the findings suggest that efforts to integrate generative AI into medical education should prioritise enhancing students’ evaluative perceptions of AI’s instructional value rather than focusing exclusively on technical deployment. Faculty-led demonstrations, structured pilot use in non-clinical instructional tasks (e.g., explanations, formative feedback, exam preparation), and guided exposure can help students meaningfully assess AI’s educational utility and credibility. The Importance–Performance Map Analysis further indicates that perception and feasibility represent the most influential leverage points for improving acceptance of AI-supported instruction. Institutional investments should therefore focus on improving usability, access, and pedagogically aligned implementation, rather than assuming that availability alone will generate acceptance. Although ethical dilemmas did not moderate feasibility-based evaluations, they remain critical for responsible adoption in medical education. These concerns should be addressed through explicit ethical framing, including discussion of accountability, academic integrity, and the limits of AI in mentorship and professional role modelling. Integrating ethical reflection alongside practical AI use may help students develop informed boundaries for when AI can support learning and when human instruction remains indispensable.

Limitations and future directions

While this study offers important insights into medical students' perceptions of generative AI as a potential replacement for teachers, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the data were collected exclusively from medical students, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to other health professions and academic disciplines. Future research should include participants from nursing, dentistry, and allied health programs to capture a more comprehensive picture of generative AI acceptance across the medical education field. Second, educators’ and curriculum designers’ perspectives were not incorporated, despite their attitudes and pedagogical readiness playing a crucial role in shaping the feasibility and ethical deployment of AI-based teaching. Including educators in future studies would provide a more balanced understanding of institutional and instructional implications. Finally, the cross-sectional nature of the data limits the ability to make causal inferences. Longitudinal or mixed-method designs could help trace how exposure to AI technologies influences the evolution of perceptions, ethical awareness, and long-term acceptance within medical learning environments.

Conclusion

This study examined medical students’ perceptions of generative artificial intelligence (AI) as a potential replacement for human teachers, focusing on the interrelationships among Feasibility, Perception, Dilemmas, and Replacement Intention. Using partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM), the findings demonstrated that both Feasibility and Perception significantly predict students’ acceptance of AI-based teaching. At the same time, perception mediates the relationship between Feasibility and Replacement. In contrast, Dilemmas did not moderate this relationship, suggesting that ethical or professional concerns play a limited role in the early formation of attitudes toward AI in education. These results collectively affirm the relevance of the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and Expectation–Confirmation Theory (ECT) in explaining how medical students evaluate and embrace emerging educational technologies. From a practical standpoint, the findings underscore the need for medical institutions to enhance the feasibility and perceived value of AI integration. Universities and teaching hospitals should invest in technological infrastructure, provide hands-on exposure to AI tools, and design training programs that build students’ confidence in AI’s reliability and educational potential. At the same time, structured discussions and ethics workshops can help students critically engage with dilemmas surrounding academic integrity, empathy, and accountability, ensuring that AI adoption remains pedagogically and ethically sound.
