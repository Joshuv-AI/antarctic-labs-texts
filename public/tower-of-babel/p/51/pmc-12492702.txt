The impact of spontaneous ventilation anesthesia on thrombosis risk for thoracic surgery: a beagle dog model study
Authors: Yuan Zeng, Weipeng Cai, Fei Cui, Ke Xu, Ying Huang, Wei Wang, Hui Liu, Jun Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients with NSCLC after surgery are at high risk of producing venous thromboembolism (VTE). The purpose of this study was to evaluate the effects of spontaneous ventilation (SV) anesthesia and one-lung ventilation (OLV) anesthesia methods on thrombosis risk for thoracic surgery.MethodsA thoracic surgery model of beagle dogs was established. Eight beagle dogs were randomly divided into two groups: four in the OLV and four in the SV groups. Levels of D-dimer during and after surgery were compared between the two groups. In addition, PAI-1, t-PA, TM, and PPBP levels in the plasma using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay were determined.ResultsThe D-dimer significantly increased one day after surgery in the OLV group (625.0 ± 153.5 vs. 2300.0 ± 922.1 μg/L, P = 0.034). However, the SV group showed no significant increase in D-dimer on postoperative day 1 (P > 0.05). Compared with the one-lung ventilation anesthesia, we found that there was no significant difference in D-dimer, PAI-1, t-PA, TM, and PPBP level during the operation (0, 2, 4, 6 h) and after the operation (day 1 and day 2) for SV anesthesia (P > 0.05). However, SV anesthesia significantly decreased the change in D-dimer (− 15 ± 255.9 vs. 1675 ± 1074.8 μg/L, P = 0.048) on postoperative day 1.ConclusionsCompared with the OLV anesthesia, there was no significant difference in thrombosis risk during the operation and the second day after the operation for SV anesthesia. However, the changes of D-dimer in SV anesthesia were significantly lower on the first postoperative day. Further investigation is warranted.Keywords: Spontaneous ventilation, D-dimer, Thrombosis

Introduction

Conventionally, Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) is carried out using general anesthesia with the insertion of a double-lumen endotracheal tube and One-lung ventilation (OLV) [1]. A characteristic of spontaneous ventilation (SV) VATS is that it does not use muscle relaxants to allow the patient to maintain a state of sedation and analgesia, which preserves the patient's spontaneous breathing without mechanical ventilation [2]. After surgery, patients with NSCLC are at high risk of producing venous thromboembolism (VTE), with previous studies reporting that the incidence of VTE exceeds 10% [3, 4]. Quantifying D-dimer levels serves an important role in the identification of patients who are at a heightened risk for VTE [5]. Thrombosis is a complex process that involves the interaction of multiple factors, including platelets, coagulation factors, endothelial cells, and fibrinolysis regulators [6]. Plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1), Thrombomodulin (TM), plasminogen activator (t-PA) and Pro-platelet basic protein (PPBP) are all involved in different aspects of this process [7–9]. Data from our center showed that SV anesthesia could significantly limit D-dimer levels after surgery for mediastinal tumors [10], suggesting that SV anesthesia may decrease the risk of venous thromboembolism or pulmonary emboli following surgery. However, no studies have evaluated these thrombotic markers in SV anesthesia, either in patients or animal models. Therefore, this study evaluated the impact of this anesthesia method on thrombosis risk by detecting intraoperative and postoperative thrombus indicators in a randomized controlled beagle dog model.

Methods

Animal preparation and grouping

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of First Affiliated Hospital of Guangzhou Medical University (approved NO. 2,020,166; Issued date 7/8/2020). Eight healthy beagles, males and females, weighing 12.0 ± 1 kg, were included. The animals were randomly divided into two groups: four in the OLV group and four in the SV group (random numbers based on computer).

Construction of the animal models

The experimental animals began fasting and being deprived of water at 10 p.m. the day before the experiment. A catheter was inserted into the left forelimb radial vein for intravenous injection of 3–4 mg/kg propofol. After sedation, animals were placed in the supine position and fixed to a conventional operating table for large animals. The limbs and maxillofacial region were fixed with elastic bandages, and then the skin was prepared. According to the animal’s reaction, propofol of 2–3 mg/kg was added as needed. Dogs were then endotracheal intubated. The beagle dogs were connected to an ECG monitor, a left femoral artery puncture was performed, a No. 20 single-lumen central venous catheter was inserted, and the pressure transducer was connected. Invasive arterial blood pressure was measured. The right external jugular vein was punctured and placed with a No. 20 single-lumen central venous catheter for infusion.

OLV Group Airway Management

A size 37 left double-lumen bronchial tube was used for endotracheal intubation. Under guidance of an electronic fiberoptic bronchoscope, the tube was advanced into the left main bronchus and secured after confirming proper positioning and absence of air leakage. Initial ventilation employed synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation (SIMV; tidal volume: 12 mL/kg, respiratory rate: 20 breaths/min, I:E ratio 1:1.5, FiO2: 100%). Before thoracotomy, ventilation was switched to volume control mode (tidal volume: 8 mL/kg, respiratory rate: 25 breaths/min; other parameters unchanged).

SV Group Airway Management [11]

A No. 7 single-lumen endotracheal tube was placed, with positioning confirmed in the trachea via electronic fiberoptic bronchoscope before fixation. SIMV mode was used for ventilation (parameters identical to OLV group’s initial settings). Mechanical ventilation was discontinued pre-thoracotomy, transitioning to spontaneous breathing.

Remifentanil and flurbiprofen were administered for intraoperative analgesia. After the operation, we covered the incision with yarn soaked in normal saline. After 6 h of SV anesthesia (after start of surgery), the chest would be closed, the thoracic drainage tube would be used for exhaust, the thoracic tube would not be retained, resuscitation and rewarming were performed, and the arterial tube was removed. After skin incision, we immediately collected the first blood sample. We collected 7 mL blood samples 0, 2, 4, and 6 h after thoracotomy as well as on the first and the second mornings after surgery. 2 ml blood samples were sent to Guangzhou Huayin Medical Laboratory Center for D-dimer testing.

ELISA

5 ml Blood was collected in a tube with EDTA at different time points. The plasma was separated by centrifugation for 10 min at 1000 g and frozen at − 80 °C until analysis. PAI-1, TM, t-PA, and PPBP levels were analyzed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits from Jianglai Biotechnology (China). ELISA was conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

The result evaluation is completed by an independent team without knowledge of the grouping. Data were analyzed by an independent statistical team. Differences in D-dimer, PAI-1, TM, t-PA, and PPBP between the two groups were compared by applying unpaired t-tests. A one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted to compare multiple groups, followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and the results are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. P-values < 0.05 were considered to be statistically significant.

D-dimer

OLV significantly increased postoperative day 1 D-dimer levels compared to baseline (625.0 ± 153.5 vs. 2300.0 ± 922.1 μg/L, P = 0.034). What’s more, there was no significant difference in D-dimer between SV group and OLV group (1032.5 ± 457.7 vs. 625 ± 153.5 μg/L, P = 0.137) 0 h during the surgery; D-dimer was not significantly different at 2, 4, 6 h after the start of the operation (1210 ± 470.8 vs. 887.5 ± 333, P = 0.311; 1297.5 ± 799.2 vs. 2630 ± 3323, P = 0.487; 2060 ± 1183 vs. 1655 ± 1632.6, P = 0.703, respectively). D-dimer was also not significantly different on postoperative day 1 and postoperative day 2 (1017.5 ± 251.3 vs. 2300 ± 922, P = 0.065; and 940 ± 328.1 vs. 910 ± 429.9, P = 0.916). However, SV anesthesia significantly decreased the change in D-dimer (− 15 ± 255.9 vs. 1675 ± 1074.8 μg/L, P = 0.048) on postoperative day 1 (Fig. 1).

Discussion

The overall incidence of postoperative VTE after VATS remains relatively high [4]. We found that there was no significant difference in the above thrombus indicators between the two groups during and after surgery except for D-dimer levels on postoperative day 1. Some studies have shown that SV anesthesia facilitated a more rapid recovery [10, 12]. However, we currently lack data on whether SV anesthesia is associated with the risk of VTE. In this study, we assessed the VTE risk of patients who underwent OLV and SV anesthesia by measuring the amount of D-dimer, PAI-1, t-PA, TM, and PPBP in plasma. We found that there was no significant difference in the above thrombus indicators between the two groups during and after surgery. However, SV anesthesia significantly decreased the change in D-dimer (P = 0.048) on postoperative day 1.

We modeled the two anesthesia methods with beagle dogs. We have previously simulated thoracic surgery in rabbits and found that lung injury may be more severe in the SV group if the operation time exceeds 4 h [13]. However, because the size of the rabbit is small, this result has been caused by surgical limitations. Therefore, in this study, we used large animals (dogs) to establish an SV anesthesia model and simulate the clinical conditions as closely as possible. Double-lumen endotracheal intubation, anesthetics, and anesthesia ventilation are basically consistent between animal models and clinical practice. Because thoracic surgeries are generally completed within 6 h and prolonged operative time is a risk factor associated with VTE and trauma [14], we used animal models to simulate the effect of 6-h SV anesthesia on patients. During surgery, the carbon dioxide levels in the SV anesthesia group were higher than those in the OLV group, which is consistent with previous data [13, 15]. Hypercapnia may induce a hypercoagulable state [16], yet in our experiment, no apparent coagulation abnormalities were noted in the SV group. Patients with pulmonary artery hypertension (especially those with right heart failure) exhibit poor tolerance to intraoperative hypercapnia, thus it is advisable to avoid SV anesthesia for this patient cohort. In addition, we found that there was no significant difference in vital signs, including heart rate, oxygen saturation, and mean arterial pressure, between SV group and OLV group during 2 and 4 h of surgery. However, the heart rate in the SV anesthesia group significantly decreased after 6 h of surgery, which may be related to prolonged elevated carbon dioxide levels. Therefore, our research suggests that SV anesthesia is very safe for thoracic surgery operations within 4 h, but more than 6 h of surgery must pay attention to and promote carbon dioxide emissions as soon as possible.

PAI-1 is a regulator of fibrinolysis, which is the process by which blood clots are broken down. PAI-1 binds to and inhibits t-PA, preventing the conversion of plasminogen to plasmin, which is responsible for the breakdown of fibrin clots [17]. Higher levels of PAI-1 have been linked to a heightened risk of thrombosis [18]. t-PA is a serine protease that facilitates the conversion of plasminogen into plasmin [19]. Plasmin breaks down fibrin clots, which helps to prevent thrombosis. TM is a glycoprotein that is expressed on the surface of endothelial cells [20]. It plays a critical role in the regulation of coagulation by binding to thrombin and altering its substrate specificity. When thrombin binds to TM, it activates protein C, which in turn inhibits coagulation by inactivating factors Va and VIIIa. PPBP, also known as platelet factor 4 (PF4), is a chemokine that is released by activated platelets [21]. PPBP has been shown to promote platelet activation and aggregation, which can contribute to the formation of blood clots. In this study, we found no significant differences in plasma levels of PAI-1, t-PA, TM, and PPBP between the SV group and the OLV group during and after surgery. What’s more, There was no significant difference in D-dimer between the two groups during and on the second day after surgery. The results at least indicate that there may be no significant difference in the risk of VTE between the SV and OLV groups during the operation and the second day after the operation.

Numerous studies have associated elevated D-dimer levels with increased VTE risk in postoperative thoracic surgery patients [22, 23]. Prospective comparisons of several VTE risk prediction tools have shown that increased D-dimer is associated with a higher risk of VTE among cancer patients [24, 25]. Our animal study showed that D-dimer was significantly increased 24 h after surgery in OLV groups, but not in the SV group. A study by Yao et al. [26] also indicated that D-dimer was significantly increased 24 h after surgery in both the thoracoscopy and thoracotomy groups. Thus, these findings strongly confirmed that the risk of VTE after thoracic surgery was significantly increased in OLV groups 24 h after surgery; thus, we need to pay more attention to the prevention of VTE for OLV group. For example, anticoagulation therapy, early activities, and lower extremity neurological therapy need to be evaluated and implemented by doctors as soon as possible.

Moreover, our study showed that the changes in D-dimer in the SV group on the first day after surgery are lower than those in the OLV group, which deserves further investigation. The study by Liang et al. [10] indicated that SV anesthesia was associated with lower postoperative plasma D-dimer levels in mediastinal tumor patients. This result indicates that anesthesia mode has a significant effect on postoperative D-dimer. There were several possible explanations for the lower postoperative D-dimer among patients who underwent SV. First, as the SV group did not use muscle relaxants, the patients recovered faster after anesthesia, resulting in faster recovery of normal blood circulation. Recently, a randomized trial reported that SV was connected with shorter post-anesthesia care unit recovery time, extubation time, and food intake time [27]. Additionally, our previous clinical study found lower levels of inflammation in the SV group, suggesting less anesthesia trauma [28]. Further clinical research is of great importance to explore the potential advantage of SV.

This study had some limitations. First, the absence of ultrasound-based thrombus detection limits our ability to confirm VTE incidence. Prospective cohort studies measuring clinical VTE outcomes are therefore needed. The main reason was that the drainage tube may lead to accidents after surgery on animals, so we adopted the technique without a chest tube. Additionally, because the canine laryngeal mask is prone to aspiration and reflux, the SV group still used a single-lumen tube inserted into the trachea to achieve SV anesthesia. Despite so many limitations, we still engineered a unique model to evaluate the risk of VTE during SV and OLV through animal experiments. Therefore, these findings were fairly limited, and further investigations are needed.

In conclusion, we established a beagle SV anesthesia model. Compared with the one-lung ventilation anesthesia, we found that there was no significant difference in thrombosis risk during the operation and the second day after the operation for SV anesthesia. However, the changes of D-dimer in SV anesthesia were significantly lower on the first postoperative day. Further investigation is warranted.
