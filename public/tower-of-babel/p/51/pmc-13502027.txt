Promoter contacts by TFB and TFEα during archaeal transcription initiation
Authors: Michael Micorescu, Kimberly Sheffield, Vlad Geshka, Hannah Tierney, Michael S. Bartlett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTranscription initiation by archaeal RNA polymerase (RNAP) is assisted by transcription factors, TBP, TFB, and TFEα, which show sequence, structural and functional homology to eukaryotic TBP, TFIIB, and TFIIEα. Previous low-resolution cross-linking studies of transcription complexes from the hyperthermophile archaeon Pyrococcus furiosus showed that the transcription bubble DNA makes extensive contacts with TFB and TFEα. Here, contacts between TFB and TFEα surfaces and promoter DNA are mapped with amino acid-to-base precision, using a high-resolution cross-link mapping method. The non-canonical amino acid p-benzoyl phenylalanine (Bpa) was incorporated into TFB1 or TFEα at several positions, transcription complexes were formed and cross-links induced, and the positions of cross-links were mapped by primer extension. In transcription pre-initiation complexes forming a transcription bubble (PICs), the TFB1 B-reader helix and loop conserved sequences interact with the DNA transcribed (T) strand near the upstream side of the transcription bubble, while the B-linker strand conserved sequence interacts with both the transcribed and the non-transcribed (NT) strands. In a 5 nucleotide initially transcribing complex (ITC), cross-links to TFB1 changed for the B-reader but not the B-linker, suggesting scrunching of part of the T strand in the RNAP main channel. The TFEα winged helix cross-linked the NT strand at the upstream edge of the transcription bubble in the PIC. In the ITC, the NT strand changed position dramatically relative to the TFEα wing tip, indicating that the DNA NT strand can bulge out of the main channel as transcription begins and downstream DNA is pulled in. The cross-linking data were compared with structural models of the archaeal PIC and ITC, and indicate that interactions between TFB conserved motifs and DNA are conserved from archaea to humans, while archaeal TFEα contacts to DNA are apparently dissimilar from what is seen in human RNAP II initiation complexes.

Introduction

The archaeal transcription machinery is highly homologous to the core transcription machinery in eukaryotes, while notably less similar to that of bacteria. Archaeal RNAPs contain from 11 to 13 subunits, and are most closely related to the eukaryotic RNAPs, although the core structure of cellular RNAPs from all three domains of life is clearly similar [1–4]. In addition, the basal transcription factors for archaeal RNAP (TBP, TFB, and TFEα) are closely related in structure and function to a core subset of the eukaryotic RNAP II basal transcription factors (TBP, TFIIB, and TFIIEα) [5–12]. In some cases, eukaryotic and archaeal transcription factors and RNAP subunits can even substitute for one another [13,14]. The relative simplicity of the archaeal transcription system and its close relationship to the core RNAP II transcription machinery makes the archaeal system a useful model for basal eukaryotic-type transcription mechanisms (e.g [15]).

Archaeal transcription begins with basal factors TBP and TFB binding to the TATA box and surrounding sequences in the promoter [16,17]. The TBP/TFB/DNA ternary complex then recruits RNA polymerase (RNAP) through interactions between the TFB and the RNAP dock domain [18]. A section of promoter DNA near the transcription start site is opened by RNAP with the assistance of TFB and TFEα. In the resulting pre-initiation complex, transcribed strand DNA is positioned next to the RNAP active site, allowing nucleotide polymerization and the start of transcription [15,19,20].

Archaeal TFB shares a high degree of sequence and structural homology to eukaryotic TFIIB, which plays a critical role in RNAP II transcription initiation [11]. Like TFIIB, archaeal TFB contains separate structural domains with distinct roles in the initiation process [15]. The TFB C-terminal domain (CTD) consists of two cyclin repeats, the second of which contains a conserved helix-turn-helix motif involved in sequence-specific recognition of the B-recognition element (BRE), a conserved promoter sequence upstream of the TATA box [17,21]. The TFB N-terminal domain (NTD) contains a conserved zinc ribbon motif that plays a key role in recruiting RNAP to the promoter [18,22,23]. In addition, the archaeal TFB and eukaryotic TFIIB NTDs play critical roles in the transcription mechanism following RNAP recruitment. A region of TFIIB called the B-reader/B-linker, or alternatively, the B-finger, interacts with the main channel of RNAP II [15,24–26]. The B-reader is located in proximity to the active site and contains a helix (residues 40–47 in P. furiosus TFB1), loop (residues 48–58), and strand (residues 59–64), while the B-linker is closer to the upstream edge of the transcription bubble, and contains a strand (residues 71–76) and helix (residues 84–98) [15,27]. Cryo-electron microscopy structures of RNAP II pre-initiation open complexes also show that the TFIIB B-reader helix and loop closely approach the T strand from about −4 to −8 relative to the transcription start site. In addition, the B-linker strand and helix were seen to span the upstream side of the transcription bubble, approaching both the T and the NT strand of the promoter from about −7 to −11 [28,29]. The positioning of the B-reader suggests that it may play a role in DNA start site scanning and placement of the DNA transcribed strand at the active site. Recent structures indicate that the B-reader also interacts with the growing RNA strand following initiation [30,31].

Function of the archaeal TFB NTD was previously examined through mutational analysis, as well as characterization of a naturally occurring TFB variant lacking conserved NTD sequences. Point mutations in the TFB B-reader helix and loop disrupt promoter opening and transcription activity, as do deletions and multiple alanine substitutions in the B-linker strand and helix [15,18]. In addition, P. furiosus TFB2, a TFB paralog with highly divergent B-reader and B-linker sequences can function in transcription, although it confers lower transcription activity than TFB1. Transcription complexes formed with TFB2 are deficient in promoter opening, and this deficiency can be partly overcome by assembly of transcription complexes on partially melted DNA templates or with the addition of TFEα [32]. Thus, the archaeal TFB B-reader and B-linker conserved sequences are critical for efficient formation of the PIC, although their location relative to DNA is only partly defined.

Previously, DNA-to-protein cross-linking experiments established that along with several RNAP subunits, the N-terminus of P. furiosus TFB1 is close to the DNA transcribed and non-transcribed strands in the transcription bubble [32–35]. In addition, a model for an archaeal PIC derived from FRET analysis indicates that the position of the transcribed and non-transcribed strands, as well as the TFB B-reader and B-linker sequences, are likely to be very similar to what is seen in RNAP II PICs [28,29,36]. Following this, a low-resolution protein-to-DNA cross-linking study confirmed proximity of the TFB1 B-reader to the T strand in initiation complexes, and illustrated movement of the B-reader relative to DNA during early RNA synthesis [37]. However, the precise positioning of the archaeal B-reader and B-linker conserved regions of TFB1 relative to promoter DNA during transcription initiation is not clear.

Archaeal TFEα is homologous to the N-terminus of eukaryotic TFIIEα, and while not required for initiation in vitro, it is essential in vivo [38]. TFEα provides a modest activation of transcription in vitro [8,39], and participates in PIC formation and the transition from initiation to elongation [20,40]. TFEα interacts with the RNAP stalk through its CTD, and its NTD winged helix motif spans the RNAP main channel. Its binding to RNAP influences the positioning of the RNAP clamp, which may mediate its role in PIC formation [41]. We previously showed that the TFEα winged helix motif interacts with NT strand DNA at the upstream side of the transcription bubble prior to initiation [42], but it isn’t clear whether this interaction contributes to TFEα’s role in promoter opening or the transition to elongation complexes.

In this study, we use protein-to-DNA cross-linking and precision mapping to identify interactions between TFB1 and TFEα surfaces and promoter DNA during transcription initiation. We replaced specific amino acids in the TFB1 B-reader and B-linker, as well as amino acids TFEα amino acids in the winged helix motif, with a cross-linkable non-canonical amino acid, p-benzoyl-L-phenylalanine (Bpa) [43]. We assembled transcription complexes with the Bpa-containing TFB1 and TFEα variants, induced cross-linking, and mapped the cross-links in the DNA by primer extension (Figure 1). This protein-to-nucleic acid cross-linking approach provides high-resolution information about surface proximities during the steps of archaeal transcription initiation, and complements existing structural studies of archaeal and eukaryotic transcription complexes. Materials and Methods

Protein production

P. furiosus TFB1 and TFEα variants containing site-specific placement of p-benzoyl phenylalanine (Bpa) were made using an engineered tRNA-aminoacyl tRNA synthetase system, as described previously [37,43]. 6xHis-tagged versions of TFB1 and TFEα were created that contain amber codons at the desired positions: W44, R52, T58, E74, M85, or F192 for TFB1, and N49 and W76 for TFEα. Expression vectors for these variants were transformed into a BL21 expression strain carrying suppressor tRNA and aaRS genes on the pSupBpaRS-6TRN plasmid [43]. The mutant proteins were overexpressed in 2xYT medium supplemented with 1 mM p-benzoyl-L-phenylalanine and purified using standard histidine-tag purification protocols. Purified proteins were concentrated and buffers swapped to storage buffer (500 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-Cl pH 8, 1 mM EDTA) using 10 kD cutoff filtration units. Purified proteins were quantified on an SDS-PAGE gel by comparison with a BSA standard following staining with Coomassie Blue (Figure S3). Recombinant TBP was prepared as described previously [32]. Native RNAP was prepared from Pyrococcus furiosus cultures as described [44], and was a generous gift from Winfried Hausner (University of Regensburg).

Protein-DNA cross-linking

Transcription initiation complexes were assembled in a volume of 25 microliters, with 40 nM linear gdh DNA template, extending from −95 to + 85 relative to the transcription start site. The reactions contained 120 nM TBP, 40 nM RNAP, 240 nM TFB1 wild type, W44Bpa, R52Bpa, T58Bpa, E74Bpa, M85Bpa, or F192Bpa as specified, and 240 nM TFEα N49Bpa or W76Bpa as specified. Where indicated, GTP, CTP, ATP, and UTP were added to 500 micromolar. Reactions were overlaid with mineral oil, and photochemical cross-linking was induced at 65°C by exposing the open tubes to UV light (365 nm) for 1 hour (Figures 2 and 3) or 10 hours (Figure 4). Incubation of the +5 ITC for up to 10 hours yields increasing amounts of abortive 5 nucleotide products, and does not inhibit the transcription machinery from producing full-length transcripts, given the opportunity (Figure S7). P. furiosus growth has been observed over a broad range of temperatures, from 70°C to 103°C, with highest growth rates seen between 90°C and 100°C [47]. Previous in vitro analyses of P. furiosus transcription components have shown that transcription factors and RNAP form initiation complexes that accomplish promoter opening and site-specific initiation on linear DNA templates at 65–70°C (see e.g. [16,20,33,34,48], with the lower temperatures avoiding complications of template denaturation and RNA instability seen at higher temperatures [49]. Cross-links were detected by primer extension as follows. Mineral oil was removed and 5 microliters paramagnetic Ni+2-NTA beads in phosphate wash buffer (WBB: 50 mM Na-phosphate pH 8.0, 300 mM NaCl, 0.01% TWEEN-20) were added to allow binding to 6xHis-tagged TFB or TFEα. Gels were imaged using a Typhoon Trio, set for detection of Cy5 or Cy3 fluorophores. Each cross-linking reaction was performed at least three times, and representative data is shown.

Structural modeling

Hybrid models for the archaeal PIC and ITC were created as follows. For the PIC, structural coordinates for an archaeal RNAP/TFEα model (PDB 6kf9 [42]) were superimposed with a human PIC model (PDB 5iyb [28]). Human chain A amino acids 398–597 (encompassing the RNAP active site), was structurally aligned to archaeal chain A using ChimeraX Matchmaker with default parameters [45]. The alignment score was 510.9, with RMSD between 166 pruned atom pairs of 1.009 angstroms; (across all 198 pairs: 1.620). The final hybrid PIC model retained archaeal RNAP and TFEα, along with human promoter DNA, TBP, and TFIIB. For the ITC, the same method was used, except with a human ITC containing a 6 nt transcript (PDB 5iyd [28]). For this alignment, the score was 496.5, with RMSD between 167 pruned atom pairs of 1.039 angstroms (across all 198 pairs: 1.594). The final hybrid ITC model retained archaeal RNAP and TFEα, along with human promoter DNA, RNA, TBP, and TFIIB.

Results

Construction of Bpa-containing mutant proteins

Transcription initiation in archaea is assisted by the transcription factors TFB and TFEα, which play roles in RNAP recruitment as well as promoter opening and initiation. The functional details are unclear, in part because the precise positioning of these proteins in transcription complexes is not known. To investigate the position of transcription factors relative to DNA, we used a cross-linking approach in which the non-canonical amino acid p-benzoyl-L-phenylalanine (Bpa) was incorporated into the surface of interest. Briefly, site-directed mutagenesis is used to replace a specific amino acid residue codon with an amber stop codon. During translation, the amber codon is recognized by an engineered tRNA charged with Bpa, which is inserted into the polypeptide at that position [43,50]. Bpa contains a benzophenone R-group, and when exposed to UV light, its activated carbonyl oxygen can form a covalent cross-link to C-H bonds in neighboring macromolecular surfaces within a 3 angstrom radius [51–54] (Figure 1). Therefore, detection of a Bpa cross-link to a neighboring molecule indicates close proximity.

Several TFB1 and TFEα mutants were made containing Bpa substitutions. TFB1 variants include Bpa at: W44 in the B-reader helix conserved sequence, R52 and T58 in the B-reader loop, E74 in the B-linker strand, M85 in the B-linker helix, and F192 in cyclin repeat I (Figure 2(B)). TFEα variants include Bpa at N49, in the helix part of the winged helix motif, and W76, in the wing tip (Figure 4(A)). To determine if TFB1 and TFEα mutants containing Bpa were active in directing transcription initiation by RNAP, we compared wild type and mutant proteins using a standard in vitro run-off transcription assay with the well-characterized gdh promoter. All mutant variants were able to direct transcription with little to no effect on transcription efficiency (Figure S1 and [37,42].

Cross-linking mapped by primer extension in a known structure

In a previous study, we found that mapping Bpa cross-links by proximity to site-specific DNA radiolabel provides a general estimate of the position of transcription factor surfaces relative to promoter DNA sequence [37]. However, only a few positions in the promoter were tested, since a separate radiolabeled promoter variant had to be made for each position tested, making a detailed interrogation of DNA contacts laborious. In addition, interpretation of the cross-linking results was complicated by low-level background radiolabeling of other proteins that do not contain Bpa.

To gain a more precise profile of transcription factor surface contacts with DNA, we used an alternative mapping method that employs primer extension following cross-linking. A labeled primer is annealed to one of the DNA strands and extended with DNA polymerase. Covalent cross-links to the DNA block primer extension, giving DNA products that when compared to a sequencing ladder allow precise mapping of the cross-link position. This approach has been previously used in mapping the path of DNA in bacterial transcription complexes (for example, see [55]). Our first attempts to use primer extension to map Bpa cross-links in archaeal transcription complexes were unsuccessful, since the cross-linking signals were obscured by spontaneous primer extension pause and termination events independent of cross-linking. To reduce background, the 6xHis-tag at the N-terminus of the TFB1 and TFEα Bpa variants was used to selectively isolate these proteins along with covalently attached DNA, followed by extensive washing to remove excess non-cross-linked DNA (Materials and Methods).

Using the modified primer extension protocol, we addressed the question of proximity of TFB1 F192Bpa to DNA in TBP-TFB1-promoter complexes (Figure 2(C)). Promoter DNA (gdhP) was incubated with TBP and wild type or F192Bpa TFB1, in the absence versus presence of UV irradiation. TFB1 was isolated by binding to Ni-NTA beads, excess DNA was washed away, and fluorescent primer extension gave a read-out of cross-links to the T strand. In the absence of UV irradiation, neither wild type nor F192Bpa showed primer extension products (Figure 2(C), lanes 1 and 2). Treatment with UV revealed a strong primer extension product at −20 for F192Bpa but not wild-type TFB1, indicating Bpa-dependent cross-links at this region of the promoter (lanes 3 and 4). Comparison with the sequencing ladder indicates the primary stop is at −20, with a weaker stop at −19. This is consistent with previous radiolabel transfer mapping showing that this surface of TFB1 is close to −19T [37], as well as with a more extensive radiolabel transfer survey showing proximity to −22 and −19T, but not −30, −26, −24, −17, or 15T (Figure S2), and with a crystal structure model of TBP and TFB1 bound to a TATA box (Figure 2(D)). Thus the primer extension mapping method provides a precise estimate of cross-link position in DNA, with less labor compared to the radiolabel proximity method.

High-resolution cross-link mapping of TFB1 B-reader and B-linker DNA contacts

We sought to confirm and extend our understanding of the positioning of the TFB1 N-terminus relative to promoter DNA. Our previous low-resolution Bpa cross-linking study focused only on T strand contacts, although in earlier studies DNA-to-protein cross-linking indicated that TFB1 contacts both the T and NT strands in the transcription bubble [34,35].

To gain a more complete picture of DNA contacts made by the B-reader and B-linker conserved sequences, we made transcription complexes using TFB1 variants with Bpa in the B-reader helix (W44), the B-reader loop (R52 and T58), the B-linker strand (E74), and the B-linker helix (M85, Figure 2(B)). Following PIC formation, cross-links were induced, and reactions processed by washing and primer extension followed by PAGE (Figure 3(A)). All but one of the TFB1 variants tested cross-linked to the T strand. As in Figure 2, the strongest T strand F192Bpa primer extension product was at −20 (Figure 3(A), lane 1). The B-reader helix Bpa at W44 showed cross-linking extension products at −6 and 7, while a B-reader loop Bpa at R52 gave extension products at −7 and −8, and weakly at −6 (lanes 2 and 3). These results are consistent with previous radiolabel proximity mapping results showing that R52Bpa cross-links near −4 of the T strand [37]. The other B-reader loop Bpa (T58) gave a series of extension products from −6 to −10 (lane 4). The B-linker strand Bpa at E74 gave strong extension products at −9 and −10, and weakly at −8 (lane 5). The B-linker helix Bpa at M85 gave no extension products, indicating it does not cross-link to the T strand (lane 6).

For this set of TFB1 variants, there was limited cross-linking to the NT strand. TFB1 F192Bpa cross-linking gave strong primer extension products at −16 and −17, with weaker products at −18 and −20, consistent with radiolabel transfer mapping (compare Figure 3(A), lane 7 with Figure S2, lanes 1–10). The B-reader helix and loop Bpa substitutions (W44, R52, and T58) did not produce significant primer extension products (lanes 8–10). The B-linker strand Bpa at E74 gave significant primer extension products at positions −4, −5, and −6 (lane 11), indicating that this region of TFB1 likely contributed to NT strand transcription bubble cross-links previously observed for TFB1 [34]. Finally, the B-linker helix Bpa M85 showed no primer extension products, indicating that it does not cross-link the NT strand (lane 12).

Cross-linking by the B-reader and B-linker following transcription initiation

As RNAP begins RNA synthesis to make an initially transcribing complex (ITC), the DNA transcribed strand must move relative to the RNAP active site, and the RNAP main channel becomes occupied by the RNA-DNA duplex. The cross-linking results in Figures 2 and 3 show that the TFB1 B-reader is close to the T strand DNA prior to initiation, and the B-linker strand is close to both the T and NT strands. In addition, B-reader helix and loop cross-links to the T strand persist in initially transcribing complexes with transcripts as long as 9 nt [37]. However, it is unknown precisely how these TFB1 structures may change position relative to DNA during synthesis of short RNA products. To examine this, transcription complexes were assembled using TFB1 with Bpa at F192, W44, R52, T58, or E74, and RNA synthesis was allowed, by providing GTP, CTP, and ATP to make a 5-mer, or all four NTPs to make a full-length transcript. Cross-linking was induced, and cross-links to the T strand were detected by fluorescent primer extension (Figure 3(B)).

In the absence of NTPs, the primer extension pattern for each Bpa-substituted TFB1 matched those previously observed (compare Figure 3(B), lanes 1, 4, 7, 10, 11, and 14, with Figure 3(A), lanes 5–9). In an initially transcribing complex (ITC) with a 5-mer transcript (pppGpCpCpApA), the primer extension products changed for some but not all TFB1 variants. TFB1 F192Bpa cross-links remained unchanged between the PIC and ITC (Figure 3(B), lanes 1 and 2). However, W44Bpa shifted downstream from −7 and −6 in the PIC to −4 and −3 in the ITC (lanes 4 and 5), suggesting that the B-reader helix maintains a fixed position relative to RNAP, while the T strand DNA moves to accommodate incoming downstream DNA. A similar result is seen for R52Bpa, which shifts from its original position at −8, −7, and −6 in the PIC, to predominantly −4 and −3 in the ITC (lanes 7 and 8). The primer extension signal also increases in the ITC for this Bpa position. For T58Bpa, there is a similar shift of cross-links downstream from −10, −9, −8, −7, and −6 in the PIC to −4, −3, and −2 in the ITC, although some of the upstream cross-links (−9, −8, −7 and −6) are retained in the ITC, suggesting more than one possible position relative to DNA for this part of the B-reader loop (lanes 11 and 12). Interestingly, less movement is observed for E74Bpa in the PIC versus ITC. There is a slight downstream shift from −10, −9, and −8 (PIC) to −9, −8, and −7 (ITC), but signal is still retained at −10 (lanes 14 and 15). In short, in the shift from PIC to ITC, the position of cyclin repeat 1 (F192) is unchanged, while B-reader helix and loop (W44, R52, and T58) move downstream on the DNA 3–4 bases, and the B-linker strand (E74) moves about 1 base downstream.

When all four nucleotides are added to these transcription complexes, RNAP can achieve promoter clearance and synthesize the run-off transcript from the promoter template. The ITC converts to an elongation complex, the TFB1 B-reader and B-linker conserved regions disengage from RNAP and DNA, no cross-linking occurs (Figure 3(B), lanes 6, 9, 13, and 16). Cross-linking by F192Bpa is retained, although somewhat reduced compared to the PIC and ITC (lanes 2 and 3), representing a subset of TBP-TFB1-promoter complexes that remain attached to the TATA box following promoter escape by RNAP, or that never recruited a functional RNAP.

Cross-linking by TFEα in the PIC and ITC

We have previously observed that TFEα is close to the DNA NT strand at the upstream end of the transcription bubble [34,42]. In TFEα Bpa cross-links mapped by radiolabel proximity, the strongest cross-linking was seen with W76Bpa at the tip of the wing in the N-terminal winged helix motif, with very slight cross-linking observed with N49Bpa in the alpha helix at the base of the wing ([42] and Figure 4(A)). To gain a more detailed picture of how the TFEα winged helix interacts with NT strand DNA, we made both PIC and ITC transcription complexes with these Bpa-containing TFEα variants. Cross-linking was induced, and cross-links were mapped by primer extension (Figure 4(B)). In the PIC, N49Bpa showed primer extension products at −12, −13, and −14 of the NT strand, consistent with previous low-resolution mapping [42]. In the ITC, the extension product at −14 was lost, but −12 and −13 were preserved, indicating DNA interactions with the alpha helix at the TFEα wing base remain largely unchanged (Figure 4(B), lanes 1 and 2). The wing tip tells a different story. In the PIC, W44Bpa gave extension products on the NT strand at positions −10 and −11, consistent with previous low-resolution mapping [42], while in the ITC, the extension products change and shift downstream, with weak cross-links to −8, −9, −10, and −11, and strong cross-links to −4 and −6 (lanes 3 and 4). This indicates that interactions between the TFEα wing tip and NT strand DNA change significantly between the PIC and ITC.

Discussion

We have completed a pilot study using protein-to-DNA cross-linking and a primer extension mapping technique to define the positions of the conserved archaeal TFB1 B-reader and B-linker structures, and the conserved TFEα winged helix structure relative to DNA in transcription complexes, with amino-acid-to-base pair resolution.

Mapping DNA-interacting surfaces of protein by cross-linking

The two cross-link mapping approaches used here and in our previous study [37] provide very similar information about protein surface localization relative to DNA. The primer extension method gives more precision in mapping DNA sequences closest to the Bpa surface and is far less labor intensive for scanning long segments of DNA. This method has been extensively used for studies of bacterial transcription initiation complexes, e.g [55–57]; however, in our experiments, the relatively low yield of cross-linked DNA required additional enrichment steps to remove free DNA and reduce primer extension background noise. The radiolabel proximity mapping technique is useful if the general location of DNA interaction is known, since it allows detection of cross-linked DNA with high sensitivity, and no enrichment steps. More generally, both cross-linking techniques are applicable to any protein-nucleic acid interaction and provide a powerful experimental approach for investigating the dynamics of protein-nucleic acid complexes.

Position and role of TFB during PIC formation

Biochemical, genetic, and structural data have provided a wealth of detail for how transcription initiation complexes function in archaea and eukaryotes. After RNAP is recruited to the promoter, the DNA duplex partly unwinds, forming the transcription bubble, in which transcribed and non-transcribed strands follow separate paths. The transcribed strand is loaded into the active site cleft of the polymerase where it base-pairs with incoming nucleotides and the growing RNA, while the non-transcribed strand is directed away from the T strand, rejoining the template strand at the edge of the transcription bubble following separation of the transcript from the template DNA strand [58]. Given the highly conserved transcription mechanism and its importance to gene regulation, there is much interest in understanding the details of the formation of the transcription bubble, the determinants of the transcription start site selection, what initially transcribing complexes look like, and the events that lead to the formation of the stable elongation complex following initiation. Archaeal TFB and TFEα conserved motifs play critical roles in these stages and transitions during transcription initiation [15,18,20,32,40,42], but the precise function of these motifs is unclear, in part because of a lack of information regarding their positioning relative to DNA in the archaeal transcription complex.

To assist in interpretation of the cross-linking results from this study, we compared cross-linking signals with existing structural models of transcription complexes. Several archaeal RNAP and transcription complex structures have been reported (see, for example [2,42,59–61], with some that show RNAP interacting with promoter DNA and transcription factors. However, none of the structures include a high-resolution picture of the full PIC with TBP and TFB. Therefore, we created an archaeal/eukaryotic PIC model, starting with a structure containing RNAP and TFEα from the archaeon Thermococcus kodakarensis, a close relative of P. furiosus (PDB ID 6KF9) [42]. Since the structure contains only a small portion of DNA in the main channel, and lacks TBP and TFB, we looked for eukaryotic transcription complex structures to use for modeling the archaeal PIC and ITC DNA along with TBP, and TFB. A 2016 study of human RNAP II initiation complexes reported the structure of a PIC at 3.9 angstrom resolution, including both strands of DNA, and all TFIIB motifs present in the RNAP main channel (PDB ID 5IYB) [28]. This structural model clearly illustrates the proximity of the TFIIB B-reader and B-linker motifs in the context of DNA in the PIC. To create a working model of the archaeal PIC, we superimposed the archaeal and human structures through structural alignment of the RNAP largest subunits from each (Materials and Methods), generating a hybrid model containing the archaeal RNAP and TFEα, along with human DNA, TBP, and TFIIB. The human nucleic acids, along with TBP and TFIIB, fit to the archaeal RNAP/TFEα structure with essentially no clashes, providing a useful model for visualizing the cross-linking results from this study. The hybrid structure is shown in Figure 5(A), with RNAP subunit B removed in the right-hand panel to allow visualization of the DNA and TFIIB in the main channel. The cross-linking data presented here agrees well with the positioning of the B-reader helix and B-reader loop, and B-linker strand in the RNAP II PIC (Figure 5(B)). The archaeal B-reader helix and loop are in close proximity to the template strand, and are not close to the non-template strand, while the B-linker strand is close to both T and NT strands (Figure 3(A,B)). Our data are consistent with the model in which the B-reader helix is poised to interact with the template strand in the transcription bubble, upstream of the transcription start site. Thus, the archaeal B-reader may participate in stabilizing the PIC by guiding the template DNA toward the active site and may also help prevent the re-association of the T strand with the NT strand, and the B-linker strand conserved sequence may be positioned to stabilize the upstream fork of the transcription bubble in the PIC and ITC.

The position of TFB in the ITC

The synthesis of a short RNA transcript leads to structural changes in the RNAP active site channel, as it accommodates the transcript as well as additional downstream DNA. The archaeal ITC analyzed here was formed at the gdh promoter in the presence of a limiting set of nucleotides (G, C, and A) that allowed synthesis of a 5 nt transcript, and is likely similar to the eukaryotic ITC and the escape commitment intermediate proposed for RNAP II [28,62,63]. The archaeal ITC is markedly stabilized relative to the PIC, likely reflecting a significant structural change relative to the PIC [33]. We reasoned that if TFB were involved in the structural transition and stabilization, its position might change between the PIC and ITC states. The experiments presented here indicate that synthesis of a 5 nt transcript caused variable movement of the all TFB1 sites tested (except F192) relative to the T strand DNA. The B-reader helix and loop motifs interact with DNA 3−4 bases downstream of the interactions observed in the PIC, while the B-linker strand interactions with T strand DNA only move about a single base downstream (Figure 3).

The human RNAP II study that illustrated the PIC structure also included a structure of an ITC core (without TFIIS) [28], made by assembling RNAP II and selected transcription factors with promoter DNA containing a 19 base mismatch region to simulate an extended transcription bubble, and a 6 nucleotide RNA to serve as a nascent transcript. The ITC structure includes all nucleic acids and all TFIIB motifs present in the main channel of the RNAP II. The upstream end of the transcription bubble is at the same position relative to RNAP and transcription factors as it is in the PIC, while the downstream end of the transcription bubble is extended by 6 bases to accommodate the RNA nascent transcript, causing scrunching of the T strand DNA. Since the archaeal ITC used in this study contained a 5 nt transcript, we reasoned that the human RNAP II ITC would provide a useful model for interpreting the cross-linking data from Figure 3(B). As with the PIC model, we transferred the structural coordinates for human DNA, RNA, TBP, and TFIIB from the ITC (−IIS) core to the archaeal RNAP/TFEα structure for a model of the archaeal ITC (Figure 5(C)).

Even though the human RNAP II ITC (with 6 nt transcript) was assembled rather than transcribed, the cross-linking data from our promoter initiated archaeal ITC (with 5 nt transcript) fits to the model remarkably well (Figure 5(C)). From our cross-linking data, W44Bpa shifts from −7/-6 in the PIC to −4/-3 in the ITC, R52Bpa shifts from −8/-7/-6 to −4/-3, T58Bpa from −10 through −6 to −4/-3/-2 (as well as some retained upstream cross-links), and E74Bpa shifts from −10/-9/-8 to −10/-9/-8/-7. Consistent with this, in the model, the shift of the T strand caused by the RNA 6-mer and the extended transcription bubble leads the TFIIB B-reader to shift interactions to T strand DNA positions further downstream. The B-reader helix and loop are now close to DNA from about −4 to −1. In contrast, the B-linker strand remains close to DNA between −10 and 8 in the ITC, similar to its position in the PIC. Since the TFB B-linker strand does not change position dramatically between the PIC and ITC, it would appear that interactions with the scrunched part of the T strand DNA are mostly managed by the B-reader helix and loop. Further elongation would lead to movement of the nascent RNA 5’ end toward space occupied by the B-reader helix, as well as pushing more T strand DNA into the scrunch. This would displace the B-reader helix and loop, allowing the RNA 5’ end access to the exit channel of RNAP, and ultimately lead to ejection of the TFB B-reader and -linker motifs from the RNAP active site channel. This is supported by a previous cross-linking study indicating that the B-reader contacts to DNA persist as RNA reaches 6−8 nt in archaeal transcription complex, and begin to vacate the DNA as the transcript reaches 10 nt in length [37].

The position of TFEα winged helix in the PIC and ITC

Archaeal TFEα functions in transcription by binding to RNAP and influencing clamp dynamics, and by resisting binding of the elongation factor Spt4/5 until RNAP commits to entering elongation mode [40,42]. However, the mechanism by which it accomplishes these functions is unclear, because of a lack of structural details of its position and movement during early initiation. We used the PIC and ITC hybrid models created for Figure 5 to visualize the positions of the two TFEα Bpa substitutions relative to the transcription bubble. In the PIC, the wing base Bpa at position 49 sits over the minor groove, close to the NT strand −12 to −14 region. There are minimal changes in this region in the ITC model, as observed by cross-linking. The fixed position of the upstream fork DNA ensures that early transcript elongation will lead to increased scrunching within the active site channel, and given the proximity of the winged helix motif to the fork, it’s possible that the TFEα wing base helix could play a role in stabilizing the interaction until productive elongation is achieved. The cross-links by W76Bpa suggest a more dynamic role for the TFEα wing tip. In the PIC, the wing tip interacts with single-stranded DNA at −10 and −11 of the NT strand, while in the ITC, the contacts shift downstream on the NT strand by about 5 bases. No flexibility has previously been observed for the beta hairpin portion of the winged helix, suggesting that it stays fixed relative to RNAP in the ITC. Therefore, we predict that the NT strand DNA is able to bulge out from the main channel to interact with the TFEα wing tip as additional downstream DNA enters the active site channel. The interactions between TFEα and the bulging NT strand DNA could help to stabilize the ITC by preventing upstream DNA from reentering the active site channel, reducing the possibility of backtracking and abortive transcript release.

Interestingly, the hybrid model shown in Figure 5C does not agree with the observed change in TFEα W76Bpa cross-links. In mammalian PICs and ITC structures, the TFIIEα winged helix is not as close to the transcription bubble as the archaeal TFEα is [28,30,31,64], so it appears that during initiation the mammalian TFIIEα wing tip is not well-positioned to interact with NT strand DNA. We note that the NT strand DNA in the mammalian transcription bubble is typically not shown in structural models because it is unresolved or very low resolution, suggesting a high degree of mobility. It may be that the wing tip can interact with NT strand DNA in the mammalian PIC and ITC, but the interaction is transient or unstable, and thus not detected in these structures. Alternatively, since the sequence of the TFEα/TFIIEα wing tip is not well conserved from archaea to eukaryotes (Figure 4(A)), it is possible that the TFIIEα wing has no functional role in DNA interactions in eukaryotes.

The cross-linking results presented here show that the archaeal TFB B-reader and B-linker are positioned very similarly to the conserved structures in eukaryotic TFIIB during transcription initiation, while some of the TFEα winged helix interactions with NT strand DNA may be unique to archaea. In addition, DNA contacts by TFB and TFEα change differentially during early events in initiation, providing information about how T strand DNA scrunches and NT strand DNA bulges as the transcription bubble expands. The proximity of archaeal TFB and TFEα to specific regions of the transcription bubble DNA within and just outside of the RNAP active site channel is consistent with the critical functions of these transcription factors in promoter opening, PIC stabilization, and the transition of RNAP from initiation to elongation, and suggests roles for specific protein surfaces in mediating these functions. Experiments are in progress to expand the cross-linking analysis to other transcription factor surfaces, and to other stages in the initiation process. We anticipate that additional high-resolution mapping will stimulate new hypotheses for transcription factor function during transcription initiation and the transition to elongation.
