Gamified sensor-based upper extremity functional rehabilitation in individuals with spinal cord injury: a pilot clinical trial protocol
Authors: Sunpreet Singh Hanspal, Gouree Sengar, Ankush Gera, Harvinder Singh Chhabra, Ankit Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSpinal cord injury (SCI) is a life-altering condition that impacts physical, functional, and psychosocial well-being. Regardless of the cause, restoring hand function is the first priority for tetraplegic people which tend to place more emphasis on independence and activity limitations in activities of daily living (ADL) in SCI. Traditional rehabilitation strategies often result in limited motor recovery, highlighting the need for adjunctive approaches. Emerging evidence suggests that gamified sensor-based interventions may augment conventional therapy by increasing patient engagement, enhancing neuroplasticity, and supporting motor relearning through task-specific, interactive training paradigms.MethodsThis single-arm, single-center pilot study is primarily designed to evaluate the feasibility of delivering gamified sensor-based upper extremity rehabilitation in individuals with cervical spinal cord injury (SCI). Feasibility parameters include recruitment and retention rates, adherence to the intervention, and participant satisfaction. Twenty participants aged 18–65 years with cervical SCI (AIS A–D) will be recruited. The intervention comprises 25 sessions over 5 weeks using a gamified sensor-based rehabilitation system. Secondary, exploratory objectives will examine preliminary trends in motor and functional outcomes through descriptive statistics and effect size estimation.DiscussionFindings from this study will provide preliminary feasibility data on the implementation of gamified sensor-based upper extremity rehabilitation in individuals with SCI. Results will help refine the methodological design for a future adequately powered randomized controlled trial.Trial registrationRegistered at ClinicalTrials.gov – CTRI/2025/06/088222.Keywords: Spinal cord injury, Sensor-based rehabilitation, Gamification, Upper extremity, Occupational therapy

Background

The global incidence of spinal cord injury (SCI) is estimated to range from 10.4 to 83 cases per million population annually [1]. In India, an estimated 1.5 million individuals are living with spinal cord injury (SCI), with approximately 20,000 new cases reported annually [2]. Cervical spinal cord injuries are more prevalent compared to injuries occurring at the thoracic or lumbar levels [3]. Impairment of arm and hand function is amongst the most distressing consequences of cervical spinal cord injury, significantly affecting an individual's level of independence [4]. The functionality of the upper limb is crucial in our daily lives and is necessary for nearly all activities of daily living; therefore, enhancing upper limb motor function is a significant objective in rehabilitation to foster independence and life satisfaction in individuals with SCI [4].

Recent evidence suggests that rehabilitation interventions grounded in motor learning theory are effective in promoting the recovery of impaired upper limb function. By engaging patients in repetitive, goal-directed movements that closely simulate the desired motor outcome, this approach facilitates the acquisition of motor skills and supports neuroplastic changes within the central nervous system (CNS), thereby enhancing functional recovery [5]. It has been proposed that repetitive, activity-based exercises can aid in recovery following spinal cord injury (SCI) by promoting practice-dependent plasticity in both the brain and spinal cord [6–8]. Hence, repetitive, intensive, and activity-based upper limb rehabilitative therapy can be effective in improving impaired upper limb function. In the injured spinal cord, this type of neural bombing encourages neural plasticity and could be the crucial stimulus needed to trigger neurologic and structural reconstruction of the relevant pathways [9].

Nowadays, a wide array of rehabilitation techniques exists, aimed at either enhancing function or compensating for the loss of upper limb function in individuals with spinal cord injury. Studies on the use of technology to support traditional therapy approaches and physical rehabilitation are becoming more and more numerous [10, 11]. A growing field that blends exergames, gamification techniques, and conventional rehabilitation procedures has emerged as a result of this interest in technology-based rehabilitation [12]. By offering a more effective and satisfying method of carrying out a sequence of repetitive and functional movements—which are necessary for the rehabilitation of patients with motor deficits—these innovative treatment approaches integrate hardware and software to speed up the process of motor learning [13].

The PABLO® Upper Extremity system (Tyromotion GmbH) is a sensor-based rehabilitation tool designed for unilateral and bilateral upper limb training. It provides interactive game-based modules with audiovisual feedback and allows assessment of strength and movement quality. The system incorporates force sensors and inertial measurement units that detect hand forces and three-dimensional upper limb movements. PABLO® is considered a cognitively engaging, task-oriented rehabilitation modality requiring active patient participation [14]. While evidence supports its use in populations such as stroke and multiple sclerosis [14–16], there remains a notable lack of research evaluating its feasibility and potential therapeutic applicability in individuals with SCI. This gap highlights the need for early-phase feasibility work to determine whether a structured gamified rehabilitation protocol can be feasibly implemented in individuals with cervical SCI.

Objectives

Primary objective

To assess the feasibility of implementing a gamified sensor-based upper extremity rehabilitation program in individuals with cervical SCI, using predefined feasibility thresholds: Recruitment rate ≥ 70%, Adherence ≥ 80%, Attrition ≤ 20%, Participant satisfaction ≥ 7/10.

Secondary (exploratory) objectives

To explore indicative trends in upper limb motor performance (GRASSP 2.0), functional independence (SCIM-III), perceived upper extremity capability (CUE-Q), neurological status (ISNCSCI-UEMS), and quality of life (ISCoS QoL) to inform outcome selection and sample size estimation for a future randomized controlled trial.

Materials and methods

This pilot clinical trial is designed to evaluate feasibility (recruitment, retention, adherence, acceptability). Exploratory clinical outcomes will be used only to identify trends and inform the design of a future randomized controlled trial.

Patient and study design

This study will employ a single-arm, pretest–posttest pilot feasibility design. This design was chosen because early-phase trials aim to determine whether study procedures, recruitment processes, intervention delivery, and participant engagement are feasible, rather than to evaluate clinical efficacy. A control group is therefore not required at this stage, as the primary purpose is to assess feasibility parameters and refine methodological aspects before planning a future randomized controlled trial. A convenience sample of 20 individuals with cervical-level spinal cord injury will be recruited. All eligible participants will receive detailed information about the study, and written informed consent will be obtained by the principal investigator prior to enrolment. The study is anticipated to take approximately 6–8 months to complete. Recruitment will begin in July 2025, with the first participant expected to be enrolled on 05-07-2025, and results anticipated by February/March 2026. Demographic details will be recorded, and participants will be selected based on the eligibility criteria following neurological examination (Table 1).

Ethical considerations

The enrolled participants will be informed orally and in writing about the purpose of this trial, its potential risks, benefits of participation, and the right to withdraw from the trial at any point during the study. A written informed consent signed by the participants will be taken from those who are willing to participate in the study.

The data of the participants will be collected and managed confidentially. All the authors related to the trial will have access to the final trial dataset. The study protocol has been approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee of Sri Balaji Action Medical Institute and the trial is registered with Clinical Trials Registry.

Randomization

As this pilot clinical trial employed a single-group design without a control or comparison group, randomization was not applicable. All eligible participants who met the inclusion criteria will be enrolled consecutively and will receive the intervention.

Blinding

As this will be a single-group, open-label pilot clinical trial, blinding of participants and therapists will not be feasible due to the nature of the intervention. However, outcome assessments will be conducted by an independent evaluator who will be blinded to the study objectives and the intervention timeline to minimize assessment bias. Furthermore, the statistician responsible for data analysis will remain blinded to participant identities and intervention details.

Gamified sensor-based training

The proposed intervention will involve a structured, gamified upper extremity rehabilitation program utilizing the PABLO® sensor-based therapy system (Tyromotion GmbH, Austria), an advanced rehabilitation device engineered to deliver intensive, interactive, and task-specific motor training. The system will comprise a hand sensor, two inertial motion sensors, and a Multiball interface, integrated with a 55-inch LED display to provide real-time multimodal feedback, including both visual and auditory cues. The training protocol will be administered over a period of five weeks, with participants engaging in therapy sessions five times per week, each lasting approximately 30 min under the guidance of a trained occupational therapist/physiotherapist. All sessions will be conducted with participants seated in their wheelchairs, secured with a safety belt, and positioned in front of a standardized wooden table to ensure consistent posture and alignment for effective upper limb engagement.

The intervention will be delivered in two sequential stages. Stage 1, referred to as “Device Orientation,” will involve familiarizing participants with the PABLO system components, usage instructions, and safety protocols. In Stage 2, termed “Gamified Sensor-Based Therapy,” participants will engage in a series of pre-programmed game-based therapeutic modules within the system. These will include five one-dimensional (1D) accuracy games, two one-dimensional (1D) reaction games, and five two-dimensional (2D) motor function games, each designed to target specific domains of upper limb motor control. Within each session, the games will be administered in a fixed sequence, beginning with one-dimensional (1D) accuracy games, followed by one-dimensional (1D) reaction games, and concluding with two-dimensional (2D) motor control games to progressively increase task complexity. The therapy tasks will simulate functionally relevant upper limb movements such as reaching in multiple planes (forward, upward, downward, and laterally), forearm supination and pronation, as well as grasping and pinching.

Each game module will be calibrated to the participant's baseline functional capacity and progressively adapted to match their ongoing performance, ensuring that the training remains optimally challenging and therapeutically effective. This personalized, adaptive approach is expected to promote high levels of motor engagement and facilitate practice-dependent neuroplasticity in the central nervous system. The PABLO system will automatically record detailed performance metrics, including movement amplitude, reaction time, trajectory accuracy, and task completion data. These metrics will be used to monitor individual progress and dynamically adjust the difficulty level of tasks in subsequent sessions.

The gamified nature of the intervention is anticipated to enhance user motivation, increase therapy adherence, and support sustained engagement—factors that are critical for achieving meaningful functional gains in individuals with spinal cord injury.

Intervention fidelity and safety monitoring

Therapists delivering the intervention will undergo standardized training on the operation of the PABLO® system, calibration procedures, and safety protocols. Each session will be documented using a structured log capturing session duration, module selection, progression level, participant engagement, and any deviations from the protocol. Fidelity will be monitored through weekly supervision meetings and periodic review of therapy logs. Safety monitoring will include screening for adverse events such as fatigue, musculoskeletal discomfort, pain, or autonomic dysreflexia. Any adverse event will be recorded, managed according to institutional guidelines, and reported to the ethics committee if required.

Progression and feedback

Throughout the intervention period, progression will be systematically managed through individualized calibration of the therapy modules within the PABLO® system. Each participant’s initial motor capabilities will be assessed during baseline testing, and the difficulty level of training tasks will be tailored accordingly. As participants demonstrate improvement in motor performance, intensity will be increased by increasing the level of the game (1–10) if the participant achieves 80% score 3 times successively. This adaptive progression will ensure that participants are continually challenged at an optimal level, which is critical for facilitating motor learning and neuroplastic changes. The PABLO system will provide real-time feedback through its integrated software, delivering visual, auditory, and performance-based cues to enhance participant engagement and self-monitoring. Visual feedback will include on-screen performance scores, movement trajectory visualization, and success rates for each task, while auditory cues will offer immediate reinforcement for correct or improved performance. Additionally, the system will automatically record detailed session data, including task completion times, accuracy percentages, and number of successful repetitions, which will be reviewed by the therapist after each session to guide further adjustments in therapy parameters. This continuous feedback and progression model is expected to maintain participant motivation, facilitate goal-oriented motor practice, and reinforce positive performance patterns—key factors in optimizing functional recovery and adherence in spinal cord injury rehabilitation.

Adherence

Adherence to the intervention protocol will be closely monitored and actively supported throughout the duration of the study. In accordance with the “intention-to-treat” analytic concept, participants who are unable to finish all of the sessions within the allotted five weeks may extend the training period by one week. Missed sessions will be documented along with the reason for absence to analyze patterns of non-adherence and potential barriers. In the event of missing data during a 5-week period, the “last observation carried forward” concept will be implemented. If the participant is lost before the study's end date, the missing final values of the outcome variable will be replaced with the last known value. The authors will also provide a detailed analysis on the kind, size, and trend of missing data across the study.

Sample size

This study will include a total of 20 participants, selected using a non-probability convenient sampling method. The chosen sample size aligns with commonly accepted guidelines for pilot studies, which recommend 12–30 participants to allow for preliminary testing of feasibility, acceptability, and initial intervention effects [24].

Statistical analysis

All analyses will focus on feasibility outcomes using descriptive statistics. Exploratory clinical outcomes will be summarized using means, medians, and 95% confidence intervals. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d or rank-based effect sizes) will be calculated to estimate the magnitude of observed trends. No hypothesis testing or claims of statistical significance will be made, as the purpose of this pilot study is not to determine efficacy but to inform the design of a future RCT.

Results

Feasibility outcomes, including recruitment rate, retention rate, adherence to the protocol, and participant satisfaction (measured via Visual Analog Scale), will be reported as primary indicators. Descriptive statistics will be used to analyze these parameters. Secondary outcome data on GRASSP 2.0, CUE-Q, SCIM-III, ISCoS QoL, and ISNCSCI will be presented to explore preliminary trends in clinical efficacy.

Discussion

Hand function plays a vital role in activities of daily living and overall well-being in individuals with cervical spinal cord injury (SCI). Even modest improvements in upper limb capability can meaningfully influence independence, participation, and quality of life. Interventions that promote task-specific practice and user engagement are therefore of considerable clinical value in SCI rehabilitation.

This pilot study is designed to generate preliminary evidence on the feasibility, acceptability, and practicality of delivering a gamified sensor-based upper extremity rehabilitation program. The interactive and feedback-driven nature of the PABLO system may enhance engagement and facilitate task-specific motor practice. However, this study is not powered to evaluate clinical efficacy, and any observed changes in outcome measures will be interpreted as exploratory trends only. Findings from measures such as ISNCSCI UEMS, GRASSP 2.0, SCIM-III, CUE-Q, and the ISCoS Quality of Life dataset will help identify responsive outcomes and inform their selection for a future randomized controlled trial. These exploratory results will also support sample size estimation and methodological refinement.

Despite its potential, several challenges may arise during implementation. These include variability in participant engagement with gamified systems, potential technological limitations such as sensor calibration issues or measurement inaccuracies, and fatigue or reduced tolerance in individuals with higher injury severity. Additionally, differences in cognitive function and motivation may influence adherence to game-based interventions. To address these challenges, the intervention protocol incorporates individualized calibration, therapist supervision, and continuous monitoring of engagement and safety.

If demonstrated to be feasible, this approach may support the integration of gamified sensor-based rehabilitation into routine clinical practice and potentially into home-based rehabilitation settings. Such systems may enhance therapy intensity, engagement, and accessibility, particularly in resource-constrained environments. Furthermore, this study may contribute to the evolving field of technology-assisted neurorehabilitation and inform the development of future interventions integrating advanced digital and adaptive rehabilitation strategies.

This study has several limitations. The single-arm design and convenience sampling may introduce selection bias, and the small sample size limits generalizability. Additionally, blinding of participants and treating therapists is not feasible due to the nature of the intervention. Despite these limitations, the findings will provide essential feasibility data required to inform the design of a fully powered randomized controlled trial.
