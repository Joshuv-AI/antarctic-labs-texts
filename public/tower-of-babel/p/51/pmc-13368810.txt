Assessing social media platform data access for automated early warning of public health threats
Authors: Katja Siling, Francisco Orchard, Laura Espinosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEpidemic Intelligence aims to promptly detect and monitor health threats, but processing large volumes of information is time-consuming. Automated social media (SM) data collection can enhance this process. We reviewed 36 pre-selected SM (as of 1 June 2024), evaluating content relevance, data extraction mechanisms, and compliance with terms of use and data protection policies to assess suitability for inclusion in an automated early warning system. Six SM (BlueSky, Mastodon, Tumblr, X, Reddit, and YouTube) met all criteria. Findings can inform considerations for building a multi-source automated data collection system to strengthen early warning in public health and provide a framework for reassessing platform suitability as SM ecosystems evolve.

Introduction

Timely detection of public health threats and rapid information sharing are key to effective decision-making and response coordination, as highlighted by recent epidemics [1]. At the European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control (ECDC), whose mission is to strengthen the defences of European Union/European Economic Area (EU/EEA) Member States against infectious diseases, early warning and response is supported by Epidemic Intelligence (EI). EI combines indicator-based and event-based surveillance to detect and assess acute public health events [2] by using diverse data sources, including routine surveillance, official reports, traditional news, and social media (SM) [3]. However, this systematic collection, verification, and investigation of signals is inherently time-consuming.

Social media offers advantages for early warning compared to traditional sources, due to their near real-time information, broader geographic coverage, and diverse user-generated content that can signal emerging health threats [4–5]. Studies have demonstrated SM utility for outbreak detection and surveillance [6, 4], and automating data collection can enhance efficiency and expand capacity for threat detection. For example, detect an outbreak 1–3 weeks earlier than traditional surveillance methods. In 2020, ECDC published epitweetr [7], an open-source R package for automated signal detection using Twitter (now X). Epitweetr was routinely used in EI workflows until April 2023, when maintenance was discontinued due to changes in X’s data access policies [8]. This experience underscored the need for diverse data sources and agile systems capable of adapting to new SM platforms or any changes to access conditions.

Research on SM for public health threat detection has largely focused on single platforms or specific diseases. This review evaluates multiple SM for their suitability for inclusion in an automated early warning system, considering their features, content relevance, and data accessibility. Its primary long-term value lies not only in identifying platforms suitable at a given point in time, but also in providing a reusable methodological framework for repeated reassessment as SM ecosystems change.

Methods

Based on the list of SM platforms on Wikipedia, we identified and reviewed 36 platforms that were likely or potentially to contain content relevant to public health. Of these, 35 SM were identified before the review (Discord, Facebook, imo, Instagram, JOSH, Kuaishou, Likee, Line, LinkedIn, Mastodon, Messenger, Myspace, Pinterest, QQ, Quora, Qzone, Reddit, Skype, Slashdot, Snapchat, Teams, Telegram, Threads, Tieba, TikTok, Tumblr, Viber, VK, WeChat, Weibo, WhatsApp, X, Xiaohongshu, Yelp!, and YouTube), and BlueSky was added during the review process.

We applied a structured selection process using predefined eligibility criteria. The eligibility criteria were operationalized using predefined categories and definitions, and systematically applied to each platform based on information extracted from publicly available sources. An iterative search strategy was employed to collect information from openly available sources published or updated between January 2019 and June 2024; data for BlueSky were extracted in April 2025. The primary sources of information were official SM websites. Additional information was obtained from Wikipedia profiles, open-source code repositories with updates after January 2023, and was complemented by targeted Google searches using keywords and operators. Online magazines and mass media websites were included only when content ownership was clearly stated and the site adhered to good data validation practices. Details on search keywords used are provided in Supplementary material S1. The search and assessment of the eligibility criteria were performed by a single reviewer and the eligibility criteria assessment was revised by two additional reviewers.

We conducted the selection process in two steps. First, we built a general profile for each SM to assess its relevance for public health. The following eligibility criteria were applied: having understandable features, Latin-based language content, searchable public content, text content, and content relevant to public health.

The definitions used for having understandable features and content relevant to public health were as follows. “Understandable features” meant that the reviewer was able to navigate through the application and read user messages. “Could contain content relevant to public health” was defined as the likelihood of finding publicly available, user-generated text content plausibly relevant to EI, such as information on unusual health events, symptoms, behaviours that may affect disease transmission or the impact of public health interventions. For this purpose, the assessment considered the stated platform purpose, content review, and available official documentation.

Where eligibility remained unclear, we applied a conservative approach and SM were excluded unless all first-step criteria were met.

Second, SM meeting the first eligibility criteria were evaluated for accessibility of targeted content via third-party tools. For these SM, we also reviewed documentation on Application Programming Interface (API) terms of use and data protection policies. All extracted data and eligibility assessments were recorded in a spreadsheet matrix (Supplementary material S2).

Results

We identified six SM for inclusion in the early warning system (Figure 1). Four microblogging platforms (BlueSky, Mastodon, Tumblr, and X), a forum platform (Reddit), and a video-sharing platform (YouTube) had content potentially relevant to public health, with usable and openly available API for retrieving user messages. In addition, all these platforms had accessible documentation on terms of use and privacy policies.

In the first step, 26 SM were excluded for not meeting one or more eligibility criteria. As shown in Supplementary material S3, in the first step SM were excluded for a range of reasons, and several did not meet multiple criteria simultaneously. These reasons included dominance of non-Latin script or Chinese-only interfaces (e.g. Tieba, QQ, and Weibo), private communication structures (e.g. Messenger, Skype, and WeChat), short-video or image-centric formats poorly suited to automated text analysis (e.g. Instagram, Pinterest, and TikTok), and platforms considered unlikely to host content relevant to public health (e.g. Slashdot and Yelp).

Among the 10 platforms evaluated in the second step, four SM with potentially relevant public health content (Facebook, Threads, Quora, and LinkedIn) were excluded due to not having publicly accessible API endpoints for message retrieval or restricted access to a limited number of partners under dedicated agreements.

Discussion

This review assessed 36 SM for accessibility and technical feasibility for potential inclusion in an envisaged automated early warning system for public health threat detection. Six SM met all selection criteria under current conditions. To our knowledge, this is the first review to assess multiple SM for public health surveillance, addressing a gap in the existing literature and providing a foundation for future automated early warning systems.

Importantly, these six platforms are suitable candidates within a specific technical and linguistic configuration of early warning, prioritizing Latin-script text content, publicly accessible APIs, and compatibility with automated data collection. The findings should therefore not be interpreted as identifying the most informative, representative, or effective SM for outbreak detection or EI in general, but rather as reflecting suitability under the constraints of the envisaged monitoring system. SM monitoring should be considered a complementary component within a broader, multi-source surveillance system. Consequently, the reduced coverage resulting from excluded platforms is likely to have limited impact on overall surveillance capacity, which relies on many additional data sources.

SM ecosystems are dynamic and complex. They enable rapid access to user-generated content that may signal emerging health threats; however, data accessibility and representativeness remain major concerns [4]. Platform-specific restrictions, evolving terms of use, and regulatory changes can affect feasibility and sustainability, as illustrated by changes to X/Twitter’s API, which disrupted several public health research projects and surveillance tools [9]. Diversifying data sources and incorporating flexible technical solutions is essential to mitigate these risks. Continuous monitoring of SM ecosystems and adaptive strategies will be critical to maintain system robustness amid rapid technological and regulatory changes. Accordingly, this review contributes by providing a first-step infrastructure assessment of SM under the established technical conditions, rather than a validation of surveillance performance, and offers a structured evaluation framework to support systematic, periodic reassessments as platform ecosystems evolve.

The review has some limitations. First, as only pre-selected SMs were assessed, other relevant SM may have been missed. Excluding non-Latin script SMs and those dominated by short video—due to technical constraints of the envisaged monitoring system—may have reduced coverage of populations that are under-represented in traditional tools monitoring open sources. Emerging technologies, including large language models, could help overcome these limitations and enable broader inclusion in the future. Reliance on a single reviewer introduces potential subjectivity bias, although predefined criteria and co-author revision of the eligibility criteria assessment helped mitigate this. Importantly, the review reflects the situation as of 1 June 2024, except for BlueSky; changes to SMs are expected, and templates for monitoring updates are in place to support system adaptability.

The identification of six SMs provides a starting point for developing an operational early warning system for public health threats. Implementation will require further legal and technical assessments, including data protection compliance, empirical testing of automated data collection workflows, and evaluation of user demographics to ensure representativeness and equity. Beyond ECDC, these insights have global relevance: they can inform strategies for integrating SM into public health surveillance systems worldwide and support the strengthening of early warning and situational awareness across sectors.
