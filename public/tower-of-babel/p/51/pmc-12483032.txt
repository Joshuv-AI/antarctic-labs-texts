Experience in Peritoneal Dialysis
Authors: Domenico Cozzo, Antonio Bellasi
Published: 2025

CKD affects up to 16% of the global population, with an estimated 800 million individuals living with CKD stages 1–5 worldwide. In this context, CKD is recognized as one of the most prevalent causes of noncommunicable diseases, significantly affecting life expectancy.1 In May 2025, the World Health Organization adopted a resolution to prioritize kidney health within its noncommunicable disease agenda, paving the way for earlier detection, better prevention, and improved access to treatment for subjects suffering from CKD.2 For the first time, renal health was explicitly recognized as a global priority in the fight against noncommunicable diseases, including cardiovascular diseases, diabetes, and cancer.2

In 2013, 88.4% of all patients with ESKD began KRT with hemodialysis.3 Of the dialysis population, only 9%–11% undergo peritoneal dialysis (PD), with a higher prevalence observed in developing countries.4 The remaining 2%–3% usually received a preemptive kidney transplant.3

When approaching the transition to dialysis, specifically from outpatient management, the patient must be thoroughly prepared for clinical needs (such as vaccinations, dialysis access, education, and psychologic support). Furthermore, this is the optimal time to select the most appropriate treatment.

PD is designed to enhance patient autonomy, as it eliminates the need for frequent hospital visits, unlike hemodialysis. PD can be performed manually, requiring the active involvement of the patient to change the dialysate; however, automated PD significantly improves patient autonomy, as it is predominantly conducted during nighttime hours. The patient's physiologic characteristics influence the decision to initiate PD, particularly anatomical considerations such as the absence of defects in the abdominal wall and prior major surgeries that may have induced structural alterations to the peritoneal membrane. Furthermore, the ability to position a Tenckhoff catheter in the abdominal cavity is essential, as well as the subjective effectiveness of dialysis, which is determined by the peritoneal membrane's diffusivity.5 In addition, there are limitations based on the patient's ability to learn and perform PD independently. The advantages of PD also include comparable survival rates to hemodialysis, effective removal of solutes and fluids, a slower decline in residual kidney function compared with hemodialysis, a potentially lower incidence of complications, and technical simplicity. Moreover, from a public health and ecologic perspective, PD contributes to reduced health care costs and a lower environmental effect.6

Nevertheless, it is not entirely accurate to assert that the PD enhances autonomy under all conditions; from both an organizational and subjective standpoint, hemodialysis may often be the preferable solution in comparison with the PD. From this perspective, there are currently no validated tools that can objectively assess the greater feasibility of PD, compared with hemodialysis. In fact, it is often believed that PD offers advantages for patient autonomy based solely on the conviction that patients do not have to travel to the clinic three times a week.

The first author of this article had the opportunity to meet a distant elderly relative, aged 76 years, who, on learning she was speaking with a nephrology resident, recounted that her late husband had ESKD and underwent PD for 4 years. They were a working-class couple who had emigrated and thus lacked a large family support network. Her account was deeply moving, especially to a health care professional, and is worth sharing:“We moved at a young age. My husband worked as a concierge in a city building. One day, he received a job offer at a factory 2,500 km from home. In search of a better future, we decided to relocate. We were young, with a 1-year-old daughter. My husband left first, and I followed shortly after. Less than a year after moving, I also found work. Although we had a small child, we finally managed to organize our lives. However, everything changed when, 9 months after starting my job, my husband had a terrible accident and, unfortunately, ended up in a wheelchair for life at the age of about 30. Over time, he developed several complications, including a kidney infection and multiple episodes of kidney stones, leading to kidney failure, and the doctor told us he needed to start dialysis. They offered us PD. I was scared and unsure, but the doctors assured me that it was the best option for both me and my husband, as it was a more convenient treatment. I trusted them. In reality, those 4 years were extremely challenging and took a toll on me. I had to learn to act as a nurse while continuing to work due to my husband's illness and taking care of our daughter (1). Everything had to be sterile, and the procedures required impeccable cleanliness. During the summer heat, we had to keep everything closed during the procedures (2). We lived in a very small house, but the supplies took up a lot of space. We did not know where to put everything, and our house was full of equipment (3). Moreover, the materials were heavy, and I needed to find someone to help me carry them from the basement to the upper floor, which proved to be quite challenging. Despite our financial difficulties, we had to install a lift (4). I worked as a factory worker and needed sleep at night, but the machine often sounded alarms. I frequently could not sleep and still had to go to work in the morning. My husband was disabled and needed assistance, so I had to get up to resolve the issues, often staying on the phone with technicians (5). Complications were frequent, and we had to take my husband to the hospital (6). Despite everything, I did it all with great love. It was hard, but it drained all my energy. If I could go back, I would not have chosen PD.”

Certainly, many aspects of patient management have changed significantly nowadays. Considering that this testimonial dates back many years (approximately 40), some aspects remain entirely relevant today.7 The testimony of a non–health care professional led to reflections on several important aspects:We often assume that PD is more convenient for the patient because they do not have to travel to a hemodialysis center and can maintain autonomy, performing the treatment even at night. However, we might overlook some critical factors in patient care, and what is perceived as “convenient” or “suitable” by the clinician may not align with reality if a thorough assessment is not conducted.While PD undoubtedly offers many advantages for the patient's daily life, as well as for public health, socioeconomic, and environmental factors, the initial assessment of the patient's family support system must be comprehensive and detailed. It should be conducted using standardized and objective parameters, rather than being rushed, and should follow a checklist to ensure no aspect is overlooked.

Following this and other similar patients' and caregivers experiences,8 and after attentively considering the opinions and challenges presented by this clinical case, we have decided to develop a comprehensive checklist of potential factors to consider during the patient and family anamnesis. This checklist aims to facilitate a thorough and precise assessment of the needs of patients who are about considering PD treatment. Our aim was to enhance the overall assessment process in these cases (Table 1).

In conclusion, a standardized checklist could be used to support the decision on which dialysis treatment is most appropriate for the patients and their families. Certainly, the environmental, economic, and public health effects are significant in this decision. Similarly, assessing the patient's capabilities and resources should not be superficial or rushed. Clinician's assessment must be accurate and meticulous, aiming to understand all of the patient's characteristics outside the hospital setting. Based on our experience, observational or randomized studies could be conducted to develop specific, structured, and codified tools for standardizing the indication for one dialysis treatment over another, thereby ensuring a standardized and comprehensive approach to decision making. These aspects should not be limited solely to assessing the patient's physiologic feasibility or level of autonomy.
