Population genetic structure reveals asymmetric hybridization between Pinus pumila and P. parviflora var. Pentaphylla in the Hakkoda Mountains, Northern Japan
Authors: Yumeko Tarusawa, Takuro Ito, Yuji Isagi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInterspecific hybridization can facilitate rapid adaptation to novel environments despite potential fitness reductions. The genomic regions of Pinus parviflora var. pentaphylla inhabiting higher temperatures could increase the fitness of the recipient alpine species Pinus pumila, which has adapted to lower temperatures, particularly under global warming. Using exome-capture techniques to overcome challenges of large Pinus genomes, we investigate the genome-wide hybridization patterns between these species in the Hakkoda Mountains, Japan. Hybrids predominantly occurred at mid-elevation and contained P. pumila chloroplasts, suggesting P. pumila is the most recent pollen parent. Genome-wide introgression patterns suggested frequent backcrossing with P. pumila and limited backcrossing with P. parviflora var. pentaphylla, likely owing to geographic isolation, phenological differences in flowering time, and natural selection favoring hybrids in specific environmental contexts. Specifically, tall hybrids may be advantageous in light competition at mid-elevations covered with moorland vegetation, whereas shorter pure P. pumila individuals may be favored in high-altitude wind-exposed fellfields. Hybrids with shorter heights may be disadvantaged in deciduous broad-leaved forests at lower altitudes compared to taller pure P. parviflora var. pentaphylla. Our findings provide insights for future functional analyses of introgressed genes to elucidate the adaptive significance of such hybridization patterns along elevational and environmental gradients.Keywords: Asymmetric introgression, Exome capture, Interspecific hybridization, Pinus pumilaSubject terms: Biogeography, Evolutionary genetics, Population genetics

Introduction

Interspecific hybridization plays a complex role in evolutionary adaptation. While this can result in reduced adaptive capacity due to intermediate traits of the parent species, it also offers the potential for rapid adaptation to novel environments1. Recent increases in available genome-wide sequence data have revealed that introgressive hybridization is a widespread phenomenon across various taxa2 and suggested that alleles acquired through such hybridization may serve as a genetic reservoir for adaptation to new environmental conditions under climate changes3,4. Additionally, population genomic analyses have revealed directional and uneven introgression among populations5,6. In wind-pollinated plants, introgression patterns may be influenced by ecological factors, such as wind direction, as well as evolutionary factors, including the adaptive capacity of introgressed loci to the environment of the recipient habitat7,8.

Pinus pumila, an evergreen coniferous shrub, is found in cold environments ranging from northeastern Eurasia to the alpine zones in Japan9 (Fig. 1). Pinus parviflora var. pentaphylla, which is closely related to P. pumila, grows up to 15–20 m at warmer and lower elevations in Japan10 (Fig. 1). Despite diverging approximately 20 million years ago11, these two species are closely related12 and known to form hybrid individuals. The hybrids of P. pumila and P. parviflora var. pentaphylla have been morphologically identified in Japan as Pinus × hakkodensis13 and analyzed using organellar DNA and a limited number of nuclear DNA markers14–18. However, genome-wide analyses have not been possible owing to the large genome size of the species in Pinus genus (Chinese red pine (Pinus tabuliformis), 25.4Gb19; Whitebark pine (Pinus albicaulis), 27.6Gb20; Korean red pine (Pinus densiflora), 21.7Gb21. To overcome this limitation, we employed an exome capture approach that focuses on expressed genes rather than the entire genome, thereby making large scale, genome-wide sequencing across numerous individuals feasible.

Pinus pumila populations in northern mainland Japan (Fig. 1) are particularly vulnerable to global warming22,23. Unlike the prevalence of higher-elevation mountains (3,000 m class) in central Japan, that of lower-elevation mountains (1,500–2,000 m class) in northern mainland Japan restricts the upward migration of the species in response to rising temperatures. Therefore, a limited elevational range may pose a significant threat to long-term survival. Phylogeographic clustering of P. pumila in Japan based on the restriction patterns of 15 different enzymes24 suggests that P. pumila contracted to the north and may have disappeared from northern mainland Japan during the interglacial periods; similar to other alpine species25, they expanded to that region again during the subsequent glacial periods24. These cycles of expansion and contraction may have led to secondary contact of the differentiated species P. pumila and P. parviflora var. pentaphylla. Changes in species distribution, adaptability, and the surrounding local environment could have influenced the formation of geographical patterns of introgression within a single hybrid zone.

To investigate these processes, we focused on P. pumila and P. parviflora var. pentaphylla in the Hakkoda Mountains, located in the northern region of mainland Japan (40° 41′ N, 140° 52′ E) (Fig. 1), where these species occur sympatrically. In the study area, P. parviflora var. pentaphylla is scattered on steep slopes, leading to cliff edges approximately 800 m above sea level (a.s.l.) (lowland in Fig. 2 (a)), whereas P. pumila dominates alpine wind-exposed slopes at 1,400–1,580 m a.s.l. (Alpine zone in Fig. 2 (a)). Potential hybrid individuals are distributed along the moorland (lower and upper moorlands in Fig. 2 (a)). The surrounding vegetation includes a broad-leaved deciduous forest of Fagus crenata spread between 400 and 800 m a.s.l., a conifer-broadleaved mixed forest occurring from 800 to 1,000 m a.s.l. near the moorland, and Abies mariesiidominates from 1,000 to 1,400 m a.s.l26,27.. In the study area, we analyzed the introgression patterns of P. pumila and P. parviflora var. pentaphylla. The exome sequences of 78 individuals of P. pumila, potential hybrids, and P. parviflora var. pentaphylla were captured using an in-house capture probe and sequenced. We then investigated the introgression patterns and discussed the putative process that formed the observed patterns, along with a comparison among the hybrid zones in the other mountains.

Results

Number of SNPs after filtering

After genotyping and filtering the capture library sequences, we obtained 9,825 nuclear genome-wide SNPs from 75 individuals of P. parviflora var. pentaphylla, P. pumila, and potential hybrids. Phylogenetic tree reconstruction, heterozygosity and hybrid index calculations, and migration rate estimations were performed using this dataset. Principal component analysis (PCA) and ADMIXTURE analysis were performed to investigate the population structure using the LD-pruned dataset, which yielded 5,518 SNPs from 75 individuals. Phylogenetic analysis of the chloroplasts was conducted using a dataset consisting of 388 SNPs, from 75 individuals that were mapped to the chloroplast genome of P. koraiensis (AY228468.2).

Population genomics analyses and introgression patterns

We inferred the ancestry proportion of individuals sampled from the Hakkoda Mountains using ADMIXTURE analysis (K = 2), in which each color corresponds to the ancestral species (orange: P. parviflora var. pentaphylla, blue: P. pumila). A genetic cline was observed between these two species and their hybrid across the elevation: pure P. parviflora var. pentaphylla in the lowland, hybrids in the lower moorland, and mostly pure P. pumila in the upper moorland and alpine zone (Fig. 2(a)). The ADMIXTURE results indicated that all individuals from the lowland (n = 7) and one individual from the lower moorland exhibited 100% ancestry from P. parviflora var. pentaphylla. In the lower moorland, 22 of 26 individuals were hybrid, having 0.06–0.51 P. parviflora var. pentaphylla ancestry proportions (Fig. 2(a)). The remaining three of the total 26 individuals in the lower moorland were identified as P. pumila (Fig. 2(a)). All except three individuals from the upper moorland (n = 21) and all individuals from the alpine zone (n = 18) exhibited 100% P. pumila ancestry (Fig. 2(a)). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation between individuals from the lowland (Fig. 2(b), orange circle) and those from the alpine zone and the upper moorland (Fig. 2(b), blue square and green triangle) along PC1 (16.9% variance). The individuals observed in the lower moorland (Fig. 2(b), green plus) were slightly distant from the individuals in the alpine zone and upper moorland along PC1 (Fig. 2(b)). The study findings revealed the occurrence of asymmetric hybridization between P. parviflora var. pentaphylla and P. pumila; the hybrid index of the hybrid individuals in the lower moorland and the upper moorland ranged from approximately 0.80 to near 1.0, approaching the value of pure P. pumila (Fig. 2(c)). This suggests the absence of F1 hybrids among the hybrid individuals, with most having undergone repeated backcrossing with P. pumila over multiple generations (Fig. 2(c)).

Phylogenetic analysis based on the whole nuclear genome SNPs

A maximum likelihood tree based on the nuclear genome using RAxML-NG with 75 individuals (9,825 SNPs each) revealed divergence between pure P. parviflora var. pentaphylla and pure P. pumila, supported by a bootstrap value of 100, with hybrid individuals positioned between the two species. (Fig. 3). Among the hybrids, those with a lower proportion of P. parviflora var. pentaphylla ancestry clustered closer to P. pumila in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3). These patterns correspond to the geographical cline from low to high elevation (lowland, lower moorland, upper moorland, and alpine zone).

Phylogenetic analysis based on the whole genome of the chloroplast

Overall, 4.62–48.09% (average 25.79%) of the total reads were mapped to the P. koraiensis whole chloroplast genome, and the average read depth was 1,569 with an average coverage of 99%. In contrast to the phylogenetic tree based on the whole nuclear genome SNPs, the phylogenetic tree based on 193 parsimony-informative sites in the chloroplast, constructed using the TVMe + G4 substitution model, revealed distinct divergence between pure P. parviflora var. pentaphylla and other individuals (P. pumila and hybrids) (Fig. 4). Notably, the chloroplast haplotypes of all hybrid individuals were P. pumila-type (Fig. 4). Within the P. pumila-type chloroplast cluster, individuals from the lower moorland (LM) were scattered throughout, showing no clear elevational clines (Fig. 4). This distribution pattern contrasts with the nuclear genome-based phylogeny (Fig. 3), in which a geographical cline was evident.

Observed heterozygosity

The observed heterozygosity was significantly higher in P. parviflora var. pentaphylla and hybrids than in P. pumila (p < 0.001). However, no significant differences were observed between P. parviflora var. pentaphylla and the hybrid individuals (Fig. 5).

Observed heterozygosity. Observed heterozygosity is shown in the boxplot. The asterisks indicate significant differences (p < 0.01).

Recent migration rate

The estimated migration rate (Fig. 6) indicated recent active migration between the alpine zone and the upper moorland (0.30 and 0.29, respectively) (Fig. 6, thick blue lines), while recent gene flow between the lowland and the alpine zone was limited (0.02 and 0.03, respectively) (Fig. 6, thin dotted lines). The lowland exhibited a negligible degree of recent gene flow with other locations (0.01–0.03) (Fig. 6, thin dotted lines). Despite their geographical proximity, gene flow between the lower and upper moorland was restricted (0.01) (Fig. 6, thin dotted line).

Discussion

In this study, we sequenced exome regions of the genomes of P. pumila, P. parviflora var. pentaphylla, and putative hybrids from the Hakkoda Mountains, where these species coexist across an elevational gradient, to analyze patterns of introgression and elucidate the evolutionary processes shaping hybridization dynamics in a single hybrid zone. We found substantial hybridization in the lower moorland, suggesting active gene flow between P. pumila and P. parviflora var. pentaphylla (Fig. 2 (a)). However, recent gene flow estimation over the past few generations using BA3SNP indicated little gene flow with P. parviflora var. pentaphylla in the lowland (Fig. 6). Therefore, the hybrid individuals observed in this study were likely derived from a past hybridization event rather than a recent one, which is consistent with the results of the triangle plot (Fig. 2(c)), suggesting repeated backcrosses to P. pumila after hybridization with P. parviflora var. pentaphylla.

While substantial gene flow occurred between the alpine zone and moorland regions (Fig. 6, thick blue lines), a clear asymmetry existed in the overall gene flow pattern, with minimal migration rates between the lowland and other sites (Fig. 6, thin dotted lines). Such asymmetric hybridization is common in hybridizing species complexes28,29. This phenomenon is attributed to a combination of pre- and postzygotic isolation factors, including the geographical distance between habitats, flowering times, hybrid incompatibility, and selection pressure on hybrid individuals. The observed distribution patterns of hybrids in the present study could be attributed to the natural selection of P. pumila, P. parviflora var. pentaphylla, and their hybrids. In the lower moorland, the tree height of the hybrid individuals was greater than that of the pure P. pumila individuals, which can be attributed to the genetic traits of P. parviflora var. pentaphylla. These hybrids also exhibit taller heights than the surrounding moorland vegetation, and the increased height resulting from hybridization has likely conferred a competitive advantage in the lower moorland. In contrast, no hybrids were observed in the area between the lowland and lower moorland, where the forest is dominated by tall tree species such as F. crenata and Quercus crispula. The tree height of hybrid individuals with a P. pumila-type allele may have been reduced, potentially leading to a diminished competitive advantage under light competition and subsequent elimination. In high-altitude wind-exposed alpine fellfields from the upper moorland to the alpine zone, hybrid individuals with taller tree heights formed by P. parviflora var. pentaphylla genetic components may have been less well-adapted to wind exposure and snow accumulation. Indeed, pure P. pumila was dominant at altitudes higher than the upper moorland, and no hybrids were identified.

In the present study, we observed that hybrid individuals exclusively exhibited P. pumila-type chloroplasts in the Hakkoda Mountains. In the genus Pinus, chloroplasts are paternally inherited from the pollen parent; consequently, in instances of interspecific hybridization, the progeny share the same chloroplast type as the most recent pollen parent. These findings indicated that all hybrid individuals had P. pumila as the probable pollen parent in the most recent crossing event. P. parviflora var. pentaphylla-type chloroplasts were observed in all individuals in the lowland and only one in the lower moorland (Fig. 4).

The observed introgression patterns of nuclear and chloroplast in the current study differed from those previously reported in other mountains12,13,18–20, as summarized in Fig. 7. These sites demonstrate distinctive features with respect to (1) the occurrence of geographical and/or vegetation barriers, (2) the spatial distribution of the parental species, and (3) the presence of hybrid individuals and introgression patterns. Mt. Apoi (Fig. 7(a)) is characterized by (1) the absence of geographical and/or vegetation barriers, (2) a mixed distribution of both the parental species, and (3) a mixture of hybrids with both P. pumila-pronounced and P. parviflora var. pentaphylla-pronounced nuclear DNA type. The Hakkoda Mountains (Fig. 7(b)) feature (1) geographical and/or vegetation barriers, (2) a segregated distribution of parental species, and (3) hybrids with only P. pumila-pronounced nuclear DNA type. Mt. Higashiazuma (Fig. 7(c)) is characterized by (1) geographical and/or vegetation barriers, (2) segregated distribution of parental species, and (3) absence of reported hybrids. The Tanigawa Mountains (Fig. 7(d)) are characterized by (1) the absence of geographical and/or vegetation barriers, (2) the predominance of P. parviflora var. pentaphylla as the primary parental species, and (3) hybrids harboring only P. parviflora var. pentaphylla-pronounced nuclear DNA type.

The Hakkoda Mountains and Mt. Higashiazuma share the characteristics of geographical and/or vegetation barriers and a segregated distribution of parental species. In Mt. Higashiazuma, a dense subalpine coniferous forest at 1,800–1,900 m serves as a gene flow barrier (Fig. 7(c)), whereas in the Hakkoda Mountains, the deciduous broad-leaved forest across a wider altitudinal range (400–1,000 m) may act as a barrier to recent gene flow, as the introgression of P. parviflora var. pentaphylla-type chloroplasts do not occur above this barrier in either region. Nuclear DNA introgression patterns revealed more complex temporal dynamics: gene flow appeared to be completely inhibited in Mt. Higashiazuma, whereas the Hakkoda Mountains show evidence of historical hybridization (Fig. 2(c)) despite the limited recent gene flow (Fig. 6). These differences between the Hakkoda Mountains and Mt. Higashiazuma may be attributed to the different altitudinal locations of the barriers and variations in forest composition and density over time. The historical hybridization and backcrossing observed in the Hakkoda Mountains (Fig. 2(c)) likely occurred during the past climate fluctuations in the region, including cold excursions around 8.2 ka30, 4.2 ka31, and 1.6 ka30, and warm excursions around 11.4, 4.8, 2.6, and 1.0 ka30. These climate shifts may have impacted the two barrier types differently: subalpine coniferous forests in Mt. Higashiazuma and deciduous broad-leaved forests in the Hakkoda Mountains. However, the detailed succession histories of both mountains remain unclear, limiting our ability to fully explain the observed patterns.

In the Hakkoda Mountains, pronounced backcrossing with P. pumila was observed, whereas no first-generation hybrid individuals (F1) between P. pumila and P. parviflora var. pentaphylla were detected (Fig. 2(c)). Considering the flowering period of P. parviflora var. pentaphylla (May to June) and P. pumila (June)32, a hypothesis for prezygotic isolation was formulated. In line with this, a previous study in Mt. Apoi, northern Japan (Fig. 7(a)) reported only two F1 hybrids among 148 P. pumila seeds and none in 81 P. parviflora var. pentaphylla seeds, although the pollen exposure of both species was heterospecific15. These findings suggest that F1 formation may be hindered by prezygotic isolation, including habitat differences, flowering asynchrony, and post-pollination hybrid incompatibility15. Notably, a study in Mt. Apoi showed that although F1 formation is rare, post-F1 hybridization can occur in both species15 (Fig. 7(a)). The same study revealed a key phenological pattern: hybrid individuals with P. pumila-type chloroplasts flowered synchronously with their P. pumila parents, whereas those with P. parviflora var. pentaphylla-type chloroplasts flowered in sync with P. parviflora var. pentaphylla parents15. This synchrony facilitates preferential backcrossing between hybrids and pure parents sharing the same chloroplast type, resulting in hybrids that maintain the chloroplast type of the most recent pollen parent while progressively increasing the proportion of nuclear DNA from the pure parental species (Fig. 7(a))15. If this chloroplast-linked flowering synchrony is a general phenomenon across hybrid zones, it would explain the observed pattern in the Hakkoda Mountains, where hybrids carrying P. pumila-type chloroplasts likely flowered with pure P. pumila individuals, facilitating successive backcrossing events that enriched the nuclear genome with P. pumila ancestry, while maintaining the P. pumila chloroplast type (Fig. 7(b)). In the Tanigawa Mountains, in contrast to the Hakkoda Mountains, individuals almost exclusively exhibited P. parviflora var. pentaphylla-type chloroplasts and P. parviflora var. pentaphylla-pronounced nuclear DNA type (Fig. 7(d)). The P. parviflora var. pentaphylla-type chloroplasts found in the Tanigawa Mountains14,17,18 indicate that pollen flowed to high altitudes during the most recent crossing. If the above-mentioned correspondence in flowering time based on chloroplast type in Mt. Apoi is also present in the Tanigawa Mountains, hybrid individuals with P. parviflora var. pentaphylla-type chloroplasts would have repeatedly backcrossed with pure P. parviflora var. pentaphylla, which share overlapping flowering times. This process may have led to the formation of the pattern currently observed in the Tanigawa Mountains, where backcrossed individuals, biased toward P. parviflora var. pentaphylla, have spread to higher elevations (Fig. 7(d)).

To identify the factors that contributed to these different patterns of introgression in the mountains, further research should include comprehensive observations of the flowering periods of both species, as well as the incidence of postzygotic incompatibility, in addition to estimating the past distribution of each species, geographical and vegetation barriers, and the timing of past hybridization events. Such observations will aid in evaluating whether past environmental conditions facilitated the overlap of habitats between P. pumila and P. parviflora var. pentaphylla and F1 formation.

In summary, this study provides an empirical illustration of the non-uniform directionality of hybridization in the field. A future objective, which the authors are already working on, would be to explore and identify the genomic regions that have introgressed from P. parviflora var. pentaphylla to P. pumila and estimate their functions associated with processes such as tree growth, shoot formation, tree form, and biotic and abiotic stress responses. Extensive interspecific hybridization has been revealed in the subgenus Pinus, indicating that such hybridization may have played a role in facilitating adaptation to diverse environmental conditions29,33,34. This phenomenon is not exclusive to Pinus; studies on other conifers and broad-leaved trees have also demonstrated adaptive responses involving introgression in response to environmental changes. For instance, studies on European white oak species (Quercus pyrenaica, Q. pubescens, and Q. robur)35, cypress species (Cupressus gigantea and C. duclouxiana)36, and poplars (Populus balsamifera and P. trichocarpa)37 have shown that the introgression of cold-adapted alleles from species inhabiting cold environments to those inhabiting warmer environments may have contributed to the expansion of species distribution in cold environments. Additionally, genes related to drought stress tolerance, a trait that can become increasingly important in the context of global warming, have been introgressed from the drought-tolerant Q. ellipsoidalis to the drought-averse Q. rubra38. Considering these insights, future research on the P. pumila and P. parviflora var. pentaphylla hybrid zone could be enhanced by the incorporation of genome-wide data from multiple independent mountain ranges, in addition to the Hakkoda Mountains. Such data could be employed to examine the correlations between environmental factors and the genotypes of parental and hybrid individuals. This approach may elucidate the disparate niches occupied by each lineage and provide insights into the evolutionary processes of adaptation to global warming.

Methods

Plant materials and DNA extraction

The plant materials used in this study were collected and handled in accordance with relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations. All necessary permissions and licenses were obtained from the appropriate authorities, including the Ministry of the Environment and Forestry Agency. Takuro Ito identified the plant specimens, and voucher specimens were deposited in the herbarium of Tohoku University (TUS 560453 - TUS560472).

Library preparation, short-read sequencing, and quality filtering of reads

We collected leaves of 78 individuals of pure P. parviflora var. pentaphylla, pure P. pumila, and potential hybrids across elevation of 780–1,580 m in the Hakkoda Mountains (40° 41′ N, 140° 52′ E) (Figs. 1 and 2 (a)). Buds on current shoots were also collected from one individual each of P. parviflora var. pentaphylla and P. pumila and preserved in RNAprotect Tissue Reagent (QIAGEN).

The collected leaves were dried using silica gel and ground in Multibeads Shocker (MB3200, YASUI KIKAI Co. Ltd., Osaka). We extracted DNA from ground leaves using DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (QIAGEN) and conducted DNA fragmentation for 2 min at 37 °C using NEBNext dsDNA fragmentase (NEB). DNA libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB), following a cost-efficient protocol that uses half the standard reagent volume39.

We modified the capture protocol described by Toussaint et al., 202140 to prepare RNA libraries for RNA probe transcription. Total mRNAs were extracted from buds on the current shoots using Multibeads Shocker (MB3200, YASUI KIKAI, Osaka), NIPPON GENE RNA extraction kit (NIPPON GENE), Assist Buffer for ISOSPIN Plant RNA (NIPPON GENE), and NEBNext Poly(A) mRNA Magnetic Isolation Module (NEB). We prepared the RNA library using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina, and inactivated RNAs using NEBNext RNase If (NEB). Next, we ligated the T7 promoter target primer for T7 RNA synthesis using NEBNext T4 ligase (NEB). These RNA libraries were transcribed into biotin RNA probes using HiScribe T7 High-Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB) and biotin-16-UTP (Thermo Fisher Scientific). We inactivated the DNAs from the generated probes with NIPPON GENE DNase I (NIPPON GENE) and purified the probes using the RNeasy Kit (QIAGEN).

Four-to-eight individuals were pooled and target-captured using myBaits reagents (Arbor Bioscience) following myBaits manual v5. Hybridization was performed for 5 min at 95℃, followed by 5 min at 65℃, and then continued for over 40 h at 65℃, then washed. The post-capture amplifications of the pooled libraries were performed using KAPA HiFi Hot Start Library Amplification Kit with Primer Mix (KAPA Biosystem), with the following cycling conditions: pre denaturation step at 98 °C for 2 min, followed by 12–18 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 20 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s, a final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min, and hold at 8 °C. These post-capture amplified libraries were purified using SPRI beads (1.0×) and sequenced on a DNBSEQ-G400 platform (150PE) (BGI Tech Solutions Co., Ltd, HongKong).

Genotyping and filtering SNPs

We mapped the filtered reads to the P. cembra transcriptome as described by Rellstab et al., 201941 using bwa mem 0.7.17 and marked the duplicated reads using markduplicates in GATK 4.1.9.0. We then collected uniquely mapped reads using sambamba 1.0.0 and genotyped using bcftools 1.18 mpileup and call. After genotyping, called SNPs were filtered for depth (DP < 3), allelic depth (AD sum < 5), AD = = 0, and AD = = AN. A VCF file was then generated, excluding three individuals with the highest missing rates and applying a missing threshold 20% and minor allele frequency cutoff of 0.01. Using uniquely mapped reads and filtering with read and allele depths, we minimized the effect of possible paralogs.

Population structure, introgression pattern, phylogenetic analysis and heterozygosity calculation

We performed LD pruning with plink v1.90 (--indep-pairwise 50 10 0.1). For PCA analysis, eigenvalues and eigenvectors were calculated using plink v1.90 and plotted using R 4.4.0. ADMIXTURE analysis was conducted on the LD-pruned SNPs using ADMIXTURE 1.3.0 and plotted in R 4.4.0. The VCF file was converted to a FASTA file using vcf2phylip.py42 and the maximum likelihood tree was reconstructed using RAxML-NG v. 1.2.1, with the GTGTR4 + R4 substitution model. While Modeltest-NG initially selected GTR4 + R4 based on The Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), we opted for GTGTR4 + R4 to explicitly model A/C, A/A and C/C as the three distinct states in our analysis.

A triangular plot was drawn based on the hybrid index and heterozygosity of each individual, calculated using the introgress package in R version 4.3.0. To calculate the hybrid index and heterozygosity, we specifically examined highly differentiated loci, with allele frequency differences between P. pumila and P. parviflora var. pentaphylla of > 0.8. To provide a broader picture of the genetic diversity of each species and hybrid, the observed heterozygosity of all SNPs from each individual was calculated using vcftools 0.1.16. We then categorized these heterozygosity values into each species and hybrids according to the PCA and ADMIXTURE results. As the Shapiro–Wilk normality test results did not reject the null hypothesis that the data were normally distributed, we conducted statistical analysis using the Tukey–Kramer method with the multcomp package in R version 4.3.0.

Chloroplast genome reconstruction and phylogenetic analysis

To reconstruct the phylogenetic tree using chloroplast sequences, we filtered and trimmed the reads using fastp 0.20.1, and mapped the trimmed reads onto the P. koraiensis chloroplast genome (AY228468.2) using bwa mem 0.7.17. SNPs were called using bcftools 1.19 mpileup and call (--ploidy 1), followed by sorting and indexing using samtools 1.19.2 and markduplicates by samblaster 0.1.26. We then we removed the three individuals with the highest missing rate in the whole nuclear genome SNP call. The VCF file was converted to a FASTA file using vcf2phylip.py42 to reconstruct the maximum likelihood tree using iqtree 1.6.12 with 1,000 bootstraps.

Estimation of recent migration rate

Recent migration rates between populations, defined as the proportion of individuals in one population originating from other populations per generation, were estimated using BA3SNP BayesAss version 3.0.5 with 100,000,000 MCMC iterations (10,000,000 burn-in, sampling on each 10,000). The input file was prepared by converting VCF file to genepop format using GenePop v4.1.3 in Stacks v2.66 and subsequently converted to BayesAss format using GenoDive v3.06.
