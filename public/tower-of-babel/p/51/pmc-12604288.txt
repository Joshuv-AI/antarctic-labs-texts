Predictors of post-partum depression amongst postnatal women in Kumasi, Ghana
Authors: Abigael Omowumi Emikpe, Hannah Obeng, Zipporah Manu Bonsu, Elizabeth Abena Tsiko, Lydia Boampong Owusu, Catherine Kroamah Dwumfour, Joseph Shahadu Issifu, Veronica Millicent Dzomeku
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOne out of four persons may develop mental disorders during their lives. Women and the poor are particularly vulnerable. Research has shown that the prevalence of postpartum depression among women is between 5% and 26% with prevalence even higher in developing countries. However, studies on this condition in Ghana are few in literature. This current study assessed the prevalence and predictors of postpartum depression among postnatal women in Kumasi, Ghana.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional study design utilizing a quantitative approach was used to conduct the study at the postnatal clinics of three selected hospitals in the Kumasi city. A multistage sampling procedure was used to select 250 postnatal women for the study. Data were gathered from the respondents through a validated Edinburg Postnatal Depression Scale. Data were analysed with SPSS version 25.0 and statistical significance was established at P ≤ 0.05.ResultsGenerally, 74.5%, 64% and 75% of the respondents had good knowledge about the risk factors; signs and symptoms; and consequences of postpartum depression respectively. The overall prevalence of postpartum depression in the selected hospitals was 14%. Regarding specific depressive symptoms, 14% of the respondents could never laugh and see the funny side of things. Maternal age (P = 0.002), postpartum blues (P = 0.000) and mode of delivery (P = 0.001) were significantly associated with PPD.ConclusionPostnatal women in Kumasi had good level of knowledge about PPD. However, gaps in knowledge were found with respect to specific signs and symptoms of PPD such as crying spells, overwhelming fatigue and insomnia. Predictors such as maternal age, partners’ support, perceived partners’ violence/abuse, and postpartum blues influenced the prevalence of PPD among the women. Healthcare providers should encourage family members and partners to provide the requisite support to mothers before, during and after childbirth.

Introduction

Depression was predicted to rank as the second most prevalent cause of disability globally in 2023 [1]. A kind of mood illness known as postpartum depression (PPD) is linked to delivery and may afflict people of either gender [3]. PPD, a severe issue that affects 6% to 26% of women after delivery, is prevalent in all cultures [2]. Disabling symptoms such as poor mood, irritability, exhaustion, sleeplessness, changes in appetite, worry, guilt, incapacity to cope, feelings of worthlessness, and suicidal thoughts may be experienced by women suffering from postpartum depression [3]. Unrealistic expectations of parenthood, poor self-esteem, and lack of confidence are common aggravating factors of symptoms. Even while postpartum depression is becoming more widely acknowledged as a disorder associated with delivery worldwide. Globally, PPD has deleterious effects on the health of the mother and the newborn, the impact is more in sub-Saharan Africa will limited resources, poor access to care and frail healthcare systems [1]. Understanding the predictive factors will aid in the development of relevant interventions and policies in order to enhance positive maternal and newborn outcomes towards universal Health Coverage (UHC) of 2030. In Ghana, there are few literature on postnatal depression varying from studies on psychosocial determinants [4], prevalence and key predictors [5], association between probable post natal depression and increased infant mortality and morbidity [6]. However, most of the studies are from rural Northern areas of Ghana with very little information from Urban centers such as Kumasi which is the second most populous city and located in the middle of Ghana. This serves as a strategic and demographically diverse setting for the diagnosis and treatment of the condition [7]. The repercussions of PPD, such as an elevated risk of suicide and infanticide, make it a significant societal concern. Because PPD is often underdiagnosed and mistreated, initiatives to enhance maternal perinatal mental healthcare are required, which is why this study is necessary.

There isn’t much data to support postpartum depression’s biological cause. Nevertheless, steroid and peptide hormone levels dramatically change throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period, affecting the mothers’ hypothalamic pituitary adrenal (HPA) and hypothalamic pituitary gonadal (HPG) axis [8]. Because mood disorders are linked to dysregulations in these endocrine axis, pregnancy and the postpartum period may have a significant impact on a mother’s mood [8]. Postpartum depression (PPD) has been linked to several variables, some of which include intimate partner violence, a history of mental illness, poor mother literacy, stress, maltreatment in early life, anxiety, and a prior history of depression [9]. A history of sexual abuse, a negative attitude towards the baby, a preference for a specific gender, a history of depression and anxiety, a risky pregnancy that included an emergency caesarean section, an umbilical cord prolapse, a preterm or low-weight infant, a lack of social support, poor eating habits, sleep cycle disruption, a lack of physical activity, and exercise are additional risk factors for postpartum depression [10]. Furthermore, mother-infant interactions which are crucial for a child’s development are hampered by postpartum depression [8]. A baby’s social, cognitive, and behavioural skills must mature in a healthy way, and this depends on the mother’s attachment, sensitivity, and parenting style. Depressed moms frequently exhibit less attachment, sensitivity, and harsher or disrupted parenting behaviours, which can have a detrimental effect on the child’s development [8]. In an environment where poverty, overcrowding, poor sanitation, malnutrition, diseases, and a lack of appropriate medical services may be common, a mother with an underlying mental illness in a low-resource setting runs the risk of giving her children sub-standard care [11].

There is a lot of variance in the published study on PPD prevalence [12].This may be because the DSM-IV and the majority of epidemiological research utilise different criteria for the period of onset [7]. Because moms themselves underreport, it has also been challenging to determine prevalence [7]. Poor people and women are more at risk. According to research, within a year after giving birth, one in seven women get a postpartum depression diagnosis [13]. Only 20% of women who suffer PPD symptoms are thought to disclose such symptoms to their medical professionals. Mothers and carers often downplay PPD symptoms as typical, inevitable side effects of delivery [7].

Numerous studies have identified a number of physiological and psychological factors that contribute to postpartum depression [14]. The degree of schooling, heavy workload, unemployment, multiparity, and marital difficulties are these factors. According to a number of research, newborns of depressive moms exhibit less engagement with their peers, slower cognitive development, more behavioural issues, and a greater chance of developing a mental disease as adolescents [15]. Although postpartum depression may affect women in any group, it is more common among young, low-income, minority moms due to the many obstacles they confront, including lack of understanding, stigma associated with mental illness, and transportation [16].

Merely 15% of women who have this illness obtain professional care, despite its severe consequences. This is due to the rarity of regular postpartum depression screening in hospitals. Following childbirth, women’s follow-up appointments often focus on the medical aspect of things, such as nursing and contraception [17]. Another factor contributing to underreporting is women’s incapacity to identify the illness. Mothers are discouraged from getting assistance from a psychiatric hospital because they fear being stigmatised and are sometimes regarded as being mentally ill [18]. Few people who seek expert assistance don’t follow their treatment plan [19]. This research aims to explore postpartum women’s awareness, prevalence, and predictors of postpartum depression in Kumasi, Ghana, in order to gather baseline data on the illness, particularly in women.

Methods

Study design

The descriptive cross-sectional design was employed in this study. A quantitative approach was utilized since it is known to focus on the quantifiable features of a phenomenon among a group of people and the use of numerical data in examining the objectives of the study [20, 21].

Study setting

The study was conducted in three hospitals within the Kumasi metropolis of the Ashanti region of Ghana. These are Kumasi South Government Hospital, Suntreso Government hospital and Manhyia Government hospital.

Study population

The study population were mothers attending post-natal clinic at the selected hospitals. Kumasi South Hospital has an average monthly postnatal attendance of 160. Suntreso Government hospital has an average monthly postnatal attendance of 150, Manhyia Government hospital has an average monthly postnatal attendance of 190. The total population for the study was 500 women.

Sampling technique/sample size

Multistage sampling procedure was used to select participants for the study. The Cochrane’s formula; n = N/1 + N (e^2) was used to estimate the sample size; where; n = sample size, N = population size and e = margin of error. 5% (0.05) margin of error was used due to a 95% confidence interval. N = 500.

Inclusion criteria

The study included women between the ages of eighteen (18) and forty-nine (49) who delivered from the first day of delivery to six weeks postpartum who were receiving postnatal care with their babies at the selected hospitals.

Exclusion criteria

The study excluded women who were below the age of eighteen years (18), not duly registered, critically ill or unwilling to participate in the study at the selected hospitals.

Data collection technique

Structured questionnaire with open- and close-ended questions was employed to collect data. The questionnaire was divided into sections. Section A focused on the demographic, obstetric and psychosocial characteristics of respondents, section B- knowledge of postpartum depression, section C- Edinburgh Postnatal depression scale (EPDS) which was used to elicit the predictors and prevalence of postpartum depression among the women. The English version of the questionnaire was translated in the local language (Twi) by the researchers. The local language version of the questionnaire accurately reflects the original questionnaire’s meaning and considered cultural applicability and it ensured proper respondents’ understanding of the content.

Analysis of data

The completed questionnaires were coded and entered in the database of the statistical package of social science (SPSS) version 25.0 for processing. Descriptive statistics of percentages and frequencies were used for the study. The demographic characteristics were presented in a table in the form of frequency and percentages, EPDS score interpretation was used to measure the prevalence of PPD in the population while graphs (bar) and pie chart were used for the knowledge. A chi-square test was run to determine the association of respondents’ demographic and socio-economic, obstetric and psychological characteristics and prevalence of PPD. An association was considered statistically significant at P ≤ 0.05.

Reliability and validity of the instrument

The questionnaire on knowledge was a modified version of tools previously used by [22] and [24] on knowledge and risk factors for postpartum depression among postpartum women in other settings. It was validated by 2 midwifery lecturers and 2 clinical midwives for scrutiny before data collection in order to ensure that the instrument covered all areas and able to provide answers to the research objectives. All data collected from respondents were analyzed. This was done to ensure that the findings reflect the actual situation on the ground. Hence the results are valid.

Results

The study had 100% response from 250 participants.

Knowledge about postpartum depression

Respondents were asked to answer 28 questions about postpartum depression under three categories i.e. (risk factors = 10, signs and symptoms = 8, complications = 7). A correctly answered question attracted 1 mark and a wrongly answered question attracted 0 mark. The option “not sure” was considered incorrect and therefore attracted no mark. The marks obtained by each respondent were quantified and aggregated. Using a 70% cut off point, respondents who score at least 70% were said to have good knowledge whereas respondents who score less than 70% were said to have low/poor knowledge. Based on the findings, majority of the respondents had good knowledge of PPD. Knowledge about risk factors of postpartum depression

Figure 1; Table 5 represents the level of knowledge of respondents about the risk factors of postpartum depression. Generally, 74.5% of the respondents had good knowledge about the risk factors of postpartum depression. However, there was variation with respect to the respondents’ knowledge on specific risk factors of postpartum depression. The commonest mentioned risk factor of PPD was lack of family support (92%) followed by financial difficulties (90%), life crises such as loss of a loved one (84%), unwanted or unplanned pregnancy (82%) and previous history of depression (80%). Other known risk factors of PPD were family history of depression (74%), genetic factors (72%) and problems with breastfeeding the baby (68%).

Figure 2; Table 6 represents the respondents’ knowledge signs and symptoms of postpartum depression. The respondents’ knowledge on specific signs and symptoms of postpartum depression varied, ranging from 94% for depressed mood or severe mood swing to 44% for overwhelming fatigue or loss of energy. Knowledge about the consequences of postpartum depression

Figure 3; Table 7 represent the respondents’ knowledge level about the consequences/effects of postpartum depression. Generally, three-quarters of the respondents (75%) had good knowledge about the consequences of postpartum depression but the respondents’ knowledge level on the specific consequences of PPD predictors differ. Specifically, 90% of the respondents correctly mentioned that PPD affects babies’ social and emotional development, 88% of the respondents mentioned that PPD cause less affection towards babies and 84% of the respondents mentioned that PPD decreases intellectual and development in children. The respondents had low knowledge level about specific consequences of PPD such as “decrease relationship satisfaction” (70%) and poor health of children (40%).

Postpartum depression

Respondents were asked to express their feelings seven [7] days post-partum using a four-point Likert scale rating of Not at all (0), not very often [1], Yes, sometimes [2] and yes, always [3]. The responses of the participants were quantified and aggregated. The minimum and maximum expected score were 0 and 30 points. Edinburg Perinatal Depression Scale (EDPS) score ≥ 16 was considered as presence of postpartum depressive symptoms. Predictors of PPD

Table 9 represents the predictors of PPD among the respondents. Discussion

This study assessed the prevalence and predictors of postpartum depression among postnatal women in the Kumasi city of Ghana. The study revealed that about three-quarters (74.5%) of postnatal women who participated in the study had adequate knowledge about the risk factors of postpartum depression. The respondents of the study knew that lack of family support, previous history of depression and financial challenges were risk factors for postpartum depression. This study finding is consistent with the findings of [22] in a Portuguese study where postnatal women had adequate knowledge about the etiologies of postpartum depression. However, the findings of our study are in sharp contrast with the findings of a similar Ghanaian study which revealed that postnatal women in rural Ghanaian communities were generally aware of PPD but lacked knowledge about how PPD comes about [7] Findings of this study also revealed that two-thirds of the respondents (64%) had adequate knowledge about the signs and symptoms of postpartum depression. The postnatal women knew that severe mood swings, withdrawal from friends and family and difficulty bonding with the baby were signs and symptoms of postpartum depression. These findings are comparable to the findings of [23] where postnatal women in India knew that mood swings and reduced interest in previously enjoyed activities were signs and symptoms of PPD. Generally, majority of the respondents of the study had adequate knowledge about the signs and symptoms, but gaps in knowledge were evident with regards to specific signs and symptoms of PPD. Specifically, more than half of the respondents lacked the knowledge that loss of appetite, crying spells and overwhelming fatigue were signs and symptoms of PPD. Similar to these findings are studies carried out in low and middle income countries which observed that postnatal women who experienced physical symptoms such as loss of appetite, insomnia and overwhelming fatigue did not attribute them to postpartum depression but regarded them as normal [14, 24]. Midwives would have to educate pregnant women at the antenatal clinic, at the delivery units after delivery and before they are discharged from the hospital on the specific signs and symptoms of PPD so that the condition can easily be identified by them.

The findings of the study also indicated that three-quarters (75%) of postnatal women who participated in this study had adequate knowledge about the effects or consequences of postpartum depression. The women knew that PPD affects children’s social and emotional development, results in less affection towards children and decreases intellectual development in children. These findings are consistent with the findings of other studies [15, 25], revealed that adequate knowledge regarding the consequences of PPD were widely documented among postnatal women in United Kingdom and Australia respectively. Similarly; [25], reported that women in rural communities in Ghana knew that maternal depression is related to infant diarrheal morbidity, breastfeeding challenges, reduced maternal-child interactions and lower cognitive development. The adequacy in knowledge about PPD observed in our study could be attributed to the fact that the participated respondents were exposed to proper and accurate information about PPD at the health facility during their ANC visits where focused ANC is practiced.

The prevalence of PPD among respondents was 14%. This prevalence is comparable to the prevalence of PPD reported among postnatal women in Malaysia (14%) [26] and Nigeria (13.4%) [27] but higher than the prevalence of PPD reported among postnatal women in developed countries such as Italy (4.7%) [28] and Brazil (8%) [29] The prevalence of PPD in the present study is also lower that that reported among postnatal women in Korea (34%) and Jamaica (36%) [30] Midwives would have to be trained on the use of the EPDS to be able to assess postpartum women for PPD for immediate management to prevent complications.

Regarding the specific depressive symptoms experienced, the study observed that 14% of the respondents could not laugh and see the funny side of things, 6% of the surveyed postnatal women always blamed themselves unnecessarily when things went wrong and 20% sometimes felt sad or miserable. Apart from these, 50% of the respondents (50%) were anxious or worried for no reason. In agreement with these findings [7], in a study conducted in Ghana identified feeling of misery, sadness and anxiety in postnatal women [7]. Midwives need to assess postpartum women at every contact and educate close family members on the signs and symptoms of PPD to enable them to identify these and report to the health facility for management. Home visits by public health nurses should be strengthened to promote early identification of symptoms.

Findings of the study revealed that psychological, demographic, and obstetric history contributed to postpartum depression. The study revealed a significant relationship between perceived partners’ support and presence of PPD. Postnatal women who had partners’ support were less likely to experience PPD compared to postnatal women who had no partners’ support. Partners’ violence was also identified as a significant determinant to PPD. Postnatal women whose partners abuse them verbally or physically (beatings) were likely to experience PPD compared to postnatal women whose partners did not abuse them. In agreement with these findings, studies of postpartum women in Korea, Canada, USA and immigrant women in Taiwan and Canada reported perceived low partners’ support and perceived high partners’ abuse as predictors of PPD [31, 32]. Women should be encouraged to report partner abuse to the police or DOVVSU for support. Midwives also need to screen women for signs of abuse and refer them to the appropriate authorities for support. Policy on violence in postpartum period to deter partners’ from abusing these women should be instituted. Male involvement in maternal and child health to promote spousal support during pregnancy and after delivery should also be encouraged.

A significant association was observed between PPD and mode of delivery, PPD and postpartum blues in agreement with the findings of [33] who reported a higher prevalence of PPD in postnatal women who delivered via CS compared to postnatal women who delivered vaginally. Demographically, maternal age was observed as a predictor of PPD. Younger postnatal women were more likely to experience PPD compared to older postnatal women. This finding corroborates the findings of a similar Ghanaian study where PPD was observed in teen mothers compared to younger adults [7]. This calls for the need to intensify efforts aimed at preventing early girl marriage and teenage pregnancy.

The study employed a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to establish the causal relationship between identified predictors and post-partum depression. Additionally, the study was conducted in one city in Ghana, Kumasi, introducing geographic bias and limiting the generalisation of findings. However, despite these limitations, the study demonstrated notable strengths. Kumasi, being the second most populous city and located in the middle of Ghana, serves as a strategic and demographically diverse setting for this study. Again, the study achieved a 100% response rate from 250 participants, which enhanced the reliability of the findings. Again, the use of multistage sampling of participants from three major hospitals in Kumasi has provided a broader representation of urban postnatal experiences. Additionally, the study followed ethically appropriate procedures such as obtaining ethical clearance with informed consent, as well as the use of a validated tool (EPDS), ensuring research integrity with methodological rigour, which are comparable with global studies.

Implications of the study

Policy and Practice Integration: The study identified maternal age, postpartum blues, mode of delivery, partner support, and partner violence as predictors of PPD. This calls for the integration of maternal mental health screening protocols into postnatal care for early detection of PPD cases.

Health Education: although the study found high PPD awareness among, gaps still exist in knowledge about specific symptoms like insomnia and crying spells. This underscores the need for targeted health education during antenatal and postnatal visits.

Male Involvement in Maternal Health: The findings of partner support and violence as predictors of PPD call for policy formulation to promote male involvement in maternal health and discourage intimate partner violence during the perinatal period.

Community-Based Interventions: home visits and community sensitizations by public health nurses can facilitate early detection and management of PPD especially in low-resource settings.

Midwifery education: the findings underscore the need to educate/train midwives to recognize psychological symptoms that can enhance early diagnosis and reduce underreporting and complications associated with PPD.

Conclusions

The prevalence of postpartum depression among postnatal women in Kumasi was 14%. Demographic factors such as maternal age, obstetric factors such as postpartum blues and mode of delivery and psychosocial factors such as partners’ support, and partners’ abuse/violence were significant predictors of postpartum depression among postnatal women in Kumasi. Generally, postnatal women in Kumasi had adequate knowledge about the risk factors, signs and symptoms and consequences of postpartum depression but knowledge on specific signs and symptoms of PPD such as overwhelming fatigue, altered sleep pattern and crying spells were limited. With respect to the obstetric factors that influenced PPD, a significant association was observed between PPD and mode of delivery; and PPD and postpartum blues.
