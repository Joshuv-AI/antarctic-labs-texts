Hepatitis C and HIV co-infection, viral load, and liver enzyme profiles among intravenous and non-intravenous drug users in Jorhat, Assam: a comparative analysis
Authors: Pinkee Phukon, Barbi Gogoi, Angshurekha Das, Sima Kumari Dutta, Purnima Barua
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis C virus (HCV) infection is a serious global public health issue, especially for intravenous drug users (IVDUs), who are more vulnerable because of risky behaviours, including sharing needles. This study sought to determine the prevalence of HIV and HCV infections, explore the relationship between liver enzyme levels and HCV viral load, and analyze sociodemographic characteristics among drug users in Jorhat, Assam, India.MethodsA total of 461 participants were enrolled, comprising 108 IVDUs and 353 non-IVDUs. For HCV screening, rapid test kits and ELISA were used. RT-PCR was performed to measure the HCV RNA viral load, and AST and ALT levels of the liver enzymes were assessed. Data were evaluated using chi-squared tests.ResultsIn comparison to non-IVDUs, IVDUs were substantially younger, more likely to be unmarried, unemployed, and less educated. HCV positivity rate was highest in the 21–30-year age group for both IVDUs (83.8%) and non-IVDUs (97.2%). Background

Hepatitis C is the most common chronic blood-borne viral infection caused by the HCV, which primarily affects the liver, leading to inflammation, liver damage, and potentially severe complications such as cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). HCV infection remains a significant global public health challenge, affecting an estimated 50 million people globally and resulting in around 1.0 million new infections each year [1]. HCV is an RNA virus classified under the Flaviviridae family and is classified into 8 genotypes and less than 90 subtypes, which poses challenges in treatment and vaccine development [2]. Chronic infection often remains asymptomatic for years, allowing the virus to silently damage the liver over time [3].

HCV infection is a substantial public health concern, particularly among high-risk populations such as intravenous drug users (IVDUs) [4]. People who inject drugs (PWID) face an elevated risk of acquiring and transmitting HCV due to behaviours such as sharing needles, syringes, and other injection paraphernalia [5]. The burden of HCV among drug users persists, contributing to a disproportionate number of new infections and increased morbidity and mortality related to liver disease [6]. In contrast, non-intravenous drug users (non-IVDUs) may acquire HCV through other routes, such as unsafe medical practices, blood transfusions, or sexual contact [7]. Comparing the prevalence and risk factors of HCV infection between IVDUs and non-IVDUs provides important insights into transmission dynamics and helps inform targeted prevention and control strategies.

In addition to HCV, human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) remains a major public health concern among PWID due to shared transmission routes, which leads to increased morbidity and mortality. Globally, PWID had an elevated risk of HIV–HCV co-infection, with pooled data suggesting that more than 25% of PWID HCV infections are also HIV-positive [8]. HIV-HCV co-infection has significant clinical implications, as HIV accelerates rate of liver fibrosis progression, increase the risk of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma incidence [9]. India reported high rate of co-infection among PWID and maximum incidence rates reported in North and Northeastern regions of India, which underscore the urgent need for integrated screening and prevention in this vulnerable population [10, 11].

HCV RNA levels, which reflect the viral load, serve as a critical marker of active infection and disease progression [12]. Liver enzymes, including aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT), are commonly used indicators of hepatic inflammation and injury. Several studies have suggested a correlation between HCV viral load and transaminase levels; however, findings have been inconsistent, and this relationship remains poorly defined, especially in populations with coexisting risk factors such as IVDU [13, 14]. There is limited national data on the relationship between HCV RNA levels and liver enzyme activity among IVDUs. It is essential to comprehend this link since it may help guide treatment choices, provide light on disease activity, and influence public health initiatives. Furthermore, the epidemiology of HCV is not consistent, with significant geographical variations in prevalence and risk factors [15]. According to recent population-based data from sub-Saharan Africa, there is considerable geographic and risk-group heterogeneity in the HCV epidemiology. Stockdale et al. [16] reported a census-based serosurvey from Blantyre, Malawi, which demonstrated a low HCV RNA prevalence of 0.2% (95% CI: 0.1–0.5%), with only 23% of anti-HCV positive individuals had active viraemia, emphasizing the limitations of serology alone and the need for confirmatory RNA testing to avoid underestimating of disease burden. This finding highlights the context-specific nature of HCV epidemiology and the requirement for targeted rather than generalized screening.

This study aims to investigate the relationship between HCV viral load and serum AST and ALT levels in IVDU patients, as well as to investigate the prevalence and associated risk factors of HCV and HIV infection among drug users, to inform public health interventions and support efforts to eliminate HCV.

Methods

Study design and participants

A hospital-based cross-sectional study was conducted at the department of Microbiology, Jorhat Medical College & Hospital (JMCH), Jorhat, Assam, from 2022 to 2023. All the consecutive cases of individuals attending JMCH during the study period who were clinically suspected of HCV infection with a history of IVDUs and non-IVDUs drug use were included. Approximately, 3 mL of venous blood samples were collected aseptically from each enrolled individuals in plain vacutainers for subsequent serological and molecular analysis. Approval from the Institutional Ethical Committee clearance was obtained in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki with letter no. SMEJ/JMCH/MEU/841/Pt-2/2011/957, Dated February 16th, 2023.

Inclusion criteria: The age range of the study participants was 18 years to ≥ 51 years (with no upper age limit). Both IVDUs and non-IVDUs were included in the study. The availability of adequate serum samples for HCV analysis and complete patients’ information with written informed consent form.

Exclusion criteria: Patient with incomplete clinical information and unwilling to provide informed consent were excluded.

Preliminary screening of HCV by rapid diagnostic test kit

All serum samples were first screened for specific HCV-antibodies using a rapid diagnostic test kit (HCV Rapid Test Device, Med source Ozone Biomedical Pvt. Ltd., Haryana, India). The HCV positive samples were further tested for anti-HCV antibodies by ELISA using third generation HCV Microlisa kit (J. Mitra and Co. Pvt. Ltd., New Delhi) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, diluted serum samples and controls were added to microwells coated with combination of HCV antigen (i.e. CORE, E1, E2, NS3, NS4 and NS5) regions of HCV genome. Antibodies to HCV, if present, bind to the immobilized HCV antigens on the microwell during the incubation period. The microwells are then thoroughly washed and enzyme-conjugated secondary (anti-human IGg conjugated with HRPO) antibodies were added followed by substrate solutions. The reaction was stopped using stop solution and optical density was measured at 450 nm using ELISA reader. Samples with OD values greater than the cutoff value were considered positive.

For the detection of HIV positives, rapid tests (Meril Tridot) were used. The test protocol was followed according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Detection of HCV RNA viral load

Extraction of HCV RNA from serum samples was done using the QIAamp Viral-RNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Serum samples (140 µl) were lysed with AVL buffer (560 µl) containing carrier RNA, followed by ethanol precipitation (560 µl). The lysate was then transferred to a silica membrane spin column, washed sequentially, and centrifuged at 8000 rpm to remove contaminants. Viral RNA was eluted in 60 µL of RNase-free elution buffer and stored at − 20 °C until further analysis.

The viral load detection of HCV was performed with artus® HCV QS-RGQ Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) on CFX96 Real-Time PCR system (BIO-RAD, California, USA) according to manufacturer’s guidelines.

This assay is based on one-step reverse transcription RT-PCR technology. The HCV RG master A and B contain enzymes that targets 240 bp region of the 5’ UTR HCV genome. The kit includes sequence-specific primers and fluorescent oligonucleotide probes along with heterologous internal control to detect and quantify the amplified genome. The kits provide 4 HCV quantification standards and lower limit of detection is 36.2 IU/ml. Viral load results were expressed as IU/mL.

Liver function tests

All specimens were referred for quantification of HCV using q-PCR subjected to quantification of liver function enzymes, including ALT and AST levels. The test was performed with the help of VITROS 5600 integrated system Microslide assays according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Statistical analysis

Absolute numbers and percentages were used to analyze data using Microsoft Excel and GraphPad Prism software. We used a chi-squared test to compare categorical variables. One-way analysis of variance was used to compare group means. P-value of ≤ 0.05 was significant.

Results

A total of 461 individuals were included in this study, comprised of 108 intravenous drug users (IVDU) and 353 non-intravenous drug users (non-IVDU) individuals. The average age of IVDU participants was 25.95 ± 9.40 years, which was considerably lower than the non-IVDU group’s mean age of 26.95 ± 9.30 (P < 0.0001). The total unmarried study population is 342 (74.2%). Of these, 24 (7.01%) were IVDUs and 318 (92.98%) were non-IVDUs.

It was found that the majority of IVDUs were unmarried 82 (71.9%) compared to non-IVDUs 32(28.1%) (P < 0.00001), and a considerably smaller percentage of IVDUs were married 24(7.0%) compared to non-IVDUs 318(92.9%). In both groups, divorce rates were very low. In comparison to non-IVDUs 118(68.2%), IVDUs saw lower unemployment rates 55(31.8), with daily wage earners accounting for 38(14.4%) of IVDUs and 226(85.6%) of non-IVDUs (P < 0.00001). Among IVDUs, students were more common 15(62.5%) than among non-IVDUs 9(37.5%). Significant differences existed in educational attainment: 88(27.2%) of IVDUs had completed higher secondary school or below, compared to 235(72.7%) of non-IVDUs; whereas only 18(13.3%) of IVDUs had completed graduation or above, compared to 117(86.6%) of non-IVDUs (Table 1). However, there was no statistically significant difference between the groups in terms of urban versus rural residence (P = 0.083), even though most individuals in both groups lived in rural areas.

Analysis of HCV status by age revealed that younger participants in both groups had a significant prevalence of HCV positivity. Among IVDUs, the highest proportion of HCV positivity was noted in the 21–30-year age group (83.8%), followed by those aged < 20 years (80.6%) (Table 2). Similarly, among non-IVDUs, the age range of 21–30 years accounted for 97.2% of confirmed HCV-positive cases and 62.3% of suspected cases. Notably, among non-IVDUs, HCV positivity was high across all age groups, with 94.4% of those over 51 having the virus. Although the suspected HCV cases were more common in the 21–30 age range for both IVDUs (62.9%) and non-IVDUs (62.3%), the positive rate for non-IVDUs was consistently high across age groups, whereas IVDUs showed greater variability. Among those who tested positive for HCV, all IVDUs were male and in the case of non-IVDUs, 92.1% of positives were male, with the remaining individuals being female.

The distribution of HCV viral load levels demonstrated a statistically significant difference between IVDUs and non-IVDUs. Significantly, in the highest viral load category (> 1001 IU/mL), the IVDUs had a relatively higher proportion (64 out of 108) compared to the non-IVDU group (90 out of 353), indicating a much higher burden of viremia among IVDUs. Conversely, undetectable viral loads were considerably more common in non-IVDUs than in the IVDU group, which had only 23 undetected cases. However, low-level viremia (< 200 IU/mL) were rare in both groups, with only 4 cases in the non-IVDUs. Moderate viral loads (201–1000 IU/mL) were also more prevalent in non-IVDUs than IVDUs, except in the 401–600 IU/mL range, where IVDUs showed a slightly higher proportion (7 out of 108) compared to non-IVDUs (15 out of 353). These data indicate a distinct viral load distribution pattern, where IVDUs exhibit fewer undetected cases but a relatively greater proportion of individuals with elevated viral loads, potentially reflecting differences in disease progression, treatment status, or other clinical factors (Fig. 1).

Among 108 HCV suspected IVDU patients, 87 participants reported engaging in high-risk injection practices, such as sharing needles and improperly cleaning syringes or needles. Additionally, drawing medications from a shared container was reported by 84 patients, which is a substantial risk factor for blood-borne infections. 21 participants additionally reported utilizing non-intravenous methods. There was a significant burden of dual infections in this cohort, as seen by the 28 individuals (25.9%) who had co-infection with both HIV and HCV. All HIV-positive were predominantly belong to younger male age group.

The evaluation of hepatic function enzymes showed that a considerable percentage of these patients had elevated mean levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), which were measured at 107.44 ± 114.30 IU/mL and 94.60 ± 97.50 IU/mL, respectively (Table 3). This suggests that there is likely liver damage or hepatic involvement.

Discussion

The findings of this study underscore the vulnerability of the IVDU population by pointing out significant sociodemographic differences between IVDUs and non-IVDUs. IVDUs were significantly younger, more likely to be single, and more likely to be unemployed than their non-IVDU counterparts. The majority of IVDUs had only completed secondary school or less, and a larger percentage were students or had lower levels of educational attainment. The high-risk behaviors that were generally reported by IVDUs in this study, such as sharing needles and not properly cleaning syringes, are probably exacerbated by these socioeconomic vulnerabilities [17, 18].

Regarding injection-related activities, almost all IVDUs reported drawing medications from a shared container, sharing needles, and improperly cleaning them actions that significantly raise the risk of spreading blood-borne illnesses [19]. Interestingly, almost one-third of participants also reported using drugs through non-intravenous routes, even though intravenous injection was the predominant method. This suggests poly-route substance use, which could further complicate risk profiles and treatment requirements.

Additionally, the study found that both IVDUs and non-IVDUs had a considerable burden of HCV infection, especially in younger age groups. The age range of 21–30 years old had the highest HCV positivity among IVDUs, and it also accounted for most confirmed and suspected HCV cases among non-IVDUs. Interestingly, HCV positivity among non-IVDUs was prevalent in all age groups, including older persons, indicating that this population may have underreported risk behaviors or non-injection-related modes of transmission [20]. These findings highlight the necessity of more comprehensive HCV screening approaches that go beyond the conventional high-risk IVDU population [21].

The virological profile further differentiates the two groups. IVDUs had significantly higher levels of HCV viremia, with 64 (59.25%) exhibiting viral loads greater than 1001 IU/mL, compared to only 90 (25.49%) in non-IVDUs. Conversely, a significantly higher proportion of non-IVDUs had undetectable or low-level viremia, suggesting either spontaneous viral clearance, treatment response, or differences in the duration and intensity of infection. The elevated virus loads among IVDUs are especially concerning because they suggest increased contagiousness and the possibility of further transmission, particularly in environments with insufficient or non-existent harm-reduction measures [22, 23].

Furthermore, about one-third 28 (25.9%) of IVDUs had co-infection with HIV, reinforcing the well-established epidemiological link between injection drug use and dual blood-borne infections [24]. When co-infection occurs, treatment and prognosis become significantly more challenging; therefore, integrated care techniques that treat both illnesses at the same time are required. The high hepatic enzyme levels (ALT and AST) in IVDUs indicate liver inflammation or damage, which is most likely caused by a persistent HCV infection, substance-induced hepatotoxicity, or HIV-related liver issues [24, 25].

Overall, this study highlights the intricate relationship among IVDUs between high-risk behaviours, sociodemographic vulnerabilities, and unfavourable clinical outcomes. Specific public health measures are required due to the high prevalence of HCV, high viral loads, and significant rates of HIV co-infection. These should include linkage to care, accessible HIV and HCV testing, comprehensive harm-reduction techniques, and education programs targeted at younger and socioeconomically disadvantaged groups. To reduce the prevalence of HIV and HCV in high-risk groups, it is critical to address the underlying causes of drug use and enhance access to prophylactic medicines [26].

Moreover, this study has several limitations. The study sample was recruited from specific clinical and community settings, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other demographics or geographic areas. In addition, dependence on self-reported injection behaviours introduces the potential for recall bias and social desirability bias. Significant confounders including the duration and frequency of drug use, alcohol consumption, previous antiviral therapy, hepatitis B co-infection, and concomitant liver disease were not fully evaluated. Moreover, apart from HCV/HIV co-infection, other possible co-infections such as HCV/HBV and HCV in patients from other high-risk groups (such as those with β-thalassemia and individuals enduring dialysis) were not assessed. Additionally, liver fibrosis staging and HCV genotyping were not executed, which confines detailed virological characterization and disease severity assessment.

Conclusion

In summary, this study highlights the intricate relationship among IVDUs between sociodemographic vulnerabilities, high-risk behaviours, and unfavourable clinical outcomes. A critical need for focused public health interventions is highlighted by the markedly higher incidence of HIV co-infection, elevated viral loads, and HCV infection in this population. Expanded screening efforts, integrated care approaches, and comprehensive harm-reduction measures are critical to minimizing transmission and improving health outcomes, especially among the young and socioeconomically disadvantaged. In both IVDU and non-IVDU groups, addressing the underlying causes of drug use is still essential to lowering the burden of HIV and HCV.
