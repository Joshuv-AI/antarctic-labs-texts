Development and validation of a preoperative nomogram for predicting residual tumor risk in breast cancer patients undergoing excisional biopsy
Authors: Yangfan Fan, Yiwei Wu, Fangfang Chen, Fang Wan, Dianlei Liu, Jingpei Long, Tao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrent research on breast-conserving surgery (BCS) focuses on recurrence and survival but overlooks the issue of residual tumors post-excisional biopsy. These remnants, crucial for surgical planning, often necessitate additional excisions, impacting BCS success. Our 5-year study of excisional biopsy patients identifies risk factors for residual tumors, offering insights to improve surgical decisions.MethodsThis study examined 233 breast cancer patients split into training and validation groups (2:1 ratio). Logistic regression models identified predictors of post-biopsy residual tumors status, leading to the creation and validation of a preoperative nomogram for residual risk.ResultsIn this study of 233 patients, 23.9% with BCS had residual tumors after biopsy, significantly less than those in the non-BCS group (P<0.001). Tumor size, biopsy method, and histopathological subtype were crucial in predicting residual tumors and were used to develop a nomogram, which showed strong predictive accuracy for preoperative residual tumor status. This tool enhances preoperative risk stratification and aids in the formulation of personalized surgical strategies by providing visual quantification of the probabilities associated with oncological clearance parameters.ConclusionsWe developed a clinically practical nomogram for predicting residual tumor status following excisional biopsy, facilitating preoperative risk stratification and personalized surgical strategy. Further prospective studies are necessary to evaluate its generalizability and accuracy.

Key findings

• This study created a practical nomogram to predict residual tumor status after excisional biopsy in breast cancer patients, with validation confirming its good accuracy and performance.

What is known and what is new?

• Breast-conserving surgery (BCS) offers good prognostic and aesthetic results for early-stage breast cancer patients, yet its use has paradoxically decreased in some developed countries due to various factors.

• Our research found a strong link between the low use of BCS and residual tumors after excisional biopsy. This study addresses the issue of tumor residuals following biopsy in breast cancer patients for the first time and presents a nomogram designed to predict the risk of residual disease.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Utilizing our nomogram enables the prediction of residual tumor status post-excisional biopsy, thereby enhancing preoperative risk stratification and informing personalized surgical strategies.

Introduction

Breast cancer continues to be the leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women worldwide (1,2). Surgical intervention remains the foundational treatment for tumor control and survival improvement (3), patients increasingly prioritize both therapeutic efficacy and cosmetic outcomes, particularly in early-stage cases (4). This dual demand has driven the widespread adoption of breast-conserving surgery (BCS) as a preferred therapeutic approach (4,5). Modern breast surgical oncologist must therefore engage in continuous skill development, integrating innovative techniques with evidence-based practice to meet evolving clinical standards (4,6,7). Nonetheless, there is a notable absence of standardized quality indicators and consensus guidelines for the comprehensive management of breast cancer, including surgical practices and the provision of high-quality care (6,8). High-volume centers (>298 cases/year) may play a crucial role in addressing this gap by offering training and facilitating the exchange of expertise (9).

Despite the good prognosis and aesthetic benefits of BCS, its rates are declining in the UK (10,11) and USA (12). At the Women’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (our center), with over 300 annual breast cancer surgeries, less than 30% are BCS. This decline is due to various factors, including preoperative communication, concerns about recurrence, and patient reluctance towards postoperative radiotherapy. We concur with previous studies (12,13) that have identified specific indications for mastectomy, including: (I) significant residual cancer burden; (II) the inability to safely administer radiotherapy; (III) failure to meet aesthetic requirements for BCS; and (IV) high-risk pathological subtypes of ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS). However, there is no evidence that the indication of mastectomy is a contraindication for BCS (12).

While existing research predominantly focuses on recurrence patterns, survival outcomes, and quality-of-life metrics in BCS (14-17), the critical yet understudied issue of residual tumor status following preoperative excisional biopsy remains overlooked. Our clinical observations reveal a novel insight: residual tumor burden post-biopsy serves as a pivotal determinant in surgical planning—a factor scarcely documented in prior studies. Notably, the persistent challenge of achieving clear surgical margins in such cases often necessitates multiple re-excisions, potentially jeopardizing the technical feasibility and cosmetic success of BCS. To address this knowledge gap, we conducted a retrospective cohort analysis of 5-year clinical data from breast cancer patients undergoing excisional biopsy at the Women’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (our institution). This investigation aims to systematically evaluate risk factors for residual malignancy, validate our clinical hypotheses, and refine evidence-based decision-making frameworks to optimize therapeutic outcomes. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-850/rc).

Methods

Patients

We performed a retrospective cohort study involving consecutive patients who underwent curative breast cancer surgery at the Women’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine between January 2019 and June 2024. A total of 284 patients were initially screened. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) preoperative confirmation of malignancy through excisional biopsy; (II) postoperative pathological diagnosis verified breast cancer; and (III) availability of comprehensive follow-up records. The exclusion criteria were: (I) incomplete clinical data or loss to follow-up; (II) extensive excisional biopsies involving multi-quadrant lesions or massive tumor burdens (>5 cm); and (III) octogenarian patients over 80 years old (predominantly receiving palliative management). Finally, 233 eligible cases were included in the analysis and then allocated to the training and validation cohorts in a 2:1 ratio through stratified randomization (Figure 1).

Measurable variables

Clinicopathological parameters included age, body mass index (BMI), tumor site, tumor size, lymph node metastasis (LNM) status (imaging-based assessment), excisional biopsy method, histopathology classification, Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) category, and residual tumor outcome. The training cohort was utilized for nomogram construction, with the validation cohort serving for performance evaluation.

Variable stratification followed these criteria—(I) age and BMI: dichotomized by median values; (II) tumor site: categorized as left or right breast; (III) tumor size: grouped into T1 ≤2 cm, 2 cm < T2 ≤5 cm, or non-mass lesions (mammogram suggestive of multiple calcifications or magnetic resonance showing non-mass enhancement); (IV) LNM status: defined as positive or negative using ultrasound, mammogram or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI); (V) excisional biopsy method: classified into ultrasound-guided Mammotome biopsy (UGMB), lumpectomy, or extended lumpectomy; (VI) histopathology: grouped as invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), DCIS, DCIS + IDC, or other subtypes; (VII) BI-RADS category: classified as low (≤4A) or high (≥4B) based on the highest category assigned from ultrasound, mammography, or MRI. Residual tumor outcome served as the primary study predicted target.

Our institution’s excisional biopsy protocol adheres to National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) (18) and European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) (19) guidelines while incorporating our clinical experience. The selection criteria are as follows: (I) UGMB is performed for masses <2 cm with low malignant potential (BI-RADS ≤4A); (II) or extended lumpectomy​is indicated when: Ultrasound/mammography demonstrates extensive architectural distortion, or MRI reveals multifocal calcifications or non-mass enhancement; (III) extended lumpectomy​ is specifically chosen when the above imaging findings (point 2) co-occur with high malignant risk assessment (BI-RADS >4A).

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were expressed as mean values with corresponding ranges, while categorical variables were reported as frequency counts accompanied by percentages. Chi-squared test was used to compare the positive rates of the resection margin after biopsy with different surgical procedures. Baseline characteristics between cohorts were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify predictors of residual tumor outcome, with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) used to quantify association strengths. Subsequently, a nomogram was developed using coefficients derived from the multivariate regression model. The nomogram’s discriminative performance was evaluated through the concordance index (C-index), slope, intercept, Brier score, and Hosmer-Lemeshow (H-L) test. Calibration curves, generated using bootstrapped resampling (n=1,000), further visualized the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed P value <0.05. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.0; http://www.r-project.org), adhering to established statistical guidelines for prognostic model development (20).

Ethical statement

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the ethics committee of Women’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine (No. IRB-20210016-R) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Results

Novel insight: residual tumor burden post-biopsy impacts surgical planning

We conducted a comprehensive review and compilation of the definitive surgical approaches employed for all 233 breast cancer patients. The incidence of residual tumor positivity following biopsy in the BCS cohort (n=67) was significantly lower, at 23.9%, compared to the 50% observed in the unilateral mastectomy (UM) or modified radical mastectomy (MRM) cohort (n=166), with a statistical significance of P<0.001 (Figure 1).

Study population and baseline characteristics

This study enrolled 233 breast cancer patients undergoing preoperative excisional biopsy, with a median age of 51 years (range, 26–78 years) and median BMI of 23.2 kg/m2 (range, 16.4–39.1 kg/m2). Subgroup distributions demonstrated predominance in: left-sided lesions (53.2%), T1 (47.2%), negative lymph node (87.1%), extended lumpectomy procedures (60.1%), IDC (38.6%), and high BI-RADS categories (57.9%), with an overall residual carcinoma rate of 42.5%. Following randomization into training (n=158) and validation (n=75) cohorts, these distribution patterns remained consistent. No significant inter-cohort differences (P>0.05) were observed in baseline characteristics including age, BMI, tumor site, tumor size, LNM, biopsy method, histopathology, BI-RADS category, or tumor residuals (Table 1). Residual carcinoma rates were 43.0% (68/158) in the training cohort and 41.3% (31/75) in the validation cohort.

Independent factors for predicting cancer residual

Table 2 presents the multivariable logistic regression analysis identifying independent predictors of residual carcinoma in breast cancer patients. Initial univariate screening demonstrated significant associations between tumor residual and two clinical parameters: (I) tumor size (non-mass lesions: OR 4.35, 95% CI: 2.06–9.52, P<0.001); (II) histopathology (DCIS: OR 3.31, 95% CI: 1.45–7.76, P=0.005; DCIS + IDC: OR 7.73, 95% CI: 2.89–22.87, P<0.001). The final multivariable model identified three independent prognostic factors: (I) tumor size: non-mass lesions as a risk factor (OR 3.30, 95% CI: 1.15–9.94, P=0.03); (II) excisional biopsy method: Protective effects for lumpectomy (OR 0.26, 95% CI: 0.08–0.85, P=0.03) and extended lumpectomy (OR 0.14, 95% CI: 0.04–0.47, P=0.002); (III) histopathology: DCIS + IDC as a risk-enhancing factor (OR 8.09, 95% CI: 2.41–30.20, P=0.001).

Development and validation of a predicting nomogram

The three validated predictors (tumor size, biopsy method, histopathological subtype) were incorporated into a clinically applicable nomogram to stratify residual tumor risk (Figure 2A). This nomogram assigned weighted points to each predictor, where the cumulative score positively correlated with residual cancer probability. For example, a breast cancer patient undergoing lumpectomy biopsy (13 points) with non-mass lesions (87 points), and IDC histology (0 points) would accumulate 100 total points, corresponding to a predicted residual cancer probability of 57%.

The nomogram demonstrated robust predictive accuracy, as evidenced by calibration curves showing close alignment between predicted and observed outcomes (Figure 2B,2C). Model performance metrics across training and validation cohorts were as follows: C-index (0.725, 95% CI: 0.647–0.803 vs. 0.755, 95% CI: 0.646–0.864), calibration slope (1.000 vs. 1.094), calibration intercept (0.000 vs. −0.126), Brier score (0.210 vs. 0.201), and H-L test (P=0.45 vs. P=0.65). Interpretation of these metrics adheres to established criteria: (I) C-index values exceeding 0.7 indicate clinically meaningful discriminative capacity; (II) a calibration slope of 1.000 reflects perfect calibration (slope <1 suggests overfitting, >1 underfitting); (III) an intercept of 0.000 denotes absence of systematic bias (positive values imply risk underestimation, negative values overestimation); (IV) Brier scores <0.1 represent excellent prediction, 0.1–0.25 acceptable performance, and >0.25 require model refinement; (V) H-L test: P>0.05 indicates that the predicted probability is not significantly different from the actual probability (good calibration).

Discussion

This study revealed a critical yet underrecognized association between residual tumor burden following biopsy and its significant impact on surgical planning. We developed a clinically practical nomogram incorporating three independent predictors—tumor size, biopsy technique, and histopathological subtype—that demonstrated robust predictive accuracy for preoperative residual tumor status. Importantly, this tool not only facilitates preoperative risk stratification but also supports personalized surgical strategy formulation through visual probability quantification of oncological clearance parameters.

While tumor size is a well-established predictor of residual disease after breast cancer surgery—with prior studies demonstrating increased risks for tumors >3 cm in invasive carcinoma (21) and >30 mm in DCIS (22)—our analysis revealed no significant residual risk difference between T1 and T2 tumors. This discrepancy likely stems from two key factors: first, our cohort predominantly comprised early-stage (I–II) breast cancers where smaller tumor size often allows complete removal during initial biopsy. Second, our study uniquely categorized non-mass lesions as a distinct subgroup—a clinically significant classification given these lesions’ inherent tendency for discontinuous spread and close association with DCIS, which elevates residual cancer risk compared to well-defined masses (23). Surgeons cannot reliably discern tumor margins by palpation during resection, making complete tumor excision challenging and resulting in a certain rate of residual cancer. In addition, non-mass lesions (microcalcifications) may persist even after neoadjuvant chemotherapy, which is administered to shrink tumors before surgery (24). This persistence can indicate the presence of residual tumors, necessitating careful surgical planning to ensure the complete removal of the tumor. Intraoperative ultrasound can effectively aid in removing non-mass lesions by providing real-time visualization of tumors and surrounding tissues, allowing for more precise resections, fewer positive margins, and minimal removal of healthy tissue, thus enhancing the surgical process (25).

Studies suggest that larger DCIS-associated masses and non-mass lesions elevate residual cancer risk (22,23), underscoring the need for preoperative DCIS size evaluation in surgical planning (26). However, growing evidence questions potential overtreatment of DCIS in breast cancer care (27,28). In the US, DCIS has been an independent risk factor (vs. IDC) for contralateral prophylactic mastectomy for over a decade (29,30). Our data show both pure DCIS and DCIS + IDC subtypes have higher residual cancer rates than other histopathological profiles. Crucially, multivariate analysis identified DCIS + IDC—not pure DCIS—as an independent predictor of residual malignancy. While aligning with the view that most DCIS cases are low-risk (27), our results highlight a critical exception: DCIS + IDC detected preoperatively often correlates with an extensive intraductal component (EIC), defined as ≥25% intraductal tumor within invasive carcinoma (31). EIC significantly raises residual disease risk post-BCS (31). Notably, a large preoperative imaging study found that while EIC-positive cancers had higher local recurrence rates than EIC-negative cases, overall and recurrence-free survival rates remained comparable (32). Thus, biopsy pathology—specifically DCIS + IDC or EIC status—is pivotal in guiding surgical strategies (33).

The prevailing consensus advocates for a minimum surgical margin of 2mm for patients with DCIS undergoing BCS, as a measure to mitigate the risk of recurrence. This standard is endorsed by the 2016 joint guidelines from the American Society of Surgical Oncology (SSO), the American College of Radiology (ACR), and the American Society of Clinical Oncology (ASCO), which highlight that 2 mm negative margins significantly reduce the incidence of ipsilateral breast cancer recurrence compared to narrower margins (34). Although evidence suggests that wider margins generally correlate with lower recurrence rates, the optimal margin width remains a subject of ongoing debate (35-37). The SSO-American Society for Radiation Oncology (ASTRO) consensus recommends “no ink on tumor” as sufficient for invasive cancers treated with BCS and radiation therapy (38). This recommendation is based on meta-analyses indicating that local recurrence rates are comparable between “ink-negative” margins and wider margins in cases of invasive disease. In this study, our criteria for determining negative margins were based on narrow margins (“ink-negative” margins).

Our center’s experience, along with the results of this study, demonstrates that negative surgical margins are associated with the biopsy method, which in turn influences surgical decision-making, particularly regarding the feasibility of BCS. The findings suggest that the biopsy method serves as a predictor of preoperative tumor burden. Over the past 30 years, UGMB has played a pivotal role in the diagnosis and management of breast lesions (39,40). Younger patients tend to prefer UGMB over traditional surgical approaches for ambiguous or low-risk lesions due to its superior cosmetic outcomes, shorter procedural duration, and expedited recovery times (41). Although UGMB is capable of excising small lesions, the 2 cm needle groove and 7-G needle size results in a sliced rather than whole removal, which may expose tumor cells and elevate the risk of cancerous implantation (42). The incidence of residual cancer following UGMB was observed to be higher than that following lumpectomy, which is consistent with clinical expectations. A significant concern is whether UGMB impacts the eligibility for BCS. While previous research indicates higher rates of residual cancer with UGMB compared to traditional methods, it does not preclude the possibility of BCS, as effective margin management during surgery can still facilitate successful breast conservation (42).

Lumpectomy and extended lumpectomy are surgical procedures utilized in the treatment of breast tumor, designed to excise tumors while preserving the breast’s aesthetic and functional integrity (43). In the treatment of breast cancer, these interventions are particularly appropriate for small, localized tumors, thereby facilitating BCS. Ensuring clear surgical margins is of utmost importance, supported by methodologies such as pre-operative imaging, intraoperative frozen section analysis, and post-operative pathological examination. Extended lumpectomy, which entails the excision of a larger portion of breast tissue, may be warranted in instances where the tumor is larger or exhibits more aggressive characteristics. This approach seeks to achieve wider margins and diminish the risk of residual disease. Nevertheless, it is crucial to balance the extent of tissue removal with potential impacts on cosmetic outcomes and patient satisfaction (44).

Our study is constrained by its single-center design and the limited number of cases examined over a 5-year period. Consequently, our findings may not be generalizable to other healthcare systems or regions. The retrospective nature of our study introduces potential confounding factors that cannot be entirely eliminated. For instance, if a patient initially declines BCS, it could influence the choice of biopsy method and result in causal reversal. Additionally, our study did not assess the impact of post-biopsy residual cancer rates on final surgical planning. Furthermore, the study did not include molecular subtype analysis, as immunohistochemical markers [estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), Ki-67 and HER2] were not tested at the time of post-biopsy, rendering the direct use of postoperative data problematic.

Conclusions

Our study establishes residual tumors after excisional biopsy as a pivotal yet underrecognized contributor to the underutilization of BCS. As the first investigation systematically addressing post-biopsy tumor persistence in breast oncology, we developed and validated a preoperative nomogram that reliably predicts residual tumor risk. This tool provides three critical clinical advantages: (I) preoperative identification of high-risk candidates requiring modified surgical planning; (II) quantitative guidance for margin assessment protocols; and (III) dynamic risk visualization facilitating patient-surgeon shared decision-making. While demonstrating robust discriminative capacity in internal validation, multicenter prospective trials are warranted to confirm its predictive consistency across diverse practice settings and biopsy techniques.
