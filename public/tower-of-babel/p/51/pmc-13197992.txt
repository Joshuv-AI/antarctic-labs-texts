Nonbacterial Tumorous-Thrombotic Right-Sided Endocarditis in a Young Patient With Testicular Germ Cell Tumor
Authors: Carlos A. Roldan, Arianna Pregenzer-Wenzler, Michelle D. Ratliff, Karla B. Almaraz, Erika L. Jones, Thomas M. Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

In patients with malignancies, assessment of left ventricular and right ventricular systolic function by transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) before chemotherapy is a guideline-directed practice. Malignant tumors can metastasize to the heart via hematogenous or lymphatic spread or by direct extension, most commonly involving the pericardium and right heart chambers, less frequently the myocardium, and rarely the endocardium.1 Malignancies are also associated with hypercoagulability and may be complicated by deep venous thrombosis, pulmonary embolism (PE), or, rarely, nonbacterial thrombotic endocarditis (NBTE).2 Therefore, TTE is valuable not only for baseline functional assessment but also for the detection of unsuspected metastatic cardiac disease or NBTE.

Detection of right heart masses in a patient with metastatic testicular germ cell tumor (TGCT) poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges and may increase morbidity and mortality.

Case Presentation

A 23-year-old man with no prior medical or family history presented to the emergency department with a 1-month history of a right testicular mass, anorexia, nausea, and 15-pound weight loss. The patient denied infectious or cardiopulmonary symptoms. On examination, findings included lack of fever, tachycardia (112 beats/min), normotension (112/75 mm Hg), and normoxia on room air. With the exception of tachycardia, results of cardiopulmonary examination were normal. A firm, nontender right testicular mass approximately the size of a tennis ball was noted, without peripheral lymphadenopathy or lower extremity edema. The leading diagnosis was a primary testicular tumor.

Scrotal ultrasound revealed a 7-cm heterogeneous mass consistent with malignancy. Computed tomography of the head, chest, abdomen, and pelvis showed no brain metastases but demonstrated multiple pulmonary, mediastinal, hilar, left axillary, and left supraclavicular lesions, as well as extensive retroperitoneal, mesenteric, and omental lymphadenopathy.

Serum tumor markers were markedly elevated: lactate dehydrogenase 7,814 U/L (normal range, 300-670 U/L), α-fetoprotein 16,877 ng/mL (normal range, 2-15 ng/mL), and β-human chorionic gonadotropin 270,000 mIU/mL (normal range, <5 mIU/mL). These findings were consistent with metastatic TGCT.

The patient underwent uncomplicated right radical inguinal orchiectomy. Histopathology confirmed a mixed nonseminomatous TGCT composed of embryonal carcinoma (70%), yolk sac tumor (20%), and teratoma (5%).

The TV and PV leaflets appeared structurally normal, with no evidence of obstruction and trace tricuspid regurgitation (TR) detected by continuous-wave Doppler.

Mild pulmonary hypertension with an estimated right ventricular systolic pressure of 40 to 45 mm Hg, mild right ventricular dilation, and abnormal septal motion consistent with right ventricular pressure overload were present. The left heart and valves were normal.

The echocardiographic features suggested vegetations but could not distinguish among tumorous, thrombotic, inflammatory, and infective etiologies. Regardless of origin, the masses posed a high risk for PE, and surgical or percutaneous extraction was considered. A multidisciplinary team (cardiology, cardiothoracic surgery, urology, infectious disease, and hospital medicine) opted for medical management, recommending (1) blood cultures to exclude infective endocarditis, (2) emergent brain magnetic resonance to rule out metastases, (3) intravenous unfractionated heparin for possible thrombotic vegetations, and (4) oncology consultation for initiation of chemotherapy.

Given clinical stability and regression of masses, the patient was transitioned to low–molecular weight heparin (LMWH), appropriate therapy in malignancy-associated thrombosis with similar efficacy and safety as factor Xa inhibitors.3 The patient was discharged in stable condition on hospital day 7.

After 6 weeks of LMWH therapy and two cycles of bleomycin, cisplatin, and etoposide chemotherapy, follow-up TTE showed complete resolution of the TV mass, but reappearance of a larger TV annular mass (1.4 × 1.2 cm; Figure 5A, Video 5) and further reduction of the RVOT mass (0.6 × 0.5 cm; Figure 5B, Video 6).Figure 5Serial two-dimensional TTE, parasternal long-axis right ventricular inflow systolic (A) and basal parasternal short-axis diastolic (B) views obtained after 6 weeks of outpatient anticoagulation and chemotherapy, demonstrates complete resolution of the anterior TV leaflet mass and the appearance of a new, larger TV annulus mass (orange arrows) and a single, smaller, and further morphologically altered RVOT mass extending to the base and right ventricular aspect of the septal TV leaflet (green arrow).

Despite the completion of five chemotherapy cycles, including salvage carboplatin and etoposide, tumor markers remained elevated and imaging continued to show diffuse metastatic disease. The patient’s condition progressively declined, and they ultimately died.

Discussion

TGCTs, comprising seminomas and nonseminomatous tumors, account for approximately 95% of testicular cancers in young men and have an excellent 5-year survival rate of about 95%.4 Radical inguinal orchiectomy with retroperitoneal lymph node resection is recommended for histopathologic staging, identification of micrometastases, risk stratification, and guidance of chemotherapy. Cardiac involvement by TGCT is exceedingly rare and typically involves the right heart chambers5; however, direct valvular involvement has not been well characterized.6

We describe a young patient with metastatic TGCT complicated by large, highly mobile right-sided cardiac masses involving the TV, TV annulus, RVOT, and PV. Initial regression of these masses with anticoagulation, despite a small PE, suggested a thrombotic component, while residual enhancement on CMR indicated possible tumor infiltration. Subsequent reappearance of a TV annular mass during ongoing therapy further supports a mixed tumorous-thrombotic etiology.

A review of the literature identifies three prior reports describing TGCTs with incompletely characterized involvement of the TV. Deck et al.7 reported the case of a 20-year-old patient with mixed TGCT and persistent metastatic disease despite orchiectomy and ongoing chemotherapy. Three years after the initial diagnosis, evaluation of a newly detected heart murmur with TTE revealed multiple mobile masses on the TV with trace TR. Given the patient’s history of inferior vena cava teratoma complicated by PE and multiple recurrences of teratoma, cardiac exploration was pursued. Intraoperatively, multiple firm, lobulated, white masses measuring 0.5 to 1.0 cm in diameter were identified on the TV. Despite preserved TV function and the absence of cardiac symptoms, the valve was replaced with a bioprosthetic prosthesis. Histopathologic examination of the valvular masses was consistent with mature teratoma.

May et al.8 described a 42-year-old man with a yolk sac tumor in whom transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) revealed a large, pedunculated right atrial mass extending from the superior vena cava to the lateral aspect of the TV. Operative findings demonstrated tumor occupying the right atrium and extending proximally into the superior vena cava and distally through the TV into the right ventricle. Following tumor resection, mild to moderate TR was treated using De Vega annuloplasty. However, TV involvement was not well characterized either echocardiographically or surgically.

Liu et al.9 reported a 51-year-old man with nonseminomatous TGCT in whom TTE and surgical findings demonstrated an extensive, apparently contiguous right-sided cardiac mass extending from the superior vena cava to the right atrium, TV, right ventricle, and bilateral pulmonary arteries. As in the prior reports, TV involvement was not well characterized echocardiographically or surgically.

Following orchiectomy, mass excision, anticoagulation, and chemotherapy, all three patients remained clinically stable at 12, 17, and 15 months of follow-up, respectively.

Distinctive features of our case compared with prior reports include (1) detailed echocardiographic characterization of multiple right-sided cardiac masses, (2) involvement of the RVOT and PV, (3) serial changes in the descriptive features of the valvular masses with general overall improvement and lack of worsening regurgitation during therapy; and, most important, (4) the avoidance of high-risk surgical intervention through appropriate medical therapy.

The pathogenesis of NBTE involves endothelial injury, thrombogenesis, inflammation, or combinations thereof. In advanced malignancy, diffuse carcinomatosis and valvular infiltration may also contribute. Thus, NBTE may encompass thrombotic, inflammatory, tumorous, or mixed vegetations.2,10, 11, 12

Libman-Sacks endocarditis, characteristic of systemic lupus erythematosus, represents the inflammatory-thrombotic variant.2,11,13 Purely thrombotic NBTE occurs in primary and secondary hypercoagulable states such as congenital hypercoagulability conditions, primary and secondary antiphospholipid syndrome, or malignancy, including TGCT.12 Most NBTE lesions (>95%) affect left-sided valves; right-sided involvement is rare.

NBTE is often asymptomatic but can manifest with embolic phenomena. Left-sided lesions frequently cause cerebral or systemic emboli, whereas right-sided lesions may lead to PE or, in the presence of a patent foramen ovale, paradoxical embolism. As seen in our case, NBTE in malignancy carries a poor prognosis.

The detection and characterization of cardiac masses depend on the spatial and temporal resolution of imaging modalities and their ability to provide tissue characterization. TTE offers excellent temporal resolution (40-120 frames/s), making it well suited for identifying highly mobile lesions. However, its moderate spatial resolution (1-3 mm) limits the detection of small, deeply located, or posterior valve masses. TEE provides superior spatial resolution (approximately 0.5-1 mm) while maintaining high temporal resolution, allowing reliable visualization of small, mobile, valve-associated lesions. Consequently, TEE is particularly valuable, and often considered the reference standard, for the detection of valve masses, which are typically small and predominate on the left-sided valves. Diagnostic yield may be further enhanced with three-dimensional imaging and contrast agents.

CMR offers good spatial resolution (1-2 mm in plane) and moderate temporal resolution, generally adequate for assessing mass size and anatomic relationships. Its principal strength lies in tissue characterization using multiparametric techniques, including T1- and T2-weighted imaging, parametric mapping, perfusion imaging, and late gadolinium enhancement. However, CMR is less sensitive for detecting small, highly mobile valve lesions. Cardiac computed tomography (CCT) provides the highest spatial resolution (approximately 0.3-0.6 mm) and excels in defining lesion margins, attachment sites, calcification, and perivalvular anatomy, though limited temporal resolution reduces sensitivity for mobile vegetations.

Hybrid imaging with positron emission tomography combined with CCT or CMR provides additional metabolic information and is particularly useful in cases with indeterminate findings or suspected inflammatory, infective, or malignant processes. These techniques also aid in disease staging and assessment of treatment response.

Given that valve-associated masses such as NBTE are often small and highly mobile, a multimodality imaging approach is frequently required. TTE remains an appropriate first-line modality for assessing mass mobility and hemodynamic consequences, particularly for anterior or right-sided valve lesions, as illustrated in the presented case, but may miss small posterior left-sided lesions. TEE offers the highest sensitivity and specificity for detection and morphologic characterization,11,13 while CMR and CCT play complementary roles in tissue characterization, anatomic definition, and evaluation of alternative diagnoses.

In summary, the optimal evaluation of valvular cardiac masses, including NBTE, may rely on multimodal imaging,14 with echocardiography, particularly TEE, as the cornerstone of diagnosis and advanced imaging reserved for problem solving and further characterization. Notably, prospective comparative data among all imaging modalities for NBTE are lacking. Finally, despite these imaging tools, definitive diagnosis will still require surgical histopathology.

Treatment of NBTE depends on the underlying cause. In systemic lupus erythematosus–related NBTE, combined immunosuppressive and antithrombotic therapy with anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy is beneficial.15 In antiphospholipid syndrome, vitamin K antagonists remain first line; direct oral anticoagulants may increase stroke risk. As illustrated in this case, in malignancy-associated or tumorous-thrombotic NBTE, chemotherapy combined with LMWH or direct oral anticoagulants may be advantageous.

Our case underscores the importance of a multidisciplinary approach in the diagnosis and management of NBTE. In patients with advanced malignancy and NBTE, medical therapy appears beneficial, whereas the risks of surgical intervention may outweigh the potential benefits. Exclusion of infective endocarditis and brain metastases, prompt initiation of anticoagulation, and timely chemotherapy likely prevented further embolic complications and contributed to short-term cardiac stability; however, the patient’s long-term prognosis remained poor because of refractory metastatic disease.

Conclusion

This case illustrates the rare association between metastatic TGCT and right-sided tumorous-thrombotic NBTE, demonstrates the dynamic changes and overall improvement of this type of NBTE with medical therapy, and highlights the value of TTE in its detection and characterization.
