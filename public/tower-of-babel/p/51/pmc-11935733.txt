Association of Prepregnancy Cardiometabolic Health With Hypertensive Disorders of Pregnancy Among Historically Underrepresented Groups in the United States
Authors: Yaa A. Kwapong, Theresa Boyer, Oluwalonimi Adebowale, S. Michelle Ogunwole, Arthur Jason Vaught, Chiadi E. Ndumele, Allison G. Hays, Roger S. Blumenthal, Erin D. Michos, Elizabeth Selvin, Josef Coresh, Anum S. Minhas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrepregnancy diabetes and obesity are associated with hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDPs). However, the proportion of cases of HDP in the population explained by diabetes and obesity (population attributable fraction), especially among American Indian and Alaska Native and Native Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander, is not well characterized.Methods and ResultsWe conducted a cross‐sectional analysis of data on individuals with a live singleton birth from the US National Vital Statistics System between 2016 and 2019. We used adjusted logistic regression to estimate the prevalence odds ratios of HDPs and tested interaction for race and ethnicity. We calculated the population attributable fraction for the effect of obesity and diabetes on HDPs. Among 13 201 338 birthing individuals, (mean age, 29±6 years), 7% had HDP. The prevalence of HDP was highest among American Indian and Alaska Native individuals (9.1%). Prepregnancy diabetes (prevalence odds ratio, 2.63 [95% CI, 2.59–2.67]) and obesity (prevalence odds ratio, 2.95 [95% CI, 2.93–2.97]) were associated with HDPs. Compared with non‐Hispanic White individuals, the association of diabetes with HDPs was strongest among Native Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander (prevalence odds ratio, 3.05 [95% CI, 2.48–3.77]), and the association of obesity with HDP was strongest among Asian individuals (prevalence odds ratio, 3.44 [95% CI, 3.35–3.54]; all P for interaction <0.05). Population attributable fractions for diabetes and obesity were highest among Native Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander individuals (diabetes, 3.7% [95% CI, 3.3%–4.0%]; and obesity, 45% [95% CI, 41.9%–47.8%]).ConclusionsPrepregnancy diabetes and obesity are associated with HDP across all racial and ethnic groups. Diabetes and obesity have highest population attributable fractions among Native Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander individuals and should be aggressively targeted during childhood, adolescence, and young adulthood to reduce risk of HDPs.

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDPs), which include preeclampsia, eclampsia, and gestational hypertension, complicate 5% to 8% of all pregnancies and are a leading cause of maternal morbidity and death worldwide. 1 HDPs have also been associated with an increased risk for hypertension, coronary artery disease, and stroke later in life for the mother 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 and increased risk for cardiometabolic derangements for the child. 7 , 8 In the United States, there has been an increase in the prevalence of HDPs in recent years. 9 , 10 According to the Center for Disease Control and Prevention, between 2017 and 2019, the prevalence of HDPs among delivery hospitalizations in the United States increased from 13% to 16%. 11 Importantly, stark disparities have been noted, with prevalence of HDPs reported highest among non‐Hispanic Black and non‐Hispanic American Indian and Alaska Native (AIAN) individuals. 11 These statistics suggest differences in the factors contributing to risk of HDPs across racial and ethnic groups.

Prepregnancy cardiometabolic factors (eg, diabetes and obesity) are associated with increased risk for HDPs. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 The prevalence of diabetes is increasing among individuals of reproductive age in the United States. 16 , 17 , 18 Similarly, there is a rapid rise in the prevalence of obesity in reproductive‐age individuals. 19 , 20 Insulin resistance and obesity play an important role in the development of endothelial dysfunction, which is a driving force in the pathogenesis of preeclampsia. 21 , 22 , 23 Prior literature shows that women who have elevated body mass index (BMI) (overweight or obese) have higher rates of gestational hypertensive disorders compared with women who have normal weight. 24 This underscores the importance of optimal cardiometabolic health before the onset of pregnancy for improved maternal and fetal outcomes.

As with HDPs, there are racial and ethnic differences in the prevalence of prepregnancy cardiometabolic risk factors. 25 For every year from 2016 to 2019, the rates of obesity were higher in non‐Hispanic Black individuals compared with other groups. 19 While the associations of prepregnancy diabetes and obesity with HDPs are well documented, the proportion of cases of HDPs in the population explained by these risk factors (population attributable fraction [PAF]) is not well characterized, especially for groups such as AIAN individuals who are recognized to have high prevalence of HDP. 11 PAFs provide a useful way for quantifying risk factor burden in an attempt to implement measures to improve public health and reduce disparities. Using the National Institute of Minority Health and Health Disparities Research Framework, 26 we conceptualized that the association of cardiometabolic disorders with HDPs will differ by race and ethnicity, which are social constructs influenced by structural, community, interpersonal, and individual‐level determinants. Additionally, we hypothesized that the PAFs due to prepregnancy obesity and diabetes will differ across groups, supporting increasing resources to target these cardiometabolic risk factors prenatally. This study, therefore, aims to (1) investigate the association of prepregnancy diabetes and obesity with HDP and (2) to evaluate the proportion of cases of HDPs explained by these risk factors stratified by race and ethnicity, particularly focused on historically underrepresented groups such as AIAN, Native Hawaiian and Other Pacific Islander (NHOPI), and non‐Hispanic Black individuals.

METHODS

Data Source, Study Design, and Study Population

In this cross‐sectional study, we used data from the US National Vital Statistics System from the National Center for Health Statistics between 2016 and 2019. 27 These publicly available surveillance data include birth registration records of all live births in the United States. Data for birth records including maternal demographics, clinical history, and complications of pregnancy are collected at birth by a health care professional, usually a physician or midwife, using self‐reported data and health records. All data and materials used are publicly available at US National Vital Statistics and can be accessed at https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nvss.index.htm.

We included all women who delivered a live singleton birth in the United States between 2016 and 2019. Exclusion criteria included nonsingleton pregnancy, non‐US residency at the time of delivery (people whose listed residence was outside the 50 US states and the District of Columbia) and history of prepregnancy hypertension (Figure 1). This study was exempt from review by an institutional review board, and the requirements of informed consent were waived because it used deidentified, publicly available National Vital Statistics data. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines.

Flowchart of the analytic sample.

Definition of HDPs

The primary outcome of interest was HDPs, which included gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, and eclampsia and excluded chronic hypertension. As per the standard protocol of National Vital Statistics, 27 these diagnoses were documented by the professional birth attendant at the time of delivery on the basis of health records.

Ascertainment of Prepregnancy Diabetes and Obesity

Prepregnancy diabetes was based on documentation from birth certificate data on the basis of health records. Prepregnancy obesity was defined as BMI ≥30 kg/m2 for all races and ethnicities except for Asian individuals in whom obesity was defined as BMI ≥27.5 kg/m2, as per the guidelines of the World Health Organization. 28 BMI was calculated from prepregnancy weight and height from birth certificate data on the basis of health records.

Assessment of Covariates

Maternal sociodemographic characteristics included self‐reported race and ethnicity (Hispanic, AIAN, Asian, NHOPI, non‐Hispanic Black, and non‐Hispanic White), maternal age at delivery (in years), educational level (high school or less, General Education Development/high school graduate, some college, bachelor's degree or higher), smoking (yes/no), nativity (US‐born/foreign‐born), insurance (Medicaid, private insurance, self‐pay, other), and duration of prenatal care (no prenatal care, beginning from first trimester, second trimester, and third trimester).

Statistical Analysis

Maternal sociodemographic, obstetric, and cardiovascular characteristics were summarized using proportions for categorical variables and mean±SD for continuous variables. We used logistic regression to estimate the prevalence odds ratio (POR) of HDPs and included an interaction term between race and ethnicity and prepregnancy diabetes and obesity, adjusting for maternal age, education, smoking, birth order, nativity, insurance, and prenatal care. We performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to nulliparous individuals. It should be noted that HDPs were an uncommon outcome (<10%), and therefore the POR can be used to approximate the prevalence ratios. 29

To assess nonlinearity in the relationships between maternal prepregnancy BMI and HDPs across maternal race and ethnicity, we fit cubic b‐splines with knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the distribution. We then calculated the predicted probability of HDPs across the continuum of BMI conditional on the median or modal values of the data.

The PAF for prepregnancy diabetes and obesity were then calculated for each race and ethnicity group. In calculating the PAF, we first calculated the proportion of HDP cases exposed to the prepregnancy cardiometabolic risk factor (eg, pregestational diabetes and obesity). Next, we multiplied this proportion by (adjusted POR−1)/adjusted POR. 30

Finally, we investigated use of timely prenatal care stratified by maternal race and ethnicity as a means to investigate access of care, which may be contributing to observed differences across groups. We also performed multinomial logistic regression to determine the association of race and ethnicity and prenatal care timing adjusting for maternal age, education, health insurance status, parity, nativity, and smoking status.

All analyses were performed from August 2023 to June 2024 with R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and a 2‐sided α level of <0.05 was used to assess the statistical significance of the results. Multinomial logistic regression was performed using the “nnet” package.

RESULTS

Baseline Maternal Characteristics

Overall, among the 13 201 338 birthing individuals included in the study (mean age, 29±6 years), self‐identified racial and ethnic groups were as follows: 53.8% non‐Hispanic White, 24.4% Hispanic, 14.1% non‐Hispanic Black, 6.7%, Asian, 0.8% AIAN, and 0.2% NHOPI (Table 1). Among individuals with an HDP, 89% were at least a General Education Development recipient/high school graduate, 40.7% were nulliparous, and 79.5% received early prenatal care starting in the first trimester. The majority of individuals with an HDP were born in the United States (84.3%), >50% were married, and 6.6% reported cigarette smoking. When comparing characteristics stratified by maternal race and ethnicity, AIAN, NHOPI, non‐Hispanic Black and Hispanic individuals had the lowest rates of private health insurance and of obtaining bachelor's degree or higher education. AIAN, NHOPI, non‐Hispanic Black individuals had the highest rates of late (second/third trimester) or no prenatal care received (Table S1).

Prevalence of HDPs, Prepregnancy Diabetes and Obesity by Race and Ethnicity

The overall prevalence of HDPs was 7%. When stratified by racial and ethnic groups, the prevalence of HDPs was highest in AIAN individuals (9.1%) followed by non‐Hispanic Black (8.7%) and NHOPI (7.5%) (Figure 2A) individuals. Overall, the prevalence of prepregnancy diabetes was 0.8%. The highest prevalence of prepregnancy diabetes was observed in the AIAN group (2.0%) followed by the NHOPI group (1.6%) (Figure 2B). The prevalence of prepregnancy obesity was 27.3% among all individuals, with the highest prevalence in NHOPI individuals (47.2%) followed by AIAN individuals (38.98%) (Figure 2C). The prevalence of both prepregnancy diabetes and obesity was 0.43%.

Association of Prepregnancy Diabetes and Obesity With HDPs

Prepregnancy diabetes was associated with HDPs, overall adjusted (POR, 2.63 [95% CI, 2.59–2.67]) and across all racial and ethnic groups (Figure 3). Compared with non‐Hispanic White individuals, the association of prepregnancy diabetes with HDPs was strongest among NHOPI individuals (POR, 3.05 [95% CI, 2.48–3.77]; P for interaction for race and ethnicity <0.05). Prepregnancy obesity was also significantly associated with HDPs overall (POR, 2.95 [95% CI, 2.93–2.97]) and across all racial and ethnic groups. Compared with non‐Hispanic White individuals, the association of prepregnancy obesity with HDPs was strongest among Asian individuals (POR, 3.44 [95% CI, 3.35–3.54]; P for interaction for race and ethnicity <0.05). Our findings were similar when we restricted our sample to nulliparous individuals (Table S2).

The estimated PAF for diabetes, obesity, and overweight BMI for HDPs per race and ethnicity is shown in Figure 4. DISCUSSION

Among this nationally representative cohort of birthing people, the overall prevalence of HDPs was 7%, with AIAN, non‐Hispanic Black, and NHOPI individuals representing the groups with highest prevalence. We found a strong association of prepregnancy diabetes and obesity with HDPs, with high PAF of obesity (25%–50%) across all racial and ethnic groups. Prepregnancy diabetes and obesity had particularly high PAFs among NHOPI individuals. When BMI was evaluated continuously, at lower levels of BMI, the predicted probability of HDPs was highest among non‐Hispanic Black individuals and second highest at increased BMI ranges. Importantly, NHOPI, AIAN and non‐Hispanic Black people had the highest rates of no or late (second/third trimester) initiated prenatal care during pregnancy. These groups also had the lowest report of having private health insurance or of obtaining a bachelor's degree or higher education.

Our study validates prior studies 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 and extends further to demonstrate the association of prepregnancy obesity and diabetes with HDPs across racial and ethnic groups in the United States. It is likely that prepregnancy obesity and diabetes contribute to endothelial dysfunction and inflammation in the development of HDPs. 21 , 22 , 23 In particular, our study includes previously understudied groups like NHOPI and AIAN individuals. We find that the association of prepregnancy diabetes and obesity with HDPs is especially high among NHOPI individuals, underscoring the importance of optimizing prepregnancy cardiometabolic health for this group. NHOPI individuals are often marginalized in the United States. They tend to experience interpersonal and structural racism, 31 defined as the totality of ways in which societies foster racial discrimination through mutually reinforcing systems of housing, education, employment, earnings, benefits, credit, media, health care, and criminal justice, which reinforce discriminatory beliefs, values, and distribution of resources. 32 There are data reporting gaps among NHOPI individuals due to lack of data collection or aggregation with other racial and ethnic groups. More future studies are encouraged to disaggregate NHOPI data and even further explore the heterogeneity of NHOPI groups at the highest granularity to advance data equity.

Aside from structural racism in the form of data erasure, NHOPI individuals also tend to experience limited access to care, low income, and reduced physical activity that may contribute to the risk of obesity and diabetes. 33 A study looking at maternal deaths in Hawaii from 2015 to 2017 showed that NHOPI women disproportionately died from pregnancy‐related conditions, similar to that among non‐Hispanic Black women. 34 We also find in our study, consistent with prior reports, that NHOPI women are more likely to have a higher prevalence of prepregnancy obesity and are less likely to register for first‐trimester prenatal care compared with other racial groups. 35 Lack of timely prenatal care has been shown to result in worse maternal and fetal outcomes. 36 , 37 This may be particularly important among individuals with prepregnancy cardiometabolic risk factors. The high PAF for diabetes and obesity among NHOPI women further highlights the need to intensely target these cardiometabolic risk factors to prevent HDPs and their attendant complications.

Similar to NHOPI individuals, we found a high prevalence of HDPs with high PAF for prepregnancy diabetes and obesity, and low timely prenatal care utilization, among individuals of AIAN descent. Historical trauma, ongoing systemic issues such as discrimination and colonization; poverty; geographic isolation, particularly in rural reservations; and low educational status among AIAN individuals impact their cardiovascular health and access to health care services. 38 Cultural factors, including language barriers and traditional healing practices, may influence health care usage. 39 Additionally, lack of representation in health care policies contributes to the neglect of AIAN perspectives in shaping relevant interventions. Efforts should be made to address these social determinants of health and provide accessible and culturally sensitive care to promote cardiometabolic health throughout the life course of birthing AIAN people.

Non‐Hispanic Black individuals have a higher case fatality rate from HDPs and higher obesity‐related cardiovascular disease mortality rates compared with White individuals. 40 Despite non‐Hispanic Black individuals having a great burden of HDPs in our study, the PAF for obesity for this group was lower than that of other racial and ethnic groups. However, across the continuum of BMI, HDP risk was highest among non‐Hispanic Black people at BMI <27 and second highest at BMI ≥27 kg/m2. Elevated BMI is an important predictor for HDP and critical to target for preventive interventions. These findings suggest that BMI contributes significantly to HDPs, but there are likely health disparities beyond BMI that are essential in the risk of HDPs among non‐Hispanic Black individuals. Differences in socioeconomic status, access to health care, and quality of health care delivery to non‐Hispanic Black women contribute to racial disparities in maternal morbidity. 41 , 42 We demonstrate that non‐Hispanic Black individuals, similar to AIAN and NHOPI individuals, represent the groups with the highest prevalence of no prenatal care or late prenatal care received, suggesting inequities in access to or usage of care. These social determinants of health and structural racism likely play a critical role in the development of HDPs among non‐Hispanic Black women in the United States.

Racial bias and structural inequities within the US health care system can hinder the screening and treatment of individuals with HDPs. 43 , 44 Obesity disproportionately affects minority racial and ethnic groups. 45 , 46 , 47 Addressing racial disparities in cardiometabolic diseases and HDPs within the National Institute of Minority Health Disparities framework involves implementing interventions at structural, community, and individual levels of influence. At the structural level, there should be advocacy for policies that address historical inequities, promote economic development, and improve health care infrastructure to ensure equitable resource allocation and culturally sensitive health care services. There is a need to devote more research attention to racial health disparities and create targeted health equity interventions to attenuate obesity and diabetes in individuals of reproductive age before the onset of pregnancy and in ensuring that all birthing people have adequate access to prenatal care. Timely prenatal care has been shown to improve maternal outcomes. 48 Community‐driven initiatives that strengthen cultural identity, social networks and educational programs must be encouraged; these should be strength based and focused on the positive cultural practices. At the individual level, early‐onset cardiovascular risk identification and improving health literacy can reduce the risk of HDP. Finally, providing access to early, effective, and appropriate maternal services can help with identifying high‐risk individuals and reducing complications associated with HDPs.

Study Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study include the use of large nationally representative data and a racially diverse cohort, inclusive of groups that have been understudied previously, including AIAN and NHOPI individuals. However, our findings must be interpreted in the context of several limitations. HDPs are clinically heterogeneous, but we could not explore the components separately because of data limitations, as the National Vital Statistics data do not have separate diagnoses for specific components of HDPs. The overall prevalence of HDPs in our study was lower than the prior Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimate, 11 likely due to the exclusion of pregestational hypertension in our study.

There is also a potential for miscoding due to differences in screening routine or diagnostic criteria from the reporting sites. Prior research that has reviewed the performance of birth certificate data to hospital data have found that while sensitivity for pregestational diabetes ranges from 47% to 52%, and specificity is >98%. 49 The prevalence of pregestational diabetes in our study may be lower than in the population. However, this would be more likely to move the association toward the null. The strong association of prepregnancy diabetes with HDPs suggests that these findings are robust. As for BMI, studies have demonstrated reasonable reliability of coding on birth certificates (correlation coefficient of 0.93 for BMI in 1 study and percentage agreement up to 91% for higher obesity class). 50 , 51 Additionally, race was self‐reported in this study, and there may have been underrepresentation. Furthermore, we recognize that there are other factors such as lifestyle and physical activity in addition to social determinants of health, such as neighborhood or geographic factors, which may be contributory but cannot be fully explored with National Vital Statistics data. Finally, we acknowledge that some women may potentially have had repeated births during the study period, for which logistic regression may not be the most applicable model, but we lacked an identifier to identify these women due to the nature of birth certificate data. However, sensitivity analyses restricted to nulliparous women was done, and the results were consistent with the main findings.

CONCLUSIONS

In the United States, AIAN, non‐Hispanic Black, and NHOPI individuals have the highest prevalence of HDPs and lowest use of timely prenatal care. Prepregnancy diabetes and obesity are associated with HDP across all racial and ethnic groups, with the highest PAF among NHOPI individuals. For all people, prepregnancy cardiometabolic risk factors should be intensely targeted for preventive interventions, and multilevel interventions should be implemented to enhance prenatal care access and use. Although non‐Hispanic Black individuals have a high prevalence of HDPs, findings suggest that factors other than obesity and diabetes carry elevated risk for HDPs among them.
