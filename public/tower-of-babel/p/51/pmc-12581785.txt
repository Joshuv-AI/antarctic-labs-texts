Integrating transcriptomic profiling and machine learning: A clinically actionable prognostic model for infant acute myeloid leukemia
Authors: Yu Tao, Yali Shen, YanLai Tang, Hui Shi, Li Wei, Hua You
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInfant acute myeloid leukemia (AML), particularly in those under 3 years of age, presents poor prognostic outcomes and distinct biological characteristics that require age‐specific risk assessment. This study, utilizing data from four pediatric AML (pAML) trials conducted by the Children's Oncology Group, aimed to develop a simple RNA expression‐based prognostic model to refine risk stratification for infant AML. Expression data from 213 infant AML patients were analyzed using machine‐learning algorithms to develop the infant‐prognostic‐score (IPSscore), or IPSgroup when categorized. To validate the stability of the model, internal validation was conducted on a set of 127 cases, and external validation was performed using a separate set of 63 patients from a different ethnic background. Furthermore, we compared its prognostic prediction capability with that of other AML models and explored its potential clinical decision‐making value for infant AML patients. The IPSgroup independently and specifically predicted outcomes in infant AML, outperforming several previously published RNA expression‐based models. Infant patients categorized into the high‐risk group based on IPSgroup may benefit from hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT), while those in the low‐risk group are not suitable for HSCT. Additionally, when combined with the current pAML stratification system used in clinical trials, the IPSgroup enabled re‐stratification of 43% of infant AML patients into more accurate risk groups, highlighting the advantage of incorporating gene expression analysis into clinical decision‐making. Infant AML demonstrates significant heterogeneity at clinical, molecular, and prognostic levels. The newly proposed model surpasses existing AML stratifications, offering a valuable tool for clinical decision‐making and treatment strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Leukemia is generally recognized as the most prevalent cancer among children. Among the different types of leukemia, acute myeloid leukemia (AML) ranks as the second most common form in children, following acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL). 1 In high‐income countries, the survival rates for children with AML who underwent treatment in clinical trials during the late 1990s and early 2000s reached as high as 70%, attributed primarily to the optimization of chemotherapy regimens, risk stratification, and enhanced supportive care. 2 However, the progress in treatment outcomes over the past decade has been modest, 3 , 4 while numerous biological studies have revealed that the possible reason lies in the significant biological heterogeneity of AML.

According to the National Childhood Cancer Registry (NCCR) data from 2015 to 2019, the incidence of AML in children and young adults (ages 0–39 years) reaches its highest point in infants (2.09 per 100,000 individuals per year at age 1) and gradually decreases thereafter until adolescence and young adulthood. 5 Consistent with its unique epidemiological presentation, infant AML is considered a distinct subgroup characterized by distinctive clinical manifestations and biological features. 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 Infant AML is frequently associated with high frequencies of M4, M5, and M7 subtypes according to the French–American–British (FAB) classification. It is also characterized by high white blood cell (WBC) counts at diagnosis and a significant high incidence of central nervous system (CNS) involvement. 10 , 11 Molecular analysis of infant AML has identified 11q23/KMT2A rearrangement (KMT2A::R) 11 and complex karyotype to be relatively common cytogenetic features observed, 12 , 13 while FLT3‐ITD mutation 11 , 14 and several favorable cytogenetic features present low frequency. Recent studies have also unveiled novel fusion genes, such as inv(16)(p13.3q24.3)/CBFA2T3::GLIS2 15 and t(11;12)(p15;p13)/NUP98::KDM5A, 16 confer prognostic value in infant patients diagnosed with FAB‐M7 AML. 17 , 18 This clinical presentation is linked to a greater risk of relapse but does not appear to impact overall survival (OS), 8 while infants, older children, and young adults exhibited significant variations in clinical risk group classifications. These observations infer that infant AML patients show unique cytogenetic/molecular features and clinical features, and thus the distinct age‐specific genetic profiles highlight a distinctive biology in pediatric AML (pAML) and emphasize the need for precise, age‐tailored diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

Currently, clinical diagnostics and risk stratification for pAML depend on cytogenetic screening for structural genomic alterations and targeted sequencing to identify prognostic and predictive genetic variants. 19 However, approximately 80% of infant AML patients are classified into an intermediate‐risk group, making it challenging to assign an optimal consolidation therapy regimen. This highlights the urgent need for improved risk stratification in infant AML patients. While current standard‐of‐care clinical guidelines for AML stratification are cautious in incorporating novel genetic markers, several recent studies have proposed updated stratification models, including two leukemic stem cell (LSC) score‐based models established for adult (LSC17) 20 or pediatric (APS) AML. 21 These models aim to provide additional stratification for AML patients. However, their impact on prognosis in infant patients remains understudied and unexplored.

To our knowledge, we collected the largest clinical and transcriptome data from four pediatric clinical trials of the Children's Oncology Group (COG) within the TARGET cohort, 22 and the largest clinical data of pAML from China, compared cytogenetic and molecular markers as well as treatment outcomes among infants, children, and adolescents and young adults (AYA). We further developed and validated a novel gene expression signature that allows for the re‐stratification of infant AML cases in this study, thereby allowing better risk stratification for clinical management. We adopted the ≤36‐month definition for infant AML to ensure consistency with TARGET cohort classifications 22 while addressing sample size limitations for model validation, preserving developmental‐stage biological relevance.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Clinical datasets

Baseline characteristics, initial and follow‐up diagnostics, including morphological, immunophenotypic, and molecular genetic testing data of pediatric de novo AML cases from four COG studies (CCG‐2961, 23 AAML03P1, 24 AAML0531, 25 and AAML1031 19 ) were clinically available and collected from the TARGET project in NCI's Genomic Data Commons (GDC) database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/, collected on April 20, 2023). The treatment protocol, clinical data, and sample collection in clinical trials were approved by the institutional review boards of each participating institution, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their parents/guardians. In total, clinical data of 2009 non‐FAB M3 pAML patients, including 483 infants, 930 children, and 596 AYA, respectively, were analyzed in the present study. The average follow‐up time for the total cohort was 3.58 ± 2.34 years. Transcriptomic data from 70 healthy pediatric bone marrow samples were collected and processed by TARGET and are accessible through the GDC (source: https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/target/using-target-data).

Clinical data from a total of 487 pAML cases were collected from the Children's Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (designated CQ‐pAML), among which 43 infant cases had available transcriptome data. These patients, combined with 20 infant samples from the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat‐sen University in Guangdong Province, collectively constituted the external validation set (designated CQSY‐infant set). The average follow‐up time for the external validation cohort was 3.19 (1.47–3.72) years. This study was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, with approvals of the Institutional Review Board of Children's Hospital of Chongqing Medical University and the First Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat‐sen University. Detailed treatment protocols for all the infant AML sets were provided in Supporting Information S1: Figure 1.

Assessments

OS is defined as the period from the study entry until the time of death. Event‐free survival (EFS) is defined as the duration from the study entry until death, induction failure, or relapse. In cases where subjects were lost to follow‐up, their data were censored at the date of their last known contact. Cumulative incidence of relapse (CIR) provides the total accumulated risk of experiencing relapse or death for patients who achieved a complete response at the end of Course 1.

Risk stratification

Detailed risk groups of the study, defined as the risk groups in COG, are provided in Supporting Information S2: Table 1. The CFM (cytomolecular risk, defined by cytogenetic/fusion/molecular features) risk stratification model was originally developed by Farrar et al., with detailed risk group classifications provided in Supporting Information S2: Table 1. 26 The cutoff value established by Duployez et al. 27 categorized the LSC17 score above the third quartile as high and below the third quartile as low. Median value was used as the cutoff value for APS. 21 Transcripts and coefficients used for risk stratification were provided in Supporting Information S2: Table 2.

Infant‐prognostic‐score construction using machine‐learning algorithms

Infant‐prognostic‐scores (IPSscores) were developed following a well‐established workflow, integrating 10 classical machine‐learning algorithms, including least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), gradient boosting machine (GBM), random survival forest (RSF), partial least squares regression for Cox (plsRcox), stepwise Cox regression (StepCox), supervised principal components (SuperPC), ridge regression, survival support vector machine (Survival‐SVM), CoxBoost, and elastic net (Enet). Among these, RSF, LASSO, CoxBoost, StepCox in both directions, and StepCox backward direction were employed for initial dimensionality reduction and variable screening, resulting in a total of 40 combinations of machine‐learning algorithms. 28 , 29

RNA‐seq data from the TARGET project were obtained from the GDC portal in the form of aligned reads (BAM files) and gene expression quantifications, including raw counts and normalized values (TPM, FPKM, and FPKM‐UQ). Differential expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 using the raw counts to identify statistically significant genes. For subsequent machine learning, gene expression levels were quantified as log2‐transformed transcripts per kilobase million (TPM). For gene set functional enrichment analysis, cancer hallmark subset (h.all.v7.4.symbols.gmt) from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) 30 was downloaded as the background gene set. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were mapped to this reference collection, and enrichment analysis was performed using the R package clusterProfiler (version 3.14.3) to obtain gene set enrichment results. A false discovery rate (FDR) of <0.01 was applied as a statistically significant threshold.

The generation of the IPSscores

Forty machine‐learning algorithm combinations were used for model construction from the DEGs. Further, all models were evaluated by calculating risk scores for patients in the discovery set, validation set, and the external validation test set using the predict function from the relevant package based on the established models. The Harrell's concordance index (C‐index) was computed by performing univariate Cox regression analysis on the risk scores of all models across the three datasets.

The model with the highest average C‐index in validation datasets was automatically selected as optimal. The risk score derived from this optimal model was designated as the IPSscore. The optimal cutoff value for continuous clinical variables in univariable Cox regression analyses was determined based on the R package “maxstat.” 31

Statistical analysis

The log‐rank test was performed to compare OS and EFS among groups. Cox proportional hazard regression models were utilized to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) for both univariable and multivariable analyses of OS and EFS. Time‐receiver operating characteristic (time‐ROC) curve, time‐area under the curve (time‐AUC), and calibration curve were used to demonstrate the efficacy of the constructed model and compared with existing models. The bootstrap technique was used, and the bootstrap‐corrected AUC, C‐index, and 95% confidence interval (CI) were reported. Pearson's chi‐square or Fisher's exact test was employed for categorical variables, while the Mann–Whitney U test was utilized for continuous variables. All P‐values reported in this study are two‐sided, and a P‐value below 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 28.0 (Chicago, IL, USA) and R software version 4.0.2 (available at http://www.Rproject.org). Graphical illustrations were created using Graphpad Prism™ 8.3 and R software version 4.0.2.

RESULTS

Age to some extent establishes the clinical and molecular traits observed in pAML

Among 2009 pAML patients in TARGET, age distribution showed 483 infants (24.0%), 930 children (46.3%), and 596 AYA patients (29.7%) (Figure 1A). Significant age‐related differences were observed across clinical, cytogenetic, and molecular characteristics (Figure 1B, Supporting Information S2: Table 3). Infant patients demonstrated distinct presentation patterns including higher WBC counts and increased chloroma incidence compared to older cohorts. Molecular profiling revealed striking variations in fusion gene frequencies, with infants showing marked enrichment of CBFA2T3::GLIS2 and KMT2A rearrangements but near absence of RUNX1::RUNX1T1. These differential patterns were further reflected in FAB subtype distributions and somatic mutation profiles across age groups. Infants demonstrated significantly worse OS compared to children (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2A, Supporting Information S2: Table 3) and significantly inferior EFS compared to both children and AYA groups (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2B), a finding confirmed in the CQ‐pAML cohort (Supporting Information S1: Figure 2C,D). Besides, a significantly higher proportion of infants (82.2%) were classified as COG standard‐risk than children (38.1%) or AYA (35.7%) (Supporting Information S2: Table 3, Supporting Information S1: Figure 3). While hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) utilization rates in CR1 (first complete remission) were similar across groups, HSCT provided significant survival benefit only for children (P = 0.001), with no significant benefit for infants (P = 0.779) or AYA (P = 0.103) (Supporting Information S1: Figure 4).

Distinct transcriptomic profiles and pathway dysregulation in infant AML

To develop a molecular prognostic model for EFS that is specific to infant AML, a total of 340 infant AML patients with both clinical and transcriptome data available in TARGET set were included, with a median follow‐up time of 3.42 ± 2.36 years. The patients were categorized into two independent datasets: a discovery set (AAM1031, n = 213) and an internal validation set (combined samples from CCG2961, AAML0531, and AAML03P1, n = 127), based on their treatment protocols. The baseline characteristics were compared and are presented in Supporting Information S2: Table 4.

A comparison between infant AML (n = 213) and healthy pediatric controls (n = 70) identified 8526 DEGs (with 6261 upregulated genes) (P < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| > 1) (Supporting Information S1: Figure 5A). Furthermore, a comparative analysis of gene expression profiles between infant AML (n = 213) and AML in patients older than 5 years (n = 645) within the COG AAML1031 cohort revealed 1955 DEGs (including 1461 upregulated genes) between the two age groups (P < 0.05 and |log₂ fold change| > 1) (Supporting Information S1: Figure 5B). Of note, overrepresentation analysis of cancer hallmarks in gene sets comparing pAML samples to healthy controls revealed significant enrichment of DEGs in multiple hallmark pathways (FDR < 0.01). Significantly enriched pathways related to immune and inflammatory processes included Tnfα signaling via NFκB, inflammatory response, complement, and allograft rejection. Additionally, pathways associated with oncogenic signaling and cellular plasticity—epithelial–mesenchymal transition and KRAS signaling up—were also significantly enriched (Supporting Information S1: Figure 5C). Comparison between infant AML patients and older pAML patients showed that DEGs were primarily enriched in six hallmark gene sets (FDR < 0.01). Pathways related to epithelial–mesenchymal transition, allograft rejection, complement, inflammatory response, and myogenesis overlapped with those dysregulated in infant AML compared to healthy controls (Supporting Information S1: Figure 5D).

Model construction using RNA‐seq data in the discovery set and validation

Following the identification of upregulated genes in infants from the AAML1031 cohort, we consolidated a final set of 4433 upregulated genes that were commonly present across the discovery, internal validation, and external validation (CQSY‐infant) datasets for subsequent analysis. The final set of genes were incorporated into a machine‐learning framework to develop multiple prognostic models using the discovery set. The model created by combining RSF and GBM was selected as the optimal model due to its highest average C‐index of 0.66 in the two validation sets (Figure 2A).

The model consisted of 24 genes, along with their respective influences, displayed in Figure 2B. Figure 2C shows the AUCs for EFS in the discovery set: 0.869 (1‐year), 0.933 (3‐year), and 0.956 (5‐year). In the internal validation set, the AUCs were 0.605 (1‐year), 0.691 (3‐year), and 0.658 (5‐year). The external validation set yielded AUCs of 0.776 (1‐year) and 0.802 (3‐year).

X‐tile software was used to determine the optimal thresholds for stratifying patients into three risk groups in the discovery set (Supporting Information S1: Figure 6). Although the optimal cutoff values yielded distinct outcomes for the three risk groups in both the discovery and internal validation sets (all P < 0.0001), no significant prognostic difference was observed between the low‐ and standard‐IPSscore groups in the external validation set (P = 1.000) (Supporting Information S1: Figure 7). Consequently, patients were divided into two risk groups using a threshold of 0.42, merging the low‐ and standard‐IPSscore groups. The high‐IPSscore group showed adverse EFS in the discovery and validation sets, which was defined as the high‐risk group. This adverse association was also observed for OS, with all comparisons being statistically significant (all P < 0.05) (Figure 3).

Considering the treatment subgroups, the IPSscore groups (referred to as the IPSgroup hereafter) demonstrated strong stratified performance in patients receiving Arm A and Arm B treatments in the AAML1031 therapy (P < 0.001; Supporting Information S1: Figure 8). For the internal validation set, patients can be classified into those who received a combination therapy of GO (Gentuzumab Ozogamicin) and chemotherapy, and those who received only chemotherapy. The prognostic ability of the IPSgroup is significant for EFS in the non‐GO treatment group (P = 0.004), as well as for OS in the GO treatment group (P = 0.043), with nonsignificant results for the rest of the analysis (P = 0.157; P = 0.055) (Supporting Information S1: Figure 9).

The IPSgroup as an independent factor for prognosis of infant AML

We performed univariate and multivariate survival analyses to evaluate the impact of IPSgroup, clinical, and molecular factors on EFS in infants with pAML (Figure 4A,B). In the discovery set, IPSgroup was the only independent predictor of EFS (HR = 5.477, P < 0.001). In the internal validation set, both IPSgroup and MRD at the end of Course 1 were independent predictors (HR = 1.997, HR = 2.554, respectively; all P < 0.05) (Figure 4C,D) (Supporting Information S2: Tables 5 and 6).

Performance comparison between IPSgroup and former published risk stratification tools

Furthermore, the comparison with existing AML prognostic models with prognostic values in infant AML (COG study defined risk, LSC17, and APS) consistently revealed the highest bootstrap‐corrected time‐AUC values of the IPSgroup for EFS in three sets (Figure 5). The 3‐year DCA curves further demonstrated the promising clinical utility of the IPSgroup. These results suggest that the IPSgroup model offers a higher net benefit in real‐world clinical decision‐making, making it more suitable for routine clinical practice of infant AML (Figure 5).

Clinical significance of the IPSgroup and IPSscore

To investigate whether IPSgroup can influence patients' decision‐making regarding HSCT treatment, we analyzed the cumulative risk of death or relapse following HSCT in the CR1 phase across various risk groups. In the low‐risk and high‐risk groups, 19.57% (9/46) and 11.43% (20/175) of patients, respectively, underwent HSCT during the CR1 phase. The results indicated that HSCT had no significant effect on the CIR in the high‐risk group (HR = 0.44 [95% CI, 0.17–1.14], P = 0.081, Supporting Information S1: Figure 10). Conversely, in the low‐risk group, HSCT significantly increased patients' CIR (HR = 1.89 [95% CI, 1.01–3.52], P = 0.041, Supporting Information S1: Figure 9).

Given that patient stratification in pAML clinical studies has been continuously updated, further analysis revealed that the CFM model presented a certain predictive performance for outcomes of infant AML, with no survival difference identified between the Standard and the Low‐risk groups (P.adj > 0.05) (Figure 6A). As shown in Figure 6B, the distribution of IPS score across the three risk groups exhibited an increasing trend with higher risk status. However, there was considerable overlap between the patient groups. We then used the best cutoff in Supporting Information S1: Figure 6 identified with X‐tile to resign the patients as follows: patients with below −0.09 IPSscore were stratified as low‐risk, patients with IPSscore between −0.09 and 0.42 retained their CFM stratification, and patients with above 0.42 IPSscore values were stratified as high‐risk. Among the CFM high‐risk patients, 30 patients (22.9%) were reclassified into the low‐risk group, while 68 patients (59.6%) and 18 patients (15.8%) were reclassified into the standard‐risk group, resulting in a reduction of the standard‐risk group to 28 patients (Figure 6C). The newly assigned CFM tool presented distinct prognostic outcomes for the three risk groups (Figure 6D, all P < 0.05), emphasizing that incorporating expression‐based IPSscore into the CFM stratification model resulted in a clear improvement in stratification.

DISCUSSION

This largest study on infant AML highlighted the distinct characteristics of the infant age group in pAML compared to older patient groups. Specifically, the infant group presented inferior EFS across all age subgroups, a finding consistent with the conclusions drawn from both the 2009‐patient Western TARGET cohort and the 487‐patient Chinese pAML cohort, and a high incidence of FAB‐M5/M7 morphologies and fusion genes, such as CBFA2T3::GLIS2, KMT2A::R (KMT2A::MLLT10 and KMT2A::MLLT3), and a low frequency of genetic mutation. Further, we present an mRNA expression‐based IPSscore (when categorized, referred to as IPSgroup) that can identify at the time of diagnosis infant AML patients with adverse outcomes. When combined with the frontline prognostic tool, the risk score enables the re‐stratification of 43% infant AML patients into the appropriate risk groups.

Beyond its distinct genomic landscape, infant AML exhibits unique transcriptomic features. Our analysis identified numerous infant‐specific genes, many of which are functionally linked to canonical cancer hallmark pathways. These findings align with prior reports—for example, independent analysis of the same TARGET dataset revealed activation of a B‐cell developmental gene regulatory network in infant AML. 32 One intriguing discovery was our observation that infant AML patients exhibit significant associations with the complement pathway and allograft rejection gene signatures. These findings highlight the potential involvement of immune dysregulation pathways in mediating poor outcomes following HSCT in high‐risk infant cases. 33

More importantly, the prognostic model identified using transcriptome data with machine learning (IPSscore and IPSgroup) can provide valuable prognostic information when applied to 340 patients from TARGET cohorts, and 63 patients from an external cohort with a different ethnic background. This is particularly important for infant patients, especially given that we observed in the TARGET dataset that 82.2% of infant patients were classified into the intermediate‐risk group, which poses a challenge for treatment decisions and the development of targeted therapies.

Compared to the COG study defined risk groups, APS score 21 and LSC17 score, 20 the IPSgroup signature demonstrated superior performance in predicting outcomes in infant AML. The improved performance of the IPSgroup signature may be attributed to the inclusion of DEGs between infants and children over 5 years of age, thereby better capturing the unique gene expression profile of infant patients. In contrast, the APS signature was developed based on the expression profiles of adult AML patients. While it provides effective risk stratification for pAML patients, its performance in infants is less optimal. Additionally, the LSC score is based on DEGs between leukemia stem cell populations and non‐leukemia stem cell populations, neglecting potential influences from the tumor microenvironment on gene expression. Consistent with the focus of our study, developing subtype‐specific prognostic models based on different AML subgroups represents an important approach to improving prognostic stratification in pAML, 20 in the context of the high heterogeneity of pAML. Our results further suggest that risk stratification using IPSgroup can identify low‐risk patients who are less likely to benefit from transplantation and may therefore be candidates for investigational therapies. This finding offers new insights into a longstanding question: whether infant AML patients undergoing HSCT can truly benefit from this treatment, with the potential influence of RNA expression levels being a key consideration. 34

CFM in the setting of ongoing clinical trials represented a multifaceted criterion that integrates complex genetic assessments. Given the low incidence rate, significant economic investment, and the technical skill requirements for testing personnel, the implementation of this system in hospitals located in underdeveloped regions is anticipated to pose substantial challenges. Therefore, expression‐based tools such as IPSgroup might be a better choice for patients' re‐stratification in clinical settings. Furthermore, our study further emphasizes IPSscore holds value in improving state‐of‐the‐art prognostics by reassigning high‐ and low‐risk infant AML patients who are currently assigned to the standard‐risk group, highlighting the potential of using combined tools for improved risk stratification.

Our study has several limitations worth noting: (1) multicenter heterogeneity in treatments and data generation, (2) inherent biases of retrospective design, (3) relatively short follow‐up duration, and (4) the use of general pediatric rather than infant‐specific healthy controls. While we mitigated technical variations through standardized bioinformatics (quality control, ComBat, and TPM normalization) and maintained the model's original parameters during validation, these real‐world heterogeneities—including potential transcriptional confounding from control selection—ultimately served as a rigorous stress test. The model's maintained performance across these diverse conditions supports its clinical potential, though prospective trials with standardized protocols, extended follow‐up, and age‐matched controls remain essential for definitive validation.

In summary, to our knowledge, we presented the first and robust mRNA‐based prognostic model and risk stratification tailored to infant AML. We anticipate that this model will contribute to altering the risk stratification of pAML, ultimately leading to reduced treatment‐related toxicities and enhanced prognosis. Furthermore, the integration of new molecular factors identified in the infant subgroup is expected to further refine the risk stratification of infant AML.
