Prenatal Concentrations of Perfluoroalkyl Substances and Maternal Beta Cell Function at 7 to 9 Years of Follow-Up
Authors: Jana Palaniyandi, Jennifer E Bruin, Mandy Fisher, Michael M Borghese, Myriam P Hoyeck, Constadina Panagiotopoulos, Jillian Ashley-Martin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextEpidemiological evidence regarding prenatal per- and polyfluoroalkyl substance (PFAS) exposure and long-term maternal metabolic health outcomes is lacking.ObjectiveQuantify associations between prenatal PFAS concentrations and maternal metabolic biomarkers of glucose homeostasis 7 to 9 years later.MethodsWe measured second trimester plasma concentrations of 9 PFAS in participants enrolled in the Maternal-Infant Research on Environmental Chemicals (MIREC) study. We measured individual biomarkers of glucose homeostasis (fasting intact proinsulin, C-peptide, insulin, glucose, and hemoglobin A1C levels) in samples collected 7 to 9 years after the MIREC pregnancy (n = 258) and derived indicators of pancreatic beta cell function (proinsulin to insulin [PI:INS], proinsulin to C-peptide [PI:CP] ratios) and insulin resistance (homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance [HOMA-IR], triglyceride-glucose index). Using multivariable linear regression models, we quantified the percent change in each outcome per doubling of individual PFAS concentrations. We used quantile g-computation and weighted quantile sum regression to evaluate the mixture of PFAS.ResultsPrenatal perfluorononanoic acid and perfluorodecanoic acid concentrations were associated with 13.9% (95% CI: 0.8, 28.8) and 10.5% (95% CI: −1.0, 23.4) higher HOMA-IR values as well as 11.9% (95% CI: 0.1, 25.1) and 8.9% (95% CI: −1.5, 20.3) higher fasting insulin concentrations, respectively. A doubling of perfluorooctanoic acid concentrations was associated with increases in intact proinsulin concentrations (12.8% [95% CI: −3.5, 31.8]) and beta cell function ratios (PI:INS: 11.5% [95% CI: −4.4, 30.1]; PI:CP: 13.5% [95% CI: −2.4, 32.0]).ConclusionPrenatal exposure to PFAS may impact long-term maternal insulin resistance and beta cell function, key risk factors for type 2 diabetes. These associations differ by specific PFAS.

Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) are a class of persistent environmental contaminants used in a wide range of consumer, commercial, and industrial products (1, 2). The highly fluorinated carbon backbone and amphipathic properties of PFAS contribute to their chemical stability and effectiveness in repelling water and oils. The half-lives of PFAS can span several years, leading to their biopersistence in humans (3, 4). In addition, PFAS exposure has been associated with multiple adverse health outcomes, including dysregulated glucose homeostasis and diabetes development (5-7). Despite global efforts to regulate PFAS (8), these compounds continue to be widely detected in human populations worldwide (9-13).

The ubiquitous nature of PFAS gives rise to exposure at all life stages, including during vulnerable windows such as pregnancy. The metabolic demands of pregnancy promote the activation of adaptive physiological mechanisms, including a transient increase in insulin resistance resulting from higher insulin secretion from pancreatic beta cells (14). Disruption of these physiological adaptations during pregnancy may increase risk of glucose homeostasis dysregulation, insulin resistance, and type 2 diabetes (T2D) later in life (15-17). Since exposure to certain PFAS has been associated with disruptions to glucose homeostasis and insulin sensitivity (18, 19), we hypothesize that PFAS exposure during pregnancy may exacerbate long-term maternal glucose homeostasis and insulin resistance.

Only one identified study has evaluated the relationship between prenatal PFAS exposure and maternal metabolic health either immediately postpartum or over the long-term after delivery (20). These authors found no association between prenatal PFAS and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels in mothers at 3 years postpartum. However, they noted positive associations between prenatal PFAS concentrations and measures of maternal adiposity at the same time point (20). No identified studies have evaluated associations between prenatal PFAS concentrations and long-term changes in biomarkers of glucose homeostasis and beta cell function, including insulin secretion or insulin resistance.

Our objective was to quantify the relationship between prenatal PFAS concentrations and maternal metabolic health outcomes measured in a 7- to 9-year follow-up study within the Maternal-Infant Research on Environmental Chemicals (MIREC) Research Platform. We focused on composite measures of beta cell function and insulin resistance. We assessed beta cell function using the proinsulin to C-peptide (PI:CP) and proinsulin to insulin (PI:INS) ratios. We assessed insulin resistance using homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) and triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

We included participants who were enrolled and provided a blood sample in both the MIREC pregnancy cohort and a follow-up study 7 to 9 years later (MIREC-ENDO). Participants were recruited in their first trimester of pregnancy between 2008 and 2011 from obstetric and prenatal clinics in 10 Canadian cities. Participants were eligible if they were able to communicate in English or French, >18 years of age, <14 weeks of gestation and planning to deliver at a local hospital (21). Participants provided anthropometric, demographic, and lifestyle information (eg, smoking, alcohol, and medication use) and biospecimens during study visits in each trimester of pregnancy. Between 2018 and 2021, approximately 7 to 9 years after the initial delivery, we conducted a follow-up study to measure the impact of prenatal exposure to environmental chemicals on maternal metabolic function (22).

For the MIREC-ENDO follow-up study, 288 participants completed an in-person clinic visit and provided biological samples for analysis of maternal biomarkers of metabolic health. The present investigation encompassed participants with both second trimester PFAS and maternal metabolic biomarkers from the 7- to 9-year follow-up visit, and excluded those who self-reported taking hypoglycemic agents or insulin, and those who were pregnant at the follow-up visit (Supplementary Fig. S1 (23)) (n = 258).

The MIREC and MIREC-ENDO studies were approved by the Research Ethics Boards at Health Canada/Public Health Agency of Canada, the Sainte Justine's Hospital (Montreal, Quebec, Canada), and the institutions of all participating recruitment sites. All participants provided informed consent prior to participating. The present investigation was approved by Health Canada's Research Ethics Board (REB 2021-003H), as well as Carleton University's Research Ethics Board (REB #115741).

Second Trimester Plasma PFAS

The mean (SD) gestational age at the time of the second trimester clinic visit and corresponding blood draw was 19.7 (2.4) weeks within our analytical sample. Within 2 hours of the blood draw, samples were centrifuged, and plasma was aliquoted into cryovials and stored at −80°C. Analysis of perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), perfluorooctane sulfonic acid (PFOS), perfluorohexane sulfonic acid (PFHxS), perfluorodecanoic acid (PFDA), perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), perfluorobutane sulfonic acid (PFBS), perfluorobutanoic acid (PFBA), perfluorohexanoic acid (PFHxA) and perfluoroundecanoic acid (PFUnDA) was performed by the Laboratoire de Toxicologie Institut National de Santé Publique du Québec (Quebec City, Quebec, Canada), which is accredited by the Standards Council of Canada (ISO 17025). Compounds were analyzed by ultra-performance liquid chromatography—mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS/MS, Waters Acquity UPLC; tandem mass detector Waters Xevo TQ-S with MassLynx software). The inter-day precision was ≤5% for all PFAS in all quality control conditions (low, medium, high). We excluded PFBS, PFBA, and PFHxA from the present analysis as more than 50% of participants had concentrations below the limit of detection (LOD). All other PFAS had 100% detection rates. LODs for the panel of PFAS ranged between 0.01 to 0.03 µg/L (Table 1).

Maternal Metabolic Biomarkers at 7- to 9-Year Follow-Up

Blood samples were collected using 10 mL vacutainer tubes. Within 2 hours of blood collection, samples were centrifuged, and serum was aliquoted into smaller cryovials and stored at −80°C. Our primary outcomes were composite measures of beta cell function and insulin resistance. We derived the PI:INS and PI:CP ratios as 2 surrogate biomarkers of beta cell function; a higher ratio reflects an inefficient conversion of proinsulin to mature insulin and connecting-peptide (C-peptide) within the beta cell, which results in co-secretion of unprocessed proinsulin into circulation alongside mature insulin and C-peptide (24, 25). To assess insulin resistance, we calculated the HOMA-IR (26) and the TyG index (27, 28), a novel indicator of insulin sensitivity. HOMA-IR was calculated using the following formula: [fasting plasma insulin (µIU/mL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)]/405]. We calculated the TyG index using the following equation: ln[fasting triglycerides (mg/dL) × fasting glucose (mg/dL)/2] (27, 28). Our secondary outcomes were the individual concentrations of fasting intact proinsulin, mature insulin, C-peptide, HbA1c, and glucose.

We measured fasting serum insulin, C-peptide, and intact proinsulin using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in the Bruin Lab at Carleton University (Ottawa, ON). C-peptide and mature human insulin concentrations were measured with C-peptide Chemiluminescence ELISA kits (#80-CPTHU-CH01, ALPCO Diagnostics, Salem, New Hampshire; RRID: AB_3665034) and Human Insulin Chemiluminescence ELISA kits (#80-INSHU-CH01, ALPCO Diagnostics, Salem, New Hampshire; RRID: AB_2894946) according to manufacturer's instructions. Intact proinsulin levels were measured using the Human Intact Proinsulin ELISA kits (#TE1012, TECO Medical Group, Sissach, Switzerland; RRID: AB_3665033). The average intra-assay and inter-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) for all 3 biomarkers were below 10% (C-peptide intra-assay CV: 4.8% inter-assay CV: 8.8%; insulin intra-assay CV: 7.9% inter-assay CV: 6.5%; intact proinsulin intra-assay CV: 5.4% inter-assay CV: 5.8%).

HbA1c, fasting glucose, and triglycerides were measured at the Advanced Research and Diagnostic Laboratory (ARDL) at the University of Minnesota. HbA1c was measured in EDTA whole blood on the Tosoh HPLC Glycohemoglobin Analyzer (Tosoh Medics, Inc., San Francisco CA 94080) using an automated high-performance liquid chromatography method. The laboratory inter-assay CV was 1.16% at a mean HbA1c value of 5.34%, and 0.55% at a higher mean HbA1c value of 10.11%. Glucose was measured by the Roche hexokinase method (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN 46250) on a Roche Cobas 6000 Chemistry Analyzer (Roche Diagnostics Corporation). The laboratory inter-assay CV was 1.3% at a mean concentration of 97.2 mg/dL and 1.8% at a mean concentration of 223.3 mg/dL. The LOD was 2 mg/dL. Triglycerides were measured in serum using Triglyceride L reagent on the Roche Cobas c502 chemistry analyzer (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN 46250). The laboratory inter-assay CV was 2.0% at a mean concentration of 112.4 mg/dL and 2.3% at a mean concentration of 182.1 mg/dL. The LOD was 9 mg/dL.

We substituted LOD/2 for proinsulin concentrations below the LOD; all other outcome biomarkers were detected in 100% of participants within our analytical sample (Table 2).

Covariates

We identified covariates using a priori knowledge of determinants of prenatal plasma PFAS concentrations and maternal metabolic outcomes (29-34) (Supplementary Fig. S2 (23)). In our final model, we included the following potential confounders recorded at the time of the index pregnancy: maternal age, pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI), race and ethnicity, parity, education, and diet quality assessment (35). We did not adjust for country of birth in this analysis due to the composition of the MIREC study population and the overlap between country of birth and race and ethnicity. We additionally included smoking status measured at follow-up in our models as shown in our directed acyclic graph (Supplementary Fig. S2 (23)). Participant pre-pregnancy BMI was obtained using self-reported pre-pregnancy weight and measured height. Maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, and diet quality assessment were modeled as continuous variables; other covariates were categorized (Tables 3 and 4). Data on dietary patterns and quality were not available in MIREC-ENDO; however, we adjusted for participant prenatal diet quality. Diet quality was calculated using data from a validated food frequency questionnaire completed by participants in the second trimester (36-38).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics

We calculated descriptive statistics for exposure and outcome variables in our analytical sample and assessed geometric mean concentrations of outcome biomarkers by participant sociodemographic characteristics (Tables 3 and 4). We calculated Spearman correlations between second trimester PFAS and metabolic outcome biomarkers (Supplementary Fig. S3 and S4 (23)). Prior to regression modeling, we visualized the associations among individual PFAS and outcome biomarkers using locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) plots to assess linearity. We conducted regression diagnostics to ensure the assumptions of linear regression were met and to ensure normality of residuals.

Individual regression models

We quantified associations between PFAS and each metabolic outcome biomarker using multivariable linear regression models, adjusting for covariates. Pre-pregnancy BMI was missing for 5.3% of participants; data were complete for other covariates. We imputed missing BMI using multiple imputation by chained equations (number of imputations = 5) (39). Plasma concentrations of PFAS and outcome biomarkers were log2-transformed to normalize distributions and beta coefficients were back transformed using the formula (2β − 1) × 100 to obtain the percent change in metabolic biomarkers per doubling of PFAS concentrations. Consistent with guidance from the American Statistical Association and epidemiologists, we interpret our results based on magnitude and precision of effect estimates rather than null hypothesis testing (40-42). We therefore focus on 95% CIs, which transparently reflects the magnitude of effect and degree of uncertainty around our effect estimates and also inherently reflects statistical significance that would be indicated by a P value (43). This approach also allows for a more nuanced interpretation of our findings. In addition, to ensure we identified all potential associations, we decided against adjusting for multiple comparisons in our analyses to avoid increasing risk of Type 2 error (44).

Recognizing that long-term metabolic health can be influenced by preexisting dysglycemia, we conducted 2 sensitivity analyses to exclude participants who had reported having gestational diabetes (GDM) or type 1 diabetes (T1D)/T2D prior to the 7- to 9-year follow-up.

Mixtures analysis

We used quantile g-computation (qgcomp) and weighted quantile sum regression (WQS) to assess the joint association of a simultaneous 1-quartile increase in all 6 PFAS (PFOA, PFOS, PFHxS, PFNA, PFDA and PFUnDA) (45-47). Qgcomp is a generalized linear model-based implementation of g-computation. This method estimates the parameters of a marginal structural model that characterize the change in outcome for a 1-quartile increase in all exposures specified within the mixture model (45). The qgcomp mixture method does not assume directional homogeneity, enabling the detection of both positive and negative associations between the chemical mixture and outcome. Negative weights will sum to −1, while positive weights will sum to 1 for each outcome variable. For WQS, we applied 1000 bootstraps with a 40/60 validation split. We constrained WQS results to both the negative and positive direction in separate models to estimate the effects of a 1-quartile increase in the PFAS mixture. The weights represent the contribution of each PFAS to either the overall positive or negative association and will sum to 1 for each outcome biomarker. We applied both qgcomp and WQS to 5 imputed datasets and calculated the pooled estimates using Rubin's Rules to appropriately account for the inter-dataset variance (39). The coefficients of the overall mixture effect can be interpreted as the percent change in outcome per 1-quartile increase in the PFAS mixture.

All statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software (Version 4.3.2).

Results

Study Population Characteristics

Participants in our analytical sample (n = 258) were predominantly White (90.7%), with pre-pregnancy BMI < 25 kg/m2 (60.5%), nonsmokers at follow-up (92.0%), and age 30 or over at the time of the index pregnancy (73.3%). Geometric mean concentrations for second trimester plasma PFAS concentrations ranged between 0.14 µg/L (95% CI: 0.13, 0.15) for PFUnDA to 3.94 µg/L (95% CI: 3.69, 4.21) for PFOS (Table 1). Spearman correlation coefficients between second trimester plasma PFAS ranged from 0.05 (PFUnDA and PFHxS) to 0.83 (PFNA and PFDA) (Supplementary Fig. S3 (23)).

Metabolic Outcome Biomarkers and Participant Characteristics

PI:INS and PI:CP ratios were both less than 1, reflecting the higher circulating concentrations of insulin and C-peptide relative to intact proinsulin (Table 2). The geometric mean concentration of fasting C-peptide was 363 pM (95% CI: 342, 385), approximately 10-fold higher than that of mature insulin (37.6 pM [95% CI: 34.9, 40.6]), and more than 350-fold higher than the mean proinsulin concentration (1.00 pM [95% CI: 0.89, 1.13]) (Table 2). The majority of participant concentrations were within reference ranges or below clinical reference thresholds obtained from the literature (28, 48-50). Metabolic biomarker correlation coefficients ranged from −0.31 (PI:INS ratio and C-peptide) to 0.79 (insulin and C-peptide) (Supplementary Fig. S4 (23)).

Geometric mean concentrations of metabolic biomarkers varied based on participant characteristics (Tables 3 and 4). Participants who were older or had higher BMI (at index pregnancy or at follow-up), were non-White, or with lower education tended to have higher average concentrations of the metabolic biomarkers when compared to their referent counterparts (Tables 3 and 4).

Associations Between Second Trimester Plasma PFAS Concentrations and Postpartum Metabolic Biomarkers

Each doubling of second trimester plasma PFNA and PFDA concentrations was associated with a 13.9% (95% CI: 0.8, 28.8) and 10.5% (95% CI: −1.0, 23.4) higher HOMA-IR, respectively (Fig. 1). We also observed that a doubling of second trimester PFNA and PFDA concentrations was associated with 11.9% (95% CI: 0.1, 25.1), and 8.9% (95% CI: −1.5, 20.3) higher fasting insulin concentrations, respectively (Fig. 2). A doubling of second trimester PFNA and PFDA concentrations were also associated with a 1.8% (95% CI: −0.4, 4.0) and 1.6% (95% CI: −0.4, 3.5) higher fasting glucose concentrations (Fig. 2), respectively. We also noted associations between second trimester PFOA and measures of beta cell function at follow-up. Specifically, a doubling of PFOA concentrations was associated with 12.8% (95% CI: −3.5, 31.8) higher intact proinsulin concentrations (Fig. 2), as well as an 11.5% (95% CI: −4.4, 30.1) and a 13.5% (95% CI: −2.4, 32.0) increase in the PI:INS and PI:CP ratios, respectively (Fig. 1). Results were generally consistent between the main analysis and sensitivity analyses excluding individuals with prior GDM or prior diabetes (Supplementary Table S1 (23)).

Discussion

Our study uniquely investigated long-term maternal metabolic health outcomes associated with prenatal PFAS plasma concentrations. We observed that prenatal plasma concentrations of PFNA and PFDA were adversely associated with HOMA-IR measured 7 to 9 years later. This association was primarily driven by increases in fasting insulin concentrations. The observed associations with HOMA-IR, but not the TyG index, suggests that PFAS may be operating through an insulin-mediated mechanism as opposed to a triglyceride-mediated mechanism. We also noted that PFOA was adversely associated with intact proinsulin and beta cell function ratios; however, these results were imprecise. Our findings suggest that elevated plasma concentrations of PFNA and PFDA during pregnancy may contribute to maternal insulin resistance in later life, while elevated concentrations of PFOA may contribute to impaired long-term beta cell function.

To date, only one other study has examined associations between prenatal PFAS exposure and postpartum markers of maternal metabolic health. Using data from the Project Viva cohort (Massachusetts, USA; recruitment 1999-2002), Mitro et al examined associations between first trimester plasma PFOA, PFOS, PFHxS, PFNA, PFDA, 2-(N-ethyl-perfluorooctane sulfonamido) acetic acid (EtFOSAA), and 2-(N-methyl-perfluorooctane sulfonamido) acetic acid (MeFOSAA) concentrations and a suite of markers of glycemia and adiposity measured in mothers at 3 years postpartum (20). Geometric mean concentrations of PFOA and PFHxS during pregnancy were 3- to 4-times lower in the current MIREC study compared to those from Project Viva, while PFOS concentrations were 6-fold lower in the current study relative to Project Viva (20). Despite the differences in PFAS concentrations between MIREC and Project Viva, Mitro et al also reported no significant associations between prenatal PFAS and postpartum HbA1c. The lack of association between PFAS and HbA1c in our study may be attributed to the limited variability in HbA1c. Most HbA1c values within MIREC were within normal range (Table 2) with a narrow interquartile range of 0.4%. Our comparison with Mitro et al is limited to HbA1c as they did not evaluate HOMA-IR or fasting serum insulin levels in relation to PFAS exposure.

Given that pregnancy is a vulnerable period for both mother and the developing fetus, the current literature on prenatal plasma PFAS concentrations and maternal metabolic health outcomes has largely focused on biomarkers of dysglycemia during pregnancy, such as impaired glucose tolerance and GDM (51-54). Comparing our study with those evaluating prenatal outcomes is challenging due to differences in the timing of outcome measurements and the insulin resistance inherent to pregnancy. Nevertheless, consistent between our findings, the Odense Child Cohort in Denmark (Odense, Denmark; recruitment 2010-2012) reported positive associations between first trimester plasma PFNA concentrations and third trimester HOMA-IR in participants with predefined risk factors for GDM (higher BMI and family history of T2D) (51). Contrary to our null findings, PFHxS was positively associated with HOMA-IR, fasting insulin and fasting glucose within the same vulnerable subgroup during pregnancy (51).

Our findings linking prenatal PFOA to measures of beta cell function at follow-up—including proinsulin—are consistent with the few studies that have evaluated this association (55, 56). In a cross-sectional analysis, Cardenas et al observed positive associations of small magnitude between PFOA and fasting proinsulin among adults at high-risk of T2D enrolled in the Diabetes Prevention Program Trial. Similarly, authors of an analysis of the Prospective Investigation of the Vasculature in Uppsala Seniors study reported modest associations between PFOA and the PI:INS ratio (56). Given the small magnitude of effect in these studies and the imprecision in our results, these findings warrant confirmation in future research.

Although no identified rodent studies have evaluated prenatal PFAS exposure and postpartum metabolic health, nonpregnancy rodent studies have provided evidence that some PFAS, including PFOA and PFOS, may elicit changes in glycemic control or insulin sensitivity (57-60). Congruent with our findings, high-fat diet–fed male mice exposed to 0.5 mg/L PFDA for 12 consecutive weeks via drinking water had significantly higher HOMA-IR and serum insulin compared to control mice (61).

While the molecular mechanisms through which PFAS alter glucose metabolism and insulin secretion are not fully understood, pathways involving the G-protein coupled receptor-40 (GPR40) signaling pathway and other nuclear receptors including the constitutive androstane receptor (CAR), pregnane X receptor (PXR) and peroxisome proliferator activated receptor-alpha (PPAR-α) may be potential mediators in humans (62-66). Since these receptors play integral roles in the regulation of glucose and lipid metabolism, disruptions to these pathways by PFAS may result in dysregulated metabolic homeostasis (67-69). Further studies are needed to identify the potential mechanism underlying our observed association between PFNA and PFDA and insulin resistance, and PFOA and beta cell function.

Our study benefited from the availability of prospective data from a multisite pan-Canadian cohort study designed to investigate associations between exposure to environmental contaminants and maternal health outcomes. The availability of follow-up data at 7 to 9 years postpartum allowed us to examine potential impacts of plasma concentrations of certain PFAS on metabolic dysfunction during a timeframe that coincides with increased risk for chronic cardiometabolic disease and the perimenopausal transition (53). Investigation of the interplay of pregnancy complications, prenatal chemical exposure, and age-related chronic disease is an underexplored research topic. Our study provides the longest identified postpregnancy assessment of metabolic health in relation to prenatal plasma PFAS concentrations. Additionally, the abundance of well-documented covariate data allowed us to consider potential confounders during both the prenatal and long-term postpartum time periods. With respect to PFAS measurements during pregnancy, we acknowledge that PFAS levels assessed beyond the second trimester are prone to plasma volume dilution, which may bias our findings toward the null. However, the majority (98%) of participants had plasma sample collection prior to the third trimester when the greatest change in plasma volume typically occurs (70). Furthermore, our results remained consistent upon excluding participants who had their PFAS measurement taken after 27 weeks. In addition, this is the first and largest study to evaluate novel measures of beta cell function (PI:INS and PI:CP ratios), and insulin resistance (TyG index) in relation to PFAS exposure. The literature on other chemicals and these metabolic markers is scarce (71). Furthermore, due to the extensive suite of complementary metabolic biomarkers in our analyses, we were able to investigate the different pathways through which PFAS may be impacting metabolic dysregulation. For example, we speculate that the positive associations observed between PFNA and PFDA with HOMA-IR and insulin, but not with beta cell function ratios, suggests that these PFAS may be disrupting glucose homeostasis through insulin signaling pathways rather than direct impairment of the beta cell. In contrast, the association between PFOA and proinsulin and beta cell function ratios, but not other measures, suggests that PFOA may directly affect the beta cell. These findings suggest that different PFAS may impact metabolic regulation through distinct mechanisms. Lastly, we employed a mixture method to assess the combined effect of all measured second trimester PFAS on composite metabolic measures and individual metabolic biomarkers.

Our study has a few potential limitations. First, we did not collect data on physical activity or diet at follow-up, both of which may have been confounders; however, we did control for diet quality during pregnancy. Additionally, the modest sample size with data at both time points precluded us from conducting other related analyses, such as evaluating effect modification by impaired glucose tolerance and GDM. In addition, while PFAS exposure was likely ubiquitous throughout the study time period, individual concentrations may have fluctuated, and it is not possible to disentangle the potential effects of pre- vs postnatal PFAS concentrations. We also cannot eliminate the possibility of selection bias due to attrition; however, any resulting selection bias likely skewed our associations toward the null. This is likely because our analytical sample, while having similar PFAS concentrations, were overall healthier and came from a more privileged background than the original cohort (Supplementary Table S4 (23)). With respect to multiple comparisons, we acknowledge that the observed statistical associations are subject to Type 1 error due to the large number of associations examined. However, given the novel nature of this analysis, we did not adjust for multiple comparisons to minimize risk of Type 2 error (44).

The generalizability of our findings may be limited by the sociodemographic composition and relatively healthy profile of the MIREC study population (21). The MIREC cohort is predominantly White with above-average income and education levels (21). However, the relatively homogenous nature of the MIREC study population minimizes confounding due to socioeconomic status. The narrow variability in outcome measures such as HbA1c may have limited our ability to detect associations. Our exposure levels, however, are representative of the general Canadian population. PFAS concentrations among the MIREC participants were comparable to those measured in Cycle 2 (2009-2011) of the Canadian Health Measures Survey (CHMS) in female participants aged 20 to 39 years (Supplementary Fig. S5 (23)) (72).

In conclusion, we provide evidence that prenatal PFNA and PFDA plasma concentrations may have long-term impacts on insulin resistance, while PFOA may adversely impact long-term beta cell function, both of which are key risk factors for T2D development. Future studies are necessary to examine whether exposure to a broader range of PFAS analytes, including shorter-chain and replacement PFAS, are linked to metabolic dysregulation.
