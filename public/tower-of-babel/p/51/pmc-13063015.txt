Mechanical, Thermal, and Morphological Properties of PBAT/PLA Biocomposites Reinforced with Jute and Banana Fibers
Authors: Md. Syduzzaman, Marzia Dulal, Dilruba Yeasmin, Tanvir Hassan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Increasing environmental awareness and the scarcity of nonrenewable resources have accelerated the development of biodegradable composites derived from natural materials. This work explores the influence of potato starch as a microscale biomodifier and jute and banana fibers as macroscale reinforcements on the mechanical, thermal, and morphological performance of poly­(butylene adipate-co-terephthalate) (PBAT)/polylactic acid (PLA) biocomposites. Six distinct composite systemsraw and starch-coated jute, banana, and their hybrid combinationswere fabricated by using compression molding to evaluate the combined effects of starch coating and fiber hybridization. The experimental results reveal that starch modification significantly enhanced fiber–matrix adhesion, leading to increased density, crystallinity, and interfacial compatibility as confirmed by Fourier transform infrared and differential scanning calorimetry analyses. Starch-treated banana composites exhibited the highest compressive strength (5.12 MPa) and modulus (691 MPa), while hybrid jute–banana systems showed an improved balance between stiffness and ductility. Tensile and flexural results followed similar trends: the starch-coated banana composites attained the greatest tensile strength (41.33 MPa) and bending strength (26.71 N/mm2), while the hybrid composites presented superior flexural modulus (1492 N/mm2) and impact energy absorption (41.08 kJ/m2). The overall improvement in strength, modulus, and toughness highlights the synergistic effect of starch treatment and fiber hybridization on interfacial bonding and load transfer efficiency. These biocomposites exhibit the potential for sustainable applications such as biodegradable packaging, automotive interior components, and structural panels where environmental compatibility and mechanical reliability are essential.

Introduction

Fiber-reinforced polymer composites have extensive applications in industry due to their high strength-to-weight ratio, corrosion resistance, sound absorption performance, and design flexibility. − While glass and carbon fibers are superior in mechanical properties, , their nonbiodegradability and greater cost pose environmental and economic problems. , Environmental concerns related to plastic waste disposal, greenhouse gas emissions, and dependence on fossil-based polymers have instigated the search for more environmentally friendly alternatives in composite materials. Biocompositesnatural fiber-reinforced polymershave been an appealing option due to their biodegradability, renewability, and minimal environmental footprint. − Natural bast plant fibers, jute and banana, however, are renewable, light, and biodegradable , and thus exhibit high potential for use as reinforcements in green composites. Jute contains 58–60% cellulose and offers tensile strength up to 800 MPa. Banana fibers, with up to 65% cellulose and lower lignin content, provide enhanced flexibility and elongation. Their more extensive application is, however, restricted by performance limitations such as moisture sensitivity, poor interface bonding, and thermal instability. Hybridization of natural fibers and blending of polymers are established methods to alleviate these limitations. , Jute fibers contribute tensile strength and rigidity, while banana fibers contribute flexibility and elongation. On the matrix side, blending PLA with PBAT combines PLA’s rigidity and PBAT’s ductility. − PBAT/PLA is an attractive option across various sectors, promoting more balanced and sustainable practices. − It was demonstrated in the previous studies , that jute-reinforced PBAT/PLA composites exhibit improved mechanical and thermal properties, including improved interfacial bonding and reduced brittleness.

Despite these advancements, there remains an entrenched problem: hydrophilic–hydrophobic polarity mismatch between natural fibers and polymer matrices. The polarity mismatch leads to poor adhesion, voids, and loss of mechanical strength. Surface treatments like alkali or silane coupling have shown improvements but often involve complex or non-eco-friendly processes. Surface modification using biodegradable compatibilizers, such as starch, is an effective solution. , As an inexpensive and renewable material, starch can potentially improve fiber–matrix compatibility and reduce void content and thus lead to enhanced composite properties. For example, potato starch has enhanced the tensile strength and thermal stability of banana fiber composites, which degrade within 42 days, thereby showing promise for sustainable design.

In our previous work, we discussed the use of potato starch as an eco-friendly interfacial modifier for jute and banana fibers in a PBAT/PLA matrix, thereby enhancing tensile and flexural strength and emphasizing bonding at the fiber–matrix interface. But there are other key performance indicators that remain underexplored, including interlaminar shear strength (ILSS), compressive strength, hardness, and detailed physical attributes such as density, water absorption, and thickness. Most existing studies, including ours, have primarily focused on the tensile and flexural behavior.

This study employs a comprehensive approach to examine the mechanical, physical, and thermal properties of starch-modified jute/banana fiber-reinforced PBAT/PLA composites, aiming to ascertain whether starch treatment enhances interfacial adhesion, thus improving the structural integrity and environmental durability. This study demonstrates thickness, density, water absorption, and thermal properties by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), along with chemical interactions analyzed via Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. Mechanical assessments, encompassing compression, interlaminar shear strength (ILSS), and hardness, are also performed. This multiscale analysis seeks to improve our comprehension of structure–property interactions and augment the material’s appropriateness for sustainable engineering applications.

Materials and Methods

Materials

A 1/1 interlacing pattern plain weave jute fabric of pure raw jute fibers with an areal density of 335 g/m2 was procured for use as a reinforcement stage in the manufacture of polymer composites. The material was procured from the jute diversification promotion center to facilitate experimental studies. The fabric had an ends-per-inch (EPI) of 14, picks-per-inch (PPI) of 13, and a thickness of 0.46 mm. The inherent density of the jute fibers was 1.46 g/cm3. In addition to jute, raw filament banana fibers without any treatment were also employed as a secondary reinforcement with a fiber density of 1.35 g/cm3. Ecovio F2332, which is a commercial biodegradable polymer composed of PBAT and PLA, was used as the matrix polymer. The density of the matrix material was 1.25 g/cm3.

Potato Starch Extraction

As shown in Figure , potato starch was derived from waste potatoes and surplus potatoes gathered in cold storage houses. The extraction involved mechanical pulping of the potatoes and subsequent sequential washing with fresh water, followed by grating and blending. The starch-rich effluent was filtered through a fine-mesh fabric prior to undergoing sedimentation for 1 h to permit precipitation of the starch. The starch sediment was therefore recovered, air-dried, and milled into a powder, harvesting approximately 212 g of starch per kilogram of raw potato material. The recovered starch was gelatinized to prepare a 5 wt % aqueous starch solution, which formed a gel-like consistency, although no instrumental viscosity measurement was performed. This starch-based treatment was used as a surface treatment for jute-woven fabric and combed banana fibers as a biobased filler for improved interfacial adhesion in the composite system.

Step-by-step process flowchart of starch extraction from potatoes.

Fiber Processing

As evident from Figure , banana fibers were initially individualized to obtain separate, linear filaments appropriate for use as reinforcement material. Subsequent mechanical combining of the fibers was followed by accurate cutting into equal lengths of 18 cm. To make the fiber–matrix interfacial bond strength better, unidirectional alignment of the aligned banana filaments was obtained and treated with a 5 wt % aqueous solution of potato starch using a controlled laboratory padding process (details presented in our previous study). After treatment, the fibers were oven-dried to attain complete fixation of the starch and hence became eligible for addition to the biocomposite matrix. At the same time, jute reinforcement was prepared by cutting 28 × 18 cm sheets from a woven jute cloth. Surface treatment of the jute cloth was carried out with the same 5 wt % starch solution, applied evenly to the cloth using a lab padder. Oven drying subsequently facilitated the formation of starch-coated jute substrates that were optimized for integration into the polymer composite system.

Composite Fabrication

As shown in Figure , composite laminates (Table ) were processed in a sequential layup operation, in both jute and banana, interchanging fiber reinforcements with films of thermoplastic matrix composed of poly­(butylene adipate-co-terephthalate)/poly­(lactic acid) (PBAT/PLA). However, the stacking sequence, from bottom to top, was as follows for all samples: PBAT/PLA film → layer R 1 reinforcement (jute fabric or banana fibers) → PBAT/PLA film → R 2 → PBAT/PLA film → R 3 → PBAT/PLA film → R 4 → PBAT/PLA film. This layout was intended to facilitate even dispersion of the fibers in the matrix and permit efficient stress transfer across the composite material.

Prior to lamination, the PBAT/PLA sheets were produced by controlled compression molding of the polymer pellets: temperature, 150 °C; pressure applied, 5 tons; and dwell time, 25 min. The conditions facilitated complete melting and consolidation of the polymer, with uniform defect-free sheets being available for assembly into composites. After fabrication, matrix sheets were placed above, below, and between each of the layers of the fiber reinforcement. A constant 20:80 fiber-to-matrix weight ratio was maintained, and the same amount of fiber mass was utilized for each reinforcement layer in order to provide equal mechanical behavior across the composite.

Characterizations

Morphological Characterizations

The water absorption was conducted as per the ASTM D570. Three specimens of each composite type were immersed in distilled water at room temperature. Three specimens of each composite type, each with dimensions of 30 × 30 mm and a uniform thickness of approximately 5 mm, were immersed in distilled water at room temperature. The specimens were prepared to have uniform thicknesses to ensure consistent diffusion paths and comparable absorption results across all composites. Prior to immersion, specimens were dried in an oven at 105 °C for 1 h to remove residual moisture. The initial weight of each dried specimen was recorded. The specimens were removed from the water after a certain period and wiped with cotton cloth before the weight was measured. The weights of the specimens were taken and then immersed again in water. The reading continued for several hours until a constant weight of the specimens was obtained. The following formula was used to calculate the water absorption 1 WA(%)=Wf−WiWi×100% here, W A (%) = water absorption, W f = final weight (weight of the specimen after immersion and a certain period of time), and W i = initial weight (weight of the specimen before immersion).

The density of the composite samples was determined following the standard ASTM D1895 at atmospheric conditions. The weight of each composite sample was measured to calculate its volume. The density was calculated as the ratio of mass to volume using formula 2 ρ=mV here, ρ = composite density (g/cm3); m = weight of the specimen (gm); and V = volume of the composite specimen (cm3).

The functional groups and bond formation in the composite materials were studied by using FTIR spectroscopy. An FTIR spectrometer manufactured by PerkinElmer (MB300, Canada) was used for the analysis. Characteristic peaks linked to particular chemical bonds were identified by analyzing spectra collected in the 4000–450 cm–1 range with a characteristic resolution set at 2 cm–1 and comparing them to the relevant transmittance values.

Thermal Characterizations

Using a PerkinElmer DSC 4000 instrument, the thermal properties of the samples were analyzed. Approximately 10 mg of each sample was sealed in aluminum pans and analyzed under a nitrogen atmosphere (flow rate: 20 mL/min). DSC measurements were performed using a two-cycle heat–cool program. In the first cycle, samples were heated from 30 to 190 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min, held isothermally for 5 min to erase prior thermal history, and then cooled to 30 °C at a controlled rate of 20 °C/min. The second cycle involved reheating the samples from 30 to 190 °C at 10 °C/min. The glass transition temperature (T g) and melting temperature (T m) were determined from the second heating curve, while the crystallization temperature (T c) was taken from the cooling curve.

Mechanical Characterizations

Using a combined loading compression (CLC) test fixture, the compressive properties of the fabricated composite structures were determined in accordance with ASTM D6641/D6641M-14. Tests were conducted under standard laboratory conditions where temperature was 23 ± 2 °C, and relative humidity was 50 ± 10% on a universal testing machine (Testometric, UK) equipped with a calibrated 100 kN load cell. To ensure uniform load distribution through combined end- and shear-loading and minimize the likelihood of premature end crushing or Euler buckling, each specimen was positioned within the CLC fixture. The loading speed was maintained at 1.3 mm/min in accordance with the standard, and the test continued until complete specimen failure. The composite samples were made with dimensions of 120 mm (length) × 25 mm (width) × 5 mm (thickness). The loading surfaces were carefully machined to achieve parallel faces, flat, ensuring uniform stress distribution during compression. At least three specimens were tested to obtain statistically reliable data for each type of performance testing. Load–displacement data were recorded continuously, and compressive stress–strain curves were derived from these measurements.

The compressive strength (F cu) and compressive modulus (E c) were calculated following the relationships provided in ASTM D6641 (eqs and )­ 3 Fcu=Pfwh 4 Ec=P2−P1(ε2−ε1)wh where P f is the maximum load at failure (N), P 1 and P 2 are loads corresponding to strain values ε1 and ε2 within the selected linear region of the stress–strain curve, w is the specimen width (mm), and h is the specimen thickness (mm). The compressive strain to failure (εf) was determined from the displacement of the crosshead at the failure point.

The tensile properties of the composites were assessed under the ASTM D3039 standard utilizing a universal testing machine (Testometric, UK) fitted with a 100 kN load cell. Experiments were conducted under standardized laboratory conditions (23 ± 2 °C; RH = 50 ± 10%) at a cross-head velocity of 2 mm/min. The specimens were measured at 150 × 25 × 5 mm, with a gauge length of 135 mm. Medium-grade emery cloth was employed on the grips to inhibit slippage. The tensile strength, modulus, and strain to failure were determined from the stress–strain curves with the chord modulus method, employing a strain range of 0.1–0.3% as specified by ASTM D3039.

The flexural properties were assessed using a three-point bending configuration in accordance with procedure A of ASTM D7264. The same testing machine was used to conduct the tests with a crosshead speed of 1 mm/min. The specimens had a span-to-depth ratio of 16:1, and they were 96 mm in length, 13 mm in breadth, and 5 mm in thickness.

The Instron CEAST 9050 impact tester (Norwood, MA, USA) was employed to measure the composites’ impact strength at 15 and 30 J energy levels in accordance with ASTM D256 (Izod impact test). The absorbed energy was directly recorded from the apparatus, and notched specimens (12.7 × 63.7 × 5 mm, with a 6 mm notch depth) were tested. The average of three valid tests was used to express the impact intensity in kilojoules per square meter (kJ/m2).

A Barcol Impressor (model 934-01) was used to assess the composites’ surface hardness in compliance with ASTM D2583. The experiment was carried out under typical laboratory settings with a temperature of 23 ± 2 °C and a relative humidity of 50 ± 10%. For precise indentation measurements, each item was set on a flat, inflexible base. On top of the composite surface, the indentor was pushed perpendicularly; it was a hardened steel truncated cone with a 26° angle and a flat tip diameter of 0.157 mm. The Barcol hardness value was recorded as the greatest dial reading. Each specimen underwent three separate readings at various sites, with a strict 3 mm buffer zone around the edges and indentations to eliminate the possibility of influence from nearby impressions. Each composite type’s quoted hardness value is an average of three reliable measurements.

Results and Discussion

Morphological Characterizations

Water Absorption

As shown in Figure , the water absorption (%) of the PBAT/PLA composites supplemented with raw and potato starch-coated jute and banana fibers was assessed throughout an immersion duration ranging from 0 to 120 h. Every sample showed a steady rise in water absorption over time, which eventually came to a plateau after roughly 96–120 h, signifying that moisture uptake had reached near-equilibrium. There was no sign of water absorption at the beginning (0 h). Nevertheless, over the first 3 h, clear differences between the various fiber–matrix systems were visible. The starch treatment clearly changed the surface properties and moisture interaction behavior of the composites, as the starch-coated jute and banana samples continuously showed absorption higher than that of their respective raw samples at all immersion intervals. Hydroxyl (–OH) groups added by the starch coating improve surface hydrophilicity and strengthen hydrogen bonds with water molecules. , Additionally, when the starch film is submerged, it expands and becomes more porous, allowing water to permeate into the interior of the composite through the capillary routes. In comparison to the untreated composites, this leads to a greater total moisture uptake.

Water absorption (%) properties of the produced raw and starch-treated jute and banana fiber-reinforced PBAT/PLA composites.

Composites made of banana fibers (BBBB) were the most water-absorbing of all the materials that were tested. There was a significant rise in the S/BBBB sample from 10.98% at 3 h to 22.85% at 120 h, compared to the similar raw sample (R/BBBB), which increased from 4.31% to 13.41% during the same time. The banana fiber composites’ rapid water uptake is due to the fibers’ intrinsic structure, which includes a relatively amorphous cell wall structure, a large lumen volume, and an abundance of microfibrillar spaces. The fibrous network can absorb and hold more water because of these characteristics. Banana fibers have a stronger water affinity because they contain a large amount of hydrophilic hemicellulose and pectin. As a result, the composite benefits from improved capillary water transport due to the banana fibers’ porous and less crystalline properties.

Composites made from jute fibers (JJJJ) had the lowest water absorption rate of any of the tests. During 120 h, the R/JJJJ sample only went up from 0.33% to 3.95%, whereas the S/JJJJ sample went up from 0.59% to 4.69%. As a result of its more crystalline cellulose structure, which limits the passage of water molecules, jute has a relatively low water uptake. There are fewer available sites for water sorption in jute fibers due to their denser cell walls, smaller lumen size, and higher lignin concentration. Consequently, jute composites exhibit an enhanced resistance to moisture infiltration. The hybrid JBJB composites, comprising both jute and banana fibers, had absorbency values that were midway between those of the JJJJ and BBBB systems. After 120 h, the R/JBJB sample attained 10.66%, while the S/JBJB sample achieved 10.48%. This behavior demonstrates a compromise between the hydrophilic, porous properties of banana fibers and the denser crystalline structure of jute fibers.

Density

The observed densities of the composite specimens provide insights into the effect of potato starch surface treatment on the microstructural properties of natural fiber-reinforced biocomposites. All samples exhibited a uniform average thickness of roughly 5 mm, thus confirming that any detected variances in density are attributable to intrinsic material variation rather than dimensional discrepancies. Prior research indicates that surface treatments, including alkali and silane, enhance the densification of natural fiber composites by improving fiber–matrix adhesion and minimizing void content. , For example, a research pointed out that jute and banana fiber composites treated exhibited higher density due to enhanced interfacial bonding, while another study showed that stacking arrangements and fiber linear density were significant in the density of banana pseudostem composites, especially when combined with biodegradable matrices. In contrast, the current investigation included starch, which exhibited a consistent increase in the density across all starch-treated composites. The jute composite density ranged from 1.33 g/cm3 in its untreated state (R/JJJJ) to 1.43 g/cm3 following starch treatment (S/JJJJ). The banana fiber composite’s density rose from 1.30 g/cm3 (R/BBBB) to 1.44 g/cm3 (S/BBBB), while the hybrid configuration (R/JBJB, S/JBJB) increased from 1.38 g/cm3 to 1.41 g/cm3. Two principal mechanisms account for this enhancement in density. Initially, starch particles infiltrate and fill the microvoids inside the fiber network and at the fiber–matrix interface, resulting in a denser composite structure. Second, starch functions as a compatibilizer, enhancing interfacial adhesion between hydrophilic natural fibers and a hydrophobic polymer matrix. The notably high density of the starch-coated banana fiber composite (S/BBBB) indicates an enhanced interaction between the starch and the finer filament structure of the banana fibers, perhaps providing a larger surface area for bonding. The noted enhancement in density serves as a superior indicator of a lower void content, which often correlates with enhanced mechanical properties in natural fiber composites.

Fourier Transform Infrared

The chemical structure and interfacial interactions of raw and starch-treated jute/banana fiber-reinforced PBAT/PLA composites (R/JJJJ, R/BBBB, S/JJJJ, S/BBBB, R/JBJB, and S/JBJB) were examined using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, as shown in Figure , to analyze functional groups and determine how starch surface treatment affected fiber–matrix compatibility. All composite spectra showed a broad band of absorption in the 3390–3400 cm–1 range, which is typical of the O–H stretching vibration of cellulose, hemicellulose, lignin, and absorbed moisture. This is in line with earlier research, which found that the OH vibrational mode, which is present in nearly all natural hydrous silicates, is responsible for the absorption bands seen at 3300–3420 cm–1 and at 2920 and 1633 cm–1. The band demonstrates how raw natural fibers are hydrophilic. The C–H stretching of aliphatic groups was found to produce peaks between 2840 and 2860 cm–1, whereas the CO stretching vibrations produced a significant band between 1710 and 1714 cm–1 that demonstrated the ester linkages of PLA and the acetyl group of hemicellulose.

FTIR spectral analysis of the produced raw and starch-treated jute and banana fiber-reinforced PBAT/PLA composites.

The other bands in 1456–1460 cm–1 and 1265–1269 cm–1, respectively, were caused by C–H bending and C–O stretching, the latter of which was suggestive of lignin’s aryl–O connections. Asymmetric C–O–C stretching bands were also seen at 1157–1151 cm–1, alcohol group C–O stretching bands at 1097–1103 cm–1 and 1016–1020 cm–1, β-glycosidic links at 933–934 cm–1, and skeletal vibrations at 872–873 cm–1 and 726–728 cm–1. However, because of the possibly improved hydrogen bonding or partial esterification of starch with lignin or PLA carbonyl groups, the starch-treated hybrid composite (S/JBJB) exhibits a novel absorption band at 1588 cm–1. In a study, FTIR spectra revealed bands between 1580 and 1600 cm–1 that were associated with hydrogen bonding and vibrations of aromatic rings in lignin following esterification with acetic anhydride. Additionally, slight peak shifts may indicate molecular-level interactions between the fiber and starch surfaces. For example, the C–O–C band shifted from 1157 cm–1 (R/JJJJ) to 1151 cm–1 (S/JBJB). The decrease in free hydroxyl groups, which could lessen the hydrophilicity and increase the moisture resistance, is reflected in these spectrum responses.

Thermal Characterization

High-performance structural and environmental applications of biodegradable polymer composites are influenced by their thermal behavior. Although the stiffness and ductility of PBAT/PLA blends are balanced, the fiber reinforcement and interfacial modification have significant impact on their thermal transitions. The thermal behavior of PBAT/PLA-based composites is strongly governed by fiber–matrix interfacial interactions, as these interactions directly influence polymer chain mobility, segmental relaxation, and phase stability. DSC was employed to examine how raw and starch-treated jute, banana, and hybrid fiber reinforcements modify the glass transition temperature (T g), crystallization temperature (T c), and melting temperature (T m) of PBAT/PLA composites (Table and Figure ). Variations in these thermal transitions provide indirect but reliable insight into the quality of interfacial adhesion between the fibers and the polymer matrix.

Neat PBAT/PLA exhibited a T g of 33.56 °C, reflecting relatively free segmental motion in the amorphous phase, along with a T c of 119.34 °C and a T m of 149.52 °C, characteristic of a PBAT-rich semicrystalline blend. The introduction of natural fibers resulted in systematic changes in T g without causing drastic shifts in T c or T m, indicating that fiber incorporation primarily affected amorphous chain dynamics rather than the intrinsic crystalline structure of the matrix. Jute-reinforced composites showed a gradual increase in T g from 35.34 °C (R/JJJJ) to 36.99 °C (S/JJJJ), demonstrating progressive restriction of polymer chain mobility due to fiber addition and surface modification. The higher T g observed in starch-treated jute composites suggests improved interfacial adhesion, where starch functional groups enhance interfacial compatibility with the PBAT/PLA matrix, leading to stronger physical interactions and reduced free volume at the interface. The near-constant T c (∼120 °C) and marginal changes in T m indicate that although interfacial adhesion limited molecular mobility in the amorphous phase, it did not significantly promote heterogeneous nucleation or alter crystal stability.

However, banana fiber-reinforced composites exhibited more pronounced interfacial effects. While the raw banana composite (R/BBBB) showed a T g comparable to neat PBAT/PLA, the starch-treated banana composite (S/BBBB) exhibited a substantial T g increase to 47.64 °C. This pronounced shift indicates a strong restriction of polymer chain relaxation, which can be directly attributed to enhanced fiber–matrix adhesion induced by starch treatment. Improved interfacial bonding increases the immobilized polymer fraction near the fiber surface, thereby elevating the level of T g. Concurrently, S/BBBB displayed a slight reduction in T c but a notable increase in T m, suggesting a delayed crystallization onset due to constrained chain diffusion, followed by the formation of more thermally stable crystalline domains. This behavior is characteristic of systems in which strong interfacial adhesion suppresses rapid crystallization but enhances crystal perfection.

Hybrid fiber composites (R/JBJB and S/JBJB) demonstrated intermediate yet consistent interfacial effects. Both hybrid systems exhibited T g values higher than neat PBAT/PLA, confirming restricted chain mobility due to the combined presence of jute and banana fibers. The starch-treated hybrid composite (S/JBJB) showed the highest T c among all samples (120.83 °C), indicating a slight facilitation of crystallization onset, possibly due to synergistic interfacial interactions arising from fiber hybridization and surface modification. Additionally, the elevated melting temperatures (>151 °C) observed in hybrid composites suggest enhanced thermal stability of crystalline regions, which can be associated with improved stress transfer and constrained polymer segments at the fiber–matrix interface. Research has shown that natural fibers can influence the crystallization behavior of PLA matrices; however, the effectiveness of such fibers as nucleating agents strongly depends on their chemical composition, surface treatment, and interaction with the polymer matrix. Previous studies have reported enhanced crystallization temperatures and enthalpies in PLA composites reinforced with certain natural fillers, including banana and coir fibers, due to heterogeneous nucleation effects. In the present study, however, starch-modified banana fiber composites did not exhibit a pronounced increase in crystallization temperature, indicating that starch treatment primarily improved fiber–matrix adhesion rather than promoting extensive crystalline ordering. In contrast, starch-treated jute composites remained largely amorphous with a small T g increase reflecting improved interfacial compatibility and chain immobilization rather than enhanced nucleation. Biodegradable composites incorporating particulate natural fillers such as walnut shell powder have previously demonstrated elevated melting and crystallization enthalpies, highlighting the role of rigid filler surfaces in promoting crystal formation. In comparison, the current PBAT/PLA–fiber systems reveal that the increased mechanical performance does not necessarily correlate with high crystallinity. Instead, enhanced tensile strength, modulus, and energy absorptionparticularly observed in banana- and hybrid-fiber-reinforced compositesare primarily attributed to improved stress transfer and constrained chain mobility arising from stronger interfacial bonding induced by starch modification.

Stress and Deformation Characteristics

The compressive stress responses (peak, yield, and break) reflect the resistance of each composite to load application and subsequent failure. In the present study, the jute-based composites exhibited the lowest peak stress and stress-at-break valuesR/JJJJ (1.47 ± 0.58 N/mm2) and S/JJJJ (1.64 ± 0.70 N/mm2)indicating a limited ability to sustain compressive loads. This behavior is consistent with earlier findings that inadequate interfacial bonding between jute fibers and PLA leads to premature failure and brittle fracture modes. By contrast, the banana fiber composites demonstrated substantially higher compressive strength. The S/BBBB sample showed the highest peak stress (5.12 ± 0.48 N/mm2) and stress at break (2.64 ± 0.35 N/mm2), slightly outperforming its untreated counterpart R/BBBB (4.58 ± 1.15 N/mm2 and 2.30 ± 0.58 N/mm2, respectively). Although the improvement due to starch treatment is modest, the data confirm that banana fibers inherently provide superior load transfer and compressive stability compared to jute fibers. Hybrid JBJB composites showed intermediate strength values (2.22–3.23 N/mm2), consistent with the expected mechanical synergy of combining stiff jute fibers with more compliant banana fibers. Similar improvements in hybrid natural fiber systems have been reported for jute–banana composites in flexural and tensile modes.

Across all composites, yield stress values were consistently slightly lower than those of peak stress, suggesting a short and limited plastic deformation zone prior to failure. The stress-at-break values for S/BBBB (2.64 ± 0.35 N/mm2) and R/BBBB (2.30 ± 0.58 N/mm2) demonstrated similar postyield resistance trends, with S/BBBB retaining marginally higher load-bearing capacity. In contrast, the notably lower stress-at-break of the jute-based composites reflects their brittle failure characteristics under compression, likely governed by fiber–matrix debonding and microcrack initiation.

Strain and Failure Behavior

The strain responses demonstrate the ductility and deformation capacity of the composites. The peak and yield strains were nearly identical for all specimens, indicating a short elastic–plastic transition phase characteristic of rigid polymer composites. The strain at break, however, exhibited substantial variation across the samples. The highest ductility, reflected in the maximum strain at break (2.86 ± 1.07%), was observed for the S/BBBB composite, followed closely by that of R/JBJB (2.77 ± 0.58%). Conversely, R/JJJJ and S/JJJJ displayed the lowest strain-at-break values (1.76 ± 0.22% and 1.28 ± 0.52%, respectively), consistent with their reduced toughness and comparatively higher stiffness. The deformation at break for S/BBBB and R/JBJB is 3.44 ± 1.29 mm and 3.32 ± 0.69 mm, respectively, indicating greater strain and energy dissipation before fracture. Nevertheless, the jute-based systems exhibit failure at significantly smaller deformations (1.5–2.1 mm), indicating a brittle fracture mode characterized by fiber–matrix debonding and microcrack propagation.

Stiffness and Energy Absorption

Young’s modulus values reflect the intrinsic stiffness of the composites. The modulus increased substantially from the jute-based composites (276–335 N/mm2) to the banana fiber composites (591–691 N/mm2), confirming the superior stiffness and more efficient load transfer characteristics of banana fiber reinforcement. The hybrid composites (466 and 679 N/mm2) exhibited intermediate modulus values, indicating that fiber blending produces a synergistic stiffness response between the two single-fiber systems. Despite their higher stiffness, the banana-based composites, particularly S/BBBB, demonstrated considerable deformation and a high energy absorption capacity (0.99 ± 0.18 N·m), suggesting a favorable balance between stiffness and ductility. On the other hand, the jute-based composites absorbed noticeably less energy (0.22–0.61 N·m), showing limited plastic deformation and uniform brittle-like failure. The hybrid systems absorbed moderate energy (0.50–0.62 N·m), reflecting partial enhancement in toughness due to hybridization.

Hardness

Figure shows the Barcol hardness results of the jute, banana, and hybrid (jute/banana) fiber-reinforced PBAT/PLA (82/18) composites. The hardness values span from 94.5 ± 0.5 to 96.5 ± 0.5, indicating the intrinsically rigid surface properties of the fiber-reinforced PBAT/PLA system. A notable enhancement in hardness was detected following starch sizing of the fibers. The hardness of the jute composite increased from 94.5 ± 0.5 to 95.8 ± 0.3, but the banana composite exhibited a modest increase from 95.3 ± 0.3 to 95.8 ± 0.8. The hybrid composite exhibited the greatest hardness, attaining 96.5 ± 0.5 following starch treatment. This enhancement is ascribed to the interfacial adhesion between the natural fibers and the polymer matrix, facilitated by the starch coating that introduces many hydroxyl groups capable of forming hydrogen bonds with both the hydrophilic fiber surface and the polar functional groups of PBAT and PLA. This connection likely diminishes interfacial gaps and augments surface compactness, hence improving indentation resistance. The increased hardness of the hybrid composite demonstrates a reinforcing effect from jute and banana fibers, resulting in a more uniform load transmission across the flexible PBAT/PLA matrix.

Consistent with the compressive strength trend, the treated banana fiber composite (S/BBBB) had the maximum tensile strength (41.33 ± 4.49 MPa) and bending strength (26.71 ± 12.63 N/mm2), signifying efficient stress transmission and enhanced interfacial adhesion between the fiber and PBAT/PLA matrix. The hybrid composite (S/JBJB) showed significant enhancements in bending modulus (1492.04 ± 156.62 N/mm2) and impact energy (41.08 KJ/m2), aligning with its improved compressive modulus and strain to failure, indicative of synergistic reinforcing between jute and banana fibers.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates the effects of starch treatment on jute, banana, and hybrid jute–banana fiber-reinforced PBAT/PLA composites. Water absorption studies revealed that jute-based composites (R/JJJJ and S/JJJJ) absorbed under 5% moisture, while banana fiber composites exhibited a higher absorption (∼23%), reflecting greater hydrophilicity. Hybrid composites (R/JBJB and S/JBJB) displayed intermediate performance, balancing durability and flexibility.

Following starch treatment, the composite density increased from 1.30 to 1.44 g/cm3 across all fiber types, with the most pronounced rise observed in banana composites. This densification may be influenced by starch occupying microvoids and enhancing fiber–matrix interaction; however, further validation, such as scanning electron microscopy analysis of composite cross sections, is recommended to confirm this mechanism. FTIR spectra indicated reduced hydrophilicity for treated composites, particularly for S/JBJB, suggesting improved fiber–matrix interactions. DSC observations are reported qualitatively, indicating thermal behavior trends without definitive claims regarding crystallinity.

The findings indicate that starch treatment primarily enhanced specific propertiesnamely, density and hydrophilicity reductionwhile other properties showed minimal or no statistically significant differences between treated (S) and untreated (R) samples. The density and thermal stability render the produced materials as appropriate candidates for practical applications, including biodegradable packaging, automotive interior components, and construction panels. This endeavor contributes to the advancement of entirely sustainable materials with enhanced mechanical and ecological attributes through the utilization of natural fibers and eco-friendly treatments. Subsequent research may concentrate on long-term endurance and scalability for industrial applications.
