Bodily Engagement in Virtual Reality Nursing Simulation: Implications for Students’ Declarative and Procedural Learning
Authors: Revital Medenitzki, Ilana Dubovi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Virtual reality (VR) nursing simulations have often limited learner movement through seated or movement-restricted formats to support safety and reduce cybersickness. However, the value of nursing students’ bodily engagement during VR procedural and declarative learning remains unclear.Purpose:This study examined whether bodily engagement in VR medication-administration simulation was associated with knowledge acquisition, cognitive load, and cybersickness.Methods:A 2-group quasi-experimental design compared free-movement and restricted seated VR among 72 first-year baccalaureate nursing students during 2025. Automated motion detection measured hand, head, and torso movements during declarative and procedural learning. Knowledge and cybersickness were assessed pre- and postsimulation, and cognitive load was assessed during learning.Results:Free-movement students showed greater task-relevant movement and higher procedural knowledge scores. Declarative knowledge, cognitive load, and cybersickness did not differ significantly.Conclusion:Supporting task-relevant, free movement in VR simulation may enhance nursing students’ procedural learning without increasing cognitive load or cybersickness.

Simulation-based learning is an established and widely used approach in nursing education, providing students with opportunities to practice clinical skills and decision-making in safe, structured learning environments. Virtual reality (VR) nursing simulation extends this approach by using immersive, interactive virtual environments to support simulated clinical practice.1 By simulating realistic clinical environments, VR allows nursing students to engage actively with procedures and scenarios in ways that closely approximate real-world practice. The proportion of nursing programs using VR is expected to grow substantially over the next 5 years, from 29% in 2024 to a projected 60% by 2029.2

Despite this growing adoption, immersive VR learning environments present several important challenges for nursing student learners. First, VR may impose a high cognitive load because students must process clinical information while simultaneously navigating and interacting with a complex virtual learning environment.3,4 This load, which is defined as the mental effort required to process information in working memory during a learning task,5 may reduce learning efficiency. Second, VR environments delivered through head-mounted displays may induce cybersickness, typically including symptoms such as nausea, disorientation, oculomotor disturbances, and general discomfort.6 Cybersickness may also be influenced by the sensory and positional demands of the VR experience, including whether students are seated, restricted in movement, or allowed to move during the simulation.3,6 Third, VR may not support all learning outcomes equally. Although immersive environments can provide realistic opportunities for practice, it remains unclear which instructional features best support declarative and procedural knowledge acquisition. These challenges suggest that the key question is not simply whether VR supports learning, but rather which instructional features can maximize its benefits while minimizing its limitations.

One instructional feature that may shape how nursing students learn in VR is bodily engagement, defined here as the extent to which learners physically interact with and move their bodies during the learning task. This perspective is informed by embodied cognition theory, which proposes that knowledge construction is shaped by bodily interaction with the environment.7,8 Consistent with this view, embodied learning has been linked to improved understanding, particularly in complex domains, because bodily engagement may help anchor abstract concepts in sensorimotor experience.9,10 In this sense, learning may become more meaningful when abstract ideas are connected to physical action and lived experience.11 Supporting this view, Rudling et al12 examined embodied word learning in immersive VR and found that physical interaction with learning content was associated with better recall, suggesting that active bodily interaction can strengthen conceptual encoding and memory. In addition, Lyu and Deng10 meta-analysis of embodied learning from a cognitive load theory perspective showed that embodied learning can improve learning performance while reducing cognitive load, particularly when bodily interactions are meaningfully aligned with the learning task.

Bodily engagement may be especially important in nursing education because VR is expected to support both conceptual understanding and hands-on clinical performance, and it may shape different forms of knowledge acquisition. There are 2 complementary forms of knowledge acquisition that are often examined in immersive learning environments: declarative and procedural learning.13 Declarative learning involves acquiring factual and conceptual knowledge that can be consciously recalled and explained, whereas procedural learning involves acquiring the ability to perform tasks through coordinated action and practice.14 In VR nursing simulation, declarative learning may be supported through contextualized explanations, patient information, and clinical rationales, whereas procedural learning may be supported through repeated enactment of task sequences and clinical actions.14 Immersive VR may, therefore, support both forms of learning by providing realistic, interactive contexts for understanding concepts and rehearsing task execution.15

Because bodily engagement involves physical interaction with the learning environment, it may be particularly relevant for procedural learning, which depends on action, sequencing, and task performance, while also supporting declarative learning by grounding abstract concepts in action and experience.7,10 Thus, bodily engagement may influence not only how students engage with VR, but also the type of knowledge that is most effectively acquired.3 However, VR learning designs vary in the degree to which they allow learners to physically interact with the virtual environment, and these design features may influence learning, cognitive load, and learner experience.16,17 This raises the question of whether free-movement VR offers additional learning benefits compared with seated VR, particularly for procedural learning.10,15

Despite the rapid expansion of VR in nursing education, bodily engagement remains underexplored as a pedagogical feature. This gap is important because VR is increasingly integrated into simulation-based curricula, yet it is unclear whether free movement, defined here as the ability to stand, turn, and move within a designated physical space during the simulation, supports learning without increasing cognitive load or cybersickness. Guided by embodied cognition theory, this study compared free-movement and seated VR conditions in terms of bodily engagement, knowledge acquisition, cognitive load, and cybersickness during declarative and procedural medication-administration learning.

Research Questions

The research questions were: How do bodily engagement patterns during declarative and procedural VR learning tasks differ between free-movement and seated VR conditions? and How do free-movement and seated VR conditions differ in terms of cognitive load, cybersickness, and knowledge acquisition?

Methods

Design

This study used a non-randomized, 2-group quasi-experimental design to compare seated VR and free-movement VR conditions. Participant allocation was conducted sequentially by recruitment phase to reduce contamination between conditions, as the researchers sought to minimize the possibility of participants discussing the study procedures with peers and influencing expectations regarding permitted movement during the simulation.

Participants and Setting

Participants were 76 nursing students enrolled in a 4-year Bachelor of Nursing Sciences program. Students were recruited through email invitations and volunteered to participate in the study. Participation was voluntary, and students were informed that participation or nonparticipation would not affect their grades, course standing, or academic progression. Inclusion criteria were enrollment as a first-year nursing student in the program and willingness to participate in the VR simulation. No exclusion criteria were applied at recruitment. Participants were allocated sequentially to one of 2 study conditions: restricted seated VR or free-movement VR. The experiment was conducted in a university-based simulation laboratory.

A total of 4 participants who had consented to participate were excluded from the analyses due to technical issues. An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power (Version 3.1) for between-group comparisons. Assuming an alpha level of .05 and the statistical power of 0.80, a total sample of approximately 68 participants would be required to detect a moderate-to-large effect size (Cohen d ≈ 0.69) in an independent-samples comparison based on Cohen conventions for statistical power analysis.18 Accordingly, the final analytical sample of 72 participants was considered adequate for the planned between-group analyses.

Procedures

Each participant engaged in a VR learning simulation delivered through the same head-mounted display. Apart from the movement instructions, all study procedures, learning content, and task instructions were identical across conditions. Participants in the seated VR condition were instructed to remain seated throughout the simulation, whereas participants in the free-movement VR condition were allowed to move freely within the designated physical space during the simulation.

To reduce potential adverse effects, participants were monitored during the VR simulation and were informed that they could pause or discontinue participation if they experienced discomfort, dizziness, nausea, or other cybersickness symptoms. The research team was present during the simulation to manage technical issues and participant discomfort. Data were collected at multiple time points throughout the study. The Medication-Administration Test (MAT) and the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ) were administered before and after the VR learning session. The Subjective Rating of Mental Effort (SRME) was administered 3 times during the learning process. After completing the VR simulation and postsimulation measures, participants were given an opportunity to ask questions.

The VR-Based Simulation

Students in both study conditions learned using an identical VR simulation, which was developed and validated in previous studies.4,19 The VR simulation was designed as an interactive, 3-dimensional hospital ward in which each participant was represented by a nurse avatar and could learn and practice the medication-administration procedure. The simulation environment included a virtual patient room containing medical equipment and objects such as medication trays, patient records, and medical equipment. Within this environment, learners could navigate the space, interact with objects, and perform the task-relevant actions required to complete the medication-administration procedure. Interaction with the environment was designed to mimic real-world clinical actions, allowing participants to select items, examine patients health conditions, and perform procedural steps through embodied interaction with virtual objects.

The VR learning experience was structured into several sequential phases. The simulation began with an orientation phase in which participants became familiar with the virtual environment, navigation controls, and interaction mechanisms. Following orientation, learners progressed through instructional activities that targeted both declarative and procedural modules related to medication administration. The declarative learning component focused on the knowledge needed for safe medication administration, including identifying the right medication and the right patient, calculating dosages, understanding the patient’s condition, and learning the safety guidelines and rationale for each step of care. The procedural learning component focused on carrying out the medication-administration process step by step (see Supplemental Digital Content, Figure 1, https://links.lww.com/NE/C413). Participants practiced these actions through repeated interaction with the simulation and received immediate feedback. The VR simulation lasted an average of 43.78 minutes (SD = 8.24).

Instruments

Bodily Engagement

Bodily engagement was assessed using automated MediaPipe motion-detection algorithms that estimate body landmarks from video data.20 Movement events were identified by calculating changes in joint angles across successive frames. A total of 3 types of movement were examined as indicators of participants’ physical engagement during the simulation: torso movements reflecting overall spatial activity, hand movements representing fine motor actions, and head movements indicating spatial orientation and attentional shifts during task performance. Movement metrics were calculated as the rate of detected movements during the task, reflecting participants’ physical engagement throughout the simulation.

To validate the automated measures, the authors manually coded hand, head, and torso movements in video recordings from approximately 25% of participants. Agreement was assessed using Spearman correlations. Results indicated high agreement for hand (ρ = .80) and torso (ρ = .90) movements and good agreement for head movements (ρ = .70), supporting the reliability of the automated motion-detection procedure.

The Simulator Sickness Questionnaire

The SSQ, developed by Kennedy et al, is a validated self-report measure used to assess simulator sickness symptoms.21 In this study, the SSQ was used to measure cybersickness across 15 symptoms rated on a 4-point scale, including nausea, disorientation, oculomotor strain, and general discomfort. Because the SSQ is a self-report questionnaire completed by participants, rater training was not required. In the current sample, Cronbach alpha was .79, indicating acceptable internal consistency. The SSQ was administered before and immediately after the VR simulation.

Subjective Rating of Mental Effort

The SRME scale, developed by Paas, is a single-item self-report measure used to assess the amount of mental effort invested in a learning activity.22 Responses are recorded on a 9-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very, very low mental effort) to 9 (very, very high mental effort). Because the SRME is a single-item self-report measure, internal consistency reliability and rater training are not applicable. In the present study, the SRME was administered at 3 time points during the simulation.

Medication-Administration Test

The MAT assessed nursing students’ knowledge of medication-administration guidelines. Validated in previous studies,4,19 the test comprises 10 multiple-choice items across 2 dimensions: (i) declarative knowledge of medication-administration procedures following guidelines, such as handling expired medication; and (ii) procedural knowledge of medication-administration tasks, such as identifying the correct medication and applying the appropriate administration sequence. The MAT showed modest but acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach α = .65), which falls within the empirically observed range for short knowledge tests.23 The MAT was administered both before and immediately after the VR simulation.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (Armonk, New York). Normality was assessed using skewness and kurtosis indices, which indicated that parametric analyses were appropriate. Baseline group differences were tested using an independent-samples t test and chi-square test. As age differed significantly between groups, it was controlled for in subsequent analyses. Multivariate analysis of covariance (MANCOVA) was used to examine between-group differences in bodily engagement measures. Repeated-measures ANCOVA was used to assess changes in cybersickness and cognitive load across time. Group differences in posttest knowledge outcomes were examined using ANCOVA, controlling for age and the relevant pretest score. Statistical significance was set at P < .05 for all analyses. A total of 4 participants who consented to participate were excluded from all analyses because of technical issues during data collection; therefore, the final analytical sample included 72 participants.

Ethical Considerations

The study was approved by the authors’ University’s Institutional Review Board (No. 0001776-6). All participants provided informed consent before participation.

Results

Baseline demographic characteristics are presented in Supplemental Digital Content, Table 1, https://links.lww.com/NE/C414. Participants in the seated VR group were significantly older than participants in the free-movement VR group (M = 25.48, SD = 2.79 vs. M = 22.84, SD = 3.49), t(70) = −3.55, P < .001. There were no statistically significant differences in gender or socioeconomic status between the study groups.

Cybersickness

The SSQ was administered at two time points, before and after the VR simulation. Mean cybersickness scores increased from pre- to postsimulation, with overall values of 18.70 (SD = 3.93) and 19.83 (SD = 4.09), respectively. At the group level, the free-movement VR condition showed an increase from 15.63 (SD = 0.98) to 17.81 (SD = 2.68), whereas the seated VR condition showed relatively stable scores, from 21.23 (SD = 3.63) to 21.49 (SD = 4.32). Repeated-measures ANCOVA controlling for age revealed a significant main effect of time (F1,68 = 11.82, P = .001, ηp2 = .148), indicating that cybersickness increased after the VR experience. A significant time × age interaction was also observed (F1,68 = 9.26, P = .003, ηp2 = .120), indicating that age influenced changes in cybersickness over time. However, the time × group interaction was not significant (F1,68 = 1.72, P = .195, ηp2 = .025), indicating that the change in cybersickness did not differ significantly between study conditions.

Cognitive Load

Cognitive load was evaluated using the SRME tool at 3 time points during VR learning. Mean SRME scores across the three time points were 2.87 (SD = 1.70), 3.73 (SD = 1.51), and 4.70 (SD = 1.78) for the free-movement VR condition, and 4.21 (SD = 2.50), 4.39 (SD = 2.26), and 5.21 (SD = 2.36), respectively, for the seated VR condition. Repeated-measures ANCOVA controlling for age showed no significant effect of time (Wilks’ lambda = .940, F2,64 = 2.04, P = .138) and no significant time × group interaction (Wilks’ lambda = .944, F2,64 = 1.91, P = .157). There was also no significant main effect of group (F1,65 = 3.42, P = .069).

Knowledge

As shown in Table 2, mean posttest knowledge scores were higher than pretest scores in both VR conditions. ANCOVA models controlling for age and the corresponding pretest score showed that posttest overall knowledge and declarative knowledge did not differ significantly between the groups. However, the group effect for posttest procedural knowledge was significant, indicating an advantage for the free-movement VR condition over the seated VR condition.

Discussion

This study examined bodily engagement as an instructional feature of VR-based medication-administration simulation. A total of 3 main findings emerged. First, bodily engagement differed between conditions, with greater hand movement during both declarative and procedural learning and greater torso movement during procedural learning in the free-movement condition. Second, students in the free-movement condition demonstrated higher procedural knowledge posttest scores, whereas declarative knowledge did not differ between conditions. Third, higher procedural knowledge in the free-movement condition was not accompanied by significant between-group differences in cognitive load or cybersickness. These findings are relevant to nursing education because bodily engagement may have pedagogical implications across simulation-based curricula.

Bodily engagement differed between the 2 VR conditions. Students in the free-movement VR condition demonstrated greater hand movement during both the declarative and procedural learning segments and greater torso movement during the procedural segment, whereas head movement did not differ significantly between conditions. These findings suggest that allowing learners to move freely in VR might change the way in which they engage physically with the learning environment, particularly through action-oriented body regions that are directly involved in task execution. From the perspective of embodied cognition, this pattern is important because it suggests that bodily engagement in VR is not merely incidental but may represent an instructional feature through which learners interact more actively with the simulated clinical environment.16,24

A particularly important point is that hand movements were the most consistently significant movement type across the 2 learning segments. A plausible explanation is that hand actions were most tightly coupled with the functional demands of the medication-administration task. Thus, hand movements were not incidental motor activity; they were directly involved in how learners searched for, verified, and applied medication-related information. Prior research has suggested that gestures and hand movements can support learning by helping learners organize information in action and by reducing cognitive demands during task performance.25 The absence of between-group differences in head movement may indicate that both conditions required similar levels of visual attention and orientation, whereas free movement primarily expanded into action-related engagement.

A central finding of the present study is that students who learned in the free-movement VR condition achieved significantly higher procedural knowledge posttest scores but did not differ in declarative knowledge. This distinction is theoretically important. Declarative knowledge involves factual and conceptual understanding that can be consciously recalled and explained, whereas procedural knowledge involves knowing how to carry out actions in a coordinated and context-sensitive manner.14,17 Because procedural learning is inherently more closely tied to action, sequencing, and interaction with the environment, it is plausible that free bodily engagement provided an added learning benefit specifically for this form of knowledge. In contrast, declarative knowledge may depend less on bodily enactment and more on remembering guidelines, concepts, and factual information, which can likely be acquired effectively in both conditions. Accordingly, the findings suggest that bodily engagement in VR may be especially relevant when the intended learning outcome is procedural rather than purely declarative.13,15 This finding also aligns with Miller Pyramid of clinical competence, suggesting that task-relevant bodily engagement may be particularly important as learners move from knowing and knowing how toward action-oriented performance in simulated clinical contexts.26

Importantly, the learning benefit associated with task-relevant free movement in VR was not accompanied by a penalty in 2 commonly reported disadvantages of immersive learning environments—cybersickness and cognitive load.3,6 Although cybersickness increased over time across participants, this increase did not differ significantly between the 2 VR conditions. Similarly, no significant between-group differences were found in cognitive load. From an embodied cognition perspective, this finding suggests that task-relevant movement may have supported procedural learning by linking clinical actions to sensorimotor experience without increasing cognitive load or exacerbating cybersickness. This suggests that the procedural learning advantage observed in the free-movement condition was achieved without negatively affecting the learning process, an interpretation that aligns with and extends prior research. For example, Yeo et al27 developed an immersive pediatric nursing simulation and found that learners viewed direct experience through hands-on manipulation as one of its main strengths, suggesting that bodily interaction may help make clinical content more concrete, active, and experientially grounded. Extending this line of research, the present findings suggest that the educational value of VR may depend not only on the immersion itself but also on the degree of bodily engagement it affords.3,24

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, a quasi-experimental design was used rather than random assignment, and the groups differed significantly in age, requiring statistical control. Although ANCOVA was used to address this difference, residual confounding cannot be fully ruled out. Second, the sample was drawn from a single institution and consisted predominantly of female nursing students, which may limit generalizability. Third, the study’s focus was on one simulation domain, medication administration, and the effects of bodily engagement may differ for other nursing tasks or other forms of immersive learning. Accordingly, future studies should consist of randomized designs, examinations of additional immersive-learning clinical contexts, and the inclusion of delayed follow-up assessments to evaluate knowledge retention over time. Fourth, although the researchers were not involved in teaching or grading the participating students, the academic setting may have created perceived power imbalances. Participation was voluntary, and students were informed that their decision to participate or not participate would not affect their grades, course standing, or academic progression. Nevertheless, perceived researcher authority and the educational context should be considered when interpreting participation and response behavior.

Conclusions

This study suggests that bodily engagement is an important instructional consideration in VR-based nursing simulation. Free movement was associated with greater task-relevant movement and higher procedural knowledge scores, without increasing cognitive load or cybersickness. For nurse educators, these findings indicate that VR simulations for procedural skills should allow students to physically enact clinically meaningful actions, such as reaching, selecting, checking, and sequencing task components. In other words, movement should be included when it directly supports the clinical skill being taught, rather than added only to make the VR experience feel more immersive.
