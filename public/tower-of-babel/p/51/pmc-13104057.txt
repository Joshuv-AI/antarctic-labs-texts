Diagnostic Challenges of Left Atrial Myxoma Presenting With Systemic Inflammatory Symptoms, Thrombocytopenia, and Diffuse Lymphadenopathy
Authors: Aditya Belamkar, Kevin Lim, Lacey Durham, Kevin Hodges, Nausheen Akhter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLeft atrial (LA) myxomas are the most common primary cardiac tumors but may present with nonspecific systemic symptoms, making diagnosis challenging.Case SummaryA 40-year-old man with rheumatoid arthritis presented with 2 months of tachycardia, low-grade fevers, and exertional dyspnea. He trialed certolizumab without improvement in his symptoms. Multimodal imaging revealed a large LA mass with features concerning for tumor thrombus and malignant cardiac mass with notable diffuse lymphadenopathy. He underwent robotic-assisted resection of the mass and mediastinal lymph node biopsy. Pathology confirmed myxoma with reactive non-necrotizing granulomatous lymphadenopathy. Systemic symptoms resolved postoperatively.DiscussionThis case illustrates an atypical LA myxoma presentation with constitutional symptoms. Imaging findings were notable for lymphadenopathy, complicating the diagnosis. Multidisciplinary evaluation and advanced imaging were critical in management.Take-Home MessagesMyxomas may mimic malignant tumors when presenting with systemic inflammation, lymphadenopathy, and atypical echocardiographic findings. Robotic-assisted surgical resection is an effective, minimally invasive option for select cardiac tumors.

History of Presentation

A 40-year-old man presented with 2 months of progressive tachycardia, fatigue, dyspnea, low-grade fever, and bilateral lower extremity edema. He had initially been evaluated by his rheumatologist for concern of a rheumatoid arthritis (RA) flare and was started on biweekly certolizumab, which provided transient symptom relief. He reported persistent resting tachycardia ranging from 100 to 140 beats/min, including during sleep, compared with his baseline of 65 beats/min. He also endorsed recurrent fevers up to 101 °F (38.3 °C), exertional intolerance with difficulty climbing 3 to 4 flights of stairs, transient orthostatic dizziness with brief darkening of visual fields, and progressive ankle swelling. He denied chest pain, syncope, or neurologic symptoms. Past Medical History

His past medical history was notable for RA treated with certolizumab, bilateral carpal tunnel syndrome, episcleritis, type 2 diabetes mellitus (hemoglobin A1c: 8.2%, reference range: <5.7%), hypertension, hyperlipidemia, prior hyperthyroidism due to temporary thyroiditis, and obstructive sleep apnea (nonadherent with continuous positive airway pressure). He quit smoking in 2009 after a 3 pack-year history and discontinued social alcohol consumption of 1 case of beer weekly 6 months prior. Family history was significant for premature coronary artery disease in an older brother who required coronary bypass surgery in his early 40s. He had no prior cardiothoracic surgeries.

Differential Diagnosis

The initial differential for his systemic symptoms is rather broad. Important considerations include congestive heart failure, anemia, hyperthyroidism, malignancy, vasculitis, and autoimmune inflammatory conditions. The transient response to certolizumab raised concern for an underlying systemic inflammatory or autoimmune process.

Investigations

A baseline electrocardiogram demonstrated normal sinus rhythm. Cardiac monitoring revealed no arrhythmias. Initial transthoracic echocardiography showed preserved left ventricular systolic function with an ejection fraction of 54%, normal diastolic parameters, normal right-sided structure and function, no valvular abnormalities, and a large 1.7- × 2.9-cm left atrial (LA) echodensity abutting the interatrial septum (Figure 1).Figure 1Initial Transthoracic Echocardiography Demonstrating a Novel Left Atrial Mass in the Apical 4-Chamber View With and Without Contrast

Subsequent transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) confirmed preserved biventricular structure and function and further characterized the heterogeneous LA mass as 3.3 × 2.2 cm, attached by a broad-based stalk to the interatrial septum adjacent to the right upper pulmonary vein ostium (Video 1). The administration of contrast revealed heterogeneous uptake, suggesting vascularity, and an echodark rim surrounding the lesion consistent with layered thrombus (Video 2).

At this time, he was referred to cardiac surgery for removal. In preparation, the patient underwent computed tomography cardiac coronary angiography and computed tomography angiography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis. These studies were notable for a lack of evidence of coronary disease with a calcium score of 0 and cervical, thoracic, and abdominal lymphadenopathy of unknown etiology. Cardiac magnetic resonance with and without contrast demonstrated heterogeneous T2 hyperintensity and mildly heterogenous enhancement (Figure 2). There was no myocardial delayed enhancement or evidence of focal or diffuse myocardial fibrosis.Figure 2Cardiac Magnetic Resonance Demonstrated Heterogeneous T2 Hyperintensity and Mildly Heterogeneous Enhancement Without Myocardial Fibrosis, Consistent With a Myxomatous Presentation

The echocardiographic description of the mass presented a differential of lipomatous hypertrophy of the interatrial septum, benign myxoma, and malignant thrombus. Follow-up TEE imaging suggested myxoma given the pedunculated nature of the mass, but a thrombus could not be definitively excluded. Magnetic resonance imaging findings suggested myxoma compared with other etiologies, and especially the presence of enhancement suggested thrombus was less likely. The presence of diffuse lymphadenopathy prompted a differential of malignancy vs granulomatous conditions.

Management

The patient developed new-onset thrombocytopenia (platelet count: 42 × 109/L, reference range: 150-400 109/L) before surgery, suspected to reflect platelet consumption by the LA thrombus mass. Platelet counts spontaneously normalized to 213 × 109/L without medical intervention before the procedure.

He underwent robotic-assisted resection of the LA mass and mediastinal lymph node biopsy. The 3.5-cm mass and a 1.5-cm prevascular lymph node were excised and sent for pathology. Robotic-assisted right thoracoscopic approach was used, providing a lateral trajectory through the interatrial groove that allowed precise tumor resection with minimal septal distortion (Video 3). Magnification via a stereoscopic camera and the dexterity of wristed robotic instruments facilitated safe and complete excision. Intraoperative TEE demonstrated a patent LA appendage without thrombus and no flow disturbance in the pulmonary veins or involvement of the mitral valve.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Postoperative TEE demonstrated complete resection of the mass, and bubble study with Valsalva maneuvers was negative for an atrial septal defect (Video 4). The patient was discharged from the cardiothoracic intensive care unit after 2 days without any complications in his hospital course.

Histopathology examination of the mediastinal thymic lymph node demonstrated non-necrotizing granulomatous changes, which was thought to be reactive in the setting of systemic inflammation, and without evidence of leukemia or lymphoma. After discharge, the patient was scheduled for longitudinal follow-up with hematology, cardiology, and cardiac surgery. He has remained asymptomatic with no postoperative complications.

Discussion

LA myxomas are the most common primary cardiac tumors, comprising 50% to 85%, and with an estimated incidence of 0.03% in the general population.1 Cardiac symptoms may be the main clinical feature in 40.9% of patients, whereas systemic and neurologic manifestations may occur in 27.3% and 13.6% of patients, respectively.2 Atrial septal tumors are more likely to present with heart failure symptoms, whereas extraseptal tumors more frequently present with neurologic and embolic features.3 Clefted tumor surfaces are also highly consistent with embolization risk, whereas solid tumors are more likely to display internal hemorrhage.3 It is important to note that 20.1% patients with myxomas may experience embolism, and risk is significantly increased by hypertension, NYHA functional class I/II, irregular tumor surface, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, elevated mean platelet volume, and high tumor mobility.4 Thrombocytopenia has been rarely described in association with myxomas and typically resolves after surgical resection. In our case, however, platelet counts normalized spontaneously before surgery and remained within normal limits thereafter. The patient was referred to hematology, and no etiology was found.

Although constitutional symptoms occur in a subset of patients (34%), this case was unique due to the degree and distribution of systemic manifestations observed.5 More commonly, myxomas may present with symptoms of mitral valve obstruction, consistent with heart failure pathophysiology (67%) or embolism with predominantly cerebral deficits (29%). The presence of extensive constitutional symptoms with myxoma raises the possibility of cytokine-mediated inflammatory response, most notably interleukin-6 production, which has been implicated in prior reports.6 However, the marked extent of symptoms in this patient was unusual and prompted consideration of alternative or concomitant diagnoses, including malignant primary cardiac tumor or secondary lymphomatous presentation. Interleukin-6 levels were not measured in this patient to confirm this correlation; however, lymph node pathology and systemics symptoms do suggest inflammatory etiology.

Although myxomas are generally well circumscribed and noninvasive, the contrast-enhanced TEE images in this case raised concern for a malignant process, particularly given the irregular surface contour and heterogeneous echogenicity particularly of the tumor surface. The differential diagnosis included tumor thrombus and malignancy, especially relevant given the lesion's broad-based attachment, possible intracavitary protrusion, and presence adjacent to the pulmonary venous ostium, underscoring the need for prompt surgical resection to confirm the diagnosis and mitigate embolic risk.

An additional unusual feature was the degree of diffuse lymphadenopathy. Although regional lymph node enlargement has been reported in rare cases of cardiac tumors, the extent observed here was thought to be atypical for benign myxoma. Additionally, the RA is more commonly associated with localized lymphadenopathy during periods of active disease, and lymphadenopathy from certolizumab would be expected to reflect lymphoproliferative processes, which were not reflected in the histopathology. This further reinforced the importance of a comprehensive immunohistopathologic evaluation to exclude metastatic or primary malignant cardiac neoplasms such as sarcoma or lymphoma.

Given the potential for embolization, hemodynamic compromise, and diagnostic uncertainty, the decision was made to proceed with minimally invasive, robotic-assisted surgical excision. Robotic cardiac surgery offers the advantages of reduced surgical trauma, faster postoperative recovery, and enhanced visualization of intracardiac structures, which facilitates precise tumor resection while minimizing atrial wall disruption. Robotically assisted surgeries have been noted to improve quality of life postoperatively through shorter length of stays and earlier return to work.7,8 They also reduce average cardiopulmonary bypass time and average aortic cross-clamping time, and overall safety is very well established.9 In this case, the minimally invasive approach allowed for complete removal of the mass with preservation of surrounding tissue integrity, supporting the growing role of robotic interventions in select patients.

Conclusions

The atypical presentation of LA myxoma in this case with extensive constitutional symptoms, thrombocytopenia, diffuse lymphadenopathy, and imaging features concerning for malignancy produced diagnostic complexity. Multimodal imaging and a multidisciplinary approach were critical for timely intervention. Minimally invasive, robotic-assisted resection allowed complete tumor excision with rapid recovery, and histopathology confirmed the benign nature of the mass and its associated inflammatory pathophysiology. This case underscores the importance of considering myxoma in patients with unexplained intracardiac masses and systemic inflammatory symptoms, and it highlights the value of minimally invasive surgical approaches in appropriate candidates. Future studies may help clarify the pathophysiological link between systemic inflammatory responses, lymphadenopathy, and benign vs malignant cardiac masses, potentially improving preoperative risk stratification and clinical outcomes.
