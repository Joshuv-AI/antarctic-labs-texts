The ‘Cross‐Trust’ Histopathology Multidisciplinary Team: Report of meeting outputs and impact on paediatric inflammatory bowel disease management in a UK tertiary centre
Authors: Valeria Dipasquale, Alenka J. Brooks, Marta Cohen, Jonathan Bury, Akshay Kapoor, Priya Narula
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the outputs and clinical impact of a Cross‐Trust Histopathology Multidisciplinary Team (MDT) created to support diagnostic clarification and management of complex paediatric inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) cases, particularly during transition to adult services.MethodsA retrospective review of eight MDT meetings held from January 2021 to March 2024 was performed. Patients were included if referred for diagnostic uncertainty, complex disease behaviour or transition planning. Extracted data included demographics, reason for referral, MDT‐agreed diagnosis, therapeutic recommendations and transition‐related decisions.ResultsTwenty‐seven patients (mean age 17.2 years; range: 14–21) were discussed. Diagnostic clarification was the primary referral reason. Following MDT review, 11 patients (41%) had a change in diagnosis, including reclassification of IBD‐unclassified to ulcerative colitis (n = 7) or Crohn's disease (n = 3). One patient was reclassified as having non‐IBD (disuse colitis). Management recommendations changed in three patients (11%), including biologic initiation or escalation. One patient (4%) had a modification to their transition pathway. MDT outputs were uploaded into the paediatric electronic health record in only three cases (11%).ConclusionThe Cross‐Trust Histopathology MDT improved diagnostic precision in complex paediatric IBD and influenced management in selected cases. The model supports harmonized interpretation across paediatric and adult services and promotes safer transition planning. Documentation of MDT outcomes remains an area for improvement. Wider adoption of similar collaborative frameworks may enhance diagnostic consistency and optimize care for adolescents with IBD.

INTRODUCTION

Paediatric inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) presents an increasing clinical challenge globally, with incidence rates rising particularly among adolescents and young adults. 1 The disease spectrum includes Crohn's disease (CD), ulcerative colitis (UC) and IBD‐unclassified (IBD‐U). IBD‐U is especially prevalent in paediatric populations, affecting up to 20% of newly diagnosed children, largely due to overlapping clinical presentations, ambiguous endoscopic findings and inconclusive histopathology. 2 , 3 Correct disease classification is crucial, as CD and UC differ substantially in their natural history, therapeutic requirements and long‐term prognostic implications. Misclassification can delay initiation of optimal therapy, expose patients to inappropriate treatments and negatively impact transition to adult services. Complex paediatric IBD cases—characterized by extensive disease, aggressive phenotypes, multi‐system involvement or atypical histopathology—pose diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. These patients frequently require nuanced decision‐making regarding immunomodulatory therapy, biologic escalation, surgical interventions and nutritional support, underscoring the need for integrated expertise across disciplines. Despite recognition of the benefits of multidisciplinary team (MDT) care, the impact of structured collaboration between paediatric and adult specialists, particularly in relation to histopathology review, remains underexplored in the literature. Existing studies suggest that MDT involvement improves diagnostic precision, informs therapeutic decisions and facilitates smoother transitions from paediatric to adult care, 4 but few reports formally quantify these outcomes in complex cases.

To address this gap, a ‘Cross‐Trust’ Histopathology MDT was established at a UK tertiary referral centre. This centre is one of only three standalone children's hospitals in the country. As the paediatric and adult trusts operate as separate organizations, the term ‘Cross‐Trust’ is used to describe the collaboration of teams from these two distinct entities during an MDT meeting. Held twice a year, this meeting unites paediatric and adult gastroenterologists with specialist gastrointestinal (GI) histopathologists to review complex paediatric IBD cases, with particular attention to patients nearing the transition to adult care. The MDT aims to harmonize interpretations of histopathological findings, reconcile diagnostic uncertainties and refine management strategies through collaborative decision‐making. The current service evaluation seeks to characterize the outputs of these meetings, assess their influence on diagnostic accuracy and treatment planning and elucidate the potential role of such cross‐speciality collaborations in optimizing care for complex paediatric IBD patients. By systematically evaluating these interactions, this study highlights the critical importance of structured, cross‐disciplinary communication in achieving precise diagnoses and personalized therapeutic strategies.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This project was approved and registered on the Trust's Clinical Audit and Effectiveness Database. Formal ethical approval was not required as this was a review of service delivery.

Study design and setting

We performed a retrospective review of recorded outputs from ‘Cross‐Trust’ Histopathology MDT meetings held between January 2021 and March 2024. The sessions were convened approximately twice per year at a UK tertiary referral centre, jointly attended by paediatric gastroenterologists, adult IBD physicians and specialist histopathologists, to discuss complex IBD patients in transition and formulate shared management and treatment strategies. Patients were selected for discussion by paediatric or adult IBD teams based on complexity, diagnostic uncertainty or need for transition planning (Figure 1). The selection of an appropriate patient can follow one of three pathways: (i) the paediatric gastroenterologist may identify the case before transition or (ii) the adult gastroenterologist may do so after transition or (iii) both specialists may select the patient collaboratively during the transition period in a joint transition clinic. Variables extracted included age, sex, reason for MDT referral, initial diagnosis, final MDT‐agreed diagnosis, therapeutic changes, surgical considerations, modifications to transition planning and whether MDT notes were uploaded into the paediatric electronic health record.

MDT meeting setting up

The first step involves identifying a mutually convenient time for all participants and selecting the appropriate patients. The paediatric histopathology team then retrieves and reviews the slides, which are then sent by post or courier to the adult histopathology team for their assessment. At the MDT meeting, both teams present their findings and discuss them, aiming to reach a consensus. Throughout the process, clinicians provide the clinical background, outline the specific questions to be addressed and summarize the patient's treatment history to guide the discussion.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes were (i) the proportion of cases in which the MDT led to a change in diagnostic labelling, (ii) the proportion of cases in which therapeutic recommendations were altered and (iii) transition‐related decisions influenced by MDT discussion. Secondary outcomes included the completeness of documentation in the electronic health record.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics are presented as means with ranges or percentages of the total cohort. No comparative statistical testing was performed, as this was a descriptive service evaluation.

RESULTS

Across eight meetings held from 2021 to 2024, 27 patients were discussed. During the same period, 98 paediatric IBD patients transitioned from paediatric to adult services at our centre, indicating that MDT review was involved in approximately 28% of transitions. The mean age at the time of discussion was 17.2 years (range: 14–21). Seventeen patients were under the paediatric team, while 10 aged 18–25 years were discussed on the adult team's request. Sixteen patients (59%) were male.

The primary reason for referral was to clarify the diagnosis. After MDT review, 11 patients (41%) had their diagnosis changed. Of these, seven initially classified as IBD‐U favouring UC were reclassified as UC, and three as CD. In the remaining case, the MDT concluded that the findings did not match IBD, leading to a diagnosis of disuse colitis. Histopathological features driving reclassification included crypt architectural distortion, basal plasmacytosis and granulomas. The other 16 patients (59%) had their original diagnosis confirmed.

Therapeutic recommendations were modified in three patients (11%). Two patients commenced or escalated biologic therapy, specifically infliximab in one and vedolizumab in the other. A third patient had a non‐biologic adjustment to their regimen. No patients were deemed candidates for surgical intervention during the study period. The time from MDT recommendation to therapy initiation ranged from 3–7 days, reflecting timely integration of MDT decisions into clinical care. Only one patient (4%) had a modification to their transition pathway. In this case, referral to paediatric immunology was recommended before transfer to adult IBD services, thereby delaying transition. The remaining 16 patients under paediatric care proceeded along standard transition pathways. MDT outputs were recorded by the adult IBD team and uploaded to the adult electronic health records. However, these were uploaded into the electronic health record of the paediatric IBD service in only three cases (11%).

DISCUSSION

This service evaluation describes the role of a Cross‐Trust Histopathology MDT in a selected cohort of complex paediatric IBD cases. While the number of patients discussed was limited, reflecting the highly specialized and referral‐based nature of the MDT, the findings show a meaningful impact on diagnostic clarification. Nearly half of the patients experienced a revision in diagnosis, and one was ultimately found not to have IBD. These findings highlight the essential role of specialist histopathology review combined with multidisciplinary discussion in refining diagnoses and guiding management, especially in late adolescence, when care is expected to transfer to an adult service. To our knowledge, this is one of the first formal evaluations of a cross‐trust MDT specifically dedicated to histopathology review in paediatric IBD.

The reclassification of seven patients from IBD‐U to UC and one patient to CD highlights the diagnostic challenges encountered in paediatric practice. Previous studies suggest that up to 20% of paediatric IBD cases are initially categorized as IBD‐U. 3 As the disease progresses, serial endoscopic evaluations are critical, and these should be reviewed sequentially to determine whether observed changes are due to disease progression or the effects of treatment. Moreover, upper GI endoscopy is typically performed only at specialized paediatric IBD centres, which can further complicate the diagnostic process for IBD‐U cases. Accurate phenotypic classification is essential, as treatment strategies for UC and CD differ significantly. In this context, the MDT model plays a crucial role in ensuring that therapeutic decisions are based on well‐established diagnostic criteria, thereby minimizing the risk of both under‐treatment and over‐treatment.

Although therapeutic changes were required in only a minority of cases (11%), their clinical relevance should be interpreted in the context of this highly selected population. Two patients required escalation to biologics, a decision with significant implications for growth, mucosal healing and reduction in hospitalization when based on accurate diagnosis. 5 , 6 One patient was spared unnecessary immunosuppression following identification of a non‐IBD diagnosis. Initiation of biologic therapy is multifactorial and rarely based on histology alone. However, in patients with diagnostic uncertainty, particularly those previously labelled as IBD‐unclassified, histopathological clarification contributed to increased diagnostic confidence within the broader clinical context. This facilitated appropriate therapeutic escalation when indicated and, importantly, prevented unnecessary immunosuppression. These findings suggest that the MDT's principal value lies in strengthening diagnostic accuracy, which in turn supports safer and more individualized treatment decisions.

Transition planning was affected in only one case, yet this highlights the broader value of structured multidisciplinary oversight during healthcare transition. Well‐designed transition pathways, supported by MDT review at key timepoints, can improve adherence, reduce disengagement and support psychosocial adjustment. 7 Beyond individual case modification, the MDT structure facilitates structured communication between paediatric and adult teams, thereby strengthening continuity of care and promoting a safer, more coordinated transition process.

The cross‐trust structure of this MDT represents a key innovation. By pooling expertise from paediatric and adult IBD specialists across two organizations and providing joint histopathology review, this model promotes diagnostic consistency across institutions—a notable advantage in regions with variable access to subspecialty pathologists. Such collaboration can mitigate any disparities and ensure uniformity in care.

Despite its strengths, the evaluation revealed areas requiring improvement, most notably documentation. Although MDT outputs were uploaded to adult electronic health records, only 11% of them were formally uploaded into the paediatric electronic health records. This limited documentation highlights a critical area for improvement to preserve institutional knowledge and facilitate audit or future research. After the service evaluation, we introduced real‐time recording of MDT outcomes within the paediatric team to ensure timely and consistent upload into the paediatric electronic medical record.

Looking forward, several perspectives emerge for enhancing the MDT model. In our service, all meetings were undertaken remotely, a factor that likely contributed to their success by enabling broader participation from clinicians across sites and reducing logistical barriers for families. This highlights the importance of maintaining virtual or hybrid formats to improve accessibility and continuity of multidisciplinary input. An additional area for development is the potential to define clinical standards or referral criteria to identify which patients are most likely to benefit from MDT discussion—such as those with diagnostic uncertainty, complex phenotypes, refractory disease, frequent flares or recurrent need for treatment escalation. Establishing such criteria could optimize case selection, ensure equitable access and maximize the impact of MDT recommendations. Integration of digital pathology platforms and artificial intelligence may further enhance diagnostic precision and reproducibility without replacing expert interpretation. 8 , 9 Prospective studies are needed to determine whether MDT‐driven diagnostic clarification and therapeutic adjustments translate into improved long‐term outcomes, such as sustained remission, surgery‐free survival and quality of life.

Limitations of this service evaluation include the small sample size, retrospective design and incomplete documentation, which limit the granularity of outcome assessment and generalizability to the wider paediatric IBD population. In addition, long‐term clinical outcomes following diagnostic or therapeutic modification were not systematically captured, limiting our ability to directly assess the downstream clinical impact of MDT‐driven decisions. Nonetheless, the findings provide real‐world evidence supporting the utility of cross‐trust MDTs in paediatric IBD. Prospective studies are needed to determine whether MDT‐driven diagnostic clarification and therapeutic adjustments translate into improved long‐term outcomes, including sustained remission and surgery‐free survival.

CONCLUSIONS

In this selected cohort of complex paediatric IBD cases, the Cross‐Trust Histopathology MDT clarified diagnosis in 41% of cases, directly influenced therapy in 11% and supported safer transition in selected patients. Importantly, the meetings prevented misdiagnosis in one patient, avoiding inappropriate immunosuppression. Although documentation remains an area for improvement, the MDT model demonstrates tangible benefits in diagnostic refinement, treatment optimization and transition planning. Wider adoption, enhanced documentation and integration with digital innovations could further improve outcomes for paediatric IBD patients across the UK and internationally.
