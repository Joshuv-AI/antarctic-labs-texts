A novel ultrasonic field-focusing nozzle for enhanced sonochemical reactor performance
Authors: Abdulmajeed Baker, Oualid Hamdaoui, Lahssen El Blidi, Abdulaziz Alghyamah
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUltrasonic reactors operating at megahertz frequencies typically exhibit low cavitation intensity and sonochemical yields. This study introduces an innovative ultrasonic field-focusing nozzle that improves the performance of a 1.7 MHz sonoreactor by concentrating the acoustic field geometrically into a defined focal zone. The sonochemical performance of the reactor with and without the focusing nozzle was compared across solution volumes ranging from 50 to 200 mL. Comprehensive characterization techniques were employed, including calorimetry, KI and Fricke dosimetries, hydrogen peroxide quantification, 4-nitrophenol oxidation, Sunset Yellow FCF (SSY) degradation kinetics, and sonochemiluminescence (SCL) imaging. The focused configuration outperformed the unfocused system across all metrics. Calorimetric power increased by up to 15 %, and radical production rates, as measured by triiodide, Fe3+, and 4-nitrocatechol yields, rose by over 50 % under optimal conditions. SSY degradation rates improved by as much as 78.5 %, and SCL imaging revealed a bright, narrowly confined cavitation zone, which is indicative of elevated local pressure amplitudes. The most significant enhancements occurred at a liquid volume of 80 mL (liquid height of 11.3 cm). This corresponded to the upper boundary of the transducer’s Fresnel (near-field) zone. In this zone, standing wave coherence and constructive interference are maximized. Even at larger volumes (up to 200 mL), the focusing nozzle sustained significant improvements in cavitational activity and sonochemical yield. These results highlight the importance of geometric focusing in overcoming the limitations of high-frequency ultrasonication, especially when acoustic energy would otherwise diminish.Keywords: Ultrasonic reactor, Focusing nozzle, Improvement, Sonochemical activity

Introduction

In recent decades, ultrasound-assisted processes have garnered significant attention due to their ability to intensify chemical reactions, degrade pollutants, and enhance mass and heat transfer in liquid systems [1]. These effects primarily stem from acoustic cavitation, which involves the formation, oscillation, and violent collapse of microscopic bubbles [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. This process creates localized hot spots with temperatures exceeding 5,000 K, pressures greater than 1,000 atm, and intense shear forces [[6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. These extreme conditions can drive physical and chemical transformations, making sonochemistry a promising technology for applications ranging from advanced oxidation processes to nanomaterial synthesis and green chemistry.

Beyond sonochemical reactors, ultrasound has been widely employed in other fields where its ability to induce physical and chemical transformations is advantageous. In emulsification, ultrasonic cavitation generates intense shear forces that break down immiscible phases into fine droplets, producing stable emulsions without the need for chemical surfactants [14]. In metallurgy, ultrasound has been applied to metal solidification processes, where acoustic streaming and cavitation promote grain refinement, degassing, and improved microstructural uniformity [15]. Ultrasound has also proven effective in extraction technologies, where cavitation enhances mass transfer and disrupts cellular structures, thereby increasing the yield of bioactive compounds from natural matrices [16]. These examples illustrate the broad utility of ultrasound-assisted processes across diverse scientific and industrial domains, reinforcing the importance of reactor design and acoustic field control in maximizing efficiency.

However, the efficiency of sonochemical reactors depends heavily on the operating frequency, reactor geometry, and acoustic field distribution. At low frequencies, cavitation bubbles grow larger and collapse more violently, resulting in efficient radical formation and mechanical effects. In contrast, high-frequency ultrasound (≥1 MHz) produces smaller, more numerous bubbles that collapse less energetically, resulting in lower overall cavitation intensity [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21]]. Despite their lower collapse energy, MHz-frequency reactors are ideal for applications requiring controlled, localized reactions or situations in which low-frequency ultrasound could cause unwanted mechanical disruption. Nevertheless, limitations of high-frequency systems, such as rapid acoustic attenuation and limited cavitation penetration depth, present significant challenges to scalability and reactor design.

The ultrasonic field within an ultrasonicated liquid is inherently non-uniform. The geometry of the sonoreactor plays a critical role in determining ultrasonic efficiency by altering the local conditions for acoustic cavitation [[22], [23], [24], [25]]. The reactor's shape and distance from the transducer determine the propagation pattern of the emitted ultrasonic beam, which is divided into two regions. With a cylindrical transducer, about 90 % of the acoustic energy is confined to a region whose diameter matches that of the transducer. This region is known as the Fresnel (near-field) zone. Beyond this zone is the Fraunhofer (far-field) zone, where the wavefront becomes convex and the acoustic intensity diminishes rapidly as it spreads over a larger area. The spatial inhomogeneity of the ultrasonic field poses challenges to the reproducibility of sonochemical processes and the accurate interpretation of the underlying physical and chemical phenomena that occur during ultrasonic irradiation.

The spatial distribution of the ultrasonic field significantly affects cavitation activity [26,27]. In flat-disk transducers, boundary interactions in the near field and impedance mismatches at the air–liquid interface may cause energy to be reflected or scattered. These phenomena result in diffuse cavitation zones and suboptimal radical production.

Several strategies have been explored to overcome the limitations of high-frequency systems. These strategies include using reflectors, phased arrays, and acoustic focusing devices [26,[28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34]]. Geometric focusing of ultrasound has shown promise in intensifying localized cavitation by increasing the amplitude and energy density of acoustic pressure within a defined focal region [26,35,36]. These approaches are particularly effective in MHz systems, where cavitation thresholds are difficult to achieve. However, most prior work has focused on externally mounted or multi-transducer systems, which can be complex and costly. There is still a need for simple, scalable, and robust focusing methods that can be integrated into compact reactor designs.

Several strategies have been reported for concentrating acoustic energy in liquid reactors. Reflectors, particularly conical or parabolic designs, can intensify cavitational activity by redirecting and converging waves toward a focal region. For example, a conical reflector increased triiodide formation by approximately 60 % at 630 kHz in a 300 mL system operated at 120 W, whereas a cylindrical reflector suppressed activity in the same study [30]. These findings underscore the critical influence of geometry and placement. Acoustic lenses fabricated from polymers, composites, or metamaterials are also capable of focusing MHz ultrasound in water, providing compact three‑dimensional convergence. However, their use introduces additional solid–liquid interfaces that can lead to alignment challenges, transmission losses, and aberrations, as noted in the focusing literature [37]. Focused or concave transducers and phased arrays generate strong, steerable foci and are widely applied in therapeutic ultrasound and nondestructive testing. Their implementation in sonochemistry, however, is limited by the need for multi‑channel electronics, calibration, and higher cost [38]. In contrast, a nozzle‑based geometric focus is monolithic and inline with the flow path. This configuration minimizes additional interfaces and enables straightforward retrofitting to flat‑disk MHz transducers, while still delivering localized pressure amplification in a robust and scalable manner.

Geometric focusing via a convergent nozzle is a promising method for concentrating acoustic waves, intensifying local pressure amplitudes, and amplifying cavitation. While focusing elements are well-established in ultrasound imaging and therapeutic applications, they have yet to be systematically implemented in megahertz-range sonochemical reactors.

To address this issue, a stainless-steel focusing nozzle was designed for use with a 1.7 MHz bottom-mounted piezoelectric ceramic transducer. The nozzle's geometry to shape and concentrate acoustic waves into a narrow focal zone within the reactor volume was engineered. It was hypothesized that this focusing effect would amplify cavitation intensity, increase radical yields, and improve sonochemical performance across various liquid volumes. In this study, the impact of mounting the nozzle directly onto a 1.7 MHz piezoelectric disk on reactor performance across solution volumes of 50–200 mL using characterization techniques was evaluated. These methods included calorimetry to assess energy input; iodide (KI) dosimetry and hydrogen peroxide quantification to measure oxidative species generation; Fricke dosimetry and 4-nitrophenol oxidation to evaluate radical production; and Sunset Yellow (SSY) degradation to probe practical sonochemical reactivity. Additionally, sonochemiluminescence (SCL) imaging to map the spatial distribution of cavitation zones with and without the nozzle was used. By comparing the performance of focused and unfocused configurations, the optimal volume condition was identified and the role of the nozzle in improving energy localization and reaction efficiency was elucidated.

Experimental setup and protocols

For the preparation of all solutions, 18.2 MΩ Milli-Q ultrapure water was used. Sunset Yellow FCF (SSY; 2-naphthalenesulfonic acid, 6-hydroxy-5-((4-sulfophenyl)azo)-, disodium salt; 90 % purity) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All remaining reagents were purchased as analytical grade and employed directly as received.

A cylindrical, jacketed glass reactor (3 cm diameter, 215 mL capacity) was used to carry out the experiments. A piezoelectric ceramic disk with a diameter of 2 cm was mounted on the bottom of the reactor and emitted ultrasound at a frequency of 1.7 MHz. Water was circulated through the surrounding jacket to control the temperature. A thermocouple positioned directly within the ultrasonicated liquid provided real-time monitoring. This reactor was used for control experiments without the focusing nozzle. To evaluate the focusing nozzle's impact, it was mounted on the piezoelectric ceramic disk at the base of the sonoreactor.

The focusing nozzle was made of stainless steel to ensure mechanical robustness, corrosion resistance, and stability during prolonged ultrasonic irradiation. It is 35 mm tall and has an external diameter of 21 mm at the base that tapers to 8 mm at the top. The internal diameter measures 18 mm at the base and narrows to 6.5 mm at the top. Fig. 1 presents schematics of the ultrasonication setups both without and with this focusing nozzle.

Schematic of the ultrasonication setups without (left) and with the focusing nozzle (right).

The 35‑mm‑high nozzle was designed with a geometry that aligns the strongest part of the 1.7‑MHz acoustic field with the height of the working liquid. This configuration produces a diffraction‑limited beam that fits within the 30‑mm reactor bore. The internal diameter of the nozzle narrows from 18 mm at the base to 6.5 mm at the outlet. For a 20‑mm circular transducer operating at 1.7 MHz in water, the natural near‑field length is N = r2/λ, where r is the transducer radius (10 mm) and λ is the wavelength (≈0.88 mm). This follows standard transducer field theory for near/far‑field transitions. The outlet diameter was determined using the circular‑aperture diffraction estimate for the first minimum, d = 2·1.22·λ·z/D, where z is the distance from the nozzle lip to the target reaction zone. With D = 6.5 mm, the predicted first‑zero diameter is nearly 25–26 mm, resulting in a beam that remains largely confined within the 30‑mm vessel. This minimizes early wall interactions and preserves on‑axis intensity. The Rayleigh criterion and circular‑aperture diffraction principles were applied to ultrasound in this design. To refine the geometry, two‑dimensional (2D), axisymmetric, parametric simulations (water + stainless steel; frequency‑domain acoustics) were conducted, scanning half‑taper angles from 6 to 14° and outlet diameters from 5–9 mm with a fixed height of 35 mm and an inlet inner diameter of 18 mm. The optimization objectives were to maximize the on‑axis root mean square pressure while constraining wall‑normal velocity at the throat to prevent microstreaming erosion, internal bubble trapping, and excessive shear at the outlet rim. The optimum was identified at a half‑angle of barely 9–10° and an outlet diameter of 6–7 mm. The final internal half‑angle of 9.3° with a 6.5‑mm outlet provided a balance between focusing gain, throughput, and robustness. The simulation‑based choice was further validated through pretests using KI dosimetry and sonochemiluminescence (SCL) imaging. Outlets of 5 mm produced brighter on‑axis SCL columns but also induced unstable neck cavitation and edge streaming. Outlets of 8 mm or greater generated wider, dimmer columns and lower dosimetry rates. The 6.5 mm/9.3° configuration yielded the most intense and stable axially confined sonochemiluminescent column.

Several characterization techniques were employed to examine the impact of the focusing nozzle on the sonochemical activity of a sonoreactor operating at 1.7 MHz with different solution volumes. These methods included calorimetry, KI dosimetry, hydrogen peroxide quantification, Fricke dosimetry, 4-nitrophenol (4-NP) dosimetry, and SSY sonodegradation. Additionally, sonochemiluminescence (SCL) was used to map the cavitation zones both in the absence and presence of the focusing nozzle across the various solution volumes.

Ultrasonic power was quantified via calorimetry [[39], [40], [41], [42], [43]], which measures thermal energy transferred to the medium during a 5-min ultrasonication period. Milli-Q ultrapure water was used as the test fluid, and the reactor jacket was emptied of the cooling liquid to minimize heat dissipation and improve accuracy. Temperature variations were recorded at a fixed point of the reactor using a Type K thermocouple positioned halfway up the fluid column. The thermocouple remained in place throughout the runs. Assuming that all acoustic energy was converted into heat via cavitation, the temperature increase was attributed solely to ultrasonication. To ensure reproducibility, each test was repeated at least three times.

Potassium iodide (KI) dosimetry [44] was used to evaluate the sonochemical efficiency of the oxidative conversion of iodide ions into iodine species via ultrasonication. In aqueous KI solutions, reactive species produced by ultrasound, such as hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and hydrogen peroxide, promote the oxidation of iodide (I−) to iodine (I2). Excess iodide ions then react with iodine to produce triiodide (I3−), which exhibits a UV absorption peak at approximately 351 nm. A 0.1 M KI solution was subjected to 21 min of ultrasonication. Triiodide formation followed zero-order kinetics, enabling determination of a constant production rate. For UV analysis using a Mettler Toledo UV7 spectrophotometer, 1.2 mL of the solution was transferred to quartz cuvettes (1 cm path length) and returned to the reactor to maintain consistent power density. Reproducibility and consistency in spatial and temporal sampling enhanced the robustness of the dosimetric evaluation.

The levels of hydrogen peroxide generated during sonolysis were determined by an iodometric assay. At predetermined intervals, 200 µL of the mixture was withdrawn from the reactor and transferred to a quartz cuvette containing 1 mL of 0.1 M potassium iodide and 20 µL of 0.01 M ammonium heptamolybdate. The mixture was allowed to react for 5 min. During this time, the H2O2 oxidized the I– to I2, and the excess iodide formed the triiodide ion (I3–). After incubation, absorbance was measured at 351 nm. Then, the hydrogen peroxide concentrations were calculated using the triiodide molar extinction coefficient of 26,300 L/mol·cm [40,44,45].

Fricke dosimetry was performed by preparing an acidic solution containing 0.4 M H2SO4, 1 × 10−3 M FeSO4·(NH4)2SO4·6H2O, and 1 × 10−3 M NaCl [40,46]. Under ultrasonication, hydroxyl radicals, hydrogen peroxide, and hydroperoxyl radicals oxidize Fe2+ to Fe3+. After ultrasonication, the Fe3+ concentration was determined spectrophotometrically at 303 nm (ε = 2,197 L/mol·cm). The increase in absorbance, and thus Fe3+ production, was linear with respect to ultrasonication time. This allowed the reaction to be characterized by its constant Fe3+ formation rate.

The dosimetry of 4-NP was based on tracking the sonochemical oxidation of 4-NP into 4-nitrocatechol (4-NC) [47,48]. A 1 mM solution of 4-NP was sonicated at pH 5 (pKa 7.15). During irradiation, 0.6 mL samples were periodically withdrawn and mixed with an equal volume of 0.1 M NaOH in a quartz cuvette. In this alkaline medium, 4-NC exhibits strong absorbance at 511 nm. The 4-NC concentrations were then calculated using a molar extinction coefficient of 12,500 L/mol·cm.

To evaluate the effect of the focusing nozzle, a fresh solution of SSY (10 mg/L) was sonicated for 30 min with identical power settings, both with and without the nozzle. Aliquots of 1.2 mL of the solution was withdrawn and the absorbance at 482 nm was recorded. Each sample was returned to the reactor within 30 s of measurement to maintain consistent ultrasonic power density.

Sonochemiluminescence (SCL) imaging was used to map the cavitation zones within the sonoreactor with and without the focusing nozzle. Various volumes of a 1 mM luminol solution in 0.1 M NaOH [27,30,49] were ultrasonicated, and the resulting light emissions were recorded. To resolve the spatial intensity distributions, a Nikon DSLR camera with an 18–55 mm zoom lens was used to capture SCL patterns with exposure times of 30 and 60 s.

All the tests were independently repeated at least three times for each experiment.

Results and discussion

Calorimetry

Calorimetry is the most common approach for evaluating the power dissipated by ultrasonication in reactors. Fig. 2 shows the variation of ultrasonic power measured by the calorimetric method with ultrasonicated volume in the absence and presence of the focusing nozzle at a fixed frequency of 1.7 MHz. The data show an increase in measured power when the ultrasonicated volume increases from 50 to 150 mL. Then, there is a modest plateau or slight decline when the volume exceeds 175 mL, particularly when using the focusing nozzle. This behavior can be attributed to the interplay of acoustic and thermal factors. As the liquid volume increases, a larger portion of the ultrasonic field is coupled to the medium. This reduces the impact of acoustic losses at the transducer-liquid interface and the reactor walls. In the lower volume range, a significant fraction of the acoustic energy is reflected or dissipated by near-field boundary interactions, limiting the net energy transferred into the liquid. Additionally, the initial coupling efficiency is low at 1.7 MHz due to the formation of strong standing wave nodes near the transducer face and impedance mismatch with the air–liquid interface. As the volume increases, the standing wave pattern becomes more stable, and the effective attenuation per unit volume diminishes. This leads to the observed rise in calorimetric power. These findings align with those of Merouani et al. [40], who conducted experiments at 300 kHz with liquid volumes ranging from 100 to 400 mL.

Variation of ultrasonic power measured by the calorimetric method with ultrasonicated volume in the absence and presence of the focusing nozzle.

The introduction of the focusing nozzle shifts the entire curve upward slightly. This is due to the geometric concentration of acoustic waves. Specifically, the nozzle's taper and outlet diameter are designed to create constructive interference, intensifying cavitation activity and localized heating. This effect is analogous to that seen in ultrasonic reactors, where thermal mapping reveals the highest temperatures and power densities near the ultrasonic emitter. By confining and directing acoustic energy into a well-defined focal zone, the nozzle increases local pressure amplitudes while minimizing divergence and diffraction losses, which typically dissipate acoustic energy. This focused configuration promotes greater energy density and ensures that a larger fraction of the input electrical energy is coupled into the liquid medium. Consequently, the system exhibits more efficient thermal energy conversion, as confirmed by calorimetric measurements across all ultrasonicated volumes.

The curve associated with the focusing nozzle shows a slight drop in measured power at the highest tested volume of 200 mL. This contrasts with the more stable behavior observed without focusing. This behavior suggests that once the liquid level exceeds the optimal focal depth, the benefits of acoustic energy concentration diminish. Under these conditions, a greater proportion of acoustic power is likely attenuated in the far field due to the increased propagation path length and enhanced viscous damping. This effect is further amplified at the operating frequency of 1.7 MHz because higher-frequency ultrasound waves are more susceptible to intrinsic absorption [50]. Consequently, while the focusing nozzle significantly enhances acoustic energy coupling at moderate volumes, its effectiveness diminishes when the focal zone is deeply submerged in larger volumes of liquid.

The difference in slopes between the two setups underscores the critical role of the focusing nozzle in generating uniform cavitation and effectively transmitting energy. Without the focusing nozzle, the acoustic field is more susceptible to turbulent scattering and wave interference. This results in greater variability in power delivery across different volumes. Conversely, the nozzle creates a more stable power-volume relationship, indicating that the focused acoustic field minimizes energy loss by concentrating ultrasound in a confined area.

Without the focusing nozzle, the acoustic power applied to the reactor corresponded to acoustic intensities of 5585.9, 6632.8, 7044.1, 7394.1, 7479.1, 7717.6, and 7785.1 W/m2, with the corresponding acoustic pressure amplitudes calculated as 129.45, 141.06, 145.37, 148.94, 149.79, 152.16, and 152.82 kPa for liquid volumes of 50, 75, 100, 125, 150, 175, and 200 mL, respectively. In the presence of the focusing nozzle, the acoustic intensities were slightly elevated, measured at 5738.7, 6694.1, 7173.5, 7585.4, 7691.2, 8046.8, and 7808.8 W/m2, while the corresponding acoustic amplitudes were 131.21, 141.71, 146.70, 150.85, 151.90, 155.37, and 153.06 kPa for the same respective liquid volumes. As the liquid volume increased, both the acoustic intensity and amplitude exhibited a slight upward trend. The introduction of the focusing nozzle consistently enhanced both parameters across all tested volumes. Importantly, the observed increases in acoustic amplitude directly translate into elevated local acoustic pressures during cavitation bubble oscillation. Since the maximum internal bubble temperatures and pressures scale with the driving acoustic pressure, even modest increases in amplitude can substantially enhance the production of hydroxyl radicals and other reactive species. Although the increases in intensity and amplitude were relatively modest in absolute terms, the nonlinear response of cavitation phenomena means that these differences can have a disproportionately large impact on sonochemical reactivity. Indeed, cavitation thresholds and radical yields are highly sensitive to incremental changes in pressure amplitude, particularly as the system approaches the upper stability limit of stable bubble oscillation.

When using the calorimetric technique, it is important to consider heat loss to the surroundings. However, in small-scale sonoreactors, this heat loss is usually negligible. Specifically, heat transfer through the glass walls of the sonoreactor can be disregarded because glass has relatively low thermal conductivity (approximately 0.8 W/m·K). In contrast, heat conduction through the focusing nozzle requires consideration. Although stainless steel has comparatively low thermal conductivity among metals (approximately 15 W/m·K), it can absorb ultrasonic energy. Consequently, stainless steel components, may act as heat sinks, slightly decreasing the energy available to heat the liquid medium. Interestingly, calorimetric power measurements in the sonoreactor with the focusing nozzle may appear higher because the energy conducted through the focusing nozzle contributes to the total measured heat dissipation.

In principle, the stainless‑steel nozzle can act as a local heat sink through its finite heat capacity, which allows temporary absorption of energy during warming, and through its increased heat‑exchange area, which enhances convective heat loss from the liquid. In classic initial‑slope calorimetry, the measured temperature rise is governed by the energy balance: dTdt=Pac-QlossCeff, where Ceff represents the effective heat capacity of the heated system and Qloss denotes the heat exchanged with the surroundings. Under these assumptions, any additional heat sink reduces dT/dt and therefore biases the calorimetric power downward rather than upward. This limitation is well recognized in ultrasonic calorimetry, which assumes near‑adiabatic conditions and minimal external heat exchange. Departures from these assumptions generally lead to underestimation of acoustic power unless explicitly corrected. Order‑of‑magnitude considerations support this interpretation. Stainless steel has a thermal conductivity of approximately 15 W/m·K and a specific heat capacity of about 500 J/kg·K. For a nozzle with the reported dimensions, the mass of steel and the associated thermal capacity are small compared to 50–200 mL of water. The net effect is a slight decrease in the initial temperature slope and, consequently, a lower inferred calorimetric power. Ultrasound can also enhance local convective heat transfer near solid surfaces, further accelerating heat removal from the liquid and lowering the calorimetric estimate if uncorrected. Therefore, the higher calorimetric power observed with the nozzle configuration cannot be attributed to heat‑sink inflation. On the contrary, the presence of stainless steel makes the calorimetric estimate more conservative.

KI dosimetry

Fig. 3 illustrates the impact of ultrasonicated volume on triiodide production rate via KI dosimetry, comparing configurations with and without the focusing nozzle. In the unfocused configuration, the triiodide yield decreases significantly as the liquid volume increases. This behavior aligns with the phenomenon of decreasing ultrasound power density and cavitation intensity as liquid height and volume increase as shown in the previous section. At low volumes (50–75 mL), the high-power density results in vigorous cavitation and rapid iodide oxidation. However, as the volume increases, the same acoustic power is spread over a taller liquid column, reducing cavitation intensity and radical generation. This trend aligns with the findings of Merouani et al. [40], who revealed an almost linear decrease in the formation rate of triiodide with increasing volume in a 300 kHz sonoreactor. They attributed this decrease to lower energy dissipation per unit volume, greater heat loss from the vessel walls and transducers, and increased acoustic attenuation at greater liquid heights. Together, these factors explain the volume-dependent reduction in sonochemical efficiency.

Triiodide production rate as a function of ultrasonicated volume in the absence and presence of the focusing nozzle.

Introducing the focusing nozzle shifts the entire curve upward, significantly enhancing radical yields and, thus, the triiodide production rate across most of the tested volumes. The nozzle’s tapered geometry concentrates ultrasonic waves into a small focal zone, raising peak pressure amplitudes and intensifying bubble collapse. Focused ultrasound fields generate elevated peak negative pressures, facilitating bubble nucleation and amplifying collapse intensity. These focused collapses can overcome the lower collapse energies typically associated with MHz-range ultrasonication, which generally produces smaller microbubbles with short lifetimes and limited expansion ratios. Zhu et al. [51] demonstrated that areas of maximum sonochemical activity correspond to regions with the highest sonoluminescence, highlighting the connection between focused acoustic fields and increased reactive species production. In our experiments, triiodide production increased by factors of 2.08, 2.11, 1.72, 1.54, and 1.23 at liquid volumes of 75, 80, 85, 93.5, and 100 mL, respectively, compared to the unfocused reactor. The greatest improvement occurred at 80 mL, where the high-power density and focused field boosted iodide oxidation.

The results with the focusing nozzle peaks at 80 mL, corresponding to a liquid height of 11.3 cm. This matches the near-field (Fresnel zone) upper boundary of the ultrasonic transducer. Within this zone, constructive interference between direct and reflected pressure waves creates sustained, high-amplitude standing waves [52]. This constructive superposition promotes a uniform distribution of cavitation activity and maximizes local energy density. However, as the volume increases beyond the optimal height, the focused beam diverges progressively and interacts more extensively with the vessel walls. This reduces cavitation intensity and radical formation. The slope of the focused curve indicates that field focusing partially compensates for the decline in sonochemical efficiency at intermediate volumes. Though, the subsequent drop reflects the discrepancy between the nozzle design and the dimensions of the sonoreactor. This behavior aligns with that reported by Zhu et al. [51], who found that excessive liquid heights dilute power density and reduce reactive species yields, even with high electrical power inputs.

The enhancement effect was most pronounced when the ultrasonicated volume aligns with the limit of the Fresnel zone of the piezoelectric transducer. Liquid volumes that showed pronounced improvement (75–100 mL) correspond to liquid heights (10.6–14.2 cm) within or near the near field. There, constructive interference of pressure waves produces stable, high-amplitude standing waves. These coherent pressure peaks promote vigorous cavitation, maximizing radical generation. At 80 mL, the liquid height equals the near-field length (11.3 cm), fully encompassing the ultrasonicated volume within the optimal pressure zone [53]. This explains the sharp increase in triiodide production.

The convergence of the curves with and without the focusing nozzle at high volumes (≥ 125 mL) indicates that geometric focusing becomes ineffective when the ultrasonicated volume exceeds the nozzle's focal length. This underscores the importance of carefully matching reactor geometry to acoustic field characteristics. Aligning focal zones with the liquid column optimally is critical to achieving reproducible, efficient radical production [36,46].

These results highlight two key design considerations for the 1.7 MHz ultrasonic reactor. First, operating within a liquid volume (i.e., 80 mL for a 2 cm piezoelectric ceramic) that aligns the upper surface of the medium with the terminus of the Fresnel zone maximizes baseline cavitation yields. Second, integrating focusing nozzles is essential for sustaining high radical densities in intermediate volumes because acoustic energy rapidly decays in these areas with unfocused transducers.

H2O2 production

Fig. 4 shows the hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) formation rate as a function of the liquid volume subjected to ultrasonication, comparing sonoreactors with and without the focusing nozzle. In both cases, the H2O2 yield decreases as the volume of liquid ultrasonicated increases. This reflects the attenuation of acoustic intensity and cavitational activity over longer propagation paths. Beyond the near-field region of the transducer, pressure amplitudes decay as the reactor volume increases. Consequently, fewer bubbles reach the threshold necessary for implosive collapse to generate hydroxyl radicals and their recombination product, H2O2.

Hydrogen peroxide production rate as a function of ultrasonicated volume in the absence and presence of the focusing nozzle.

The focusing nozzle concentrates energy into a tighter, high-pressure zone near the focal point, thereby modifying the spatial distribution of the acoustic field. These zones promote the formation of additional cavitation bubbles and sustain their oscillations. Focused fields minimize lateral dispersion and destructive interference of acoustic waves. The localized enhancement of cavitation activity increases the homolytic cleavage of water molecules, forming hydroxyl radicals that recombine to yield hydrogen peroxide. This is evident from the consistently higher rates across all volumes. Compared to the unfocused reactor, H2O2 production increased by 16.0 %, 9.4 %, 54.4 %, 45.9 %, 29.3 %, 7.2 %, and 21.1 % at liquid volumes of 50, 70, 80, 85, 100, 150, and 200 mL, respectively. The pronounced peak in H2O2 generation at 80 mL indicates an optimal balance: there is enough liquid to sustain the cavitation bubble population, yet the nozzle's focusing effect still dominates due to sufficient confinement. As mentioned in the previous section (KI dosimetry), 80 mL corresponds to a liquid height of 11.3 cm, which is precisely the upper boundary of the ultrasonic transducer's near field, also known as the Fresnel zone. The focusing nozzle appears to have the greatest impact at the interface between the near and far fields. Unlike KI dosimetry, the enhancement effect of the focusing nozzle was more pronounced in the far-field region (> 80 mL) than in the near-field region (≤ 80 mL). A slight increase in the H2O2 production rate was observed at a liquid volume of 150 mL. This behavior is due to acoustic beam divergence and greater attenuation with distance. These factors limit the propagation of high-intensity regions into the upper parts of the liquid column. However, when the ultrasonicated volume increased to 200 mL, the H2O2 production rate was 21.1 % higher than that of the unfocused sonoreactor. The same behavior was observed in KI dosimetry.

Comparing Fig. 3 with the previously reported triiodide production data in Fig. 4 reveals significant differences in the underlying mechanisms. Triiodide yield primarily reflects the immediate reaction of hydroxyl radicals with iodide ions. However, hydrogen peroxide accumulation is more sensitive to multistep radical recombination processes that depend on bubble dynamics and mass transfer. Therefore, disruptions in cavitation conditions, such as an increase in liquid volume, suppress peroxide accumulation more than other dosimetry indicators do. This explains why the decline in H2O2 production with increasing volume appears steeper than the decline in iodide oxidation measurements.

These findings demonstrate that combining appropriate volume selection with acoustic focusing significantly offsets the limitations of 1.7 MHz cavitation intensity. The focusing nozzle extends the operating range of the reactor by maintaining sufficient localized energy density and radical flux as the volume increases. Together, these results support the conclusion that a 1.7 MHz ultrasonic reactor with a focusing nozzle can produce robust oxidative yields.

4-nitrophenol dosimetry

Fig. 5 illustrates 4-nitrocatechol (4-NC) production rate via 4-NP dosimetry as a function of ultrasonicated volume in the absence and presence of the focusing nozzle. The unfocused reactor shows a steady decrease in the formation rate of 4-NC as the ultrasonicated volume increases from 50 to 200 mL. At small volumes, the liquid remains entirely within the transducer’s near field. There, coherent standing waves cause vigorous bubble collapse and generate a high radical flux. However, as the volume increases, the acoustic beam attenuates more rapidly over the taller liquid column. This results in energy loss due to wall reflections and viscous damping. Consequently, the yield of 4-NC over time declines nearly monotonically, mirroring the classic observation that high-frequency cavitation becomes increasingly diffuse outside the near-field region. Additionally, the decrease in production rate as the volume increases aligns well with established sonochemical theory: as the liquid volume increases under constant power input, the acoustic energy density decreases, leading to fewer and less intense cavitation events per unit volume. As a result, the yield of reactive species needed to oxidize 4-NP into 4-NC diminishes.

4-nitrocatechol production rate as a function of ultrasonicated volume in the absence and presence of the focusing nozzle.

The use of the focusing nozzle lifts the entire profile by 20–55 %, with the greatest uplift at volumes in the range of 50–150. Similar to KI dosimetry, the focusing nozzle's enhancement effect is more pronounced in the far-field region than in the near-field region. As the liquid level rises, the nozzle funnels acoustic energy into a tighter focal zone, maintaining high local pressure amplitudes. This geometric concentration counteracts the natural divergence of the MHz beam, enabling more bubbles to experience the extreme pressures necessary for hydroxyl radical formation. The enhancement persists up to 150 mL. However, beyond this volume, the nozzle’s focal length no longer overlaps optimally with the liquid surface. This causes the production rate to taper off again. Unlike with KI dosimetry and H2O2 formation, enhancement was minimal at 200 mL. There are several reasons why the nozzle is less effective at this volume. These factors include attenuation and scattering of ultrasound waves over longer paths, partial dissipation of focused energy before reaching the bulk, and difficulty maintaining an effective standing wave or focal point in large, heterogeneous volumes.

Ultrasound in the MHz range produces small, short-lived cavitation bubbles. This results in a high repetition rate of mild collapses rather than a few violent events. The focusing nozzle creates zones where pressures exceed the threshold for efficient radical generation. This compensates for the lower collapse energy of these microbubbles.

Fricke dosimetry

Fig. 6 depicts Fe3+ production rate via Fricke dosimetry as a function of ultrasonicated volume in the absence and presence of the focusing nozzle. A distinct trend emerges across the tested ultrasonicated volumes: the Fe3+ production rate declines as the volume increases, both in the presence and absence of the focusing nozzle. This inverse relationship can be attributed to the decrease in acoustic energy density with higher solution volumes, which leads to lower cavitation bubble density and, consequently, diminished radical generation and Fe3+ production rate.

Fe3+ production rate as a function of ultrasonicated volume in the absence and presence of the focusing nozzle.

Crucially, across all volumes, the system equipped with the focusing nozzle exhibits a consistently higher Fe3+ production rate than the system without it. Fe3+ formation rate increased by 15.7 %, 39.4 %, 49.9 %, 29.8 %, 10.2 %, 20.8 %, and 0.6 %, respectively, compared to the unfocused sonoreactor at liquid volumes of 50, 70, 80, 85, 100, 150, and 200 mL. This enhancement highlights the ability of the focusing nozzle to concentrate acoustic energy spatially, increasing the local intensity of cavitational collapse events and boosting radical yields. The most significant increase (49.9 %) occurs at the near-field matching volume of 80 mL. The nozzle funnels acoustic waves into a narrower beam, producing constructive interference and maintaining high-pressure amplitudes deeper into the liquid column. Similar to 4-NP dosimetry, but unlike KI dosimetry and H2O2 formation, enhancement was negligible at 200 mL. However, at lower volumes, acoustic attenuation and scattering losses are minimal, allowing the nozzle to effectively converge ultrasonic waves and reinforce cavitation activity. At higher volumes (>100 mL), although the focusing nozzle continues to improve Fe3+ production, the relative enhancement diminishes. This is likely because the acoustic field becomes increasingly diffuse, and the energy losses from wave attenuation, diffraction, and absorption reduce the effectiveness of the focusing action.

The choice of 1.7 MHz is significant, as high-frequency ultrasound typically promotes a greater number of cavitation bubbles with smaller radii, but these bubbles collapse less violently, reducing the intensity of •OH formation compared to lower frequencies. In this context, the focusing nozzle compensates for the lower collapse energy by increasing local acoustic pressure and bubble density. The result is a substantial net increase in Fe3+ production, validating the nozzle’s role as an effective intensification strategy.

SSY sonodegradation

Fig. 7(a–c) shows SSY sonodegradation rate calculated after 2, 5 and 10 min as a function of ultrasonicated volume in the absence and presence of the focusing nozzle. The results reveal a clear trend: as volume increases, degradation rate decreases due to reduced energy density and cavitational activity. Specifically, the smallest tested volume (50 mL) produced the highest degradation rate. This finding highlights the critical role of acoustic power density in promoting radical formation and accelerating sonodegradation.

SSY sonodegradation rate as a function of ultrasonicated volume in the absence and presence of the focusing nozzle (a) 2 min, (b) 5 min and (c) 10 min.

Interestingly, the presence of the focusing nozzle always increases the degradation rate, regardless of volume. Focusing nozzle concentrate acoustic energy, increase cavitational hot spots, and thus promote radical production and SSY degradation. For the 2-min sonodegradation curve, the unfocused system exhibits a rapid decrease in the SSY degradation rate as the volume increases: from 0.551 µM/min at 50 mL to 0.110 µM/min at 200 mL. This steep decline reflects the loss of cavitational intensity as the ultrasonic beam is attenuated by the taller liquid column. Consequently, more energy is dissipated by wall reflections. When the focusing nozzle was engaged, the rate curve shifted upward across the entire volume range. The sonodegradation rate increased by 31.1 %, 35.0 %, 45.7 %, 2.7 %, 18.3 %, 28.5 %, and 63.6 %, respectively, compared to the unfocused reactor at liquid volumes of 50, 70, 80, 85, 100, 150, and 200 mL. The maximum enhancement was obtained at 80 and 200 mL, while the lowest enhancement was observed at 85 mL. The nozzle’s convergent geometry concentrates acoustic pressure maxima, dramatically boosting •OH generation and accelerating SSY breakdown.

Extending the computation of the sonodegradation rates to 5 min preserves the same overall trends, though notable kinetic smoothing occurs. In the unfocused reactor, the difference in rates between 50 mL and 200 mL decreases. This suggests that longer exposure times partially offset the lower instantaneous radical flux through cumulative bubble events. At liquid volumes of 50, 70, 80, 85, 100, 150, and 200 mL, however, the nozzle-enhanced curve outperforms the unfocused reactor by 7.5 %, 3.5 %, 53.9 %, 64.8 %, 62.9 %, 34.3 %, and 48.4 %, respectively.

For the sonodegradation rate curves determined after 10 min, the unfocused rate curve flattens. The focused reactor retains a clear advantage. Compared to the unfocused reactor, the sonodegradation rate increased by 6.0 %, 9.8 %, 27.4 %, 23.4 %, 9.1 %, 78.5 %, and 72.6 % at liquid volumes of 50, 70, 80, 85, 100, 150, and 200 mL, respectively. The greatest enhancement occurred at 150 and 200 mL and the least at 50, 70, and 100 mL.

The sonodegradation data of SSY (Fig. 7(a–c)) shows that the benefits of the focusing nozzle persist. This emphasizes the nozzle's effectiveness in sustaining cavitation activity and counteracting the decline in natural degradation efficiency over long periods of irradiation. However, longer irradiation appears to diminish the difference between systems with and without the focusing nozzle. This is likely because cumulative energy input compensates for initial differences in cavitational intensity. These trends can be explained by the principles of acoustic cavitation. At a high frequency of 1.7 MHz, the number of cavitation bubbles increases; however, their collapse intensity is lower than at lower frequencies. The focusing nozzle compensates for this by concentrating acoustic pressure and amplifying local cavitational effects, even during high-frequency operation. Overall, the data convincingly demonstrate that combining a focusing nozzle with optimized low-volume conditions maximizes the degradation rate of SSY through enhanced cavitation effects.

Although several indicators reach their maximum at 80 mL, assays integrating radical production over space and time demonstrate continued improvement at larger volumes. Fig. 4, Fig. 7 show that peroxide accumulation and SSY sonodegradation are notably higher with the focusing nozzle than without it, particularly at volumes ranging from 150 to 200 mL. This behavior is consistent with the coupled effects of geometric focusing at MHz frequencies. First, the nozzle launches a quasi-collimated, high-amplitude acoustic column that extends into the far field, sustaining a dense bubble cloud above the exit. Second, the focused beam drives acoustic streaming, which continually convects fresh liquid through the hot spot. This limits local scavenger buildup and supports bulk-phase reaction pathways. Consequently, performance losses due to increasing height are delayed for metrics governed by cumulative radical throughput and mixing. However, near-emitter dosimeters (e.g., KI, Fricke, and 4-NP) demonstrate smaller gains at 200 mL.

Together, Fig. 7(a–c) illustrate two design imperatives. First, align the working volume with the near-field length of the piezo disk to take advantage of standing-wave coherence. Second, incorporate a pressure-focusing nozzle to maintain high local cavitation intensity.

Sonochemiluminescence

Fig. 8 presents SCL images taken for four liquid volumes (50, 80, 100, and 150 mL) with and without the focusing nozzle. SCL is a photon emission phenomenon resulting from radical recombination during acoustic cavitation. SCL serves as a direct qualitative marker of cavitational activity, particularly that of transient cavitation bubbles undergoing violent collapse. Thus, the spatial and intensity distributions in the images can be interpreted as proxies for the location and strength of active cavitation zones.

SCL images obtained for various volumes in the absence and presence of the focusing nozzle.

In the unfocused configuration, SCL images exhibit a broad, spatially diffuse emission profile. At 50 mL, the SCL zone is concentrated near the transducer surface, showing minimal axial extension. This indicates that the cavitation is localized to the near-field region. As the volume increases from 80 to 150 mL, the cavitation zone appears to stretch axially. However, it becomes progressively weaker and more dispersed. This behavior reflects the ultrasonic field's natural divergence from a flat ceramic transducer, leading to energy dissipation and reduced acoustic intensity at greater distances from the source. The significant decrease in SCL brightness at higher volumes in the unfocused case may also indicate sub-threshold pressure amplitudes unable to sustain transient cavitation throughout the reactor volume.

In contrast, the focused configuration dramatically increases the intensity and confinement of the SCL signal. The cavitation zones are sharply defined and axially elongated across all volumes, with particularly pronounced focal points above the focusing nozzle. At 50 mL, the SCL image reveals a compact, nearly spherical luminescent region positioned directly above the nozzle's top. Images taken at 80, 100, and 150 mL clearly show narrow columns of intense luminescence extending into the liquid and ending in localized hot spots. These patterns suggest that the nozzle effectively concentrates acoustic energy into a confined focal region, thereby enhancing pressure amplitude and promoting stronger bubble collapse. This focusing effect arises from constructive interference and reduced beam divergence due to the geometry of the nozzle, which shapes the propagating wavefronts into a converging beam.

This focused cavitational activity corresponds to a more efficient conversion of electrical input energy into acoustic energy, which then converts to thermal and chemical energy. Calorimetric measurements confirm an increase in thermal power output and highlight the nozzle’s role in transferring electrical energy to the liquid medium. All tested dosimetries (i.e., KI oxidation, H2O2 formation, 4-NP degradation, and the Fricke reaction), as well as SSY sonodegradation and calorimetric characterization, have shown that focused ultrasonic fields result in higher energy densities and improved sonochemical yields compared to unfocused configurations. The presence of a focal hot spot in the SCL images indicates elevated local pressures that drive the formation of reactive radicals, such as •OH and H•. These radicals subsequently recombine to produce photon emission. These spatially localized emissions suggest that the focusing nozzle increases the intensity, spatial predictability, and reproducibility of cavitation zones.

Beyond intensity, the spatial rearrangement of SCL signals sheds light on bubble dynamics. Without the nozzle, multiple luminescent nodes appear at standing-wave antinodes, especially at intermediate volumes. The focusing nozzle suppresses these artifacts by converting the field into a traveling-wave-dominated regime, which results in a single, coherent bubble cloud.

SCL evidence provides a mechanistic explanation for the sustained effectiveness of the nozzle at higher volumes. The images reveal an axially confined, bright luminescent column that functions as an acoustic waveguide for cavitation. By suppressing multi‑node standing wave patterns, the nozzle maintains a traveling wave‑like focal region that preserves local pressure amplitudes and collapse intensity over an extended axial distance. In addition, renewal of the focal zone through acoustic streaming further stabilizes cavitation activity. This combined wave‑shaping effect accounts for the significant improvements in hydrogen peroxide formation and SSY degradation observed at volumes of 150–200 mL.

Experimental characterization provides strong evidence of enhanced cavitation activity with the focusing nozzle; however, numerical modeling offers an additional means of clarifying the underlying mechanisms. Finite element simulations of the acoustic field distribution can visualize pressure nodes, beam convergence, and energy density gradients within the reactor. Such simulations would complement calorimetric, dosimetric, and SCL results by disentangling the relative contributions of geometric focusing, wave interference, and attenuation. Previous studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of finite element modeling in predicting near‑ to far‑field transitions in high-frequency sonoreactors [22,23,28]. Incorporating this approach in future work would strengthen mechanistic interpretations and provide a predictive framework for optimizing nozzle geometry and reactor design.

Conclusions

This study has demonstrated that integrating a stainless-steel focusing nozzle with a 1.7 MHz bottom-mounted ceramic transducer significantly improved the sonochemical performance of various techniques and solution volumes. Calorimetric measurements consistently show increased thermal power, and KI, Fricke, and 4-NP dosimetries report higher rates of radical production. H2O2 yields and SSY degradation kinetics also improved. SCL imaging confirmed the presence of a sharply confined, high-intensity cavitation zone. Converging acoustic waves into a narrow focal zone increases local pressure amplitudes. Calorimetry showed elevated thermal power, and dosimetric assays (KI, Fricke, 4-NP, and H2O2) indicate up to twofold increases in radical yields. SSY degradation rates increased by up to 78 %, and SCL imaging revealed a brighter, more confined cavitation region, highlighting improvements in intensity and spatial reproducibility.

All metrics peak at 80 mL when the liquid height matches the upper boundary of the transducer's Fresnel zone. This maximizes constructive interference. Triiodide and Fe3+ production nearly doubled, and H2O2 formation increased by more than 50 %. Though the benefits gradually decreased beyond this focal depth, significant enhancements persist at 200 mL, demonstrating the nozzle’s resilience against diffraction and attenuation.

The sustained efficacy observed at 150–200 mL can be attributed to waveguiding and flow effects induced by the nozzle. The quasi‑collimated focal column, reinforced by acoustic streaming, maintains elevated local pressure amplitudes and effective throughput rates even beyond the Fresnel length. This extended confinement of energy is particularly advantageous for performance metrics that integrate reactivity over both space and time, such as hydrogen peroxide accumulation and dye degradation, where cumulative radical flux rather than instantaneous intensity governs the overall outcome.

The gains measured with the focusing nozzle are comparable to those reported for reflector‑assisted focusing. For example, the SSY degradation rate increased by up to 78.5 % at 150–200 mL, while calorimetric power rose by as much as 15 %. These improvements were achieved using a single compact insert rather than external reflectors or multi‑element arrays. Previous studies have shown that reflector performance is highly sensitive to geometry and placement within the near and far fields; conical reflectors can enhance cavitational activity, whereas cylindrical reflectors may suppress it [30]. Acoustic lenses and phased arrays are also capable of producing strong foci, but they introduce additional interfaces, alignment requirements, and system complexity. In contrast, the nozzle provides a monolithic, integration‑friendly MHz focusing method that delivers localized pressure amplification and reproducible gains, even at larger volumes.

These findings establish the focusing nozzle as an effective and practical design enhancement for high-frequency sonoreactors. It overcomes the limitations of MHz ultrasound, such as weaker cavitational collapse and rapid attenuation, by concentrating acoustic energy within the reactor. This approach is particularly effective for intermediate volumes, where standard reactors have poor energy density. These results provide new design guidelines for optimizing sonochemical reactors for use in pollutant degradation, advanced oxidation processes, and other applications requiring efficient radical generation in aqueous media.
