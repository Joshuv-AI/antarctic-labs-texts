A rapid resistance heating apparatus for thermal shock and fatigue testing of oxidation resistant coatings
Authors: Dongyang An, Zhao Liu, Shi Cheng, Jiaye Chen, Xianglin Zhang, Shuangshuang Cheng, Jingmin Dai, Peng Xiao, Zengwu Tang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe oxidation-resistant coating of aeronautic engines must withstand severe thermal fluctuations that can degrade performance over time. Conventional thermal shock and fatigue tests using external heaters suffer from high thermal inertia, complex setups, and poor adaptability at elevated temperatures. To address these challenges, we developed a thermal resistance tester based on ultra-high temperature rapid direct resistance heating. This method enables fast and precise temperature control without the need for external heaters, significantly improving system responsiveness and reliability. The apparatus covers a wide temperature range (500 °C to > 3000 °C), with temperature accuracy of ± 5 °C and weighing precision of ± 0.1 mg. It is suitable for evaluating oxidation-resistant coatings and metallic materials with symmetric structures, such as those fabricated via 3D-printed topology optimization. The tester’s performance has been validated through systematic thermal cycling experiments. In addition to coating research, the system shows strong potential in the thermal testing of optical materials and components, particularly in high-power laser and space applications, where material stability under rapid temperature variation is critical.Keywords: Coating, Apparatus, Thermal shock, Wide temperature range, Direct resistance heatingSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Refractory metals coated with oxidation-resistant materials are widely employed in the thrust chambers of aeronautic engines due to their ability to withstand extreme temperatures while maintaining structural integrity. The refractory metal ensures the mechanical performance of the component, while the oxidation-resistant coating prolongs its operational lifespan under harsh thermal conditions1–3. In actual aerospace applications, particularly during orbital operations, spacecraft are exposed to complex space environments. For example, when transitioning in and out of the Earth’s shadow, engines undergo rapid environmental temperature cycling, which is a key factor contributing to the degradation of oxidation-resistant coatings and poses a significant risk to the reliability and safety of spacecraft systems4–6.

To evaluate the durability of oxidation-resistant coatings under such conditions, ground-based thermal shock resistance testing has become an essential technique. This method simulates abrupt temperature variations experienced during operation and helps predict coating performance. As a result, thermal shock and thermal fatigue resistance testing of coatings has garnered significant attention in recent research7–9.

Traditional testing approaches typically rely on external heaters, which suffer from large thermal inertia and require long rebalancing times when the test conditions change. Moreover, achieving controlled boundary conditions necessitates extensive thermal insulation and protective setups, leading to complex equipment and prolonged testing durations10–12. These limitations become especially problematic at ultra-high temperatures (> 2000 °C), where heat transfer enhancement, chemical reaction-induced evaporation and diffusion, and the degradation of mechanical strength make accurate and repeatable measurements extremely challenging13,14.

To overcome these barriers, this study explores a rapid thermal shock testing technique based on direct resistance heating. In this method, a large current is directly passed through the test specimen, enabling it to reach ultra-high temperatures at extremely fast heating rates. This approach minimizes thermal inertia, simplifies system design, and significantly shortens testing time. A novel thermal shock/thermal fatigue resistance testing apparatus was developed based on the principle of direct electrified heating and heat transfer dynamics. The system is capable of reaching temperatures from 500 to over 3000 °C, depending on the material, with temperature control accuracy of ± 5 °C and weighing accuracy of ± 0.1 mg (uncertainty ± 0.2 mg). An optical pyrometer with a microsecond-level response time, integrated with a high-speed data acquisition system, enables precise control and monitoring during rapid heating cycles.

This apparatus eliminates the need for an external heating unit and enables straightforward establishment of the required thermal boundary conditions, making the test system highly stable and reliable for prolonged use. In addition to oxidation-resistant coatings, the device is applicable to metallic materials with symmetrical geometries, such as those fabricated through 3D printing and topology optimization techniques15.

Furthermore, this testing system offers significant potential for research and development in laser optical components and high-performance materials for laser processing. For instance, new materials synthesized specifically for laser cutting, drilling, or surface treatment often require comprehensive understanding of their thermal shock and fatigue responses over a wide temperature range. The proposed tester provides a valuable platform for assessing the thermal stability and durability of such materials under controlled ultra-high temperature cycling, thereby supporting material optimization for advanced laser-based manufacturing processes16–18.

Measuring principle and temperature control method

Measuring principle and method

Thermal shock or thermal fatigue resistance refers to the ability of an oxidation-resistant coating material to withstand rapid and extreme temperature fluctuations without causing structural damage. This property results from the combined influence of mechanical and thermal performance, which varies depending on the heating conditions. Thermal shock resistance testing is typically performed through cyclic oxidation, in which either the oxidation duration or the number of oxidation cycles is controlled and evaluated. During the test, the specimen may undergo natural cooling, forced air cooling, or water cooling, and the oxide scale is allowed to detach naturally. Two primary cyclic oxidation testing methods are commonly used: time-resolved and cycle-resolved approaches. The time-resolved method involves three stages: rapid heating, long-duration heat preservation, and cooling. And the cycle-resolved method consists of rapid heating, short-duration heat preservation, and cooling. Thermal shock resistance of materials is typically evaluated by mass loss measurement or microstructural and compositional characterization after the test.

Temperature control method

In this study, a thermal cycling test was conducted using a direct electrification and rapid heating method for both the heating and holding phases, while natural cooling was employed during the cooling phase. Each cycle consisted of three distinct stages: rapid heating, isothermal holding, and cooling. During the rapid heating stage, the specimen was quickly heated to the target temperature by adjusting the system’s input power. In the isothermal holding stage, the temperature was maintained by fine-tuning the power input, ensuring the fluctuation remained within the acceptable error range. Finally, in the cooling stage, the specimen was allowed to cool naturally. Because the specimen remained clamped between water-cooled brass electrodes throughout the process, it experienced a rapid temperature drop once the heating was stopped. Therefore, natural cooling was deemed sufficient and effective in this testing approach.

Accurate temperature control is a critical challenge during the rapid heating and isothermal holding stages when using the direct electrification heating method. This difficulty arises from the nonlinear, time-varying, and hysteretic behavior of the specimen’s temperature response during heating. If the input power adjustment interval is too short or the adjustment amplitude is too large, the specimen temperature tends to oscillate and becomes unstable. Conversely, if the adjustment interval is too long or the amplitude is too small, the heating rate becomes insufficient, resulting in a prolonged time to reach the target temperature. Therefore, both the adjustment interval and amplitude of the input power are critical parameters that must be carefully tuned to ensure both rapid heating and stable temperature maintenance. To address this issue and fulfill the requirements of fast temperature rise and accurate control in the direct resistance heating process, this study proposes a piecewise temperature control strategy, developed through extensive experiments and analysis. Apparatus description

Testing platform

A thermal shock resistance testing apparatus for oxidation-resistant coatings was developed based on the principle of heat transfer, utilizing an ultra-high temperature rapid direct resistance heating method, as shown in Fig. 3. This apparatus enables thermal shock resistance evaluation across a wide temperature range (500 °C to 3000 °C). The system primarily comprises a test bench, a thermal shock test unit, a fiber-optic pyrometer, and a control system. The thermal shock test unit is mounted on the test bench, while real-time temperature measurements of the specimen are acquired using a fiber-optic pyrometer. Weight measurements of the specimens are performed outside the test chamber. Overview of test apparatus.

The core requirement in the electrode design is that it must securely hold the specimen while accommodating internal channels for water cooling. Each electrode is sized at 60 × 45 × 30 mm, with the upper and lower electrodes supported and powered independently via guide posts. To ensure both high electrical conductivity and mechanical strength, brass is selected as the material for both the electrodes and guide posts. The structural design of the electrodes and guide posts is shown in Fig. 4a. The upper water-cooled electrode is mounted on the guide post using bolts, while the lower electrode is affixed to a height-adjustable platform. Both electrodes are connected to the guide posts through a flexible drag chain, enabling stable electrical connection. Power is delivered to the electrodes via the guide posts, which are linked to an external power supply. The distance between the upper and lower electrodes can be adjusted by repositioning the height-adjustment plate along a threaded rod. Each electrode is hollow to allow internal routing of the cooling water pipes. The actual physical layout of the electrode is shown in Fig. 4b.

Air drying system and water cooling system

The air-drying system supplies dry air for the thermal shock resistance test. Introducing dry air into the test chamber helps to remove potentially harmful gases generated by the high-temperature heating of test specimens, thereby ensuring the safety of personnel during the experiment. The schematic diagram of the air-drying system is shown in Fig. 5.

Air drying system’s flowchart diagram.

The water cooling system plays a critical role in protecting the electrodes and ensuring the overall safety of the experiment. It comprises a 1 m3 water tank, a refrigeration unit, a feedwater pump, and a purification filter. The system’s schematic diagram is shown in Fig. 6. During operation, cooling water is pumped by the feedwater pump into the test equipment to absorb heat. The return water is then passed through a purification filter before flowing back into the water tank, where it is cooled by the refrigeration unit. The refrigerated water, maintained at a temperature not exceeding 25 °C, is recirculated into the system to ensure effective thermal management. To prevent degradation in cooling efficiency and to extend the service life of the cooled components, both the circulating and makeup water are continuously purified by the filtration system. This process eliminates potential contaminants that could otherwise reduce the refrigeration performance of the system.

Control system and software

The thermal shock resistance tester for oxidation-resistant coatings developed in this study features a control system composed of an industrial control computer, a measurement and control unit, a temperature sensing unit, and a power output module, forming a closed-loop temperature control circuit. Thermal shock test signals, including chamber temperature and specimen temperature, are transmitted to the industrial control computer for real-time display, data logging, and analysis. Meanwhile, the control cabinet sends temperature control signals via cables to the corresponding components of the thermal shock test system. The test process can be automatically regulated using a PID temperature controller or manually adjusted through a knob located on the control cabinet.

The PID temperature controller is connected to the industrial control computer via an RS232 industrial serial bus. The temperature of each test specimen is continuously measured in real time by a fiber-optic pyrometer. The temperature signal is then converted into a 4–20 mA analog signal through digital-to-analog (D/A) conversion and transmitted to the input terminal of the PID controller for display. Based on the measured signal, the PID controller outputs a control signal to a thyristor module. This module is composed of two antiparallel KP2000A/1600 V high-power silicon-controlled rectifiers (SCRs), an air-cooled radiator, and an RC (resistor–capacitor) snubber circuit. By adjusting the conduction angle of the SCRs, the thyristor module modulates the output power of the power supply unit. The flowchart diagram of the control system is shown in Fig. 7.

control system’s flowchart diagram.

The LabVIEW-based thermal shock test system enables fully automated control of the thermal shock testing process. The program is capable of reading the real-time temperature displayed on the digital PID temperature controller, as well as setting key parameters such as target heating temperature, control accuracy, heating rate, number of cycles, holding time, and cooling duration. Alternatively, users can opt for manual control by adjusting the knob on the control cabinet while monitoring the temperature displayed on the PID controller.

Experimental results and uncertainty analysis

During the experiment, each test specimen was clamped between brass electrodes and subjected to thermal cycling at a preset test temperature. The specimen temperature was monitored in real time using a fiber-optic pyrometer. The power output was dynamically adjusted to ensure the specimen reached the target temperature within a specified time frame, followed by a temperature holding phase and subsequent cooling. The mass of each specimen was measured outside the test chamber using an FA2204 analytical balance with a readability of 0.1 mg. In the time-resolved cyclic oxidation test, the specimen was weighed after each thermal exposure cycle. In the cycle-resolved cyclic oxidation test, the specimen was weighed periodically after a specified number of thermal cycles. In both cases, the specimen was temporarily removed from the chamber for weighing and then re-mounted for continued testing.

The specimens were niobium-hafnium (Nb-Hf) alloys coated with a silicide (Si-Cr-Ti) layer. The coating was applied via spraying and vacuum sintering. Each specimen measured 70 mm in length, 10 mm in width, and 1 mm in thickness. The brass clamping surface was 70 mm × 10 mm, while the exposed (non-clamped) part of the electrode measured 45 mm × 10 mm. Three specimens, all prepared under the same conditions, were subjected to different tests: a time-resolved cyclic oxidation test, a cycle-resolved cyclic oxidation test, and a combined time– and cycle–resolved cyclic oxidation test.

Time-resolved cyclic oxidation test

During the time-resolved cyclic oxidation test at 1700 °C, the specimen was weighed at 0 h, 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h, and 5 h, with the recorded weights being 7.2888 g, 7.2972 g, 7.3043 g, 7.3061 g, 7.3042 g, and 7.3008 g, respectively. As shown in Fig. 8, the specimen’s weight changed over time, initially increasing and then gradually decreasing. The surface area of the specimen was 4.5 × 10⁻4 m2. Over the 0–5 h interval, the weight per unit area changed by 26.7 g/m2, corresponding to a rate of change of 5.3 g/(m2·h).

Relationship between sample weight and constant temperature test time at 1700 °C.

This behavior can be explained as follows: In the initial stage of the time-resolved cyclic oxidation test, the coating surface material reacts with oxygen through oxidation accompanied by diffusion, with oxidation being the dominant process. This leads to a rapid increase in the specimen’s weight. After a certain duration, however, diffusion becomes the controlling factor due to the continuous growth of the oxide layer on the coating surface, which lengthens the oxygen diffusion path. As a result, the weight gain slows down. Over time, as the oxide layer fully develops and considering the mismatch in thermal expansion coefficients between the oxide and the metal substrate, cracks and spalling occur on the coating surface, causing a gradual loss in specimen weight.

Cycle-resolved cyclic oxidation test

The cycle-resolved cyclic oxidation test was performed at 1700 °C for 50 cycles. Each thermal cycle consisted of three stages: rapid heating, isothermal holding, and natural cooling. In this test, the heating phase lasted approximately 30 s, followed by a holding time of 25 s at 1700 °C, and then natural cooling, which took approximately 25 s. This resulted in an average total cycle duration of about 80 s. The specimen was weighed at Cycle 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50, with the recorded weights being 7.3897 g, 7.4065 g, 7.4102 g, 7.4109 g, 7.4117 g, and 7.4122 g, respectively. As illustrated in Fig. 9, the specimen’s weight increases with the number of cycles, showing a rapid initial rise followed by a slower rate of increase. The specimen has a surface area of 4.5 × 10⁻4 mm2, with the weight per unit area increasing by 50 g/m2 over 50 cycles, corresponding to an average rate of change of 1 g/(m2·cycle) during the test period.

Relationship between sample weight and thermal shock cycle number at 500 ~ 1700 °C.

At the beginning of the cycle-resolved thermal cycling test at 1700℃, the continuous accumulation of oxide on the coating surface, driven by chemical reactions between the surface material and oxygen, led to a significant increase in the specimen’s weight. As the test progressed and the oxide layer thickened, oxygen diffusion became the dominant factor influencing weight changes. Meanwhile, the formation rate of new oxides slowed down, and slight spalling of the specimen surface occurred.

Time-resolved and cycle-resolved cyclic oxidation tests

In the time-resolved cyclic oxidation test conducted at 1800 °C, the specimen’s weight was measured at 0 h, 0.5 h, 1 h, 1.5 h, 2 h, and 2.5 h, yielding values of 7.3958 g, 7.4056 g, 7.4106 g, 7.4135 g, 7.4159 g, and 7.4181 g, respectively. Based on the fitted weight-versus-time curve shown in Fig. 10a, with a specimen surface area of 4.5 × 10⁻4 mm2, the weight change per unit area over 0–2.5 h was 49.6 g/m2, corresponding to a weight change rate of 19.8 g/(m2·h). In the cycle-resolved cyclic oxidation test of the coating at 1300 °C, the specimen weights at Cycle 0, 50, 70, 90, 110, and 130 were 7.4181 g, 7.4086 g, 7.4044 g, 7.4010 g, 7.3974 g, and 7.3947 g, respectively. According to the fitted curve shown in Fig. 10b, the weight change per unit area from Cycle 0 to Cycle 130 was 52 g/m2, with a weight change rate of 0.4 g/(m2·cycle).

Relationship between sample weight and constant temperature test time, thermal shock cycle number: (a) constant temperature at 1800 °C, (b)thermal shock temperature at 500 ~ 1300 °C.

In the time-resolved thermal cycling oxidation test at 1800 °C, the specimen’s weight continuously increased. This is attributed to the high test temperature, which facilitated the surface material’s reaction with oxygen and accelerated oxygen diffusion, despite the continuous spalling of oxidation products from the specimen surface. Since the oxidation reaction dominated the process, the specimen’s weight showed an overall upward trend. Conversely, in the cycle-resolved thermal cycling test at 1300 °C, the specimen’s weight exhibited a decreasing trend. From the previous test, the oxide layer on the specimen surface had thickened to some extent. With ongoing accumulation of thermal stress both on the surface and within the specimen, the thermal stability of the coating deteriorated. This led to continuous cracking of the surface oxide layer and large-scale spalling, ultimately resulting in a sustained loss of specimen weight.

Based on the developed thermal shock resistance test device, this study conducted time-resolved and cycle-resolved thermal cycling tests to investigate the relationship between specimen weight and the duration and number of oxidation cycles. In addition, the surface morphology of specimens after testing was examined. The results align closely with findings from the Institute for Advanced Ceramics at Harbin Institute of Technology and the Materials Architecturing Research Center 20, as well as the Korea Institute of Science and Technology 21. The conclusions indicate that at relatively high temperatures, the specimen’s weight initially shows an increasing trend. However, with prolonged exposure time and an increased number of oxidation cycles, the specimen weight begins to decrease. Initially, the specimen surface is relatively smooth, featuring some sintered particles and micro-pores. After a certain test duration, the surface oxide layer becomes dense and uniform but develops holes and cracks. With further cycling, pronounced cracking and spalling occur on the specimen surface.

(2) Environmental factors include temperature, humidity, atmospheric pressure, air buoyancy, gravitational acceleration, electromagnetic interference, airflow, and vibrations. Conclusion

In this study, a thermal shock and thermal fatigue resistance tester for oxidation-resistant coatings was developed using rapid direct resistance heating, capable of operating from 500 to over 3000 °C with temperature control accuracy of ± 5 °C and weight measurement accuracy of ± 0.1 mg. The system achieves precise, stable, and long-term reliable testing without the need for external heaters, as verified by experimental validation. This tester enables simulation of rapid thermal cycling at ultra-high temperatures, making it suitable for investigating oxidation coatings’ performance under harsh conditions. It is particularly useful for assessing materials used in laser optical components exposed to significant temperature fluctuations, such as those in high-power lasers or space laser communication. The system helps identify materials with excellent thermal stability and shock resistance, ensuring optical components maintain key properties like transmittance and reflectance during prolonged operation.

By replicating realistic thermal shock and fatigue scenarios, the device provides crucial data for optimizing coatings and component designs, which can improve engine performance and extend service life, as well as enhance the reliability and longevity of laser systems under varying thermal stresses.
