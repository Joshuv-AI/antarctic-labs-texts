Beyond single encounters: a mixed methods study of access to continuous asthma care using Levesque’s framework
Authors: Irem Sevik, Meltem Ciceklioglu, Gulistan Karadeniz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAchieving symptom control in asthma depends on regular follow-up and access to continuous care, and inadequate control is associated with reduced quality of life and increased healthcare utilization. This study aimed to examine access to continuous care and associated factors among adult asthma patients attending tertiary care, and to explore the dynamics of continuity through patient experiences.MethodsThis is an explanatory sequential mixed-methods study within a critical realist paradigm, guided by Levesque’s framework. The cross-sectional phase involved 312 participants and data were analyzed using SPSS 30.0. Chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, Student’s t-test, and univariate and multivariate logistic regression were conducted. In the qualitative phase, semi-structured in-depth interviews were conducted with 12 participants selected through maximum variation sampling. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim, coded using MAXQDA 24, and analyzed through a critical grounded theory approach.ResultsThe mean age was 49.62 ± 14.54 years; 72.8% were female, and 17.3% were uninsured. Only 29.2% of participants had access to continuous care. The strongest determinant of continuity was receiving clear explanations about asthma (OR = 3.30). Individuals with well-controlled asthma were more likely to access continuous care than those with uncontrolled asthma (OR = 2.67). Patients reported delayed diagnoses and insufficient involvement of family physicians in asthma management. 28.2% of family physicians were unaware of the asthma diagnosis. Individuals reporting no difficulty in securing appointments were paradoxically more likely to lack continuous care (OR = 1.89). Qualitative findings suggest that difficulties in obtaining appointments with previously consulted physicians may underlie this paradox. Multimorbid patients reported significant care fragmentation due to poor coordination between institutions. Insurance status was not significantly associated, but participants reported a healthcare-related financial burden. Trust in physicians, care quality, and stigma emerged as relational factors associated with access to continuous care. Access to care was experienced as a cyclical and context-dependent process shaped by recurring barriers and facilitators and findings informed a conceptual refinement of Levesque’s framework.ConclusionsAccess to continuous care among asthma patients is limited. Strengthening diagnosis, follow-up, and referral processes in primary care, establishing coordination mechanisms between institutions, and expanding guideline-based practices in asthma management are necessary.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12890-026-04516-4.Keywords: Asthma, Access to healthcare, Continuity of care, Tertiary care, Quality of care

Background

There are approximately 260 million asthma patients worldwide, and this number is projected to reach 275 million by 2050 [1]. Asthma contributes to approximately one-fifth of the years of life lost due to chronic respiratory diseases [2, 3]. In Türkiye, asthma prevalence has been reported as 8.1% [4]. Poor asthma control is associated with adverse outcomes, including reduced quality of life, increased healthcare utilization, and elevated treatment costs [5–9]. Due to its high prevalence and burden, asthma constitutes a public health concern in Türkiye [10].

Asthma requires continuity of care (CoC) to achieve effective disease control [10, 11] and continuity of asthma care is associated with lower rates of premature mortality, fewer hospitalizations and emergency use, and lower healthcare costs [12]. Moreover, physician continuity improves self-management behaviors [13]. However, continuity of asthma care is lower compared with diabetes, COPD, and heart failure [14].

Continuity of care has evolved from a concept primarily focused on repeated contact with the same physician into a multidimensional framework encompassing relational, informational, and management dimensions across healthcare settings [15]. Haggerty et al. define continuity as the extent to which healthcare is experienced as coherent, connected, and consistent with patients’ needs over time [15]. This perspective conceptualizes continuity not only in terms of healthcare organization and provider arrangements, but also through patients’ experiences of navigating and engaging with care processes over time. Such an understanding is conceptually compatible with Levesque et al.’s framework of healthcare access, which approaches healthcare access as a dynamic interaction between healthcare systems and individuals’ abilities to perceive, seek, reach, utilize and engage with care [16]. Within chronic disease management, continuity involves not only regular follow-up and sustained patient–provider relationships, but also coordination across services and patients’ ongoing engagement with care processes. In asthma care, these dimensions are particularly important due to the need for long-term monitoring, self-management, and adaptation of care according to changing disease control and healthcare needs.

Continuity is dependent on access, yet asthma patients encounter barriers at multiple stages of healthcare access. These barriers include the underrecognition of symptoms, long waiting times, cost, and distance [17–19]. Asthma-related burden and CoC vary by age, gender, race and socioeconomic status, often resulting in delayed and/or inadequate care among underserved populations [20, 21]. Older age, higher education, and residence in large urban areas are associated with better CoC [12, 22, 23]. When continuity is not ensured, asthma exacerbations and exacerbation-related healthcare utilization occur more frequently in lower socioeconomic groups [21]. Socioeconomic position, place of residence, and race shape access to continuous and specialized asthma care, resulting in persistent unmet health needs regardless of insurance [24, 25]. Preventable asthma-related hospitalizations are observed more frequently among migrant patients [26].

In Türkiye, asthma care is primarily delivered through primary care and specialist outpatient clinics, particularly pulmonology and allergy clinics in secondary and tertiary healthcare institutions [10]. National asthma management practices are guided by national and international guideline recommendations emphasizing regular follow-up and long-term disease control [10]. Universal health insurance coverage provides broad access to asthma-related healthcare service [27]; however, coordination and continuity across providers may remain challenging in practice. In Türkiye, asthma patients experience barriers related to appointment availability and cost [27]. Cost-related unmet health needs are significantly more common among asthma patients [27].

The 78th World Health Assembly (2025) marked the first formal recognition of the importance of lung diseases and the need for a holistic approach to their prevention, calling for a stronger action across prevention, diagnosis, and treatment [28]. Despite strong evidence supporting continuity of asthma care, little is known about how adults with asthma in Türkiye access continuous care and how individual and structural factors shape this process.

Methods

This study aims to examine continuity of care among adult asthma patients attending outpatient pulmonary services, to evaluate prevalence of the continuity of care and associated factors and to explore how these factors shape continuity of care, through patients’ experiences. This is an explanatory sequential mixed methods research study, grounded in critical realist paradigm and informed by Levesque et al.’s framework [16, 29–31]. Within critical realism, healthcare access is conceptualized not as a fixed or directly observable phenomenon, but as a process shaped through the interaction of structural conditions, healthcare organization, and individuals’ capacities and experiences. From this perspective, observed patterns of healthcare utilization and continuity may reflect underlying social and organizational mechanisms that are not immediately visible at the empirical level. This approach was considered particularly relevant for examining continuity of asthma care, as continuity is shaped not only by healthcare availability, but also by how individuals perceive health needs, navigate healthcare systems, sustain engagement with care, and experience relationships with providers over time.

Levesque et al.’s framework was adopted because it conceptualizes access as a dynamic and relational process emerging through the interaction between healthcare system characteristics and individuals’ abilities to perceive, seek, reach, utilize, and engage with care [16]. Consistent with a critical realist perspective, the explanatory sequential mixed-methods design enabled us not only to identify statistical patterns associated with continuity of care but also to explore the contextual and relational mechanisms that may underlie these patterns through qualitative inquiry.

Quantitative phase

A cross-sectional study was conducted in the outpatient pulmonary clinics of a single public chest diseases hospital in Izmir. Hospital records show approximately 1,800 adult asthma patients had attended the pulmonary clinics within the previous year. Sample size was calculated using a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error. As no national data were available regarding follow-up attendance among asthma patients in Türkiye, the prevalence parameter was conservatively assumed to be 50%, resulting in a minimum required sample size of 317 participants. Inclusion criteria included being aged 18 years or older, having an asthma diagnosis for at least one year, and residing in Izmir. Participants were initially identified through self-report, and asthma diagnoses were subsequently verified through the electronic medical records. Data were collected between May and September 2024 during outpatient clinic hours in the hospital. The questionnaires were researcher-administered by the principal researcher, a public health physician. 312 patients completed the survey.

Conceptualization of continuity was informed by Haggerty et al.’s patient- and needs-oriented framework, which conceptualizes continuity through relational, informational, and management dimensions [15]. In the present study, access to continuous asthma care was operationalized through relational and management continuity, reflecting both the presence of an ongoing patient–provider relationship and adherence to guideline-recommended follow-up care. Relational continuity was defined as having a regular physician for asthma-related consultation. Management continuity was operationalized based on follow-up need, determined according to national asthma guidelines and varying by time since diagnosis [10]. Participants diagnosed within the previous two years were required to have attended at least two scheduled follow-up visits in the past year, whereas those diagnosed for two years or longer were required to have attended at least one such visit [10]. Participants meeting criteria for both relational and management continuity were classified as having access to continuous care.

Quantitative phase employed a five-domain approach to determinants of continuity, including: (1) sociodemographic and economic characteristics, (2) general health status, (3) asthma-related characteristics, (4) psychosocial factors, and (5) healthcare access and utilization. The data collection tool used in the quantitative phase was a structured questionnaire organized around these five domains, composed of both previously validated instruments and study-specific items developed by the research team based on the study objectives and relevant literature. Sociodemographic and economic characteristics included age, sex, education, marital status, employment, perceived income, and health insurance. Employment was operationalized reflecting both labor market participation and time availability for healthcare use. General health status included self-rated health, multimorbidity, regularly used non-asthma medications, and smoking. Asthma-related characteristics comprised asthma control level assessed using the Asthma Control Test (ACT), asthma exacerbations and hospitalizations in the previous year, family history of asthma, features of the diagnostic trajectory (time since diagnosis, type of first contacted institution and diagnosing institution) and family physician awareness of the diagnosis. The Turkish version of ACT, a validated five-item instrument, was used (Cronbach alpha: 0.84) [32, 33]. Total scores range from 5 to 25 and are categorized as uncontrolled asthma (5–15), partially controlled asthma (16–19), and well-controlled asthma (≥ 20) [32, 33]. Psychosocial factors were assessed using the Turkish version of Chronic Illness Anticipated Stigma Scale (CIASS), which measures anticipated stigma related to living with a chronic illness across three domains (friends and family, work colleagues, and healthcare workers) (Cronbach alpha: 0.85) [34, 35]. Items are rated on a five-point Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater anticipated stigma [34, 35]. Subscale scores were calculated as the mean of item responses within each domain, and a total stigma score was computed as the mean of all items [34, 35]. Healthcare access and utilization included the types of institutions used for asthma-related services, perceived availability of services, perceived appropriateness of care, and indicators of interpersonal continuity.

Service utilization was assessed by asking participants which institutions they most frequently used for asthma-related services, including pulmonary function testing, imaging, skin prick testing, prescription services, allergy immunotherapy, and emergency care. Responses were categorized according to whether services were received from the identified source of continuous care, multiple providers, other institutions or “not received”.

Perceived availability of services was evaluated using Likert-type items assessing ease of physical access to healthcare facilities and the ability to obtain appointments without long waiting times. Responses were rated on a five-point scale ranging from “never” to “always” and were dichotomized for analysis. Participants who responded “never” or “rarely” were categorized as having difficulty accessing care, whereas those who responded “sometimes”, “usually”, or “always” were categorized as having easy access to care.

Perceived appropriateness of care was assessed through Likert-type items evaluating the adequacy of consultation time and access to clear and understandable explanations regarding disease and treatment. These items were similarly measured on five-point scales and recategorized into binary variables.

Interpersonal continuity was assessed among participants reporting a regular physician. This domain included duration of the patient–physician relationship (measured as a continuous variable) and Likert-type items evaluating the physician’s knowledge of the patient’s medical history, daily life, and socioeconomic context, as well as the patient’s comfort in asking questions and communicating openly. These items were rated on five-point agreement scales and dichotomized for analysis.

SPSS 30.0 was used. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Chi-square, Fisher’s exact tests, independent samples t-tests and logistic regression were used. Candidate variables for the multivariable model were identified through univariate logistic regression analyses. Consistent with commonly recommended modelling strategies, a p-value threshold of < 0.25 was used to identify candidate variables for multivariable analysis [36–39]. In addition to statistical criteria, conceptual relevance within the study framework was considered when selecting variables. Consideration was also given to maintaining an appropriate events-per-variable ratio [40, 41]. All variables retained after univariate screening and conceptual assessment were entered as covariates into the multivariable logistic regression model. Backward LR elimination was then applied to obtain the final model. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), and the assumption of linearity between continuous predictors and the logit was evaluated using the Box–Tidwell test. Model explanatory power was assessed using Nagelkerke’s R², while discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Overall model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Qualitative phase

Participants, who consented to follow-up contact, were selected from the quantitative phase. Following completion of the quantitative phase, maximum variation sampling was used to purposively select participants for the qualitative phase. The dimensions of variation were determined prior to qualitative recruitment based on the quantitative findings, including unexpected results that warranted further exploration, together with Levesque et al.‘s conceptual framework on healthcare access. Participants were intentionally selected to ensure variation in access to continuous care, asthma control level, sex, age, education, employment status, perceived income, and insurance status. These dimensions were chosen to capture diverse experiences of continuity of care and to facilitate explanation of the quantitative findings. (Supplement 1). The sex distribution reflected that of the quantitative phase.

Data were collected through semi-structured in-depth interviews. Twelve participants were interviewed in January and February 2025. Interviews were conducted in-person or online (2 interviews) at times convenient for participants, lasted approximately 30 min, and were audio-recorded. Sample size was considered adequate to achieve analytical depth in critical grounded theory–informed studies [42]. Interviews continued until the research team considered that sufficient depth and diversity of experiences had been captured to address the research question and explain the quantitative findings.

An initial interview guide was developed based on the aim and relevant literature. Following quantitative analysis, the interview guide was iteratively refined to deepen inquiry into findings requiring further explanation. The final interview guide consisted of six open-ended questions with probes and was informed by the Levesque et al.’s framework (Supplement 2) [16].

Analysis was conducted in line with the critical grounded theory. Classical grounded theory, developed by Glaser & Strauss, proposes a data-driven and inductive analytic process, emphasizing the development of theory through systematic analysis while minimizing the influence of a priori assumptions [43, 44]. The critical grounded theory (CrGT) preserves the data-driven and process-oriented nature of grounded theory while simultaneously seeking to make visible the interaction between social structures and individual experiences [43–45]. CrGT also moves beyond the rigid inductive logic of classical grounded theory by incorporating additional forms of reasoning, including abduction [46]. Abduction refers to an iterative process in which the researcher develops possible explanations from the data, compares these explanations with existing theoretical frameworks, and then returns to the data to refine interpretations through an ongoing dialogue between empirical observations and theory. Consistent with a critical realist perspective, this approach aims not only to describe what is happening, but also to explore why and how these phenomena emerge in relation to underlying structures and mechanisms [43–45]. Accordingly, participants’ experiences of access to healthcare were not examined solely at a descriptive level. Theoretical explanations developed from the data were not based exclusively on inductive reasoning; rather, they were constructed through abductive and, where appropriate, retroductive inferences informed by the prior literature and the study’s conceptual framework.

All interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed using automated transcription software (Transkriptor), and later reviewed by the researcher. The analysis was based on 179 pages of transcripts. MAXQDA 24 was used. Interviewer researcher, who had the first-hand familiarity with the data, initiated the coding and 63 open codes were developed. Coded materials were regularly shared with the supervisor, who provided critical input. When differences in interpretation arose, the data were re-examined in relation to the Levesque et al.’s framework [16]. Four overarching themes emerged (Supplement 3).

Integration

Quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated in a staged and iterative manner. The sampling and iterative refinement of the interview guide contributed to integration between the quantitative and qualitative phases. All findings were re-examined in relation to Levesque et al.’s framework [16] through an abductive and iterative reasoning process (Fig. 1). Abductive reasoning involved an iterative movement between empirical findings and theoretical explanations, whereby emerging interpretations were repeatedly compared with the framework and refined in light of the data. Consistent with the study’s critical realist orientation, the framework was not treated as a fixed analytical template, but was reconsidered in light of the study findings to explore how structural, contextual, and relational factors shaped access to continuous asthma care.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ege University Medical Research Ethics Committee (Decision No: 24-3.1T/58) Authorization was granted by the Izmir Provincial Directorate of Health under the Scientific Research Protocol framework. Written informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants.

Results

Quantitative and qualitative findings are presented in an integrated manner in relation to Levesque et al.’s dimensions of healthcare access: approachability and the ability to perceive, acceptability and the ability to seek, availability and accommodation and the ability to reach, affordability and the ability to pay, and appropriateness and the ability to engage. Consistent with the explanatory sequential mixed-methods design and the abductive integration process, findings were organized across interacting dimensions of access rather than presented as separate quantitative and qualitative phases. Building on this integrated analysis, the findings were used to extend Levesque et al.‘s framework and develop a conceptual model of continuity of asthma care for adults with asthma, illustrating how the interacting dimensions of healthcare access operate within the study context.

More than one-third of participants (37.8%) had completed primary education and 34.3% were unemployed (Supplement 4). Access to continuous care and its dimensions

Overall, 29.2% (n = 91) were classified as having access to continuous care, meeting the predefined criteria for both relational and management continuity. The majority of participants (90.4%, n = 282) reported having a regular healthcare institution for asthma-related care, whereas 48.7% (n = 151) reported continuity with the same physician. Most participants with a regular source identified tertiary care hospitals (university hospitals and training and research hospitals / specialized referral hospitals) as their regular source (84.7%), whereas family health centers (FHC) were rarely reported (2.1%) (Supplement 4). Participants frequently distinguished between institutional access and relational continuity with a specific physician. Several participants described repeated encounters with different physicians within the same institution, which limited the development of an ongoing therapeutic relationship despite regular service use.

“Being under regular follow-up by the same specialist would be much better. But every time we see a different doctor, therefore we have to explain our medical history all over again.” (Participant with access to continuous care).

Management continuity was markedly lower among recently diagnosed participants, with only 4.8% meeting the criteria compared with 41.0% among those diagnosed for two years or longer.

Follow-up visits were widely perceived as important for monitoring disease progression and treatment effectiveness.

“Follow-up is important, at least for keeping track of how the disease is progressing and what is actually going on.” (Participant without access to continuous care).

Despite this perceived importance, participants also described difficulties in maintaining regular follow-up.

Health care needs, approachability and ability to perceive

More than half of the participants rated their health as moderate (51.3%), while 27.3% reported poor health. Multimorbidity was common (67.6%) and 40.1% of participants reported current smoking (Supplement 4). The mean number of regularly used non-asthma medications, included as an indicator of multimorbidity and overall treatment burden, was 1.73 ± 2.69 (range: 0–15; median: 1.0).

In univariate regression, perceived good health was associated with higher odds of access to continuous care (OR = 1.90; 95% CI: 1.08–3.36), although this variable was not retained in the multivariable model. A higher number of non-asthma medications was inversely associated with CoC, remaining borderline significant in the multivariable analysis (adjusted OR = 0.88; 95% CI: 0.78–1.00; p = 0.050) (Table 2).

The mean duration of asthma was 10.58 ± 8.97 years. Slightly more than half of the participants (51.6%) reported no exacerbations in the previous year. Asthma-related hospitalization in the previous year was 3.8% (Supplement 4). Duration since asthma diagnosis was not statistically significant in the multivariable model (Table 2).

Based on ACT categories, more than half of participants had uncontrolled asthma (56.4%), while only 17.9% had well-controlled asthma. However, self-perceived poor asthma control was less frequent (40.1%) than ACT-defined uncontrolled asthma (Supplement 4). ACT-defined good asthma control was independently associated with higher odds of access to continuous care compared to ACT-defined uncontrolled asthma (adjusted OR = 2.67; 95% CI: 1.31–5.43; p = 0.007) (Table 2).

In line with the quantitative findings indicating an inconsistency between self-perceived and clinically defined control, participants frequently described prolonged periods of symptoms, which were attributed to alternative explanations such as psychological conditions before receiving a diagnosis. These attributions were not limited to patients’ interpretations. Physicians frequently framed their symptoms within non-respiratory explanations, reinforcing delays in diagnostic recognition, which were often accompanied by repeated and fragmented service use.

“I had breathing and psychological problems for many years. Therefore, both I and my doctors thought it was related to stress or anxiety. I’ve had symptoms since childhood. Still, the possibility that it could be asthma never crossed my mind.” (Participant with access to continuous care)

“I first visited my family physician, but no diagnosis was made. I returned several times, with the same outcome. I then sought care at a public hospital, where again no diagnosis was established. My symptoms worsened. I was repeatedly referred to different departments, including pulmonology. Eventually, another physician suggested asthma and started treatment. Within a few days, my life changed.” (Participant withou access to continuous care)

Acceptability & ability to seek

Only 4.8% of participants initially applied to FHCs, whereas 55.4% first sought care at tertiary care and 29.2% at public secondary care. Similarly, asthma was diagnosed largely in tertiary care (70.5%) and public secondary care (20.5%). Diagnosis at the primary care was rare (0.6%). The role of family physicians in asthma management appeared limited as 28.2% of participants reported that their family physician was not aware of their asthma diagnosis. Only 3.8% of participants had a family physician who referred them to a specialist when needed (Supplement 4).

FHCs were commonly described as technically insufficient for asthma diagnosis and follow-up, particularly due to limited access to diagnostic tests and structured monitoring. Family physicians were often viewed as having limited capacity to manage asthma beyond initial evaluation and referral. These perceptions discouraged participants to seek care at the primary care and resulted in a preference for secondary or tertiary care.

"Family physicians are much easier to access, and they have many advantages, but there is no specialization in family medicine (…) They know a bit about everything, but when it comes to moving further in care, they fall short."

Availability, accommodation & ability to reach

Most participants (74.0%) stated that they could physically reach health services easily, and 57.6% reported being able to obtain appointments without long waiting times (Supplement 4). In multivariable analysis, difficulty in obtaining timely appointments was associated with a higher likelihood of access to continuous care (adjusted OR = 1.89; 95% CI: 1.08–3.32; p = 0.026) whereas ease of physical access to health services was not independently associated with CoC (Table 2).

Qualitative findings help to clarify this pattern by showing that difficulties in obtaining timely appointments were more commonly experienced by participants who actively attempted to maintain continuity with the same physician and to attend follow-up visits at recommended intervals. In contrast, participants who were more flexible in accepting care from different physicians or institutions reported fewer difficulties in securing appointments, yet this flexibility often came at the expense of relational continuity.

“I was receiving medication every three months, and the doctor wanted to see me every three months as well. But I can’t manage to match the third month with the same doctor. Getting an appointment with the same physician is very difficult.” (Participant without access to continuous care)

“I spent days and nights constantly checking the appointment system to find an available slot with my doctor. That was the only way to get an appointment with her.” (Participant with access to continuous care)

Participants described the long waiting times as a central constraint shaping access, particularly when care required coordination across specialties or diagnostic services. Participants also described difficulties accessing allergy and otolaryngology services, which were often required as part of the broader management of respiratory and allergic conditions related to their asthma.

“Getting an appointment at an allergy or an ENT clinic is very difficult. I’m an allergy patient, it also affects ear–nose–throat and other parts of the body” (Participant without access to continuous care)

Many participants adopted compensatory strategies aimed at preserving access rather than continuity. Regular monitoring of the national appointment system (MHRS), emergency department visits, use of private healthcare, and out-of-pocket payment for medications were reported. While such strategies occasionally enabled timely service use, they rarely supported continuity and often shifted the burden of time, effort, and financial cost onto patients.

“Even though you have a green card (type of public social security for uninsured), you may have to pay for your medications, because of appointment unavailability.” (Participant without access to continuous care)

“I book an appointment for later, but in the meantime, I come to the emergency department. My treatment has already been started there” (Participant without access to continuous care)

Outpatient clinic hours were frequently described as complicating the planned follow-up, particularly for the employed. The location of health facilities, combined with inadequate public transportation infrastructure and age-related mobility limitations, created challenges in geographical access. Although geographical access was not independently associated with access to continuous care in quantitative analyses, participant narratives indicated that these barriers may be unevenly distributed across the study population (e.g. older people, people with disabilities).

“If someone is retired or not working, maybe it’s still manageable to go. But when you work, it becomes very difficult (…) Considering your workplace conditions, you end up saying: since I already know which medication I need, I’ll just go and buy it myself.” (Participant without access to continuous care)

More than half of the participants (51.6%) reported having a family member with asthma, and 36.9% had a first-degree relative with asthma (Supplement 4). Participants without a first-degree relative diagnosed with asthma were significantly more likely to have access to continuous care compared to those with such history (adjusted OR = 1.89; 95% CI: 1.06–3.39; p = 0.031) (Table 2).

For some participants, family and social networks functioned as sources of emotional support, shared spaces of care, and knowledge exchange. These participants described receiving practical assistance from family members in daily asthma management and navigating health services. However, female participants experienced asthma through overlapping roles as both patient and caregiver. They described taking on responsibility for the care of other family members with chronic conditions, which in turn constrained their ability to prioritize their own health needs and attend regular follow-up. This dual positioning of family and social networks, as both sources of support and sites of caregiving responsibility, partially explains the quantitative pattern and shared illness experience does not necessarily enhance the ability to engage with care.

Affordability & ability to pay

Majority (59.0%) reported that their income did not meet their expenditures and 17.3% was uninsured. Neither perceived income nor health insurance coverage was statistically associated with access to continuous care. Similarly, employment (labor market participation and time availability for healthcare use) did not show a significant association (Supplement 4; Table 2).

Qualitative findings suggest that affordability operates in practice beyond insurance coverage. Participants described difficulties in accessing medications and follow-up care despite being insured, primarily due to high and repetitive co-payments, cumulative out-of-pocket expenses, and organizational barriers such as appointment availability and prescription renewal. These challenges were particularly relevant as their financial burdens accumulated over time rather than arising from single care episodes.

“I have to use asthma medication for my whole life… there should be a different co-payment for that.” (Participant without access to continuous care)

Appropriateness & ability to engage

The majority of participants reported sufficient consultation time (74.4%) and access to clear explanations during care (76.6%) (Table 1). Participants who reported sufficient consultation time were significantly more likely to have access to continuous care compared to those with limited consultation time (p = 0.003) (Supplement 4). Access to clear explanations was strongly associated with continuity in univariate regression. In multivariable analysis, access to clear explanations remained independently associated with CoC (adjusted OR = 3.30; 95% CI: 1.52–7.14; p = 0.002) (Table 2).

Participants emphasized that continuity with the same physician enabled greater familiarity with their history, more patient-centered care, and a stronger sense of being understood beyond symptoms. When physicians were perceived as attentive, explanatory, and responsive to patients’ everyday circumstances, trust was more easily established, reinforcing ongoing engagement with follow-up and treatment plans. Participants described how short encounters constrained opportunities for meaningful communication and patient-centered assessment, leading participants to demand clear and informative communication and adequate time to discuss follow-up and referral processes.

“Because of time constraints, they have difficulty examining us properly. Everyone is evaluated within the same category. But I am not the same as a person living in a village (…) When they place both of us in the same context and try to attribute the same effects, problems arise.” (Participant with access to continuous care)

“Going to the same physician is important(…) What happens throughout care changes over time, and it is important for the physician to know my previous experiences to choose more appropriate treatment approaches” (Participant without access to continuous care)

These accounts emphasize the value of interpersonal continuity while also indicating its limits, particularly regarding the integration of patients’ everyday social context into care, a pattern that is also reflected in quantitative findings. Among participants who identified a regular physician, 85.4% reported that their physician was familiar with their previous asthma-related experiences and treatment process, and 76.8% did not hesitate to ask their physician questions when they had concerns. However, 45% of the participants agreed that their physician was aware of their daily life routines and how these were related to asthma, and 37.1% reported that their physician was aware of their family and social circumstances and their relevance to asthma management (Supplement 4).

Participants who had access to continuous care were significantly more likely to receive asthma-related services (spirometry, imaging, prescription, allergy related diagnostic procedures) from the regular institution rather than from different providers, whereas those without access to continuous care more frequently obtained these services from different institutions, indicating a fragmented pattern of service use (Supplement 4). In multivariable regression analysis, receiving prescriptions from institutions other than the continuous care source was independently associated with lower odds of CoC (adjusted OR = 0.17; 95% CI: 0.06–0.50; p = 0.001) (Table 2).

Fragmented service provision and insufficient coordination translated into tangible burdens for patients. The multi-disciplinary nature of asthma management further intensified these burdens, particularly for individuals with comorbid conditions, as diagnostic tests, consultations, and treatments were often distributed across multiple institutions without effective linkage. Participants emphasized that inadequate coordination resulted in disruptions in information flow between providers, creating uncertainty regarding referral pathways and follow-up responsibilities. The burden of ensuring continuity (tracking test results, coordinating appointments, and seeking appropriate referrals) was frequently shifted onto patients, resulting in a desire for more direct and informed referral mechanisms, particularly across specialties involved in asthma care.

“I wish I had been referred directly to the allergy outpatient clinic by physicians from other specialties, including the chest physician or even my family doctor. During that period, I was unable to work or even go outside, I could not do anything at all.” (Participant without access to continuous care)

Participants reported relatively low levels of anticipated stigma related to asthma across different social domains. Mean stigma scores were highest for family and friends (mean = 1.88 ± 0.99) and work colleagues (mean = 1.81 ± 1.06), while lower scores were observed for healthcare workers (mean = 1.58 ± 0.79). The overall stigma score was low (mean = 1.76 ± 0.67). In the “work colleagues” subscale, housewives were excluded from the analyses, as they were not employed and did not plan to work in the future and therefore did not respond to subscale items. When examined in relation to CoC, anticipated stigma did not differ significantly between participants across any subscale (Supplement 4). Anticipated stigma from healthcare workers was included in multivariable regression, however it was not associated with continuity (OR = 0.80; 95% CI: 0.57–1.11; p = 0.186) (Table 2).

Qualitative findings, however, reveal a more nuanced picture of stigma as an implicit and situational experience rather than an overt barrier to care. Participants described being perceived as disruptive or unreliable in work settings, feeling pressure to conceal their condition to avoid negative judgments, and internalizing responsibility for managing symptoms without burdening others. Within family, similar dynamics were evident, as participants reported being accused of “creating an illness” or exaggerating symptoms, which further reinforced tendencies toward concealment and self-management. In interactions with healthcare workers, stigma was not articulated through explicit discrimination but through experiences of being dismissed and psychologized.

“They (colleagues) treated my asthma as an inconvenience to their work. This created such intense pressure for me that I felt I should not draw attention to it. I felt as if being ill were something shameful, as if I had to hide it, and I even took my medication secretly” (Participant with access to continuous care)

Rethinking the model

Based on the integrated findings, Levesque et al.’s framework [16] was revised to better capture access to continuous care in asthma (Fig. 2). Unlike Levesque et al.’s framework [16] access to continuous asthma care did not unfold as a linear process, rather it emerged as a process shaped by repeated encounters and reciprocal interactions over time. In the revised model, healthcare utilization is positioned both as realized access and as part of a cyclical process that generates new needs for access. Although Levesque et al.’s framework [16] conceptually acknowledge the iterative nature of access, the visual representation of the model does not fully capture this. While Levesque’s framework [16] theoretically allows for interactions across dimensions, revised model prefers to demonstrate these bidirectional interactions.

The Revised Form of the Levesque et al. [16] Access to Healthcare framework in the Context of Continuity of Asthma Care. Note: The original dimensions of healthcare access (approachability, acceptability, availability and accommodation, affordability, and appropriateness) and corresponding patient abilities are derived from Levesque et al.’s framework of access to healthcare [16]. The cyclical representation of access, bidirectional interactions across dimensions, parallel care cycles, and the central role assigned to care coordination were developed by the study team based on the integrated quantitative and qualitative findings

Moreover, asthma care often gives rise to parallel clinical care cycles. In case of comorbidities, patients are referred to other specialties, initiating additional care trajectories. These parallel cycles involve similar access stages and unfold concurrently, rendering coordination across services a critical component of continuity. While coordination is situated under the “appropriateness” dimension in the original framework, in the revised model, it functions as an essential mechanism that links parallel care cycles rather than merely reflecting service quality.

Discussion

This study found that only 29.2% of participants had access to continuous care. Access to continuous care emerged as a process shaped by the interaction of multiple factors, including the health system, organization of healthcare, patients’ socioeconomic context, health needs, and interpersonal and inter-institutional communication. Across all stages of access, patients encountered multiple barriers and facilitators, addressing the fragmented and uneven nature of continuity in asthma care.

Sociodemographic context

The mean age and gender distribution of the participants were consistent with findings from the 2019 Türkiye Health Survey (THS) [27]. The proportion of uninsured participants was higher than that reported for asthma patients in the THS 2019 (5.24%), but lower than the general population without social security coverage (27.4%) [27, 47].

Management continuity in access to continuous care

The higher proportion of participants identifying a regular physician compared to those accessing continuous care points to barriers in timely and need-based service use. Existing literature similarly suggests that asthma care is often oriented toward acute symptom relief rather than structured follow-up [48, 49]. Data on continuity of asthma care in Türkiye is limited, but regular attendance at follow-up visits remains limited among COPD patients, with only 19.4% reporting participation [50].

In this study, engagement in appropriate follow-up care differed substantially according to time since diagnosis, revealing a clear gap in early-stage management. Evidence from a multicenter prospective study in Türkiye supports this pattern, showing that adherence to scheduled follow-up visits declines markedly after the initial post-diagnostic period [51]. Despite these, patients consistently view routine follow-up as essential for understanding their condition, monitoring its course, and accessing effective treatment according to both literature and our study [52]. The limited continuity, even in the presence of this awareness, underscores the role of structural and organizational barriers in shaping access.

Health needs, approachability and ability to perceive

Previous studies from Türkiye consistently report suboptimal asthma control, although reported rates vary substantially depending on study population and setting [53, 54]. The level in this study is within this range. Participants with well-controlled asthma were 2.67 times more likely to access continuous care than those with uncontrolled asthma. Patients with poorer asthma control, despite having greater need, appear more likely to face unmet needs, reflecting an inverse care pattern.

Limitations in service delivery may partly explain this association. Previous studies have shown that asthma control is not systematically assessed during clinical encounters and that patients with poor control are not consistently referred to appropriate specialist care [48, 55–57]. Our qualitative findings support this interpretation, as participants frequently reported that their symptoms were insufficiently acknowledged and that referrals were delayed or inadequate. In a purely cross-sectional design, the direction of the association between asthma control and CoC would remain ambiguous, as better asthma control could also be a consequence of continuity. However, the qualitative findings provided contextual insight into how patients with poorer asthma control experienced delayed recognition of symptoms, fragmented follow-up, and difficulties accessing continuous care, suggesting a possible inverse care pattern.

Participants experienced delayed diagnosis due to the underrecognition of their symptoms by health professionals. Delayed asthma diagnosis and its consequences, such as repeated and fragmented healthcare use prior to diagnosis and feelings of not being taken seriously, are evidenced in the literature and our study [58]. Together, delayed diagnosis and insufficient symptom recognition may weaken the early relationship between patients and the health system, thereby constraining access to continuous care.

Acceptability and ability to seek

Asthma-related service use was concentrated in tertiary care, suggesting limited use of primary care as the first point of contact and provider of continuous care. Previous research in Türkiye similarly demonstrates weak integration of primary care into chronic disease management with only 11.2% diagnosed in primary care, 15.8% followed at this level, and 41.8% not followed by a family physician [59]. Our findings suggest a more pessimistic pattern for asthma. Earlier research also reported that one in five children with asthma had a family physician unaware of the diagnosis, while this proportion was higher among adults in this study (28.2%) [60].

Participants reported perceiving FHCs as technically inadequate, particularly regarding diagnostic capacity and structured follow-up. Prior studies indicate that perceived technical competence facilitates relational continuity across care levels [61]. In this context, perceived limitations in family physicians’ capacity to diagnose and monitor asthma appear to reduce engagement with primary care and contribute to direct use of higher-level services. This interpretation is supported by studies showing limited self-reported competence among family physicians in asthma diagnosis, treatment initiation, and follow-up [55, 62, 63]

These findings should be interpreted within the broader organization of the Turkish health system. Although technical and organizational challenges are also reported internationally, primary care retains a central role in asthma diagnosis, follow-up, and care coordination across countries [58, 61, 64]. In Türkiye, the absence of an effective referral system and declining institutional trust in primary care have been identified as key factors undermining its role in chronic disease management [65].

Availability and ability to reach

Participants who reported no difficulty obtaining appointments had 1.89 times lower odds of accessing continuous care as individuals attempting to secure follow-up appointments with the same physician within the recommended timeframe encountered greater difficulty. Consistent with prior literature, difficulties in accessing timely and need-responsive appointments including long waiting times, challenges in securing follow-up visits with the same physician, and limited-service hours, emerged as key obstacles regarding availability [66–70]. Participants similarly reported that when relational continuity could not be maintained due to appointment-related barriers, access to regular prescriptions became harder, in line with previous findings [70].

Participants without a first-degree relative with asthma had nearly twice the odds of accessing continuous care compared with those who had an affected relative (OR = 1.89). Qualitative accounts suggest that this association may reflect caregiving burden rather than protective social support. Similar associations between caregiving burden and reduced continuity have been reported elsewhere [66].

Affordability and ability to pay

No statistically significant association was observed between insurance and CoC. However, insured participants described financial barriers, including repeated co-payments, indirect costs related to time off work, and resorting to private care or out-of-pocket medication purchases due to appointment unavailability. In addition, 59.0% of participants had income below their expenses, suggesting limited ability to pay even among those formally insured.

Evidence from other settings similarly indicates that insurance expansion does not uniformly improve access to asthma-related services. Studies evaluating Medicaid expansion in the United States have shown increases in prescription medication use, but no corresponding improvement in asthma-related outpatient visits or broader service utilization [71, 72]. Moreover, high cost-sharing insurance models have been associated with delayed or forgone care among low-income patients, with nearly half identifying cost as a barrier to access [73, 74]. In Spain, patients seeking physician continuity in secondary care have also been reported to incur out-of-pocket expenses [61]. Additional studies document both direct costs and indirect costs as barriers among asthma patients [19, 66 , 68, 70]. Also, the analyses of the 2019 THS found no significant association between insurance and asthma, while reporting higher levels of cost-related unmet need among individuals with asthma.27 Taken together, these findings suggest that, in the Turkish context, insurance coverage alone is insufficient to explain access to continuous asthma care, and that affordability should be understood as a multidimensional constraint extending beyond formal coverage.

Appropriateness and ability to engage

Access to clear explanations regarding asthma was the strongest facilitator of continuity (OR = 3.30). Consultation time also functioned as a key dimension of appropriateness. Thus, asthma care was strongly shaped by communicative and relational aspects of care. Consistent with the previous studies, participants emphasized the need for clear communication as well as sufficient time to discuss follow-up and referral [61]. However, dense appointment schedules and short consultation times frequently prevented meaningful dialogue and trust-building, a pattern also reported in previous studies [66, 67].

Continuity was more likely to be established and sustained when physicians were perceived as knowing patients’ medical histories, engaging in patient-centered communication, and fostering an environment in which patients felt comfortable asking questions. However, there was limited attention to patients’ daily lives and social contexts, with asthma care frequently experienced as standardized and focused primarily on medication. Prior research shows insufficient involvement in shared decision-making and limited consideration of social determinants were associated with weaker relational continuity and reduced engagement with care [66, 69, 75–79].

Our findings indicate that continuity of asthma care is undermined by fragmented service delivery and weak coordination mechanisms. Participants frequently described difficulties related to appointments, logistics, unexpected costs, and limited inter-physician communication, which often shifted the burden of coordination onto patients themselves. These experiences point to disruptions in information continuity, particularly when diagnostic and treatment processes were distributed across multiple institutions. Accordingly, individuals obtaining prescriptions outside a regular source of care showed markedly lower continuity.

Existing evidence reports low referral rates to specialist care among patients with uncontrolled asthma [80]. Also, research from Türkiye indicates limited access to test results across institutions and infrequent consultation between family physicians and pulmonologists [55, 60]. Patients also report insufficient communication during referral processes [64]. These findings reflect long-standing structural coordination gaps within the Turkish health system. Kringos (2011) similarly identified the absence of formal mechanisms supporting coordination between primary and secondary care, alongside limited bidirectional communication between referring physicians and specialists [81].

The mean total score of the CIASS among participants was 1.76 ± 0.67, lower than scores for other chronic and neurological conditions [35, 59]. Although no significant associations were identified between stigma subscales and access to continuous care, qualitative findings suggest a more complex picture. Participants described experiences of stigma within family and social environments, workplaces, and healthcare settings. Previous studies similarly highlight that dismissive or minimizing attitudes from family members, peers, or health professionals may contribute to diagnostic delays, inadequate treatment, and embarrassment related to medication use in public [19, 64, 70, 75, 82]. Taken together, these findings indicate that stigma in asthma may operate through subtle, context-specific mechanisms not fully captured by existing scales.

Rethinking the model

This study examined the multi-layered determinants of access to continuous asthma care using the Levesque et al.’s framework and based on the empirical findings, proposed a structural refinement of the model [16]. By making the bidirectional relationships between access dimensions visually explicit and incorporating the temporal and cyclical nature of access, the proposed model addresses key limitations identified in prior syntheses, including the underrepresentation of time, insufficient articulation of interactions between components, and the model’s predominant application in high-income settings [83].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Conducted in a single tertiary care clinic and during outpatient clinic hours, the findings may not be generalizable to other care levels and may underrepresent employed individuals. Recruitment from a tertiary pulmonary clinic may also have resulted in overrepresentation of individuals with more complex healthcare needs or more severe asthma-related experiences. Although the achieved quantitative sample size (n = 312) was slightly below the calculated target sample size (n = 317), the shortfall was minimal and is unlikely to have substantially affected the study findings. Self-reported measures are subject to recall and social desirability bias, and the ACT reflects symptom control over the previous four weeks rather than long-term control. Housewives did not complete the “Employer and Work Life” subscale of the CIASS, as it was not applicable to their circumstances; these responses were therefore excluded from analysis. Quantitative data were collected during the summer, which may have influenced service utilization patterns, and qualitative findings may be affected by volunteer bias, as only participants consenting to follow-up interviews were included. Multiple univariate statistical comparisons were performed as part of the exploratory variable selection process for multivariable modelling. Formal adjustments for multiple comparisons were not applied because the analyses were intended to identify potential predictors rather than to test prespecified hypotheses. Consequently, statistically significant associations should be interpreted cautiously, as some findings may reflect an increased risk of type I error and require confirmation in future studies. In addition, the proposed refinement of Levesque et al.’s framework was developed based on findings from a specific population of adults receiving asthma care in a tertiary care setting. As such, the applicability of the revised model to other populations, conditions, and healthcare contexts should be interpreted cautiously and warrants further empirical examination.

Conclusions

Access to continuous asthma care in Türkiye remains limited and is shaped by the interaction between individual experiences and the health system. Strengthening guideline-based monitoring and referral for uncontrolled asthma is essential. Enhancing patient-centered communication requires sufficient consultation time and integration of communication and social history skills into medical training. Clear referral criteria, bidirectional information-sharing, and integrated service models are needed to reduce the burden on patients to navigate care.

Barriers related to appointment systems, restricted service hours, and financial constraints disproportionately affect working and low-income patients. Policy responses should diversify service hours, reduce out-of-pocket payments, and prevent income loss associated with care-seeking. Although insurance coverage is widespread, affordability and continuity cannot be ensured through coverage alone, highlighting the need for supportive payment mechanisms. Addressing stigma through professional training, workplace education, and improved measurement tools is also necessary. Ultimately, continuity depends not only on service availability but on how care is organized, coordinated, financed, and experienced over time.
