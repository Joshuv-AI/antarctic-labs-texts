Patient derived xenograft models of hormone receptor positive and HER2 negative breast cancer from Indian patients
Authors: Khusbhoo A Gandhi, Rohan Chaubal, Elizabeth Talker, Jaya Chitra Aadhi, Shwetali Pandey, Rushikesh Mukhare, Anushree Kadam, Ankita Singh, Mamta Gurav, Pallavi Parab, Yogesh Kembhavi, Rahul Thorat, Anbarasan Sekar, Seema Gulia, Kunal Gala, Nitin Shetty, Shalaka Joshi, Suyash Kulkarni, Tanuja Shet, Sudeep Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLimited success has been achieved in the development of patient-derived xenografts (PDX) models of hormone receptor-positive (HR+), Human Epidermal growth factor Receptor 2-negative (HER2-negative) metastatic breast cancers that are resistant to hormone therapy (HT). We aimed to establish and characterize PDX models from HR+/HER2-negative breast cancer. A total of 28 tumors were orthotopically implanted in the fourth mammary fat pad of female NOD-SCID mice. Seven PDXs were developed, five from heavily pretreated HT-resistant patients and two from treatment-naïve patients. Three of the seven PDXs (two from HT-resistant and one from treatment-naive) converted into lymphoma by generation 2 (G1). The median time for developing palpable G0 PDX was 100 (45–150) days for five HT-resistant tumors, and the mean time was 178 (160–196) days for two treatment-naïve tumors. Successful PDXs were developed from three HT-resistant tumors (two up to G4, one up to G0), including one of solid papillary morphology, and one treatment-naïve tumor (up to G2). Histopathological analyses showed complete concordance for all 14 PDX generations (11 resistant, 3 treatment-naïve) with their corresponding patient’s tumor for estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR) and HER2. Short tandem repeat (STR) profiling using 18 markers was done in 8/11 HT-resistant generations and 3/3 treatment-naïve generations and showed good concordance of 51–100% and 91–100%, respectively, with patient tumors. We successfully established PDXs of HT-resistant and treatment-naïve HR+/HER2-negative breast cancer, which will be valuable tools for understanding molecular resistance mechanisms and for exploring novel treatment strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-22361-z.Keywords: Patient derived xenografts, ER, PR, HER2, Hormone therapy-resistant, Breast cancerSubject terms: Cancer, Oncology

Introduction

Hormone receptor-positive (HR+), Human Epidermal growth factor Receptor 2-negative (HER2-negative) is the most common subtype of breast cancer, accounting for about 50–60% of patients in India1. Owing to its strong dependency on the endocrine receptor signaling, the most important treatment for HR+/HER2-negative breast cancer is estrogen suppression and estrogen receptor (ER) antagonism2. Although estrogen suppression and antagonism have reduced mortality, about 10–25% of patients eventually develop resistance, leading to metastatic disease3. Investigating mechanisms of hormone therapy resistance continues to be an important avenue of research to devise new treatments and improve outcomes in patients with HR+/HER2-negative breast cancer.

Tumor sequencing has provided valuable insight into the genomic profile of hormone therapy resistance4,5. However, obtaining tumor tissue from patients, especially from metastatic sites, is difficult and such samples have substantial tumor heterogeneity. Moreover, metastatic tumors often arise from aggressive clones that have typically acquired resistance to earlier treatments, and such clones may constitute only a small proportion of the primary tumor6,7. It is of great translational interest to be able to test new candidate drugs on resistant tumor tissue8. Current preclinical models, primarily tumor-derived cell lines, inadequately recapitulate the complexity of human tumors, especially treatment-resistant disease. Patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models have emerged as powerful preclinical tools that more comprehensively recapitulate the histopathological, genomic, and therapeutic response profiles of the original patient tumors9,10. PDX retain tumor heterogeneity and are particularly valuable for understanding mechanisms of treatment resistance and for testing novel therapeutic agents in a biologically relevant context10,11.

PDX models have been successfully established for several breast cancer subtypes worldwide12,13. Our group has previously reported successful development of a single PDX model from a patient with hormone therapy-resistant HR+/HER2-negative breast cancer14. However, there is a paucity of PDX models from such patients that reliably recapitulate the variation in human breast cancer, including uncommon histopathological subtypes and exposure to a variety of prior treatments. Moreover, it is technically challenging to create PDX models of HR+/HER2-negative breast cancer because of lower engraftment efficiency. Here we present the development of four PDX models from patients with HR+/HER2-negative breast cancer, with a focus on hormone therapy resistance.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients were recruited and their tumor samples were obtained after obtaining written informed consent under study number 239, registered with the Clinical Trials Registry of India (CTRI) as CTRI/2017/11/010553, as approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) of Tata Memorial Centre. All in vivo experiments involving animal subjects were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, adhering to ARRIVE guidelines 2.0 (https://arriveguidelines.org).

Study participants and sample collection

Eligible patients had histopathologically confirmed estrogen receptor (ER) and/or progesterone receptor (PR)-positive and HER2-negative breast cancer. ER, PR and HER2 evaluation were performed by routine immunohistochemistry as per institutional protocol, and fluorescent in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed to clarify HER2-equivocal immunohistochemical status. All patients with early-stage or locally advanced breast cancers (LABC) received the standard curative-intent treatment, including surgery, (neo)adjuvant chemotherapy as indicated, adjuvant hormone therapy, and radiotherapy if indicated. Patients who underwent upfront surgery were classified as treatment naïve, whereas those who had disease relapses within two years of starting hormone therapy, or disease progression on hormone therapy for metastatic disease, were considered resistant. Tumor tissue was collected either from the breast surgical specimen or biopsy from the most accessible site of relapse (Fig. 1). Fresh tumor specimens were collected in MACS Tissue Storage Solution (Miltenyi Biotech, USA) and transported on ice from the operating room to the laboratory animal facility. Under aseptic conditions, tissues were rinsed, then minced in ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) into ~ 3–4-mm fragments using a sterile scalpel, and fragments were used immediately for implantation into mice.

Detailed description of the PDX establishment pipeline.

Animals

All animal studies were conducted after approval from the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) of the Advanced Centre for Treatment, Research and Education in Cancer (ACTREC) as per the norms of the Committee for Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CCSEA) guidelines (Protocol No. 40/2022, dated October 20, 2022). Female NOD-SCID mice were obtained from the Laboratory Animal Facility of ACTREC and housed in individually ventilated cages (IVCs) under standard conditions of temperature (22 ± 2 °C), relative humidity (45 ± 10%) and light (12-h light–dark cycle). Animals were given a standard pelleted diet and sterile plain drinking water ad libitum.

Establishment of orthotopic PDX models

Tumor fragments (~ 3–4 mm) were orthotopically implanted into the intact right fourth mammary fat pad of anesthetized female NOD–SCID mice, and the incision was closed with Vetbond tissue adhesive (3M). This cohort constituted the G0 generation. Mice were observed weekly for tumor engraftment and growth. Animals implanted with tumor tissue that did not develop PDX were monitored until natural death. Tumor size was measured using sterile calipers, and volume (V) was estimated as V = (W2 × L)/2, where W is the shorter diameter and L the longer diameter. When tumors reached ~1,000 mm³, animals were euthanized by CO₂ asphyxiation, a complete necropsy was performed and tumors were harvested and processed in five preparations: (1) snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent DNA and/or RNA extraction; (2) fixed in formalin and embedded in paraffin for histopathology and immunochemistry; (3) placed in RNAlater Stabilization Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific) until nucleic acid extraction and sequencing; (4) Aliquots of tumor fragments were cryopreserved for biobanking in 90% fetal bovine serum (FBS)/10% dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO); and (5) propagated by serial in vivo passaging (Fig. 1). For serial transplantation from G0 to G1, a tumor fragment (~3–4 mm) was orthotopically implanted into the intact right fourth mammary fat pad of female NOD–SCID mice (n = 1–3 recipients per line). At necropsy, lungs, liver, brain, and kidneys were collected and processed to assess metastatic dissemination.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections (5 μm) were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) using standard procedures. Briefly, patient and PDX sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through descending grades of ethanol (100%, 95%, 75%) to distilled water at room temperature. Sections were stained with hematoxylin, washed with water, treated with 1% HCl in 70% ethanol, and rinsed in running water. Sections were then counterstained with eosin, dehydrated through absolute ethanol, cleared in xylene (5 min each), and coverslipped. Slides were reviewed by a pathologist to assess histopathological concordance between patient tumors and corresponding PDXs, and representative images were acquired on a Zeiss Axio Imager Z1 microscope equipped with an Axiocam MRC5 digital camera.

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed using the EnVision™ FLEX Mini Kit (Dako, Denmark) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, FFPE sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through a graded series of ethanols, and subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval using the kit’s antigen-retrieval buffer via microwave treatment. Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with the peroxidase-blocking reagent (15 min), followed by serum blocking. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies to ERα (clone SP1, 1:100; LifeSpan BioSciences, USA), PR (clone 1E2, 1:100; Abcam, UK), and HER2 (clone EP1045Y, 1:100; Abcam, UK). Detection employed an anti-rabbit HRP system, and signals were visualized with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. Slides were reviewed and scored by a pathologist, and representative images were captured on a Zeiss Axio Imager Z1 microscope with an Axiocam MRC5 digital camera.

Short tandem repeat (STR) profiling

Genomic DNA was extracted from patient tumors and matched PDX samples using the QIAamp DNA Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. DNA quantity and purity were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Short tandem repeat (STR) profiling was performed using the PowerPlex® 18D System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA)15, which co-amplifies 17 autosomal loci: D3S1358, TH01, D21S11, D18S51, Penta E, D5S818, D13S317, D7S820, D16S539, CSF1PO, Penta D, vWA, D8S1179, TPOX, D2S1338, D19S433, and FGA, along with the Amelogenin marker for sex determination. Because all patients were female (XX), Amelogenin showed a single X band. Under diploid assumptions, up to 34 autosomal alleles plus a single Amelogenin marker were expected per sample. Any deviations (e.g., tri-allelic patterns) were recorded as potential indicators of aneuploidy or copy-number alterations.

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification and capillary electrophoresis were performed on an Applied Biosystems 3500 Genetic Analyzer. Allele calling and sizing were conducted using GeneMarker software (laboratory-validated version). Each run included appropriate controls. STR profiles from patient and PDX samples were compared locus-by-locus, and percent concordance was calculated as 100 × (number of matching alleles / total alleles compared).

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 28 patients were included, with a median age of 46.5 years (range, 28–72). Disease trajectories were heterogeneous: two patients developed isolated loco-regional recurrences after initial therapy; six patients developed distant metastases and received systemic treatment, with some experiencing disease progression despite treatment; and 11 patients presented with de novo metastatic disease and eventually experienced disease progression on initial treatment. Of the eight treatment-naïve patients who underwent upfront surgery, seven remained progression-free at 2-year follow-up, while one patient relapsed within two years. Patients with resistant disease received a median of three lines of systemic therapy at the time of data cut-off. Table 1 summarizes the clinical characteristics of included patients.

Establishment of PDX models

Between March 2022 and March 2024, tumor tissues from 28 patients (20 hormone therapy-resistant, 8 treatment-naïve) with HR+/HER2-negative breast cancer were orthotopically implanted in the fourth mammary fat pad of female NOD-SCID mice. The site of the tumor tissue collection is shown in Fig. 2.

Overview of developed PDX. G0 is the generation of mice in which the patient tumor was implanted. G1, G2, G3 & G4 are generations of PDXs after G0. #: two out of four developed PDXs were lymphoma. *: Developed PDX was lymphoma. BCS: breast conserving surgery, MRM: modified radical mastectomy.

Among 20 tumor specimens from hormone therapy-resistant patients, five PDXs were established at G0 (5/20; 25%), comprising one derived from a lung metastasis and four from liver metastases. In the treatment-naïve cohort, two PDXs were generated from eight upfront-surgery specimens at G0 (2/8; 25%). Thus, the patient-to-mouse engraftment rate was 25% in both groups, consistent with prior reports12,16. Of the seven G0 PDXs, three converted to murine lymphoma by G2; two resistant (P171R, P175R) and one treatment-naive (UPS-24).

Time to palpable engraftment (G0) differed by clinical context. For hormone therapy–resistant tumors, the median time to a palpable PDX was 100 (45–150) days, whereas treatment-naïve tumors required a mean of 178 (160–196) days to become palpable at G0. The G0 to G1 intervals (time from initial engraftment to first passage) were a median of 134 days for resistant tumors and 248 days for treatment-naïve specimens. The patient P156R, from whom a successful PDX was generated, had mucinous solid papillary carcinoma, an uncommon breast cancer subtype. Hence, clinical and histopathological characteristics of P156R are described in detail in the manuscript.

Clinical characteristics of P156R

This 52-year-old postmenopausal woman presented with right locally advanced breast cancer, with an 8 cm mass and matted ipsilateral axillary nodes. Histopathology showed invasive breast carcinoma, grade III, with pools of extracellular mucin, ER-positive (Allred score 7/8), PR-weak positive (Allred score 3/8), and HER2-equivocal by IHC and non-amplified by FISH. She received four cycles of neoadjuvant doxorubicin/cyclophosphamide (AC) followed by weekly paclitaxel (from May to October 2018), followed by right mastectomy with axillary clearance. Pathology revealed 5/14 positive lymph nodes with perinodal extension. She was treated with tamoxifen but developed metastatic disease involving lungs, lymph nodes, bone marrow, bones, and adrenal glands after 3 years. A lung biopsy showed GATA3-positive, S100 and TTF-1 negative atypical cells consistent with metastatic breast carcinoma, grade II, of solid papillary type, which was ER-positive (90%), PR-positive (10%) and HER2-negative (1+). Tissue from this biopsy was implanted to establish a PDX. She subsequently received capecitabine, letrozole, and oral cyclophosphamide for 3 months and remained clinically stable during that period. The patient died in November 2023.

Serial passaging of PDX from P156R

After orthotopic implantation of patient tumor tissue (G0), the graft became palpable (~80 mm³) by day 85 and reached the endpoint volume (~1,000 mm³) by day 134. At the endpoint, tumors were harvested and serially passaged to generate the next generation (G1) PDXs in female NOD–SCID mice. The growth rate accelerated with passaging. The time-to-tumor-take (palpable ~80 mm³) shortened to 43 days (G1), 32 days (G2), and 24 days (G3), and time to endpoint decreased to 82, 56 and 43 days, respectively. Accordingly, G2–G3 grew faster than G0–G1, with growth kinetics stabilizing by approximately G3. Histopathological characteristics of the patient tumor and PDX of 156R

Haematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining was performed on the patient’s tumor and matched PDX tissues of this patient to compare histopathological features and tissue architecture and to evaluate stability across passages. Overall, the patient’s tumor and corresponding PDXs showed concordant histology. Immunohistochemistry for ER, PR, and HER2 was also undertaken. The index G0 PDX (P156R) demonstrated invasive solid papillary carcinoma with extracellular mucin (~30%) and Nottingham grade 2 with ER-positive (80%), PR-negative, and HER2-negative (IHC-1+), closely mirroring the patient’s tumor (ER 90%, PR 10%, HER2-negative). Histology remained consistent in later passages. In G1, two of four PDXs contained ~5% mucin, while the other two retained ~30%; all four were ER-positive (100%), PR-negative, and HER2-negative (IHC-1+). In G2–G4, tumors continued to show invasive solid papillary carcinoma with ER-positivity, PR-negativity, and HER2-negativity. Overall, the P156R PDX reproduced the key features of the original tumor, with variability primarily in mucin content across generations (Fig. 5).

Discussion

Patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models are robust platforms for translational cancer research, recapitulating tumor heterogeneity, architecture, and treatment response more faithfully than immortalised cell lines. In this study, we established four PDX models from HR+/HER2-negative breast cancers, including three from hormone therapy-resistant patients and one from a treatment-naïve patient. One PDX model from a patient with hormone therapy–resistant invasive solid papillary carcinoma is especially noteworthy because this is an uncommon breast cancer subtype which is under-represented in existing PDX repositories. Our models help address this gap and provide clinically annotated tools for investigating mechanisms of hormone therapy resistance and for preclinical therapeutic testing.

Tumors from treatment-naïve and hormone therapy-resistant samples engrafted at comparable rates (≈25%), consistent with prior reports12,16 and support the feasibility of modeling resistant disease in vivo. Whether take rates differ by prior treatment status remains unresolved17; in our series, they were equal (25% in each group), but the treatment-naïve subset was small (8 patients with 2 successes). Across passages, we observed histological fidelity, retention of hormone-receptor expression, and concordant STR profiles, consistent with prior evidence that PDXs largely preserve the architectural and molecular features of their parent tumors. By contrast, patient-derived organoids from hormone receptor-positive breast cancers frequently lose hormone receptor expression with passaging18.

STR profiling demonstrated patient- and passage-specific variability in PDX fidelity, but overall genetic stability. Most models showed high patient–PDX concordance at early passages (G0–G1), which declined in later generations, suggesting the value of early-passage PDXs for experimental studies and the need for ongoing genetic monitoring (e.g., serial STR profiling) to evaluate model integrity over time. Spontaneous lymphoma overgrowth occurred in three PDX lines, consistent with prior reports of lymphoma emergence during PDX propagation19.

Although HR+/HER2-negative breast tumors are generally less aggressive, hormone therapy-resistant cases often acquire molecular and phenotypic changes that make them harder to treat20,21. In our series, PDXs from hormone therapy-resistant tumors reached palpability sooner than those from treatment-naïve tumors (~100 versus ~178 days at G0), indicating greater intrinsic growth potential of resistant tumors21,22. This is consistent with our recent observation of PIK3CA, ESR1, and TP53 mutational profile, and DNA-repair defects in hormone therapy-resistant tumors, which are linked to genomic instability and accelerated engraftment23.

A key strength of this study is the clinical annotation, including treatment history and disease progression patterns, which enhances the translational utility of each PDX. For example, the P156R PDX was derived after failure of multiple treatments in a patient with widespread metastases. It will provide a platform to test next-generation agents and combinations targeting metastatic mechanisms. Because hormone therapy resistance is heterogeneous, encompassing genomic and non-genomic mechanisms, evaluating each patient’s clinical resistance profile with xenograft drug responses can help discover the drivers. The availability of treatment-naïve and therapy-resistant PDXs enables direct comparison of pathway alterations and microenvironmental factors when resistance occurs without canonical genetic lesions24. This is relevant clinically because current guidelines for advanced hormone therapy-resistant breast cancer rely on genetic markers for therapy selection, while predictive markers of non-genomic resistance remain understudied22. In this context, we recently reported that relapse-site samples showed higher mutational burden and frequent actionable driver mutations as compared to treatment-naïve samples, including PIK3CA and TP53, in hormone therapy-resistant patients25.

Our work has limitations. It relies on conventional murine PDX systems, which lack human immune and stromal compartments, limiting assessment of microenvironment-mediated treatment response and resistance. Incorporating patient-derived immune cells or humanized mouse models will be a step towards creating more contextual models. In addition, our cohort size is modest, which constrains generalizability. Future studies should expand and diversify the PDX panel to include additional molecular subtypes and metastatic niches.

Our future work will aim to expand PDXs from heavily pretreated patients, enabling empirical testing of drugs and integration with patient-derived organoids and immune-humanized systems to evaluate immune-targeted agents11. We will also use these models to generate primary cultures/organoids for high-throughput kinome-wide screens, drug panels, and knockouts using suitable (e.g., CRISPR) assays26. Notably, our group has recently reported the results of combination chemo-hormonal therapy in HR+/HER2-negative metastatic breast cancer Indian patients, which is not a commonly used therapeutic strategy27. We will aim to establish PDX models from patients whose tumors respond or are resistant to this treatment strategy. This will help to elucidate mechanisms of multiple drug resistance spanning chemotherapy and hormonal therapy drugs.

This study establishes clinically annotated PDX models from HR+/HER2-negative breast cancers, including hormone therapy-resistant cases. These models recapitulate the key histopathological and molecular features of patients’ tumors, and provide clinically relevant platforms to investigate mechanisms of resistance and emerging treatments. They expand the resources available for the development of personalized treatment of hormone receptor-positive breast cancer.
