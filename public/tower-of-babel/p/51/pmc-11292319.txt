Exploiting Metabolic Defects in Glioma with Nanoparticle-Encapsulated NAMPT Inhibitors
Authors: Matthew A. Murray, Katelyn J. Noronha, Yazhe Wang, Anna P. Friedman, Sateja Paradkar, Hee-Won Suh, Ranjini K. Sundaram, Charles Brenner, W.M. Saltzman, Ranjit S. Bindra
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe treatment of primary central nervous system tumors is challenging due to the blood–brain barrier and complex mutational profiles, which is associated with low survival rates. However, recent studies have identified common mutations in gliomas [isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH)-wild-type and mutant, WHO grades II–IV; with grade IV tumors referred to as glioblastomas (GBM)]. These mutations drive epigenetic changes, leading to promoter methylation at the nicotinic acid phosphoribosyl transferase (NAPRT) gene locus, which encodes an enzyme involved in generating NAD+. Importantly, NAPRT silencing introduces a therapeutic vulnerability to inhibitors targeting another NAD+ biogenesis enzyme, nicotinamide phosphoribosyl transferase (NAMPT), rationalizing a treatment for these malignancies. Multiple systemically administered NAMPT inhibitors (NAMPTi) have been developed and tested in clinical trials, but dose-limiting toxicities—including bone marrow suppression and retinal toxicity—have limited their efficacy. Here, we report a novel approach for the treatment of NAPRT-silenced GBMs using nanoparticle (NP)-encapsulated NAMPTis administered by convection-enhanced delivery (CED). We demonstrate that GMX1778 (a NAMPTi) can be formulated in degradable polymer NPs with retention of potency for NAMPT inhibition and anticancer activity in vitro, plus sustained drug release in vitro and in vivo. Direct injection of these drugs via CED into the brain is associated with reduced retinal toxicity compared with systemic administration. Finally, we show that CED of NP-encapsulated GMX1778 to NAPRT-silenced intracranial GBM xenografts in mice exhibit significant tumor growth delay and extends survival. These data support an approach to treat gliomas harboring defects in NAD+ metabolism using CED of NP-encapsulated NAMPTis to greatly improve the therapeutic index and treatment efficacy for this class of drugs.

Introduction

Glioblastomas (GBM) are deadly brain cancers with a 6% 5-year survival rate in the United States (1). These tumors grow rapidly and are highly invasive into surrounding tissue (2, 3). The current standard of care for these cancers is maximal gross total resection and adjuvant care with chemotherapy and radiation therapy, however, recurrence occurs rapidly with limited treatment options for salvage therapy. As such, there is an unmet need to develop effective therapeutics for patients with these tumors.

One of the most significant obstacles to treatment efficacy is the presence of the blood–brain barrier (BBB; refs. 1, 4). The BBB restricts uptake of more than 98% of small molecules and other therapies that are in systemic circulation (5). To allow BBB penetrance, agents must possess specific physiochemical properties including lipophilicity, charge, and size, which have been challenging for drug development (4, 6). Convection-enhanced delivery (CED) of drugs directly into the brain is a promising approach to bypass the BBB. This approach uses mechanical force to slowly distribute the drug volume into the intracranial tissue (4). However, studies have shown that free drug alone rapidly dissipates in situ after CED (7). One strategy to overcome this is nanoparticle (NP) encapsulation of the drug to enhance drug retention at local CED injection sites (8, 9).

In addition to treatment limitations in GBMs, the mutational profile of these tumors, and also lower grade gliomas, is complex. Recent sequencing efforts in gliomas have revealed that mutations in genes such as isocitrate dehydrogenase-1 and -2 (IDH1/2) and protein phosphatase Mg2+/Mn2+ 1∆, or PPM1D (also referred to as WIP1) are commonly observed in these tumors. IDH1/2 defects typically occur in lower grade glioma (IDH-mutant, WHO grades I–II), whereas PPM1D mutations are seen in in high-grade gliomas (WHO grade IV glioma being synonymous with GBM; ref. 10). Interestingly, both mutations dramatically alter the epigenome (11, 12). IDH1 and IDH2 catalyze conversion of cytosolic isocitrate into α-ketoglutarate (α-KG). Mutations in IDH1/2 lead to a neomorphic activity of the enzyme, which preferentially converts α-KG into 2-hydroxyglutarate (2-HG). Accumulation of 2-HG driven by mutant IDH1/2 inhibits epigenetic regulators such as the ten-eleven translocation (TET) family of DNA 5-methyl-cytosine “erasers”. This results in a global increase of DNA methylation, particularly at CpG islands, often referred to as a CpG-island methylator phenotype (CIMP; ref. 13). Moreover, our laboratory recently demonstrated that PPM1D mutations (14–16), which are seen in 20% to 30% diffuse intrinsic pontine gliomas (DIPG), a subset of gliomas, also induce a CIMP-like phenotype (11).

We and others have reported that IDH1/2 and PPM1D mutations induce promoter silencing of the nicotinic acid phosphoribosyl transferase (NAPRT) gene, which plays an important role in NAD+ production (11, 12). Briefly, NAD+ biogenesis in most tissues is largely directed through two pathways that originate with pyrimidine bases, namely the Preiss–Handler salvage pathway and the nicotinamide (NAM) salvage pathway (17). In the Preiss–Handler pathway, nicotinic acid (NA) is converted to its corresponding mononucleotide by NAPRT. In NAM salvage pathway, NAM is converted to its corresponding mononucleotide by NAM phosphoribosyl transferase (NAMPT). Our work and others demonstrated that small molecule inhibitors of NAMPT, such as GMX1778, exhibited synthetic lethality in cells lacking NAPRT (18, 19). Based on these and others findings, NAPRT silencing represents an important biomarker for treatment with NAMPT inhibitors (NAMPTi).

NAMPTis have been developed and tested in multiple clinical trials (20), but they suffer from two main limitations as treatment options for brain tumors. First, systemic delivery of these drugs results in low exposure to the brain, as BBB penetration is low (21). Second, clinical evidence reveals that NAMPTis are associated with on-target dose-limiting hematologic and retinal toxicities (22, 23). However, new drug delivery modalities present an opportunity to revisit NAMPTis as a viable therapeutic option for gliomas. Here, we report nanoparticle formulations capable of encapsulating the NAMPTi GMX1778 that allow for rapid cellular uptake of the drug and consequent intracellular NAD+ depression. Most importantly, these formulations demonstrate efficacy in selectively targeting in vivo models of GBM when delivered intracranially via CED. Moreover, we observe no discernable hematological or retinal toxicity with our NAMPTi formulation. Overall, this work identifies a highly effective NAMPTi drug delivery system for use in NAPRT-silenced high-grade gliomas.

Materials and Methods

Materials

GMX1778 was purchased from Selleck Chemicals (catalog #: S8117). Polyethylene glycol-b-polylactic acid block polymer (MW of PLA – 10 kDa; MW of PEG – 5 kDa) was from Polysciences, Inc., and Tween 80 was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. PLA (M = 20.2 kDa and M = 12.4 kDa) was purchased from Lactel. Artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) was purchased from Harvard Apparatus. Fluorescent dye was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Ethyl acetate, acetonitrile, and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) were obtained from J.T. Baker. NA (#: N4126) and the NAD+ quantification kit (MAK037) was from Sigma-Aldrich. Tetramethyl indocarbocyanine perchlorate (Di-I) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Scigen Tissue Plus O.C.T. compound (#: 23-730-571) and Superfrost Plus microscope slides (#: 12-550-15) were purchased from Fischer Scientific. Common cell culture reagents (i.e., DMEM, PBS, Penicillin-Streptomycin, Trypsin EDTA, etc.) were purchased from GIBCO. FBS (#: F0926-500 mL) is from Sigma Aldrich.

Cell culture

Immortalized normal human astrocytes (referred to as NHAs in this manuscript) were a gift from Timothy Chan. PPM1D-truncated NHAs (PPM1Dtrnc.) were generated previously and described in ref. 11. U251-MG was purchased from Sigma Aldrich (#09063001; RRID: CVCL_0021) and cultured in DMEM+10% FBS. T98G (ATCC CRL-1690; RRID: CVCL_0556), U87 (ATCC HTB-14; RRID: CVCL_0022) and U87 IDH1 (R132H; ATCC HTB-14IG) were purchased from ATCC. T98G was cultured in DMEM+10% FBS, whereas the U87 pair was in DMEM:F12+10% FBS. U251+BLIs were a cell line created via lentiviral infection of a firefly luciferase plasmid (Cellomics Technology PLV-10003), stably expressed with the treatment of G-418 (Thermo Fischer Scientific: 10131035) for 2 weeks, then single cell cloned.

Nanoparticle encapsulation of GMX1778 and characterization

PLA-PEG NP preparation

PLA-PEG NPs were prepared through a nanoprecipitation method. Polymer was first dissolved in DMSO at 20 mg/mL then added into deionized water with a ratio of 1:5 under vortex. The resulting solution was diluted in deionized water and washed three times using an Amicon centrifugal filter unit (100 kDa). The obtained NPs were used freshly in the experiment or snap-frozen at −80°C for future use. For drug-loaded NPs, GMX1778 was dissolved in DMSO and mixed with polymer solution at given weight ratio before nanoprecipitation step. For dye-loaded NPs, Di-I was dissolved in the polymer solution at 0.5% by weight to polymer.

PLA-hyperbranched polyglycerol NPs preparation

PLA-hyperbranched polyglycerol (HPG) NPs were prepared via a single-emulsion method. Polymer was dissolved in ethyl acetate overnight. The resulting solution was added dropwise to deionized water under vortex and then sonicated for 10 seconds for three cycles. The emulsion was diluted in deionized water and concentrated in a rotovap for 20 minutes. The particle solution was transferred to an Amicon centrifugal filter unit (100 kDa) and washed by water. Finally, the nanoparticles obtained were resuspended in deionized water. To create GMX/BNP, GMX/PLA-HPG NPs (25 mg/mL) were incubated with 0.1 mol/L NaIO4 (aq) and 10× PBS (1:1:1, v:v) for 20 minutes on ice. A total of 0.2 mol/L Na2SO3 (0.2 mol/L) at 1:1 (v/v) ratio to NaIO4 was added to quench the reaction. The particle solution was washed with water at 13,000 × rcf using Amicon centrifugal filter unit and resuspended in diluted water. For drug-loaded NPs, GMX1778 was dissolved in DMSO and then added to the polymer solution to obtain a drug/polymer mixture. Dye-loaded NPs was prepared using same procedures at a weight ratio of 0.5% to the polymer.

Characterization of NPs

Size and zeta potential

The hydrodynamic diameter of NPs was measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS). Zeta potential was measured using a Malvern Nano-ZS (Malvern Instruments).

Drug loading and release

NPs were dissolved in acetonitrile overnight then filtered and analyzed using a Shimadzu HPLC System (SpectraLab Scientific) with comparison against standard curves. Release profile of drug was studied by dispersing the NPs in PBS containing 0.5% Tween 80 at 37°C. The drug loading for the PLA-PEG GMX1778 was 12% and 8% for the BNP GMX1778. At designed time points, this suspension was removed and filtered for HPLC analysis. The same volume of solution removed was added back to the suspension for continued release.

Particle stability in aCSF

The NPs were resuspended in aCSF (Harvard Apparatus) at 37°C and size was determined at given timepoints by DLS.

Drug retention in mouse brain

Healthy mice were administered with free GMX or GMX/PLA-PEG NPs at the same dose via CED; then mice were sacrificed immediately after infusion (day 0, day 1, and day 5). The brain was harvested and homogenized in Precellys tubes and then drug was extracted into organic phase by a modified protocol (24). HPLC was utilized to determine the amount of drug retained in the brain at given timepoints.

Functional assessment of NP GMX1778

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed as described in ref. 11. In brief, lysates are sonicated and prepped at 3 µg/μL/sample. Samples are run on a 4% to 12% NuPAGE© gel, then wet transferred for 90 minutes at 90 V on a polyvinylidene difluoride membrane. Membranes are incubated in blocking media for 2 hours: 5% milk in 1× TBS-T: PPM1D (SCBT F-10 sc-376257), GAPDH (Proteintech group HRP-60004), IDH1 (Cell Signaling Technology 8137S), IDH1-R132H (DIANOVA DIA-H09); 5% BSA in 1× TBS-T: NAPRT (Proteintech group 66159-1-Ig). Membranes were incubated in primary antibody overnight (1:1,000 for all in designated blocking media), then washed with 1× TBS-T before being placed in secondary antibody for 1 hour [HRP conjugated anti-mouse (Thermo Fischer Scientific 31432, 1:10,000; RRID: AB_2534069) or anti-rabbit (Thermo Fischer Scientific 31462, 1:10,000; RRID: AB_2337910)]. Blots were exposed after using Clarity Western ECL substrate (BioRad) and imaged with a ChemiDoc (Biorad) imaging system.

Cell viability assessments

Cells were grown to 70% confluency and used for either short-term or long-term cell viability assays, as described in ref. 25. Briefly, cells in short-term cell viability assays were seeded as 1,000 cells per well in a 96-well plate. After treatment with increasing concentration of the drug, cells were incubated for 72 hours before being fixed (4% PFA), stained (Hoescht 33342), and counted on a Cytation 3. For the long-term cell viability assay [or clonogenic survival assay (CSA)], cells were treated with drug at indicated concentrations and grew for 2 weeks before being fixed and stained with Crystal Violet (Sigma-Aldrich).

Immunofluorescence and flow cytometry

Indicated cells were trypsinized with 0.05% Trypsin EDTA and live cell counts were obtained by counting with 0.4% Trypan blue solution (GIBCO). Cells were then seeded in either four-chamber slides at 4 × 104 cells (Millipore Sigma: PEZGS0416) or in six-well dishes at 1 × 105 cells. For immunofluorescence, cells on chamber slides were treated with 0.5 ug/mL of Di-I NPs and incubated for designated times. Cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% PFA for 15 minutes followed by treatment with Vectashield (Vector Laboratories), mounted, and sealed with a cover glass. Finally, images were taken on a Keyence BZ-X800. For flow cytometry, cells from six-well dishes were trypsinized after incubation of Di-I NPs at the indicated times and concentration. Cells were then taken and passed through a 0.22 μm filter into a column and used on a CytoFlex. Di-I signal was evaluated through the TexasRed-A filter with cell count. Data were analyzed through the FlowJo© software.

mRNA expression

Indicated cells were trypsinized and 5 × 106 cells were pelleted down. RNA extractions were performed using a QIAGEN© RNeasy kit and stored at −80°C or processed immediately. RNA was converted into complementary DNA (cDNA) using Applied Biosystems High-Capacity RNA-to-cDNA kit and stored at −20°C or used immediately. Using TaqMan© probes and master mixed all provided by Applied Biosystems, cDNA was probed for NAPRT (#4351372), and Actin (#4333762F) probes and analyzed with a StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR system. Data were collected and converted into ∆∆Ct values as described in ref. 11. Performed three biological replicates with four technical replicates per experiment.

NAD+ quantification

Cells were grown and pelleted into 5 × 105 cell aliquots and stored at −80°C before processing. Pellets were processed using the NAD+ quantification kit from Sigma-Aldrich per manufacturer protocol. Data are presented from three biological replicates with three technical replicates.

Animal studies

Animal usage

All animal maintenance and procedures were performed in accordance with the guidelines and policies of the Yale Animal Resource Center, which were approved under the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. The relevant protocol is: #2021-20071. All conducted mice studies used male and female athymic nu/nu mice (Hsd:Athymic Nude-Foxn1nu, Envigo). All mice were purchased at 6 weeks old and used at 7 weeks.

Animal tissue immunofluorescence

For retinal sections, mice were euthanized, and the eyes were removed at the indicated times, placed in 4% PFA for 2 hours, and then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose in PBS overnight. The eyes were mounted in O.C.T. freezing media and stored at −20°C overnight. Eyes were cryosectioned at 15 μm on a Leica CM1950 Cryostat and mounted on glass slides. The tissue was washed with PBS then incubated at 4°C overnight in 1:1,000 wheat germ agglutinin (WGA) conjugated to AlexaFluor 488 (Thermo Fischer Scientific #: W11261). Coverslips were mounted with Vectashield containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI; Vector Laboratories #: H-1200-10) and sealed before being analyzed using a Keyence BZ-X800. As a positive control, free GMX1778 was administered orally at 30 mg/kg 4 days QD for retina extraction (three biological replicates, nine technical replicates), further described in ref. 23. The CED-injected cohort contained three biological replicates, with 11 technical replicates per group.

In vivo toxicology and tumor efficacy

For blood toxicity studies, mice were treated intracranially with 100 mg/kg of PLA-PEG GMX1778 or 50 mg/kg of BNP GMX1778 using a stereotactic injector. At indicated times, mice weights were taken before sacrificed and ∼500 μL of blood was extracted. Blood was placed in 0.5 mol/L EDTA and immediately analyzed via a Hemavet 950FS (Drew Scientific) where white blood cells (WBC), red blood cells (RBC), and platelets were counted. “Lower limit of normal” was calculated as described in ref. 25.

For tumor efficacy studies, mice were injected intracranially with 8.0 × 105 with U251 cells that expressed luciferase (U251+BLI). Each cohort of mice had n = 6 mice. One-week post-injection, mice were then injected intracranially with either vehicle (10% Cyclodextrin), 100 mg/kg free GMX1778, 100 mg/kg PLA-PEG GMX1778, or 50 mg/kg BNP GMX1778. Tumor-bearing mice were imaged and analyzed as described in ref. 25. Briefly, mice were intraperitoneally injected with d-luciferin (150 mg/kg) and imaged on an IVIS Spectrum In Vivo Imaging System (PerkinElmer) 10 minutes post-injection every week for 6 weeks in total. Quantification of BLI flux (photons/second) was determined by analyzing the intensity for the region of interest of each tumor.

Statistical analysis

All statistical tests were performed using GraphPad Prism software (RRID:SCR_002798). IC50 for short-term cell viability assays was calculated using a nonlinear regression fit, log[inhibitor] versus normalized response with variable slopes. All NAD+ quantification and drug retention study statistics were done using student t tests using a Gaussian distribution, unpaired. mRNA expression via qPCR statistics was determined using a one-way ANOVA test comparing all to the control. Statistical differences in ONL thickness were found through also using a one-way ANOVA test comparing all to control. Statistics for tumor efficacy study were performed using ANOVA with the last data points for each group that was compared to the free GMX1778 control. Kaplan–Meier statistical analysis was performed for the survival study, evaluating survival rate based on removal of samples from the study (body weight loss exceeding 20%) or death. All groups were again compared to Free GMX1778 and made by a log-rank Mantel-Cox test with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. NS: not significant (P > 0.05); *, P < 0.05; **, P < 0.01; ***, P < 0.001; ****, P < 0.0001.

Results

Creation and optimization of nanoparticles encapsulating GMX1778

We began by testing various biocompatible polymers or copolymers capable of effectively encapsulating the NAMPTi, GMX1778. We found that PLA-PEG (polylactic acid – polyethylene glycol) was effective at encapsulating the drug with high yields and desired physical properties, hereby referred to as PLA-PEG GMX1778 (Fig. 1A; refs. 23, 26). NP formulation suitability was evaluated using two key parameters: (i) hydrodynamic diameter and (ii) ζ-potential. A hydrodynamic diameter > 100 nm would ensure local retention of particles at target site and a ζ-potential < −10 mV results in a low probability of NP aggregation in vivo (27). Using DLS, we found optimized NPs to have an average size of 109 nm, and an average charge of −14.6 mV, which are within the parameters for desired NP creation (Fig. 1B). Next, we established whether particle size and charge remain constant for a prolonged period. To this end, we incubated the particles for 21 days in artificial cerebrospinal fluid to recapitulate the environment in which the nanoparticles will be infused. We found little change in either parameter, indicating that the drug-encapsulated NPs are stable under intracranial conditions (Fig. 1C). To evaluate the stability of the NPs, we measured the release of drug from the NPs over time in the presence of either PBS or the hydrophilic surfactant Tween 80. With PBS, we found minimal release of the drug payload over time. However, in the presence of Tween 80, we observe approximately 50% of drug is released over a 2-week period (Fig. 1D). Lastly, we tested the retention of these optimized PLA-PEG GMX1778 NPs in vivo in healthy mouse brain tissue (Fig. 1E). We performed an intracranial single-dose injection of 5 mg/kg of either free or PLA-PEG GMX1778 via CED. We then measured the concentration of drug extracted from the local brain tissue at the indicated times. Importantly, we found that the PLA-PEG GMX1778 formulation allowed for greater drug retention—which was reflected in higher tissue concentrations at day 1 and 5—compared to free drug.

In vitro use of NP-GMX1778 reduces cell viability and NAD+ concentrations in multiple glioma models

We and others have identified PPM1D and IDH1/2-mutant cancer cells exhibiting NAPRT loss. This loss is attributed to hypermethylation of the CpG island located on the NAPRT promoter (Fig. 2A; refs. 11, 12). Because cells lacking NAPRT cannot convert NA to NAD+, they have greater dependence on NAMPT-mediated conversion of NAM to generate NAD+ coenzymes. Blocking NAMPT activity through inhibitors such as GMX1778 allows for selective cell killing of NAPRT-silenced cell lines via NAD+ depletion (Fig. 2B and C). On the basis of this knowledge, we evaluated the in vitro efficacy of PLA-PEG GMX1778 NPs through three cell-based assays: (i) cellular uptake with Di-I-filled PLA-PEG nanoparticles (Fig. 3A and B) and (ii) NAD+ quantification (Fig. 3C and D) and cell viability (Fig. 3E–G). Utilizing immunofluorescence and flow cytometry to probe for Di-I, we assessed the ability of the NPs to penetrate NHAs and the glioma cell line, U251, over time. We found that the NPs were taken up by both cell lines within 48 hours of treatment (Fig. 3A and B).

We next performed NAD+ quantification studies with the PLA-PEG GMX1778 to determine whether the expected NAMPTi-induced depression of NAD+ occurs. In the PPM1Dtrnc. NHAs model, NAD+ is already depressed from lack of NAPRT expression, inactivating the Preiss–Handler salvage pathway (11). We confirmed these results and demonstrated that free GMX1778 exhibits the expected NAD+ depletion in both cell lines (Fig. 3C). To rule out confounding effects with the NP polymers, we also treated cells with empty PLA-PEG NPs and found no differences in NAD+ levels in either cell line. We observe the expected depression of NAD+ with PLA-PEG GMX1778 demonstrating NAMPT activity consistent with free GMX1778 (Fig. 3D).

We tested the cytotoxic effects of PLA-PEG GMX1778 in PPM1Dtrnc. cells. To first test for any inherent toxicity of the PLA-PEG NPs on our cell line models, we performed a short-term cell viability test and found that there was no impact on cell survival (Fig. 3E). Significantly, we found that the NPs showed cell-killing activity with IC50 ∼1.2 nmol/L as we and others (11, 12, 28) have found with GMX1778 in the presence of NA (Fig. 3F).

We next hypothesized that the PLA-PEG GMX1778 formulation would demonstrate increased potency if allowed to incubate in cells for a longer time period as per our observations in Fig. 1D. To test this, we performed a long-term CSA. NHA and PPM1Dtrnc. cell lines were treated with either free drug or PLA-PEG GMX1778, with or without 10 μmol/L NA supplementation, and incubated for 2 weeks (Fig. 3G). We observed that the PLA-PEG GMX1778 retained selectivity against PPM1Dtrnc. cells and similar potency compared to the free drug.

We also established the efficacy of the NPs in the U87 IDH1(R132H)-mutant isogenic glioma model. This mutation leads to 2-HG production, which has been shown to increase DNA methylation throughout the genome (12), resulting in NAPRT silencing (Supplementary Fig. S1A and S1B). Using this model, we performed similar experiments as in Fig. 3A–G to validate the in vitro efficacy of the NPs in the IDH1-mutant context. We found that the PLA-PEG GMX1778 demonstrated (i) incorporation into cells within 48 hours (Supplementary Fig. S1C and S1D); (ii) NAD+ depression (Supplementary Fig. S1E and S1F); and (iii) cytotoxicity in both short-term cell viability and long-term CSAs (Supplementary Fig. S1G and S1H). Flow cytometry analysis on the U87s revealed a bimodal distribution of the cell population, which is hypothesized to occur from the diverse morphology of this cell line (29). Together, we demonstrate that the PLA-PEG GMX1778 formulation is effective across various NAPRT cell lines with highly comparable potency to the free NAMPTi.

NAPRT status determines free and NP-encapsulated NAMPTi sensitivity

We next aimed to evaluate NAPRT expression across multiple GBM cell lines to potentially extend the clinical utility of the PLA-PEG GMX1778 formulation beyond PPM1D and IDH1-mutant cell lines. Through mRNA and protein expression analysis, we found diverse NAPRT expression across three GBM cell lines. These included LN229, U251, and T98G. LN229s were NAPRT expression positive (NAPRT+), whereas both U251 and T98G lack NAPRT expression (NAPRT−) but all are wild type for both PPM1D and IDH1/2 (Fig. 4A and B).

Using this information, we probed NAD+ concentrations in the NAPRT− cell lines following NP and free drug treatment and showed that both caused similar depression in NAD+ levels (Fig. 4C and D). Given this, we next investigated whether NAPRT expression in these cell lines would predict NAMPTi sensitivity. We treated the cells with NAMPTi with NA supplementation in a short-term cell viability assay. We found, like PPM1Dtrnc., that U251 and T98G show potent cell kill with NAMPTi, but because LN229s are NAPRT+ they are resistant to both free drug and PLA-PEG GMX1778 (Fig. 4E and F). Last, similar to results in Fig. 3G, we observe that NAPRT− cell lines are as sensitive to PLA-PEG GMX1778 as the free drug, providing further evidence that the PLA-PEG GMX1778 nanoparticles retain the NAMPTis therapeutic benefit (Fig. 4G–I).

Increasing bio-adhesivity of NPs augments the potency of GMX1778

Currently, NAMPTis suffer from repeated, highly concentrated dosing regimens to sustain effective concentrations in patients, but these treatments are highly toxic (22). Our long-term survival assays with PLA-PEG GMX1778 have demonstrated that prolonged incubation of the NAMPTi-filled NPs with cells leads to greater potency in vitro. Given this, we considered the use of an alternate polymer with bio-adhesive properties to further increase the potency of our NP GMX1778 formulation. Recently, our laboratories have reported on the viability of using oxidized PLA-HPG polymers in the encapsulation and release of different drugs both in vitro and in vivo (30, 31). When PLA-HPG polymers are oxidized with sodium periodate, the vicinal diol end groups on the HPG are converted to aldehyde end groups that bind strongly to exposed moieties on proximal cells and tissues. Referred to as bio-adhesive NPs, these NPs have shown greater retention at the injected site (30–32). Following the creation of BNP-encapsulated GMX1778 (BNP GMX1778), we first tested the in vitro activity of the formulation through three cellular assays in the PPM1Dtrnc. isogenic cell lines. We found that the BNP GMX1778 formulation was taken up by cells through immunofluorescence and flow cytometry experiments (Supplemental Fig. S2A and S2B), was capable of depressing NAD+ (Supplemental Fig. S2C) and causing a reduction in cell viability both in short- and long-term cell viability assays when supplemented with NA (Supplemental Fig. S2D–F). As such, the BNP GMX1778 formulation retained the potency of the free NAMPTi in vitro.

In vivo administration of NP GMX1778 limits toxicity and increases overall survival

After the successful in vitro characterization of two GMX1778 NP formulations, we tested these formulations in mouse glioma models. One major drawback of free NAMPTi administration includes dose-limiting hematological and retinal toxicities, specifically thrombocytopenia and retinal degeneration. We hypothesized that the local drug retention achieved by the NP formulation would limit these toxicities. We compared the effects of intracranial injection of free or NP (PLA-PEG and BNP) GMX1778 on blood cells and retinal tissue in mice. Free GMX1778 and PLA-PEG were administered at 100 mg/kg concentrations, whereas BNP GMX1778 was administered at 50 mg/kg due to viscosity restraints with using a stereotactic injector. After drug injection, blood and retinas were collected from mice at designated time points for toxicity analysis (Fig. 5A). We found that there were no differences in weight (Fig. 5B), or WBC (Fig. 5C), RBC (Fig. 5D), or platelet (Fig. 5E) counts found in either free or NP versions of the drug, finding no hematologic toxicity.

To assess retinal toxicity, retinas were cryosectioned and stained with DAPI and WGA after drug treatment (Fig. 5A). We measured the thickness of the outer nuclear layer (ONL) of the cross-sections, which has previously been shown to be reduced through GMX1778-induced toxicity (23, 33). As a positive control, free GMX1778 was administered orally to mice once a day (QD) for 4 days before retinas were sectioned (Fig. 5F), to allow for the expected reduction in ONL thickness. With our CED-treated cohort, we found no significant differences in ONL thickness between the treatment groups, including the free GMX1778 group, which indicates that this delivery method circumvents the retinal toxicity (Fig. 5G). Altogether, the intracranial delivery of the NPs likely did not cause any systemic or retinal toxicity owing to the inability of the NPs to pass the BBB or the blood-retina barrier.

Finally, we evaluated the antitumor efficacy of the NP GMX1778 formulations in NAPRT– intracranial xenografts. To allow for tumor visualization and monitoring, we developed a cell line expressing a bioluminescent (BLI) reporter in U251 cells (Fig. 6A). We intracranially implanted the U251+BLI cells in mice, and, 1 week later, drugged with either vehicle (PBS), free GMX1778 (100 mg/kg), PLA-PEG GMX1778 (100 mg/kg), or BNP GMX1778 (50 mg/kg). Strikingly, we found both PLA-PEG GMX1778 and BNP GMX1778 were able to significantly reduce tumor size as compared to free GMX1778, which led to an overall increase in mouse survival (Fig. 6B–D). Furthermore, we found that increased bio-adhesivity of the NP formulation was able to improve overall potency, as evidenced through tumor growth and survival rate differences between PLA-PEG and BNP formulations (Fig. 6E). Together, we demonstrate that nanoparticle encapsulation of GMX1778 constitutes a highly effective therapeutic approach in NAPRT– tumors that circumvents previously observed systemic and retinal toxicities associated with NAMPTi usage.

Discussion

Here, we report a NP drug delivery strategy for NAMPTis delivered intratumorally through CED. While substantial progress has been made in development of effective treatment options for central nervous system cancers, there is still a dire need for better therapeutic modalities that extend the survival rate for these patients (6, 34). These tumors are difficult to treat with current standard-of-care for these tumors typically being fractionated radiotherapy followed by temozolomide treatment with or without surgical resection when possible. Unfortunately, reoccurrence frequently occurs (35). The use of more targeted therapies presents an opportunity to effectively treat these tumors, limiting the possibility for reoccurrence (36). For instance, NAMPT inhibitors exhibit potential as curative treatments for these cancers so long as NAPRT status was to serve as a treatment-qualifying biomarker. However, existing NAMPTis suffer from severe toxicity and poor delivery to the brain (37–39). It is hoped that NP formulations presented herein provide a rational path to development of improved drugs for NAPRT− glioma in humans.

NP-loaded drugs are being tested in clinical trials with increasing frequency, with currently over 500 ongoing trials for various indications and diseases (clinicaltrials.gov). One advantage of these drug delivery systems is the sustained retention of the NP and slow release of the drug at the target site, which we have observed for encapsulated NAMPTi in our experiments. A mechanistic explanation for this has previously been suggested by the observation that severe short term depression of cellular NAD+ can be insufficient to kill cells (40) while sustained depletion of NAD+ over multiple days results in cell killing (41). Thus, NP formulations that allow for prolonged exposure of the drug and hence prolonged reduction in NAD+ levels, have the potential to improve efficacy. This was particularly evident for the BNP formulation used in this study, which has been previously shown to persist for up to 2 weeks at sites of administration, whereas PLA-PEG has been shown to persist only up to ∼5 days at sites of administration (30, 42). Moreover, these NPs are invisible to immunosurveillance, making them uniquely powerful vehicles for drug delivery (9, 30, 31, 43, 44). There are aspects of NPs that do require further optimization (30–32). For example, stereotactic injection of high concentrations of the PLA-HPG polymer was precluded by the highly viscous nature of this formulation. Nonetheless, our data demonstrate that increasing the bio-adhesivity of the formulation leads to greater tumor regression and survival benefit compared to the PLA-PEG NPs and free GMX1778 treatment (Fig. 6C–E).

The other major advantage offered by our NP formulation is the significant reduction in the dose limiting toxicities associated with NAMPTis. These toxicities have largely limited the use of NAMPTis in the clinic (19, 21, 40). Through this study, we demonstrate that these toxicities could be circumvented through targeted delivery and local retention of the drug. By designing NPs that are retained at the site of infusion, we prevented leakage back into systemic circulation and prevented toxicity in the ONL of the retina (Fig. 5). By utilizing the bio-adhesive properties of nanoparticles, we present evidence that even poorly tolerated therapies can be reconsidered for clinical efficacy if the drug delivery strategy is optimized.

Finally, our results reinforce NAPRT status as a major predictor of NAMPTi sensitivity. Interestingly, pan-cancer epigenome studies reveal that NAPRT silencing is frequently observed in cancers found in lung, soft tissue, and breast (45). While mutations in IDH1/2 and PPM1D have been shown to cause NAPRT promoter methylation, there are many cases where the underlying cause of NAPRT silencing is unknown. As observed in this study, the U251 and T98G cell lines are NAPRT− (Fig. 4A and B) but have no known defects in either IDH1/2 or PPM1D, underscoring additional mechanisms of NAPRT silencing. Uncovering these addition mechanisms that induce NAPRT silencing will be critical in expanding the use of NAMPTi approaches to other cancers. Further, exploring previously reported synergistic combinations of NAMPTi with chemotherapies such as temozolomide or PARP inhibitors could further enhance the efficacy of our approach (46, 47). Even with our monotherapy NAMPTi NP formulation however, we report significant reductions in tumor burden and overall survival advantages in intracranial mouse glioma xenograft models. Overall, this research has the potential to revive NAMPTis clinically, by suggesting an optimized delivery strategy that leads to safe and efficacious treatment of GBMs and other gliomas.
