Differential role of CREBBP missense and truncating mutations in germinal center development and lymphomagenesis
Authors: Chuanjiang Yu, Mara Holloman, Andrew Kim, Yunchao Chang, Stefanie N. Meyer, Antony B. Holmes, Bowen Cai, Tongwei Mo, Katia Basso, Kostiantyn Dreval, Ryan D. Morin, Govind Bhagat, Charles G. Mullighan, Riccardo Dalla-Favera, Laura Pasqualucci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•GC-specific expression of a CREBBP KAT missense mutation leads to GC hyperplasia, memory B-cell expansion, and clonal lymphoproliferation.•CREBBP KAT domain missense and truncating mutations induce distinct transcriptional changes in the GC.

Introduction

Diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) and follicular lymphoma (FL), the most common hematologic malignancies,1 are heterogeneous diseases derived from the clonal expansion of B cells in the germinal center (GC).2,3 Despite the significant advances made in the understanding and treatment of these diseases, one-third of patients with DLBCL do not achieve durable remissions, and FL remains largely incurable, with prognosis being dismal in ∼20% of patients whose otherwise indolent disease transforms into a high-grade malignancy.4,5 Therefore, further efforts are needed to improve the management and cure rates of these cancers.

Over the past decades, the analysis of the genomic landscape of DLBCL and FL has identified multiple genetic alterations of pathogenic significance, each associated with distinct lymphoma subtypes.6, 7, 8 Among the identified lesions, mutations of the CREBBP gene have emerged as the second most common alteration in both FL (60% of cases) and DLBCL (60% of cases belonging to the EZB/C3 genetic cluster of GCB-DLBCL).6,7,9, 10, 11, 12, 13 CREBBP encodes for a lysine acetyltransferase (KAT) that facilitates transcription by acetylating lysine residues on histones (H3K27 and H3K18) and nonhistone proteins involved in the control of cell identity and cell fate decisions. Lymphoma-associated CREBBP mutations include truncating events that abrogate the KAT domain, generally resulting in the complete loss of protein expression, and missense mutations clustering in the KAT domain, which similarly inactivate the protein enzymatic activity without significantly impairing its expression as a full-length protein.10,14 Both mutation types represent early (truncal) events acquired by a common mutated precursor cell (CPC) prior to its final clonal expansion, which, in the context of FL transformation, occurs before a genetically divergent evolution leads to FL or to an aggressive form of DLBCL known as transformed FL (tFL).11,15, 16, 17

Previous work using conditional GC-specific Crebbp knockout (CrebbpKO) mice to mimic the consequences of truncating mutations documented a role for Crebbp as a haploinsufficient tumor suppressor gene. In particular, although the loss of Crebbp alone is insufficient to drive lymphomagenesis, it cooperates with the deregulation of Bcl2 (commonly resulting from its chromosomal translocation) to promote the development of human-like FL and DLBCL.18,19 Mechanistically, CREBBP loss contributes to the malignant transformation process by impairing the acetylation of enhancer histones as well as nonhistone substrates. These include the GC master regulator BCL6, which is negatively modulated by acetylation10; the tumor suppressor p53, which requires acetylation for its activity20; and the KMT2D methyltransferase, whose acetylation enhances its enzymatic function.14,21 Consequently, select transcriptional programs required for GC exit are attenuated in CrebbpKO GC B-cell models, although changes are modest, due, in part, to the compensatory activity of the Crebbp paralog p300.22 In contrast, the potentially specific in vivo role of missense mutations targeting the CREBBP KAT domain remains unknown. Notably, although both event types lead to the loss (truncating mutations) or functional impairment (missense mutations) of the protein enzymatic activity, recent observations suggest that they play distinct pathogenic roles. In particular, missense mutations are differentially represented in FL vs DLBCL13 and, importantly, leave a largely intact protein that could maintain physiologic interactions, hindering compensatory mechanisms by other acetyltransferases such as p300.22,23

In this study, we contrasted the function of missense and truncating mutations in vivo, using a conditional, GC-specific knockin mouse model designed to recapitulate a highly recurrent Crebbp amino acid change, along with CrebbpKO mice. The results indicate that the missense mutation has shared but also unique biological and transcriptional outputs, leading to skewed GC B-cell fate decisions toward memory B-cell differentiation, the development of lymphoproliferative diseases (LPDs), and differential sensitivity to CREBBP-targeted drugs.

Methods

A detailed Materials and methods description is provided in the supplemental Methods (available on the Blood website).

Mouse models and strains

The conditional CBPfloxed strain18,24 and the Cγ1Cre knockin25 mouse model have been described previously. The conditional CrebbpRH knockin allele was generated by C.G.M.

Cell lines

Isogenic CREBBPKO and CREBBPRH DLBCL cell lines (SUDHL4 and U2932) were engineered by using the Alt-R CRISPR-Cas9 System (Integrated DNA Technologies) as reported previously26 and detailed in the supplemental Methods.

CREBBP genetic data

The CREBBP mutation pattern in human FL, tFL, and DLBCL was obtained by integrating data generated in our previous work, resulting in a total of 472 FL, 185 tFL, and 302 DLBCL primary cases, along with 29 DLBCL cell lines.10,11,13,27, 28, 29

Mouse immunizations

For the analysis of T-cell dependent immune responses, age-matched 3- and 6-month old mice were immunized by intraperitoneal injection of sheep red blood cells (SRBC) in phosphate-buffered saline or 100 μg of 4-hydroxy-3-nitrophenylacetyl hapten conjugated to keyhole limpet hemocyanin (NP-KLH) along with Complete Freund’s Adjuvant and analyzed at day 10 or 42 (memory B cells).

Multicolor flow cytometry

Analysis of the B- and T-cell compartment was performed as previously reported,30 using the antibodies in supplemental Table 9.

Histological and immunohistochemical analysis of mouse tissues

Analysis of mouse lymphoid organs was performed on formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue sections, stained with hematoxylin and eosin or specific antibodies listed in supplemental Table 9, following standard procedures.31

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis

GC B-cell transcriptional profiles were obtained using total RNA extracted from sorted B220+CD95highPNAhigh splenocytes. Bioinformatics analyses are detailed in the supplemental Methods.

Protein extraction and immunoblot analysis

Protein expression studies were performed following previously described protocols.14,32,33

Drug dose response assays

The response to CREBBP/p300 small-molecule inhibitors were determined by the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega).22

Statistical analyses

Data analyses were performed using the R statistical language and packages specified in the supplemental Methods or the GraphPad Prism 9.0 software (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).

Results

Differential distribution of CREBBP missense and truncating mutations in FL and DLBCL

To model the consequences of missense mutations in vivo, we comprehensively assessed the frequency of somatic events targeting CREBBP in a collection of 472 FL, 185 tFL, and 302 DLBCL primary biopsies from our own studies,10,11,13,27, 28, 29 along with 29 cell lines, focusing on those with predicted functional consequences (supplemental Methods). Truncating mutations were prevalent in DLBCL (63.3% of all events, almost exclusively in EZB/C3 GCB-DLBCL, vs 26.8% in FL: P < .0001), whereas the opposite was true in FL, in which missense mutations targeting the KAT domain comprise ∼70% of sequence variants (80% of mutated cases) (Figure 1A-B; supplemental Figure 1; supplemental Table 1).13 DLBCL arising from tFL showed a pattern similar to FL, as expected for this truncal genetic alteration,11,12,15,16 suggesting selective pressure for a preferential role of missense mutations in the malignant transformation process leading to FL/tFL. Compared with CREBBP truncating mutations, which are monoallelic in most cases, hemizygous/homozygous missense mutations leading to the exclusive expression of a full-length mutant protein were present in 35% of the affected samples (Figure 1C; supplemental Table 1; supplemental Methods). Consistent with prior data10 and AlphaFold2 prediction models, R1446 mutants exhibited marked reduction in acetyltransferase activity, albeit with some variability across different substrates (Figure 1E-G). In particular, R1446H, whose side-chain configuration most closely resembles that of the wild-type (WT) protein, retained the highest residual enzymatic activity across multiple substrates, including H3K18, H3K27, p53, and self-acetylation. Collectively, these data suggest that the full-length CREBBP protein in missense mutant cases may have distinct, noncatalytic functions that are absent in the case of complete protein loss.

Construction of conditional GC-specific Crebbp-R1447H knockin mice

To investigate the in vivo role of CREBBP missense mutations in B-cell lymphomagenesis, we generated a conditional knockin mouse model in which the recurrent R1446H mutant allele (R1447H in mice, hereafter CrebbpRH) is expressed specifically in GC B cells, under the control of its endogenous promoter (supplemental Figure 2A; supplemental Methods). Mice were bred with the Cγ1-cre deletor strain, in which the Cre recombinase, driven by the Cγ1 promoter, is induced in response to immunization with T cell–dependent antigens (supplemental Figure 2B-C).25 Reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction and sequencing of Crebbp in unimmunized and SRBC-immunized mice documented the expression of the WT allele in splenic B220− (non-B) and B220+CD95−PNA− (pre-GC) B cells (ie, before Cre-mediated recombination), indicating there was no leakage of the manipulated allele outside the GC B-cell compartment (supplemental Figure 2D left). In contrast, the efficient conditional activation of the mutant allele was detected in GC B cells sorted from mice carrying the knockin allele in heterozygous (hereafter referred to as CrebbpRH/+) and homozygous (CrebbpRH/RH) configuration (supplemental Figure 2D right panel; supplemental Figure 2E). Bulk RNA-seq and immunoblot analysis confirmed comparable Crebbp transcript and protein levels across all genotypes, indicating that the CrebbpRH protein is stable (supplemental Figure 2F-G), and showed reduced acetylation of H3K18 and H3K27, confirming the impaired enzymatic activity in CrebbpRH/RH mice (supplemental Figure 2H-I). Consistent with the GC-restricted recombination, the percentages of immature B, transitional B, and mature pre-GC B cells were comparable in the bone marrow and spleen of Crebbp+/+, CrebbpRH/+, and CrebbpRH/RH littermates, demonstrating the absence of detrimental effects by the manipulated allele in these compartments (supplemental Figure 3).

CrebbpRH and CrebbpKO mutations drive distinct GC B-cell responses

To assess whether expression of the enzymatically defective R1446H protein is equivalent to complete protein loss, we studied the GC B-cell response in 3-month-old mice 10 days after immunization with SRBC, when the GC reaction reaches its peak, contrasting the phenotype of CrebbpRH mice with that of a synchronously established, age-matched GC-specific CrebbpKO cohort. We found a significant, gene dosage–dependent expansion of the B220+PNAhighCD95high GC B-cell population in both CrebbpRH/+ and CrebbpRH/RH mice compared with that in WT littermates (4.3% and 5.7%, respectively, vs 3.3%; P < .05 and < .0001; Student t test) (Figure 2A-B,D). In contrast, consistent with previous reports,18,22 the overall percentage of GC B cells was modestly affected in CrebbpKO mice (Figure 2C-D). The expanded GC B-cell population in CrebbpRH/RH mice was not due to a higher number of GCs, although a trend could be observed, but rather to the formation of GCs that were significantly larger in size, with a consequent increase in the overall GC area (P < .01; Student t test) (Figure 2E-F). In addition, CrebbpRH/RH, but not CrebbpKO mice, displayed a significant proportion of residual GC B cells when analyzed 42 days after NP-KLH immunization, at which point the GC reaction is largely extinguished (1.5% vs 0.9% in WT and KO mice; P < .01; Student t test) (Figure 2G). Crebbp missense and truncating (protein-loss) mutations exerted opposing effects on the ratio between the GC dark zone (DZ), where centroblasts proliferate at high rates and undergo somatic hypermutation (SHM) of immunoglobulin (IG) genes, and the light zone (LZ), where centrocytes are selected based on antigen affinity to either differentiate into plasma cells and memory B cells, reenter the DZ, or undergo apoptosis.34 In particular, the GCs of CrebbpRH/RH mice displayed an abnormal polarization manifesting as an increased proportion of CD86highCXCR4low centrocytes (P < .001) and a diminished DZ:LZ ratio (mean: 1.5 vs the typical ratio of ∼2 in WT controls) (Figure 3A-B; supplemental Figure 3O). In contrast, this ratio was significantly increased in CrebbpKO mice at the expense of LZ B cells (P < .01). The absolute number of DZ B cells was not reduced, whereas LZ B cells were significantly elevated in CrebbpRH/RH mutant mice (Figure 3C-D). Such marked LZ expansion was not due to enhanced proliferation nor reduced apoptosis, as assessed by the analysis of annexin V (supplemental Figure 4A-B) and 5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation, which identifies actively dividing cells. Finally, CrebbpRH/RH mice showed a significantly higher fraction of immunoglobulin M (IgM)-expressing GC B cells, a phenotype opposite to that observed in the CrebbpKO model (IgM+: ∼70% vs 50%, and IgG+: ∼5% vs ∼20%; P < .0001; Student t test; Figure 3F-G). We conclude that missense mutations endow the Crebbp protein with distinct properties, leading to differences in both the magnitude of the GC reaction and the dynamics of GC progression.

By applying DESeq2 to the RNA-seq data of each cohort, we identified 432 downregulated genes in CrebbpRH/RH cells (q < 0.05; fold change ≥ 1.2) (supplemental Table 2), of which 149 were commonly downregulated in the KO cells (Figure 4B; supplemental Table 3), consistent with the known function of CREBBP as a transcriptional coactivator and with the impaired enzymatic activity induced by both truncating and KAT missense mutations.10 A cross-comparison of the signatures obtained from the 2 mouse models using gene set enrichment analysis 2 (GSEA2)38 demonstrated high concordance among the top differentially expressed genes and their relative ranking (P < .05 and P < .01; Figure 4C). However, a remarkably large number of unique changes were also detected, suggesting nonoverlapping functional consequences (Figure 4B).

When we investigated these profiles for enrichment in specific signaling pathways/biological programs, GSEA revealed major differences between the 2 genotypes in the relative representation of discrete GC B-cell states. In particular, signatures reflecting the transitional state of cells migrating from DZ to LZ and committed to GC exit were attenuated in CrebbpKO cells, whereas CrebbpRH/RH and CrebbpRH/+ cells were enriched in LZ-associated (eg, Cd83, Batf, and Nfkbia) and prememory B-cell signatures (eg, Ccr6, Klf2, Gpr183, and Zeb2)39, 40, 41 (Figure 4D-F; supplemental Tables 4 and 5). Accordingly, Crebbp loss hindered signaling pathways typically activated in the LZ, such as antigen presentation and processing through major histocompatibility complex II (MHC II),16 genes modulated by the IRF4 and BLIMP1 plasma cell master regulators, and ribosomal proteins, in line with impaired plasma cell differentiation (Figure 4G; supplemental Figure 5E; supplemental Tables 4-6).42, 43, 44 In contrast, these programs were not significantly affected in the presence of the mutant CrebbpRH protein. Fluorescence-activated cell sorting analysis of surface MHC II confirmed its negligible reduction in CrebbpRH cells (supplemental Figure 5F; supplemental Table 7). Instead, the expression of CrebbpRH led to a significant enrichment in transcripts controlled by Bach2 (supplemental Figure 5I) and by the transcriptional modulator Tbl1xr1 (Figure 4H; supplemental Figure 5H), both of which are implicated in GC B-cell differentiation toward memory B cells40,45,46 (see also supplemental Tables 4 and 5). Together, these data reveal significant transcriptional reprogramming by the missense mutant protein and suggest that, whereas Crebbp deletion interferes with plasma cell differentiation,18,19 the presence of KAT domain mutations favors prememory B-cell expansion in the GC LZ.

CrebbpRH skews GC exit toward memory B-cell differentiation

The upregulation of memory B-cell precursor markers prompted us to directly investigate whether the expression of the R1446H mutant results into skewed post-GC B-cell differentiation. To this end, we first analyzed the expression of surface CCR6 in the GC B-cell population, revealing a significantly higher fraction of CCR6+ cells in CrebbpRH mice (Figure 5A). We then analyzed the 2 cohorts 42 days after NP-KLH immunization, which allows to monitor the generation of NP-binding memory B cells. We found a significant increase in the memory B-cell compartment, identified as CD138−CD38+CD95−CD19+ NP+ cells (P < .05; Student t test; Figure 5B-C), whereas this was not detectable in the CrebbpKO animals (Figure 5B,D). In particular, CD80+CD273+ B cells were preferentially expanded, whereas double negative cells were comparable to WT, with similar trends in CrebbpKO mice (Figure 5E,F). CrebbpRH expression in GC B cells leads to increased TFH cells

The GC response is limited by the number of T follicular helper (TFH) cells that can deliver survival signals to B cells in the LZ; conversely, increases in the number of TFH cells can disrupt GC homeostasis, leading to autoimmunity and expanded GCs.47, 48, 49 We therefore wondered whether Crebbp missense mutations amplify the GC response by providing feedback to the T-cell microenvironment and subverting the interaction between B and TFH cells. Fluorescence-activated cell sorting analysis in SRBC-immunized, 3- and 6-month-old CrebbpRH mice revealed a significant and dose-dependent accumulation of CD4+ T cells coexpressing CXCR5 and PD-1, indicating an expanded TFH population (6% of CD4+ T cells at 3 months and 8.3% at 6 months of age vs a stable 3.5% in WT mice; Figure 6A-C). A trend toward increased TFH cells was also observed in the CrebbpKO mice, but was not statistically significant (Figure 6A bottom and Figure 6D-E). Although the higher percentage of TFH cells could indirectly reflect the formation of hyperplastic GCs and further studies are needed to distinguish these scenarios, many of the transcripts deregulated in CrebbpRH B cells generate surface receptors and mediators implicated in the cross talk between B and T cells during adaptive immunity, including inducible T-cell costimulator ligand (Icosl) and the cytokine receptors Il4ra, Il6ra, Il2rb, and Tnfrsf13c/Baffr (Figure 6F). CrebbpRH mutation is sufficient to initiate LPDs

The individual loss of Crebbp in the GC is insufficient to drive lymphomagenesis in vivo, which requires the cooperation of Bcl2 deregulation.18,19 Given the distinct phenotype observed in CrebbpRH mice, we examined whether the expression of the mutant protein could facilitate the development of tumors in the absence of concurrent Bcl2 expression. Mice were monitored for lymphoma incidence over 15 to 18 months of age and analyzed by histology, immunophenotype, and clonality. Although no significant differences were observed in overall and event-free survival (not shown), 50% of CrebbpRH mice showed splenomegaly by 12 months of age, suggestive of a lymphoproliferative process (P < .05; Figure 7A). Moreover, 31% (5/16) of CrebbpRH/RH and 11% (3/27) of CrebbpRH/+ mice displayed a disrupted splenic architecture due to the presence of histologically defined LPDs harboring clonally rearranged IG genes, supporting their premalignant nature, alongside 1 FL and 1 DLBCL (Figure 7B-E). In contrast, no pathologies were identified in the CrebbpKO mice. Although the low tumor penetrance and limited cohort size preclude statistical significance, the occurrence of overt LPDs and B-cell lymphomas was over fourfold higher than the background observed in Crebbp+/+ mice (n = 2/28 [7%]). Finally, we investigated the expression profiles of genetically annotated human FL and DLBCL samples for enrichment in the signatures identified in our mouse models (n = 112 and 145 samples, respectively, with 43 GCB-DLBCL). Despite potential confounding effects from nontumor cells in the biopsy, FL (not shown) and GCB-DLBCL carrying missense mutations displayed coordinate downregulation of genes suppressed in CrebbpKO and CrebbpRH murine GC B cells compared with that in WT samples (Figure 7F). These signatures were also significantly depleted in DLBCL missense mutant cases relative to those harboring truncating mutations, validating the mouse data as relevant to the human disease (Figure 7F left panels). The fact that the CrebbpKO signature is also preferentially lost in these samples supports our conclusion that CrebbpRH affects both shared (loss-of-function) and unique (noncatalytic functions) transcriptional programs.

CREBBPRH and CREBBP loss induce differential sensitivity to CREBBP/p300-targeted drugs

We previously showed that the p300 paralog represents a liability in CREBBP-deficient lymphoma cells,22 and recent reports suggest a role for CREBBP KAT mutants in interfering with p300-mediated compensation.23 Therefore, we tested whether the loss of CREBBP protein and expression of the missense mutant protein differentially affect the sensitivity of lymphoma cells to 2 selective CREBBP/p300 inhibitors (no p300-specific inhibitors are currently available). We engineered isogenic SUDHL4 and U2932 DLBCL cell lines carrying WT, R1446H mutant, and truncated alleles leading to the loss of protein expression (CREBBPKO) (supplemental Figure 6A-D) and treated them with CU329 (a KAT domain inhibitor)50 and CCS1477, a clinical grade bromodomain inhibitor currently in clinical trials for hematologic malignancies,51 which we have previously shown to induce cell cycle arrest in CREBBPKO cells.22 Treatment with submicromolar concentrations of CCS1447 (100nM) and CU329 (50nM) was preferentially toxic to CREBBPKO relative to CREBBPWT cells (P < .001; Student t test; n = 4 independent clones each; Figure 7G-H; supplemental Figure 6E-G: green and black curves). However, CREBBPRH/RH cells were significantly more sensitive to these drugs than both CREBBPWT and CREBBPKO cells (Figure 7G-H; supplemental Figure 6E-G: red curve; P < .0001 and P < .01, respectively). Therefore, the expression of the missense mutant protein enhances sensitivity to acetyltransferase inhibition.

Discussion

Missense mutations in the KAT domain represent most somatic events targeting CREBBP in FL and are also detected in a fraction of DLBCL samples, yet no in vivo studies have addressed their role in the pathogenesis of lymphoma. Our work reveals that missense and truncating (loss of protein) mutations are not functionally equivalent; rather, they modulate partially nonoverlapping transcriptional programs implicated in divergent GC B-cell fate decisions, with missense mutations leading to skewed memory B-cell differentiation, TFH-cell expansion, clonal LPDs, and higher sensitivity to CREBBP/p300 inhibitors. These findings provide insights into the early steps of lymphomagenesis, with implications for the understanding and therapeutic targeting of FL and DLBCL, as well as of other cancers in which CREBBP is recurrently mutated.

GC B cells expressing CrebbpRH, but not those lacking Crebbp, were enriched for genes implicated in the commitment to memory B-cell differentiation, with a parallel increase in the production of memory B cells. The significant expansion of this population, coupled with the skewed distribution of missense mutations in FL vs DLBCL, aligns with prevailing models of FL pathogenesis that infer IgM+ BCL2-translocation+ memory B cells as potential precursors of the disease.4,52 These findings are also consistent with recent single-cell studies that uncovered memory-like FL populations in human biopsies,53, 54, 55, 56 although the fraction of CD80−CD273− cells, which has been shown to be prone to GC reentry,57 was comparable to WT. Considering that this potential was revealed upon adoptive transfer experiments, future studies should address whether the CrebbpRH memory B cells are primed to reenter the GC and/or whether this requires the expression of Bcl2 or other players.

CREBBP missense mutations (all within the KAT domain) could be detected in the circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) of apparently healthy BCL2-translocation+ individuals as early as 12 years before FL diagnosis,17 indicating that the advantage conferred to the tumor precursor cell is subtle. This is consistent with the low tumor penetrance and mild phenotype observed in our CrebbpRH mice, as well as in several mouse models recapitulating mutations of epigenetic modifier genes, often requiring a sensitizing background. We propose a model whereby CREBBP missense mutations favor GC exit as long-lived memory B cells, fueling the reservoir of initiating tumor cells that could be repeatedly engaged in secondary GC reactions,58 where the acquisition of additional genetic alterations drive their final clonal expansion. In contrast, truncating mutations have a more subtle effect, in part by decommissioning plasma cell–associated enhancers18,19,59; this would expose the B cell to the persistent genotoxic activity of SHM and class-switch recombination, favoring the development of DLBCL.

Interestingly, a recent study identified 2 genetically distinct subgroups of FL, in which CREBBP KAT missense mutations represented the strongest distinguishing feature: a so-called “constrained FL,” highly enriched for these mutations and less likely to undergo transformation to DLBCL, and a “diffuse FL,” characterized by a higher frequency of AID-mediated aberrant SHM and higher risk of transformation.13 Notably, the mutations enriched in the memory-like FL are nearly identical to the defining genetic features of the “constrained” FL subtype.

Toward the mechanistic understanding of the differences between the 2 mutation types, a major consideration is that, although both impair the protein enzymatic activity, the truncated CREBBP proteins are generally not expressed whereas missense mutations do not significantly compromise protein abundance, maintaining a full-length protein expressed at physiologic levels10 (and supplemental Figure 2G). We have previously shown that p300 can partially compensate for CREBBP loss and that GC B cells lacking Crebbp are addicted to the residual p300 activity.22 Therefore, the distinct phenotype observed in CrebbpRH mice may result from the ability of the enzymatically inactive protein to hinder p300-mediated compensatory mechanisms, either by retaining physiologic interactions with associated transcription factors (ie, acting as a sink) or by maintaining occupancy at its target proteins, with consequent redistribution of acetylation. This model is supported by recent observations demonstrating increased loading of p300 on chromatin in CREBBP-null DLBCL cell lines over isogenic cells engineered to carry point mutant alleles.23

Given that both missense and truncated proteins lack or exhibit minimal acetyltransferase activity, it is conceivable that the differences between the 2 strains are attributable to other (noncatalytic) functions of CREBBP, which are preserved in the missense mutant. This hypothesis is in line with recent reports of noncanonical, nonenzymatic roles for other histone-modifier proteins.60 An alternative, possibly complementary, explanation may involve an active function of p300 as a compensatory player in cases with truncating mutations. Although highly related, these paralogs have nonoverlapping roles in the fine-tuning of GC cell-state–specific enhancers/superenhancers, with CREBBP being preferentially involved in the upregulation of LZ genes and the commitment to plasma cell differentiation, and p300 loss preferentially affecting the expression of DZ-associated genes.22 Therefore, the differences imposed by missense vs truncating mutations may be due to the unique function of p300 when intervening to substitute for Crebbp protein loss.

The lack of MHC II downregulation in CrebbpRH GC B cells was unexpected given prior studies16,23,59 and may reflect differences in context (normal GC B cells in vivo vs transformed DLBCL cells), mutation type (R1446H vs R1446C), or the presence of a p300-disruptive mutation in the previously published model cell lines,23,59 which could enhance the phenotype. Indeed, CREBBPRH induced a milder MHC II reduction compared with that produced by CREBBP loss in p300-WT DLBCL lines (supplemental Figure 7). Therefore, the distinct combinations of mutations and/or their timing may shape target gene regulation and prime cells toward divergent fates.

Finally, the finding that RH-mutant lymphoma cells were more sensitive to CREBBP/p300 inhibitors, related to cells in which CREBBP is not expressed, has therapeutic implications. GC-derived B cells require a minimum level of acetyltransferase activity (provided by CREBBP/p300), below which survival is impaired.22 We surmise that the CREBBP mutant protein, which hinders the compensatory activity of p300 by either trapping transcription factor partners23 or preventing its binding to chromatin, lowers the threshold for KAT inhibitors-mediated toxicity, sensitizing the cells to these drugs. This result aids the development of strategies targeting the CPC, the elusive precursor of both FL and DLBCL. In this context, the mouse models presented here represent valuable tools for the preclinical testing of novel treatment approaches targeting the distinct biology arising from these 2 mutants.
