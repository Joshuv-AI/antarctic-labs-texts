Cytokinin-mediated trichome initiation in Nicotiana benthamiana following Agrobacterium tumefaciens infiltration
Authors: Alejandro Brand, Robert Säbel, Dario Esposto, Gerd U Balcke, Frank Syrowatka, Stephanie Krüger, Claudia Horn, Sylvestre Marillonnet, Alain Tissier
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Agrobacterium tumefaciens is widely used in plant genetic engineering owing to its tumor-inducing (Ti) plasmid, which contains a transferable T-DNA region and a virulence (vir) region encoding a type IV secretion system (Lacroix and Citovsky, 2019). In biotechnology, disarmed strains lacking oncogenes are routinely used together with binary vectors that carry genes of interest for stable or transient transformation. Agrobacterium-mediated leaf infiltration of Nicotiana benthamiana is an efficient method for transient gene expression. One commonly used disarmed strain is GV3101, which harbors the Ti plasmid pMP90. Within the vir region, this plasmid contains the trans-zeatin synthase (tzs) gene, which triggers the production and release of the cytokinin (CK) trans-zeatin (t-zeatin) upon infection (Beaty et al., 1986). Exogenous CK application mimics several effects observed with GV3101(pMP90) (hereafter referred to as GV3101+Ti), including induction of stromules, repositioning of plastids around the nucleus, and accumulation of soluble sugars and starch (Erickson et al., 2014).

While performing routine transient expression experiments with GV3101+Ti, we observed a striking macroscopic phenotype: a marked increase in leaf trichome density within the infiltrated areas. This effect became visible to the naked eye approximately 7 days post-infiltration (dpi) and was absent in leaves infiltrated with EHA105 or AGL-1 (Figure 1A). The induced trichomes were glandular trichomes (GTs) that produce and secrete acylsugars (ASs), sticky specialized metabolites involved in stress protection. Here, we demonstrate that GV3101+Ti triggers de novo GT formation in N. benthamiana leaves and that this response is mediated by CK production from the pMP90 Ti plasmid.

To determine whether trichome induction is specific to GV3101 + Ti, we infiltrated N. benthamiana leaves with GV3101+Ti and several other commonly used strains, including LBA4404, EHA105, AGL-0, and AGL-1. At 10 dpi, GV3101+Ti induced approximately 3-fold higher trichome density in the infiltrated areas than in untreated or mock-infiltrated controls, whereas the other strains did not increase trichome density (Figures 1B and 1C; Supplemental Figure 1A). By 14 dpi, some strains, particularly AGL-0, caused yellowing and senescence in the infiltrated tissue, whereas GV3101+Ti areas remained green (Supplemental Figure 1A). Microscopic time-course observations revealed progressive trichome initiation following GV3101+Ti inoculation, starting as early as 2 dpi, when pavement cells began to bulge and round glandular tips emerged. By 5 dpi, clearly elongated trichomes were visible (Figure 1D; Supplemental Figure 3). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) further showed that new trichomes frequently emerged from neighboring cells or even from the basal cells of existing trichomes (Figure 1D), indicating that de novo initiation can occur in different epidermal cell types. The extent of trichome induction varied among leaves and was more pronounced in younger leaves and on the adaxial surface (Supplemental Figure 2). Trichome development was further assessed by quantifying AS levels, revealing an approximately 2-fold increase in GV3101+Ti-infiltrated samples (Figure 1E). To test whether trichome induction was caused by the presence of the pMP90 plasmid or the GV3101 chromosomal background, we generated a Ti plasmid-cured GV3101 strain (GV3101-Ti). Trichome density and AS levels in GV3101-Ti-infiltrated areas remained comparable to those in the controls (Figures 1B, C, and 1E). A key distinguishing feature of pMP90 is the presence of the tzs gene. Consistent with this, t-zeatin levels rose within 24 h after infiltration with GV3101+Ti and remained elevated at 5 dpi, accompanied by an accumulation of t-zeatin-O-glucoside, an inactive form of t-zeatin (Supplemental Figure 4). An LBA4404 strain transformed with a binary vector carrying tzs (LtZ) (Erickson et al., 2014) induced trichome densities comparable to those induced by GV3101+Ti and doubled AS levels, demonstrating that CK production via tzs is sufficient for this effect (Figures 1B, 1C, and 1E; Supplemental Figure 4). Similarly, a C58 strain harboring the tzs gene within its Ti plasmid was recently reported to induce trichome formation, yielding trichome numbers comparable to those induced by GV3101+Ti (Chen et al., 2026). Direct infiltration of synthetic CKs (t-zeatin, kinetin, and 6-benzylaminopurine [BAP]) reproduced the phenotype, although to a lesser extent: t-zeatin and BAP each caused an approximately 2-fold increase in trichome density and elevated AS levels, whereas kinetin produced a smaller but significant trichome increase without significantly affecting AS content (Figures 1F–1H).

CKs regulate cell division and leaf expansion and cooperate with auxin in controlling meristem formation and maintenance. Although the roles of jasmonic acid and auxin in trichome development are well established, the contribution of CK signaling remains less explored. In Arabidopsis, which bears only non-GTs, ectopic expression of the Agrobacterium isopentenyl transferase gene, which encodes a CK biosynthesis enzyme, induced trichome formation on carpels (Greenboim-Wainberg et al., 2005). In tomato, which produces both GTs and non-GTs, exogenous BAP specifically induced GTs, including types with uni- and multicellular heads, whereas jasmonic acid had broader effects across all trichome types (Maes and Goossens, 2010). These studies highlight the distinct regulatory mechanisms by which CKs and other phytohormones control different trichome types and their associated metabolism across species.

To gain deeper insight into the molecular basis of Agrobacterium- and CK-induced trichome formation, we performed RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) on leaves infiltrated with GV3101-Ti vs. GV3101+Ti at 1, 2, and 3 dpi. Principal-component and differential expression analyses revealed a gradual divergence between the two strain treatments over time (Supplemental Figures 5A and S5B). The number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) increased over time. Using thresholds of |log2 fold change| > 1.5 and adjusted p < 0.05, we identified 319, 474, and 605 DEGs at 1, 2, and 3 dpi, respectively (Supplemental Figures 5B and S5C; Supplemental Tables 1–5). Gene Ontology enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed significant overrepresentation of terms related to cellular responses to hormone stimulus and hormone-mediated signaling pathways (Supplemental Figure 5D). Among the top DEGs were multiple CK catabolic genes (e.g., CK dehydrogenase/oxidases (CKXs)) as well as core components of the CK signaling pathway, including histidine kinase (AHK)-like genes and several Arabidopsis response regulator-like genes (ARRs) (Figure 1I; Table S6).

Trichomes are epidermal outgrowths. In N. tabacum and other species, multicellular GTs arise from enlargement of protodermal cells, followed by asymmetric cell divisions (Tissier, 2012). Thus, trichome morphogenesis involves dynamic cell wall remodeling, beginning with wall loosening and followed by wall thickening. Consistent with this, a gene annotated as expansin B15-like was significantly upregulated as early as 24 h after infiltration with GV3101+Ti (Figure 1I; Table S6). Expansins are positively regulated by auxin and CK and play a key role in cell wall loosening, thereby promoting cell expansion (Samalova et al., 2024). Furthermore, genes encoding several peroxidases (PRX), an xyloglucan endotransglucosylase/hydrolase, and a protodermal factor 1-like protein were differentially expressed across the three time points, supporting roles for cell wall remodeling and expansion in trichome morphogenesis. To our knowledge, the genes that link CK signaling and trichome development have not been characterized in tobacco. We observed consistent upregulation from 1 dpi onward of a cyclin gene, a growth regulating factor gene, and the MIXTA-like transcriptional regulator gene MYB106, which was previously implicated in GT initiation and trichome branching in tomato (Li et al., 2026) (Figure 1I; Supplemental Tables 5 and 6). The HD-zip transcription factor Woolly (Wo), a master regulator of multicellular trichome morphogenesis in both tomato and tobacco, displayed progressive upregulation from 1 dpi (Figure 1J; Supplemental Tables 7 and 8) (Wu et al., 2020, 2023). This upregulation coincided with marked downregulation of NbJAZ3, which encodes a repressor of NbWo, and strong induction of its target, the NbMIXTA2 transcription factor (Yan et al., 2022), which showed an approximately 7-fold increase at 3 dpi (Figure 1J). NbMIXTA2 shares high sequence similarity with the tomato MX1 (Supplemental Table 8), a key determinant of cell fate in capitate trichomes, which are analogous to the GTs of N. benthamiana (Wu et al., 2023). Notably, both NbWo and NbMIXTA2 are induced later (primarily at 3 dpi) than the early CK-responsive genes, indicating that trichome development genes are likely indirect targets of CK signaling. Numerous additional candidate regulators showed significant expression changes at one or more time points; their potential roles in trichome morphogenesis warrant further investigation (Supplemental Table 7).

In conclusion, CK-producing Agrobacterium strains induce GT initiation and AS accumulation in N. benthamiana. Transcriptional changes induced by GV3101+Ti infiltration support a role for CKs in triggering de novo trichome morphogenesis from epidermal cells. However, whether cell cycle re-entry is required for this process remains unresolved. This trichome induction phenomenon, which may have gone unnoticed despite the widespread use of GV3101(pMP90) in plant laboratories, provides a novel experimental system for dissecting the molecular control of cell fate and the contribution of CKs to trichome development and specialized metabolism. Because N. benthamiana leaf infiltration by Agrobacterium is routinely employed for a wide range of assays—including pathway reconstitution and disease resistance studies—we recommend routinely comparing infiltration with GV3101(pMP90) and a non-CK-producing strain such as LBA4404 to determine whether CK production, GT induction, or AS accumulation influences experimental outcomes.
