Tissue-level RBE determination and normal lung responses along the proton SOBP
Authors: Xiaoxin Zuo, Xiangkai Guan, Anhang Zhang, Yungang Wang, Liangjie Yuan, Zheqi Li, Jian Zhu
Published: 2026

Background

As an advanced radiotherapy technique, proton therapy has been widely applied in tumor treatment [1]. Compared to photons, protons exhibit a favorable depth-dose distribution with a maximum energy deposition at the end of the ion range, the so-called Bragg peak, which enables high-dose concentration in the tumor region while minimizing radiation exposure to surrounding normal tissues [2, 3]. This dosimetric advantage significantly improves tumor control and reduces radiation-induced toxicity. In addition to this physical advantage, protons are generally assumed to demonstrate an approximately 10% higher biological effectiveness than photons.This means that it is assumed that protons can achieve the same therapeutic effect as photons while using a 10% lower dose of protons. This assumption is based on early radiobiological studies conducted in the 1970s and 1980s using a limited number of predominantly acute endpoints, low-kVp X-rays as reference radiation, and proton irradiation mainly at the center of the SOBP. These studies provided a conservative estimate of the relative biological effectiveness (RBE) [4–6]. In clinical practice, a fixed RBE value of 1.1 is commonly adopted to facilitate dosimetric calculations and ensure a conservative margin for tumor control [7–9].

However, studies have shown that this fixed RBE assumption is not constant across different energy ranges, tissue types, Linear Energy Transfer (LET), dose per fraction, position in the spread-out Bragg peak (SOBP) and biological endpoint [10–13]. The use of a uniform RBE therefore represents a conservative simplification that may lead to unintended dose escalation in normal tissues, potentially increasing the risk of treatment-related toxicity [14]. Indeed, uncertainties in proton range and RBE variability have been associated with adverse clinical outcomes in some patients receiving proton therapy [15–18]. Specifically, the distal region of the SOBP is characterized by a sharp increase in LET, which can result in a local elevation of RBE beyond the conventional value of 1.1 [11]. According to a survey by the European Particle Therapy Network, most particle therapy centers reduce the potential risks arising from enhanced distal edge effects by ensuring that the particle beam does not terminate within sensitive organs and by avoiding the overlap of distal edges from multiple beams [19].

Moreover, subsequent investigations of proton RBE have largely relied on in vitro systems or macroscopic endpoints, such as cell survival or gross tissue damage [20–23]. In contrast, histological and cellular-level responses of normal lung tissue to proton irradiation, especially across different regions of the SOBP, remain incompletely characterized. To address this gap, we established a radiation-induced lung injury model in rats to investigate the histological dependence of proton RBE on LET. Specifically, we evaluated the effects of different regions within the proton SOBP on type I and type II alveolar epithelial cells (ATI and ATII), as well as on inflammatory responses, including transforming growth factor-β1 (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{TGF}-\beta 1$$\end{document}) and mast cells, thereby providing in vivo histological insight into LET-dependent normal lung tissue responses to proton irradiation.

Methods

Animals

A total of 290 specific pathogen-free (SPF) female Sprague–Dawley (SD) rats aged 8–9 weeks were used in this study (Beijing Huafukang Bioscience Co., Ltd.). Six animals were included in each irradiation group, while eight animals were included in the control group. Animals were housed in the animal facility of the Shandong Proton Center Laboratory and acclimatized for three days before radiation. Rats had free access to standard chow and water and were maintained under controlled environmental conditions (temperature: 20–\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$22^{\circ}\textrm{C}$$\end{document}; relative humidity: 50–55%; 12-h light/dark cycle). All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Shandong Cancer Hospital (Protocol No. SDTHEC202412021) and were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional guidelines and the ARRIVE guidelines. Prior to irradiation, animals were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 2% pentobarbital sodium (45 mg/kg; Sigma-Aldrich, Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany). Four weeks after irradiation, rats were deeply anesthetized using the same protocol until loss of consciousness was confirmed by the absence of the pedal withdrawal reflex and markedly slowed, regular respiration, followed by exsanguination as a secondary method. Death was confirmed before thoracotomy.

Irradiation procedure and dosimetry

All experiments were conducted at the Proton Center of Shandong Cancer Hospital. Proton irradiation was performed using a Varian ProBeam accelerator (Varian Medical Systems, USA). A SOBP with a width of 9 cm and an energy range of 72–136 MeV (corresponding to a water-equivalent depth of 4–13 cm) was used at a dose rate of 1.7 Gy/min. Rats were positioned supine, and whole-lung irradiation was performed at four distinct positions within the SOBP: entrance, proximal, central, and distal (Fig. 1A–D). Dose delivery for proton irradiation was verified prior to exposure. A PPC05 detector (IBA, Germany) was used to confirm the absolute dose. Beam flatness and symmetry were assessed using EBT3 Gafchromic films (GafChromic® EBT3, Ashland Specialty Ingredients, NJ, USA). Beam range was evaluated using a Zebra ionization chamber (IBA, Germany). In addition, during 16 Gy irradiation at the proximal, central, and distal SOBP regions, thermoluminescent dosimeters (TLDs; LiF:Mg,Ti) were placed bilaterally in the lung region of the rats to verify local dose deposition. The corresponding results are provided in the Additional file 1. These measurements supported the accuracy and feasibility of dose delivery in the present experimental setup.

Despite these verification procedures, residual dose uncertainties remain unavoidable in proton irradiation experiments conducted in vivo. In the present study, the overall dose uncertainty was mainly driven by proton range uncertainty. Contributing factors included respiratory motion–induced variations in lung position and water-equivalent thickness along the beam path, geometric uncertainties associated with the use of stacked solid water slabs (e.g., slight gaps or misalignments between adjacent layers), and uncertainties in the conversion from CT Hounsfield units to stopping power ratio (SPR) during treatment planning (typically on the order of 1–1.5%).

Histological assessment and biological endpoint

For regional analysis, the right caudal lung lobe was selected as the region of interest because its dimensions (approximately \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1.4 \times 1.0~\textrm{cm}$$\end{document}, with a maximal thickness of about 0.7 cm) provided a relatively large and uniform parenchymal area for consistent and reproducible sampling. In contrast, other lobes, particularly the left lung adjacent to the heart and the right cranial lobe close to the hilar bronchovascular structures, may be more susceptible to variability arising from organ motion and anatomical complexity. The selected lobe was harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h, followed by dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$4\,\mu\,\textrm{m}$$\end{document}. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E; Beyotime, C0105s, Shanghai, China). Whole-slide images were acquired using a 3DHISTECH Pannoramic Slide Scanner (3DHISTECH Ltd., Budapest, Hungary) and examined using Slide Viewer software.

Histological evaluation was performed blinded to the experimental groups, according to the histological scoring system recommended by the American Thoracic Society [24]. Five randomly selected fields per section (200×) were evaluated. The scoring system comprised five parameters: (A) neutrophils in the alveolar lumen, (B) neutrophils in the interstitium, (C) hyaline membrane formation, (D) proteinaceous debris filling the alveolar lumen, and (E) thickening of the alveolar septa. The final lung injury score was calculated as: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eqalign{ & {\rm{Lung injury score}} \cr & \,\,\, = {{(20 \times A) + (14 \times B) + (7 \times C) + (7 \times D) + (2 \times E)} \over {{\rm{number of fields}} \times 100}} \cr}$$\end{document}

A lung injury score ≥ 0.8 was defined as the biological endpoint of the study.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

IHC staining was performed to identify ATI and ATII, and to evaluate \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{TGF}-\beta 1$$\end{document} expression in radiation-induced lung injury. Paraffin-embedded lung sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in Tris–EDTA buffer (PR30002-PTG, Wuhan, China). Endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide, followed by blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA, biofroxx).

Sections were incubated overnight at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$4^\circ\mathrm{C}$$\end{document} with primary antibodies against AQP5 (ab315855, Abcam; 1:8000), SFTPC (10774–1-AP, Proteintech; 1:200), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{TGF}-\beta 1$$\end{document} (ab315254, Abcam; 1:1000), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (RGARO11-PTG, Proteintech). Immunoreactivity was visualized using DAB substrate (ZLI-9018, Zhongshan Golden Bridge, Beijing, China), and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin.

Following dehydration, clearing, and mounting, slides were scanned using a 3DHISTECH Pannoramic Slide Scanner. Three randomly selected fields per section were analyzed at 365 × magnification for AQP5 (ATI) and SFTPC (ATII), and at 200× magnification for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{TGF}-\beta 1.$$\end{document} The positively stained area was semi-quantitatively assessed using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Toluidine blue staining for mast cells

To evaluate mast cell distribution and density in irradiated lung tissue, paraffin-embedded lung sections were stained with toluidine blue (Yuanye, S19058, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Following staining, slides were scanned using a 3DHISTECH Pannoramic Slide Scanner. Three randomly selected fields per section (365× magnification) were analyzed, and mast cells were counted manually. The mean value per section was used for subsequent statistical analysis.

Statistical analysis

Dose–response relationships were analyzed using a binomial logistic regression model, in which the number of responding animals and the total number of animals at each dose level were used as input, with the total number serving as weights. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{ED}_{50}$$\end{document} values were derived from the fitted model. The RBEs were calculated as the ratio of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{ED}_{50}$$\end{document} values for photons and protons, respectively. The standard errors (SE) of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{ED}_{50}$$\end{document} and RBE were estimated using the delta method based on the covariance matrix of the fitted parameters. The 95% confidence intervals (CI) for \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{ED}_{50}$$\end{document} were calculated using the normal approximation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{ED}_{50} \pm 1.96 \times$$\end{document} SE), whereas the 95% CI for RBE were calculated using Fieller’s theorem. Dose–response curve fitting was performed using Python (version 3.10.12). Quantitative data for epithelial cells and inflammatory responses were expressed as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{mean}\pm\textrm{standard}$$\end{document} deviation (SD) and analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Data were assessed for normality, and comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05 (*P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001).

Alterations in ATI and ATII cells along the proton SOBP

ATI cells were marked by AQP5 immunostaining. In the control group, AQP5 staining was continuous and uniformly distributed along the alveolar septa, indicating an intact epithelial structure. Following irradiation at both 16 Gy and 20 Gy, AQP5 staining became reduced and discontinuous in both proton and photon groups, with a more pronounced effect observed in the distal region of the proton SOBP (Fig. 4). Quantitative analysis of three random fields per section confirmed that the AQP5-positive area (% of alveolar epithelial area) was decreased in all irradiation groups at both dose levels. At equivalent doses, the proximal and central SOBP regions showed values comparable to those in the photon group, with no significant differences. Discussion

The present study indicates that proton RBE tends to increase along the 9-cm SOBP in association with increasing LET. Lung injury and inflammatory responses were observed following both photon irradiation and across all investigated positions within the proton beam, with greater severity toward the distal region. Histological analyses further characterized these responses, including loss of ATI cells, elevated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{TGF}-\beta 1$$\end{document} expression, increased mast cell infiltration and morphological alterations of ATII cells, which were more pronounced in the distal high-LET region. By contrast, responses in the proximal and central low-LET regions were broadly comparable to those observed after photon irradiation. Overall, these findings support that proton-induced lung toxicity is spatially heterogeneous within the SOBP and is associated with LET variations, suggesting that the use of a constant generic RBE may underestimate biological effects in distal high-LET regions. In interpreting these findings, it should be noted that the distal edge was not selected for investigation because it is highly sensitive to range uncertainties, which may compromise stable and reproducible dose delivery in lung tissue. Moreover, LET-related biological effects may arise within the SOBP and are not necessarily restricted to the distal edge. Therefore, distal positions within the SOBP, rather than the distal edge itself, were selected to enable a more robust evaluation under relatively stable dosimetric conditions.

Taken together with previous evidence, the sensitivity of proton RBE to variations in LET has been a central question in proton radiobiology. Although proton is generally regarded as low-LET radiation, its LET increases progressively along the beam path and may reach values of approximately 10 keV/μm near the distal end of the SOBP [2, 11]. Higher LET has been associated with greater severity of biological damage across multiple tissues. Consistent with our findings, Saager et al. [25] reported an increase in RBE from 1.13 ± 0.04 at an LET of 1.4 keV/μm to 1.26 ± 0.05 at 5.5 keV/μm, using bilateral hindlimb paralysis as the biological endpoint in a rat spinal cord model positioned at different locations within a 6-cm proton SOBP. Glowa et al. [26] reported that a fixed RBE of 1.1 provides a good approximation of biological effects up to the central region of the SOBP. However, toward the distal high-LET region, the RBE increases markedly and may exceed 1.4 under clinical fractionation conditions. Similarly, Overgaard et al. [22] demonstrated a more pronounced increase in RBE at the distal end of the SOBP compared with its mid-region in mouse skin. At the cellular level, Chaudhary et al. [27] showed that DNA damage responses along most of the SOBP resemble those induced by photons, whereas more complex DNA damage and prolonged repair kinetics occur in the distal SOBP region. In line with these observations, our study showed that RBE increased from 0.89 ± 0.07 at the SOBP entrance (1.89 keV/μm) to 1.21 ± 0.10 in the distal (3.68 keV/μm) region.

At the cellular level, ATI and ATII cells play distinct but complementary roles in radiation-induced lung injury. While ATI cells are highly radiosensitive and form the structural basis of the alveolar–capillary barrier, ATII cells serve as facultative progenitors involved in epithelial repair [28, 29]. The enlarged ATII cell morphology observed in the distal SOBP region may reflect a stress-associated response to high-LET irradiation; however, its functional significance requires further investigation using additional endpoints and longer follow-up. At four weeks after irradiation, the marked loss of ATI cells together with hypertrophy of ATII cells may represent an early phase of epithelial repair, in which ATII cells expand and adapt to compensate for injury before the ATI population is effectively restored.

Alongside epithelial injury and repair processes involving ATI and ATII cells, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{TGF}-\beta 1$$\end{document} plays a central role in radiation-induced lung injury and has been consistently associated with radiation pneumonitis and pulmonary fibrosis [30–33]. In the present study, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{TGF}-\beta 1$$\end{document} expression was significantly higher in the distal region of the proton SOBP than in the photon irradiation group and other SOBP positions, suggesting a stronger tissue stress response in the distal high-LET region [34, 35]. Because LET increases toward the end of the proton range, this region may induce more complex and persistent DNA damage responses, which could in turn contribute to enhanced inflammatory and profibrotic signaling, including increased \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{TGF}-\beta 1$$\end{document} expression or activation [36, 37]. Therefore, the elevated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{TGF}-\beta 1$$\end{document} observed in the distal SOBP region may indicate greater susceptibility of normal lung tissue to proton-induced injury in high-LET areas. However, the present findings demonstrate an association rather than a causal relationship between LET elevation and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{TGF}-\beta 1$$\end{document} upregulation.

In parallel with enhanced epithelial and cytokine responses, mast cells are key innate immune cells involved in radiation-induced lung injury. Previous studies have shown increased mast cell accumulation in irradiated lung tissue, whereas mast cell deficiency attenuates radiation-induced injury [38–41] cell-derived mediators can exacerbate alveolar–capillary barrier dysfunction and may interact with profibrotic signaling pathways. Mast cell infiltration was observed across irradiation conditions and was more pronounced in the distal region of the proton SOBP, accompanied by elevated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{TGF}-\beta 1$$\end{document} expression, consistent with a more pronounced inflammatory microenvironment. The functional interplay between mast cells, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{TGF}-\beta 1$$\end{document} signaling, and DNA damage complexity warrants further investigation.

An emerging issue in proton radiobiology is the extent to which biological responses induced by protons differ from those induced by photons. Overall, tissue responses in the proximal and central low-LET regions of the proton SOBP appeared broadly consistent with those observed after photon irradiation. In contrast, more pronounced or persistent biological effects of protons have been reported in other tissues, including sustained damage in rat oropharyngeal glands [42], enhanced intestinal crypt cell apoptosis [43], and increased neuronal dendritic injury [44]. Together with our observation that epithelial injury and inflammatory activation were confined to the distal high-LET region of the SOBP, these findings indicate that proton–photon biological equivalence is highly context-dependent and influenced by tissue type, irradiation conditions, and spatial LET distribution.

Several limitations should be noted. First, lung injury was assessed at a single early time point (4 weeks post-irradiation), reflecting primarily acute and early subacute responses; therefore, progression to late-stage fibrosis could not be evaluated. As proton- and photon-induced lung injury may differ in latency, RBE estimation based on a single time point should be interpreted with caution. Second, although histological and IHC analyses provided detailed cellular information, functional assessments of lung injury were not included. Third, in this study, the whole-lung thickness along the beam direction was used for dose and LET averaging. Therefore, the reported RBE values should be interpreted as volume-averaged estimates across the irradiated lung region rather than voxel-level distributions, which may obscure spatial heterogeneity in RBE. In particular, because LET increases toward the distal edge of the SOBP, the local RBE at the voxel level may be higher.

In addition, the present study was performed using a single-fraction irradiation scheme, whereas clinical proton therapy is typically delivered in multiple fractions. Previous in vitro studies, along with limited in vivo evidence, suggest that RBE may increase with fractionation, particularly in regions of elevated LET such as the distal edge of the SOBP [45, 46]. This may be related to reduced sublethal damage repair and the increased effectiveness of high-LET radiation. Therefore, the RBE values reported in this study may underestimate the biological effectiveness under clinically relevant fractionated conditions. In particular, the increase in RBE at the distal edge could be more pronounced in clinical settings, which may have important implications for normal tissue toxicity and treatment planning. Further studies incorporating fractionated irradiation schemes are warranted to better characterize these effects in vivo.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings provide in vivo histological evidence that proton RBE increases along the SOBP with rising LET. The enhanced injury observed in the distal high-LET region indicates greater normal lung tissue susceptibility and highlights the spatial heterogeneity of proton biological effects, with important implications for normal tissue protection in clinical proton therapy.
