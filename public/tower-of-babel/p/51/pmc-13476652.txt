Imaging of spinal vascular malformations
Authors: Clinton Schaefer, Monique A. Mogensen
Published: 2026

INTRODUCTION

Spinal vascular malformations (SVMs) are a rare yet important cause of spinal cord dysfunction. These malformations can be classified as shunting or nonshunting, with shunting lesions further divided into arteriovenous fistulas (AVFs) and arteriovenous malformations (AVMs). 1 , 2 , 3 Dural AVFs are the most common shunting type and account for 60%–80% of these SVMs with a mean age of presentation in the sixth decade of life. 1 , 2 Dural AVFs result from a fistulous connection between a radiculomeningeal artery and a radicular vein within the dura. Spinal AVMs also have arteriovenous shunting but contain a vascular nidus. Spinal AVMs typically occur in the third decade of life but may also occur in the pediatric population in association with inherited disorders. 2

Numerous classification systems for SVMs have been developed, but one of the most widely adopted is the Takai classification. 4 It classifies SVMs by both the type of vascular malformation (AVF or AVM) and the location relative to the dura. Using the Takai classification, malformations are divided into five types as follows: Type I: dural AVF, Type II: intradural intramedullary glomus AVM, Type III: intradural intramedullary juvenile AVM, Type IV: perimedullary AVF with subtypes based on single versus multiple arterial feeders, and Type V: extradural AVF with subtypes based on intradural versus extradural venous drainage.

Depending on the type of malformation, there may be an acute, subacute, or chronic presentation. 2 AVMs can present acutely secondary to subarachnoid or intramedullary hemorrhage. 2 , 3 In contrast, dural AVFs rarely cause hemorrhage and typically present with subacute to chronic progressive myelopathy due to venous congestion within the cord (Foix‐Alajouanine syndrome). 2 , 3 Symptoms include motor and sensory deficits, as well as bowel and bladder dysfunction. 1 Although SVMs are pathologically similar to those in the brain, they generally have worse outcomes. 2

The primary treatment goal for AVMs is to prevent future hemorrhagic events, whereas the goal for AVFs is to halt or reverse the progression of myelopathy. 2 Timely and accurate identification by imaging is essential to prevent disease progression and reduce the risk of permanent spinal cord damage. 2 In patients with unexplained myelopathy, spinal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) has high sensitivity for detection of SVMs. 5 The abnormal fistulous or nidal shunt may be treated via a microsurgical or endovascular approach. 1 , 3 Neurologic function prior to surgery is closely correlated with recovery outcomes after treatment. 2 A coordinated, interdisciplinary team approach is needed to address myelopathic symptoms.

IMAGING

Computed tomographic angiography

Computed tomography angiography (CTA) can be used as a screening tool for suspected SVMs prior to catheter digital subtraction angiography (DSA) in patients with contraindications to MRI (Figure 1). 5 The goal of both CTA and MRA is to assess for abnormal vessels to help classify the type of malformation and to locate the level of a feeding artery to guide the DSA procedure, which may reduce the number of selective catheterizations needed. 6 Spinal CTA has been shown to be successful in localizing the feeding arteries, fistulous connection, and draining veins in dural AVFs, however temporal resolution remains inferior to DSA and makes characterizing complicated SVMs more challenging. Although CTA is fast and widely available, it is also limited for evaluation of the spinal cord.

Computed tomography with contrast in an elderly female (patient 1) presenting with progressive lower extremity weakness and urinary incontinence over several months shows enhancing vessels dorsal to the spinal cord (black arrow) within the lower thoracic spine suggestive of a spinal vascular malformation.

Magnetic resonance imaging

MRI is the initial study of choice to evaluate suspected SVMs, because it can assess the spinal cord and may show cord edema, hemorrhage, and enlarged vessels within or surrounding the spinal cord. 1 , 2 , 3 Spinal cord edema resulting from venous congestion appears as T2 hyperintense signal in the cord spanning multiple levels (Figure 2A). 2 , 3 Spinal AVFs (Figure 2A) and AVMs (Figure 3A) may be visible as perimedullary flow voids on T2‐weighted sequences with some AVMs showing intramedullary nidal flow voids. On postcontrast sequences, AVFs typically show dilated, enhancing perimedullary veins (Figure 2B) and AVMs may show enhancing perimedullary and intramedullary vessels (Figure 3B,C). 1 , 2 , 3 Contrast enhanced MRA using time‐resolved technique has superior temporal resolution and can often identify the fistula or nidus and locate the spinal level of the feeding artery, which is crucial information to the interventionalist performing the DSA procedure (Figure 2C). 2 , 3 , 6

Magnetic resonance imaging and time‐resolved magnetic resonance angiogram were obtained in patient 1. Sagittal three‐dimensional T1‐weighted postcontrast sequence (B) shows enhancing, dilated, perimedullary veins (white arrow) that correspond to the flow voids on the T2‐weighted sequence. Coronal time‐resolved magnetic resonance angiogram (C) better demonstrates a prominent vessel (dashed white arrow) associated with an abnormal arteriovenous connection at the left T12 foramen with enlarged arterialized draining veins adjacent to spinal cord (white arrow). Findings are characteristic of a type 1 dural arteriovenous fistula with a left T12 feeding artery.

Magnetic resonance imaging of the spine in a middle‐aged female (patient 2) presenting with progressive back pain and lower extremity paresthesia. Digital subtraction angiography

Catheter DSA remains the gold standard for diagnosing and classifying SVMs. 1 , 2 , 3 DSA provides superior temporal and spatial resolution to better characterize the location and the internal structure of a fistulous connection (Figure 4) or vascular nidus (Figure 5) and determine the vascular supply and venous drainage. 3 Because DSA requires large volumes of iodinated contrast, incurs a higher radiation dose, and is invasive and time consuming, noninvasive angiographic imaging options such as MRA remain important tools in planning the DSA. 2 Some SVMs may be treated by embolization at the time of the DSA.

Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) in patient 1. A catheter is positioned in the left T12 radicular artery. Left T12 radicular arteriogram in early (A) and later (B) posterior–anterior views and lateral (C) view show the T12 radicular feeding vessel (A, dashed black arrow) coursing through the left T12 neural foramen to enlarged, serpiginous veins within the spinal subarachnoid space extending rostrally and caudally (black arrows) consistent with a T12 foraminal dural arteriovenous fistula. The fistula was successfully treated with onyx embolization at the time of the digital subtraction angiography.

Digital subtraction angiography in patient 2. Arterial phase contrast injection (A) demonstrates irregular vasculature with an intramedullary nidus (black arrow). Later arterial phase contrast injection (B) shows arteriovenous shunting with prominent draining veins (black arrows). Findings are most consistent with a type II intramedullary glomus arteriovenous malformation.
