Investigation of the relationship between shock index and BUN/Albumin ratio with clinical outcomes in patients presenting to the emergency department with upper gastrointestinal bleeding
Authors: İsmail Ayan, Melih Yüksel, Mehmet Oğuzhan Ay, Yeşim İşler, Halil Kaya, Mustafa Akar, Hasan Hüseyin Sarkı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate whether the shock index (SI) and blood urea nitrogen (BUN)/albumin ratio (BAR) measured at admission can serve as effective markers for predicting rebleeding and mortality at 28, 90, and 180 days in patients presenting to the emergency department with upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB).Material-MethodIn this single-center prospective study, patients with UGIB admitted to a tertiary care emergency department between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2023, were prospectively analyzed. ROC analysis was conducted to assess the predictive performance of SI and BAR for rebleeding and mortality at 28, 90, and 180 days.ResultsA total of 120 patients who met the inclusion criteria were included in the study. The area under the curve (AUC) values for BAR in predicting mortality at 28, 90, and 180 days were 0.83, 0.79, and 0.81, respectively. The corresponding AUC values for the shock index (SI) were 0.66, 0.61, and 0.62. For predicting recurrent upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB) within 28, 90, and 180 days, the AUC values of BAR were 0.74, 0.73, and 0.73, respectively, whereas the AUC values for SI were 0.57, 0.57, and 0.61. In terms of 180-day mortality, age ≥ 65 years [OR: 6.12 (95% CI, 1.86–20.15), p < 0.001] and the presence of comorbidities [OR: 3.79 (95% CI, 1.28–11.27), p < 0.05] were identified as significant risk factors for recurrent UGIB within 180 days. were identified as significant risk factors for recurrent UGIB.ConclusionIn patients with UGIB presenting to the emergency department, BAR demonstrated the highest predictive performance for recurrent UGIB and mortality at 28, 90, and 180 days. Based on these findings, BAR may serve as an effective marker for the follow-up of these patients.Keywords: Emergency department, Upper gastrointestinal bleeding, Mortality, Shock index, BUN/Albumin ratio

Introduction

Upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB) refers to bleeding originating proximal to the ligament of Treitz. It is a common cause of hospital admissions and ranks as the fifth most frequent gastrointestinal (GI) disorder leading to emergency department visits. In the United States, UGIB is the most common GI condition requiring hospitalization, accounting for more than half a million hospital admissions annually [1, 2]. Early access to endoscopy and advancements in treatment modalities have been shown to reduce mortality rates associated with UGIB [3]. Studies and meta-analyses have reported that mortality rates for UGIB range between 2% and 14% [1, 4, 5].

Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) is a key biochemical marker in UGIB. Both the digestion of blood and hypovolemia lead to reduced renal perfusion, resulting in decreased urea excretion and elevated BUN levels [6]. Albumin is a crucial protein involved in various metabolic functions. In critical illness, alterations in albumin distribution are associated with increased capillary leakage. Disruption of the capillary barrier results in the extravasation of proteins, inflammatory cells, and large volumes of fluid into the interstitial space. Low albumin levels can reduce plasma oncotic pressure, promoting extravascular fluid leakage and hypovolemia [7, 8]. This may increase the risk of shock during gastrointestinal bleeding. The blood urea nitrogen/albumin ratio (BAR) has recently emerged as a novel prognostic marker. Recent studies have demonstrated its association with mortality and poor prognosis in various diseases [9–11]. BAR has also been shown to be a significant predictor of in-hospital mortality in patients with gastrointestinal bleeding [12, 13].

The shock index (SI), defined as the ratio of heart rate to systolic blood pressure, was first introduced by Allgöwer and Burri in 1967. It has been highlighted as a valuable tool for assessing hemodynamic instability, as it provides a simple yet effective mathematical calculation for clinical evaluation [14]. Within this scope, this study aimed to evaluate whether the shock index (SI) and blood urea nitrogen/albumin ratio (BAR), derived from vital signs and laboratory findings at admission, can serve as effective markers for predicting recurrent UGIB and mortality at 28, 90, and 180 days in patients presenting to the emergency department.

Material and method

Study location and patient selection

This study was conducted at the Emergency Medicine Clinic of the University of Health Sciences Bursa Yüksek İhtisas Training and Research Hospital, with approval from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the same hospital (protocol number: 2011-KAEK-25 2022/12 − 05). Patients with upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB) presenting to the Emergency Department of the aforementioned hospital between January 1, 2023, and December 31, 2023, were prospectively analyzed. Informed voluntary consent forms were obtained from patients who agreed to participate in the study. For patients who were unable to give consent, consent was obtained from their relatives.

Inclusion criteria

Patients who presented to the emergency department of our hospital, were diagnosed with upper gastrointestinal bleeding (UGIB) based on medical evaluation, and provided consent to participate in the study were included.

According to the G power analysis, a total of 147 patients were planned to be included in the study based on an effect size of d = 0.3, a power of 0.95, and a margin of error of 0.05.

A total of 196 patients were initially included in the study. However, 7 patients were excluded for not providing consent, 23 for liver dysfunction, 6 for chronic renal failure, 3 for pregnancy, 4 for trauma-related UGIB, and 33 for lower gastrointestinal bleeding. Eventually, a total of 120 UGIB patients who met the inclusion criteria were included in the final analysis.

Demographic information, vital signs (including pulse rate, systolic and diastolic blood pressures), and laboratory data were recorded at the time of initial presentation. The shock index (SI) was calculated by dividing the pulse rate by the systolic blood pressure at the time of admission. The blood urea nitrogen/albumin ratio (BAR) was calculated by dividing the BUN value by the albumin value at admission. Patients’ comorbidities, history of previous gastrointestinal bleeding, ECG findings, emergency room outcomes, and endoscopy results were documented on the case report form. Endoscopy was performed at the University of Health Sciences Bursa Yüksek İhtisas Training and Research Hospital, and peptic ulcer-related bleeding was classified according to the Forrest classification. Consent was obtained from both patients and their relatives, and contact information was recorded. After discharge from the emergency department, follow-up information was obtained through the hospital information management system or by contacting the patients and/or their relatives by telephone on the 28th, 90th, and 180th days.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), released in 2019, was utilized for statistical analyses. Descriptive statistics for numerical variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (minimum–maximum), median, and range and/or interquartile range (IQR), while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was employed to assess the normality of data distribution, and Levene’s test was conducted to evaluate the assumption of homogeneity of variances. For continuous numerical variables that met the assumptions of parametric statistical tests, differences between groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test. In cases where parametric assumptions were not satisfied, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to examine relationships between variables following a parametric distribution, whereas Spearman correlation analysis was used for non-parametric distributions. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis was conducted to assess the predictive value of BAR and the shock index for mortality and rebleeding at 28, 90, and 180 days. A logistic regression analysis was performed to determine the factors associated with 180-day mortality and recurrent UGIB. Results were reported with a 95% confidence interval, and a p-value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Results

A total of 120 patients who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled in the study. The median age of the study population was 67 years (IQR 25–75: 54–76), with 69 patients (57.5%) aged 65 years or older and 86 patients (71.7%) being male. A history of previous gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding was reported in 29 patients (24.2%), while 80 patients (66.7%) had documented comorbidities. The most frequently observed endoscopic finding was Forrest classification type III (n = 28, 23.3%). Among the study cohort, 74 patients (61.7%) required hospitalization, 34 patients (28.3%) experienced mortality within 180 days, and 47 patients (39.2%) had recurrent bleeding within the same period (Table 1).

A Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the shock index and BAR in predicting mortality at 28, 90, and 180 days. The analysis revealed that for the prediction of 28-day mortality, the area under the curve (AUC) was 0.66 [(95% CI: 0.53–0.79), (p < 0.05)] for the SI and 0.83 [(95% CI: 0.74–0.91), (p < 0.001)] for BAR. For 90-day mortality, the AUC value for BAR was 0.79 [(95% CI: 0.69–0.88), (p < 0.001)]. In predicting 180-day mortality, the AUC values were 0.61 [(95% CI: 0.50–0.740, (p < 0.05)] for the SI and 0.81 [(95% CI: 0.73–0.89), (p < 0.001)] for BAR (Fig. 1).

Similarly, in the ROC analysis assessing the predictive value of the shock index and BAR for recurrent UGIB within 28, 90, and 180 days, the AUC value of BAR for predicting recurrence within 28 days was 0.74 [(95% CI: 0.63–0.83), (p < 0.001)]. For predicting recurrence within 90 days, the AUC value of BAR was 0.73 [(95% CI: 0.63–0.83), (p < 0.001)]. At 180 days, the AUC value for the SI in predicting recurrence was 0.61 [(95% CI: 0.50–0.71), (p < 0.05)], while the AUC value for BAR was 0.73 [(95% CI: 0.64–0.82), (p < 0.001)] (Fig. 2).

Discussion

Uncontrolled bleeding is increasingly recognized as a critical cause of mortality in patients with GI bleeding, similar to its impact on patients with multiple trauma. The SI, an effective marker for predicting acute hypovolemia, is particularly useful in cases where systolic blood pressure and heart rate remain within normal ranges. Given its clinical relevance, SI serves as a valuable tool in guiding clinical decision-making processes. Furthermore, the European guidelines for the management of post-traumatic hemorrhage recommend the use of SI to assess the severity of hypovolemic shock [15]. The AUC values of the shock index in predicting mortality vary across studies in the literature on UGIB. In the study by Saffouri et al., the AUC value of the shock index for predicting 30-day mortality in UGIB was reported to be 0.61 [16]. In the study by Alimoglu et al., the AUC value of SI in predicting mortality in UGIB was found to be 0.47 [17]. In the study by Wu et al., the AUC values of the shock index for predicting mortality in patients aged 65 and older, and 75 and older, with UGIB were 0.70 and 0.66, respectively [18]. Additionally, in the study by Saribas et al., the AUC value of the shock index for predicting 30-day mortality in UGIB was 0.73 [19]. In a study conducted by Doğru et al., the SI value for predicting 30-day mortality in patients with UGIB over 65 years of age was found to be 0.91, while the value for predicting 180-day mortality was 0.96 [20]. In our study, the AUC value of the SI for predicting 30-day mortality was 0.66, while for predicting 180-day mortality, it was 0.61. Upon reviewing the existing literature, we observe that different studies report varying AUC values for SI in predicting mortality.

There are limited studies on the use of BAR in the literature, but it has been found to be associated with mortality across various disease groups, beyond UGIB. In a study by Allameh et al., conducted in patients with Fournier’s gangrene, BAR values were significantly different between survivors and non-survivors [21]. Similarly, in the study by Sahin et al., BAR was identified as a significant predictor of mortality in patients with ileus [22]. Ryu et al. demonstrated that BAR was an important predictor of 28-day mortality in patients with aspiration pneumonia [9]. In a study by Akahane et al., high BAR values were found to be an independent prognostic factor for the risk of death in patients with non-HIV pneumocystis pneumonia [10]. Additionally, in their meta-analysis, Ulloque et al. reported that high BAR values were associated with increased mortality risk in COVID-19 patients [11]. Finally, Dundar et al. found that BAR was an independent predictor of in-hospital mortality in patients over the age of 65 admitted to the emergency department [23].

In the study by Bae et al., the AUC value of the BAR for predicting in-hospital mortality in UGIB was found to be 0.77. Additionally, Bae et al. demonstrated that the AUC was higher for predicting in-hospital mortality in individuals over 65 years of age [12]. Similarly, in the study by Kurt et al., the AUC value of the BAR for predicting in-hospital mortality was 0.78 [13]. In our study, the AUC value of BAR for predicting 28-day mortality was 0.82 and it was 0.78 for 90-day mortality. For predicting 180-day mortality, the area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUROC) value was 0.80. Statistically, we found that our study yielded a higher AUROC value, along with improved sensitivity and specificity rates. Furthermore, we established the effectiveness of BAR in predicting both short- and long-term mortality, not only in the elderly population but also across all age groups.

Rebleeding is a significant factor contributing to mortality in patients with UGIB. Approximately 80% of patients experience spontaneous cessation of bleeding, while the remaining 20% require endoscopic, medical, or surgical intervention [2, 24]. Rebleeding occurs in approximately 15% of patients following UGIB and is a critical determinant of mortality [24]. Although widely used scoring systems have demonstrated efficacy in predicting mortality in various studies, their ability to predict rebleeding is generally limited. In the study by Stanley et al., the Progetto Nazionale Emorragia Digestiva (PNED), Glasgow-Blatchford Score (GBS), Rockall score, and AIMS65 scoring systems were compared for their ability to predict rebleeding within 7 days. The AUROC curve for the PNED score was 0.85, while the AUROC values for the other scoring systems were below 0.7 [25]. Similarly, in the study by Liu et al., the ABC, AIMS65, GBS, and pRS scoring systems were compared, with AUROC values for predicting rebleeding within 90 days all found to be below 0.7 [26]. In our study, the AUROC value for the SI in predicting rebleeding within 180 days was 0.60. For BAR, the AUROC values for predicting rebleeding at 28, 90, and 180 days were 0.73, 0.72, and 0.72, respectively. Our findings suggest that BAR demonstrated more effective performance in predicting rebleeding over 28, 90, and 180 days compared to SI.

In our study, we assessed the performance of the SI and BAR scores in predicting rebleeding and mortality in patients with UGIB over 28, 90, and 180 days. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in the literature to evaluate the performance of the BAR score in predicting rebleeding in UGIB. Notably, our findings indicate that the BAR score demonstrated superior effectiveness compared to the SI in predicting rebleeding at 28, 90, and 180 days.

The study has several limitations. Firstly, its single-center design and relatively small sample size represent important limitations. Future research could explore the recurrence of UGIB and mortality in patients with follow-up periods extending beyond 180 days. Another major limitation is the inability to compare the predictive performance of BAR and SI with established scoring systems (e.g., AIMS65, Rockall, and GBS), which are commonly used in the prognostication of UGIB patients. This restricts direct performance comparisons and should be considered when interpreting the clinical implications of our findings. Therefore, BAR and SI should be regarded as complementary tools rather than alternatives to established scoring systems until further validated in prospective comparative studies.

Conclusions

We believe that BAR is effective in predicting both mortality and recurrence of UGIB in patients admitted to the emergency department. Our study found that BAR performed better than the SI in these areas. Additionally, we identified that being 65 years of age or older was an independent risk factor for 180-day mortality, and the presence of comorbidities was an independent risk factor for the development of UGIB within 180 days.

Providing that our findings are corroborated by further studies, measuring routine laboratory parameters such as BUN and albumin levels could offer valuable insight into the risk of rebleeding and mortality in UGIB patients upon admission. These parameters are commonly tested in most laboratories, potentially offering a simple, accessible method to assess patient risk.
