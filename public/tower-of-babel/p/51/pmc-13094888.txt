Empowering nurses through risk management interactive training program: A pre-experimental study of eastern India
Authors: Rashmita Devi, Athar Javeth, Poonam Joshi, K. Jayakrishnan, Rajita Devi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Patient safety in hospitals is crucial to prevent harm, ensure quality care, and build trust between patients and healthcare providers. According to the who report, the incidence of adverse events because of unsafe care is one of the ten leading causes of mortality and disability globally. This study was conducted to assess the effectiveness of interactive training for nurses on patient safety in eastern india. The study was conducted during february 2024. A group of pre-test post-test design was conducted with a sample of 30 nurses working in government medical colleges using the purposive sampling technique. Risk management interactive training was conducted according to the global patient safety action plan 2021–2030 and the who patient safety curriculum guide. The data were collected using the hospital survey on patient safety culture (psc) (version 2.0) And knowledge questionnaire. The data were analyzed using ibm spss version 16. The demographic characteristics of participants as well as the psc were analyzed using descriptive statistics. The difference in mean score between pre- and post-test was analyzed using a paired t-test. A significant improvement in knowledge scores was observed after implementation of the patient safety training program (pre-test: 20.4 ± 3.03, post-test: 22.8 ± 3.03; t = 6.0, P = 0.001). PSC ratings were significantly associated with experience level (χ² = 9.800, P = 0.007) but not with education level (χ² = 5.400, P = 0.067). Investing in practices and systems that focus on improving various aspects of PSC is likely to enhance patient safety. Future research should explore long-term outcomes of patient safety interventions, assessing whether knowledge gains translate into sustained improvements in practice.

Introduction

One of the most important human rights is the right to be safe from hazards and harm in receiving healthcare. According to the World Health Organization’s (WHO) global patient safety action plan 2021–2030, patient safety is defined as “a framework of organized activities that creates cultures, processes, procedures, behaviours, technologies and environments in healthcare that consistently and sustainably lower risks, reduce the occurrence of avoidable harm, make error less likely and reduce its impact when it does occur.”[1]

According to the WHO, quality of care is the delivery of health services to individuals, populations, and patients that enhances the desired outcome.[2] According to the WHO report, the incidence of adverse events because of unsafe care is one of the ten leading causes of mortality and disability globally. Available evidence suggests that 134 million adverse events due to unsafe care occur in hospitals in low- and middle-income countries, contributing to around 2.6 million deaths every year.[2]

It has been reported that unsafe patient care is responsible for the loss of nearly 64 million disability adjusted life years. When the collected retrospective data were analyzed from 24 hospitals in the United States, it was seen that harm reduction strategies could reduce total healthcare costs by US$108 million and generate a saving of 60,000 in patient days.[3]

Nurses’ safety behavior is an indispensable part of improving hospitals’ safety management, ensuring patient safety, and preventing adverse events. It requires the leadership, commitment, and involvement of personnel at all levels, especially frontline healthcare professionals.

Patient safety culture (PSC) is the extent to which an organization’s culture supports and promotes patient safety. It refers to the values, beliefs, and norms that are shared by healthcare practitioners and other staff throughout the organization that influence their actions and behaviors. SC is defined by the UK Health and Safety Commission as the collective product of individual and grouP values, attitudes, perceptions, competencies, and patterns of behavior that determine the commitment to, and the style and proficiency of, an organization’s health and safety management.

In the healthcare sector, PSC is a critical subset of organizational safety culture. It encompasses the shared values, beliefs, and norms among healthcare professionals that influence their actions and behaviors regarding patient safety.[4]

There is a need for healthcare organizations to assess patient safety culture, areas of patient safety requiring attention. The strengths and weaknesses of the healthcare organization’s current safety culture also need to be identified to intervene a change in the safety culture. Assessing the current state of the PSC makes it possible for the hospital to recognize its strengths and weaknesses.[56]

Nurses are the main workforce of the nursing team, accounting for more than 40% of nurses in Southeast Asia. The systematic development and implementation of programs to enhance instructional behavior by nursing managers are essential to improving patient safety and nursing.[78]

Adherence to and compliance with recommendations related to patient safety are influenced by various factors, like willingness, culture, economic and social conditions, and level of knowledge. In a study by Ammouri et al. (2015), it was found that the older the nurses’ ages and the longer their length of service, the higher their professional titles were.[7] Nurses with higher professional titles have a longer length of nursing experience, a stronger awareness of adverse event risk perception, and more experience in addressing emergencies, forming a positive feedback system to promote their safety behavior.[9]

Various studies have applied stepped training for nurses and concentrated on safety behavior and interrupted coping strategies, which are essential elements for patient safety.[10]

Yan J et al. (2022) conducted a quasi-experimental study in which participants underwent a stepwise interactive situated training program on patient safety. There was a significant difference in the pre-test and post-test scores in safety behavior and practice (P < 0.001).[10] The stepwise interactive situated training program was effective at improving young nurses’ safety behavior.[1011]

This study evaluated the current state of PSC as well as the effectiveness of an interactive training session for the nurses on patient safety in eastern India. The study was limited to nurses working in government hospitals of West Bengal during the data collection period.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

Quantitative research approach was used with a group pre-test/post-test design. The study was conducted in February 2024 in a tertiary care hospital in eastern India.

Study participants and sampling

A total of 30 nurses working in different government medical colleges and tertiary care institutes were included in the study. The subjects were selected using the purposive sampling technique.

Inclusion criteria: Nurses working in government medical colleges and hospitals and willing to participate were included in the study.

Exclusion criteria: Nurses who are not available during the study period and who did not complete the training were excluded from the study

Study variables were age, professional experience, area of work, maximum qualification, previous training on patient safety, and the outcome variable was PSC and patient safety knowledge score.

After an extensive review of the literature, the tool for the socio-demographic profile and knowledge questionnaire was prepared.

Socio-demographic profile, which includes personal data of the participants (age, level of education, gender, and years of experience in the unit).

Content validity of the questionnaire was ensured by a panel of experts in the field of nursing and hospital administration.

Patient safety knowledge questionnaire

This tool was prepared for assessing the nurses’ understanding and knowledge of PSC with special emphasis on international patient safety goals. It included a total of 30 multiple-choice questions. The participants were asked to choose the correct response among four options. Each correct response in the questionnaire was given a score of one. There was no negative marking for incorrect responses. The validity of the tool was ensured by a panel of experts from the field of nursing and hospital administration. The content, coverage, clarity, wording, length, format, and overall appearance of the tool were assessed. The content validity index of all items in the tool was more than 90%.

Hospital survey on PSC (version 2.0)

The survey was used to assess nursing staff perception of patient safety culture. In the year 2019, Hospital Survey Version 2.0 was released for use by healthcare institutions. It was adopted from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality’s model. This tool, composed of 42 items, is divided into 12 dimensions. The 12 dimensions of PSC are teamwork within units (4 items), supervisor/manager expectations and actions promoting patient safety (4 items), organizational learning-continuous improvement (3 items), management support for patient safety (3 items), overall perceptions of patient safety (4 items), feedback and communication about error (3 items), communication openness (3 items), frequency of events reported (3 items), teamwork across units (4 items), staffing (4 items), hands-off and transitions (4 items), and non-punitive response to errors (3 items).

Respondents were asked to respond to nine dimensions of PSC (including teamwork within units, supervisor/manager expectations and actions promoting patient safety, organizational learning-continuous improvement, management support for patient safety, overall perceptions of patient safety, teamwork across units, staffing, handoffs and transitions, and response to errors) using a 5-point Likert scale based on agreement levels (ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree).

In addition, the method proposed by the HSOPSC questionnaire designers was applied to compare the results. The questions with negative connotations, “Strongly Disagree” and “Disagree,” were considered negative responses, whereas “Strongly Agree” and “Agree” were considered positive responses. The average percentage of positive responses was calculated by summing the positive scores within the range. It is important to note that this questionnaire was used and validated in a master’s project to evaluate the culture of patient safety in Iranian educational hospitals.[12] The validity of this questionnaire was confirmed, and its reliability was reported to be 82% (Cronbach’s alpha = 0.82).[12]

Intervention

The risk management interactive training program was tailored for nurses based on the WHO Global Patient Safety Action Plan 2021–2030 and the WHO Patient Safety Curriculum Guide. This comprehensive seven-day program included six daily sessions, combining instructor-led teaching with interactive, hands-on activities to foster practical application and deeper engagement.

The training covered diverse modules addressing critical topics, such as contemporary trends and challenges in patient safety, nursing quality indicators, safety in surgical, medication, and diagnostic procedures, hospital-acquired infections (HAI), infection control policies, disinfection practices, safe handling of chemotherapy drugs, hospital equipment safety, and effective communication strategies.

Sessions were facilitated by multidisciplinary experts from medicine, surgery, microbiology, and nursing, ensuring a diverse and thorough learning experience. The hands-on training elements were designed to reinforce theoretical knowledge with practical application.

To enhance experiential learning, scenario-based activities were incorporated, particularly focusing on real-world challenges like medication error prevention, HAI management, and effective communication techniques. These activities provided participants with realistic simulations, enabling them to apply their learning in a controlled, practical setting and improve their decision-making skills in critical situations.

Nurses who were fulfilling the inclusion criteria were identified, and informed consent was taken. Permission to use the hospital survey on patient safety questionnaire (version 2.0) was obtained. PSC was assessed using a hospital survey on patient safety questionnaire (version 2.0) just before the training program at one point in time. The pre-test data were collected from the participants just before the training program at one point in time. Post-test was conducted using the knowledge questionnaire at the end of the training program, only at one point in time.

Ethical considerations

Written permission for conducting the study was obtained from the Departmental Research Committee (DRC) and the Institute Research Committee (IRC). Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the Institute Ethical Committee (IEC). Written permission from the department was obtained. Care was taken during the data collection procedure to ensure there was no interference with the routine care of patients and the activities of the ward. Informed written consent was taken from the subjects. Anonymity and confidentiality of the participants were taken care of and protected while reporting the study.

Statistical analysis method

The data were analyzed using IBM SPSS version 16. The demographic characteristics of participants as well as the PSC were analyzed using descriptive statistics. The difference in mean score between the pre- and post-test was analyzed using a paired t-test. An analysis of variance test was used to compare mean scores between variables. The frequency of reporting adverse events in the last 12 months was calculated by a simple frequency percentage distribution. The various dimensions of patient culture were assessed using the 5-point Likert scale (ranging from Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree). Fisher’s exact test was used to analyze the association of patient safety rating with demographic variables.

The frequency of reporting adverse events in the last 12 months was assessed using a simple frequency percentage distribution. Half (50%) of the participants reported no adverse events, while 23% reported one to two events. Discussion

Patient safety remains a cornerstone of high-quality healthcare, requiring a robust framework of knowledge, attitudes, and practices among nursing professionals. The findings of this study demonstrate a significant improvement in nurses’ knowledge following the implementation of a risk management interactive training program. The increase in post-test scores highlights the effectiveness of structured training interventions in enhancing patient safety awareness and competencies. These results align with previous studies, such as those conducted by Saaid et al. (2020), which found that targeted educational interventions significantly improve nurses’ understanding of patient safety principles (Saaid et al., 2020).[12] Similarly, Agbar et al. (2023) emphasized that structured patient safety education interventions positively impact healthcare professionals’ safety culture and adherence to best practices (Agbar et al., 2023).[13]

The study also revealed that nurses’ perceptions of PSC were strongly associated with their years of experience, a finding supported by Ammouri et al. (2015), who reported that nurses with more clinical exposure and time spent in teaching hospitals exhibited a more developed understanding of patient safety (Ammouri et al., 2015).[7] This association could be attributed to accumulated hands-on experience, greater exposure to adverse event management, and repeated engagement with institutional safety protocols. However, no significant relationship was observed between educational qualification and perceptions of PSC, indicating that theoretical knowledge alone may not suffice in shaping safety-oriented behaviors. This finding underscores the need for ongoing, experience-based learning rather than solely relying on academic credentials to ensure competency in patient safety.

A crucial aspect of PSC is open communication and error reporting, yet this study found that a considerable proportion of nurses had not reported any adverse events in the past 12 months. This trend suggests persistent challenges in fostering a nonpunitive culture, where healthcare professionals feel empowered to disclose errors without fear of retribution. Prior research by Ko and Yu (2017) highlights that leadership support, error feedback mechanisms, and coaching behaviors play a pivotal role in shaping an environment where nurses actively participate in safety practices (Ko and Yu, 2017).[8] Given that only 42% of participants in this study expressed positive perceptions of how management responds to errors, there remains an urgent need for administrative policies that reinforce transparent reporting and continuous learning from near misses and adverse events.

Another notable finding was the varying levels of positive responses across different dimensions of PSC. While teamwork within units and communication openness were rated positively by the majority, other crucial aspects, such as hospital management support and staffing adequacy, received lower ratings. Similar concerns were echoed in the work of Abdelaliem and Alsenany (2022), who found that nurses’ confidence in PSC was contingent on organizational support, the presence of effective communication channels, and opportunities for professional development (Abdelaliem and Alsenany, 2022).[9] The relatively low positive response regarding staffing and workplace conditions in this study suggests that systemic barriers such as workload distribution, resource constraints, and administrative oversight may still pose risks to patient safety. Addressing these challenges requires a holistic approach that not only equips nurses with knowledge but also ensures that institutional policies facilitate safe working environments.

The findings further reinforce that patient safety training must be tailored to address real-world clinical challenges, integrating both theoretical instruction and hands-on learning experiences. Stepwise interactive training models, as demonstrated by Yan et al. (2022), have been particularly effective in enhancing safety behavior among nurses by immersing them in realistic clinical scenarios (Yan et al., 2022).[10] The training program implemented in this study incorporated similar methodologies, including scenario-based activities and instructor-led sessions, which may have contributed to its effectiveness. Such training approaches foster critical thinking, problem-solving, and teamwork, which are essential for sustaining a culture of safety.[14]

Despite the encouraging outcomes, the study’s limitations must be acknowledged. The absence of a control group limits the ability to attribute improvements solely to the training intervention, as external factors such as workplace learning and peer influence may have contributed. Additionally, the relatively small sample size constrains the generalizability of the findings. Expanding the study to a larger and more diverse population would provide a more comprehensive understanding of how different contextual factors influence training effectiveness and patient safety perceptions.

Limitations and Recommendations

Limitations

We acknowledge the sample size limitation; our study findings provide valuable preliminary evidence for intervention effectiveness. However, we have explicitly recommended a larger study with a larger sample size, diverse geographical representation, and a control group. This study serves as an important foundation for large-scale investigation.

Recommendations

A large controlled study with a larger sample size is recommended. A longitudinal follow-up component for measuring whether the knowledge improvements translate to actual safety practice changes with safety metrics by observation of safety practice using a standardized checklist, tracking adverse event reporting rate, analysis of near miss documentation, patient outcome indicators where nurses received training, and safety climate re-assessment at 6 and 12 months post-intervention is also recommended. Also, our study found significant differences in knowledge scores based on qualification level. We recommend tailored approaches based on qualification backgrounds. For higher-qualified nurses, advanced application and leadership components could be emphasized. A customized modular approach while maintaining core content is recommended.

Conclusion

The overall findings reinforce the critical role of continuous education and structured training in strengthening patient safety competencies among nurses. While knowledge acquisition is a vital step, sustaining a culture of safety requires ongoing institutional support, transparent communication, and the active engagement of frontline healthcare workers. Addressing systemic barriers such as staffing constraints, leadership involvement, and organizational responsiveness to errors will be essential in transforming safety culture into an integral component of routine clinical practice. Future research should explore long-term outcomes of patient safety interventions, assessing whether knowledge gains translate into sustained improvements in practice and reductions in adverse events.
