Case Summary

A 72-year-old woman with coronary artery disease status post–coronary artery bypass grafting and end-stage renal disease initially presented with dyspnea, chest discomfort, and persistent wide complex tachycardia (WCT). She was hypotensive and required urgent electrical cardioversion, which was unsuccessful. Clinical uncertainty remained regarding ventricular tachycardia (VT) vs supraventricular tachycardia with aberrancy, because electrocardiographic morphology was inconclusive despite application of the Brugada criteria.1 Intravenous amiodarone restored sinus bradycardia. Despite restoration of sinus rhythm and intravenous diuresis, the patient remained dyspneic with rising troponin levels.

Anticoagulation was briefly interrupted, with planned coronary angiography for non–ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. Before the procedure, WCT recurred. Coronary angiography revealed patent bypass grafts and no culprit ischemia. Shortly thereafter, the patient developed an acute ischemic stroke. Neurology requested a transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) for further stroke evaluation. There was heightened concern that the arrhythmia might represent atrial flutter with rapid conduction and possible left atrial appendage (LAA) thrombus. Because of this uncertainty, we were uncertain about which treatment course to pursue. Using amiodarone could potentially be harmful if the rhythm were atrial flutter and an LAA thrombus was present. Rate-control medications such as atrioventricular nodal blockers and digoxin were options, but these were limited because of the patient’s borderline hypotension and end-stage renal disease.

Discussion

LAA Doppler pattern on TEE was the most useful in this case, showing no evidence of thrombus and revealing atrial mechanical activity inconsistent with atrial flutter. Given these findings, we were able to safely resume amiodarone therapy.

LAA Doppler velocities during TEE directly reflect atrial mechanical activity. In atrial flutter, repetitive atrial contractions produce characteristic systolic emptying and diastolic filling waves at a rate matching flutter activity. In VT, the atria are typically dissociated, producing independent atrial Doppler signals, in this case a quadriphasic signal consistent with sinus rhythm.2

LGE on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging was used to help identify ischemic scar substrate, but it would not provide any help in determining the active tachycardia mechanism.

Variable E’ velocities were noted in this case, which is likely a reflection of atrioventricular dissociation and asynchronous timing between atrial contraction and ventricular relaxation. Mitral inflow Doppler may suggest atrioventricular dissociation, but in marked tachycardia, transmitral E/A waves become fused, which reduces the diagnostic accuracy. Pulmonary vein Doppler may also help with identifying atrioventricular dissociation but was not performed in this case because of the focused nature of the examination in the setting of hypotension and intolerance to sedation.

Coronary angiography defines coronary anatomy and ischemia but does not distinguish supraventricular tachycardia from VT.
