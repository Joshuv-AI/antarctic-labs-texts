Artificial Intelligence–Assisted Design of Plant‐Protein Meat Analogues: Integrating Nutrition, Functionality, and Fibrillation‐Based Texturization
Authors: Amir Amiri, Klaudia Masztalerz, Srishty Maggo, Anika Singh, Vladislav Korolev, Anubhav Pratap‐Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPlant proteins provide nutritional value and techno‐functional performance, yet their predominantly globular structures often form isotropic gels that lack the fibrous, anisotropic, and water‐retaining network of animal muscle. This review examines plant‐protein meat analogue design as a multi‐objective challenge requiring the simultaneous optimization of nutrition, digestibility, functionality, texture, sensory quality, scalability, and sustainability. It first summarizes the nutritional benefits and limitations of plant proteins, including amino acid balance, digestibility, allergenicity, anti‐nutritional factors, and health‐related outcomes. It then reviews extraction and techno‐functional modification strategies, followed by fibrillation‐based texturization technologies such as high‐moisture extrusion, shear‐cell processing, 3D printing, spinning, and amyloid‐like fibril formation. Across these stages, artificial intelligence is positioned as an integrative design layer linking protein selection, processing conditions, rheology, microstructure, texture, nutrition, and sustainability. Overall, AI‐assisted workflows may accelerate the development of structurally convincing, nutritionally credible, and industrially scalable plant‐protein meat analogues.

Introduction

Carbohydrates, lipids, and proteins are fundamental macronutrients that serve distinct physiological roles. Carbohydrates function primarily as the body's main energy source, while lipids act as long‐term energy reserves. Proteins, in addition to being a key energy source, are essential for numerous biological processes, including immune function, tissue repair, and growth (Pratap‐Singh et al. 2024). As such, high‐protein diets are strongly recommended for maintaining overall health and supporting physical performance. There is a growing focus on dietary patterns that emphasize increased protein intake due to its significant impact on human health (Hertzler et al. 2020). Beyond their nutritional value, proteins also possess unique functional properties critical to food formulation—such as water‐holding capacity, gelation, and emulsification—which influence a product's texture, appearance, and taste. Therefore, proteins play a dual role in meeting both nutritional and sensory needs in food systems (Lara‐Parra et al. 2025). However, the large‐scale production of animal‐based proteins poses serious environmental challenges. It is projected that per capita consumption could reach approximately 60 kg per year by 2050, with global production of meat and dairy expected to rise to 1250 million tons annually by the same year (Monnet et al. 2019). Animal‐based protein production is resource‐intensive, generating substantial greenhouse gas emissions and requiring large volumes of water and land. For example, livestock farming consumes ∼6 kg of plant protein to yield just 1 kg of animal protein (McClements and Grossmann 2021). Furthermore, the consumption of meat is subject to various cultural and religious restrictions, which limits its universal acceptance. In contrast, plant‐based products face fewer ethical and religious barriers and are more aligned with animal welfare principles, making them particularly appealing to vegetarian and ethically conscious consumers (Nikbakht Nasrabadi et al. 2021).

The urgent need to address environmental degradation and ethical concerns has intensified the search for sustainable alternatives to animal‐derived proteins. In this context, plant‐based proteins have emerged as promising candidates due to their potential to meet global nutritional demands while exerting less pressure on natural resources (Hertzler et al. 2020). Many of these proteins can be sourced from crop residues and by‐products of the oil industry, offering a cost‐effective and renewable protein supply (Sá et al. 2020). Additionally, plant‐derived proteins are often regarded as healthier options compared to animal proteins, largely due to their lower risk of contamination and infection (Kalse et al. 2025). Mounting evidence has also linked the consumption of animal‐based products to negative health outcomes, including an increased risk of cancer, cardiometabolic disorders, obesity, dyslipidemia, and hypertension (Hertzler et al. 2020; Ulhas et al. 2023). Therefore, advancing the structural and functional qualities of plant proteins remains essential (Kumar et al. 2021; McClements and Grossmann 2021). This includes improving extraction yields and enhancing their techno‐functional and structural attributes—such as solubility, emulsification, gelation, and creation of protein fibrils that mimic the layered, striated structure of animal muscle tissue (Cheng et al. 2024). Developing such meat‐like architectures is critical for replicating the texture and mouthfeel of traditional meat products, thus increasing consumer acceptance and the marketability of plant‐based alternatives (Keerthi et al. 2025).

Plant‐protein meat analogue development is better understood as an integrated formulation‐processing problem than as a simple protein substitution exercise. A successful design must satisfy nutritional adequacy while also producing flow behavior, phase organization, water retention, anisotropy, flavor quality, cost, and manufacturability compatible with the target product. In this setting, AI is useful only when it is tied to measurable variables, such as amino acid profile, protein purity, particle size, solubility, rheology, extrusion conditions, cooling‐die geometry, image‐based fibrousness, instrumental texture, sensory response, and sustainability indicators (Harikrishnan et al. 2025; Theng et al. 2025). This review therefore examines plant‐protein meat analogue design from source selection through extraction, functional modification, and fibrillation‐based texturization, whereas considering where data‐driven tools can realistically reduce experimental burden and where conventional experimental validation remains essential.

Nutritional Aspects of Plant Proteins

From a nutritional standpoint, two key criteria define a high‐quality protein: (i) digestibility and (ii) the presence of a balanced composition of essential amino acids. These include histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, and tryptophan, among others. High‐quality proteins must be efficiently digested and absorbed by the body while also supplying adequate levels of essential amino acids that serve as critical building blocks for growth, maintenance, and overall physiological function (Hertzler et al. 2020; Ulhas et al. 2023). The nutritional quality of a protein is primarily evaluated based on its digestibility and the content of indispensable amino acids (Baig et al. 2024). Several standardized methods have been developed for this purpose, with the most prominent being the protein digestibility‐corrected amino acid score (PDCAAS) and the digestible indispensable amino acid score (DIAAS). These approaches assess protein quality by comparing the essential amino acid profile of the test protein to that of a reference protein, thereby identifying any limiting amino acids (Messina et al. 2025; Opazo‐Navarrete et al. 2025). Additionally, protein digestibility is factored into the evaluation, with final scores typically expressed either as a percentage or a decimal value (Messina et al. 2025; Juhrich et al. 2025). Proteins derived from plant sources often exhibit lower nutritional quality compared to those from animal origins. As a result, specific strategies must be adopted to overcome these limitations (Kadam et al. 2025). As shown in Table 1, PDCAAS and DIAAS scores for animal proteins—such as meat, milk, and eggs—typically approach 100%, reflecting their high digestibility and complete amino acid profiles. In contrast, most plant proteins score below 100%, indicating the presence of limiting amino acids. Among plant‐derived proteins, soy consistently demonstrates the highest nutritional quality, whereas other sources like canola, potato, pea, and quinoa also offer acceptable scores. Animal proteins generally supply all essential amino acids in sufficient quantities to support biological functions. In contrast, many plant proteins lack one or more essential amino acids (Oh et al. 2025; López‐Moreno and Kraselnik 2025; Tormási et al. 2025). A recent modeling study found that replacing all animal meat with plant‐based meat alternatives slightly reduced protein adequacy from 93% to 86% (Wanders et al. 2025). To address this, combining different plant‐protein sources can help achieve a balanced amino acid profile. For instance, where one plant protein is deficient in a specific essential amino acid, another may provide it in adequate amounts. Therefore, strategic blending of complementary plant proteins can ensure a complete set of essential amino acids, thereby improving the overall protein quality in plant‐based diets (Monnet et al. 2019; Broucke et al. 2025). Moreover, processing methods such as cooking can significantly improve the digestibility of plant proteins. For example, traditional home‐cooking methods enhanced the in vitro protein digestibility of Caribbean pulses by approximately 14% (Thomas et al. 2025). Compared to animal meat, legumes and cereals contain substantial amounts of protein, typically comprising approximately 20%–40% and 10%–15% of their dry weight, respectively. Moreover, plant‐based foods are generally less calorie‐dense, allowing for greater energy and protein intake through larger portion sizes without excessive caloric load (Hertzler et al. 2020). Protein‐quality indices and empirical blending rules remain useful starting points, but they are not sufficient to design plant‐protein meat analogues with both nutritional and structural credibility. DIAAS and PDCAAS describe amino acid adequacy and digestibility, yet they do not account for extraction history, denaturation state, residual starch or fiber, anti‐nutritional compounds, heat treatment, enzymatic modification, or extrusion‐induced changes in the food matrix (Duque‐Estrada et al. 2026). Computational screening is most valuable when these nutritional descriptors are analyzed together with functional constraints such as solubility, gelation, water‐holding capacity, interfacial behavior, and compatibility with extrusion or printing (Kircali Ata et al. 2023; Mandal et al. 2026). In this context, AI can help rank protein sources and blends, but the ranking should be treated as a prioritization step for laboratory testing rather than as a substitute for digestibility and amino acid analysis. Sequence‐ and structure‐based tools can add another layer to this screening process. AlphaFold‐based platforms, ESMFold, and related protein language models (pLMs) can indicate likely folding patterns, exposed residues, stable domains, and potential cleavage regions from amino acid sequences (Jumper et al. 2021; Lin et al. 2025; Hýsková et al. 2026). These outputs are relevant because surface accessibility, conformational stability, and susceptibility to hydrolysis can influence both digestibility and techno‐functionality. However, for food proteins, predicted structures must be interpreted in the context of hydration, pH, salt, shear, heating, aggregation, and interactions with polysaccharides or lipids. Thus, these tools are best used to select candidates for experimental validation rather than to infer final product performance directly. Optimization algorithms can also support protein‐blend design when nutritional, functional, economic, and environmental constraints are considered simultaneously (Harikrishnan et al. 2025). For example, a blend may be nutritionally attractive but unsuitable for high‐moisture extrusion if it lacks sufficient melt strength, phase separation, or water retention. AI‐assisted dietary modeling illustrates how computational tools can balance several targets at once, although their outputs remain sensitive to the input database, constraints, and validation criteria (Köktürk and Yardımcı 2026). The main limitation is not the absence of algorithms, but the absence of standardized food‐protein datasets that connect sequence, extraction method, structure, digestibility, processing behavior, and product‐level performance. Static structure predictions do not fully represent protein dynamics under acidic heating, enzymatic hydrolysis, high shear, extrusion, or 3D printing conditions (Lin et al. 2025; Hýsková et al. 2026; Yang et al. 2026). Similarly, generative tools can propose formulations, but they cannot resolve matrix effects, sensory acceptability, allergenicity, regulatory feasibility, or industrial processability without experimental data (Köktürk and Yardımcı 2026). Therefore, AI in nutritional design should be framed as a candidate‐screening and uncertainty‐reduction tool that helps narrow the experimental design space.

Health Promoting Association

Plant Proteins and Cancer Risk Modulation

Cancer is characterized by the uncontrolled growth of abnormal cells, which often invade surrounding tissues and other organs (World Health Organization 2017). It remains one of the leading causes of death worldwide. The incidence of cancer is largely influenced by lifestyle factors, including dietary habits, physical activity, environmental exposures, and genetic predisposition. Although inherited factors such as genetics are non‐modifiable, lifestyle choices can be actively managed to reduce cancer risk (Yasin et al. 2021). Dietary protein source has been a subject of increasing interest in cancer research. Several studies have examined the relationship between protein type and cancer risk. For instance, Andersen et al. (2019) reported an indirect association between high consumption of red and processed meats and colorectal cancer, suggesting that these animal‐based proteins may promote carcinogenic mechanisms. In contrast, Møller et al. (2020) found no significant association between the ratio of animal to plant‐protein intake and colorectal cancer risk, indicating that total protein composition may not be a standalone risk factor. Similarly, a large, pooled cohort study investigating bladder cancer found no significant association between total protein intake, whether animal or plant‐based, and cancer risk after controlling for confounders such as smoking and alcohol. However, in partially adjusted models, substituting 30 g day−1 of animal‐based protein with plant‐based protein was associated with a modest reduction in bladder cancer risk (Beigrezaei et al. 2025). More recent evidence suggests that higher plant‐protein intake may be associated with lower overall or site‐specific cancer risk in some populations, although the strength and direction of these associations vary depending on cancer type, population characteristics, dietary pattern, and confounding lifestyle factors (Hu et al. 2026). Therefore, plant protein should not be evaluated only as an isolated exposure but rather as part of a broader dietary and metabolic context. In this regard, AI‐assisted dietary modeling may add value by integrating dietary intake, clinical variables, behavioral factors, food‐processing level, cooking method, and the presence of protective dietary components such as fiber and phytochemicals into predictive models of cancer‐related risk. Such models could help identify plant‐protein substitution patterns that are more likely to align with low‐risk dietary profiles and may support the ranking of raw materials, protein sources, and meat analogue formulations according to their predicted nutritional and health‐related profiles (Chen, Wu, et al. 2026; Ahmed et al. 2025; Hu et al. 2026). For cancer‐related outcomes, computational dietary models are most useful when plant‐protein intake is analyzed as part of a broader dietary pattern rather than as an isolated exposure (Köktürk and Yardımcı 2026). Useful inputs would include protein source, fiber and phytochemical intake, processing level, cooking method, clinical variables, smoking, alcohol intake, body composition, and long‐term dietary pattern (Chen, Wu, et al. 2026; Ahmed et al. 2025; Hu et al. 2026). Such models can help identify substitution patterns that align with lower risk dietary profiles, but they cannot establish causality between a specific plant‐protein ingredient and cancer protection. Prospective cohort data and controlled nutritional studies remain necessary, especially because cancer risk varies strongly with cancer site, host phenotype, genetics, and lifestyle background (Sguanci et al. 2025).

Diabetes and Glycemic Control

Another important area of interest in evaluating the benefits of plant‐protein intake is its potential role in reducing the risk of diabetes. Diabetes develops when the pancreas fails to produce sufficient insulin or when the body becomes resistant to insulin, impairing glucose transport into cells (Ahnen et al. 2019). Risk reduction strategies for diabetes include regular physical activity and dietary interventions, such as the incorporation of plant‐based or vegan products (Anjom‐Shoae et al. 2024). Recent research has increasingly highlighted the impact of protein source on Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2D) risk and management. An umbrella review by Schulze et al. (2024) concluded that although total protein intake is possibly associated with higher T2D risk, there is insufficient evidence to establish clear effects of animal or plant protein alone. However, more recent epidemiological studies consistently show that replacing animal‐based protein with plant‐based sources may be protective. For instance, a large‐scale prospective cohort study found that replacing 5% of energy from animal protein with plant protein was associated with a 21% lower risk of T2D (Li et al. 2022). Supporting these findings, Anjom‐Shoae et al. (2024) emphasized that plant proteins tend to exert either neutral or protective effects on long‐term glycemic control, potentially due to their amino acid profile and reduced insulinotropic and inflammatory responses compared to animal proteins.

In fact, although many studies suggest potential benefits of substituting plant proteins for animal proteins, the findings remain somewhat inconsistent. Therefore, more comprehensive and well‐controlled long‐term studies are needed to directly compare their effects on diabetes prevention and management. For glycemic control, the computational question is not simply whether plant protein is beneficial but how the whole formulation affects postprandial response. Relevant variables include protein source, starch digestibility, fiber level, fat type, particle size, processing intensity, cooking method, and individual factors such as microbiome composition, medication use, body composition, and physical activity. Zeevi et al. (2015) showed that machine learning models can predict postprandial glycemic responses when dietary, clinical, lifestyle, and gut microbiota variables are combined. For plant‐protein meat analogues, similar models could be used to compare formulations for predicted metabolic impact, but these predictions should be verified in controlled feeding or intervention studies before health claims are made (Singh et al. 2025; Popova et al. 2025).

Cardiometabolic Outcomes and All‐Cause Mortality

Cardiovascular diseases are predominantly driven by metabolic abnormalities such as obesity, hypertension, elevated fasting glucose, high low‐density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol, and low HDL cholesterol levels (Ahnen et al. 2019). Plant‐derived proteins may contribute to cardiovascular health by enhancing the binding of LDL to its receptors, thereby lowering serum and hepatic lipid levels. Additionally, dietary proteins can support cholesterol degradation, improve fat metabolism, and reduce oxidative stress, all of which may positively influence cardiometabolic risk factors (Angeles et al. 2021). Shi et al. (2019) found that individuals consuming a chickpea protein (CP)‐rich diet experienced decreased levels of cholesterol, triglycerides, and LDL. Beyond cardiovascular benefits, plant‐protein intake has also been associated with a reduction in all‐cause mortality. A large‐scale prospective cohort study demonstrated an inverse relationship between dietary plant‐protein consumption and mortality from all causes, including cardiovascular and dementia‐related deaths (Sun et al. 2021). These findings are supported by Huang et al. (2020), who investigated the effects of substituting 3% of daily energy intake from animal‐based proteins with plant‐based proteins in approximately 450,000 individuals. The study revealed a 10% reduction in overall mortality among those with higher plant‐protein intake, regardless of smoking status, fruit consumption, or supplement use. Similarly, in a UK‐based cohort study involving over 98,000 individuals, substituting 5% of energy from animal protein with plant protein was associated with a 5% reduction in cardiovascular disease risk (Qian et al. 2024). In summary, although many animal‐derived proteins are linked to increased cardiovascular risk, some animal proteins, such as those from milk, poultry, and fish, as well as plant‐based proteins, have demonstrated beneficial effects in attenuating these risks (Zhubi‐Bakija et al. 2021). Therefore, adopting a balanced diet that includes appropriate proportions of plant proteins along with selected animal proteins may contribute to improved cardiovascular health and overall well‐being. Cardiometabolic evaluation also requires product‐level modeling rather than a simple plant‐versus‐animal comparison. The health profile of a meat analogue depends on the protein source, saturated‐fat replacement, fiber enrichment, sodium level, degree of processing, amino acid profile, lipid matrix, and serving context. Machine learning studies using dietary, biomarker, anthropometric, and lifestyle variables have shown that complex nonlinear relationships can be captured more effectively than by single‐nutrient comparisons alone (Martin‐Morales et al. 2023; Morgenstern et al. 2022). Emerging precision‐nutrition work, including proteomic signatures of healthy dietary patterns, further suggests that diet–disease relationships can be analyzed at the systems level (Zhu et al. 2025). For meat analogue development, these approaches are most useful for screening formulations and identifying risk‐related trade‐offs, which should then be tested through biomarker‐based and long‐term dietary studies.

Lean Body Mass and Muscle Strength Affected by Plant Protein

Skeletal muscle plays a fundamental role in maintaining posture and overall body stability. A decline in muscle strength and loss of muscle mass can have serious consequences, including increased risk of disability and mortality (Reid‐McCann et al. 2025). Muscle protein turnover is governed by two opposing processes: (i) muscle protein synthesis and (ii) muscle protein breakdown (Yang et al. 2025). Although physical exercise stimulates both pathways, muscle protein breakdown often exceeds synthesis during or immediately after exercise, resulting in a negative net protein balance. In such conditions, the consumption of high‐quality dietary protein is essential for stimulating muscle protein synthesis and restoring a positive protein balance, thereby supporting muscle maintenance and growth (Joanisse et al. 2021; Reid‐McCann et al. 2025; Yang et al. 2025). It is widely accepted that animal‐derived proteins are generally more effective in promoting skeletal muscle development due to their higher digestibility and essential amino acid content (Goldman et al. 2024). According to Reid‐McCann et al. (2025), plant proteins like soy tend to undergo greater splanchnic extraction and nitrogen loss, reducing the free amino acid pool available for muscle protein synthesis compared to animal proteins such as milk or whey. These differences are particularly important for older adults, who already experience reduced anabolic responsiveness. As a result, protein quality, measured by digestibility and essential amino acid content, becomes a key factor in preserving muscle mass, strength, and functional performance in aging. Nonetheless, other research has reported no significant differences in muscle strength or lean body mass outcomes between animal proteins and plant‐based alternatives such as soy, rice, and pea proteins (PPs) (Messina et al. 2018; Kalman et al. 2018; Banaszek et al. 2019). To resolve these mixed clinical outcomes and maximize the anabolic potential of plant‐based diets, AI and ML frameworks are increasingly being explored for the discovery of plant‐derived bioactive peptides and functional protein ingredients that may support muscle health. For example, machine learning–assisted screening has been used to identify potentially bioactive peptides within a faba‐derived hydrolysate, suggesting that plant‐protein fractions may contain peptide sequences capable of modulating pathways related to muscle protein synthesis, inflammation, and muscle maintenance. Subsequently, in a human immobilization–remobilization study, supplementation with a faba peptide network did not differ from milk protein in preventing muscle size loss or supporting muscle size regain, but it increased myofibrillar protein synthesis rates during the remobilization phase compared with milk protein concentrate (Corrochano et al. 2021). This finding suggests that AI‐discovered plant peptide networks may have potential as targeted functional ingredients, although their clinical efficacy for preventing muscle loss requires further validation. Beyond ingredient discovery, AI‐based prediction tools are also being integrated into clinical and nutritional settings to identify individuals at risk of sarcopenia, muscle wasting, or impaired recovery. Machine learning models can combine demographic, anthropometric, biochemical, inflammatory, dietary, and functional variables to predict sarcopenia risk and support earlier intervention. For instance, recent models using random forest, gradient boosting, and SHAP‐based interpretability have identified factors such as BMI, serum albumin, age, creatinine, hemoglobin, C‐reactive protein, and total protein as important predictors of sarcopenia risk in clinical populations (Seok et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2026). For plant‐protein meat analogue development, these approaches create two complementary opportunities: First, AI can accelerate the discovery of plant peptides or protein fractions with potential anabolic or anti‐catabolic activity; second, predictive models can help define the nutritional needs of target populations, such as older adults or individuals at risk of sarcopenia. However, AI‐predicted peptide bioactivity, muscle‐health benefits, and clinical risk scores should be interpreted as decision‐support outputs rather than definitive evidence of efficacy. Future research should combine AI‐guided peptide discovery, controlled feeding studies, resistance‐exercise interventions, and longitudinal clinical validation to determine whether specific plant‐protein ingredients or meat analogue formulations can meaningfully support muscle mass, strength, and healthy aging.

Health Concerns and Plant Proteins

Allergenicity

Nonanimal proteins are widely recommended by healthcare professionals as part of a balanced and health‐promoting diet. Although the intake of plant‐based proteins offers numerous health benefits, potential unintended effects should also be acknowledged, particularly allergenicity, which is often overlooked (Fasolin et al. 2019; Milana et al. 2025). Food allergies are abnormal immune responses triggered by specific food components, most commonly proteins (Gonzalez et al. 2025; Milana et al. 2025). These reactions typically occur when antibodies bind to ingested allergens, leading to the release of histamine and other inflammatory mediators. Symptoms can range from mild responses such as sneezing or skin irritation to severe outcomes, including anaphylaxis and loss of consciousness (Fasolin et al. 2019; Li et al. 2024; Jadhav et al. 2024; Milana et al. 2025). In regulatory frameworks across the European Union, Canada, and the United States, eight major food allergens, eggs, milk, fish, shellfish, soybeans, peanuts, tree nuts, and gluten‐containing cereals, are required to be clearly labeled (Jadhav et al. 2024; Ham et al. 2025). However, legumes such as peas, chickpeas, and lentils are generally considered less allergenic, particularly in Europe, Asia, and the Mediterranean regions. These pulse crops are associated with significantly lower incidences of allergic responses compared to the primary allergens (Li et al. 2024; Jadhav et al. 2024; Ham et al. 2025). Allergenicity prediction is one of the more practical safety applications of sequence‐based modeling because allergenic risk is partly linked to amino acid sequence similarity, conserved motifs, and potential IgE‐binding regions. Recent deep‐learning tools can rapidly compare emerging plant proteins with known allergen databases and may help flag candidates such as pea, lupin, chickpea, or other legume proteins for further testing (Liu, Negi, et al. 2025; Dhouib et al. 2025; López‐Pedrouso et al. 2023). In product development, this type of screening is useful before scale‐up, especially when novel isolates or blends are being introduced. However, predicted allergenicity should only guide risk prioritization; clinical relevance still requires immunological assays, cross‐reactivity assessment, exposure evaluation, and regulatory review.

Antinutrients

Although plant proteins provide important nutritional and health benefits, their anti‐nutritional components must be controlled during ingredient processing (Anyiam et al. 2025; Popova and Mihaylova 2019). Antinutrient reduction is a processing‐optimization problem because phytic acid, tannins, trypsin inhibitors, lectins, and saponins respond differently to soaking, heating, fermentation, enzymatic treatment, ultrasound, and extrusion (Das et al. 2022; Tuncel et al. 2025). Data‐driven models are useful when they link processing variables such as pH, temperature, moisture, residence time, enzyme dose, fermentation conditions, or extrusion severity to both antinutrient reduction and protein functionality (Lu et al. 2024; Ravindran et al. 2024). This dual‐output structure is important because aggressive processing may reduce antinutrients while also decreasing solubility, gelation, emulsification, or fibrillation capacity (Rashwan et al. 2025; Jiang et al. 2025). Therefore, optimization should target a processing window that improves bioavailability without compromising the structural role of the protein in the final meat analogue.

Protein Extraction and Techno‐Functional Modification

Beyond amino acid profile and digestibility, plant proteins must be extracted and structurally tuned to reach equivalent techno‐functional performance. These proteins are encased within the rigid plant cell wall, necessitating the use of efficient extraction techniques to disrupt the cell wall and release the protein content (Figure 1) (Wen et al. 2023). High‐yield extraction technologies are therefore critical to the advancement of plant‐protein alternatives. In addition to conventional extraction methods, nontraditional cell disruption techniques, such as ultrasound‐assisted and enzymatic extraction, have gained traction for their ability to enhance protein recovery (Tang et al. 2024). These methods work by disrupting the cellular matrix and breaking protein–fiber, protein–polyphenol, and protein–lipid complexes, thereby improving the penetration of extraction solvents. Some of these innovative approaches not only increase extraction yield but also enhance the techno‐functional properties of the resulting plant proteins (Tang et al. 2024; Ravindran et al. 2024). Unlike animal proteins, which are predominantly composed of fibrous and hierarchical protein structures, plant proteins are mainly globular in nature. This structural difference often results in inferior functional properties for plant proteins (Großmann and Weiss 2021). Thus, improving the functionality of plant proteins, through controlled rearrangement of protein chains, is a crucial step in the development of viable plant‐based food products (Sim et al. 2021). Although certain extraction methods can improve functional attributes such as solubility, emulsification, and gelling behavior, harsh processing conditions may negatively impact protein structure, highlighting the importance of optimization of extraction factors (Moll et al. 2021; Sim et al. 2021; Ravindran et al. 2024). For extraction and functional modification, predictive models are most useful when they are built around measurable process–structure–function links. Inputs may include pH, temperature, extraction time, solvent‐to‐solid ratio, ultrasound power, enzyme concentration, conductivity, protein purity, particle size, denaturation state, and residual polyphenol or fiber content. Outputs should extend beyond protein yield to include solubility, water‐holding capacity, emulsifying behavior, gelation, digestibility, and energy or solvent demand. ANN, RSM, and related models can identify processing windows that improve recovery while avoiding excessive protein damage or loss of functionality (Vartiya et al. 2025; Đermanović et al. 2025). The value of these models increases when the same dataset connects extraction conditions to downstream behavior in extrusion, shear‐cell structuring, or printing. Digital‐twin concepts could eventually connect extraction conditions, structural changes, and functional performance, but their use in plant‐protein extraction is still early. A useful virtual model would need to represent protein unfolding, aggregation, solubility loss, mass transfer, and matrix‐specific barriers such as cell‐wall composition or protein–polyphenol interactions. Until such models are trained with standardized experimental datasets, they should be used cautiously for hypothesis generation and process comparison rather than for direct scale‐up decisions.

Physical Techniques

Ultrasound

Sonication is a novel, environmentally friendly, and sustainable technology that has been increasingly applied in the food industry. Its mechanism is based on acoustic cavitation and the action of mechanical waves at ultrasonic frequencies beyond the range of human hearing (Amiri et al. 2018). During sonication, microscopic bubbles form and collapse violently, generating localized zones of high temperature and pressure. This leads to intense shear forces and turbulence within the medium, resulting in functional, structural, and physicochemical changes in proteins (Xu and Xu 2021). Ultrasonic treatment affects the secondary structure of proteins and partially denatures their tertiary and quaternary structures by disrupting non‐covalent interactions (Gharibzahedi and Smith 2020; Amiri et al. 2021). This disruption can break protein aggregates into smaller particles, expose polar residues, and promote stronger protein–water interactions. As a result, protein solubility, water‐holding capacity, and gel strength may be enhanced under optimized conditions (Amiri et al. 2018, 2021; Badjona et al. 2025). Furthermore, the localized hotspots of temperature and pressure created by bubble implosion near plant cells can rupture cell walls, enabling solvents to penetrate more effectively and extract intracellular macromolecules (Kumar et al. 2021). For example, Wang et al. (2020) developed an ultrasound‐assisted extraction method to optimize PP recovery and reported a maximum protein yield of 82.6 g/100 g at 33.7% ultrasonic amplitude and an extraction time of 13.5 min. In addition, functional properties, such as solubility, water‐holding capacity, foaming capacity, emulsifying capacity, and gel strength, were improved following sonication (Wang et al. 2020). Besides ultrasonic intensity and duration, other critical factors influencing extraction efficiency include pH, temperature, sample matrix, and solvent‐to‐protein ratio (Xu and Xu 2021; Kumar et al. 2021). Yeasmen and Orsat (2024) demonstrated that pulsed ultrasound‐assisted alkaline extraction at pH 9 significantly enhanced protein recovery from sugar maple leaves, yielding up to 8.25% protein compared with 3.5% by maceration. The combination of sonication and optimized pH conditions not only improved protein yield, but also enhanced solubility, emulsion capacity, water‐holding capacity, and antioxidant activity. These findings confirm that both sonication parameters and alkaline pH play critical roles in maximizing extraction efficiency and functional quality (Yeasmen and Orsat 2024). Numerous studies have confirmed the beneficial effects of ultrasound treatment in improving extraction yields and enhancing the techno‐functionality of plant proteins (Table 2). For ultrasound‐assisted extraction, the useful modeling variables are not limited to power and time. Cavitation intensity, probe geometry, sample temperature, pH, solvent‐to‐solid ratio, protein concentration, viscosity, and plant matrix structure all influence extraction efficiency and protein functionality. SVR, ANN, and genetic‐algorithm approaches can help map these nonlinear effects, as shown for ultrasound‐assisted extraction systems such as mosambi peel protein and other bioactive compounds (Khursheed et al. 2022; Chakraborty et al. 2025). In plant‐protein meat analogue development, the most valuable models would predict both extraction yield and downstream traits such as solubility, particle size, water‐holding capacity, and gel strength rather than optimizing yield alone. Ultrasound optimization should also identify the point at which beneficial cavitation becomes overprocessing. Excessive power, long exposure, or poor temperature control can promote free‐radical formation, disulfide‐driven aggregation, reduced solubility, and lower protein recovery (Nikbakht Nasrabadi et al. 2021; Xu and Xu 2021). Therefore, model outputs should include warning indicators such as temperature rise, turbidity, particle‐size growth, sulfhydryl loss, or declining solubility. This would make ultrasound modeling more useful for selecting protein‐ and equipment‐specific operating windows before pilot‐scale trials.

Pulsed Electric Field (PEF)

PEF processing is an emerging nonthermal technology in which plant cells are exposed to a series of high‐voltage pulses, typically ranging from 1 to 80 kV cm−1, for very short durations (Ren et al. 2025). The core principle of PEF involves generating a strong electric field between two electrodes with differing electrical potentials (Mousakhani‐Ganjeh et al. 2021). When plant tissues are subjected to these pulses, a critical transmembrane potential is induced across the cell wall, leading to electroporation, a phenomenon characterized by the formation of pores, loss of cell wall integrity, and subsequent release of intracellular components such as proteins (Contreras et al. 2019). Unlike ultrasound, which relies on mechanical shear and heat, PEF applies electrical stress to achieve similar structural changes, but with lower thermal input. Beyond cell disruption, PEF treatment also induces protein unfolding and structural modifications, largely due to the formation of free radicals. These radicals disrupt both covalent and non‐covalent interactions within the protein network, thereby affecting properties such as solubility and emulsification capacity (Barbhuiya et al. 2021; Van De Walle et al. 2025). Experimental studies have shown that moderate PEF intensities and durations can improve protein solubility and emulsifying properties (Van De Walle et al. 2025; Wang, Guo, Yang, et al. 2025). However, at higher intensities or prolonged exposure times, adverse effects such as protein aggregation may occur, leading to reduced functionality (Van De Walle et al. 2025). At moderate intensities (10–15 kV cm−1), PEF improves solubility by disrupting non‐covalent interactions and unfolding proteins. However, at higher intensities (≥20 kV cm−1), exposed hydrophobic and sulfhydryl groups promote aggregation, reducing solubility and functionality (Wang, Guo, Yang, et al. 2025). This reaggregation correlated with a decline in solubility and the formation of large insoluble aggregates, confirming that PEF functionality is intensity‐dependent, with moderate intensities favoring functional enhancement and high intensities promoting structural destabilization (Wang, Guo, Yang, et al. 2025). In fact, the operating window is narrow. At excessive field strength, pulse number, or treatment time, exposed hydrophobic and sulfhydryl groups may promote aggregation and reduce functionality (Wang, Guo, Yang, et al. 2025). For this reason, modeling PEF requires inputs such as electric field strength, pulse width, pulse number, frequency, conductivity, temperature, pH, and matrix composition (Ramaswamy and Bala Krishnan 2024). Machine learning models can help identify conditions that improve protein release and interfacial functionality without crossing into aggregation‐dominated behavior, but their transferability across plant sources and equipment geometries still requires validation (Rashvand et al. 2025; Navarro and Ventura 2022).

High‐Pressure Processing (HPP)

HPP is primarily employed as a nonthermal safety technique for the inactivation of spoilage microorganisms and endogenous enzymes (Chen et al. 2018; Baldelli et al. 2024). Beyond its preservation role, HPP also significantly influences protein structure by disrupting non‐covalent interactions, particularly hydrophobic and electrostatic bonds. Notably, hydrogen bonds remain largely unaffected by pressure, making HPP particularly effective in altering the tertiary and quaternary structures of proteins (Baldelli et al. 2024; Kaur et al. 2025). At higher pressures, the reactivity of thiol groups increases, promoting the formation of disulfide bonds that contribute to protein aggregation and gelation (Baldelli et al. 2024; Braspaiboon and Laokuldilok 2024). Mild HPP treatments unfold protein chains, exposing buried hydrophobic and thiol groups on the surface. In contrast, prolonged or high‐intensity HPP treatments can lead to protein agglomeration due to the formation of new linkages, including disulfide bridges (Baldelli et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025). To optimize these complex transitions, the field of HPP is progressing towards digitalized quality control through integration of artificial intelligence. Unlike experimental approaches, modern data‐driven ensembled frameworks are being employed to predict specific pressure levels and their impact on protein functionality by analyzing spectroscopic fingerprints (Alasi et al. 2024). For example, deep learning architectures have been deployed to decode the subtle shifts in the Amide I band of HPP‐treated proteins, allowing for the real‐time quantification of secondary structure changes without the need for destructive analysis (Rashvand et al. 2025). Furthermore, feedforward neural networks have proven effective in mapping relationships between HPP‐induced physicochemical markers such as alpha‐helix content and zeta potential and the resulting protein digestibility, enabling researchers to accelerate the screening of novel plant isolates by up to 80% (Liu, Zhang, et al. 2025). These structural modifications initially enhance solubility and water‐holding capacity, but excessive pressure eventually reverses these effects due to aggregation and decreased dispersibility (Xu et al. 2024; Zhang et al. 2025). Studies have shown that HPP treatments below 400 MPa can improve the solubility and water‐holding capacity of various plant proteins, whereas pressures exceeding 400 MPa often result in reduced solubility (Liu et al. 2020). Mulla et al. (2022) further demonstrated that HPP‐induced protein unfolding enhances emulsifying and foaming properties, as more functional groups become accessible, also contributing to improved protein digestibility. In addition to functional enhancement, HPP has been reported in some specific matrices to increase protein extraction yields by promoting cell wall and membrane disintegration (Hegde et al. 2025; Braspaiboon and Laokuldilok 2024). However, a substantial body of work on plant‐protein systems indicates that static HPP alone has little or no effect on overall protein recovery (Das et al. 2023). Instead, HPP is more commonly applied as a post‐extraction modification step or as a pretreatment that accelerates other extraction techniques by increasing cell permeability and mass transfer (Kumar et al. 2021; Kaur et al. 2025). Microwave

Microwave processing is another promising physical technique for enhancing protein functionality and facilitating protein extraction (Shawky et al. 2025). Microwaves are a form of electromagnetic radiation with frequencies ranging from 300 to 300 GHz and wavelengths between 1 mm and 1 m. During microwave processing, electromagnetic waves are absorbed by dielectric materials, causing the rotation of polar molecules (Chen, Wu, et al. 2026). The friction generated by this molecular movement produces heat, which can be harnessed for targeted thermal effects (Mousakhani‐Ganjeh et al. 2021). Microwave processing is considered an efficient, eco‐friendly, and green technology (Chen, Wu, et al. 2026). It can disrupt hydrogen bonds within plant cell walls, thereby increasing membrane permeability (Tang et al. 2025). Additionally, the rapid heating and moisture evaporation caused by microwave create internal pressure that leads to the formation of microscopic punctures in the cell wall (Peñas et al. 2023). These structural disruptions facilitate solvent penetration and the release of intracellular proteins (Peñas et al. 2023; Tang et al. 2025). A significant barrier to industrial scaling of microwave extraction is the inherent nonuniformity of electromagnetic field, which frequently results in localized overheating or development of cold spots. To solve this, physics‐guided neural networks apply governing heat transfer and Maxwell's equations directly into the deep learning architecture. It enables highly accurate prediction of temperature field within complex plant matrices, allowing for a 70% reduction in maximum predictive error compared to traditional black‐box models (Yang et al. 2026). Furthermore, new multimodal thermal sensing frameworks are being integrated with these predictive models to provide real‐time correction of heating patterns (Meng et al. 2026). These advanced algorithms can identify shifting dielectric properties as proteins denature and further autonomously pulse the microwave power to maintain a precise thermal window. It ensures that the structural disruption of cell wall occurs without reaching the temperature thresholds that trigger irreversible protein aggregation (Sun et al. 2026). Beyond assisting protein extraction, microwave treatment can also improve the techno‐functional properties of proteins by affecting disulfide and hydrogen bonds, ultimately altering protein secondary and tertiary structures (Agarwal et al. 2025). For instance, Wang et al. (2022) reported an increase in free sulfhydryl group content and significant modifications in the secondary structure of buckwheat proteins following microwave treatment. These structural changes enhanced solubility, water‐holding capacity, and emulsifying and foaming properties. Microwave‐assisted extraction of mung bean protein resulted in the highest water absorption capacity (5.20 g g−1) and oil‐binding capacity (3.32 g g−1) among all tested methods, including ultrasound, high‐pressure homogenizer, and microwave. It was likely due to increased availability of polar groups and protein unfolding. In addition, microwave‐treated proteins exhibited the highest foam stability and surface hydrophobicity, suggesting improved interfacial activity and structural rearrangement beneficial for techno‐functional performance in food systems (Agarwal et al. 2025). Similar effects have been observed in quinoa protein, where microwave‐induced denaturation and unfolding improved water‐holding capacity and gel strength (Huang et al. 2022). Moreover, microwave processing can be employed in combination with other modification methods. For example, Gohi et al. (2019) used microwave pretreatment prior to enzymatic hydrolysis of lotus seed protein. The pretreatment facilitated protein unfolding and disaggregation, making peptide bonds more accessible to enzymatic action. Similarly, recent research demonstrated that combining microwave and ultrasound treatments significantly enhanced the extraction yield and protein recovery of lupin protein isolates within a much shorter processing time compared to conventional methods. Notably, lower power combinations improved solubility and reduced particle size, whereas higher power settings increased aggregation and viscosity, highlighting the importance of optimizing treatment intensity for desired functionality (Tang et al. 2025).

Biological Techniques

The push for sustainable, high‐quality proteins has accelerated the use of biological methods to extract and tailor plant proteins (Singhi and Ozturk 2025). Biological methods align with circular‐economy goals by valorizing by‐products and co‐streams. Fermentation and enzyme‐assisted processing operate under mild, aqueous conditions, making them attractive, low‐impact alternatives to harsh chemical methods (Kaur et al. 2025). However, enzyme and culture costs, strain selection, and process control at scale can affect consistency and economics, and over‐hydrolysis may impair functionality (Zolqadri and Li 2025). Nonetheless, when properly tuned or coupled with physical aids such as ultrasound, PEFs, or microwaves, biological processing can raise extraction yields while delivering ingredients with superior techno‐functional performance (Singhi and Ozturk 2025).

Fermentation

Fermentation is a biological method to both release and restructure plant proteins by coupling microbial acidification with endogenous/exogenous enzyme action (e.g., proteases and phytases) (Singhi and Ozturk 2025). In legumes and cereals, it consistently degrades anti‐nutritional factors, phytate, lectins, trypsin inhibitors, and raffinose‐family oligosaccharides, thereby improving protein extractability and bioavailability (Zolqadri and Li 2025). By deconstructing cell walls and loosening protein–polysaccharide complexes, fermentation enhances solvent access and raises soluble protein, peptide, and free‐amino‐acid contents (Kaur et al. 2025). Fermentation using different microorganism strains has been widely reported to modify plant‐protein functionality, with measurable shifts in solubility, interfacial activity, and network‐forming capacity. Techno‐functional outcomes depend on species, organism, and pH (Kaur et al. 2025). Lactic fermentations typically increase water absorption/holding and foaming while variably affecting solubility and emulsification (Zolqadri and Li 2025). For PP isolates, replacing chemical acidification with lactic fermentation suppressed off‐flavors and markedly reduced bitterness without impairing digestibility; water absorption and foaming increased, whereas powder solubility declined (Kaleda et al. 2025). Fermented pea and lupin systems illustrate pH‐specific behavior (e.g., improved solubility near pH 5 but decreased at pH 3), underscoring the need to tune culture/conditions for target applications (Arteaga et al. 2021; Kaleda et al. 2025). Mechanistically under fermentation, microbial proteases and the drop in pH (e.g., lactic acid fermentation) drive partial hydrolysis and unfolding, generating more surface‐active, amphiphilic peptides and exposing hydrophobic/charged residues (Kaleda et al. 2025; Zolqadri and Li 2025). This accelerates adsorption at oil–water interfaces and promotes interfacial unfolding, thus improving emulsifying properties (Batbayar et al. 2023). Foaming similarly benefits when fermentation‐derived peptides and partially unfolded proteins migrate rapidly to the air–water interface, lower surface tension, and pack efficiently (Arteaga et al. 2021). Water‐holding capacity increases as fermentation exposes polar groups that strengthen hydration shells; however, excessive proteolysis or acidification can weaken 3D networks and depress WHC (Arteaga et al. 2021; Zolqadri and Li 2025). Controlled fermentation also exposes sulfhydryl and hydrophobic groups that engage in disulfide and hydrophobic associations, yielding stronger, more elastic networks; over‐degradation produces fragments too short to percolate, giving softer, less cohesive gels (Zolqadri and Li 2025). Extending these trends, solid‐state fermentation with Pleurotus ostreatus on quinoa, chickpea, oat, and a Chlorella vulgaris “honey” with oat blend demonstrated matrix deconstruction and selective tuning of functionality: the SSF increased solubility in oat and the mix systems, enhanced gelling and water absorption in quinoa, raised oil absorption in chickpea and oat, and improved emulsifying performance in chickpea (Ayllón‐Parra et al. 2025). These shifts aligned with peptide generation and protein redistribution seen by SDS–PAGE, modest, matrix‐dependent pH changes, and substrate‐specific trade‐offs (e.g., solubility decreases after fermentation in some grains). Amino‐acid profiles were also rebalanced (e.g., higher valine/isoleucine/threonine with relative decreases in lysine/sulfur amino acids), underscoring the need to match SSF settings to texture and nutrition targets in meat‐analogue design (Ayllón‐Parra et al. 2025).

Overall, fermentation offers a mild, aqueous means to elevate extraction efficiency and tailor functionality for plant‐protein restructuring, while trade‐offs (e.g., solubility losses at certain pH or excessive aggregation) highlight the importance of organism selection and substrate pretreatment. Microbial growth is nonlinear and adds to the complexity of fermentation as a process, which is otherwise dependent on the inherent dynamic enzymatic cascades. This makes fermentation process a suitable candidate for advanced data analytics and artificial intelligence integration. Recent breakthroughs have been achieved by deploying convolutional neural networks (CNNs) to identify several fermentation trajectories in real‐time. In a published study, CNNs were utilized to analyze the first 24 h of fermentation process variables to predict final cell counts and acidification endpoint with a classification accuracy of 97.8%, enabling early manual interventions to prevent batch failure (Wu et al. 2025; Wang, Zeng, Zhu, et al. 2025). Furthermore, machine learning algorithms also offer automated process control and data mining, which can be easily tuned to be adapted for numerous plant‐protein isolates. In the field of precision fermentation, AI is now being combined with CRISPR‐based microbial optimization to drive the yield up to 300% for alternative proteins by predicting the metabolic flux and enzymatic pathways that lead to superior functional qualities (Priyadharshini et al. 2025).

Enzymatic Modification

In extraction contexts, proteases and cell‐wall‐degrading enzymes synergize to weaken protein–polysaccharide associations and disrupt plant microstructures, boosting protein yield while enhancing digestibility and functionality compared with alkaline extraction (Olatunde et al. 2023; Mosibo et al. 2024; Gasparre et al. 2025). In sugar beet leaves, pectinolytic step increased the protein yield by 43%, producing a concentrate with 69% protein (dry base) and 99% solubility at pH 7.5 (Akyüz and Ersus 2021). Van den Wouwer et al. (2025) evaluated the potential of aqueous pretreatments of potato trimmings and soybean okara using cellulase and pectinase as cell‐wall‐degrading enzymes. Cellulase and pectinase increased the crude protein yield in potato trimmings from 57.2% ± 8.6% to 75.6% ± 10.1% and 69.3% ± 11.7%, respectively. Moreover, cellulase and pectinase raised the crude protein yield in okara from 16.6% ± 5.2% to 19.5% ± 4.7% and 34.5% ± 8.7%, respectively. Together, these results argued for enzyme selection based on cell‐wall composition (Van den Wouwer et al. 2025).

Enzymatic processing is also a precise, mild, and scalable method to improve the structure–function profile of plant proteins. Two complementary strategies are mainly used: (i) proteolysis, where proteases (e.g., papain, pepsin, trypsin, Alcalase) cleave peptide bonds to generate lower molecular‐weight peptides and free amino acids with improved solubility, digestibility, interfacial activity, and often bioactivity; and (ii) enzymatic cross‐linking, where non‐proteolytic enzymes (e.g., transglutaminases and oxidoreductases) form covalent bonds, increasing molecular weight and enhancing texture and rheology (Olatunde et al. 2023; Mosibo et al. 2024; Gasparre et al. 2025). Enzymatic hydrolysis of kidney bean protein achieved a hydrolysis degree of 23.9%, showed clear polypeptide breakdown by SDS–PAGE, increased DPPH radical scavenging, with concurrent gains in solubility, features desirable for functional/nutraceutical applications (Al‐Ruwaih et al. 2019). Fungal protease treatment of peanut protein concentrates reduced particle size, increased surface hydrophobicity, and improved solubility, water‐holding ability, foaming capacity, and foam stability (Yadav et al. 2022). Across five proteases applied to perilla seed meal, trypsin‐treated hydrolysates delivered the highest water‐ and oil‐absorption capacities while maintaining broad pH‐range solubility (Kim and Yoon 2020). Partial cleavage exposes hydrophobic/charged residues and releases surface‐active peptides, boosting solubility, interfacial activity, and water‐holding capacity while preserving enough chain length for network formation. Excessive hydrolysis shortens chains to the point that gels weaken and foams destabilize, and it can also intensify bitterness (Olatunde et al. 2023; Gasparre et al. 2025). In practice, selecting enzyme type and enzyme dose, as well as tightly controlling pH, temperature, time, then tuning the hydrolysis degree, keeps the hydrolysis in the optimal range for the intended application. A published study employed deep learning framework DeepSolNet to utilize pLM embeddings and bidirectional LSTM layers to predict the solubility of recombinant and modified plant proteins directly from sequence data with high accuracy (Cui et al. 2026). Various such published body of work helps to navigate complex variables of enzymatic reactions and directs the evolution of this field towards artificial intelligence‐driven computational enzymology. Furthermore, bitterness intensity prediction engines allow researchers to quantitatively regress bitterness thresholds in log space, enabling design of low‐bitterness peptide profiles even before physical hydrolysis occurs (Yue et al. 2026). To facilitate the discovery of an optimal hydrolysis level, generative adversarial networks have been used to simulate how specific protease combinations will alter secondary structures like alpha‐helix and random coil content, which are key indicators of final protein digestibility (Liu et al. 2025). Conversely, cross‐linking enzymes strengthen gel networks and elasticity but can reduce solubility and emulsification if overexposed. In mung bean protein isolate, transglutaminase markedly increased gel hardness and storage modulus, tripled water‐holding capacity, and raised the emulsifying activity index, with microscopy showing a denser, more continuous network at 2 IU g−1 and 3 h reaction time (Vijayan et al. 2024). Pairing cross‐linking enzymes with limited proteolysis leverages complementary mechanisms. Mild hydrolysis first increases solubility and interfacial activity and exposes glutamine/lysine sites, after which cross‐linking enzyme forms a stable gel (Meng et al. 2020; Anvar et al. 2024). In zein, limited Alcalase hydrolysis maximized EAI/ESI, and a subsequent transglutaminase redistributed hydrophobic/hydrophilic residues in cross‐linked peptides to enhance amphiphilicity at the oil–water interface (Anvar et al. 2024). Their findings showed that transglutaminase treatment improved emulsifying behavior after hydrolysis, plausibly by altering the distribution of hydrophobic/hydrophilic residues and enhancing amphiphilicity at interfaces (Anvar et al. 2024). Despite their many advantages, enzymatic modifications of plant proteins can cause bitterness in the resulting hydrolysates, which limits applicability. Bitterness arises largely from increased surface hydrophobicity after exposure of buried hydrophobic groups and from low‐molecular‐weight peptides. However, enzyme selection and control of degree of hydrolysis are critical levers for managing sensory outcomes (Mosibo et al. 2024).

Chemical Techniques

Chemical modification works by adding, removing, or derivatizing functional groups within the protein structure (Kaur et al. 2025). In practice, these chemical reactions alter protein structure to fine‐tune surface charge, polarity, and conformation, thereby shifting key techno‐functional attributes such as solubility, emulsification, gelation, and even digestibility (Kaur et al. 2025; Singhi and Ozturk 2025). Mechanistically, targeted substitution or reaction of side‐chain functional groups is used to adjust net surface charge and isoelectric point. The most common targets include the ε‐amine of lysine, hydroxyls of serine and threonine, and where relevant, carboxyl groups of aspartate and glutamate (Singhi and Ozturk 2025). Typical methods include acylation (succinylation and acetylation), glycosylation, deamidation, and phosphorylation. Together, these approaches offer targeted control over interfacial behavior and network formation in complex foods (Kaur et al. 2025; Singhi and Ozturk 2025). However, the field is undergoing a paradigm shift from empirical batch testing towards a more computational design. As chemical derivatization is complex and degree‐dependent, predictive modeling is used to establish direct linkages between targeted structural alterations and macroscopic functional outcomes (Cao et al. 2026). Specifically, in a study published by Mandal et al. (2026), support vector machine and support vector regression models have been used to predict protein solubility and emulsifying activity with high predictive accuracy for solubility (R 2 = 0.8906) and gel strength (R 2 = 0.8822) by inputting only a few macromolecular structural predictors utilizing macromolecular structural predictors such as surface hydrophobicity, zeta potential, and beta‐sheet content (Mandal et al. 2026). This approach allows researchers to calculate precise chemical input needed to achieve desired functional target in silico before physical synthesis begins. Glutaminase‐driven deamidation of PP increased surface hydrophilicity and negative charge, but when subsequently heated, it yielded softer, less elastic gels. Rheological parameters and SEM images indicated that heightened electrostatic repulsion hindered aggregation and cross‐linking during gelation. In short, deamidation could boost dispersion‐related functionalities yet compromised gel network strength in a degree‐dependent manner (Wei et al. 2026). However, phosphorylation with sodium tripolyphosphate enabled 10% peanut protein isolate to form strong gels by increasing surface hydrophobicity and promoting hydrophobic interactions and disulfide bonds. At 3% sodium tripolyphosphate, gels showed the most ordered microstructure and peak mechanical performance (Hou et al. 2025). Using a pH‐shifting approach, dipotassium glycyrrhizinate complexation substantially increased solubility and encapsulation efficiency of naringenin across diverse plant proteins (faba bean, pumpkin, buckwheat, and zein). Zein (prolamin‐rich) exhibited the greatest gains, with a 117.6% increase in solubility and a 13‐fold rise in encapsulation efficiency (Wang, Jia, Zhang, et al. 2025). Chemical modification has a wide potential in protein engineering because it is operationally simple, cost‐effective, and efficient (Kaur et al. 2025). Despite demonstrated gains in techno‐functionality, adoption in commercial food systems remains limited due to concerns about residual reagents and by‐products, negative consumer perception, and regulatory scrutiny, pressures amplified by the shift toward clean‐label, minimally processed products (Zhang et al. 2018). Together, these examples show how chemical modification offers precise tools to tailor protein surface properties and guide macroscopic behavior, such as gelation, solubility, and encapsulation.

Plant Protein Retexturizing to Meat Analogue

Meat analogues include products designed to mimic either whole‐muscle cuts (e.g., steaks and fillets) or restructured meats (e.g., burgers, patties, sausages, and nuggets) (Wagner et al. 2024). These products are derived from three broad technological approaches: plant‐based formulations, animal‐cell cultivation, and fermentation‐derived biomasses (e.g., mycoprotein). This section focuses on plant‐based systems, where proteins are directly transformed into structures that replicate the texture, appearance, juiciness, and mouthfeel of animal meat (Pratap‐Singh et al. 2024; Wagner et al. 2024). A central challenge is structural mismatch between animal and plant proteins. Animal muscles are hierarchically organized, with myofibrillar assemblies aligned into fibers, creating strong compartments that retain water during cooking (Pratap‐Singh et al. 2024). This hierarchical alignment is critical for delivering meat's characteristic bite, juiciness, and directional fracture. By contrast, most legume and oilseed proteins are globular, forming isotropic gels and aggregates that lack inherent fiber orientation and the water‐holding microenvironments characteristic of muscle. Consequently, plant proteins must be restructured, unfolded, aligned, phase‐organized, and fixed into fibrous matrices to deliver meat‐like bite and directional fracture (McClements and Grossmann 2021). This transformation is achieved through restructuring techniques, high‐moisture extrusion, shear‐induced structuring, fiber spinning, and 3D printing, which play a central role in aligning, unfolding, and reassembling plant proteins into fibrous matrices.

Thermo‐Extrusion

Thermo‐extrusion is a continuous thermo‐mechanical process used to restructure plant proteins into meat‐like textures, typically operated across low‐ (<30%–35%), intermediate‐ (∼30%–50%), and high‐moisture (>50%, typically 50%–80%) regimes. At low feed moisture (<30%), expansion and aggregation yield textured vegetable protein (TVP) with a porous, rehydratable granule or flake structure suitable for crumbles, strips, and chunks after soaking (See et al. 2025). At higher moisture (>50%; high‐moisture or wet extrusion), protein dispersions, typically soy or pea, frequently complemented by wheat gluten (WG) and texturing polysaccharides/starches, are processed through a heated barrel and slit cooling die to generate continuous, anisotropic, fibrous networks suited to whole‐muscle analogues (Sha and Xiong 2020). Across protein sources, covalent (e.g., disulfide exchange) and non‐covalent interactions contribute to consolidation of the extrudate, with gluten‐containing systems often showing a stronger disulfide‐mediated contribution to rigidity and chew (Aghagholizadeh and Amiri 2025).

Functionally, extrusion couples mixing, hydration, shear, compression, and shaping with controlled thermal processes to transform a particulate protein blend into a viscoelastic melt and, ultimately, a set fibrous matrix (Aghagholizadeh and Amiri 2025). In the feed and kneading zones, proteins are mixed with water and fed into an extruder barrel, where they are subjected to high pressure and elevated temperatures. Inside the barrel, proteins undergo denaturation, where their native globular structures are disrupted, and intermolecular bonds such as hydrogen bonds, disulfide bridges, and hydrophobic interactions are broken. As the protein matrix flows through the barrel under shear, it aligns in the direction of flow. The aligned proteins exit the extruder through a die and enter a cooling zone, where a laminar flow environment promotes the reformation of new molecular interactions. These include the re‐establishment of disulfide bonds and other non‐covalent interactions that stabilize the new fibrous arrangement (Sandoval Murillo et al. 2019; Aghagholizadeh and Amiri 2025).

In high‐moisture extrusion, evidence points to the cooling die as the main structuring zone (Wagner et al. 2024; Aghagholizadeh and Amiri 2025), where four flow‐assisted mechanisms can explain fiber formation (Figure 2); (i) laminar shear with temperature‐induced viscosity gradients: The die walls cool and stiffen the near‐wall melt, whereas the core remains hotter and less viscous, steepening the velocity profile and aligning material along the flow (Sui et al. 2024), (ii) phase deformation and alignment: In multiphase protein systems (often created by thermodynamic incompatibility), a dispersed phase is stretched and coalesces into elongated domains within a continuous phase under shear (Wittek et al. 2021), (iii) elongational pre‐stretch in the breaker plate/transition zone upstream of the die can pre‐align phases or create strands that carry through into the die (van der Sman and van der Goot 2023), (iv) spinodal demixing during cooling in the die: An initially homogeneous hot melt separates into protein‐rich and water‐rich phases; concurrent shear orients the emerging bi‐continuous structure (Wang, Chen, Yan, et al. 2025). These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive and may act together depending on formulation, moisture, temperature profile, and die geometry, with the low‐Reynolds‐number, laminar flow expected for protein melts providing the common backdrop (Cornet et al. 2022).

Across these pathways, especially fourth one, the layer thickness and orientation are set by the balance of shear rate (advective alignment), Cahn–Hilliard mobility/diffusivity (M′) (phase‐separation kinetics), and the axial temperature gradient (α). Moderate α/M′ (demixing mid‐die) favors streamwise lamellae and strong anisotropy; rapid demixing at the die entrance (high M′ relative to shear) can give transverse/vertical layers; very high α:M′ (cooling too fast for mobility) suppresses macroscopic separation, yielding more isotropic texture (Sandoval Murillo et al. 2019; Aghagholizadeh and Amiri 2025). Practically, die height/length, coolant set point, and throughput control the velocity/temperature fields; longer, cooled slits and sufficient die height generally promote fibrillation (Aghagholizadeh and Amiri 2025).

Adjusting parameters such as screw speed, barrel temperature, moisture content, and die design allows for the tuning of textural attributes such as juiciness, chewiness, and elasticity (Aghagholizadeh and Amiri 2025; Wang, Lyu, Fu et al. 2023). Building on this, a recent study of the cooling‐die unit operation compared two cross‐sections (tall‐narrow vs. short‐wide), two throughputs (∼2.7 vs. 4.5 kg h−1), and four coolant inlet temperatures (36–72°C), with dense axial thermometry and an energy balance to link die heat transfer to structure formation (Wagner et al. 2024). The findings indicated that hardness of extrudates increased with the axial temperature gradient, whereas anisotropy decreased with the axial gradient but increased with die height (p < 0.05). Extruder motor torque and die inlet pressure scaled with the coolant inlet temperature and the apparent Newtonian shear rate in the die (p < 0.05) (Wagner et al. 2024). Moreover, a recent parametric study on faba bean protein‐based formulated with brewers’ spent grain showed that raising barrel temperature (∼130–160°C) and screw speed (∼100–300 rpm) promoted stronger phase separation and physical cross‐linking, yielding more pronounced fibrous morphology, enhanced textural performance, and higher water retention, without compromising protein digestibility (Fan et al. 2026).

Strategic combination of plant proteins can yield extruded matrices with layered, fibrous morphologies that reproduce meat‐like texture. WG is particularly effective because its two principal fractions provide complementary rheology: Gliadins supply viscosity/flow, whereas glutenins confer cohesiveness and elasticity (Wang et al. 2023). In a comparative study of PP with soy isolate (SPI), CP, and WG, the PP–CP–WG blend delivered the greatest hardness, chewiness, and fibrous degree (25.80 ± 0.20, 4.65 ± 0.14, and 1.25 ± 0.04, respectively; p < 0.05). Mechanistically, extrusion of all blends increased disulfide bond content by ∼35%–80% (Jiang et al. 2024). Despite these techno‐functional advantages, consumer interest in gluten‐free formulations has been extended, driven by celiac disease, gluten intolerance, and elective gluten avoidance. In soy‐based systems, adding zein showed that protein type and concentration were the primary levers for texture, rheology and microstructure of extrudate. Textural outcomes were governed by protein–protein associations and conformational organization. Fine‐tuning the zein:soy ratio modulated melt strength, anisotropy, and water retention, with zein acting as a thermoplastic, film‐forming phase that sustained fiber continuity, enabling gluten‐free products with meat‐like layering (Rolandelli et al. 2025). Nonetheless, Aslam et al. (2026) reported that although soy protein isolate could produce a dense fibrous structure, pea involvement softened structure and limited fiber development. These data highlight the importance of protein selection and ratio as important levers for tuning cross‐link density, molecular packing, and, ultimately, anisotropic texture in a multi‐protein system.

Despite its advantages, extrusion has limitations. Excessive thermal–mechanical input can drive over‐denaturation and aggregation, diminishing digestibility and sensory quality (West et al. 2024). The operation is energy‐intensive and requires tight control of process variables (Sui et al. 2024). Even so, extrusion remains the benchmark technology for plant‐protein structuring, with a consistent record of delivering meat‐like textures (McClements and Grossmann 2021).

For high‐moisture extrusion, AI is most useful when formulation variables are analyzed together with process variables and measurable structural outcomes. Relevant inputs include protein source and ratio, moisture content, barrel temperature, screw speed, specific mechanical energy, die pressure, cooling‐die geometry, temperature gradient, and residence time. Outputs should include not only hardness or chewiness but also anisotropy, tensile strength, water retention, visual fibrousness, and digestibility. Recent studies in AI‐assisted extrusion modeling have demonstrated how predictive frameworks can improve extrudate quality, reduce experimental burden, and support real‐time quality assessment. For example, Wu et al. (2023) combined machine‐vision image analysis with a back‐propagation neural network optimized using particle swarm optimization and genetic algorithms to predict the hardness, resilience, and chewiness of PP extrudates. This approach linked non‐destructive image features to textural properties and therefore provides a potential route for in‐line quality monitoring without relying solely on destructive texture analysis (Wu et al. 2023). Similarly, Jiang et al. (2025) compared machine learning‐guided Bayesian optimization with conventional response surface methodology for plant‐based protein extrusion and showed that Bayesian optimization could identify promising high‐moisture extrusion conditions with fewer experimental trials (Jiang et al. 2025). Further progress has been made using multi‐objective Bayesian optimization. Theng et al. (2025) developed a human‐guided multi‐objective Bayesian optimization framework for high‐moisture meat analogues, using a Gaussian‐process surrogate model to map extrusion parameters, including moisture content, barrel temperature, and screw speed, to textural properties and generate candidates for subsequent trials (Theng et al. 2025). In parallel, Gulzar et al. (2025) applied AI‐assisted optimization to soy protein concentrate‐based high‐moisture meat analogues, using sorbitol level and processing variables to tailor textural properties toward chicken‐ and beef‐like targets. These studies show that AI can support not only process optimization but also formulation design, where the goal is to match specific meat‐like texture profiles rather than simply maximize one response (Gulzar et al. 2025). AI can also assist extrusion design upstream by predicting the rheological behavior of protein‐rich formulations before processing. Dahl et al. (2025) used compositional data from pea‐ and faba‐protein biopolymer mixtures to predict rheological parameters using machine learning models, showing that ML can help identify relationships between formulation composition and structure‐forming potential during high‐moisture extrusion (Dahl et al. 2025). Together, these studies suggest that AI‐enabled extrusion workflows can integrate formulation data, process variables, image‐based quality attributes, rheology, and final texture into a closed‐loop optimization system while still requiring validation across protein sources, extruder geometries, and industrial scales.

Shear‐Cell Technology

Shear‐cell technology offers another restructuring method focused on replicating the alignment of muscle fibbers under controlled shear conditions (Figure 3). It is a batch process where protein blends are placed between a rotating and stationary cone or cylinder (Cornet et al. 2022). Shear‐cell processing structures plant proteins under well‐defined shear at elevated temperature using sealed conical or concentric cylinder devices operated typically around 95–140°C; hydrated proteins are subjected to a shear for ∼15 min during heating and then cooled at rest, which aligns partially denatured domains into anisotropic, fibrous matrices whose properties depend on temperature, solids level, and co‐ingredients (Velichko et al. 2019; Cornet et al. 2022). Compared with thermal extrusion, shear cells are batch rather than continuous, decouple shear rate from throughput, and offer a narrower thermal range and longer residence times, making them well suited for mechanistic studies and formulation screening even though thermal extrusion remains superior for high‐throughput manufacturing (Velichko et al. 2019; Cornet et al. 2022). Scale‐up from conical cells (∼200 g) to cylinder units (∼7.5 kg) has been demonstrated without fundamental barriers (Krintiras et al. 2016). Recent high‐pressure shear‐cell work under extrusion‐like conditions (140–160°C, 20 bar, 5–40 s−1) showed that both soy protein concentrate and PP isolate could form fibrous structures. In‐process rheology revealed polymerization onset followed by a viscosity drop, consistent with fracturing/lamination of an emerging network as a key structuring pathway, and plate‐plate geometries (with radial shear‐rate gradients) promoted more cross‐connected fibers than cone–plate geometries (Sägesser et al. 2025). Process–structure response patterns broadly mirrored extrusion conditions for soy systems, supporting the use of shear cells as rapid screens for raw materials, although higher mechanical energy capacity and improved sealing may be required to replicate extrusion results across diverse ingredients (Sägesser et al. 2025). Recent advances in AI‐assisted modeling can further strengthen the role of shear‐cell technology as a rapid screening platform for plant‐protein structuring. Because shear cells decouple shear rate from throughput and provide better‐controlled thermal and mechanical conditions than extrusion, they can generate structured datasets linking formulation variables, temperature, shear rate, residence time, cooling profile, in‐process rheology, and final anisotropy (Cornet et al. 2022). These datasets can be used to train machine learning models that predict whether a given protein blend is likely to form fibrous structures before being transferred to high‐moisture extrusion. In addition, image‐analysis and computer‐vision tools, such as automated fibrousness quantification, can be integrated with shear‐cell experiments to objectively score fiber alignment and structural anisotropy (Ma et al. 2024). Similarly, ML‐based rheology prediction models may help identify formulation windows with suitable viscoelastic properties for shear‐induced structuring (Dahl et al. 2025). Thus, shear‐cell systems can serve not only as mechanistic tools but also as data‐generation platforms for AI‐guided formulation screening and scale‐up toward extrusion‐based manufacturing. This method allows for the development of layered, anisotropic structures without the extreme conditions of extrusion. It provides greater control over texture formation and is particularly suitable for laboratory and pilot‐scale research and protein structuring and prototyping (Velichko et al. 2019; Sägesser et al. 2025).

3D Printing

3D printing builds plant‐protein cuts layer‐by‐layer by depositing a viscoelastic ink through a nozzle along pre‐programmed paths; shear and mild extensional flow inside the nozzle orient protein aggregates along the path, and rapid post‐print setting (thermal, enzymatic, or ionic) preserves that orientation (Figure 3) (Wen et al. 2023). Compared with high‐moisture extrusion or shear‐cell processing, 3D printing offers (i) precise spatial control (placing fibers where a steak or chicken breast would naturally have them); (ii) separation of geometry from microstructure, where the computer‐aided design defines the external form while the deposition pattern, nozzle‐induced alignment, and post‐print setting determine fibrillation; and (iii) a lower thermal load that helps preserve heat‐sensitive constituents (Wang and Liu 2021; Wen et al. 2023). However, key limitations include lower production rates, potential interlayer weakness, and tight rheology demands; inks must flow cleanly under pressure, then recover structure fast enough to avoid slumping, beading, or layer bleed (Wang and Liu 2021; Tian et al. 2024). Fabrication of fibrillated, animal‐like cuts commences with ink design and rheological control. A practical printability method combines shear‐thinning, a modest yield stress, and rapid elastic recovery (G′ rebound); targeting a nozzle‐scale Deborah number (De = λ·γ̇) at or above unity helps ensure that orientation achieved in the nozzle is not immediately lost after deposition (Wang and Liu 2021). In a controlled study on protein‐rich inks (25% total solids), soy protein isolate and WG were blended with rice protein to tune flow, self‐support, and build accuracy meat analogue (Qiu et al. 2023). Rheological results showed strong shear‐thinning, whereas the consistency index (K) and G′ decreased as rice protein increased, easing extrusion. Moreover, G′ > G″ for all formulations within the LVR, but tanδ rose with rice protein, indicating greater flowability without losing elastic dominance (Qiu et al. 2023). Practically, SPI–WG pastes alone were too stiff and elastic for precise deposition, whereas partial replacement with rice protein produced cleaner filament formation, better interlayer registration, and higher dimensional fidelity (Qiu et al. 2023). Thus, efficient fibrillation depends on two stages: low resistance in‐nozzle to generate alignment, followed by fast structural setting on the bed, maintaining fiber continuity and line fidelity without oozing. Thermosensitive lipids (e.g., small cocoa‐butter additions) can further lower pressure demand inside the nozzle and then solidify on the bed to maintain filaments, but they require tight temperature control to avoid premature setting or post‐print oil migration (Wang and Liu 2021). Microstructural evidence confirmed that printing imposed directional order on fibril‐containing pastes (Calton et al. 2023). X‐ray microtomography of ion‐cross‐linked slabs printed as parallel strands showed PP fibrils aligned along the nozzle path within each strand, whereas molded control exhibited random fibril orientation across planes. They discussed that flow in the nozzle and oriented fibrils; then, deposition fixed that orientation within discrete strands, and finally, post‐print calcium‐alginate cross‐linking fused strands into a continuous gel embedding aligned fibrils. However, molding removed the flow‐alignment step and yielded isotropic texture (Calton et al. 2023). Although a 3D printer cannot deliver the screw‐induced deformation of extrusion, it compensates with voxel‐level control: The nozzle generates alignment, post‐print chemistry secures it, and toolpaths place those fibers exactly where whole‐cut meats need them, enabling fibrillated, animal‐shaped structures with tunable bite and juiciness and a clear pathway to hybridization with extrusion‐made filaments if higher line speeds are required. AI‐assisted modeling is particularly relevant for 3D printing because printability depends on multiple interacting variables, including ink composition, protein–polysaccharide ratio, moisture content, yield stress, shear‐thinning behavior, elastic recovery, nozzle diameter, extrusion pressure, printing speed, layer height, and post‐print setting conditions. Machine learning models can help predict whether a given formulation is able to exhibit sufficient flow through the nozzle while maintaining shape fidelity after deposition (Dahl et al. 2025). For example, ML‐based rheological prediction frameworks can link formulation composition to viscosity, viscoelasticity, and nonlinear deformation behavior, which are critical parameters for extrusion‐based food printing (Lee et al. 2024; Dahl et al. 2025). Such models could reduce the number of empirical trials needed to identify printable plant‐protein inks with suitable self‐supporting capacity and interlayer adhesion. Computer vision and image‐analysis methods can quantify filament spreading, dimensional accuracy, layer collapse, pore structure, and fiber alignment, thereby providing objective feedback for adjusting printing parameters (Kok Wah 2025). Recent reviews on AI‐driven 3D and 4D food printing emphasize that coupling formulation databases, rheological data, printer settings, and image‐based quality metrics could enable closed‐loop control of food printing processes (Kok Wah 2025; Ekrami et al. 2025). For plant‐protein meat analogues, this approach is especially valuable because 3D printing aims not only to generate an external meat‐like geometry but also to place fibrous or anisotropic domains in predefined locations. Therefore, AI‐enabled printing workflows could help connect ink formulation, nozzle‐induced alignment, deposition pattern, post‐print gelation, and final texture into a more systematic design framework (Table 3).

Other Fibrilization Methods

Spinning, including electrospinning, solution‐blow spinning, and wet spinning, adapts textile processes to food biopolymers by which a protein solution or dispersion is stretched into a continuous jet and then solidified by solvent evaporation, coagulation, or thermal/ionic setting, yielding fibers from tens of nanometers to tens of micrometers in diameter with controllable orientation and packing (Chen et al. 2023). Spun plant‐protein fibers typically exhibit high surface‐area‐to‐volume ratios and molecular orientation, enabling tunable fiber diameter, alignment, and porosity toward meat‐like textures (Cui et al. 2022). In phase‐separation wet spinning of soy protein isolate, spectroscopy showed a shift toward β‐sheet/β‐turn secondary structure (with reduced α‐helix), consistent with chain extension and stronger network formation (Wang, Wang, Ji et al. 2025). With appropriately tuned processing parameters, the resulting filaments may even achieve higher structural integrity and more water retention along with improved chewiness and thermal stability suitable for culinary processing (Cui et al. 2022; Qian et al. 2025). The spinning dope generally exhibits strong shear‐thinning; thus, higher protein levels raise viscosity and jet stability, improving filament continuity (Wang, Wang, Ji et al. 2025). However, many formulations still rely on volatile or nonfood‐grade solvents (though water/ethanol and fully aqueous systems are emerging), wet spinning typically requires washing/coagulation steps with associated water use, and stable jet/filament formation demands tight control of viscoelasticity plus conductivity and interfacial stability (Chen et al. 2022; Falsafi et al. 2023).

For amyloid‐like fibrils, globular plant proteins are partially hydrolyzed and reorganized into β‐sheet‐rich fibrils by heating under acidic, low‐salt conditions (80–95°C, pH 1.6–2), producing semiflexible filaments micrometers long and a few nanometers thick. These fibrils exhibit high aspect ratio, interfacial activity, and network‐forming capacity (Chen et al. 2023; Khalid et al. 2025). Functionally, amyloid fibrils reinforce interfaces and gels (Wang, Guo, Li et al. 2025). A recent work showed long soy glycinin fibrils diffused rapidly to oil–water interfaces, lowered interfacial tension, and built elastic interfacial films with the highest dilatational modulus, widening the linear viscoelastic regime of high‐internal‐phase emulsions and improving extrudability, printability, and shape fidelity of 3D‐printed constructs (Wang, Guo, Li et al. 2025). Similarly, other studies reported that chickpea and pea fibrils exhibited more surface functional groups and enhanced interfacial properties, thus leading to more emulsion stability and foaming properties, supporting their generality as colloidal structuring agents in plant‐based foods (Wu et al. 2022; Xiao et al. 2025). In the context of meat analogues, amyloid‐like fibrils are particularly suited to high‐moisture matrices (gels/foams) where their high surface functionality and entanglement sustain anisotropy, and they can be combined with other processes (Chen et al. 2023).

Integrated AI‐Driven Design Framework, Limitations, and Future Directions

The previous sections show that AI has the greatest value when it is connected to the physical and biochemical variables that control plant‐protein structuring. Rather than adding AI as a separate final step, data‐driven methods should be embedded where decisions are actually made: protein‐source selection, extraction severity, functional modification, rheology control, texturization, quality assessment, and sustainability comparison. In such a framework, product targets such as amino acid adequacy, digestibility, fibrousness, water retention, texture, sensory quality, cost, and environmental impact are translated into measurable inputs and outputs. Machine learning, Bayesian optimization, computer vision, and physics‐informed modeling can then help prioritize formulation–process combinations for experimental testing.

Closed‐Loop AI‐Assisted Design Workflow

A closed‐loop AI‐assisted design workflow for plant‐protein meat analogues can be conceptualized as five interconnected layers. First, the product target is defined, including nutrition, texture, fibrousness, juiciness, flavor, cost, allergen or antinutrient constraints, and sustainability (Jiang et al. 2025; Dahl et al. 2025). Second, relevant data are collected from ingredient composition, amino acid profile, protein purity, rheology, microstructure, processing conditions, instrumental texture, sensory results, and environmental indicators (Ma et al. 2024; Ticǎ et al. 2025). Third, models are trained to relate ingredient and process variables to product attributes using tools such as random forests, support vector machines, neural networks, Bayesian optimization, or computer vision. Fourth, the predicted candidates are tested experimentally through extraction, rheology, extrusion, shear‐cell processing, printing, imaging, digestibility, and sensory analysis. Finally, the new results are returned to the dataset so that the model becomes more specific to the protein source, equipment, and target product. This cycle is most useful when it reduces the number of trials without removing experimental verification. Cross‐Stage Integration From Protein Source to Final Product

AI‐assisted design can support decision‐making across the entire plant‐protein meat analogue pipeline. At the raw material level, AI can help rank protein sources and blends according to amino acid complementarity, digestibility, allergenicity risk, anti‐nutritional factors, cost, and sustainability. At the extraction and modification stage, machine learning models can link processing variables such as pH, temperature, time, ultrasound intensity, electric field strength, enzymatic treatment, or fermentation conditions to protein yield, purity, solubility, emulsification, gelation, and structural integrity (Jiang et al. 2025). This is important because aggressive processing may improve extraction yield but simultaneously reduce functional quality. At the structuring stage, AI can integrate formulation, rheology, thermal history, shear conditions, cooling profiles, image‐based fiber metrics, and instrumental texture into predictive models for extrusion, shear‐cell processing, and 3D printing. For example, Bayesian optimization has been applied to high‐moisture extrusion to identify process conditions with fewer experimental trials, while computer vision has been used to quantify visual fibrousness and predict texture‐related attributes (Jiang et al. 2025; Ma et al. 2024). Similarly, ML‐based rheology models can predict viscoelastic parameters of plant‐protein and hydrocolloid mixtures, supporting the design of printable or extrudable formulations with suitable flow and structure recovery (Lee et al. 2024; Dahl et al. 2025). Beyond structure and texture, AI can contribute to flavor, sensory, and sustainability optimization. Machine learning models can mine volatile‐compound datasets, sensory descriptors, and consumer preference data to identify ingredient combinations that reduce beany, bitter, or earthy off‐notes while enhancing savory, roasted, umami, or kokumi attributes (Hao et al. 2025; Hiregoudar et al. 2026). AI‐assisted sustainability models can also connect formulation and processing choices to water use, energy consumption, greenhouse gas emissions, yield losses, and waste generation (Nath et al. 2024; Ticǎ et al. 2025). Therefore, AI enables a shift from sequential product development to systems‐level design, where nutrition, function, processing, sensory quality, and sustainability are evaluated together.

Challenges, Limitations, and Industrial Readiness

Despite its potential, AI implementation in plant‐protein meat analogue development remains limited by several scientific and industrial challenges. First, most available datasets are small, fragmented, and generated under different experimental conditions. Rheological, textural, microstructural, sensory, and digestibility data are often measured using different protocols, making cross‐study model training difficult. This is especially problematic for plant proteins, where functionality depends strongly on cultivar, extraction history, protein purity, denaturation state, particle size, residual fiber, starch, lipid, polyphenols, and processing history (Ravindran et al. 2024; Rashwan et al. 2025; Opazo‐Navarrete et al. 2025). Second, model generalizability remains a major limitation. A model trained on one protein source, such as soy or pea, may not transfer reliably to faba bean, chickpea, canola, lentil, or algae proteins. Similarly, models developed using a specific extruder, shear cell, or 3D printer may not predict outcomes accurately at pilot or industrial scale because residence time, heat transfer, shear profile, cooling rate, and equipment geometry differ significantly. Therefore, AI models must be tested across protein sources, formulations, and equipment scales before being considered industrially robust (Jiang et al. 2025; Rashvand et al. 2025). Third, many AI models remain difficult to interpret. Black‐box models may provide accurate predictions but limited mechanistic insight into why a formulation succeeds or fails. This can reduce trust among food scientists, process engineers, regulatory teams, and industry users. Explainable AI approaches, such as feature importance analysis, SHAP values, and interpretable surrogate models, should therefore be integrated into plant‐protein design workflows to identify which variables most strongly influence texture, fibrousness, digestibility, or sensory quality (Rashvand et al. 2025). Fourth, AI predictions require experimental and regulatory validation. Predicted nutritional adequacy does not replace digestibility measurements, DIAAS/PDCAAS assessment, or amino acid analysis (Opazo‐Navarrete et al. 2025; Tormási et al. 2025). Predicted allergenicity does not replace immunological testing or regulatory review. Predicted sensory similarity does not replace consumer testing. Similarly, AI‐optimized processing conditions must be validated for safety, reproducibility, scalability, and economic feasibility. Thus, AI should accelerate and prioritize experimental work, but validated measurements remain essential.

Future Roadmap

Future progress should focus on building AI‐ready infrastructure for plant‐protein meat analogue development. In the short term, the field needs standardized datasets linking protein source, extraction method, composition, amino acid profile, anti‐nutritional factors, rheology, processing conditions, microstructure, texture, digestibility, sensory attributes, and sustainability metrics. Standardized fibrousness scores, rheological protocols, image‐analysis methods, and extrusion/shear‐cell reporting formats would greatly improve model comparability across laboratories. In the midterm, physics‐informed machine learning and hybrid modeling should be prioritized. These approaches can combine data‐driven prediction with known physical principles, such as rheological constitutive models, heat and mass transfer, phase separation, Cahn–Hilliard‐type demixing, protein aggregation kinetics, and flow‐induced alignment. Such models would be particularly valuable for high‐moisture extrusion, shear‐cell processing, and 3D printing, where texture formation depends on coupled thermal, mechanical, and molecular events (Cornet et al. 2022). Digital twins of extrusion, shear‐cell, or printing processes could then simulate how changes in formulation, moisture, shear, cooling, and geometry influence anisotropy and final texture before physical trials are conducted. In the long term, AI‐assisted plant‐protein design may evolve toward autonomous or semi‐autonomous product development platforms. These systems could combine computational protein design, precision fermentation, ingredient screening, closed‐loop extrusion or printing, real‐time imaging, in‐line rheology, and sensory prediction to generate customized plant‐protein foods. Such platforms could be used to design products for specific nutritional needs, such as older adults, athletes, or individuals with cardiometabolic risk, while also meeting sustainability and affordability targets. Achieving this vision will require collaboration among food scientists, process engineers, computational biologists, data scientists, sensory scientists, and regulatory experts. Overall, AI is most valuable when it functions as an integrative design layer connecting plant‐protein chemistry, processing, structure, nutrition, sensory quality, and sustainability. With standardized datasets, interpretable models, and experimental validation, AI‐assisted workflows can help move plant‐protein meat analogues beyond empirical formulation toward more predictable, scalable, and nutritionally credible product design.

Conclusion and Future Trends

Plant‐protein meat analogues require coordinated control of nutrition, digestibility, functionality, fibrous structure formation, sensory quality, scalability, and sustainability. Their main challenge is not only that plant proteins differ from animal proteins in amino acid profile but also that many are globular, matrix‐dependent, and sensitive to extraction and processing history. Upstream extraction and modification must therefore be designed to improve protein recovery, solubility, interfacial activity, gelation, rheology, and compatibility with structuring technologies. High‐moisture extrusion remains the most mature platform for continuous anisotropic structures, while shear‐cell processing provides controlled mechanistic screening, 3D printing enables spatial placement of fibrous domains, and spinning or amyloid‐like fibril formation can reinforce interfaces and gels. AI can strengthen this development pipeline when it is tied to measurable food‐science variables rather than treated as a generic optimization layer. Machine learning, Bayesian optimization, computer vision, structure prediction, and physics‐informed modeling can help prioritize protein sources, processing windows, printable or extrudable formulations, and quality‐control parameters. Their usefulness depends on standardized datasets, transparent models, and validation through digestibility assays, rheology, microstructure, texture analysis, sensory testing, and scale‐up trials. The next generation of plant‐protein meat analogues will therefore require closer integration of food chemistry, process engineering, nutrition science, sensory science, sustainability assessment, and computational modeling. With stronger experimental datasets and validated modeling frameworks, plant‐protein architectures can move beyond simply being meat‐like toward products that are nutritionally credible, sensorially acceptable, scalable, and environmentally defensible.
