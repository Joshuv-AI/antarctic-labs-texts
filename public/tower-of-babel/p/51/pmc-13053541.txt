Post-traumatic Candida infection leading to bilateral wrist rice body tenosynovitis: a case report and literature review
Authors: Shaohua Wang, Hyosim Ro, Hexing Xu, Longyun Fang, Xun Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRice body tenosynovitis is a rare condition of uncertain etiology characterized by the formation of multiple fibrinous particles within the synovium or synovial fluid. It is frequently associated with chronic inflammation due to infection or rheumatoid arthritis and most commonly affects large joints such as the shoulder and knee. Simultaneous bilateral wrist involvement following trauma is exceptionally uncommon.Case presentationA 59-year-old man presented with persistent bilateral wrist swelling for 10 months after a fall. MRI demonstrated extensive fluid accumulation and synovial thickening within the flexor tendon sheaths of both wrists. Surgical exploration revealed numerous yellow rice-like loose bodies and inflammatory proliferative tissue in the tendon sheaths. Pathology confirmed inflammatory granulation tissue with fibrinous exudate, and fungal culture identified Candida species infection. The left wrist recovered well after the initial surgery, but the right wrist recurred after 6 months and required a second thorough debridement, after which it resolved completely.ConclusionsThis report describes a rare case of bilateral wrist rice body tenosynovitis secondary to post-traumatic Candida infection with unilateral postoperative recurrence. It emphasizes that incomplete removal of inflammatory tenosynovium remains the primary cause of recurrence, even with targeted itraconazole antifungal therapy. Thorough exploration and radical excision of all inflammatory tissue and rice bodies during surgery are essential to prevent relapse.Keywords: Rice bodies, Tenosynovitis, Bilateral wrist, Candida infection, Recurrence prevention

Introduction

Rice body (RB) tenosynovitis is a rare clinical entity of unknown cause. It is defined by the presence of multiple small, loose fibrin particles in the synovial fluid or attached to the synovial membrane. Riese first described these structures in tuberculous arthritis in 1895 [1]. Histologically, rice bodies feature an eosinophilic core surrounded by fibrin. Their pathogenesis is not fully understood; proposed mechanisms include synovial microinfarction resulting in necrosis and shedding, with subsequent fibrin encapsulation in the synovial fluid [2], or independent formation followed by fibronectin and fibrin deposition [3]. Rice bodies have been observed in rheumatoid arthritis [4, 5], though without clear correlation to disease activity, severity, or outcome. They have also been linked to tuberculous and nontuberculous mycobacterial infections, fungal infections [6], chronic foreign body reactions, and tenosynovitis. Typical locations include the shoulder, knee, elbow, and ankle [7], with the shoulder and knee predominating. Wrist involvement, especially bilateral simultaneous disease, is extremely rare.

In the present case, both wrist tendon sheaths contained abundant yellow fibrinous debris and rice-like bodies accompanied by synovial thickening. Given these distinctive clinical and imaging features, we present this case below.

Case presentation

The patient was a 59-year-old man who sustained a fall in August 2021 resulting in bilateral wrist swelling without open wounds, accompanied by upper limb swelling and limited motion. Initial antibiotic treatment provided no relief, and the swelling persisted. He was admitted in June 2022 with a 10-month history of bilateral wrist swelling. His medical history included hypertension for 3 years, diabetes mellitus for 7 years (well controlled), arrhythmia (premature atrial contractions) for 11 years, chronic nephritis for 3 months, and asthma for 34 years. He had a known allergy to compound phenobarbital.

On physical examination, there was localized swelling on the ulnar aspect of both wrists with mildly elevated skin temperature but no significant tenderness. Grip strength and wrist range of motion were essentially normal. Laboratory tests showed an erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 3.0 mm/h. Blood cultures and β-D-glucan assay were negative, excluding systemic fungal infection.

MRI demonstrated clear bony structures with minimal joint effusion. Extensive fluid collections and synovial thickening were noted in the flexor tendon sheaths from the distal radius and ulna to the metacarpal level bilaterally, along with surrounding soft tissue swelling

Surgical treatment

A palmar Z-incision was made on the left wrist. After soft tissue dissection, the flexor tendons and sheaths were exposed. The carpal tunnel was released, and the median nerve along with the flexor tendons to the index, middle, ring, and little fingers were identified. Profuse yellow rice-like loose bodies were found in the deeper tissue planes, with inflamed and adherent synovium. The median nerve was meticulously protected, and the rice bodies along with the inflammatory tenosynovium were carefully excised. Given the patient’s diabetes, we considered that a Becker-Gilbert fasciocutaneous flap would be a reliable option for coverage if soft tissue defects or nerve exposure occurred [8]; however, it was not required. Specimens were sent for histopathology and fungal culture. The right wrist was treated in an identical manner. Postoperative fungal culture grew Candida species sensitive to itraconazole. The patient received itraconazole 200 mg twice daily for 6 weeks.

Recurrence and reoperation

Six months postoperatively, the patient returned with recurrent swelling of the right wrist. Examination revealed an old scar at the surgical site, swelling over the right wrist and proximal phalanx of the index finger, normal skin temperature, no tenderness, limited fist clenching and extension, but preserved wrist motion. Re-exploration through the previous incision showed numerous yellow rice-like bodies around the tendon sheath and a small amount of pale yellow viscous fluid. The lesion contents and rice bodies were completely removed, and the wound was thoroughly irrigated.

Intraoperative views during the second right wrist surgery (A, B) demonstrating rice bodies within the flexor tendon sheath and excised palmar tissue

Follow-up

The surgical wounds healed uneventfully, with complete closure by 3 months. At 12-month follow-up, there was no evidence of recurrence, and wrist function had returned to normal. The patient reported no pain or limitation in daily activities such as gripping a cup, writing, or dressing.

Discussion

Rice body tenosynovitis is an uncommon disorder whose precise formation mechanism remains elusive. Possible contributing factors include genetic predisposition, autoimmune processes, chronic irritation, hypersensitivity, and abnormalities in fibrin metabolism [3]. Repetitive microtrauma or overuse may promote wear leading to protein fiber deposition and rice body formation, though this typically occurs in the shoulder, hip, knee, and elbow [9, 10]. While associations with rheumatoid arthritis and tuberculosis have been reported, causal relationships are not firmly established [11, 12]. Other proposed pathways involve synovial injury, repeated microtrauma, repair processes, and secondary degeneration leading to chronic accumulation of protein fibers [13]. Foreign body reactions to prosthetic materials can also induce rice bodies in chronic tenosynovitis, though these differ histologically from synovium-derived bodies [14].

Various etiologies can produce similar rice body structures, yet the underlying formation mechanisms may differ [15, 16]. Arthroscopic and open surgical specimens typically show numerous small particles with a pale yellow, granular, or fish-eye appearance on gross examination. Histopathology usually reveals acellular extracellular matrix composed primarily of type I collagen with minimal inflammation [17]. Rice body characteristics vary with the inciting injury; for instance, synovial fluid in osteoarthritis differs from the fibrinous fluid in rheumatoid arthritis [18]. Mycobacterial infections often exhibit distinctive features such as necrotizing granulomas, multinucleated giant cells, and calcification around rice bodies [19].

In this case, bilateral wrist disease developed after a single traumatic fall. We hypothesize that the trauma caused local synovial injury, hematoma organization, and Candida colonization, triggering rice body formation—a mechanism distinct from most previously reported causes. Differential diagnosis of wrist tenosynovitis should exclude common entities such as De Quervain tenosynovitis. Recent case-control studies found no significant radiographic differences in radial styloid morphology between De Quervain patients and controls [20]. MRI in our case showed only diffuse fluid and synovial thickening without clearly depicting rice bodies, likely due to resolution limitations, consistent with prior reports [21]. Although non-traumatic wrist disorders are prevalent, high-quality clinical guidelines remain scarce [22], contributing to the lack of standardized management for infectious rice body tenosynovitis.

Treatment and prognosis depend on identifying the underlying cause—whether rheumatoid arthritis, tuberculous or nontuberculous mycobacteria, idiopathic tenosynovitis, trauma-related, or fungal infection such as Candida. We performed a systematic PubMed (MEDLINE) search of the literature from 2021 to 2025 and identified 18 reports describing 21 cases, whose clinical features are summarized in Table 1. Our patient shared similarities with many reported cases: middle-aged, multiple comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes with good glycemic control, arrhythmia, chronic nephritis, asthma), progressive bilateral wrist swelling after trauma, culture-proven Candida infection, and right-sided recurrence at 6 months after initial surgery that resolved after repeat thorough clearance of residual inflammatory synovium. In the reviewed literature, surgery (thorough synovectomy) was the mainstay in 90% of cases, yielding significant symptom improvement without recurrence. Adjunctive therapy was tailored to etiology, including antituberculous, antimycobacterial, antirheumatic, or anti-brucella agents.

Schindele et al. [40] noted that rice bodies are often linked to rheumatoid arthritis, with hand surgery for RA shifting from fusion toward joint-preserving procedures. However, in infectious cases such as ours, surgical intervention remains primary. The Candida infection likely arose from post-traumatic skin flora inoculation or hematogenous spread in the setting of diabetes-related local immunosuppression (no evidence of systemic candidemia was found despite multiple comorbidities). The unilateral recurrence (right side only) was attributed to more limited exploration of the flexor tendon sheath compartments or residual inflammatory synovium during the index procedure, as confirmed by findings at reoperation. Itraconazole (200 mg twice daily for 6 weeks) was well tolerated with normal liver and kidney function monitoring and therapeutic drug levels.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. As a single-case observational report, causality between trauma, Candida colonization, and rice body formation cannot be definitively established. The 12-month follow-up period limits assessment of long-term recurrence risk and late complications. Finally, the literature review was restricted to English-language publications from 2021 to 2025, potentially missing relevant reports in other languages. Despite these constraints, this unusual case of post-traumatic fungal infection with rice body formation provides valuable reference for orthopedic trauma surgeons. It particularly highlights the critical role of thorough surgical debridement in achieving infection control in such complex patients.

Conclusions

This case involved bilateral wrist rice body tenosynovitis caused by Candida infection following trauma. The left wrist remained recurrence-free at 12 months after initial radical debridement, while the right wrist recurred at 6 months due to residual inflammatory synovium. Complete resolution followed reoperation with thorough lesion clearance on both sides, with sustained good outcomes. This report underscores that, even with antifungal therapy, failure to fully explore and excise all inflammatory tenosynovium is the main driver of recurrence. Particular vigilance for hematogenous dissemination is warranted in immunocompromised patients (e.g., those with diabetes or chronic nephritis).
