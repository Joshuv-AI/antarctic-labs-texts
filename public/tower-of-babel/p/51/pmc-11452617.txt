Exploring the role of TNF-α, TGF-β, and IL-6 serum levels in categorical and noncategorical models of mood and psychosis
Authors: Maja Pantovic-Stefanovic, Milica Velimirovic, Vladimir Jurisic, Marija Puric, Marta Gostiljac, Sara Dodic, Ivana Minic, Milica Nesic, Tatjana Nikolic, Natasa Petronijevic, Maja Ivkovic, Bojana Dunjic-Kostic
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Psychotic and mood disorders are discussed as part of the same continuum. The potential role of immune dysregulation in defining their clinical presentations, however, remains unclear. Differences in TNF-α, IL-6 and TGF-β levels were investigated in 143 patients with schizophrenia (SCH = 63) and bipolar disorder (BD = 80), in remission. Cytokines were evaluated against the dimensional assessment of psychosis and affective symptoms using the schizo-bipolar scale, together with the severity of the same symptom domains measured by the brief psychiatric rating scale (BPRS). Lower TGF-β was associated with more lifetime episodes, family risk for psychosis, and more severe mood and psychotic symptoms in all patients. BPRS Affect symptoms domain correlated with lower TGF-β levels in BD, and higher TGF-β levels in SCH patients. Using moderated mediation analysis, TGF-β was a relevant predictor only in the setting of non-categorical symptom distribution, with familial risk for psychosis confirmed as a significant moderator. Severity of BPRS Affect symptoms domain was an independent predictor of inclination towards the psychosis spectrum. The underlying immune dysregulation may be shared by the disorders, rather than a unique characteristic of each, having significant implications for our understanding of the continuum vs. categorical approach to psychosis and mood disorders.

Introduction

Psychosis and mood dysregulation are not mutually exclusive and can co-occur, with psychotic episodes emerging in the context of mood disorders, whereas mood symptoms may accompany primary psychotic illnesses1. The disorders are also believed not to be entirely distinct and are discussed as part of the same continuum, particularly in the case of schizophrenia (SCH) and bipolar disorder (BD)2,3. This non-categorical approach posits that these disorders exist on a spectrum, with prototypic SCH and BD cases at the extremes with varying degrees of severity and type of symptomatology between them4. In contrast, the categorical approach views the disorders as distinct entities with separate etiologies and pathophysiological mechanisms3.

Previous studies have reported a disbalance in pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines in both SCH and BD patients, suggesting a potential role of immune dysregulation in their development and progression5–9. Specifically, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) have all been implicated in their pathophysiology10–13.

Patients with SCH typically exhibit higher levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6, while those with BD show an increase in anti-inflammatory molecules like TGF-β14. Though some studies report contrasting results15, significant correlations between these cytokines and psychopathological symptoms have also been documented. TNF-α and IL-6 show strong associations with hallmark symptoms of SCH (blunted affect and alogia) and BD (depression)16,17. In contrast, TGF-β is linked to psychosis10,18 as well as changes in both manic and depressive symptoms in patients with mood disorders14, suggesting immune-mediated changes in these cytokines may contribute to the shared psychopathological profiles of the disorders. However, the specific patterns and relationships between cytokines and psychopathology, in the context of potential overlap between psychotic and mood symptoms, remain unclear.

TNF-α and IL-6 are mainly produced by T-helper type 1 (Th1) and T-helper type 2 (Th2) cells, respectively. They are found to affect neuronal plasticity, synaptic connectivity and alter neurotransmission pathways driving mood alterations19,20 and psychotic symptoms21–23. TNF-α can mediate neurodegeneration23, but also neuroprotection with high expression in the amygdala24, while IL-6 is crucial for neuronal development and survival21. In experimental studies, TNF-α and IL-6 are found to be related to both SCH and BD-like behaviors in pro-inflammatory conditions20,21,25.

TGF-β, on the other hand, is considered a regulatory molecule, responsible for maintaining the balance between Th1 and Th2 cytokines. This mechanism is an essential component in etiopathogenesis of psychiatric disorders such as SCH and BD15,26,27. Its anti-apoptotic functions foster neuronal survival and inhibit cell death with TGF-β signaling, modulating synaptic transmission and maintaining balance of the excitatory/inhibitory pathways28.

Despite the prominent role of these cytokines in regulation of mood and psychotic symptoms, studies assessing their relationship to the course of illness and symptoms in SCH and BD, using both categorical and non-categorical approaches, are lacking.

Our study investigates the levels of TNF-α, IL-6 and TGF-β in patients diagnosed with SCH and BD, using a categorical approach. By also applying a non-categorical approach, it further aims to assess the relationship between these immune mediators and specific symptom domains, perceiving the patients as a unified group, possibly pertaining to one continuum.

Methods

Patients and study design

The study was conducted at the Clinic for Psychiatry, University Clinical Centre of Serbia, Belgrade, and the Institute for Medical and Clinical Biochemistry, School of Medicine, University of Belgrade. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Clinical Centre of Serbia (622/1) and conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1989.

One hundred-seventy patients treated for SCH or BD (SCH = 81 and BD = 89) provided written informed consent to participate in the study. Subsequent screening for inclusion/exclusion criteria derived a final sample of 143 patients (SCH = 63 and BD = 80).

Patients were enrolled in the study if they met the following criteria: (a) Clinical Global Impressions-Severity (CGI-S) score ≤ 329; (b) for patients with BD: Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS)30 score ≤ 7 and Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS)31 < 8 and for patients with SCH: Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS)32 scores of ≤ 3 on each of the following BPRS items (unusual thought content, suspiciousness, hallucinations, conceptual disorganization, mannerisms, and posturing) and ≤ 3 points on the eight items of the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) that are assumed to present the three dimensions of psychopathology: psychoticism (P1, P3, and G9), disorganization (P2 and G5), and negative symptoms (N1, N4, and N6)29,30,33–38.

Diagnosis and psychometric evaluations were made by two blinded, licensed psychiatrists through complete semi-structured interviews in combination with all other available data from medical records. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) the history or current diagnosis of any other ICD-10 disorder excluding personality disorders, (b) > 8 mood episodes within the previous 12 months for BD, (c) previous history of treatment resistance36,37, (d) medical conditions associated with changes in inflammatory response38, and (e) sociodemographic characteristics previously associated with alterations of immune mediators39 obesity [body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2] or undernutrition with recent weight loss, smoking ≥ 20 cigarettes/day or cannabis use. In addition, all patients were screened for acute infection by a complete physical examination, body temperature quantification, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, complete blood count, hepatic enzymes, and electrolytes. In the patient’s group, psychiatric diagnosis was confirmed on the basis of the ICD-10 and the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI)40,41. To capture the interaction between psychosis and affective symptoms dimensionally, the Schizo-Bipolar Scale (SBS) was used4. Global Assessment of Functioning (GAF) Scale was used to take into consideration psychological, social, and occupational functioning on a mental health-mental disorder continuum39.

The overall psychiatric symptoms were assessed by the Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS), using 5 subscales (with items in parentheses) to assess the severity of particular symptom domains: Affect (anxiety, guilt, depression, somatic); Positive symptoms (thought content, conceptual disorganization, hallucinatory behavior, grandiosity); Negative symptoms (blunted affect, emotional withdrawal, motor retardation); Resistance (hostility, uncooperativeness, suspiciousness); and Activation (excitement, tension, mannerisms–posturing)32. The severity of depressive symptoms was assessed with the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HDRS)30. Manic symptoms were evaluated by Young Mania Rating Scale (YMRS)31.

For patients with BD, the duration of untreated disorder was calculated as the time from onset of first affective episode to initiation of mood stabilizer42. For patients with SCH, the duration of untreated disorder was calculated as the time from onset of first psychotic symptoms to initiation of antipsychotic treatment43. All psychometric testing was done at the regular outpatient follow up. All biochemical measurements were conducted by investigators who were blinded to the diagnostic status of the patients.

Cytokine measurements

Blood samples were taken from the antecubital veins of patients between 7 a.m. and 8 a.m. after an overnight fasting. The blood was allowed to clot by leaving it undisturbed at room temperature for 30 min and an additional 30 min at 4 °C. Afterwards, the tubes were centrifuged for 15 min at 3000 rpm, allowing serum separation. Until used, serum was stored at − 80 °C.

Concentrations of cytokines were measured using Human TNF-α Elisa MAXTM set deluxe, Human IL-6 Elisa MAXTM set deluxe (7.8–500 pg/ml, sensitivity 2 pg/ml) (BioLegend, San Diego, CA) and TGF-β1 Human ELISA Kit (intraassay CV is 4.9, interassay CV is 3.2, assay range is 31–2000 pg/ml) (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, USA), in serum samples and according to the manufacturer’s instructions and literature suggestion44. Standard curve was used to calculate the levels of TNF-α, IL-6 and TGF-β for each patient sample previously measured in triplicate. All biochemical measurements were conducted by investigators who were blind to the patient’s diagnostic state.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using the Software Package for Social Sciences for Windows v.26.0 (SPSS Inc. Chicago, IL). The data are described using standard descriptive statistics (i.e., frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and mean value with standard deviation for numerical variables). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to determine the normality of the distribution of the numerical values. For discrete variables, χ2, with continuity correction according to Yates or Fisher’s exact probability test were used. To compare the variables between the groups, the independent t-test between two groups was used.

To estimate the association between cytokines’ levels and clinical variables, the partial correlation coefficients controlling for age, sex, BMI, and number of smoked cigarettes/day were used. Multivariable analysis, after adjusting for the same confounding factors, was performed by ANCOVA to assess the differences in the cytokines’ levels in regard to patients’ family history. Adjustment for multiple testing was carried out by post-hoc LSD test.

Additionally, three forward binary logistic regression analyzes models, one for each cytokine, were performed to identify predictors of the category of the disorder. Age, sex, BMI, and number of smoked cigarettes/day were used as confounding factors.

We further investigated the relationship and the direction of the relationship between TGF-β and categorical vs. non-categorical disorder presentation using moderated mediation analysis, Model 5 from PROCESS45. TGF-β was used as the predictor (X) and family risk for psychosis was used as the moderation variable (W). BPRS Affect or BPRS Positive symptoms domains were used as mediating variables (M) in two models each. Category of the disorder (SCH vs. BD) or SBS score were used as the outcome variables (Y), also in distinct models. As a result, four models were generated in total. Age and sex, which appeared to be significant confounders in previous testing, were included as covariates.

In all analyses, p-values of < 0.05 were considered significant. In ANCOVA, binary logistic regression and moderated mediation analysis models, the effects were additionally estimated using 10,000 bootstrap samples and corresponding confidence intervals (95% CI).

We also analyzed the relationship between cytokines and clinical characteristics within each patient group. In the BD group, TGF-β was associated with more lifetime episodes of BD (r = − 0.261, p = 0.001), and exhibited a trend towards significance in regard to the number of previous psychotic episodes (r = − 0.268, p = − 0.054). TNF-α and IL-6 showed no associations with clinical characteristics in BD groups, while in the SCH group, we could not identify the relationship between any of the cytokines and clinical characteristics of the disorders.

Cytokine levels in regard to mood-psychosis non-categorical approach

In the collective cohort of SCH and BD patients, those falling more to the psychotic vs. affective spectrum of psychopathology, measured by SBS, had lower TGF-β levels (r = − 0.407, p < 0.001).

TGF-β was lower in patients with more lifetime episodes (r = − 0.296, p = 0.005), and more particularly, in those with more previous psychotic episodes (r = − 0.291, p = 0.006), regardless of the disorder. The patients with family risk for psychosis (positive family risk: 1462.85 ± 1326.93, negative family risk 2999.95 ± 2811.29, F = 7.532, p = 0.007) also had lower TGF-β. Other cytokines showed no relationship with clinical characteristics of the overall sample, in our study.

The patients with worse overall functioning, according to GAF score, showed lower TGF-β levels (r = 0.294, p = 0.023). Similarly, those with more severe overall pathology, measured by the BPRS score, also had lower TGF-β (r = − 0.301, p = 0.006). More specifically, the TGF-β was significantly related to all BPRS symptom domains. When patient groups were further analyzed for severity of affective or psychotic symptoms, TGF-β showed moderate negative correlation in BD group (r = − 0.324, p = 0.022) and strong positive correlation (r = − 0.596, p = 0.004) in SCH group. Severity of psychotic symptoms measured by BPRS was related to TGF-β, but only in a subsample of BD patients (r = − 0.285, p = 0.045). Severity of psychotic symptoms measured by PANSS was related to TGF-β in SCH group (r = − 0.416, p = 0.001). Discussion

In our study, lower TGF-β levels have been associated with less favorable characteristics of the course of the disorders, as well as the severity of both mood and psychotic symptoms. The relationship between TGF-β and mood symptoms, however, was inverse in SCH and BD. Namely, in patients with mood disorders, the presence of more mood symptoms was related to lower levels of TGF-β, while in patients with psychosis more mood symptoms were related to higher TGF-β levels. Furthermore, TGF-β was a significant predictor only in the case of a non-categorical symptom distribution, with both mood symptoms and familial risk for psychosis playing an important part in defining the clinical presentation.

Cytokines have been frequently linked to various psychiatric conditions, including SCH and BD, suggesting a potential role of immune dysregulation in the pathophysiology of these disorders. While pro-inflammatory cytokines, particularly.

TNF-α11,12 and IL-613, have been associated with both disorders as well as related symptoms, the role of regulatory markers such as TGF-β has been frequently neglected.

Our study did not find the role of pro-inflammatory cytokines in clinical presentation of mood-psychosis spectrum disorders. It is to note, however, that previous findings are mostly based on comparisons with healthy controls or patients in acute exacerbation11,46–48. In line with our results, studies using the categorical approach and directly comparing patients with mood and psychotic disorders seldom found intergroup differences13,47,49,50, mainly when patients were in a state of remission50. More often, pro-inflammatory cytokines have been related to the severity of psychotic symptoms in psychosis and mood symptoms in affective disorders16,47,48,51,52, while the reverse hasn’t been frequently evaluated.

The similarities of pro-inflammatory cytokine profiles across mood and psychotic disorders raises the possibility of shared underlying mechanisms driving immune dysfunction11–13. Studies frequently propose an overlapping immune indicators’ pattern in patients with SCH and BD5. Though patients showed a similar overlapping pattern, elevated levels of IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, and interferon-γ were revealed in SCH patients compared to healthy controls, suggesting an immune-regulated basis of both disorders, but potentially more pronounced in non-affective psychosis5. This immune activation may be present even in early stages of the disorders, before psychotic conversion, with some markers such as IL-17 potentially predicting future transition to psychosis53. Thus, identification of biological underpinnings of symptoms rather than the disorders further calls for evaluation of a clear-cut dichotomy between the disorders, taking into account the non-categorical distribution of psychotic and mood symptoms16,48,51.

Opposite to studies on pro-inflammatory cytokines, the literature on TGF-β in mood and psychotic disorders offers even more contrasting results. Increased TGF-β levels have been frequently reported in SCH13,54, with only a few studies observing decreased or absent TGF-β response55,56. It is to note that the studies have mainly evaluated the patients in the acute phase of the disorder or within a predefined amount of time following the exacerbation, defining it a state marker of acute psychosis26,57,58. In BD, to date, TGF-β has only been investigated in manic episodes, with studies on both drug-naïve or drug-free patients of similar characteristics, initially exhibiting opposing results14,15. However, studies point out higher TGF-β as a positive indicator of immune state in BD, with Kim et al.15 finding post-treatment TGF-β rising and Li et al.14 finding better prognosis in the patients with initially higher TGF-β values. Although, to the best of our knowledge, there have been no studies directly comparing TGF-β in SCH and BD, the results so far point to partial similarities in immune dysregulation, calling for further evaluation of specific symptom clusters within these disorders.

In this study, the presence of mood symptoms was differently related to TGF-β levels in SCH and BD. More mood symptoms within a mood disorder were related to lower or what appeared to be less favorable TGF-β levels. On the other hand, TGF-β levels were higher, or more favorable, in case more mood symptoms were present in patients with SCH. Ambiguous at first sight, the results could point to the prognostic importance of affect within mood and psychotic disorders. Specifically, the presence of affect in SCH has traditionally been considered promising for the course of the disorder59, though conversely, the same symptoms remaining within mood disorder could point to the more severe form of the disorder60,61. We did not observe this for positive symptoms, which underlines the affect rather than the psychotic symptoms as the discriminating factor shaping the final clinical presentation of the disorders. This has been confirmed by recent epidemiological studies assessing the importance of mood symptoms in a transition risk to psychosis62.

Studies assessing mood or psychotic symptoms related to TGF-β are still sparse and inconclusive, finding no differences in patients with SCH or in regard to predominance of positive vs. negative symptoms18,26,54. Others report lower TGF-β levels in patients with more pronounced overall symptoms18,56. The specific discrepancies in findings with our study could lay in the fact that patients in the study of Kim et al.26 had lower BPRS and appear to be in the earlier stages of the disorder compared to ours. Although the comparison of mood symptoms was not possible due to different psychometric instruments, our result could pair with those of Boiko et al.18, particularly as the study included a patient sample similar to ours.

Mood symptoms, on the other hand, have been previously assessed in regard to TGF-β only in mood disorders, with studies finding lower TGF-β in more severely depressed patients63,64. Similarly, higher TGF-β was recorded in those with more prominent drops in depressive or manic symptoms in remission14,64. Studies in BD, though not directly evaluating the symptoms, found higher TGF-β to be a positive predictor of improvement of manic features14. Results that directly and uniformly compare immune regulation and mood and psychosis symptoms in affective and psychotic disorders, on a continuum, are lacking. However, different indicative values of TGF-β in SCH and BD in our study, coupled with TGF-β also being related to severity of affective symptoms in the overall sample, would not support the categorical nature of the mood-psychosis spectrum disorders.

So far, clinical studies on SCH and BD point to the activation of the compensatory anti-inflammatory response, as a counter-regulatory mechanism that inhibits the primary inflammatory response65,66. Although this study did not specifically compare patients with healthy controls, lower TGF-β levels were related to unfavorable characteristics of the disorder including symptom severity. Taking this into account, together with the fact that our patients were in remission, it is reasonable to speculate that the compensatory anti-inflammatory response could be related to more acute states of the disorder, and more pronounced in patients with still preserved compensatory mechanisms having better prognosis.

In vitro and animal studies67,68 support the findings, suggesting that TGF-β is involved in balancing excitatory and inhibitory transmission pathways and that its persistent down-regulation can result in schizophrenia-like behavior or in other, inflammation-mediated, psychiatric symptoms. This is further confirmed by TGF-β exhibiting a neuroprotective role in dopaminergic and serotonergic neurons, vital in both mood disorders and psychosis, with higher TGF-β increasing neurogenesis69–72.

Regretfully, studies to date on expression or onset of mood disorders or psychosis predominantly explored the relationship between inflammation and clinical presentation50,73, with only rare studies including other, more delicate factors underpinning the relationship or directing the outcome74. Some have found the course of the disorder to be interrelated to immune alterations52, or even to direct the clinical expression12,23. Other reports, however, showed that variables such as duration of illness, age of onset and number of episodes were not related to TGF-β26. The fact that the results of our study differed to those of Kim et al., with our patients having multiple previous episodes, indicates TGF-β levels could also be dependent on disorder progression, with additive value to psychopathology.

Epidemiological studies also found familial risk for severe mental illness being related to severity of the disorder, but not specific to psychotic symptoms up to adulthood75,76. Conversely, in adulthood, at the first full-blown psychotic episode, it is considered as a proxy genetic risk factor for psychosis77. Likewise, our results also point to the importance of familial risk for psychosis in directly modulating immune response, but at the same time shaping the relationship between regulatory mechanisms and more severe clinical presentation. This was only the case in the model using a non-categorical approach, with affect as one of the contributing factors to expression of more severe, psychotic disorder.

Previous results are suggestive of mutually causal connections between mood and psychotic symptoms. Longitudinal studies exploring bidirectional associations between mood episodes and psychosis indicate that the affective dysregulation may be a marker for dysfunction associated with psychotic experiences76. Moreover, mood symptoms, rather than psychotic features, contributed the most to the delineation of subgroups of psychosis patients78. Notably, the TGF-β signaling pathway plays an important role in neurodevelopment of amygdala, a prime center of subcortical emotional regulation79. Our study builds upon this hypothesis and finds affect to be an explanatory factor of severity of non-categorical distribution of symptoms and possibly connected to TGF-β.

Recent studies explaining the differences in affect between SCH and BD find that trait affect profile can indeed predict clinical presentation60,80. Coupled with experimental studies claiming that TGF-β signaling is regulatory and context-dependent81, this could underscore the affective pathway to mood and psychosis on a continuum80 rather than utilizing a categorical definition.

Our results should be interpreted by taking into account both study strengths and challenges. The study did not include patients with schizo-affective disorder which would further improve the interpretation of the results and add value to analyzing categorical vs. non-categorical approach. However, we did use psychometric instruments that enabled us to consistently assess the symptoms within the collective cohort of patients, thus creating a unified group. The inclusion of healthy control subjects in our study would also provide further insight into the relationship between psychopathology and mental health through the categorical vs. non-categorical approach. The effect of treatment should not be forgotten as the variations in cytokines could have been in part the epiphenomenon of medication. Bearing in mind that BD patients in our study were older compared to SCH patients, further assessment and incorporation of the staging of the disorders would also provide additional information on association between cytokine variations and clinical presentation. Although we have excluded the patients with the history or current substance abuse disorders based on the ICD-10 criteria, it is to note that the patients were not additionally biochemically screened for substance abuse.

Our clinical study builds upon the findings of previous experimental research, providing further support for the role of cytokines in SCH and BD, addressing certain methodological limitations identified in prior studies14,15,27,64. Notably, the study uses both categorical and non-categorical approach, but by analyzing patients collectively, we aimed to identify common biological mechanisms underlying the diverse clinical presentations within the mood-psychosis spectrum. This methodology allowed us to examine the relationships among cytokines, specific symptom domains, family history, and clinical presentation of mood disorders. We employed moderation analysis to confirm the direction and magnitude of these relationships, controlling for potential confounding factors that could influence the associations.

Conclusion

Our findings provide new insights into the role of cytokine dynamics in the pathophysiology of SCH and BD, and highlight the potential for immune-based biomarkers to inform diagnosis and treatment strategies. They support the non-categorical model, as the immune-mediated changes in cytokines appear to be more related to symptom severity and distribution rather than category of the disorder. This suggests that the underlying immune dysregulation may be a shared feature across these disorders, rather than a unique characteristic of each. These findings have significant implications for our understanding of the continuum versus categorical approach to psychosis and mood disorders.
