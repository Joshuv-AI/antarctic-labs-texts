Predictors of HIV/AIDS risk behaviours among in-school and out-of-school youths in Taraba State, Nigeria - A comparative study
Authors: Garba Danjuma, Muhammad Lawan Umar, Abubakar Mohammed, Sonnen Atinge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundYouths are particularly at risk of HIV/AIDS because of their high-risk sexual behaviours and low level of perceived vulnerability to the infection. They are therefore at the centre of the global HIV/AIDS epidemic, concerning both new infections and opportunities for halting the transmission of HIV. The study aimed to determine and compare the predictors of HIV/AIDS risk behaviours among in-school and out-of-school youths in an HIV high-burden State in Nigeria.MethodsThis was a comparative cross-sectional study among in-school and out-of-school youths in Jalingo, Nigeria. Multi-stage sampling was used in selecting study participants. Data collection was done using a pre-tested, interviewer-administered questionnaire. SPSS version 20 was used for data analysis. The level of significance was set at 0.05.ResultsA total of 810 questionnaires, comprising 409 from in-school youths and 401 from out-of-school youths, were completed and analysed. Over half (51.9%) of out-of-school and about 12% in-school youths were engaged in at least one HIV/AIDS risk behaviour. Being an older youth [(AOR = 0.28; 95% C.I = 0.10–0.75) for in-school; (AOR = 0.11; 95% C.I = 0.07–0.17) for out-of-school] and male [(AOR = 2.20; 95% C.I = 1.10–4.40) for in-school; (AOR = 2.65; 95% C.I = 1.66–4.24) for out-of-school] were predictors of HIV risk behaviour among the youths.ConclusionAge and sex predicted HIV/AIDS risk behaviour among in-school and out-of-school youths in the area. Interventions and strategies to reduce HIV/AIDS risk behaviours should therefore give particular attention to the male child and older youths while sustaining existing efforts targeting females and younger youths as well.Keywords: HIV risk behaviours, In-school, Out-of-school youths

Introduction

Background

Acquired Immune Deficiency Syndrome (AIDS) remains a disease of public health importance and one of the leading causes of death in sub-Saharan Africa [1, 2]. Globally, it is estimated that 38.4 million people were living with the virus in 2021 [3]. Sub-Saharan Africa, with only 10% of the world population, is home to 60% of People Living With HIV/AIDS (PLWHA) [4]. Furthermore, Nigeria, with an estimated 1.9 million adult population of PLWHA in 2018 [5] is ranked second to South Africa, which has the highest rate of HIV infection in the world [6]. The last National HIV/AIDS Indicator and Impact Survey (NAIIS), which was the largest HIV population-based survey conducted globally in 2018, reported the HIV prevalence among persons aged 15-64 years in Nigeria as 1.4% while in Taraba State, the area of the study was 2.6 % [7]. This was the second-highest in northern Nigeria and the fourth-highest in the country [7].

Youths are at the centre of the global HIV/AIDS epidemic, concerning both new infections and opportunities for halting the transmission of HIV [8]. Over half of all new HIV infections in Africa are said to be among young persons aged 15–24 years, and at least 50% of young people become sexually active by 16 years [9]. Yet, it is projected that the population of youths in the world will become increasingly concentrated in Africa and Asia. By 2050, the number of youths will rise from just under half a billion in 1950 to 1.2 billion. At that point, about nine in every 10 adolescents will be in developing countries [10]. In Nigeria, the 2015 Global Response Country Progress Report (GRCPR) showed that out of 227,518 new HIV infections in Nigeria, youths aged 15–24 years were responsible for 54,766 (24%), which was about a quarter of new infections in the country [11].

Studies have indicated that many youths are still at risk because of their high-risk sexual behaviours and low level of perceived vulnerability to the infection [6]. Despite this, the 2018 Nigeria Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) reported that only about 24% of women and 34% of men who were aged 15–24 years had comprehensive knowledge about HIV and its ways of transmission [5]. The report further revealed that among youths who had sexual intercourse in the 12 months preceding the survey, only about 45% of youths used condoms, while in Taraba State, condom use among men was 6.2% [5]. This may contribute significantly to the high HIV prevalence in the State as reported by the last NAIIS of 2018 [7]. There is growing evidence from several countries where HIV prevalence is decreasing that youth intervention programmes contribute to reversing the trends [8]. Therefore, generating evidence for HIV prevention interventions among the youth is of national and public health importance. The Joint United Nations Program on HIV/AIDS (UNAIDS) defines youths as those individuals who fall within the age group of 15–24 years [12].

According to the United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organisation (UNESCO), Nigeria is ranked fifth among the ten countries that contribute to 72% of the world’s out-of-school children [13]. In-school youths have been privileged to be reached with various strategic HIV prevention and sexual health promotion through school-based programmes [14, 15] However, there are limited structures, opportunities, and access to correct and accurate information for out-of-school youths concerning HIV prevention, sexual and reproductive health. Furthermore, most studies on youths in sub-Saharan Africa and Nigeria target in-school youths because they are easily accessible, easier to organise, and monitor compared to those who are out-of-school. But comparative studies done between in-school and out-of-school youths in some African countries have shown that out-of-school youths are more sexually active, have earlier sexual debut, lower contraceptive usage and therefore suffer the consequences of unsafe sex more, especially contracting HIV infection [16, 17]. Therefore, it is important to clarify the needs of both groups, taking into cognisance the social and environmental factors, peer influences, beliefs and values of the different groups in order to develop and implement successful prevention programmes for the two groups.

Even though there are some studies done on HIV risk behaviours in northern Nigeria among the youths, most of the studies target in-school youths. Few studies compare in-school and out-of-school youths. Yet, a comparative study of the two groups is of great importance as restricting studies to in-school youths ignores the issues and problems pertinent to out-of-school youths. In Taraba State, there are no studies that have looked at HIV risk behaviours among youths, especially comparing in-school and out-of-school youths. Therefore, this study will be meaningful in the State and will assist in developing specific interventions to reduce the toll of HIV in the State. Hence, the study aimed to determine and compare the predictors of HIV/AIDS risk behaviours among in-school and out-of-school youths in Jalingo, Taraba State, Nigeria.

Methods

Study area, study design, and study population

The study area was Jalingo Local Government Area, one of the 16 LGAs in Taraba State. Jalingo is an urban LGA with ten political wards. It has a population of 139,845 people according to the 2006 census and a projected population of 186,123 in 2016 [18]. The study area has a large youth population and several spots where youths congregate, especially out-of-school youths. In addition, the LGA has a large number of secondary schools with a good public-private mix as compared to the other LGAs in the State [19]. The LGA has 22 public and 45 private secondary schools. Out of this one is a boys-only school, and the other 66 are mixed schools. The net schools’ attendance is male: 8,234, and female: 6,903 [19]. The study was a comparative cross-sectional design. The study population consisted of in-school and out-of-school youths in Jalingo LGA. In-school youths are those youths attending school, whereas out-of-school youth as defined by the Nigeria’s National HIV Strategy for Adolescents and Young People, refers to any adolescent or young person that dropped out of primary or secondary school for at least two years; never attended primary and/or secondary school; completed primary school but did not continue with secondary school [20]. For the in-school, youths within the age range of 15–24 years who were currently in senior secondary school (SSS1 –SSS3) were included, whereas for out-of-school, those within the age range of 15–24 years who were not in school or had completed less than secondary school level of education were recruited. In-school and out-of-school youths with speech, hearing, mental health challenges or other disabilities that will not allow the youth to participate in the study were excluded

The first student numbers between one and the sample interval were randomly selected, and the first participant was drawn and approached to participate in the study. Subsequent students were then selected in the order of the sample interval.

Selection for out-of-school youths

Multi-stage sampling technique was also used to select the out-of-school youths.

First stage (selection of wards)

Jalingo LGA has 10 wards, 25% sample of the wards (≈ 3 wards) were selected by a simple random sampling technique through balloting.

Second stage (selection of clusters – places where out-of-school youths congregate)

Clusters were mapped in the three selected wards. Youth clubs, mechanical workshops, and youth-friendly centres served as clusters. The list of clusters mapped served as the sampling frame for this stage. Three clusters, each from the three wards, were selected by a simple random sampling technique through balloting.

Third stage (selection of participants)

The sample was allocated equally to the clusters. This was because there was no register to be used as a sampling frame, and the clusters may have had approximately a similar population size. Any out-of-school youth who was present at the cluster during the time of the study was approached to respond to the questionnaire, after obtaining consent from the participant and the youth leadership. When more participants than the required number of out-of-school youths were present during the study, a simple random sampling technique was used to select the required participants. This sampling procedure was undertaken due to the absence of a reliable sampling frame to obtain a sample interval for the study.

Data collection methods, recruitment, and training of research assistants

A pre-tested semi-structured interviewer-administered questionnaire was used as the study instrument and was applied to both in-school and out-of-school youths to ensure uniformity. The questionnaire was translated into the local (Hausa) language by a professional Hausa translator, and the Hausa translated version was again back-translated to English by another independent English translator, this was to ensure consistency of the instrument. The questionnaire was pretested among in-school and out-of-school youths in Gassol LGA, a location outside the study area. This was to ensure there was no contamination or spillage of study subjects; the pre-test was also to ensure tool validity, cultural acceptability, and appropriateness. The study instrument was adapted from a research protocol [23]. Four female and four male research assistants were recruited and trained for this study. To reduce social desirability bias, research assistants did not come from settlements where they collected data. The research assistants had at least a minimum qualification of an ordinary national diploma. They were fluent in English and local languages and sensitive to the local culture. The research assistants were trained in the contents of the questionnaire, consent, and interviewing processes for one week.

Data management

Measurement of variables

Independent variables for the study included age, age at first sexual intercourse, sex, educational level, religion, and occupation. The dependent variable for the study was the HIV/AIDS risk behaviours of the respondents.

Knowledge of HIV/AIDS variables

In the questionnaire, HIV/AIDS related knowledge was assessed using 27 items. Correct response to each of the items attracted a score of one (1) mark while a wrong or ‘don’t know’ response attracted a score of zero (0) mark, as adopted from a previous study [24]. Thus total knowledge score ranged from 0 to 27, scores of 0–13 were considered as having poor knowledge, while scores of 14 to 27 were considered as having good knowledge regarding HIV/AIDS, as adopted from a previous study [24]. For questions with four possible answers, for example, a spontaneous correct response (yes) and a correct response (yes) after prompting of respondents attracted a score of one (1), whereas a wrong (no) response or a don’t know response attracted a zero (0) score.

HIV risk behaviours variables

Furthermore, HIV/AIDS risk behaviours were assessed using: condom use, having multiple sexual partners, condom use at last sex, transactional sex, sex with a non-regular partner, early sexual debut, use of alcohol and/or drugs before sexual intercourse [24], and the report of at least one of these negative behaviours related to HIV transmission classified respondents as having high-risk behaviour, while the absence of a negative behaviour classified the respondents as having low-risk behaviour to HIV infection, as was adopted in a previous study [24].

Statistical analyses

A trained data entry clerk entered the data into Excel. Data cleaning was done before analysis, and where necessary, recourse was made to the survey questionnaire to verify inconsistent, improbable, or missing data. The data gathered from the questionnaire were entered into a personal computer and analysed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 20. Frequency distributions of variables were generated and represented in tables. The Chi-square test was used to analyse factors associated with HIV/AIDS risk behaviours where appropriate. The Student t-test and confidence interval at 95% were used to determine the significance of differences between means. In all tests of significance and associations, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. At the multivariable level, logistic regression analysis was used to obtain crude and adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals for predictors of HIV/AIDS risk behaviours. Statistically significant variables (p < 0.05), those known to be significant in the literature review but not in the study and p < 0.10[25] in this study were included in the logistic regression model.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Health Research Ethical Committee of the Aminu Kano Teaching Hospital (AKTH/MAC/SUB/12A/P-3/VI/1936). Permission to conduct the study was obtained from the Taraba State Ministry of Health, the Ministry of Education, the Ministry of Youth and Sports Development, and the Primary Health Care department of Jalingo LGA. Written informed consent/assent was obtained from all respondents selected to participate in this study. Respondents under 18 years of age were required to sign an assent form; additionally, their parent or guardian signed a consent form. For respondents who could not read, the research assistants explained in detail to them and were requested to thumbprint. All provisions of the principles of research ethics were respected during the study. For instance, youths who indicated interest in HCT benefited from referral and linkage to care at the HCT testing site. Participants were assured of the confidential nature of their responses, and research assistants were trained to keep information strictly confidential. The study did not involve any invasive or intrusive actions.

Results

Four hundred and eighteen (418) questionnaires were administered both to in-school and out-of-school youths. However, 409 and 401 were completed by the in-school and out-of-school youths, representing a response rate of 98.0% and 96.0% respectively. The mean ages of in-school and out-of-school youths were 16.90 ± 6.6 and 20.3 ± 2.97, respectively. Among the in-school, 54% of the respondents were male, while among the out-of-school youths, 76.3% and 23.7% were male and female, respectively. More than half (56.6%) of the out-of-school youths were unemployed, and above two-thirds (70.8%) had ever attended school, with more than half (56.1%) of this at the secondary school level. The majority of the in-school youths were Christians (60.6%), while most of the out-of-school youths were Muslims (67.6%) (Table 1).

Discussion

This study found that the proportion of HIV risk behaviours among out-of-school youths was more than fourfold that of in-school youths. This high prevalence of high-risk behaviours has far-reaching health effects on the youths, family and the community at large. Almost a third of sexually active out-of-school youths, but a very few of sexually active in-school youths, had sexual intercourse with two or more sexual partners in the past 12 months preceding the study. This result showed that the prevalence of multiple sex partners among out-of-school youth was also higher than that of in-school youth. The findings among in-school youths in this study were comparably lower than those found in a study in three states (Bauchi, Borno and Gombe) of Northeast Nigeria, where the prevalence of multiple sexual partners of 37% was reported in 2006 [26]. A descriptive cross-sectional study conducted in 2009 among in-school youths in Osogbo, Oyo State, Southwestern Nigeria also found a higher prevalence of multiple sexual partners among the respondents [27]. A much higher prevalence was reported in a study in Jos, North Central Nigeria, where more than half of the in-school youths had two or more sexual partners [28]. Similarly, in 2011, a study among in-school youths in Ibadan, Southwestern Nigeria, showed a higher prevalence of 48.4% of multiple sex partners among in-school youths [29]. Furthermore, Onayade et al. in their study among adolescents in Ile-Ife, southwestern Nigeria, found that 41.7% of the respondents had more than one sexual partner [30]. However, in contrast to the finding in our study where the prevalence of multiple sex partners was much lower among in-school youths as compared to out-of-school youths, a comparative cross-sectional study in Tanzania reported a higher prevalence of multiple sexual partners among in-school youths as compared to out-of-school youths, in-school being twice as high as out-of-school [31]. A Similar contrasting position was reported in 2014 in a study in Ethiopia, where more than one-third, 223 (37.1%) of in-school and 190 (32.6%) of out-of-school youths reported that they had two or more sexual partners [32]. A study in Uganda corroborates our finding, as more out-of-school youths than in-school youths also reported having multiple sexual partners in the last 12 months preceding the study [33]. Furthermore, in 2010, Zuma et al. in South Africa reported only 3.8% of the respondents having more than one sexual partner [34]. Having multiple sexual partners directly contributes to the heterosexual transmission of HIV.

Among the sexually active respondents, consistent use of condoms was only about 2.0% for in-school youths and 15.0% of the out-of-school youths. This rather poor preventive culture among the youths has also been reported by other similar studies [27, 35–37]. The results of consistent condom use among the respondents in our study are lower than reported in 2010 by Olugbenga-Bello et al. in Osun, Southwestern Nigeria, where consistent condom use among young people was about 50 per cent [27]. Similarly, Alemu and colleagues in another study in Bashir Dar Town, Northwest Ethiopia, reported a higher consistent condom use among out-of-school youths than in our study [38]. Furthermore, a much higher prevalence of consistent use of condoms than our result was found by Andargie et al., where over two-thirds of high school students in Gondar, Northwest Ethiopia, reported using condoms consistently [39]. For condom use at last sexual intercourse, this was seven times higher among out-of-school than among in-school youths. The finding in this study among in-school youths was lower than reported by Ajuwon and colleagues, who assessed sexual behaviour and experience of sexual coercion among secondary school students in three states (Bauchi, Borno and Gombe) in Northeastern Nigeria, where about a quarter of the sexually active youths used a condom during their last sexual intercourse [26]. Much lower result than found in this study was reported in 2012 in Kano, Northwest Nigeria, where less than three per cent of the respondents used a condom during their last sexual intercourse [40]. The prevalence of condom use in this study was also lower than reported by Kazaura and colleagues in Tanzania among unmarried adolescents, where condom use at last sex with was reported at 42%[31].

Age at first sex is another important indicator of exposure to the risk of STIs, including HIV/AIDS. Young people who initiate sex at an early age are at a higher risk of contracting STIs than young people who delay the initiation of sexual activity [5]. This study found that the prevalence of early sexual debut (sexual debut at age of 15 years and below) was higher among out-of-school youths than among in-school youths. The finding in our study is lower than reported by Lawan et al. in Kano, Northwestern Nigeria, where 11.7% of adolescents had their first sex before the age of 15 years. Similarly, another study conducted in 2011 among secondary school students in a Sub-Urban Community in Osun, Southwestern Nigeria, reported a prevalence of early sexual debut of 24.2% among the respondents [29]. A study on age at sexual debut among South African youths found that about four in ten of the respondents indicated that they experienced early sexual debut [34]. This is lower than the prevalence reported in this study. Early sexual debut, especially with a lack of condom use, is detrimental and can lead to HIV infection. This is more critical since in most cases, sexual debut is often unprotected, unguided and uninformed. Youths who had an early sexual debut were more likely to report multiple sexual partners, and youths who had multiple sexual partners were more likely to be infected with HIV [34]. This is of concern since most people are not aware of their HIV status and thus likely to unknowingly infect others. The prevalence of sex in exchange for money, gift or favour in this study was several-fold higher in out-of-school youths than among in-school youths. Our finding is lower than reported in 2009 among in-school youths in Osogbo, Southwest Nigeria [27]. Similarly, the result in this study is lower than found by Andargie et al. among in-school youths in Gondar, Northwest Ethiopia, where more than a tenth of the sexually active high school students were involved in sex for money, gift or favour [39]. However, the result of our study among out-of-school youths is higher than the NDHS 2018 reported figure of 2.4% of ever being involved in sex for money, favour or gift among young people [5].

We found sex, age, current level in school, and religion to be significantly associated with HIV/AIDS risk behaviour among in-school youths. Among out-of-school youths, sex, age, educational status, ethnicity, and religion, except knowledge of HIV, were statistically associated with HIV risk behaviours. The result showed that there was a higher proportion of males than females, both among in-school and out-of-school youths who reported engaging in at least one HIV/AIDS risk behaviour. This finding is consistent with previous studies in Nigeria, Ethiopia and Tanzania, where being a male was found to be associated with engaging in HIV risk behaviour [26, 31, 38, 41]. In contrast, a study in southeast Nigeria that assessed condom use amongst out-of-school youths in 2009 reported that being a female was associated with HIV/AIDS risk behaviour (non-use of condom during last sexual intercourse) [42]. Surprisingly, there was no association between knowledge of HIV and HIV/AIDS risk behaviours among in-school and out-of-school youths. This finding agreed with a study in Delta, Nigeria, among in-school adolescents, where knowledge of HIV was not found to be associated with HIV risk behaviour [43]. Other studies in Nigeria [44], Kenya [45] and Cameroun [46] also concur with this position. The study found sex, age, and current level in school as the predictors of HIV risk behaviour among in-school youths, while among out-of-school youths, sex and age predicted HIV/AIDS risk behaviours. Among the in-school, male respondents were twice as likely to engage in HIV risky behaviours as females. In contrast, among out-of-school youths, males were almost three times more likely to engage in HIV behaviours than their female counterparts. In-school youths aged 15–19 years were less likely to be engaged in HIV risky behaviour as compared with their counterpart in the reference category, whereas out-of-school youths within the age group 15–19 years were less likely to be involved in HIV risk behaviour as compared to those older. Again, HIV knowledge did not predict HIV/AIDS risk behaviour among the youths. This finding is consistent with those of Okonta and colleagues, and Sunday and colleagues in Nigeria, where they also found that knowledge of HIV/AIDS had no significant effect on sexual behaviour among adolescents [43, 44].

Limitations

Due to the sensitive nature of the topic, some youths found it difficult to respond to some questions. In addition, some parents did not allow their children to participate, especially parents of out-of-school youths. The study relied on self-reported information by the respondents and may be subject to information bias, as respondents may give information that is perceived as ideal rather than real practice or deliberately skip some sensitive information. There could also be recall bias since youths were asked about events that had already happened within the last 12 months before the survey. However, possible precautionary measures were taken to reduce biases in this study, for instance, selection of competent research assistants, vigorous training of the interviewers on administration of the study instrument and pre-test of the study questionnaire.

Conclusion

The study found that a significant number of youths engaged in at least one form of HIV/AIDS risk behaviour, especially among out-of-school. Being male and within the age group of 15–19 years predicted HIV/AIDS risk behaviours for both in-school and out-of-school youths. Interventions and strategies to reduce HIV/AIDS risk behaviours should therefore give particular attention to the male child and older adolescents while sustaining existing efforts targeting females and all adolescents in general.
