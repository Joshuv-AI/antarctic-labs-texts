Clinical effectiveness and side effect profile of contraceptive implant (Implanon) among women at Sulaimaniyah, Iraq: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Rezan Abdulrahman Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContraception is an effective way to prevent unintended pregnancies that have a significant impact on women’s health and the budget of countries.ObjectivesTo assess the clinical effectiveness and side-effect profile of progesterone implant as a contraceptive method among the participants.Patients and MethodsThis cross-sectional study recruited 101 patients who used a contraceptive implant, Sulaimaniyah, Iraq, from January 2022 to January 2025. Then, participants’ sociodemographic and medical data were collected using a self-structured questionnaire validated questionnaire (provided in the supplementary data). Then, the correlations between some variables were determined to find the effectiveness and side effects of the contraceptive implant.ResultsAbout 45.5% of women using a contraceptive implant were aged ≥ 35 years, and 36.6% were obese. The common side effects of the contraceptive implant were weight gain (24.8%), headache (23.8%), and loss of libido (19.8%). Most women had a contraceptive implant removed or intended to remove it (54.5%) within 3 years, mainly due to menstrual irregularities (43.6%). During the period when the contraceptive implant was in situ, there was no unintended pregnancy (0.0%), while 75% of women who wished to conceive after removal of the contraceptive implant got pregnant within the first 6 months of removal, indicating early return of fertility after discontinuation of this contraceptive method. Women aged 35 years or older with three or more children experienced significantly more side effects (p ≤ 0.05).ConclusionsContraceptive implant is considered a highly effective and safe method to be used in women of reproductive age, despite some side effects, and there was early return of fertility after discontinuation of Implanon in women wishing to conceive.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-025-04072-9.Keywords: Contraception implant, Implanon, Effectiveness, Reproductive age, Side effects

Introduction

Contraception is a successful method for reducing maternal mortality and morbidity associated with unplanned pregnancy. Also, it prevents pregnancy in women who are not allowed to get pregnant due to medical issues until their condition has improved [1, 2]. The vast majority of married women want contraceptives to either space their next birth or cease childbearing [3]. In Iraq, the prevalence of contraceptive use is 58% (44% modern and 14% traditional), which is lower than the global average (63%) and other countries in the region, such as Jordan (63%) and Tunisia (67%) [4]. Among contraceptive users, small proportions of sexually active women are using implants, while others are using progesterone-only pills, combined oral contraceptive pills, and injectable [5]. Family planning offers numerous benefits by helping individuals avoid unintended pregnancies and plan births. It reduces pregnancy-related health risks, particularly for adolescent girls. Beyond health, family planning enables women to pursue education and employment opportunities [6]. The avoidance of intended pregnancies becomes less dependent on rates of initially adopted contraceptives and more dependent on the ability and willingness of couples to use methods with maximum effectiveness [1]. Short-acting contraceptive methods (patch, vaginal ring, or oral contraceptives) are characterized by impaired adherence, in terms of poor compliance and/or improper intake, with a relatively high risk of unplanned pregnancies. Conversely, long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARC), such as intrauterine devices and subdermal contraceptive implants, provide at least 3-year continuous pregnancy protection and do not require any attention by users [3].

The contraceptive implant (Implanon) is a subdermal, single plastic rod inserted under the skin of the inner upper arm to stop pregnancy by slowly releasing a synthetic progestogen, similar to a natural hormone produced by the body [6]. The contraceptive implant (2 mm in diameter and 4 cm in length) is composed of etonogestrel (68 mg) surrounded by ethylene vinyl acetate. It releases initially at a rate of 60–70 µg/day, which decreases gradually to 25–30 µg/day by the end of 3 years [7]. The contraceptive implant is a favourable, cost-effective LARC for women in developing countries, and expanding its use represents a priority for family planning programs [8] in some Mediterranean countries. The contraceptive implant has been made available internationally since 1998 and is in use in over 40 countries worldwide. It is considered a highly effective method of contraception with an effectiveness rate of 99.9% comparable to permanent contraception. Clinically, it is supposed to be safe in obese and hypertensive women, and it has a rapid onset of action and fertility returns almost immediately after the device is removed [7].

Generally, the contraceptive implant duration is 3 years. Still, it can be removed before if the woman plans pregnancy or due to its side effects (headache, bleeding, weight gain, and menstrual irregularity). The side effects of the contraceptive implant were the reason for early discontinuation of the contraceptive implant among women who still require contraception [7]. Pre-insertion counselling should empower women to make informed decisions on discontinuation and transition to other options. Thus, this study aimed to find the clinical effectiveness of the contraceptive implant, continuation rate and side-effect profile among eligible women of the reproductive age group.

Patients and methods

Study design and setting

This cross-sectional study was conducted among 101 adult females of reproductive age at multiple private clinics in Sulaimaniyah, Iraq, from January 2022 to January 2025 using a convenience sampling method. The sample size was calculated according to https://www.calculator.net/sample-size-calculator.html.

Inclusion criteria

Married women (cultural manner) of reproductive age (14–45 years old) who were regularly menstruating, sexually active, not sterilized, at least 3 months postpartum, and those who wished for long-term contraception were included.

Exclusion criteria

Married women on any medication for infertility, with hormonal imbalance, had irregular menstrual cycles, and those who had no compliance with continuing and follow-up were excluded.

Study protocol

A standard questionnaire was developed (Supplementary 1), validated by five experts in the field, and used to collect data from participants through face-to-face interviews. The first part of the questionnaire includes sociodemographic characteristics of women, such as age, body mass index (BMI), residency, occupation, and educational status. The second part covered the medical attributes of women, including parity, medical history, duration of menstrual bleeding, duration of contraceptive implant in situ, contraceptive implant removal and its cause, difficulty in inserting or removing the contraceptive implant, and pre-insertion counselling. The contraceptive implant (Etonogestrel, NV Organon, Oss, Netherlands) was inserted subdermally under the effect of local xylocaine of the non-dominant arm with the aid of a specific applicator. Then, women were asked to record side effects for a minimum of six months after contraceptive implant insertion and the likelihood of contraceptive implant removal. Pregnancy follow-up was done at any point while the contraceptive implant remained in place. For women who wanted to become pregnant after removal, return of fertility was assessed within six months of removal. Then, the correlations between variables were determined, analyzed, interpreted, and explained, and their comparisons were made.

A pilot study was conducted on 10 replicated patients, and Cronbach’s alpha was calculated. Its value (0.834) indicates a high level of internal consistency and reliability of the tool. Also, it demonstrates that the questionnaire is trustworthy and suitable for use in the main study with studied participants.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (IBM, Chicago, USA, version 27). Descriptive statistics, including mean, standard deviation (SD), frequencies, and percentages, were calculated for the variables. Inferential statistics, including the Chi-Square test, examined group associations and differences. Statistical significance was considered at p ≤ 0.05.

Association between sociodemographic characteristics and side-effects

A significant association was found between age and the occurrence of side effects (p = 0.036). Women aged ≥ 35 years were more likely to experience side effects (52.3%) than younger age groups, whereas women < 20 years did not report any side effects (0.0%). In contrast, no significant associations (p ≥ 0.05) were found between the occurrence of side effects and other sociodemographic factors, including BMI, residence, education, and occupation. Obese women showed higher side effects (43.1%) than those with normal BMI (23.1%) (p = 0.128). Similarly, the incidence of side effects was similar between urban (53.8%) and rural (46.2%) residents (p = 0.711). Regarding education, women with only primary school education reported higher side effects (30.8%) compared to those with intermediate (16.9%), high school (24.6%), or university education (27.7%) (p = 0.261). Unemployed women comprise the majority in both groups (72.3%) (p = 0.909), as shown in Table 6.

Discussion

The contraceptive implant is known for its high effectiveness, but like all contraceptives, it carries potential side effects. Consequently, in this study, we aimed to find a detailed overview of women using the contraceptive implant as a contraceptive method. Several sociodemographic characteristics, such as age, BMI, educational status, and occupation, were found to have varying influences on the women using a contraceptive implant. The age distribution of this study’s participants indicates that a significant proportion of women using contraceptive implants were ≥ 35 years old (45.5%), which suggests that contraceptive implants may be a preferred contraceptive option for women in their 30 s, possibly due to their long-term nature and effectiveness. Similar to this finding, Boah et al. 2022 [9] and Negash et al. 2022 [10] showed that women aged ≥ 35 were more likely to choose LARC due to its lower failure rate and the completion of childbearing or family planning goals. Age was the only sociodemographic factor significantly associated with side effects (p = 0.036) among the studied women, with older women (≥ 35 years) being more likely to experience more side effects. This finding is consistent with other studies showing that older women are more likely to experience hormonal contraceptive side effects, particularly those related to mood, libido, and weight gain [11]. In the cultural context of our population, women typically seek long-term contraceptive methods after completing their families (often having ≥ 3 children). Thus, 45.5% of participants were aged >35 years, and 66.3% had three or more children. These characteristics may contribute to the higher incidence of reported side effects in this group. Other sociodemographic factors, such as BMI, education, and occupation, did not show significant associations with side effects, suggesting that these factors may not be as influential in predicting women’s experiences with contraceptive implants.

The majority of participants in this study were either overweight or obese, which could influence their experience with side effects, as several studies have noted the potential impact of body weight on contraceptive effectiveness and side effects. These outcomes are consistent with another study showing that women with higher BMI tend to experience more side effects with hormonal contraceptives [12]. Although this study did not find a significant association between BMI and contraceptive implant removal, other studies have indicated that women with higher BMI are more likely to experience weight gain, which could be a key factor in their decision to remove the implant [13]. Women from urban areas (52.5%) slightly outnumbered those from rural areas (47.5%), indicating a relatively balanced geographical distribution. Educational levels were also evenly distributed, with women having varying levels of education, ranging from primary school to university. In contrast, Bongaarts and Hodgson, 2022 reported that unemployed women had limited access to contraceptive services and were less likely to use modern contraceptive methods [14]. This suggests the need for targeted interventions to improve contraceptive access and education, especially in rural or low-income populations. Another study found a preference for LARC among women with higher education levels, likely due to better access to information [15].

Women and couples who receive better counselling on their method may be more aware of potential side effects and how to cope with them. In this study, most women had preinsertion counselling (78%), but still, the removal rate was high (40.6%). Another study reported a direct correlation between not having received pre-insertion counselling and being more likely to have early implant removal [16]. The use of contraceptive implant for 1–3 years was most prevalent in this study (59.4%), and 40.6% removed it in less than a year, which is higher than that found by Crockett et al. 2017 (4.0%) [17], Banafa et al. 2017 (9.4%) [18], Kahansim et al. 2022 (33%) [19], and Al Jeborry et al. 2024 (22.9%) [7] in less than one year. On the contrary, Beyene et al. 2022 [20] reported an early contraceptive implant discontinuation of 56.4% and Melkamu et al. 2018 reported 65% [1].

Parity was another key factor, with 66.3% of women reporting ≥ 3 children, indicating that many of them may have already completed their childbearing and opted for a LARC-like contraceptive implant. This outcome aligns with that of Negash et al. 2022 who indicated that women who have completed their childbearing are more likely to opt for LARC-like contraceptive implants [10]. Simultaneously, women with higher parity were more likely to have a contraceptive implant removed, which is consistent with that found by Crockett et al. 2017 [17]. Still, whether this was due to side effects or other factors, such as the desire to conceive or dissatisfaction with the method, is unclear. Further studies exploring the relationship between parity and contraceptive implant usage are needed to understand this dynamic fully.

In this study, 34.65% patients experienced amenorrhea, 28.70% frequent bleeding, 25.75% oligomenorrhea, and 10.90% had normal bleeding. Similar bleeding patterns were found by Wahab et al. 2016 [21]. Also, this study did not report unintended pregnancy when the contraceptive implant was in situ, which indicates its effectiveness of 100% and 75% (6 out of 8) of women planning to become pregnant, conceive within 6 months of removal of the contraceptive implant, so there was early return of fertility after discontinuation of the device. The results indicate that the contraceptive implant is an effective contraceptive and allows fertility to return promptly after discontinuation. Similarly, Al Jeborry et al. 2024 [7] and Adeyemi et al. 2018 [22] reported no cases of pregnancy during contraceptive implant use. On the contrary, Sajid and Mehmood, 2017 [23] reported pregnancy in 13.1% of cases, which might be because they used estrogen. These disparities might be related to the quality of the contraceptive implant, its insertion technique, the Gynaecologist’s experience, device/method failure, and user factors. Accordingly, Rowlands et al. 2020 [24] reported that the majority of the pregnancies in those using contraceptive implants were due to actual method failures and missing implants (58% and 26%, respectively). Moreover, women can be advised that there is no delay in the return of fertility after implant removal, as 75% became pregnant within 6 months of removal of the contraceptive implant, parallel to that of Adeyemi et al. 2018 [22] who found ovulation returning within 3 weeks of implant removal in 90% of women.

The most notable findings in this study were the significant associations between specific side effects and contraceptive implant removal. Weight gain, headaches, and loss of libido were strongly associated with the decision to remove the contraceptive implant. These results are consistent with Beesham et al. 2019 who reported these side effects as the most common reasons for contraceptive implant removal [25]. Whereas Naiyer et al. 2024 mentioned that the most common reasons for implanon removal were menstrual irregularity (48.4%), followed by amenorrhea (9.6%), weight gain (7.2%), and breast pain (7.8%) [26].

Weight gain, in particular, has been identified as a significant concern for women using hormonal contraceptives, and many women cite it as a reason for discontinuation [27]. These findings emphasize the importance of healthcare providers addressing potential side effects during contraceptive counselling and offering solutions for managing or mitigating these effects. In contrast, pain/numbness in the arm, dizziness, and abnormal bleeding did not show a significant association with contraceptive implant removal, suggesting that these side effects might not significantly influence women’s decision to discontinue contraceptive implant.

Collectively, contraceptive implant usage guides reproductive health programs by indicating demand for highly effective, long-term methods, influencing method mix decisions, identifying barriers like cost and access, and informing training for providers on insertion, removal, and counselling on benefits like lighter periods.

The limitations of this study include its single-centred design, small sample size, self-reported outcomes, short follow-up, and the nature of the study. Also, it is worth noting that the study did not capture the severity or duration of these side effects, which could influence their impact on removal decisions. Future studies should explore the severity of side effects and their direct impact on the decision to remove the contraceptive implant.

Conclusions

No pregnancies were reported during contraceptive implant use in this study; however, factors influencing contraceptive implant usage and discontinuation among women should be reviewed. The findings suggest that age, parity, and side effects potentially determine women’s experiences with the contraceptive implant. Healthcare providers should take these factors into account when counselling women about contraceptive options, and efforts should be made to minimize the side effects associated with contraceptive implants, particularly weight gain, headache, and loss of libido. Additionally, future research should focus on understanding the underlying reasons for side effects and exploring alternative contraceptive methods for women who are dissatisfied with the contraceptive implant.
