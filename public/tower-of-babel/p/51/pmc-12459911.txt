Assessing the impact of perfluoroalkyl substances on liver health: a comprehensive study using multi-donor human liver spheroids
Authors: Lucy Golden-Mason, Matthew P Salomon, Chikako Matsuba, Yufen Wang, Veronica Wendy Setiawan, Lida Chatzi, Ana C Maretti-Mira
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Although per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS) have been linked to chronic liver diseases, the specific cellular and molecular mechanisms by which different PFAS contribute to human liver dysfunction remain unclear. This study aims to elucidate those mechanisms.Methods:We exposed a multi-donor human liver spheroid model composed of multiple cell types to 20 μM of PFHxS, PFOA, PFOS, or PFNA for seven days, followed by single-cell RNA sequencing and lipid staining.Results:PFAS impacted liver spheroids in a compound- and sex-specific manner. PFOA and PFHxS increased lipid accumulation, while PFOS and PFNA triggered multiple cancer-related pathways. PFOA upregulated de novo lipogenesis, particularly in female-derived hepatocytes, whereas PFHxS downregulated lipid transportation and efflux pathways across all hepatocytes. PFNA upregulated pathways involved in cell cycle progression, oxidative stress, DNA repair, and inflammation in hepatocytes from both sexes. Notably, 61.3% of the PFNA-exposed cells expressed a transcriptomic cancer signature. PFOS predominantly affected male-derived hepatocytes, showing a mild effect. All compounds impaired immune-related pathways in T/NK and Kupffer cells. Furthermore, PFAS exposure reduced cell–cell communication and elicited cellular interactions involved in angiogenesis, apoptosis, cell proliferation and adhesion, lipid metabolism, and inflammation.Conclusions:Our findings suggest that PFAS disrupt liver metabolism and may promote pro-oncogenic signaling through compound- and sex-specific mechanisms. These insights enhance our understanding of PFAS hepatotoxicity and underscore the importance of considering sex as a biological variable in future toxicological and public health assessments.Keywords: Liver Spheroids, PFAS (Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances), scRNA-seq, Lipid Metabolism, Cancer, CellChat

Introduction

Per- and Polyfluoroalkyl Substances (PFAS) are a class of synthetic chemicals with hydrophobic, lipophobic, and heat-resistant properties that have been broadly used in industry since the 1940s. They are present in several consumer products, including food packaging, PTFE-coated non-stick cookware, clothes, furniture and carpets, and aqueous film-forming foams (Begley et al., 2005; Sajid and Ilyas, 2017; Lang et al., 2016; Bečanová et al., 2016; Vecitis et al., 2010). Due to their strong carbon–fluorine (C-F) bonds, PFAS are highly resistant to degradation and are ubiquitously present in the environment, often referred to as “forever chemicals”. Humans are continuously exposed to these compounds through the ingestion of contaminated food and water (Sunderland et al., 2019). According to the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the most common PFAS detected in human blood are perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS), perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), perfluorohexanesulfonic acid (PFHxS), and perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA) (CDC, 2023). PFOA and PFOS production was discontinued in the U.S. in the early 2000s (Letter by William Weppner, 2000), and several European countries have adopted the Stockholm Convention guidelines, restricting the use of PFOA, PFOS, and PFHxS (Stockholm). Yet, many countries have limited or no restrictions on PFAS use, and PFNA remains unregulated. Following the U. S. phase-out of PFOA and PFOS, PFAS levels in human plasma have declined in the American population (CDC, 2023). Nevertheless, chronic illnesses affecting nearly every organ system continue to be reported in association with PFAS exposure, leading to impaired immune function, endocrine disruption, cancer, and reduced fetal growth (Fenton et al., 2021).

Once inside the body, PFAS tend to bind to serum albumin and fatty acid-binding proteins, resulting in differential partitioning in various tissues, including the liver (Pérez et al., 2013; Baumert et al., 2023; MacManus-Spencer et al., 2010; Luebker et al., 2002). PFAS excretion is very slow and is directly impacted by factors like chain length and chemical structure, where a higher elimination rate indicates lower bioaccumulation (Cui et al., 2010). The liver plays a critical role in detoxification, metabolizing and removing harmful substances from the bloodstream. Several human studies have linked exposure to PFOA, PFHxS, PFOS, and PFNA to hepatic diseases and metabolic dysfunction, with effects ranging from lipid metabolism abnormalities in childhood to liver cancer in adulthood (Sunderland et al., 2019). Animal studies suggest that exposure to PFOA, PFNA, and PFOS has hepatotoxic effects in mice, inducing steatosis, hepatocyte proliferation, and hepatocellular hypertrophy (Costello et al., 2022). Similarly, in vitro studies using cell lines suggest that PFOA, PFNA, and PFOS alter hepatocyte lipid metabolism by activating the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPAR-α) (Behr et al., 2020).

Despite these insights, the molecular mechanisms linking PFAS to human liver dysfunction remain poorly understood. Traditional models have significant limitations: animal models fail to fully recapitulate human disease, while conventional in vitro models typically rely on single-cell cultures, limiting their ability to capture the complex tissue environment necessary for disease progression. In contrast, 3D human multicellular liver spheroids provide a more physiologically relevant model, incorporating hepatocytes and non-parenchymal cells within a three-dimensional structure that better mimics the liver. Although previous studies have explored PFAS toxicity using liver spheroids, their assays were performed in high-glucose maintenance media, focusing primarily on stress response genes rather than characterizing the biological changes occurring within the individual cell populations or considering sex-specific differences (Rowan-Carroll et al., 2021; Reardon et al., 2021; Barutcu et al., 2024).

To bridge this gap, we developed an integrated in vitro framework that combines 3D human liver spheroid cultured in normal-glucose media with whole-transcriptome single-cell RNA sequencing. Our findings reveal key molecular and cellular pathways disrupted by PFAS, as well as potential cell–cell communication alterations that may drive PFAS-induced liver dysfunction. This knowledge will support the development of targeted intervention strategies to mitigate PFAS-associated liver disease and provide policymakers with a scientific foundation for regulating PFAS use and ensuring safer disposal practices.

Methodology

PFAS preparation

PFHxS-Perfluorohexanesulfonic Acid (CAS:355–46-4, Toronto Research Chemicals, Cat#P999738), PFOA-Perfluorooctanoic Acid (CAS:335–67-1, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat#sc-250662), PFOS-Heptadecafluorooctanesulfonic Acid (CAS:1763–23-1, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat#sc-235283), and PFNA-Perfluorononanoic Acid (CAS:375–95-1, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Cat#sc-250678). Single PFAS stock solutions were dissolved in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich) at 20 mM to ensure complete solubility before dilution into culture medium. PFAS working solutions were diluted to 20 μM in 3D InSight™ Human Liver Lean Maintenance Medium (CS-07-305B-01, InSphero Inc.), and DMSO final concentration was 0.1 %.

Liver spheroid assay

Spheroid Model:

3D InSight™ Human Liver Model (Cat# MT-02–302-04, InSphero Inc.) composed of human primary hepatocytes from 10 donors (5 male and 5 female), and non-parenchymal cells from 1 donor were used to test PFAS impact on liver functions. This model has been previously validated through hepatotoxicity screenings of over 150 FDA-approved small molecule drugs, demonstrating strong predictive accuracy for human outcomes (Fäs et al., 2024).

Spheroids were shipped by the company in culture, in a 96-well format, with a single spheroid per well, under sterile conditions. Upon receipt, spheroid media was replaced with 3D InSight™ Human Liver Lean Maintenance Medium (Cat# CS-07-305B-01, InSphero Inc) and spheroids were allowed to rest overnight in an incubator at 37°C with 5 % CO2. PFAS exposure was initiated in the following day. Of note, the Human Liver Lean Maintenance Medium, a proprietary formulation from InSphero, has no additives or serum and contain glucose concentration comparable to normal glucose range found in human plasma.

Treatment:

PFOA, PFOS, PFHxS, and PFNA were each diluted to a final concentration of 20 μM in spheroid maintenance medium. Control spheroids were treated with 0.1 % DMSO in the same medium. Treatment began on day 0, with media replaced by fresh PFAS (or DMSO)-containing medium on days 2 and 5. On day 7, media was harvested, and spheroids were collected for single-cell transcriptomic analysis. This media replacement regimen was applied uniformly across all experimental batches. For each condition, a total of 96 spheroids was treated independently with a single compound, and 96 spheroids served as DMSO-treated controls. To guarantee a homogenous dosing, PFAS working solutions were prepared immediately before treatment to minimize compound adsorption. Solutions were gently resuspended multiple times before being added to culture wells to promote uniform distribution. Spheroids were maintained in 75 μL of medium in Akura™ 96 ultra-low attachment microplates, which feature a conical, tapered bottom that localizes spheroids in a 1.0 mm observation chamber. This design facilitates consistent media exchange while reducing variability in compound exposure. Additionally, media exchanges were conducted every 2–3 days to maintain consistent PFAS exposure and mitigate issues related to their amphiphilic nature and their tendency to accumulate at the air-medium interface (Fang et al., 2025). No further quantification of PFAS concentrations was performed in the working solutions.

Rationale for PFAS Concentration and Exposure Duration:

We selected a final PFAS concentration of 20 μM based on several key considerations: (i) although human plasma PFAS concentrations are typically in the nanomolar range, PFAS concentration in the liver tissue may reach higher levels in the different areas due to hepatic metabolism and zonation, as observed with other chemicals and drugs (Wu et al., 2020; Kinoshita et al., 2010); (ii) in vitro studies often require higher concentrations to simulate cumulative exposure effects observed in vivo over extended periods; (iii) previous toxicological studies using the same liver spheroid model demonstrated that higher-than-blood PFAS concentrations are necessary to detect subtle physiological changes within a short exposure window, with 20 μM inducing measurable cellular responses without compromising cell viability (Rowan-Carroll et al., 2021); and (iv) while 20 μM of PFAS exceeds typical plasma concentrations in the general population, it is environmentally relevant for occupational workers exposed to high PFAS levels (Gao et al., 2015). Additionally, we selected a 7-day exposure duration based on multiple factors: (i) the functional integrity of the 3D liver spheroid model lasts approximately 28 days, and a 7-day window allows for assessing early molecular and cellular responses before significant aging or nutrient depletion occurs; (ii) previous toxicological studies indicate that shortterm PFAS exposure (1–14 days) (Rowan-Carroll et al., 2021) can induce measurable transcriptional changes, with the highest number of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) observed on days 4 and 10, followed by a decline in DEGs by day 14, enabling the detection of early liver function perturbations without long-term adaptation or compensation; and (iii) prolonged PFAS exposure may lead to adaptive cellular responses that could mask early metabolic changes, whereas a 7-day exposure provides a balance between acute and sustained effects.

Cytotoxicity assessment

PFAS cytotoxicity to liver spheroids was assessed using culture media collected on days 2, 5 and 7 of exposure with the LDH-Glo Cytotoxicity Assay (Promega Corp, Cat# J2380), following manufacturer’s instructions. Harvested media (n = 3 per condition) were preserved in storage buffer (200 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.3, containing 10 % glycerol and 1 % bovine serum albumin) at a 1:20 dilution, and stored at −80°C until analysis. Briefly, samples were thawed at room temperature, mixed with an equal volume of LDH Detection Reagent, and incubated for 60 min at room temperature. Luminescence was then measured using the SpectraMax® iD3 multi-mode microplate reader (Molecular Devices) to obtain relative luminescence units (RLU). Culture media with 0.1 % DMSO was used as blank (medium background), and spheroids lysed in Triton-X served as the maximum LDH release control. Lipid accumulation

Four spheroids per condition were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde (Cat#1004960700, Sigma-Aldrich) diluted in 1x PBS (Cat#10010023, Gibco) for 1 h, permeabilized with 0.2 % Triton-X100 (Cat#85111, Thermo-Scientific) diluted in 1x PBS for 30 min, and 1 % bovine serum albumin (BSA; Cat#A2153, Sigma-Aldrich) diluted in 1x PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Spheroids were stained with Nile Red (Cat#72485, Sigma-Aldrich) at 1 μg/mL, and DAPI (Cat#D9542, Sigma-Aldrich) at 2 μg/mL in 1 % BSA/PBS for 30 min, washed 3x with PBS and mounted with Prolong Gold Mounting Medium (Cat#P36934, Invitrogen). Five sequential images were acquired for each spheroid using Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope.

Luminex

We measured specific proteins involved in cell–cell communication in spheroid culture supernatants at multiple time points (days 2, 5 and 7 of exposure) using the customized Human Luminex® Discovery Assay panel (Cat#LXSAHM, R&D systems), following the manufacturer’s instructions. In addition, we measured key pro-inflammatory cytokines relevant to liver inflammation in the supernatants at day 7 using the Human XL Cytokine Luminex® Kit Performance Assay (Cat# FCSTM18B, R&D Systems). Values are expressed as Log2 fold changes relative to control samples.

Single-cell RNA-sequencing

Spheroids Dissociation:

Spheroids were dissociated individually in the 96-well plates. Briefly, spheroids were washed twice with Hanks’ Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS-1X; Cat#14175095, Gibco) without phenol red, calcium, or magnesium. Then, 0.25 % trypsin/EDTA (Cat#0103, ScienCell) was added to each well and incubated at 37°C for up to 15 min. Every 5 min, spheroids were mechanically dissociated using an automated pipette and observed under a microscope to confirm dissociation. Once dissociation was satisfactory, cells were pooled into a tube containing HBSS with 10 % fetal bovine serum (Cat#A5670801, Thermo-Scientific). Cells were then centrifuged at 400g for 10 min at 4°C. Cell pellet was resuspended in the Dead Cell Removal Kit microbead solution (Cat#130–090-101, Miltenyi Biotech), and dead cell removal was performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After viable cells were recovered, they were centrifuged again at 400g for 10 min at 4°C, and the pellet was resuspended in 50 μL of PBS + 0.04 % BSA. Cells concentration and viability were assessed using trypan blue exclusion. All samples showed viability above 95 %.

scRNA-seq library and sequencing:

An average of 7,000 cells/condition were partitioned using the Chromium Next GEM Single Cell 3′ Kit (Cat#1000269, PN-1000130 and PN-1000129, 10X Genomics). One scRNA-seq library per experimental condition was constructed following the manufacturer’s instructions (Cat#1000269, PN-1000196, 10X Genomics). Libraries were sequenced at the USC Molecular Genomics Core using the Illumina platform, and following the 10X Genomics’ parameters, with a sequencing depth of 40,000 reads per cells.

Bioinformatic data processing

Raw data processing and cell quality control:

Raw sequencing data was processed using the Cell Ranger count pipeline (v7.0.1, refdata-gex-GRCh38-2020-A, 10X Genomics) to produce a gene expression by cell barcode count matrix for each sample. Low quality cells were removed from the data set by filtering out cells with less than 3 median absolute deviations (MADs) bellow the median number of reads, cells with less than 3 MADS bellow or above the median number of genes, and cells with greater than 3 MADS above the median percent of reads mapping to mitochondrial genes (Fig. S1). Quality control filtering was applied to each sample individually and the filtered data sets were used for downstream analysis.

Sample integration, clustering, and cell annotation:

Comparison between PFAS-treated and control samples was performed by integrating samples using the R package Seurat (Stuart et al., 2019; Butler et al., 2018). Differences in mitochondrial gene content among samples were corrected during sample integration by passing the percent of reads mapping to mitochondrial genes to the vars. to.regress parameter in the SCTransform() function. The first 30 PCs were used as the number of dimensions for the FindNeighbors() and FindClusters() functions. A range of cluster resolutions was visualized using the R package clustree (Zappia and Oshlack, 2018), and a final resolution value of 0.4 was used for downstream analyses. Dimensionality reduction for visualization was performed using the Seurat RunUMAP() function. Cells were annotated manually by examining the expression of key marker genes among clusters. Differentially expressed genes between PFAS-treated and control groups were detected using the Wilcoxon Rank Sum test in the Seurat FindMarkers() and genes with a Bonferroni adjusted p-value < 0.1 and a fold change ≥ 1.2 were considered significant.

The raw sequencing data and processed count data generated for this manuscript were deposited into the NCBI Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database under the accession number GSE280527 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE280527).

Sex Difference:

We used the R package cellXY (v0.99.0, https://github.com/phipsonlab/cellXY) to predict the sex of individual cells (Male/Female) based on feature gene expression. The sex of each cell was determined across the entire Seurat object. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the treated and control groups were then identified separately for each sex using the Wilcoxon Rank Sum test in the Seurat function FindMarkers, applying a significance threshold of a Bonferroni adjusted p-value < 0.1 and a fold change ≥ 1.2. Sex differences were analyzed only using the hepatocyte population.

Transcriptomic cancer signature:

Cells with cancer like expression profiles were identified with the R package scATOMIC (v2.0.3) (Nofech-Mozes et al., 2023) using the gene by cell counts extracted from the integrated Seurat object.

Cell-to-cell communication:

Cell-cell communication analysis was performed using the R package CellChat (v2.1.2) (Jin et al., 2024) for each sample. Significant interactions at ligand-receptor and pathway level were measured by the communication probability with a p-value of 0.01 as the significance threshold.

Biological interpretation:

To assess the biological significance of differentially expressed genes, pathway enrichment analysis was performed using the g:GOSt tool from the g:Profiler platform (online version e112). Data sources included KEGG, REACTOME, WikiPathways, and Gene Ontology (GO). Pathways or biological processes were considered significant with a threshold of −log10(p-value) ≥ 1.32. Additionally, Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) software (v122103623, Qiagen) was used to evaluate the significance and direction of regulation of canonical pathways and biological functions across experimental conditions, applying the criteria of −log10(p-value) ≥ 1.32 and Z-score ≥ 2 or ≤ −2.

Results

PFAS impact on human liver spheroid transcriptome

To investigate the mechanism of action of PFOA, PFOS, PFNA, and PFHxS on human liver metabolism, we utilized the multi-donor liver spheroid model composed of primary human hepatocytes and non-parenchymal cells. Single spheroids were individually exposed to 20 μM of PFOA, PFOS, PFNA, or PFHxS for 7 days (Fig. 1A). At the endpoint, cells from 92 spheroids were pooled and evaluated using whole-transcriptome single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNAseq), while 4 spheroids were analyzed for lipid accumulation using Nile Red staining. Supernatant collected on days 2, 5 and 7 of PFAS exposure were further analyzed for cytotoxicity using LDH assay, and for specific protein secretion using Luminex.

As expected, exposure of liver spheroids to 20 μM of PFOA, PFHxS, PFOS, and PFNA did not induce a significant increase in cytotoxicity over time, with values comparable to those observed in DMSO-treated controls (Fig. S2), confirming 20 μM as a non-cytotoxic dose for a 7-day exposure period.

We profiled 8,580 cells from controls (DMSO-exposed spheroids), 6,617 cells from PFOA, 4,420 cells from PFOS, 2,222 cells from PFNA, and 4,493 cells from PFHxS groups. We identified five cell clusters across all experimental groups: hepatocytes, T/NK cells (T/NKs), Kupffer cells (KCs), endothelial cells (ECs), and B cells (Fig. 1B). The top genes used for cell cluster annotation are shown in Fig. 1C. Overall, hepatocytes were the predominant cell population among the other cell types, followed by T/NK cells and Kupffer cells. ECs and B cells did not represent more than 2 % of the total cells (Fig. S3). Differentially expressed genes (DEG) were calculated by comparing each PFAS-exposed group to its respective batch control group, obtaining the DEGs for whole spheroids (global) and individual cell populations (Fig. 1D). Exposure to PFNA and PFOS elicited the highest number of DEGs, with 2,296 and 2,133 altered genes in the global transcriptome, respectively (Fig. 1D), whereas PFOA affected 313 genes, and PFHxS exposure changed the expression of 164 genes. Long-chain PFAS (PFNA, PFOS, and PFOA) predominantly disturbed hepatocyte gene expression, while the short-chain PFHxS preferentially affected the transcriptome of non-parenchymal cells, particularly Kupffer cells (Fig. 1D). Hierarchical clustering of the top upregulated genes in the whole spheroids revealed that PFHxS had minimal impact, showing a transcriptomic pattern resembling the control group, while PFNA induced the most pronounced transcriptomic changes in liver spheroid (Fig. 1E).

Distinct and overlapping PFAS effects on whole liver spheroid transcriptome

We evaluated the whole spheroid (global) gene expression and observed that PFOA, PFOS, PFNA, and PFHxS commonly affected the expression of 28 genes (Fig. 2A). Notably, PFNA downregulated most of them (Fig. 2B). The only genes commonly upregulated by all four PFAS were HADHB, ACADVL, and PLIN2, which are PPAR-α target genes involved in lipid metabolism (de la Rosa Rodriguez et al., 2018), and RPL37A and SQSTM1, involved in protein synthesis (Wool et al., 1995) and degradation (Geetha and Wooten, 2002). The pathway enrichment assay of the 28 genes using g:Profiler highlighted pathways related to chemical response, drug metabolism, lipid and fatty acid metabolism, PPAR-α signaling, and amino acid degradation (Fig. 2C). Although these PFAS shared target genes, most DEGs were PFAS-specific (Fig. 2D). PFOA affected the expression of 34 unique genes that enriched lipid metabolism pathways. PFOS altered 1,438 unique genes related to immune and inflammatory responses. PFNA changed the expression of 1,512 unique genes involved in protein and lipid homeostasis. PFHxS, altered the expression of only 8 unique genes, with no significant pathway enrichment.

We further investigated the whole spheroid transcriptome utilizing the Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA) software and identified the canonical pathways and biological processes significantly altered by PFAS exposure (−log10(pval) ≥ 1.3 AND Z-score ≥ 2 or ≤ −2). We then grouped the pathways according to their biological function. The major categories upregulated by PFHxS were immune response and lipid metabolism, while it downregulated cancer pathways and lipid metabolism-related events (Fig. 3A). PFOA upregulated lipid metabolism (Fig. 3B), and PFOS and PFNA upregulated cancer-related pathways (Fig. 3C, 3D). PFOA, PFOS, and PFNA exposure demonstrated immunosuppression.

Unique and shared effects of PFAS on cells from liver spheroid

To assess the impact of PFAS on different hepatic cell populations, we analyzed the transcriptomes of hepatocytes, KCs, and T/NKs from liver spheroids exposed to PFAS using IPA. We did not conduct similar evaluations with endothelial and B cells due to the small number of cells in their respective clusters. Similarly to the whole spheroid, hepatocytes exposed to PFHxS and PFOA displayed strong upregulation of lipid metabolism pathways, while PFOS and PFNA exposure predominantly increased cancer-related events (Fig. 4A). KCs were mainly affected by PFHxS and PFOS, with upregulation of several pathways involved in cancer (Fig. 4B) and inhibition of immune response events (Fig. S4). T/ NKs from spheroids exposed to PFOS and PFNA demonstrated upregulation of cancer-related events (Fig. 4C) and downregulation of immune response pathways (Fig. S4). T/NKs exposed to PFHxS displayed an increase in immune response and downregulation of lipid metabolism, while PFOA-exposed T/NKs exhibited both up and downregulation of pathways related to cancer, immune response, and lipid metabolism.

Sex-Specific response to PFAS exposure

The spheroids used in this study were composed of primary hepatocytes isolated from five male and five female donors. Using the R package cellXY, we classified single-cell hepatocytes by sex, identifying female-derived hepatocytes with the gene XIST and male-derived hepatocytes with RPS4Y1, DDX3Y, EIF1AY, and KDM5D (Fig. S5A) (Ontsouka et al., 2021; Nafian Dehkordi et al., 2021). Across the control, PFOA, PFHxS, and PFOS groups, female-derived hepatocytes accounted for an average of 32 % (±2.9 %), while male-derived hepatocytes made up 64 % (±3.6 %). In contrast, the PFNA group had 48 % female- and 52 % male-derived hepatocytes. A small fraction (2.8 % ±2%) of hepatocytes could not be assigned a sex (Fig. S5B).

Our analysis revealed that hepatocyte responses to PFAS exposure are not only compound-specific but also sex-specific (Fig. S6). PFHxS altered the expression of 435 genes in female hepatocytes and 2,497 genes in male hepatocytes, with 312 genes commonly differentially expressed (Fig. 5A). Among them, GATAD1 and PEX2 were upregulated in males but downregulated in females. PFOA exposure induced 1,262 differentially expressed (DE) genes in females and 1,915 DE genes in males, with 806 genes commonly affected (Fig. 5B). The genes ULK4, LRRC2, AL845331.2, and RETSAT were upregulated in males but downregulated in females, whereas ING3, PYROXD1, MFSD14B, ZBTB21, NUDT19, IPCEF1, ADIPOR1, FUBP3, LIPA, CREG1, RAB29, and ABCA9-AS1 were upregulated in females but downregulated in males.

PFNA exposure altered 8,153 genes in female hepatocytes and 9,345 genes in male hepatocytes, with 7,375 overlapping genes. Among these, 26 genes exhibited opposing regulation patterns between sexes (Fig. 5C). FAM49A, ELF4, ACSL5, FBXL3, SECISBP2L, TTN-AS1, USP38, RBM23, AC027307.2, HDAC5, and HYAL2 were upregulated in males but downregulated in females, while CTSC, IER5L, HSD17B10, SMAD1, THAP1, SPG21, ZFP36L2, SLC25A24, MAT2B, C2orf74, SYTL1, ARL4C, FKBP2, PHF19, and RPP14 were upregulated in females but downregulated in males.

PFOS exposure led to 8,906 DE genes in female hepatocytes and 9,721 DE genes in male hepatocytes, with 6,802 genes commonly affected. Notably, PFOS had the greatest impact on genes that switched regulation direction between sexes (Fig. 5D). Among these, 1,012 genes that were upregulated in males but downregulated in females were primarily associated with transcriptional regulation, ribonucleotide metabolism, immune responses involving IL-15, IL-23, and NF-κB, and activation of the PPAR-α/RXR-α pathway (Fig. 5E). Conversely, the 327 genes upregulated in females and downregulated in males were predominantly involved in lipid metabolism, including sphingolipid, glycerophospholipid, and triacylglycerol metabolism.

PFOA and PFHxS disrupt lipid metabolism through distinct mechanisms

Given the steatogenic effects of PFOA and PFHxS in the bulk transcriptomic analysis, we evaluated whether exposure to PFAS could also induce lipid accumulation. Nile red staining revealed that exposure to PFOA or PFHxS significantly increased lipid accumulation compared to control, PFOS, and PFNA (Fig. 6A). To investigate the molecular mechanisms underlying this lipid accumulation, we analyzed the canonical pathways and biological processes in hepatocyte from male and female donors affected by PFAS exposure.

Intracellular lipid accumulation was visualized with Nile Red (red), and cell nuclei were stained with DAPI (blue) (n = 4). Images were captured using confocal microscopy at 400x magnification, and lipid levels were quantified using ImageJ software. Statistical significance was determined using one-way ANOVA (***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001). PFHxS downregulated pathways involved in lipid uptake, transport and efflux across all hepatocytes, as well as fatty acid metabolism, DHCR24 signaling and LXL/RXR activation. In addition, PFHxS exposure upregulated the lipid accumulation pathway in male hepatocytes and fatty acid β-oxidation and PPAR-α-regulated lipid metabolism in females (Fig. 6B). Hepatocytes exposed to PFOA showed greater upregulation of pathways associated with de novo lipogenesis than lipolysis, including fatty acid oxidation, conversion and activation, and the biosynthesis of stearate and γ-linolenate (Fig. 6C). Interestingly, PFOA had a stronger impact on female hepatocytes, upregulating more pathways than in male, particularly those involved in lipid metabolism regulation by PPAR-α, lipid oxidation, modification of palmitoyl-CoA, modification and conversion of and acyl-CoA, and phospholipid metabolism.

PFOS primarily affected hepatocytes from male donors, upregulating pathways related to lipid oxidation and synthesis. PFOS upregulated the regulation of lipid metabolism by PPAR-α pathway, and downregulated cholesterol biosynthesis, DHCR24 signaling and LXR/RXR activation in both sexes (Fig. 6D). PFNA strongly downregulated lipid metabolism pathways, including cholesterol biosynthesis, DHCR24 signaling, LXR/RXR activation and gene expression activation by SREBP across all hepatocytes. Hepatocytes from female donors exhibited additional downregulation of fatty acid metabolism, lipid uptake, transport and conversion. In contrast, PFNA upregulated regulation of lipid metabolism by PPAR-α pathways in males (Fig. 6E).

Differential pro-oncogenic effects of PFNA and PFOS

As identified by the biological process categorical analysis, PFOS and PFNA upregulated cancer-related events in liver spheroids. To further determine whether the cells also carried a known liver cancer transcriptomic signature, we used the R package scATOMIC (Nofech-Mozes et al., 2023) (Fig. 7A). Remarkably, 61.3 % of the cells from spheroids exposed to PFNA carried a cancer signature, and 99 % of them were hepatocytes. Cells from control spheroids or those exposed to PFHxS, PFOA and PFOS did not have a significant number of malignant cells (0.06 % ±0.01).

We then further analyzed several pathways in the hepatocyte subpopulations from PFAS-exposed spheroids that participate in cancer development, including cell cycle, oxidative stress, DNA damage repair and inflammation. Within the cell cycle, we analyzed pathways related to mitotic phases and Hedgehog (Hh) signaling. For oxidative stress, we identified NRF2 (NFE2L2)-mediated antioxidant responses. Inflammation pathways included NF-κB, TNF, TGF-β, and VEGF signaling (Fig. 7B).

PFNA and PFOS exposure triggered the upregulation of most of these pathways, while PFHxS and PFOA showed minimal impact. PFNA affected hepatocytes from male and female donors equally and impacted more pathways than PFOS, particularly those related to cell cycle progression, DNA repair, and inflammation. In contrast, PFOS had a stronger effect on male-derived hepatocytes, which showed robust upregulation of cancer-related pathways but limited upregulation of cell cycle pathways and minimal impact on DNA repair and inflammation, except for VEGF signaling. Notably, we observed sustained VEGFA secretion in the supernatants of PFOS-exposed spheroids over the 7-day exposure period (Fig. S7). Female-derived hepatocytes from PFOS-exposed spheroids showed weaker and fewer cancer-related changes, with modest upregulation of DNA repair and inflammation pathways.

PFAS Suppress hepatic immune cell pathways

We observed that PFHxS, PFOA, PFOS, and PFNA exposure also altered the transcriptome of immune cells (Fig. 8A). The pathway analysis revealed that PFOS and PFNA downregulated T/NK cell migration, proliferation, viability, cytotoxicity, TCR signaling, cell-mediated response, maturation, and MHC-I mediated antigen processing and presentation (APP) (Fig. 8A). PFOA and PFHxS had milder effect on T/NKs, downregulating cellular activation, fatty acid metabolism and transport, and triggering cellular oxidative stress. Additionally, PFOS, PFNA, and PFOA inhibited the T/NK inflammatory response.

PFHxS, PFOA, and PFOS exposure downregulated immune-related pathways in KCs, particularly pathways involved in cell migration, immune response, engulfment, and cell differentiation (Fig. 8B). Additionally, PFOS downregulated inflammasome pathway and upregulated oxidative phosphorylation pathway. Conversely, PFNA upregulated the pro-inflammatory pathway IL-12 signaling and production and lipid uptake.

We further measured key cytokines involved in hepatic inflammation in the supernatant of the spheroids exposed to PFAS for 7 days (Fig. 8C). Compared to DMSO-treated controls, we observed a marked downregulation in the secretion of the chemokines CXCL-10, CXCL-8 and macrophage migration inhibitory factor (MIF), and the interleukins IL-12, IL-6, IL-1β. No changes were detected in the secretion of the tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α and the chemokine CCL-5 (data not shown).

CellChat identified communication patterns driven by PFAS

We used CellChat (Jin et al., 2024) to uncover intercellular communication changes triggered by PFAS among liver spheroid cells. Control spheroids had 223 significant cell–cell communication (CCC) pathways involving 116 ligand-receptor (L-R) pairs (Excel Table S1), with a robust communication flow between KCs and between hepatocytes (Fig. 9A). PFAS exposure reduced the overall number of CCC events, with 148 in PFHxS-, 182 in PFOA-, 58 in PFOS-, and 183 in PFNA-exposed spheroids (Fig. 9A).

(B-E) Ligand-receptor (L-R) pairs uniquely upregulated by specific PFAS: (B) PFHxS, (C) PFOA, (D) PFOS, and (E) PFNA. Sig. After excluding CCC pathways shared with control spheroids, we identified distinct effects of each PFAS: PFHxS triggered L-R pairs involved in lipid metabolism and inflammation (Fig. 9B); PFOA induced L-R pairs associated with apoptosis, ECM/cell adhesion, lipid metabolism, and inflammation (Fig. 9C); PFOS exhibited the weakest cell–cell communication induction, activating only three L-R pairs related to inflammation (Fig. 9D); and PFNA upregulated the highest number of L-R pairs, which were involved in angiogenesis, apoptosis, cell proliferation, ECM/cell adhesion, lipid metabolism, and inflammation (Fig. 9E).

Most of the lipid metabolism-related CCC events triggered by PFHxS, PFOA, and PFNA originated from hepatocytes and used ANGPTL4 as the ligand (Fig. 9B, 9C, and 9E). We detected elevated and sustained levels of secreted ANGPTL4 protein in the supernatants of spheroids exposed to these PFAS, as well as PFOS (Fig. S7). Although PFOS-exposed spheroids secreted ANGPTL4, very few hepatocytes expressed the SDC receptor (pct.1 values (%): SDC1 = 10.24, SDC2 = 0, SDC4 = 9.08), which likely explains the absence of this L-R pair in the PFOS group. Additionally, the L-R pair 27HC-CYP3A4_NR1H4, involved in bile acid metabolism, was detected in spheroids exposed to PFHxS and PFOA, while the pairs DHEAS-SULT1E1_PPARA and DHEAS-SULT1E1_PPARG, involved in steroid synthesis, were detected in spheroids exposed to PFOA.

A pro-inflammatory CCC event shared by all tested PFAS was the L-R pair PPIA_BSG, predominantly involving the T/NK cell cluster (Fig. 9B–E). Since MMP-9 is the primary product of this interaction, we measured its levels in the supernatants of PFAS-exposed spheroids. We observed a progressive increase in MMP-9 secretion in spheroids exposed to PFHxS, PFOA, and PFOS, whereas PFNA induced an early secretion pattern (Fig. S7). Additionally, MIF-driven CCC was significant in spheroids exposed to PFHxS, PFOS, and PFNA, also involving the T/NK cell cluster (Fig. 9B, 9D, and 9E). In PFOA-exposed spheroids, the L-R pairs ICAM1_ITGAX_ITGB2 and ICAM1_ITGAM_ITGB2 were upregulated in KC-KC communication.

PFOA and PFNA also induced apoptotic CCC signaling via IGFBP3_TMEM219 (Fig. 9C and 9E). In PFNA-exposed spheroids, we detected early IGFBP3 secretion in the supernatant, whereas PFOA triggered a delayed wave of IGFBP3 secretion (Fig. S7). Notably, PFNA was the only PFAS that upregulated L-R pairs involved cell proliferation. These pathways primarily involved hepatocytes, utilizing the receptor EGFR for the TGFA and AREG ligands, and the receptor BMPR2 for BMP2 (Fig. 9E). PFNA also upregulated anti-inflammatory CCC pathways using TGFB1 as ligand.

Compared to CCC pathways observed in control spheroids, we found that PFHxS-exposed spheroids lacked several immune response pathways, particularly those involving chemokines and HLA-class I /II interactions (Fig. S8). PFOA inhibited multiple L-R pairs targeting T/NKs (Fig. S9), especially those using HLA-class I and CD8B. PFOS suppressed the highest number of CCC events (Figs. S10 and S11). Among them, we found several immune and inflammatory L-R pairs, particularly interactions between chemokines and the receptor CCR1, as well as HLA-class II and CD4 interactions between KCs. PFNA inhibited cholesterol production via dehydrocholesterol reductase (DHCR) and steroid synthesis via dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS) (Fig. S12).

Discussion

Recent advances in PFAS research have strengthened the association between PFAS exposure and chronic liver diseases in humans, including MASLD and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) (Zhang et al., May 2023; Goodrich et al., 2022). Among the most studied PFAS are PFOS, PFOA, PFHxS, and PFNA. Overall, studies have shown that exposure to these PFAS and their partition in liver tissue can disrupt lipid metabolism, potentially leading to abnormal hepatic fat accumulation, oxidative stress, DNA damage, unfolded protein response, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and dysregulation of nuclear receptors activity (e.g., PPARα, PXR, CAR), ultimately contributing to liver damage, inflammation, and cancer (Costello et al., 2022; Obiako et al., 2024; Qi et al., 2023; Wolf et al., 2008). However, mechanistic studies that can identify potential interventional targets remain limited.

To address this knowledge gap, we developed an in vitro framework to investigate the cellular and molecular mechanisms by which PFAS contribute to liver metabolic dysfunction. We integrated a human-relevant model, 3D human liver spheroids, with whole-transcriptome scRNA-seq. Our findings demonstrate that PFAS exposure disrupts the liver spheroid transcriptome in a compound- and sex-specific manner. Notably, there was minimal overlap in gene expression changes across PFOS, PFOA, PFNA, and PFHxS exposures, emphasizing distinct compound-specific effects at both global and cell-type-specific transcriptomic levels. We identified the pathways involved in the steatogenic traits of PFOA and PFHxS, as well as the potential pro-oncogenic mechanisms of PFOS and PFNA. Additionally, we identified key immune regulatory pathways that were downregulated by all four PFAS, along with the potential alterations in hepatic cell–cell communication resulting from PFAS exposure.

The observed differences in the impact of the four PFAS tested in this study are likely related to their distinct physicochemical properties, which influence their partitioning in the liver and their potential effects on specific cell types from spheroids. PFOS is typically found at the highest concentrations in human liver, followed by PFOA, PFHxS, and PFNA (Pérez et al., 2013). Differential interaction with hepatic uptake and efflux transporters, as well as varying binding affinities to plasma and cellular proteins, likely contribute to the degree of PFAS accumulation in the liver (Vujic et al., 2024; Fan et al., 2025). PFOA and PFNA have high affinity for fatty acid-binding proteins, while PFHxS and PFOS demonstrate significant affinity for albumin (Fan et al., 2025). PFOS and PFOA also have strong affinities to lipids and phospholipids, suggesting that their mechanism of tissue accumulation also involves binding to cell membrane (Sanchez Garcia et al., 2018). Additionally, PFAS chain length may affect tissue permeability, with short-chain PFAS more readily penetrating tissues, including in 3D culture models, compared to long-chain PFAS (Ragnarsdóttir et al., 2024). Based on these findings, we speculate that the nominal concentration of 20 μM likely resulted in varying intra-spheroid concentrations, with long-chain PFAS (PFNA, PFOS, and PFOA) accumulating predominantly at the spheroid periphery and primarily affecting hepatocytes, whereas the short-chain PFHxS may have penetrated deeper into the spheroids, increasing its effect on non-parenchymal cells. The distribution pattern may explain the distinct cell-specific transcriptional responses observed in our experiments (Fig. 1D).

Mechanisms of PFAS-Induced hepatic lipid metabolism disruption

Previous studies suggest that PFAS exposure disrupts lipid metabolism homeostasis, particularly through PPAR-α signalling. PPAR-α is a transcription factor member of the steroid hormone receptor superfamily that is activated by endogenous and synthetic ligands. More than 75 % of the genes regulated by PFHxS, PFOA, and PFNA in mouse liver were found to be PPARα-dependent, with target genes modulating amino acid homeostasis and fatty acid β-oxidation (Nelson et al., 2010; Haug et al., 2023). In contrast, PFOS is described as a weak PPARα activator (Rosen et al., 2010; 2010.).

Lipid metabolism imbalance can lead to dyslipidemia, a hallmark of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Elevated plasma concentrations of PFOA, PFOS, and PFNA have been associated with dyslipidemia in adults, while high PFHxS levels were linked to low cholesterol levels (Nelson et al., 2010; Haug et al., 2023). Additionally, PFOA and PFHxS exposure promote liver triglyceride production in mice, contributing to steatosis (Das et al., 2017). Furthermore, PFOA, PFHxS, PFOS and PFNA have been implicated in MASLD development in humans, affecting different aspects of the disease spectrum (Zhang et al., May 2023; Jin et al., 2020). Sex-specific differences have also been observed. Human studies suggest that females may be more susceptible to PFAS-induced liver dysfunction, showing higher levels of hepatic enzymes in association with increased PFOA and PFNA exposure (Attanasio, 2019). PFAS exposure in females was also linked to elevated levels of hepatic lipids associated with MASLD (Sen et al., 2022). However, the precise mechanisms underlying these effects remain unclear.

Rowan-Carroll et al. (Rowan-Carroll et al., 2021) and Reardon et al. (Reardon et al., 2021) evaluated the impact of multiple PFAS on liver metabolism by conducting a toxicological screening across a wide range of PFAS doses (0.02 to 100 μM) and exposure durations (1, 4, 10 and 14 days). They employed the same 3D human liver spheroid model used in our study but cultured the spheroids in high-glucose maintenance media and used targeted bulk-RNA sequencing for transcriptomic analysis. Later, Barutcu et al. (Barutcu et al., 2024) performed a deeper transcriptomic analysis of these datasets. Their findings showed that PFOA, PFHxS, and PFOS altered lipid metabolism pathways in liver spheroids. Specifically, exposure to 20 μM PFOA consistently upregulated stearate biosynthesis and fatty acid oxidation from day 1 to day 14, whereas higher doses of PFOA, such as 50 μM, had the opposite effect. They also reported a significant increase in the expression of genes involved in fatty acid oxidation and regulation of lipid metabolism via PPAR-α in spheroids exposed to 20 μM of PFOA or 100 μM of PFHxS after 10 days of treatment. Conversely, 20 μM of PFOS significantly downregulated pathways related to cholesterol biosynthesis over the same 14-day exposure period. The downregulation of lipid metabolism genes could be detected in lower concentrations, such as 10 μM.

Our findings provide new insight into these mechanisms, revealing that most of transcriptomic changes related to lipid metabolism in PFAS-exposed spheroids originate from hepatocytes and exhibit sex-specific patterns. PFOA exposure upregulated more pathways related to de novo lipogenesis than lipolysis, particularly in female-derived hepatocytes, leading to the strongest lipid accumulation in spheroids among the tested PFAS. Interestingly, PFHxS downregulated fatty acid metabolism, as well as, lipid and cholesterol transport and efflux, and upregulated pathways contributing to lipid accumulation, such as fatty acid β-oxidation. This may explain the significant lipid accumulation observed in PFHxS-exposed spheroids.

Our data demonstrates that PFOS and PFNA exposure does not result in spheroid lipid accumulation, corroborating the findings previously published by Rowan-Carroll et al. (Rowan-Carroll et al., 2021). Our results suggest that the downregulation of fatty acid metabolism and cholesterol biosynthesis caused by PFOS and PFNA exposure may be linked to the suppression of 3β-hydroxysterol-Δ24-reductase (DHCR24) signaling, an enzyme essential for cholesterol synthesis (Luu et al., 2014). This disruption may impair sterol regulatory element-binding protein (SREBP) activity, also downregulated, ultimately suppressing fatty acid metabolism (Ye and DeBose-Boyd, 2011). These PFAS also inhibited the Liver X receptor-retinoid X receptor (LXR-RXR) signaling, which regulates the expression of genes controlling cholesterol efflux from cells (Repa and Mangelsdorf, 1999). However, PFOS and PFNA have been positively associated with dyslipidemia in humans (Haug et al., 2023), and animal studies suggest that PFOS and PFNA can enhance steatosis in combination with a high-fat diet (HFD) (Pfohl et al., 2021). This discrepancy between in vivo and in vitro findings may reflect the controlled experimental conditions of this in vitro system, which was specifically designed to isolate the direct effects of PFAS on hepatic lipid and carbohydrate metabolism. The absence of inter-organ interactions, along with the use of a normal-glucose maintenance medium lacking high levels of fatty acid, likely minimized confounding factors such as diet-induced lipid accumulation.

PFAS Pro-Oncogenic and immunosuppressive mechanisms

Recently, the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) classified PFOA as carcinogenic to humans and PFOS as possibly carcinogenic (Zahm et al., 2024). PFOA has been linked to an increased risk of kidney and testicular cancer among individuals living near or working in PFOA-related chemical plants (Barry et al., 2013). Additionally, population-based studies have shown an association between PFOS exposure and non-viral HCC (Goodrich et al., 2022). Although PFNA has not been included in the IARC report, PFNA exposure has been linked to a higher risk of kidney cancer (Rhee et al., 2023). More broadly, PFAS have been linked to various cancers, including those of the urinary system, brain and other parts of nervous system, as well as leukemia, and soft tissues in males, and cancers of the endocrine system, oral cavity and pharynx, and soft tissue in females (Li et al., 2025). Nonetheless, the precise mechanisms by which PFAS contribute to cancer development remain largely underexplored.

Previous studies using the same 3D spheroids model to assess PFAS toxicity did not report cancer-related transcriptional response to PFAS (Rowan-Carroll et al., 2021; Reardon et al., 2021; Barutcu et al., 2024). A likely explanation is that those studies used the TempO-seq S1500 panel (BioSpyder Technologies Inc) for transcriptomic profiling, which targets less than 3,000 genes focused on cellular stress and toxicity. In contrast, our study used whole-transcriptome analysis at the single-cell level, enabling a more comprehensive characterization of PFAS-induced biological changes in the liver spheroids.

Our findings reveal that PFOS- and PFNA-exposed liver spheroids upregulated numerous cancer-related pathways, particularly in hepatocytes. Notably, PFNA-exposed spheroids displayed a large number of hepatocytes with transcriptomic cancer signature. PFNA equally affected hepatocytes from both sexes, upregulating multiple pathways associated with cell proliferation and Hedgehog (Hh) signaling. Hh signaling is an essential regulator of liver homeostasis, and its persistent activation has been implicated in liver tumor development (Machado and Diehl, 2018).

PFNA also upregulated oxidative stress response via NRF2 (NFE2L2), a master regulator of anti-oxidative stress response and detoxification pathways (Vomund et al., 2017). While NRF2 activation plays a protective role under normal conditions, it is often hijacked by cancer cells to evade oxidative damage (Vomund et al., 2017; Xu et al., 2023). Elevated levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS) are commonly associated with DNA damage, mutations, genome instability contributing to tumor initiation, key events in tumor initiation (Visconti and Grieco, 2009). In our data, the upregulation of DNA repair pathways following PFNA exposure may indicate oxidative stress-induced DNA damage. Moreover, PFNA-exposed hepatocytes showed upregulation of inflammatory pathways driven by NF-κB and TNF signaling. NF-κB is a family of dimeric transcription factors linked to inflammation associated-cancers, where it promotes cancer cell survival and is activated by the tumor microenvironment (DiDonato et al., 2012). In the context of hepatic low-grade inflammation, NF-κB activation enhances TNF production and signaling in hepatocytes, contributing to tumor development (Pikarsky et al., 2004). TNF also plays roles in chronic inflammation and cancer progression by inducing ROS production (Yan et al., 2006) and promoting angiogenesis via vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA) signaling (Giraudo et al., 1998).

In contrast, PFOS-exposed hepatocytes exhibited significant upregulation of cancer-related pathways without corresponding upregulation of cell cycle, DNA repair or inflammation pathways. PFOS affected female and male hepatocytes differently. Female-derived cells showed modest upregulation of NF-κB and TNF signaling, while male-derived hepatocytes upregulated VEGF signaling – key to angiogenesis, tumor growth and metastasis (Shibuya and Claesson-Welsh, 2006).

All PFAS tested inhibited immune-related pathways in innate and adaptive immune hepatic cells from liver spheroids and markedly decreased the secretion of cytokines critical for hepatic inflammation. PFOS and PFNA exhibited a robust immunosuppressive impact on T/NKs, particularly through inhibition of T cell receptor (TCR) signaling and cytotoxicity pathways, which may facilitate cancer development and progression (Buchbinder and Desai, 2016). This observation is supported by protein analysis, which showed that spheroids exposed to PFOS and PFNA had the deepest inhibition of CXCL-10 secretion. CXCL-10 is a potent chemoattractant for cytotoxic T lymphocytes and NK cells, promoting their activation and polarization into highly effective cytotoxic effector cells, particularly in cancer environments (Dong et al., 2024; Saudemont et al., 2005). PFOS and PFNA exposure also resulted in the strongest inhibition of CXCL-8 secretion, a chemokine involved in recruiting various immune cell types, including neutrophils, monocytes, NK cells, and T cells, and in coordinating innate and adaptive immune responses (Cavalieri et al., 2005; Gerszten et al., 1999; Vujanovic et al., 2012; Larsen et al., 1989). All PFAS inhibited secretion of IL-12, IL-1β, and MIF. IL-12 is a pro-inflammatory cytokine critical for the activation of effector T and NK cells, while IL-1β is typically secreted by pro-inflammatory Kupffer cells, and MIF is involved in the recruitment and M1 polarization of hepatic macrophages (Gately et al., 1998; Nguyen-Lefebvre and Horuzsko, 2015; Heinrichs et al., 2014). PFOA, PFOS and PFNA also inhibited the secretion of IL-6, a pro-inflammatory cytokine involved in acute hepatic inflammation, primarily secreted by innate immune cells (Schmidt-Arras and Rose-John, 2016). Additionally, PFOS inhibited inflammasome pathways and upregulated oxidative phosphorylation in KCs, suggesting that KCs in PFOS-exposed spheroids may acquire anti-inflammatory properties (Flores-Costa et al., 2020).

Although PFOA is classified as carcinogenic, we did not observe significant upregulation of cancer-related pathway in PFOA-exposed spheroids. This may reflect cell-specific effects, limited exposure duration, or the absence of additional oncogenic stimuli. Interestingly, Mei et al. (2024) reported that PFOA promotes lung adenocarcinoma metastasis by reducing cancer cells stiffness, adhesion, and immune recognition. These finding suggest that PFOA may not directly activate oncogenic pathways in primary healthy cells but could exacerbate tumor progression in pre-existing cancerous cells.

Hepatic Cell-Cell communication induced by PFAS exposure

Cell-cell communication (CCC) is essential for tissue homeostasis, development, disease and repair. It typically involves ligand-receptor interactions that trigger downstream signaling in the receiver cell (Armingol et al., 2021). The advent of scRNA-seq has enabled the identification of complex changes in cellular communication patterns. In this study, we used the CellChat tool to identify CCC alterations induced by PFAS exposure (Jin et al., 2024).

We identified several major CCC categories affected by PFAS, particularly those related to lipid metabolism, cell adhesion to the extracellular matrix, inflammation and immune response. Notably, PFAS exposure reduced the overall number of CCC events compared to controls (Fig. 9). PFOS, in particular, disrupted most spheroid cellular communication originating from hepatocytes and Kupffer cells.

Hepatocytes exposed to PFOA, PFHxS and PFNA primarily used the ligand angiopoietin-like 4 (ANGPTL4) to drive lipid metabolism-related CCCs (Kersten, 2021). Hepatocyte-specific ANGPTL4 secretion has been linked to hepatic lipid accumulation and systemic effects, including increased plasma triacylglycerol, total cholesterol, and overall body fat mass in mice (Singh et al., 2021). Consistently, we detected elevated levels of ANGPTL4 in supernatants of spheroids exposed to PFHxS, PFOA, PFNA and PFOS (Fig. S7), suggesting its potential role as a mediator of systemic lipid dysregulation in response to PFAS exposure.

All PFAS, particularly PFHxS, upregulated the L-R pair PPIA_BSG. PPIA encodes cyclophilin A (CypA), which plays a significant role in inflammation by recruiting immune cells via CD147 (BSG) (Yurchenko et al., 2010). CD147 is a potent inducer of extracellular matrix metalloproteinase (MMP) (Nigro et al., 2013), and its interaction with CypA enhances MMP-9 production and activation, accelerating extracellular matrix degradation (Yang et al., 2008). Consistently, we observed an increase in MMP-9 secretion in PFAS-exposed spheroids, suggesting that PFAS exposure may promote tissue remodeling and degradation, contributing to hepatic inflammation and fibrosis. Additionally, spheroids exposed to PFHxS, PFNA and PFOS displayed CCCs driven by MIF, particularly targeting T/NK cell clusters, while PFOA upregulated the ICAM1_ITGAX_ITGB2 ligand-receptor pair in KC-KC communication. MIF has been linked to hepatic oxidative stress and inflammation (Vukícevíc et al., 2021). ICAM-1 (Intercellular adhesion molecule-1) is upregulated by inflammation to facilitate immune cell recruitment (Benedicto et al., 2017), while ITGAX (integrin alpha X/CD11c) is expressed by pro-inflammatory macrophages (Zhang et al., 2022).

Interestingly, PFOS inhibited several immune and inflammatory ligand-receptor interactions (Figs. S8 and S9), particularly those involving chemokines and CCR1, as well as HLA-class II and CD4 interactions between KCs. Since the HLA-class II_CD4 interaction is crucial for monocyte differentiation into functional mature macrophages (Zhen et al., 2014), PFOS may exert significant immunosuppressive effects on KCs. These findings suggest that hepatic inflammation induced by PFHxS and PFNA involves both innate and adaptive immune cells, whereas PFOA primarily affects innate immune cells, and PFOS predominantly influences lymphoid cells.

Hepatocytes exposed to PFOA and PFNA upregulated apoptosis-related CCC driven by IGFBP3_TMEM219 interaction, targeting both hepatocytes and immune cells. IGFBP3 (insulin-like growth factor binding protein 3) is primarily secreted by the liver, and we detected increased IGFBP3 levels in the supernatants of PFAS-exposed spheroids, peaking at day 7 of exposure, with PFNA showing a modest increase as early as day 2. The local effects of IGFBP3 secretion remain controversial. While IGFBP3 secretion by macrophages has been suggested to control liver inflammation by inhibiting hepatocytes IL-8 production in cell lines (Min et al., 2016), IGFBP3 secretion by ethanol-activated hepatic stellate cells has been shown to induce lipid droplet formation and cellular triglyceride content in primary mouse hepatocytes. Systemically, IGFBP3 may also play a role in diabetes progression, as the IGFBP3_TMEM219 interaction has been implicated in pancreatic β–cells loss and dysfunction (D’Addio et al., 2022).

Remarkably, PFNA enhanced several CCC interactions associated with fibrosis and tumor development, particularly those related to cell proliferation and adhesion. PFNA increased epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) interaction with tumor growth factor alpha (TGFA) and the amphiregulin (AREG), both of which promote HCC cell proliferation, differentiation and survival (Garcia-Lezana et al., 2021). PFNA also increased cellular interaction with collagen and laminin via integrins α6β1 and α3β1, both of which commonly expressed in HCC cells and critical for their motility on laminin and collagen surfaces (Hao et al., 2022; Giannelli et al., 2001). Additionally, Kupffer cell CCCs involving collagen VI (Shahin et al., 1992) and the TGFB1(Wang et al., 2023) signaling suggests PFNA contributes to fibrosis.

Although our study provides valuable insights into the mechanisms of PFAS-induced liver dysfunction, it has limitations. We tested a single PFAS concentration (20 μM) and one exposure duration (7 days), which may not fully capture the effects of chronic, low-dose exposures relevant to human environmental exposure. Additionally, our assessment of PFAS impact on liver spheroids relied primarily on single-cell transcriptomics, and no technical replicates were included. However, we believe that the use of hepatocytes from 10 individual donors within each spheroid provides a robust inter-individual biological variability, partially mitigating the need for separate biological replicates in this model system. While our 3D liver spheroid model closely mimics key aspects of liver physiology, it represents a single organ system and lacks systemic influences that occur in vivo, such as hormonal regulation and inter-organ interactions. Future studies incorporating lower and prolonged PFAS exposures, along with direct experimental validation in animal models and human cohort, will be critical for further elucidating the mechanisms of PFAS-induced hepatic dysfunction and their broader health implications. Nonetheless, our findings lay a foundation to establish a new approach methodology framework for investigating PFAS-induced hepatotoxicity using in vitro and in silico tools and support the development of targeted strategies to prevent or mitigate PFAS adverse health effects.
