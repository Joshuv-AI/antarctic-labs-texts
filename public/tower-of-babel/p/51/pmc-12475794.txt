Paper 56: Sex Differences in Personalized Strength Recovery Thresholds after ACL Reconstruction
Authors: Ryan Whelan, Devin K Kelly, Andrew M Koessler, Joe M Hart
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives:1) To identify sex, age, and activity level-specific strength recovery thresholds for patients post-ACL reconstruction (ACLR) that correlate with patient acceptable symptom state (PASS) scores on the International Knee Documentation Committee Subjective Evaluation form (IKDC), and 2) to compare thresholds between sexes.Methods:676 males who underwent primary ACLR at a single institution [age: 23.7 ± 9.2 years (10-62 years); post-op: 8.1 ± 11.2 months (2.7-234.8 months)] and 732 females [age: 22.3 ± 10.5 years (12-65 years); post-op: 10 ± 22.2 months (2.6-379.9 months)] were included. Patients were excluded if they had a history of ACLR, lower extremity surgery, concomitant multi-ligament reconstruction, or a condition that would prevent them from completing the protocol. Patients were stratified into eight groups based on sex (M, F), age (≤22, >22 years), and activity level [Tegner Activity Scale (TAS) ≥7, <7] to personalize the outcomes. Patients completed a postoperative musculoskeletal assessment as part of a larger IRB-approved point-of-care study, which involved isokinetic and isometric knee exercises measuring torque generated in extension and flexion at 90 and 180 degrees/second and completed patient-reported outcomes questionnaires, including IKDC, to mark progress through rehabilitation. Peak torque and peak torque normalized to body mass were recorded for the ACLR limb and then compared to strength in the contralateral leg to generate a limb symmetry index (LSI = ACLR limb/contralateral limb * 100%). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated for each test relative to the IKDC PASS score (75.9%) to identify the outcomes that maximized both sensitivity and specificity. Area under the curve (AUC) and standard error for each curve were recorded and the predictive capacity was assessed through the AUC where values of 0.50 to 0.59 were labeled as “no discrimination,” 0.60 to 0.69 as “minimal,” 0.70 to 0.79 as “fair,” 0.80 to 0.89 as “good,” 0.90 to 0.99 as “excellent,” and 1.0 as “perfect” predictability.Results:Sex differences in strength recovery thresholds post-ACLR were present across ages and activity levels; however, the strongest associations were observed in the young, active groups, which are presented in Table 1. In females, significant associations were seen in all 18 strength metrics in the young, active group. In males, the young, active group had 13 significant associations, one less than the older, less active group, which had the most among males. The predictability of the results for young, active patients post-ACLR ranged from non-discriminant to minimal in males and minimal to fair in females. At least 10 significant associations were seen in all groups, except the male young, less active group, which had no significant findings. In young, active patients, all raw peak torque values were higher in males compared to females. Normalized peak torque values were much closer between sexes among young, active patients, although still higher in males. LSI ranged from 65% to 100% in young, active males and 64% to 99% in young, active females.Conclusions:These findings suggest that strength performance is most predictive of IKDC PASS post-ACLR in female patients, especially in those that are more active and younger (≤22 years of age). This may be related to this group having the largest sample size, the expectations of knee function and goals to return to competitive sports, or better surgical outcomes in this population. Interestingly, most significant associations involved tests of knee extension, suggesting that extension ability may be a better predictor of adequate knee function than tests of flexion. Additionally, these results suggest that an LSI of 90% may not be necessary for all patients as some achieved acceptable functionality with LSI as low as 64%. The findings of the present study highlight the importance of personalized approaches, especially based on sex, age and activity levels, when establishing standard benchmarks of recovery for patients after ACLR.
