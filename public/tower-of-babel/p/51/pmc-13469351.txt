Associations between serum uric acid levels and risk of dementia in community‐dwelling older adults
Authors: Md Golam Rabbani, Sheikh M. Alif, Joanne Ryan, Zhen Zhou, Cammie Tran, Amanda J. Rickard, Catherine Robb, Robyn L. Woods, Suzanne G. Orchard, Raj C. Shah, Anne M. Murray, John J. McNeil, Md Nazmul Karim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONEvidence on the association between serum uric acid (SUA) and dementia risk is inconsistent, and prospective data in older adults are limited. This study examined associations of SUA levels and trajectories with incident dementia.METHODSThis study included 11,411 community‐dwelling adults aged ≥70 years without dementia at baseline. Incident dementia was diagnosed using Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders – Fourth Revision (DSM‐IV) criteria. Sex‐stratified Cox proportional hazards models assessed associations of baseline SUA and 3‐year SUA changes with dementia risk.RESULTSOver a median 9‐year follow‐up, 1000 (8.8%) participants developed dementia. Low SUA levels were associated with higher dementia risk among females (age‐adjusted hazard ratio [HR]: 1.31, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.07–1.61; fully adjusted HR: 1.24, 95% CI: 1.01–1.53), but not males. A decline in SUA over time was associated with higher dementia risk among males only (age‐adjusted HR: 1.30, 95% CI: 1.03–1.63; fully adjusted HR: 1.29, 95% CI: 1.02–1.62).DISCUSSIONAssociations between SUA and dementia risk varied by sex and SUA trajectory, suggesting potential sex‐specific biological mechanisms.

BACKGROUND

Serum uric acid (SUA), the end product of purine metabolism, has long been recognized for its role in gout, a condition characterized by hyperuricemia, monosodium urate crystal deposition, and inflammation. 1 Beyond gout, elevated SUA levels have been identified as an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease and chronic kidney disease (CKD). 2 In contrast, the consequences of low SUA levels are less clear, although observational studies have linked low SUA levels to increased risk of Parkinson's disease, 3 and possibly multiple sclerosis. 4 Recent research has focused on the potential role of both low and high SUA levels in neurodegenerative disorders. SUA exhibits both antioxidant and pro‐oxidant properties depending on its concentration and biological environment. Under normal physiological condition SUA may provide antioxidant effect, 5 whereas elevated SUA levels can exert pro‐oxidant effect that increases oxidative stress, thereby promoting chronic inflammation and vascular dysfunction, which are key contributors to the pathogenesis of dementia. 6

Dementia is currently the seventh leading cause of death worldwide and is a major cause of disability among older adults. 7 In 2021, an estimated 57 million people were living with dementia globally, with nearly 10 million new cases each year. 7 Dementia is a multifactorial condition influenced by both neurodegenerative and vascular mechanisms. Cardiovascular and systemic factors such as hypertension, diabetes, inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction have been associated with increased dementia risk. 8 SUA has also been linked to several of these vascular and metabolic factors, suggesting that it may play a role in pathways involved in neurodegeneration and cognitive decline. As SUA is routinely measured in clinical practice and is potentially modifiable through lifestyle changes or medications, a better understanding of its relationship with dementia is needed to inform strategies for prevention and treatment.

Several prospective cohort studies have examined the association between SUA levels and dementia risk in adults, but findings remain mixed with reports of increased risk 9 or U‐shaped association, 10 decreased risk, 11 , 12 or no association. 13 , 14 Considering the fact that SUA levels differ by sex 15 and that sex hormones influence SUA metabolism, 16 sex‐specific analyses have been conducted; however, these have also yielded inconclusive evidence. 10 , 11 , 13 , 14

SUA levels are also more prone to variations due to lifestyle factors, 17 which contribute to overall variability in SUA concentrations. Changes in SUA levels over time have been associated with brain white mater volume as well as decline in cognitive function. 18 However, evidence on whether longitudinal changes in SUA influence the risk of dementia remains limited. Notably, most previous studies have relied on a single baseline SUA measurement, which may be insufficient to capture the effects of long‐term SUA exposure on dementia risk.

Moreover, previous studies were not conducted exclusively among older adults, which may have introduced heterogeneity in their findings, as dementia risk differs substantially across age groups. 19 Given these knowledge gaps, the present study aimed to investigate the associations of baseline SUA levels and longitudinal changes in SUA with the risk of dementia in older adults using sex‐stratified analyses.

METHODS

Study design and population

The study population includes individuals from the ASPirin in Reducing Events in the Elderly (ASPREE) study, a double‐blind, randomized, placebo‐controlled clinical trial investigating the effect of low‐dose aspirin (100 mg daily) in older individuals. 20 A total of 19,114 participants aged ≥70 years from Australia (87.4%) and aged ≥65 years from the United States (12.6%) were recruited between March 2010 and December 2014, and followed until June 2017. At enrolment, participants were community‐dwelling without diagnosed dementia, independence‐limiting physical disability, prior cardiovascular disease (CVD) events, or any chronic illness likely to limit survival less than 5 years. All participants also had to score ≥78/100 for the Modified Mini‐Mental State Examination (3MS), used to ascertain global cognitive status at baseline. 21

The ASPREE trial ended in June 2017 after which remaining participants were invited to consent to continue annual follow‐up in the post‐trial observational phase (ASPREE‐XT) through 2024. 22 For the present analysis, only participants who provided baseline (n = 11,878) and 3‐year follow‐up visit (n = 9724) blood samples to the ASPREE Healthy Ageing Biobank (a subset of willing Australian ASPREE participants) were include. 23 Participants using anti‐gout medications at baseline (allopurinol, colchicine, and probenecid n = 467) were excluded due to the lack of data on pre‐treatment SUA levels. 24

Baseline SUA levels and changes in SUA levels over time

Non‐fasting blood samples were collected at ASPREE clinical trial centers or via mobile laboratories and stored at –80°C. 23 SUA concentrations were analyzed at the Alfred Hospital Pathology Service (Melbourne, Australia), using a uricase/peroxidase colorimetric method (Alinity ci, Abbott Diagnostic, Australia). 25 Results were reported in µmol/L and later converted to mg/dl (dividing by 59.49).

Given the possibility of a non‐linear association between SUA and dementia risk suggested by previous studies, 10 , 11 baseline SUA levels were categorized into sex‐specific quintiles, with the middle quintiles (Q2–Q4) used as the reference group. This reference group align with sex‐specific normal SUA ranges in ASPREE 26 and reflect typical physiological ranges in the general population. 27 Dementia risk was assessed both with lowest (Q1) and highest (Q5) SUA quintiles.

Absolute changes in SUA were calculated as the difference between SUA levels at the 3‐year follow‐up and baseline and categorized into quintiles. These were further grouped as declined (Q1), stable (Q2–Q4), and increased (Q5), with the stable group used as the reference as it represented the central, most normally distributed range of SUA change (Figure S1).

Incident dementia

Dementia assessment and adjudication followed established protocols described previously. 28 Briefly, individuals with suspected dementia (“trigger”) underwent additional evaluation including standardized cognitive and functional assessments. Dementia triggers were defined as a 3MS score < 78, 21 a drop of > 10.15 points from the predicted 5‐year score based on their own baseline 3MS score, and after adjustment for age and education), 29 a report of memory concerns or other cognitive problems to a specialist, or as noted on the participant's medical records, a clinician diagnosis of dementia or prescription of cholinesterase inhibitors (in Australia, where such prescription is necessary). Additional assessments included the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale‐cognitive subscale, 30 color trails, 31 Lurian overlapping figures, 32 and the Alzheimer Disease Cooperative Study Activities of Daily Living Scale 33 were then performed at least 6 weeks after the initial trigger to reduce the possibility of delirium. Other documentation relevant to dementia assessment, including laboratory investigations, brain computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) findings, blood test results, clinical case notes, and clinicians’ initial impressions regarding cognitive change, was also obtained from treating healthcare providers and hospitals.

All available information was reviewed by an independent dementia adjudication committee comprising neurologists, neuropsychologists, and geriatricians with expertise in dementia who were blinded to treatment allocation. Dementia was adjudicated according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders – Fourth Revision (DSM‐IV) criteria (2nd edition). 34 This required evidence of memory impairment plus evidence of at least one of the following: aphasia, apraxia, agnosia, or executive dysfunction. The date of dementia diagnosis was defined as the date of the initial trigger event that subsequently resulted in a confirmed diagnosis by the adjudication committee.

In the ASPREE clinical trial, dementia was subclassified into clinically probable and possible Alzheimer's disease (AD) according to previously published criteria. 28 Among 575 incident dementia cases, 41.4% were classified as probable AD, 57.2% as possible AD, and only 1.2% likely non‐AD dementia. 28 Dementia subclassification was not undertaken during ASPREE‐XT.

Operational definitions of participants’ baseline characteristics

Potential baseline confounders were pre‐specified based on their plausible association with SUA, dementia or both. These included age, education, smoking, alcohol use, body mass index (BMI), family history of dementia, apolipoprotein E (APOE) ‐ε4 carriers (one or more ε4 alleles), diabetes (self‐report, fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dl or treatment for diabetes), hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg or antihypertensive use), frailty, depressive symptoms, and randomized ASPREE treatment (aspirin/placebo). Frailty was defined using the Fried criteria (non‐frail, pre‐frail or frail), 35 and depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10‐item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES D‐10), with score of ≥ 8 out of 30 indicating presence. 36

Statistical analysis

Baseline descriptive participant characteristics, stratified by sex, were summarized using mean (standard deviation [SD]), median (interquartile range [IQR]) or frequency (%), as appropriate. Univariate associations were assessed using t‐tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA).

The associations between baseline SUA levels and risk of dementia were analyzed with Cox proportional hazards regression models, adjusting for baseline covariates. Two models were developed to assess the robustness of the findings: (1) a model adjusted for age, to account for its potential confounding effect, and (2) a fully adjusted model controlling for additional baseline covariates.

Crude cumulative incidence of dementia according to SUA quintiles was plotted. The proportional hazards assumption was checked using Schoenfeld Residuals and was found to be satisfied. Restricted cubic splines with three prespecified knots, placed at 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles of the distribution of SUA levels, were plotted to visualize the potentially nonlinear relationship between SUA levels and risk of dementia. The reference point was set at the sex‐specific median SUA levels (6.27 mg/dl for males and 5.25 mg/dl for females) and the hazard ratios (HR) were adjusted for confounding variables (as Model 2).

Stratified analyses were performed to explore the effect modification by age, education, family history of dementia, APOE‐ε4 carrier status, diabetes, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease (CKD), with prior evidence suggesting these factors may influence the cognitive outcomes. 37

Sensitivity analyses were performed by re‐running the Models 2 after excluding those with CKD (estimated glomerular filtration rate of < 60 mL per minute per 1.73 m2 or a urine albumin: creatinine ratio of 3 mg/mmoL or more) and those who used diuretics (defined by Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical (ATC) classification codes) at baseline as they are known to directly influence the SUA levels rather than acting as confounders. 38 , 39

Associations between changes in SUA levels over time and incident dementia were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards regression models, adjusting for baseline covariates. Sensitivity analyses were performed after excluding participants who developed dementia before the 3‐year follow‐up visit to assess the robustness of the associations.

All analyses were conducted separately for males and females as females generally have a higher dementia risk than males, 40 and there are known biological differences in SUA metabolism by sex, 15 these factors may influence disease risk and progression differently in males and females. Follow‐up time was calculated from randomization to event or censoring for death or loss‐to‐follow‐up. Results are reported as HR with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All p values were from two‐sided analyses, and p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using Stata software, version 17 (StataCorp).

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study participants. A total of 11,411 participants were included, of whom 55.3% were female. At baseline, 467 participants (3.9%) using anti‐gout medications were excluded. The participants selection flow diagram has been published previously. 24 The mean (± SD) age was 74.9 (± 4.2) years for males and 75.1 (± 4.2) years for females. Overall, the median SUA levels were 5.8 mg/dL (IQR: 4.8–6.8). The median SUA levels were 6.3 mg/dL (IQR: 5.6–7.3) in males and 5.3 mg/dL (IQR: 4.4–6.1) in females. SUA levels were normally distributed with in each sex. Among males, common risk factors included smoking (56.4%), alcohol consumption (90.6%), overweight or obesity (77.9%), and hypertension (74.9%). Among females, prevalent risk factors were alcohol consumption (78.7%), overweight or obesity (70.6%), and hypertension (73.3%). Diabetes was present in 11.4% of males and 7.8% of females, while kidney disease was reported in 24.0% and 25.6% respectively. Overall, 22.7% of participants carried the APOE‐ε4 allele (23.4% males; 22.1% females). Over a median (IQR) follow–up of 9.0 years (6.0–11.0), 1000 participants (8.8%) developed dementia (475 males and 525 females). Baseline SUA levels and risk of dementia

The unadjusted cumulative incidence of dementia across SUA quintiles is shown in Figure 1. Among males, those in the highest SUA quintile (Q5) had a lower risk of dementia compared with the middle quintiles (Q2–Q4; reference group) (Figure 1A). Among females, those in the lowest SUA quintile (Q1) had a higher risk of dementia over time compared to middle quintiles (Q2–Q4) (Figure 1B).

The blue, red, and green lines represent the cumulative incidence for the lowest quintile (Q1), middle quintiles (Q2–Q4), and highest quintile (Q5), respectively. Shaded areas indicate 95% confidence intervals (CI). SUA, serum uric acid.

The adjusted estimates of dementia risk are presented in Table 2. Among males, no significant differences were observed for those in either the highest (Q5) or lowest SUA quintile (Q1) compared with the reference group, in both age‐adjusted and fully adjusted models. Among females, the lowest SUA quintile (Q1) had a significantly higher risk of dementia compared with the reference group, both in age‐adjusted (HR: 1.31, 95% CI: 1.07–1.61, p = 0.010) and fully adjusted models (HR: 1.24, 95% CI: 1.01–1.53, p = 0.043) compared with the reference group. No association was observed among females in the highest SUA quintile (Q5) in either the age‐adjusted (HR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.77‐1.23, p = 0.804) or fully adjusted models (HR: 1.06, 95% CI: 0.83‐1.35, p = 0.635).

Changes in SUA levels over time and risk of dementia

The adjusted estimates for the association between changes in SUA levels and dementia risk are presented in Table 3. Among males, a decline in SUA levels over 3 years was significant associated with the higher risk of dementia both in the age‐adjusted (HR: 1.30, 95% CI: 1.03–1.63, p = 0.026) and the fully adjusted models (HR: 1.29, 95% CI: 1.02–1.62, p = 0.030), compared with the stable group. Increased SUA levels were not associated with dementia risk among males. Changes in SUA levels were not associated with dementia risk in females.

After excluding participants with CKD, the association between SAU and dementia risk remained unchanged for both sexes (Table S2). DISCUSSION

In this large prospective cohort study of community‐dwelling older adults, females with low SUA levels had a significantly higher risk of incident dementia, whereas high SUA levels were not associated with dementia risk. No association was observed among males. Additionally, a decline in SUA levels was associated with a higher dementia risk in males, but not in females.

Consistent with our findings for females, a study by Jiang et al., analyzed data from 384,517 participants aged 38‐73 years in the UK Biobank and reported that low SUA levels were associated with a higher dementia risk. 10 However, that study also reported a U‐shaped association with higher dementia risk among males, whereas we did not observe any significant association in males. In contrast, Shimizu et al. in a study of 1685 middle‐aged and older Japanese participants aged 40–69 years, reported no association between plasma uric acid and dementia risk in either sex. 14 Distinctively, our study focused exclusively on older adults without dementia or major cognitive impairment at baseline, whereas the previous studies included substantially younger cohorts in whom dementia incidence is relatively low. 41 In addition, unlike the Japanese cohort, we used SUA instead of plasma uric acid, as uric acid concentrations in plasma are generally lower than those in the serum. 42

A possible explanation for the higher dementia risk associated with low SUA levels lies in the antioxidant properties of SUA. Under normal physiological conditions, SUA helps mitigate oxidative stress by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS), particularly superoxide (O2 −) and nitric oxide (NO), thereby slowing neurodegenerative processes and delaying the onset of cognitive decline. 6 , 43 Consequently, low SUA levels hence likely to reduce the ability to counteract oxidative stress. 5 Nutritional status may also contribute to this relationship. Hypouricemia (SUA < 4 mg/dl), is a biomarker of poor nutritional status, 44 and malnutrition is common among older adults with dementia. 45 However, this mechanism could not be examined in our study due to the lack of dietary data.

As ASPREE was a randomized trial of low‐dose aspirin, all analyses were adjusted for aspirin randomization status. Although low‐dose aspirin may modestly influence uric acid metabolism, 46 previous ASPREE findings showed no significant effect of aspirin on incident dementia, 28 making it unlikely that aspirin materially influenced the observed associations.

Sex‐specific differences in the associations between low SUA levels and dementia risk may reflect physiological and hormonal mechanisms. Females generally have lower SUA due to uricosuric effect of estrogen, 15 although this diminishes after menopause. 16 In our sample, residual estrogen effects are unlikely to explain low SUA as the median age of females was 73.9 years. Instead, females with low SUA may be more vulnerable to oxidative stress related neuronal damage. In contrast, males maintain higher SUA levels across life, and even their lowest quintile (median 4.75 mg/dl) may provide sufficient antioxidant capacity. The non‐overlapping IQRs between sexes highlight how much lower SUA levels were in females, suggesting that the antioxidant threshold required to counteract oxidative stress may not be met. This disparity may explain why low SUA levels were more strongly linked to dementia risk in females.

To date, no previous studies have examined the association between longitudinal changes in SUA levels and dementia risk. Our findings show that a decline in SUA was associated with higher dementia risk among males, possibly reflecting reduced antioxidant capacity with decreasing SUA over time. As SUA is routinely monitored in clinical practice, our results highlight the importance of paying attention to changes in SUA levels and maintaining long‐term stability for brain health.

In our study, the association between SUA levels and dementia risk appeared more pronounced among males with diabetes and APOE‐ε4 carriers. Higher SUA levels are linked to type 2 diabetes 47 and diabetes is a known dementia risk factor. 48 Among males with diabetes, both low and high SUA levels were associated with lower dementia risk. These unexpected patterns may reflect survivor bias, medication use, the relatively small sample size (diabetes subgroup; n = 579), or distinct underlying disease mechanisms compared with individuals without diabetes. Given the limited statistical power of subgroup analysis, further studies are needed to confirm these findings.

High SUA levels appeared to be associated with lower dementia risk among male APOE‐ε4 carriers, although a similar association was not observed in females. To our knowledge, no previous studies have examined the association between SUA, APOE‐ε4 status and incident dementia risk. However, a prospective cohort study by Lee et al., reported that higher SUA levels may interact with APOE‐ε4 to attenuate cognitive decline among females with mild cognitive impairment. 49 APOE‐ε4 is the strongest genetic risk factor for AD, the leading cause of dementia. 49 In addition to AD, APOE‐ε4 has also been associated with other dementia subtypes, including vascular dementia, and Lewy Body disease. 50 Previous ASPREE clinical trial data showed that, among 575 incident dementia cases, 41.4% were classified as probable AD, 57.2% as possible AD, and only 1.2% as likely non‐AD dementia. 28 Furthermore, previous studies in community‐dwelling older adults have shown that individuals with dementia often have multiple coexisting brain pathologies, including AD pathology together with infarcts and/or Lewy body pathology, indicating that mixed dementia pathologies are common among older adults. 51 , 52 Therefore, although our outcome was dementia, the majority of cases were clinically consistent with AD‐related pathology. The lower dementia risk observed among male APOE‐ε4 carriers with high SUA levels warrants further investigation in cohorts with subtype‐specific dementia outcomes.

Diuretics use has been suggested to reduce dementia risk, 53 but it also affects SUA regulation by reducing urate excretion and increasing SUA levels. 54 Previous studies have not examined its impact on the associations between SUA and dementia risk. In our study, excluding diuretics users attenuated the associations between low SUA levels and dementia risk in females, likely because some individuals with metabolically low SUA levels were shifted into the reference group. Their inclusion may have made the reference group appear at lower risk than it truly was, and once excluded, the difference between groups was reduced.

Our findings provide new insights into the role of low SUA levels in dementia risk among females. Strengths of the study include its prospective design, large community‐based cohort of adults over 70 years, and exclusion of participants with CVD events or major cognitive impairment at baseline. This healthier profile may have reduced confounding from severe vascular comorbidity and enabled a clearer assessment of the independent association between SUA and dementia risk. This is important because CVD and dementia, including AD and vascular dementia, share common risk factors and pathophysiological mechanisms such as chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction. Consequently, the exclusion of individuals with CVD may have reduced the influence of these vascular factors on the observed associations. Rigorous protocols ensured systematic data collection, regular cognitive assessments, and robust dementia adjudication improve outcome accuracy. Extensive phenotypic data allowed adjustment for multiple covariates, and excluding anti‐gout medication users minimized confounding from medication‐induced changes in SUA levels, reflecting the natural relationship between SUA and dementia risk in older adults. Finally, examining changes in SUA levels over time allowed us to capture dynamic associations with dementia risk, providing a more comprehensive understanding beyond baseline measurements.

This study has several limitations. First, due to its post hoc, observational design, we cannot establish a causal relationship between low SUA levels and the risk of dementia. Indication bias and residual confounding from unmeasured or unobserved factors may also have influenced the results. Second, most ASPREE participants had SUA levels within the reference range, and inclusion was not based on SUA levels. Therefore, results cannot be generalized to populations with hyperuricemia. In addition, as the cohort comprised healthy older adults without prior CVD, the findings may not be generalizable to populations with greater vascular burden and multimorbidity, where the interplay between uric acid, cardiovascular risk factors, and dementia may differ. Finally, despite including follow‐up SUA data, other factors that could influence SUA levels over time, such as diet, kidney function changes, or medication use, were not fully captured and may have affected the observed associations.

CONCLUSION

Overall, the results provide evidence that older females with low SUA levels had a higher risk of developing dementia, whereas no such association was observed among older males. In addition, a decline in SUA levels over time is also associated with higher risk of dementia among older males but not among females. Monitoring SUA levels, and maintaining them within a normal physiological range, may have relevance for dementia risk assessment in older adults.
