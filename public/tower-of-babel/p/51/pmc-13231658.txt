Radiographic evaluation of degenerative findings in the temporomandibular joint using CBCT: a prevalence-based study
Authors: Mustafa Çağdaş Öçal, Merve Daldal, Sümeyye Coşgun Baybars, Ezgi Katı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDegenerative changes of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) are common radiological findings and may reflect aging, functional adaptation, or pathological remodeling. Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) provides high-resolution visualization of osseous structures and is widely used for detailed evaluation of TMJ bony alterations. The present study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of degenerative changes and anatomical variations of the TMJ using CBCT and to investigate their associations with age, gender, and with each other.Materials and methodsIn this retrospective study, 1636 condyles were analyzed in CBCT images of 818 patients (214 men, 604 women) who obtained TMJ imaging at two university centers between 2022 and 2025. Two experienced radiologists independently evaluated the CBCT images. The parameters assessed included flattening, erosion, osteophyte, sclerosis, subchondral cyst, glenoid fossa thickening, joint space narrowing, bifid condyle, loose joint body, and condyle-fossa position. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 22.0, with a significance level set at p < 0.05.ResultsAt least one degenerative change was detected in 78.6% of the condyles. The most common findings were flattening (31.4%), erosion (28.9%), and osteophyte formation (27.2%). Erosion, osteophyte, subchondral cyst and bifid condyle were significantly more prevalent in females (p < 0.05). The frequency of osteophytes, subchondral cysts, and posterior condyle position increased with age. Osteophyte formation showed significant associations with other degenerative changes (p < 0.001).ConclusionDegenerative changes in the TMJ frequently coexist and are influenced by demographic factors such as age and gender. CBCT is an effective and reliable imaging modality for detailed evaluation of osseous changes in the temporomandibular joint. From a clinical perspective, the presented prevalence data provide a reference framework for radiologists and clinicians when interpreting CBCT findings of the temporomandibular joint. This framework may support more informed reporting and help avoid overdiagnosis when radiological findings are not accompanied by relevant clinical symptoms.

Introduction

The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is a bilateral synovial joint that is highly susceptible to degenerative processes, as it is responsible for important functions such as chewing, speaking, breathing, and swallowing. Functional overload on the TMJ may be caused by factors such as parafunctional behaviors, occlusal disorders, trauma, or the failure to replace teeth that have been lost with age [1]. This condition can often lead to temporomandibular disorders and, over time, degenerative joint disease in the TMJ. This condition, also defined as TMJ osteoarthritis, is characterized by bone changes such as destruction of the articular cartilage, erosion of the condyle, flattening, formation of osteophytes, subchondral sclerosis, and cysts. The identification of these degenerative changes observed in TMJ is of critical importance for the diagnosis of disease-related dysfunctions and effective treatment planning. Despite extensive research, the pathogenesis of temporomandibular joint osteoarthritis remains incompletely understood. However, factors such as occupational strains, repetitive microtraumas, obesity, gender, genetic predisposition, and advanced age are thought to play a significant role in the development of the disease [2]. In the temporomandibular joint, these general osteoarthritic risk factors may exert a distinct impact due to the joint’s unique anatomical structure, functional loading patterns, and fibrocartilaginous articular surfaces.

Furthermore, there are also some rarer anatomical variations and structural changes in the TMJ that are noteworthy. Bifid condyle is defined by the mandibular condyle exhibiting a two-headed structure instead of a single mass, and although it is generally considered a developmental anomaly, acquired forms associated with trauma or degenerative processes have also been reported. Clinically, although most cases are asymptomatic, in some individuals, it can cause limited range of motion or pain that may affect TMJ functions [3]. The glenoid fossa, along with the mandibular condyle, is one of the anatomical structures that compose the TMJ, and cortical thickening or sclerotic increase observed in the roof of the glenoid fossa is generally considered an indication of chronic stress or long-term occlusal loading. These findings are more commonly observed in older individuals or patients with a history of parafunctional habits, and they can be one of the early indicators of degenerative processes [4, 5].

The diagnosis of degenerative joint disease of the TMJ is based on clinical and imaging findings. However, TMJ imaging is challenging because superimpositions originating from the skull base often make it difficult to visualize the bony components in conventional radiographs. Changes such as the morphology of bony joint structures, cortical bone integrity, and subcortical bone resorption/formation can be more accurately assessed with cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) images. CBCT is a relatively low-dose radiation and cost-effective imaging option; it provides high-quality images for diagnosing bony abnormalities in TMJ [6, 7].

The aim of this study is to evaluate the prevalence of degenerative changes (erosion, flattening, osteophyte, subchondral cyst, subchondral sclerosis, loose body, glenoid fossa thickening, and joint space narrowing) observed in the TMJ using CBCT images; additionally, to determine the presence of a bifid condyle and the position of the condyle in the glenoid fossa, and to examine the relationship of these findings with demographic factors.

Materials and methods

This retrospective prevalence study was conducted using CBCT images obtained from two different centers. The research was coordinated by an academic department specializing in Oral and Maxillofacial Radiology, and ethical approval was obtained from the relevant institutional Non-Invasive Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: 136995, Approval Date: 13.01.2022). CBCT images acquired between 2022 and 2025 were retrospectively retrieved from the radiology archives of the participating centers and evaluated solely for radiological analysis.

Study population and selection criteria

In this retrospective study, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) images acquired between 2022 and 2025 at two university centers were screened. The CBCT examinations had been originally obtained for various clinical indications, including implant planning, evaluation of jaw lesions, and orthodontic or surgical purposes, and were not acquired specifically for temporomandibular joint (TMJ) assessment.

Initially, a total of 1,614 CBCT scans were reviewed (896 females and 718 males). As part of the eligibility assessment, CBCT scans with a limited field of view that did not include the temporomandibular joint region, examinations imaging only a single jaw, and scans with artifacts or insufficient image quality precluding reliable TMJ evaluation were excluded.

Following the application of these exclusion criteria, 818 CBCT scans with adequate bilateral visualization of the temporomandibular joints were included in the final analysis. Of these patients, 214 were male (%26.2) and 604 were female (%73.8). This dataset enabled the evaluation of a total of 1,636 condyles.

The inclusion criteria were age ≥ 16 years and sufficient image quality of the temporomandibular joint region to allow reliable radiological assessment. The exclusion criteria were limited field-of-view scans not encompassing the TMJ region, severe motion or metal artifacts, unilateral jaw imaging, and inadequate visualization of the temporomandibular joint structures.

The age of the included patients ranged from 16 to 86 years, with a mean age of 49.78 ± 14.60 years. The age distribution was as follows: 16–30 years (11.7%), 31–45 years (25.8%), 46–60 years (36.9%), and ≥ 61 years (25.6%).

All CBCT images were obtained using the same imaging protocol with the Planmeca ProMax 3D MID CBCT device at both centers (tube voltage: 90 kV, tube current: 10 mA, field of view: 16.0 × 10.2 cm, voxel size: 0.200 mm). Images were acquired in the maximum intercuspation position and reconstructed into axial, sagittal, and coronal sections with a slice thickness of 1 mm. Multiplanar reconstruction was performed, with coronal sections oriented parallel to the long axis of the condyle and sagittal sections obtained perpendicular to this axis.

Eligible CBCT images were independently evaluated for degenerative TMJ findings by two experienced oral and maxillofacial radiologists. To assess intra-observer reliability, 20% of the CBCT images were randomly re-evaluated by each observer one month after the initial assessment. Inter-observer reliability was assessed by comparing the initial evaluations of both observers. In cases of disagreement between the two observers, the images were jointly re-evaluated, and a final decision was reached by consensus. Cohen’s kappa analysis demonstrated high intra-observer (κ = 0.86 and 0.88) and inter-observer agreement (κ = 0.83).

Statistical analysis

The SPSS Software version 22.0 (IBM Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was employed to analyze the data collected during the investigation. Descriptive statistical methods (minimum, maximum, mean, standard deviation, frequency) were applied in the evaluation of the data. In the comparison of qualitative data, the Chi-square test, Fisher’s Exact Chi-square test, Fisher-Freeman-Halton Exact Chi-square test, Continuity (Yates) correction, and McNemar test were used. The Continuity (Yates) correction was applied in 2 × 2 contingency tables when appropriate, particularly in analyses with small expected cell frequencies. In all statistical analyses, a significance level of p < 0.05 was accepted.

Results

A retrospective evaluation of 1636 temporomandibular joint condyles from 818 patients was conducted using CBCT images. The prevalence of degenerative changes was analyzed in detail in relation to age, gender, and other radiological parameters.

In 78.6% of the cases, degenerative findings were observed, while no degenerative findings were seen in 21.4% of the cases (Fig. 2).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to determine the prevalence of various degenerative changes in the TMJ evaluated by CBCT and to compare the obtained findings with the current literature by examining similar parameters in different patient groups, as well as to assess the possible clinical implications of these changes based on age, gender, and radiological associations. It should be emphasized that the presence of at least one radiographic degenerative finding does not equate to clinically significant TMJ pathology, as certain changes may reflect physiological remodeling or age-related adaptation rather than disease.

Condyle flattening is one of the most frequently reported findings among the degenerative changes observed in the TMJ [9]. In a study by Alexiou et al., condylar flattening was defined as the most common pathology in cases of osteoarthritis evaluated with CBCT, with an incidence rate of 74.4% [10]. Similarly, Cömert Kılıç et al. reported this rate at a remarkably high level of 92.3% in individuals diagnosed with TMJ dysfunction [11]. On the other hand, studies involving the general population or asymptomatic individuals report significantly lower rates of condylar flattening. For example, Alkhader et al. reported that only 14.5% of the condyles showed flattening in their study conducted with CBCT [12]. Vasegh et al. detected flattening in 42% of a sample of 200 symptomatic individuals, emphasizing that it is the most common finding after osteophyte [13]. In the systematic review covering 33 studies conducted by Dygas et al., the rate of condylar flattening in the Latvian population was reported to be 39.8%, while in the Brazilian population, it was 52.3% [14]. In our study, flattening of the condylar articular surface was observed in 31.4% of cases, and this finding was the most frequently detected change among all evaluated degenerative parameters. The wide range of reported rates in the literature suggests that condylar flattening can be commonly observed in both symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals; however, the prevalence may vary significantly depending on patient selection criteria, age distribution, and the imaging techniques used. Indeed, in our study, analyses conducted by age groups revealed that the flattening rate significantly increased with age; particularly, in the 61 years and older group, this rate reached 36.1%. In addition, statistically significant associations have been found between condylar flattening and other degenerative findings such as osteophytes, erosions, and sclerosis. This situation suggests that condylar flattening should be evaluated not as an isolated finding but rather within a broader spectrum of osteoarthritic degeneration.

Erosion of the condylar surface is considered an important indicator of advanced degenerative changes in the TMJ, and the prevalence rates reported in the literature vary widely. Especially in patient groups with osteoarthritis, very high rates have been reported, and this finding has been described as the most frequently observed pathological change [15]. In contrast, a study conducted by Cho et al. on young individuals reported an erosion rate of 15.6% [16]. In the retrospective CBCT-based study conducted by Alkurt et al. on symptomatic individuals, the prevalence of erosion was found to be 41.9%; it was noted that this finding was more frequently observed in female individuals and that the frequency of erosion significantly increased in older age groups [17]. In our study, erosion was detected in 28.9% of the 1636 examined condyles. This rate, while lower than the value reported by Alkurt et al., is similar in that it shows a higher prevalence in female individuals and older age groups. On the other hand, in a CBCT-based evaluation conducted by Silva et al. on a sample consisting only of asymptomatic individuals, erosion was detected at a rate of 23.5%. In this study, erosion was observed in 26.4% of women and 20.6% of men [18]. When all these data are evaluated together, it is understood that erosion can be observed with significant frequency not only in symptomatic cases but also in asymptomatic individuals. Erosion usually occurs alongside other degenerative findings; it is rare to see it in isolation. Indeed, in our study, statistically significant associations were found between erosion and findings such as flattening, osteophytes, and sclerosis. Radiologically characterized by irregularity in the cortical contour, surface erosion, and mineral loss, this finding can lead to progressive deterioration of the joint surface, paving the way for the development of symptoms such as pain, crepitus, and restricted mandibular movements at the clinical level [19].

The formation of osteophytes is characterized by the development of new bony protrusions at the edges of the condyles and is considered one of the typical radiographic indicators of osteoarthritis. In the literature, the prevalence of osteophytes has been reported over a wide range. In a study conducted by Wu et al., the prevalence of osteophytes was reported to be 30% [20]. In contrast, in samples consisting of younger individuals or studies focusing on the general population, the rates of osteophytes are observed to be significantly lower. For example, Nah et al. found osteophyte formation in only 8% of individuals diagnosed with TMJ dysfunction [21]. Ağırman et al. reported an osteophyte rate of 36.9% in the osteoarthritis patient group in their study [22]. In our study, osteophytes were detected in 27.2% of the 1636 condyles examined. This variability in the rates reported in different studies is largely due to the symptom profile, age distribution, and study criteria of the patient groups. The frequency of osteophytes showed significant differences among age groups, particularly demonstrating a marked increase in individuals aged 46 and older. However, the formation of osteophytes is significant not only as an isolated pathological finding but also in the context of the meaningful associations it shows with other degenerative changes. In our study, the presence of osteophytes was statistically significantly associated with other degenerative parameters such as flattening, erosion, sclerosis, and subchondral cyst. These data indicate that the osteophyte is not an isolated change in the temporomandibular joint but rather a progressive and multifaceted part of the degenerative process, and therefore it should be considered an important determinant of advanced osteoarthritic changes.

Subchondral sclerosis is considered one of the important components of the osteoarthritic process, and the prevalence rates reported in the literature show a wide variability. In a study conducted by Koç et al., sclerosis was detected in 9.3% of the examined cases [2]. Silva et al. reported this rate as 24.3% in a sample consisting solely of asymptomatic individuals, while in Vasegh et al.‘s study, sclerosis was reported at only 10% [13, 18]. The differences observed in these studies are largely due to the imaging methods used, variations in diagnostic criteria, and the demographic and clinical characteristics of the evaluated patient population. In our study, however, subchondral sclerosis was observed in 16.1% of the general population. This rate, when compared to other studies in the literature, indicates a moderate level of frequency and provides insight into its prevalence in the population. The statistically significant association of sclerosis with other degenerative findings such as osteophytes, flattening, and erosion supports the notion that this finding is part of a multifactorial and progressive osteoarthritic process rather than an isolated pathological change. Additionally, evaluating sclerosis alongside other reactive changes such as glenoid fossa thickening and joint space narrowing may allow for a more comprehensive and accurate determination of the overall degenerative load in the TMJ. This situation demonstrates that sclerosis is a valuable indicator in the staging of the osteoarthritic process from both radiological and clinical perspectives.

Subchondral cyst is another important radiological finding observed in the advanced stages of degenerative disease in TMJ. Subchondral cysts are generally considered one of the rarest detected changes and are often reported in the literature in the range of 1–5% or at negligible levels [13]. In the study by Vasegh et al., which evaluated 400 joints with a diagnosis of TMJ dysfunction, the subchondral cyst was reported as the finding with the lowest prevalence among all parameters, at only 4.1% [13]. Similarly, in an evaluation conducted by Koç et al. on a general patient group, this rate was found to be 2.7% [2]. Talaat et al., in their study, detected subchondral cysts in 6.98% of individuals without TMJ problems and in 6.52% of individuals with TMJ problems [23]. In our study, however, the prevalence of subchondral cysts was notably higher than the rates reported in the current literature. Several interrelated factors may explain the relatively high prevalence of subchondral cysts observed in the present study. From a pathophysiological perspective, subchondral cyst formation is widely considered a manifestation of advanced osteoarthritic remodeling, arising from chronic mechanical overload, microfracture, and increased intraosseous pressure. Accordingly, cystic changes are more likely to occur in joints exhibiting long-standing degenerative adaptation rather than early-stage disease. In the present cohort, a substantial proportion of cases belonged to older age groups, in whom prolonged functional loading and cumulative microtrauma may contribute to a higher likelihood of intraosseous cyst formation. Furthermore, unlike investigations focusing exclusively on symptomatic or clinically defined populations, this archive-based analysis captures degenerative changes across a broad continuum of severity, including advanced and asymptomatic stages. These methodological and biological considerations collectively suggest that the elevated prevalence of subchondral cysts observed in this study reflects enhanced detectability and population-specific characteristics rather than an artificial inflation of pathological findings. Indeed, in the analyses conducted by age groups, the frequency of subchondral cysts in the in the 61 years and older group increased to 17.2%. This finding suggests that the formation of cysts may be related to age-related increases in subchondral remodeling and changes in intraosseous pressure.

Narrowing of the joint space is closely related to the degeneration of the disc structure or articular cartilage and is described as a radiological finding expected in the advanced stages of osteoarthritis [10]. In a study conducted by Koç et al. on the general population, they reported a narrowing of the TMJ space at a rate of 11.7% [2]. In our study, similarly, a narrowing of the joint space by 9.4% was observed. In the literature, it is stated that the narrowing of the joint space increases with age, and this situation is a result of the thinning of the disc structure and degenerative changes on the condylar surface in older age groups [24]. In parallel with this finding, in our study, the rate of narrowing in the joint space was 4.2% in the 16–30 age group, while in individuals aged in the 61 years and older group, this rate increased to 13.2%. The relationship between the narrowing of the joint space and the reactive changes observed at the level of the glenoid fossa is also noteworthy. In our study, glenoid fossa thickening was observed in 15.0% of the joints where narrowing in the joint space was detected. In contrast, in the joints where no narrowing was observed, this rate remained at only 2.8%. This situation suggests that the narrowing of the joint space is not solely a change related to the condyle; it also involves reactive adaptations at the level of the glenoid fossa, and therefore, a degenerative process progressing through bidirectional, reciprocal interaction in the TMJ may be effective.

Glenoid fossa thickening is a radiological finding characterized by an increase in thickness, forming the roof of the TMJ. This change is often considered a reactive adaptation process that develops in response to chronic occlusal loading [25]. In the literature, data on glenoid fossa thickening are limited, and this parameter has not been evaluated in many studies. In our study, a 3.9% thickening of the glenoid fossa was detected, with a tendency for this rate to increase with age, reaching 6.3% in individuals aged while in individuals aged in the 61 years and older group, this rate increased to 13.2%. Additionally, our study found a statistically significant relationship between glenoid fossa thickening and narrowing of the joint space; glenoid fossa thickening accompanied 15% of joints with joint space narrowing, while this rate was only 2.8% in joints without narrowing. This finding indicates that the adaptive remodeling process in the TMJ is not limited to the condylar surfaces but is also actively ongoing at the fossa level, and that glenoid fossa thickening should be considered an important radiological indicator of altered load distribution.

Bifid condyle is a rare anatomical variation that is usually asymptomatic in most cases and is often incidentally detected during routine radiological examinations. The prevalence of bifid condyle reported in the literature varies significantly. In recent studies, Koç et al. reported a prevalence of 1%, and Alkurt et al. reported 1.8% for the presence of bifid condyle [2, 17]. In our study, similar to the literature, bifid condyle was detected at a rate of 1.3%, and was among the rarely observed findings among the evaluated degenerative parameters. Sargurunathan et al. detected a 4.53% incidence of bifid condyle in their study [26]. The high rate reported in Sargurunathan et al.‘s study can be explained by technical factors such as the slice thickness of the CBCT devices used, the imaging plane (sagittal vs. frontal), whether or not multiplanar reconstruction techniques were employed, and the observer’s level of experience. Indeed, Görürgöz et al. highlighted the importance of multiplanar reconstruction in the diagnosis of bifid condyle and emphasized that unidirectional evaluations in the sagittal plane might be insufficient to detect this variation [27]. No statistically significant relationship has been found between the bifid condyle and other degenerative findings such as osteophyte, erosion, and flattening. This situation supports the idea that the bifid condyle may be an anatomical variation related to developmental or previous traumas, rather than a degenerative process.

Loose bodies are a rare pathological condition characterized by the presence of calcified fragments of bone or cartilage, typically detached from the joint surfaces, within the joint cavity. This finding is often associated with advanced degenerative joint diseases or rare syndromes such as synovial chondromatosis [10]. In the literature, data regarding this finding are limited, and the reported prevalence rates are low. Koç et al. reported an intra-articular loose body rate of 2.3%, Alkurt et al. reported 0.6%, and Görürgöz et al. reported 1.56% [2, 17, 27]. In our study, this rate was found to be 1%, which is consistent with the literature. Another significant finding in our study is that in the majority of the condyles where free bodies were detected, other advanced degenerative changes such as osteophytes, erosions, and subchondral cysts were also observed simultaneously. This situation suggests that intra-articular loose bodies mostly develop secondarily and reflect the advanced stages of the degenerative process. The prevalence of loose bodies in the general population is quite low, so their diagnostic value can be considered limited. However, this finding should be carefully evaluated, especially in patients presenting with clinical symptoms such as joint locking, disc displacement, or severe functional impairment. In this respect, despite being rare, loose bodies should be considered a parameter that can yield clinically significant results.

Liu et al., in their CBCT-based study evaluating condyle-fossa positions across different age groups, reported that anterior condylar positioning decreased with advancing age, whereas posterior positioning significantly increased. This shift was attributed to reduced translational capacity and adaptive bone remodeling associated with aging [28]. Similarly, Paknahad et al., observed that the condyle was predominantly located in the posterior position among asymptomatic individuals, interpreting this finding as a potential outcome of subclinical structural changes related to physiological aging [29]. Our findings are largely consistent with these reports. Although the most frequent condylar position was centric (62.0%), comparison among age groups revealed that anterior positioning was more prevalent in younger individuals, while posterior positioning significantly increased with age, showing a statistically significant difference. These results suggest that age-related functional and structural alterations in the temporomandibular joint complex may play an important role in the posterior displacement tendency of the condyle.

The main limitation of this study is its retrospective design and the fact that all evaluations were based exclusively on radiological images. As a result, clinical information such as temporomandibular joint–related diagnoses, pain intensity, functional limitations, systemic disease history, trauma, and parafunctional habits was not available. The absence of these parameters precluded disease-based subgroup analyses and limited the ability to establish direct associations between radiological findings and potential etiological factors. Although all CBCT examinations were acquired using the same imaging devices and standardized protocols, the scans were performed by different technicians. This may have led to minor variations in patient positioning and image quality, which could have influenced the assessment of certain radiological features. In addition, despite the fact that patients were not selected based on temporomandibular joint–related complaints or referral indications, the archive-based nature of the study resulted in a higher proportion of female cases after exclusion of scans with inadequate bilateral condylar visualization. This imbalance may limit the robustness of gender-based comparisons and the generalizability of the findings to the male population. In addition, the proportion of female patients increased after the application of exclusion criteria requiring adequate bilateral visualization of the temporomandibular joints. This shift from the initially screened population may be related to differences in field-of-view protocols used for various clinical indications, gender-based referral patterns for CBCT imaging, or technical and anatomical factors influencing joint visualization within the imaging volume. These factors may have contributed to a higher exclusion rate among male patients and should be considered when interpreting gender-based findings. Furthermore, because the study relied on a retrospective imaging archive, detailed information regarding the original clinical indications for CBCT acquisition was not available. Consequently, the study population may not represent a truly population-based random sample. This aspect should be considered when interpreting the relatively high prevalence of degenerative findings observed in the present study. In addition, the assessment of glenoid fossa thickening in this study was based on an operational threshold, as there is currently no universally accepted cutoff value defining abnormal glenoid fossa roof thickness. Although the selected threshold of ≥ 1.7 mm was chosen to standardize measurements and was supported by previously reported CBCT findings and the distribution of values in the present sample, different threshold values may yield varying prevalence estimates. Therefore, glenoid fossa thickening findings should be interpreted with caution, and future studies are needed to establish standardized reference values. In addition, inter-rater reliability statistics specific to continuous glenoid fossa thickness measurements were not calculated. Reliability assessment was performed only for the dichotomized classification based on the operational threshold (≥ 1.7 mm), which may limit the interpretation of measurement variability in continuous values. Finally, multivariate statistical analyses were not performed; therefore, the potential confounding effects of age and gender could not be simultaneously controlled. Future prospective studies incorporating comprehensive clinical data should employ multivariate logistic regression to simultaneously control for confounding effects of age, gender, and demographic variables, thereby better elucidating the complex relationships associated with temporomandibular joint degenerative changes.

Conclusion

This study has revealed the prevalence of commonly observed degenerative changes in the TMJ using CBCT. Flattening, erosion, and osteophyte formation were identified as the most common findings, showing significant relationships with demographic factors such as age and gender. The findings highlight the multifactorial nature of TMJ degeneration and the diagnostic value of CBCT.
