Co-designing an intergenerational dance programme using the 6SQuID framework: a development and proof-of-concept study
Authors: Siobhán M O’Reilly, Orfhlaith Ni Bhriain, Lehana Thabane, Amanda Clifford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundDance is an artistic and social form of exercise and has been shown to be effective across the lifespan. Intergenerational dance programmes can have beneficial effects in reducing discrimination and fostering communities. Previous intergenerational dance programmes included small sample sizes or were not designed to target physical outcomes. There is a need for well-designed community-based intergenerational dance programmes to target the needs of older adults (OAs) and adolescents addressing physical activity, ageism and loneliness.Aims and objectivesThe aim of this study was to co-design and refine the content of an intergenerational dance programme with OAs and adolescents using the ‘six steps in quality intervention development framework’ (6SQuID). The objectives were to complete steps 1–4 of the 6SQuID framework and run a short pilot study with OAs.DesignA proof-of-concept pilot study.SettingThe programme took place in a local community centre.Participants12 participants were recruited (n=5 adolescents; n=7 OAs). Adolescents were aged between 14 and 16 years. OAs were aged 60 years and older.InterventionsThe intervention was led by a physiotherapist and dance teacher and comprised of intergenerational practice and social dance.Primary and secondary outcome measuresOutcome measures focused on physical activity, well-being, ageism and mobility. Accelerometers were used to establish programme intensity. Focus groups were conducted to explore the opinions of participants. Qualitative data were analysed using Thematic Analysis.ResultsSix OAs and four adolescents completed the programme. The average age of OAs was 72.8±6.69 years and the adolescents were aged 14–16 years. The intensity of the classes was driven by participants, with some achieving vigorous intensity, and most achieving light-moderate intensity. This accounted for an average of 28.36 (±11.02) min of the class. The outcome measures were found to be meaningful for participants; however, more challenging balance measures were suggested by both cohorts. The social dance was enjoyable, especially when the music was tailored to participants’ preferences. Meeting with other age groups was valued, and it was suggested that more time should be given to icebreakers and socialising.ConclusionsIntergenerational dance may be a promising way of improving well-being, intergenerational connections and achieving moderate-intensity activity. The process of designing an evidence-based intervention in this paper can be used to guide researchers and practitioners in designing an intergenerational arts-based programme.Keywords: Adolescent, Community Participation, Aging, Methods

Background

It is estimated that half of the global population have experienced ageism in their lifetime1 and billions of dollars spent in healthcare for the most common medical conditions are a sequela of ageism.2 The negative consequences of ageism include negative self-perceptions of ageing, age-related discrimination, and stereotypes. Thus, age-related stereotypes must be eliminated to promote positive self-belief, optimise health and well-being of older adults, and subsequently reduce healthcare costs. The WHO’s Global report on ageism highlighted the importance of intergenerational practice as one of the most effective methods in reducing prejudice, stereotypes, and ageism and recommended future investment to implement these programmes more widely.1

Intergenerational programmes have been researched for decades3 and are increasing in popularity more recently.4 Intergenerational practice spans several areas from community planning,5 educational,6 activity7 and arts-based programmes.8 Research has found that intergenerational programmes can improve attitudes towards other age groups, self-esteem, and general well-being for both younger and older age groups involved.4

As the population continues to age, the need for programmes that bring together different generations through mutually beneficial activities with the aim of building communities and optimising health becomes increasingly important. There are many shared issues across generations. Two key concerns that require immediate attention are physical inactivity and loneliness. Over 30% of the global population are not sufficiently active,9 which can have significant negative physical and mental effects on older adults and adolescents alike. Older adults who are physically inactive have increased risk of falling, fractures, cognitive decline, depression and functional limitations.10 Physical inactivity in adolescents can lead to/increase mental health issues, cardiometabolic disorders, and obesity.11 Loneliness can also affect these cohorts in similar ways. Roughly one-quarter of older adults in high-income countries have experienced loneliness,12 with Ireland facing the highest levels of loneliness in Europe.13 The estimates of loneliness in adolescents range from 9.2% to 14.4% globally.14 Those experiencing loneliness report worse mental health, well-being (depression, anxiety) and cognitive outcomes.15 Moreover, associations between loneliness and poorer physical health have been highlighted in adolescents, with those affected at increased risk of conditions such as asthma, hypertension, back pain and osteoarthritis.16 Therefore, targeted enjoyable programmes that address physical inactivity and loneliness in older adults and adolescents are needed.

Dance is both an enjoyable form of physical activity (PA) and a creative art form that has historically brought communities together,17 and emerging evidence suggests that intergenerational dance programmes can foster relationships between generations, reduce age-related stereotypes, and, for some older adults, improve their self-reported health and quality of life.18 However, a recent scoping review highlighted a lack of such programmes.18 To date, most studies have focused on qualitative outcomes, well-being or intergenerational measures, with no study targeting or examining PA as an outcome. Non-intergenerational dance programmes that involved older adults or adolescents, respectively, have been shown to benefit certain health outcomes. For community-dwelling older adults, dance can be as, or more, effective than conventional PA programmes in improving mobility, endurance, balance, strength, flexibility and cognition.19 Similarly, dance is a feasible alternative to conventional PA for adolescents,20 eliciting improvements in depressive symptoms, PA and cognitive outcomes.20 21 To explore the physical and psychosocial outcomes of dance for intergenerational groups, it is important to establish a robust, evidence-based programme that can be rolled out on a larger scale and evaluated. An interdisciplinary co-design approach that combines physiotherapy, dance, and intergenerational practice, together with its target population, is important in ensuring the content is tailored, meaningful and purposeful.22 Therefore, the aim of this paper is to describe the development of an intergenerational movement to music programme using an intervention development framework and co-design methods.

Methods

Both qualitative and quantitative methods were used to design the intergenerational dance programme. The development process was guided by the first five steps of the ‘six in quality intervention development’ (6SQuID) framework.23 These five steps involved (1) defining the problem and its causes, (2) clarifying changeable contextual or casual factors, (3) identifying the change mechanism, (4) identifying how to deliver the change mechanism and (5) testing and refining the intervention on a small scale. The issues, contextual factors, and behaviour change mechanism were largely identified prior to the pilot study. Therefore, this paper focuses on the design of the dance programme content to elicit changes in the factors identified. Furthermore, this study identifies how the active ingredients should be delivered in the context of this intergenerational dance programme. An initial plan was created for the programme through steps 1–4. Following the pilot study, the research team reflected on the findings to refine and adapt the programme content for another iteration to be rolled out in step 6. Public and patient involvement

A public and patient involvement (PPI) group comprising dance teachers, dance movement therapists, older adults who are grandparents, academics and physiotherapists was established to inform the design of the programme and assist with recruitment procedures. This panel was a separate entity from the participant group which trialled the initial programme. PPI is important to enable the researchers to work with members of the public to receive feedback and suggestions throughout all stages of the research project.24

The PPI panel was involved in the programme development, recruitment strategies and outcome measure choice. Throughout the project, the PPI members attended progress meetings, were updated on results, and liaised with their local communities regarding the project. Monthly meetings adopted a hybrid approach, with an option to join in person or online depending on individuals’ availability. The meetings began in October 2023, a year prior to the start of the pilot.

6SQuID Steps 1–4

In advance of the pilot study phase, steps 1–4 were undertaken to design an interaction of the programme to be tested in step 5. Literature reviews were explored to form the background of this study and identify gaps in the literature, and the research team held meetings to determine the changeable factors that would be targeted within the programme. Key theories were identified through previous programmes and the wider literature. The lead author (SMO’R) completed training on intergenerational practice where common theories used in practice were explained.

The two theories identified as most appropriate to implement in this context were the Social Cognitive Theory (SCT)25 and the Intergroup Contact Theory.26 27 SCT comprises three components to elicit behaviour change that have a key influence on human behaviour. They are personal factors (knowledge, expectations, attitudes), behavioural factors (practice, self-efficacy, skills) and environmental factors (social norms, access in community). This model has been applied to promote PA programmes in older adults28 and predict PA behaviours in adolescents.29

The Intergroup Contact theory was identified as the most frequently used theory within intergenerational programme development and therefore was integrated in this programme.30 This theory has been used to improve intergroup contact across cohorts of different ages, races and religious groups. There are four key tenets that comprise this theory. These are common goals, intergroup cooperation, equal group status and support of governing bodies or authorities. With this theory, it is important for the group to work towards a shared goal, however the priorities can differ among participants. In the context of this programme, the shared goal was participating in a movement and music programme with intergenerational groups. For the older adult participants, PA and balance were key priorities, while for the adolescents, having fun and meeting others was the priority.

6SQuID Step 5: Proof-of-concept pilot study with integrated co-design

In line with step 5 of the 6SQuID, a proof-of-concept and co-design study was conducted to co-design the programme content and assess the acceptability/appropriateness of the outcome measures and programme material.

Participants

A pragmatic sample size of n=12 was targeted to account for an estimated dropout of n=4. Sample size was based on the aim of the study, and for safety and enjoyment reasons. The sample was kept small enough to be monitored by the physiotherapist and allow participants to give feedback on the content but still have enough participants to maintain a community group dynamic within the classes. Recruitment was carried out from September-November 2024. Community-dwelling older adults aged 60+ years and adolescents aged 14–16 years were considered for inclusion. Eligible participants were capable of mobilising three metres independently, with or without a mobility aid, were able to follow instructions, and had a basic level of English or attended with someone who could translate. Participants were not considered eligible to participate if they had an acute medical condition, which may have included acute stroke, unstable neurological conditions, unstable cardiac/respiratory conditions or on oxygen therapy.

A purposive sampling and snowball sampling technique was used31 to recruit older adults and adolescents. The research team contacted gatekeepers in schools, local older adult community groups, homework clubs and dance and theatre groups. Individuals who expressed interest in participation were contacted by a member of the research team and screened to ensure they met the eligibility criteria. Participants were screened using the Physical Activity Readiness Questionnaire.32 Older adult participants provided informed written consent. For adolescent participants, parents/guardians provided written informed consent, and the adolescents provided informed assent.

The key aspects of the proof-of-concept itself are described using the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) framework. The aim of step 5 was to trial this iteration with a target group and augment and refine for the final iteration of the programme to be rolled out at a future date.

The intergenerational icebreakers were chosen weekly from the Intergenerational Activities Sourcebook.33 The activities included ‘This or That’ where participants were giving two options, that is, ‘singing or dancing?’ and asked to move to the side of the room that reflected their response. There was then an open discussion between sides about why each answer was chosen. ‘Human Bingo’ was used to get participants familiar with each other. Each participant filled out prompts such as ‘favourite colour’ and then had to find another participant who had the same response.

The social dance was led by an experienced dance teacher. The music was guided by participants’ preferences. During the baseline assessments, each participant was asked what music they like to dance to and what genre of dance they most prefer. A playlist was created for the group using the suggested music and was incorporated into the warm-up, cool down and mirroring activities. ‘Dancing in the Mirror’ from the Intergenerational Activities Sourcebook was used to get participants used to moving. This involved pairs repeating the movements of each other. The pairs consisted of an older adult with an adolescent, or facilitator where necessary. Participants were encouraged to use different amplitudes of movements, move at different heights, and to move outside the centre of balance. The next section involved learning a short dance phrase and moving in a circle. This involved changing directions, standing on one leg and reaching with arms. Following this, participants paired up again and were taught a traditional Irish dance, which challenged both balance and endurance.

The programme was held once a week for 4 weeks, with each session lasting 1 hour. The sessions were held in the afternoon, 30 min after school ended.

Feasibility measures were recruitment rate, retention rate, session attendance, and acceptability. The drop-out rate was also measured. These were recorded each week using an Excel spreadsheet.

Co-design

To ensure that the programme was tailored to the needs and wants of the target population, co-design was a key element of the programme. The original plan for the study was to include participatory learning and action methods, such as flexible brainstorming, to enhance the co-design process.46 Due to time constraints, an adapted method was used to obtain feedback from participants.

Three large sheets of paper were available each week, with three questions regarding preferred PA, what were participants’ goals from taking part, and how can the facilitators ensure that everyone feels welcome and respected. Time was given at the end of each session to allow participants to write or draw their responses using colourful markers. The same posters were laid out each week so participants could amend or add to their previous responses. These were reviewed following each session by the facilitators and the plan for the next session was adapted accordingly. The responses were analysed using content analysis47 48 to identify what was most important across the group which influenced the next iteration of the programme.

Focus groups

Semistructured focus groups with the older adults and adolescents were held following the programme and analysed using reflexive thematic analysis49 to provide valuable insight on the programme acceptability and relevance for participants to inform the sixth and final step of the 6SQuID framework. Participants were asked about their opinions of the content and what they would suggest changing for the larger-scale feasibility trial.

Data analysis

Focus groups were analysed using Braun and Clarke’s50 reflexive thematic analysis. This involved six steps including familiarisation, initial coding, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining themes and write-up. The transcript was coded independently by SOR using NVivo software (London, UK). A peer debriefing session was held with other members of the research team where codes and possible themes were discussed. The findings from these focus groups were used to iteratively review and establish the modifiable factors and theory of change.

The quantitative results from the baseline and postintervention assessments were analysed using ActiLife software V.6.13.4 (ActiGraph) and Microsoft Excel respectively. The categorical demographic data was reported as frequencies and percentages, while the continuous data were described using means and SDs. As the purpose of the outcome measures in this context was to test the meaningfulness and purpose to participants, inferential statistics were not appropriate to report. Any open-ended questions in the questionnaires were analysed using content analysis.

Co-design

Due to time constraints, the participatory methods were adapted; however, the poster sheets were used frequently by participants following each dance session. The key findings from the poster sheets, which can be found in table 7, were that it was important to participants to be treated with respect, having an inclusive environment would help them feel valued, many joined for physical benefits with some joining for the social aspect, and dancing was a popular way for this group of participants to be physically active. The programme was iteratively adapted based on the responses. The dance movements were adapted to incorporate the group’s interests and hobbies—for example, one warm-up exercise involved passing an imaginary basketball, as several of the adolescents had recently played each other in a basketball game. Education was provided on certain exercises and how they can benefit physical health, and the icebreaker activities were adapted to ensure everyone was included. The participants unanimously requested a home exercise booklet. While it was not possible to provide this during the short timeframe, a programme was subsequently created using the ideas provided by the focus group. As the weeks progressed, participants were paired up and encouraged to create their own dance moves to share with the class based on what was taught in previous weeks. The pairs each shared their movements, which resulted in a sequence of movements created by the group.

FIndings from focus group

The duration of the focus groups ranged from 9 min to 32 min.

Two main themes were identified from the focus groups. There were many similarities between the older adult and adolescent focus groups; however, less data were collected from the adolescents. The themes were Interest in Programme and Intergenerational Interaction, which explored participants’ reasons for participating and their experiences of the intergenerational interaction, and Timing, Duration, and Content, which explored participants’ opinions on the scheduling of the programme and the activities incorporated into the classes.

Interest in programme and intergenerational interaction

Both the older adults and adolescents found the programme enjoyable. Many of the older adults found the concept of taking part in a research project appealing, and this was their motivation.

I would be very enthusiastic about these kinds of projects or surveys. I suppose having limited medical background I would be really interested—I felt it would only enhance my well-being. It would have to be a positive experience. I felt, moreover, coming from the calibre of people like yourselves. (Older adult 4)

While one adolescent felt similar, the remainder participated either out of enjoyment or because their grandparent was involved.

All participants enjoyed the intergenerational aspect of the programme. None had previous experience with intergenerational programmes, however, one adolescent commented that her dance classes have involved people of different ages. The older adults commented on their appreciation of participating with the adolescents, “Every one of them were so, so lovely” (Older adult 3). They spoke about the understanding that formed between the age groups, through the medium of dance. One older participant felt apprehensive to dance as she had previously injured herself while dancing.

I was dancing with and like out of the blue, she (Adolescent) just says “I won’t leave you go now!”, we were going on underneath. I thought that was lovely when they’d say that, because in fact I broke my wrist dancing, and I hadn't said it to [her]… Very thoughtful. (Older adult 1)

The older adults felt that their age did not impact their ability to participate or form connections with others and enjoyed also seeing the adolescents become friends with each other.

And I loved the interaction with the kids, the way they became friends, and we were waiting to take them home. I mean, they got on so well. (Older adult 2, who participated with their grandchild).

I feel at times I should be applying for a job so (laughs). We were joking about that, but I think it’s so important to [be] young at heart. And forget about the chronological age. (Older adult 3)

Most participants, bar two adolescents, had participated in dance classes prior and enjoyed the idea of a programme incorporating dance.

I was involved in dance in [local dance organisation] as well, but it was just for the over 50s…it was absolutely marvellous; you came out of there always with your big smile. We always danced across the carpark back to the cars. Literally! (Older adult 1)

TIming, duration and content

Adolescents who were participating felt that holding the sessions later in the evening would be preferable as there was not much time to travel after the school day: “Time management because like getting out of school on time and being here.” (Adolescent 1)

The group enjoyed the fun element of the dance, but some felt the dance aspect of the programme should have been longer. “Maybe make it a tiny bit longer, don’t just do it for the hour because by the time you get in and do like the warmup and the cool down, you’ve probably only done 10 minutes of stuff.” (Adolescent 3). Others would have appreciated the content to be more challenging, specifically to have more difficult balance exercises included. The older adults felt that balance was an important factor to consider when running a programme that incorporated older ages. The adolescents felt similar in that they could be challenged more during the assessments.

Generally, the outcome measures were found to be meaningful to participants. Some adolescents, however, felt that they were tedious to complete and would have preferred to be challenged further. No home exercise programme was provided to participants; however, when asked about this during the focus groups, all participants were keen to have one. They stated that having balance exercises or movements from the dances in a booklet to do at home would be appreciated: “Yeah, see it as like, oh, we did this in class, work on this movement.” (Adolescent 3); “They’re all telling us to do [balance exercises at home]. It’s so important… We’re all aware anyway.” (Older adult 3)

Yeah, definitely. And I mean the cognitive ones, it’s always good to do things like that.

Finally, the adolescents felt that Transition Year of secondary school would be the most suitable time to partake in such a programme. Moreover, it would be beneficial to link it into the school curriculum, rather than as an after-school activity.

In general, all participants felt that they would recommend the programme and would participate in it again.

Scondary outcome measures

The descriptive results from the outcome measures can be found in online supplemental appendix 1. The Timed-up-and-go was found to be too easy for the older adult participants, with all completing the measure in under eight seconds at baseline. All older adult participants had full, valid wear time preintervention and postintervention. The adolescent participants had an average valid wear time of 5 days preintervention and 4 days postintervention.

Discussion

The development of this intergenerational dance programme was designed in line with steps 1–5 of the 6SQuID framework. These steps were informed by the literature, working with the PPI panel and running a proof-of-concept pilot study. This resulted in defining physical inactivity, loneliness and ageism as the three key changeable issues to be targeted. Both SCT and Intergroup Contact Theory were chosen as the behaviour change theories based on the wider literature of PA and intergenerational programmes. A previous scoping review conducted by members of the research team highlighted the lack of intergenerational dance programmes that have been conducted, and the inconsistencies that exist between them. Additionally, no programme has specifically objectively targeted PA. The pilot study ran for 4 weeks, with 12 participants and a focus group following this to receive feedback on the programme content. The programme was largely enjoyable; however, factors such as length, timing and difficulty must be considered. All participants enjoyed the intergenerational aspect. Due to difficulties with recruitment, it was decided that linking with local schools is important to ensure the programme’s longevity is feasible. Finally, participants felt that having a home exercise programme would be beneficial to encourage activity outside of the programme sessions and to improve balance. A scoping review that mapped the available evidence for intergenerational dance programmes showed that the current evidence base is limited.18 Methods and populations have varied significantly between existing studies. The qualitative experiences of participants in the current study mirror many of the findings from the other qualitative studies. In general, participants across intergenerational dance programmes can feel initially nervous, but enjoy the connection between generations and getting to know others through the medium of dance. While co-creation of dances has been described,852,54 the studies did not take an involved participatory approach to designing an intervention similar to what is described in this paper. Additionally, objective or subjective PA levels have not been assessed in such programmes. This study tracked both using questionnaires and accelerometers. Quantitative data are lacking in this area; however, by assessing the acceptability and feasibility of using a variety of outcome measures, it is hoped that future researchers may be able to gather more data.

The use of the 6SQuID framework provided an iterative, yet systematic approach to developing an intergenerational movement to music programme that targets health and well-being. The initial two steps enabled the research team to identify relevant and meaningful outcomes to target, that would benefit from mechanisms of change. These mechanisms were then established using the subsequent two steps and consequently tested and refined in the fifth step. Despite being a relatively new framework compared with other frameworks,23 it has been used in developing several programmes, many of which were specifically designed for child, adolescent or young adult populations.55,58 It has also been used for older adult populations, such as for programmes targeted towards people with dementia.59 The framework aims to test the hypotheses of the researchers through testing and refining with the target population, which should result in a theory of change and an established programme.59 The original plan for the programme was informed by an arts-based programme for community-dwelling older adults,60 which was adapted as the weeks progressed. The first week of this pilot highlighted that the older adults and adolescents mirrored each other’s energy, and consequently the pace of the class had to be increased. Therefore, it is important for such programmes to be facilitated by trained professionals who can adapt to the needs and abilities of both the group, and individuals in the class to encourage both participation and behaviour change.61 62

The 6SQuID promotes use of coproduction throughout all stages. The PPI panel associated with this project was involved in all steps. The participants involved in the pilot were encouraged to voice and write what aspects of the programme were enjoyable and what should be changed or amended, ensuring everyone felt involved and autonomous over the content. Coproduction has many benefits, including addressing inequalities, recognising experiential knowledge, enhancing recruitment, and delivering meaningful and impactful research programmes.63 The democratic nature of coproduction and the goal of equality in intergenerational practice worked well in the context of this programme. The opportunity to write feedback using markers and posters at the end of the classes was a good way to bring participants together to discuss their thoughts. Participatory learning and action (PLA) methods, such as flexible brainstorming or card sort, would be useful in an intervention development such as this. This was part of the original plan to happen alongside the baseline assessments; however, due to time constraints, it was not possible to conduct fully. The visual and verbal nature of such techniques positions them as a powerful tool for engaging PPI and participants in the process of developing such a programme.64

Recruitment of adolescents for the pilot study was more difficult than anticipated. Initially, recruitment was through gatekeepers in schools in the local area; however, this was largely unsuccessful. Many schools had the year’s activities planned prior to recruitment starting; therefore, it was not feasible to host this additional programme. Researchers/practitioners should prioritise future planning when rolling out such programmes. By providing advance notice of 8 months to a year, it will allow the schools to integrate their programme into their yearly scheduling. Transition Year may be a more feasible time to run a programme such as this as there is less exam pressure on students and they are encouraged to try new activities. By posing it as an optional activity to join in the Transition Year programme, it will help to improve recruitment rates, while keeping the authenticity of voluntary participation. Having videos showing what the sessions look like, or providing short taster sessions to schools, may encourage more schools to become involved.

Difficulties recruiting adolescents have been reported in previous studies that included a variety of programmes and methods mental health interventions,65 those with physical differences,66 longitudinal health studies,67 and PA programmes.68 A study from 2011 found that there are key factors to consider when recruiting adolescent girls to after-school dance programmes,69 including modern music, ensuring the programme is co-designed, involving friend groups, providing a taster session and goal setting. Regarding recruitment to intergenerational studies, the research is limited. One study that explored the barriers and facilitators of older adults’ participation in intergenerational programmes identified several influential factors which included a lack of time, interest in exercise, fear of fatigue, and lack of knowledge of intergenerational programmes.70 In contrast, the need for activity, benefits of exercise, improving interpersonal relationships and a convenient and accessible location were identified as facilitators.70 While more work is needed to determine the barriers and facilitators to adolescents’ participation, future research might explore school-based delivery integrated into curriculum to enhance sustainability. No older males were recruited to the study, mirroring what has been found in previous reviews on intergenerational dance studies,18 and other community-based dance programmes with older adults.71 Men generally have lower participation levels in community programmes compared with women.72 Knowing that other men are participating is an important factor in encouraging this cohort to get involved. Future studies may look at pairing schools with organisations, such as Men’s Sheds, to increase the number of males getting involved.

The three targeted outcomes of PA, socialisation and ageism were found to be relevant and meaningful by the pilot study participants, and the PPI panel. Sessions contained an average of 28.4 min of light-to-moderate activity, with some participants achieving moderate-vigorous PA (MVPA). While the accelerometers showed that 29.13 min of class time was active and the remaining time was inactive, as participants were standing for the duration of the sessions, it cannot be classed as true sedentary time. The ‘Dancing in the Mirror’ activity involved more arm movements for many participants, with little trunk movement. To progress this activity, participants can be encouraged to do full-body movements to increase the active time in the class. This pilot ran for 4 weeks; in order to elicit physical responses, a minimum of 8 weeks is required.73 Therefore, the future rollout of this programme will be 10 weeks to allow for physical improvements to occur, and for greater community formation between participants. The preintervention and postintervention accelerometer data showed a decrease in steps and MVPA. It is important to consider the time of year when viewing these results. The postintervention data was collected in December, when the daylight was reduced and the weather was cold and wet. In general, people complete less MVPA and accumulate more sedentary time in these months.74 The adolescents involved had exams in December, so they were spending more time studying in preparation. It is not possible to infer from a 4-week pilot whether the programme can impact PA levels and should be assessed over a longer duration.

The participants found dance to be a fun form of PA, so it is hoped that this will encourage individuals to include more activity in their week that is enjoyable. For adolescents, it is important that they are offered a variety of activities that are fun and enjoyable.75 Similarly, enjoyment of fun activities is a motivating factor for older adults to be physically active.76 The focus on dance as a mutually beneficial and purposeful activity was a key aspect of the intergenerational practice to bring the two groups together and improve understanding of each other.77 It is planned that when this programme is rolled out via a longer feasibility trial, this will help to break down age-related stereotypes and help to build supportive, intergenerational communities.

Intergroup contact theory26 27 worked well in the context of an intergenerational dance programme. Equal group status, intergroup cooperation and common goals were established throughout the programme. The co-creation of dance movements allowed for more connection between generations, establishing both equal group status and intergroup cooperation. While overarching government bodies were not explicitly involved, the intervention development was supported by governmental funding. Future programmes would benefit from explicit government support of intergenerational contact and programming. Increased awareness among the general population of the benefits of intergenerational contact from government support would help with recruitment. While SCT25 was successful regarding observational learning, reinforcements and behavioural capabilities, however, self-efficacy was not tracked. The use of validated self-efficacy scales, such as the Self-Efficacy for Exercise scale78 in future trials would be helpful.

Strengths

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first intergenerational movement to music, or dance, programme to be designed with a complex intervention development framework, such as the 6SQuID. The 8-month process ensured that all aspects of the process were thoughtful and intentional, and designed with the target populations’ input. The coproduction of the programme, and continued intention of co-design, means that the programme is tailored and specific to the intergenerational group of older adults and adolescents in Ireland.

Arts-based programmes are beneficial across the lifespan. The interdisciplinary nature of the research team, which included dancers and dance practitioners, healthcare professionals, and an intergenerational practitioner, meant that each element of the programme was chosen through best practice, using the literature, previous experience, and the values of the participants. This sets out a plan for future researchers and practitioners who are interested in running an intergenerational dance programme.

Limitations

The brief nature of the adolescent focus group (9 min) poses issues regarding interpretation and representation within the wider context. Future studies should explore alternative ways of conducting focus groups, such as with more probing or mixed age groups. Due to the small sample size and location, it is not yet possible to generalise to wider intergenerational groups. It is hoped that a future larger feasibility randomised trial will examine feasibility and acceptability of the proposed programme and identify preliminary evidence of effect in a larger number of participants.

Implications for future practice and research

It is important for practitioners to consider building institutional partnerships with schools or community groups for recruitment purposes. Interdisciplinary facilitation (dance teachers, physiotherapists, etc) can support artistic quality while maintaining safety for participants. Within an intergenerational dance programme, it is possible for individualised intensity to be achieved by pairing similar adolescents and older adults.

For researchers planning to pilot similar programmes, a duration of 8 weeks or more should be implemented to allow sufficient time for changes to outcomes of interest. This may allow researchers to examine preliminary evidence of effect prior to running a large-scale randomised control trial. Fidelity outcomes should be assessed from the outset and optimal delivery settings should be evaluated.

Conclusions

To establish an evidence-based co-designed intergenerational movement to music programme, the 6SQuID framework was used. A preliminary programme was trialled through a short proof-of-concept pilot study to receive feedback from participants on how to adapt the programme for a future feasibility study. The established programme incorporated theories and mechanisms from both health sciences and intergenerational practice. Participants enjoyed the programme, with participants appreciating the opportunity to participate in an intergenerational programme. Many felt that more difficult physical outcome measures and a home exercise programme would make the programme more beneficial. The feedback was used to create a refined, longer iteration of the programme. This established programme will be run as a larger feasibility study to explore the possible evidence of effect in larger sample sizes. The process followed in this may be of use for researchers and practitioners hoping to run or design arts-based intergenerational programmes for older adults and adolescents.
