Assemblage structure of ichthyoplankton communities in the southern tyrrhenian sea (western mediterranean sea)
Authors: Antonia Granata, Roberta Minutoli, Letterio Guglielmo, Daniela Giordano, Adriana Profeta, Davide Di Paola, Angelo Bonanno, Simona Genovese, Gualtiero Basilone, Ylenia Guglielmo, Salvatore Aronica
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe spatial distribution and abundance of fish larvae are useful indicators to better understand the reproductive strategies of the species and to identify the possible spawning areas. This study defines the major composition patterns and the community structure of the ichthyoplankton assemblage in the Southern Tyrrhenian Sea and analyse the influence of environmental factors that control these patterns. Samples were taken along a wide coastal area of Sicily extending for 2300 km2 during early spring 2007, in 24 stations across 11 shelf-slope transects with a Bongo net. The larval fish community consisted of 2073 individuals, further to 2750 fish eggs; 33 species belonging to 17 families were identified. Myctophum punctatum was the most abundant species followed by Cyclothone braueri, Lampanyctus pusillus, Lampanyctus crocodilus, Hygophum hygomii and Notoscopelus bolini. The family most represented was Myctophidae followed by Gonostomatidae, Paralepididae, Bothidae, Stomiidae, Callyonimidae and Gobiidae. Ichthyoplanktonic community abundance and composition in the Southern Tyrrhenian Sea were strongly correlated with the environmental factors, like bottom depth, salinity, temperature and fluorescence. Two different areas were highlighted as oceanographic constrains, grouping together the westernmost and the easternmost stations, that resulted influenced by different water masses. Different species assemblages between the two areas were also identified.Keywords: Ichthyoplankton, community structure, spatial distribution, hydrology, Southern Tyrrhenian SeaSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Ocean sciences, Zoology

Introduction

It is now widely recognized that the study of ichthyoplankton ecology in the marine ecosystem can provide us with important data on the life cycle of adults and, consequently, for a rational use of fishing resources1. As for zooplankton, fish larvae have poor swimming ability which forces them to drift with the currents and tides2. The spatial distribution and abundance of fish eggs and larvae are useful indicators to better understand the reproductive strategies of the species and, above all, to identify the possible spawning areas3. However, all this information requires an in-depth knowledge of the biotic and abiotic factors that control them, such as the characteristics and topography of the seabed, water masse structure, currents, upwelling areas and thermal fronts location4–18. Indeed, these advection processes, together with temperature, salinity, and chlorophyll a, stimulate retention mechanisms, controlling egg deposition, development, and larval concentration19–22, which in turn influence the number of predators and therefore the survival of the eggs and larvae themselves23–26.

It is also known that neritic-coastal areas boast a greater biodiversity and richness of zooplankton and ichthyoplankton, as a consequence of a higher primary production27. Therefore, the interaction between these oceanographic and biological processes, control the spatial distribution patterns of fish larvae in conjunction with seasonal and climatic variations28–31, to which these lower trophic level larval organisms should be more sensitive, but could respond more rapidly than the higher trophic levels32.

The study area is an integral part of the Aeolian Islands Archipelago (South Eastern Tyrrhenian Sea), a large pelagic ecosystem considered an important fishing, reproduction and spawning area for many commercial fish species33–42. Previous studies have shown that this area is a suitable habitat for mesopelagic fish and squid, which play a key role in the energy flow of the deeper ecosystem43–45. So, another important resource of this basin concerns the local fishing of European flying squid (Todarodes sagittatus) whose diet, particularly in the juvenile stages, consists basically of mesopelagic fish43, whose larval stages are very abundant in the Southern Tyrrhenian Sea and Strait of Sicily ichthyoplankton46–48. The Aeolian Islands Archipelago is considered a typical oligotrophic area with high biodiversity49–58. The basin is outside the influence of upwelling, the thermocline is deeper and primary production and mesozooplankton biomass are moderate59–62. And it is precisely because of these environmental and ecological characteristics, together with field observations, that this area is a suitable site for reproduction, spawning and early larval development but, probably not for post larval growth and development. This last consideration could be supported by a recent study on the life cycle of Pelagia noctiluca63, in which a strong connection between the Aeolian Islands Basin and the eastern coast of Sicily was highlighted, such as the transport of early larval stages from pelagic waters to the Sicilian coast, caused by specific hydrodynamic conditions. The connectivity between these two ecosystems, especially in the first half of the year, supports the hypothesis that the Aeolian Islands Archipelago and the northern Sicilian coasts are two connected systems.

Unlike the western and eastern Mediterranean basins where the scientific literature on the distribution of fish larvae is quite satisfactory (e.g. [NW64–72,28,9,73–80,30,81–83, the central Mediterranean area, where our study area falls, studies on this topic are still limited3,45,14,84,48 and/or in progress.

The oceanographic and biological assumptions have led the Italian Government to finance and carried out a two-year multidisciplinary National Project (1994-95) so called “Marine environmental characterization of the Aeolian system and the surrounding basins of Cefalù and Gioia” (EOCUMM85,86, coordinated by the National Interuniversity Consortium for Marine Sciences (CoNISMa). Despite a good knowledge of the adult stages of these fish species, little information is available on the ichthyoplankton community and its response to environmental variables. The aim of this study was to define the major composition patterns and the community structure of the ichthyoplankton assemblage in the region and to assess the influence of environmental factors that control these patterns. Furthermore, considering the age of the data (samples collected in 2007), the obtained dataset can represent an opportunity for temporal comparisons on potential changes in distribution, biodiversity and ecology of ichthyoplankton community, linked to climate changes that involved ​​the lower Tyrrhenian Sea.

Materials and methods

Study Area

The surface circulation of the Southern Tyrrhenian Sea is characterized by the less salty Atlantic Water (AW) that, through the Strait of Gibraltar, flows from west to east, occupying the upper layer about 200 m thick. In its path, the AW enters the area near the northern Sicilian coast, where it forms a large cyclonic vortex87–89. More recent data90 have highlighted that the AW, during the winter and spring seasons, forms large meanders around several anticyclonic cells. One of these cells affects the Sicilian coast which, through a local sub-cell91,92 drags the AW out of the large main vortex to enter the Aeolian Islands Archipelago basin from the northwest and flow south-eastward towards the coasts of Sicily and the Strait of Messina. As the summer season approaches, the anticyclonic cells of the coastal recirculation seem to move westward, towards the coast. The AW flowing, also, through the Strait of Sicily turns right and enters the Tyrrhenian basin, both due to the topographic trend of the seabed and the Coriolis effect, flowing along the Sicilian continental slope. Thus, the thermohaline vertical structure shows the presence of three different water masses: Tyrrhenian surface water (TSW), Atlantic water (AW) and Tyrrhenian intermediate water (TIW)93–96.

Biological sampling

A multidisciplinary survey was carried out in a wide coastal area of Sicily facing the Southern Tyrrhenian Sea, extending for 2300 km2 from Cape Cefalù (west side) to Cape Rasocolmo in the east, during early spring (April 16–24, 2007). At 24 stations, across 11 shelf-slope transects (Fig. 1), ichthyoplankton was sampled with a Bongo net (diameter 60 cm; mesh size 500 μm) in obliquely tows, on board the scientific research vessel Luigi Sanzo. When the water depth exceeded 200 m, it was towed from a maximum sampled depth of 180 m to the surface. The Bongo was towed at a constant speed of 2 knots and the filtered water was measured with an Idromar (Genova, Italy) type MF 315 flowmeter. All samplings were carried out during daylight hours (Table 1). The collected samples were fixed in 5% buffered formaldehyde and seawater solution.

Laboratory analyses

Fish larvae were sorted from the plankton, counted and identified to the lowest possible taxonomic level under a Wild M8 stereoscopic microscope. All specimens were measured as Total Length (TL) and Notochord Length (NL) for larvae in preflexion stage, and Standard Length (SL) for flexion and postflexion larvae84. Taxonomic identification was based mainly on an ichthyoplankton database created at the Institute of Coastal Marine Environment (IAMC) of Messina, including existing literature (see48. The nomenclature and systematic order of the species were updated to the website www.fishbase.org. Eggs and fish larvae counts were standardised to number per 10 m− 2 of sea surface97, dividing the number of individuals counted by the cubic metres of filtered water, then multiplying by the thickness of the sampled layer and finally multiplying by 10. Relative percentage abundance for each fish larva species was calculated as percentage contribution on the total counted specimens. Frequency percentage index was calculated considering how many stations the species is present in compared to the total number of stations.

Environmental data

Vertical profiles of temperature (°C), salinity (PSU), oxygen (mg l− 1) and fluorescence (µg l− 1) were collected in the 24 stations from surface to a maximum depth of 200 m by means of a Sea-Bird Electronics 9/11 plus (Bellevue, USA). The SBE Data Processing™ software was used for processing the collected data in order to eliminate spikes and low-quality values. Potential temperature (θ, °C) and potential density anomaly (σ0, kg m− 3) were estimated using the Ocean Data View software98.

For the analysis of the dynamic regime of surface water masses, the Eastward (u in m s− 1) and Northward (v in m s− 1) components of sea water velocity, downloaded from the E.U. Copernicus Marine Service Information through the GLOBAL_MULTIYEAR_PHY_001_030 product99,100, were considered. The RNetCDF Library101 was used to read and analyze the NETCDF Data, while the metR Library102 allowed to visualize sea surface currents map in R environment for the considered period, before and during the survey (from 1 to 24 April 2007) in the study area domain (latitude in the range 37°N − 40°N and longitude between 11.5°E − 16.5°E). The spatial resolution was 1/12° (approximatively 8 km).

Statistical analysis

In order to provide information on the similarity of stations based on fish larvae composition in the investigated area of the Southern Tyrrhenian Sea, the stations were classified by hierarchical clustering method103 using the Bray-Curtis distance measure and the Ward’s linkage as grouping method. To this aim, the stats R package104 was used. An analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) was then performed through the vegan R library105 to test the significance of differences in groupings separated in the cluster analysis. The Indicator Species Analysis was used to identify the species significantly contributing to each cluster; such analysis was performed by means of the indicspecies R package.

The geographical distinction between species and groups of stations was therefore used as subjective criterion to refine the identification of clusters. To evaluate the adequacy of the biological samples size, the species richness inside the clusters was obtained with the R package iNEXT106.

The larval abundance matrix was subjected to a Mantel test107 with the matrix of environmental variables with the aim to test if dissimilarities in larval abundance data were correlated to distances in environmental data.

A Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) was carried out on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity of the larval fish clusters to describe relationships among clusters and to show their general spatial pattern. A PERMANOVA test (the Adonis test from vegan R package) was combined with the PCoA in order to quantify how much variation is explained by grouping factors, using permutation testing to assess the statistical significance of group separation.

A Canonical Analysis of Principal coordinates (CAP), based on the Bray-Curtis dissimilarities, was performed to explore which habitat variables best explained the spatial variation observed in larval fish assemblage structure108. Environmental variables were standardized to obtain comparable scales109. CAP biplots were generated to visually display the direction and magnitude of the relationships between significant habitat factors and larval fish species. All the statistical analyses were performed using the Posit Software PBC, RStudio (The Integrated Development Environment for R, http://www.posit.co/) version 2026.1.0.392, which required the R language Version 4.5.2110.

Results

Environmental data

The hydrological profiles report the values of environmental variables associated to the uppermost layer mainly constituted by the MAW (Modified Atlantic Water). The salinity minimum, i.e. the signature of the MAW core, is found at depths between 10 m (easternmost stations) and 30 m (westernmost stations). The temperature profiles highlight relatively higher values of surface water to the western area rather than to the eastern one with an initial thermocline evident only in the western part (Fig. 2). The highest oxygen values are found at depths between 30 m and 70 m, while the DCM (Deep Chlorophyll Maximum) is found between 60 m and 75 m, with deeper DCM position in the westernmost stations.

Larval fish composition and abundance

The larval fish community consisted of 2073 individuals, further to 2750 fish eggs. Thirty-two species belonging to seventeen families were identified (Table 2). Considering all the sampled stations, Myctophum punctatum was the most abundant species (20.9% of the total fish larvae abundance), followed by Cyclothone braueri (11%), Lampanyctus pusillus (10.2%), Lampanyctus crocodilus (9%), Hygophum hygomii (4.6%) and Notoscopelus bolini (3%). The family most represented was Myctophidae (61.6%, 13 identified species), followed by Gonostomatidae (12.4%, 2 species), Paralepididae (5.6%, 3 species), Bothidae (3.9%, 2 species), Stomiidae (2.5%, 1 species), Callyonimidae (2.3%, 1 genus of unidentified species) and Gobiidae (2.2%, 1 species). Each other family was represented at most by 1.7%.

The Mantel test results show that dissimilarities in larval abundance data were correlated to distances in environmental data. Discussion

This study concerns the abundance, community structure and spatial distribution patterns of larval fish assemblages in the Southern Tyrrhenian Sea, and how environmental parameters at local scale can influence them. On the continental shelf and slope, ichthyoplankton species composition is strongly influenced by local ocean currents and water masses14,111,83, whilst closer to the coast, continental runoff and coastal wind-induced upwelling may play additional important roles112,113,111. The study of the relation between larvae abundance and environmental conditions gives the opportunity to identify suitable habitats and observe habitat shifts linked to anthropogenic activity that may influence the survival and recruitment of fish larvae114–116,83.

With this study, it was highlighted that ichthyoplanktonic community abundance and composition in the Southern Tyrrhenian Sea during early spring season are significantly influenced by environmental factors, like bottom depth and salinity.

Considering the environmental parameters, two different areas were highlighted in terms of salinity, temperature and fluorescence, influenced by different water masses. Furthermore, a coastal–offshore gradient influenced larval species assemblages. In this context, it should be observed that, in the first part of the survey at sea the MAW mainly affected the most offshore stations (i.e. 14E, 22G and 28G) and during the second phase of the survey the MAW moved near the north-eastern coast of Sicily, thus affecting all the eastern stations. It is important to note that along its path through the Tyrrhenian Sea, the MAW mixes with the surrounding waters of the upper layer, thus causing an increase in salinity and a decrease in temperature. These results are in agreement with previous ones obtained in late spring in the same study area, where statistical analyses revealed well defined groups of stations and assemblages of larvae in accordance with an inshore-offshore gradient48. It’s already well known that fish larvae species composition reflects the spawning area of adult fish populations14. Our results, in agreement with Cuttitta et al.15, allow us to identify in the study area two main fish larvae assemblages: one group constituted by larvae belonging to neritic families, more abundant in shallower stations; a second one constituted by oceanic taxa, particularly myctophid and gonostomatid larvae in deeper stations. The easternmost stations are strongly influenced by upper water layer (saltier and colder than in the westernmost area).

The study of species composition, abundance, and spatial and temporal distribution of fish larvae can provide valuable data on the locations and seasons of spawning, particularly commercially important species117. To understand the life cycle, behaviour, and migration of fish, provides important scientific information to evaluate the reproduction success of different fish, and further state of recruitment and fish stocks that can be used for rational exploitation of fish resources117. The seasonality and the duration of their meroplanktonic life stage, lead to different spatial and temporal distributions of fish larvae118,119,48,83. The significant number of fish eggs found in this study leads us to think that some areas of this coastal ecosystem could be important for some inshore fish species reproduction and spawning120,3,46,15. Giordano et al.48 indicated for the same study area, a higher chlorophyll a concentration near the coast between Cape d’Orlando and Cape Milazzo, as phenomenon quite independent of the thermohaline properties of the water column and instead related to the physical constrains of the water masses and to nutrient concentrations. A positive relation among fish eggs and larvae and chlorophyll a and nutrients concentration was already demonstrated18. This association reflects the importance of the availability of food items for the first life stages of fishes, when very high mortality is associated with the lack of food items or the inability to capture them121,122,18. Along the trophic chain, high abundance of phytoplankton and so of zooplankton, can support the developmental stages of fish species116,123,124,82. The trophic dependency of ichthyoplankton on zooplankton is unquestionable and results in a positive relationship125. The maximum levels of ichthyoplankton richness and abundance as known to be coincident with the spring peak of zooplankton biomass in April118,123,124,82. In our study area, ichthyoplankton standing stocks peaked in February (6.28 g m− 2), preceding both the first primary production peak and the spring maximum zooplankton biomass in May27.

The timing and biomass of the spring phytoplankton bloom are of fundamental importance for the survival of copepods and fish larvae. Sameoto126 stated that the total number of fish larvae (anchovy and sardine) showed a linear increase with the zooplankton biomass. Zooplankton and ichthyoplankton are always present, and this ensures the availability of food throughout the year, as well as the uninterrupted generation of growth and reproduction of specimens125. It’s well known that larvae of different species of the family Myctophidae feed upon the zooplankton, especially copepods and euphausiids127–129,18.

We believe that our entire study area is an integral part of the Aeolian Islands Basin (South Eastern Tyrrhenian Sea), a large oligotrophic pelagic ecosystem56,48 considered an important fishing, reproduction and spawning area for many commercial fish species, including Swordfish (Xiphias gladius,33), Bluenfin Tuna (Thunnus thynnus,34,35,36,37,38), European hake (Merluccius merluccius,39,40), Greater amberjack (Seriola dumerilii,41), and European anchovy (Engraulis encrasicolus,42). We support the very interesting hypothesis that, as reported for the jellyfish Pelagia noctiluca life cycle63, two interconnected systems exist, which could be the result of the transport of permanent populations of some commercial fish larvae in pelagic waters towards the southern Tyrrhenian coast, caused by a surface west-east flow modified Atlantic water that forming a broad anticyclonic cell off the Sicilian coast63. The Aeolian Islands Archipelago favours the reproduction and spawning of numerous fish, while the Southern Tyrrhenian coastal ecosystem favours their growth due to higher productivity59, offering a suitable rich habitat without compromising the survival of the population.

The highest concentrations of larvae belonging to mesopelagic species were found at stations located on the slope between 400 m and the pelagic zone, at a distance from the coast of about 7 nautical miles. This shows that the Aeolian basin system influences especially the eastern part of the study area. Similar to other region, Myctophidae was the most represented family, although, sometimes, with a different percentage of species abundance in relation to different sampling periods, environmental and biological parameters. With this study it was highlighted that in the Southern Tyrrhenian Sea during the sampling period, the most abundant species was Myctophum punctatum, followed by Cyclothone braueri, Lampanyctus pusillus and Lampanyctus crocodilus. In fact, among the deep-sea fishes, it was already reported that the myctophids have high abundance and widespread distribution in all the oceans constituting an important link in the pelagic ecosystems130,131. Their larvae are well represented in plankton, playing a very important role in the planktonic food chain, scarcely feed on copepod eggs and naupliar stages, instead preferring larger sized prey such as calanoid copepodites131. Although our data refer to a study carried out 18 years ago, due to the scarcity of studies on fish larvae ecology in this area, the data presented in this manuscript highlight a need for further studies, in order to analyse the influence of sea water global warming on the composition and abundance of fish larvae. These data also present a knowledge base against which to compare other and/or more recent studies to highlight any structural changes attributable to ongoing climate change in the Southern Tyrrhenian Sea.

Despite the relatively narrow spatial and temporal constraints of the present study, the sampling plan developed and the results obtained refer to a specific area of the Tyrrhenian Sea. In this area, the general model of surface water circulation foresees a counterclockwise movement of MAW (Modified Atlantic Water) parallel to the Italian coast88. This pattern moves the spawning products parallel to the northern coast of Sicily, limiting their offshore movement. The general surface water circulation model and the limited extent of the continental shelf along the northern Sicilian coast have driven the sampling strategy (both in terms of the number and location of stations). It may also be useful to note, as already highlighted by Cuttitta et al.15 in the same study area, that the highest larval densities are mainly found at stations closest to the coastline. The results obtained in this study allowed to highlight the differences in the zooplankton community related to the bathymetric gradient (coast-offshore) and the environmental conditions associated with the complex water masses circulation. We believe that the adopted sampling design was appropriate for the objectives set, while at the same time limiting a costly offshore sampling plan with likely limited benefits.
