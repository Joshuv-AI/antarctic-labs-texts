Rates of maximal blood lactate accumulation in the extreme intensity exercise domain: implications for training prescription
Authors: Ozgur Ozkaya, Hakan As, Arda Peker, Mark Burnley, Andrew M. Jones
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study investigated the rate of blood lactate accumulation (vLa) across six maximal sprint efforts and identified a breakpoint (vLaBP) in the vLa–power relationship.MethodsTwelve physically active males completed 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30-s all-out sprints in randomised order on separate days, with resistances set at 10 to 12.5% of body mass. The vLa was calculated as the change in blood lactate between the resting and peak value (∆La) divided by the test duration. A piecewise linear regression was used to identify the vLaBP and its corresponding power output (P-vLaBP) in the vLa–power relationship. Pulmonary V̇O2 responses were modelled using V̇O2 data obtained from exercise and the subsequent 15-min recovery period.ResultsA distinct P-vLaBP was identified at 795 ± 135 W and 11.2 ± 1.5 W·kg−1, corresponding to a vLa of 0.50 ± 0.10 mM·s–1, ∆La of 6.50 ± 1.27 mM, and sprint duration of 13.3 ± 1.9 s. The vLa did not increase significantly during the 30, 25, 20, and 15-s all-out sprint tests (0.41 ± 0.04 to 0.48 ± 0.10 mM·s⁻1; p > 0.05) but increased markedly in the 10 and 5-s tests (0.58 ± 0.14 mM·s⁻1, p < 0.05; 0.7 ± 0.20 mM·s⁻1, p < 0.01). V̇O2 on-kinetics below the P-vLaBP (30 and 15 s) was best described by linear function (R2 ≥ 0.96; SEE ≤ 272 mL·min–1; p < 0.001), while the V̇O2 data could not be modelled for sprints performed above the P-vLaBP (10 and 5 s).ConclusionThe present study demonstrated that the P-vLaBP separates ranges of power outputs with discrete V̇O2 kinetics which are characteristic of different subzones within the extreme intensity exercise domain.

Introduction

Within the exercise sciences, exercise intensity domains are typically classified as moderate, heavy, severe, and extreme (Hill et al. 2002; Poole et al. 2005; Burnley and Jones 2007; Poole and Jones 2012; Bishop et al. 2025). The moderate, heavy and severe intensity exercise domains are characterised by distinct dynamic behaviours of V̇O2 kinetics and blood lactate concentration (La), and these behaviours are consistent within each domain even at different absolute power outputs (Whipp and Mahler 1980; Gaesser and Poole 1996; Poole et al. 2005; Burnley and Jones 2007). However, V̇O2 and blood La kinetics have been less well characterised for the extreme intensity exercise domain. This is surprising given that, for a healthy and physically active individual, the extreme intensity exercise domain incorporates a potential range of power outputs almost twice as large as the sum of the moderate, heavy, and severe intensity domains (Ozkaya et al. 2023).

Notably, a recent study reported that V̇O2 kinetics change significantly as the power output increases within the extreme intensity exercise domain, and recognised three subzones, termed extreme-1, extreme-2 and extreme-3 (Ozkaya et al. 2023). Based on this framework, the V̇O2 kinetics in the extreme-1 intensity exercise zone is exponential, but it is not possible to attain maximal O2 uptake (V̇O2max) at task failure. In the extreme-2 zone, V̇O2 kinetics are best described by a linear function, while V̇O2 cannot be modelled due to the very brief exercise duration in the extreme-3 zone. Given the breadth of the extreme intensity domain, it has been suggested that these sub-categories may help in understanding physiological responses to exercise and in guiding training. Nevertheless, although a subzone approach within the extreme intensity exercise domain has been proposed, supported by discrete V̇O2 on- and off-response kinetics, methods for determining the transition intensities (i.e., as the power output or running speed) that separate the subzones within the domain have not yet been established.

Recently, there has been increasing interest in the rate of blood lactate accumulation (vLa) during short-term maximal exercise and especially its potential role in the evaluation of athletes’ performance (Quittmann et al. 2020, 2021; Yang et al. 2023; Dunst et al. 2023; Meixner et al. 2024a, b; Langley et al. 2024; Thron et al. 2024; Quittmann 2026). It has been reported that end-exercise peak blood La obtained from 5–10-s maximal efforts is significantly lower than that measured following 30–60-s sprint durations (Heck et al. 2003; Dunst et al. 2023; Langley et al. 2024). However, those 5–10-s short sprint bursts lead to a higher maximal lactate accumulation rate (vLamax) (Mavroudi et al. 2023; Langley et al. 2024; Porter and Langley 2025; Quittmann 2026). If there is an inverse relationship between longer sprint durations that elicit a higher end-exercise peak blood La response and shorter sprint durations that elicit a higher vLa response, this might be detectable as a breakpoint in the vLa–power relationship (vLaBP). This breakpoint would occur at a specific power output (P-vLaBP) and have a corresponding specific sprint duration (t-vLaBP). It is possible that this P-vLaBP may represent a transition between exercise in which there are adequate V̇O2 data that may be described by a linear function (i.e., extreme-2) and exercise in which V̇O2 cannot be modelled due to the very brief exercise duration dominated by breath-holding consistent with a Valsalva manoeuvre (i.e., extreme-3).

The aim of this study was, therefore, to investigate blood La dynamics during recovery from maximal sprint exercise of different durations and to calculate the corresponding vLa. We hypothesised that plotting the vLa values against the corresponding mean power outputs might reveal a P-vLaBP, which separates the extreme-3 from extreme-2 subzone based on both blood La dynamics and V̇O2 kinetics.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study received ethical approval from the Ege University Ethics Committee (Protocol no: 22-2T/16, 2 October 2022) and conformed to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (1983). Prior to participation, all procedures, along with potential risks and benefits, were clearly explained to the volunteers, and written informed consent was obtained in accordance with the approved documentation. The study recruitment was open to both sexes, but only male participants volunteered to take part. A total of twelve healthy, physically active males took part in the study (mean ± SD: age: 26.6 ± 6.6 years; height: 1.76 ± 0.07 m; body mass: 71.3 ± 12.9 kg). None of the participants reported any cardiovascular, pulmonary, metabolic or neuromuscular disorders. Participants were instructed to maintain consistent dietary habits throughout the study period, refrain from strenuous exercise in the 24 h preceding each testing session and avoid caffeine or alcohol intake during this same time frame.

Experimental overview

This study utilised a counter-balanced, randomised, within-subject crossover (repeated measures) design to examine different sprint durations, integrating blood lactate measurements, vLa–power relationships, and V̇O2 on- and off-kinetics. The study protocol comprised eight laboratory visits per participant, each separated by 24–48 h, and was completed over a period of 2–3 weeks. A familiarisation session was undertaken before the sprint trials to minimise single practice effects. Following a ramp incremental exercise test, each participant performed six all-out sprint tests (i.e., 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 s) on separate days. The calculated vLa values were plotted against the corresponding power outputs to define a breakpoint (i.e., vLaBP) in the relationship. The vLamax was also determined for each participant. Time-dependent V̇O2 responses were modelled to describe V̇O2 kinetics. All exercise tests were conducted in a well-ventilated laboratory, under standardised conditions (20–22 °C temperature, ~20.8% O2, ~0.05% CO2, and 50–60% relative humidity). The time of the day allocated for testing was also standardised (1.5 ± 0.4 h) to control for diurnal variance.

Procedures

Ramp incremental tests

Ramp incremental tests were performed on an electromagnetically braked cycle ergometer (Monark LC7, Vansbro, Sweden) with a sampling interval of 1 Hz. The manufacturer certifies that measurement error is below 2% when the ergometer is regularly calibrated. Based on previous studies indicating that an optimal knee angle at the pedal’s bottom dead centre position is approximately 150° (Bohlmann 1981; de Vey Mestdagh 1998), the seat height was adjusted to provide a knee angle of 150° for each participant. Ramp incremental tests commenced with 4 min of cycling without resistance before the power output was increased by 30 W·min−1. Participants were asked to maintain their preferred cadence for as long as possible (i.e., approximately 70–90 rpm). The tests were terminated when participants’ cadence fell more than 10 rpm below their preferred cadence for longer than 10 s, despite verbal encouragement. Pulmonary gas exchange and ventilation data were measured breath-by-breath throughout the ramp tests using a metabolic cart (Cosmed Quark CPET, Rome, Italy). Data were initially filtered to remove occasional errant breaths resulting from e.g., swallows, coughs, or sighs. V̇O2 data points lying more than four standard deviations from the local mean (five breaths) were deleted and were smoothed using a 5-breath moving average. Data were then exported in 5-s binned averages. The highest 30-s moving average of V̇O2 (i.e., V̇O2max) and the corresponding power output (i.e., P-V̇O2max) were determined for each participant.

Familiarisation sessions

Familiarisation sessions for the all-out sprint tests were performed on a mechanically braked cycle ergometer (Monark Peak Bike 894E, Varberg, Sweden). During these sessions, all participants accelerated the flywheel to its maximum speed, and 7–8 s sprints were performed without additional frictional resistance. The performance criterion in this first stage of familiarisation was reaching peak flywheel speed within 5–6 s depending on flywheel inertia (Ozkaya 2013). Participants who met the criterion in two consecutive trials were given 5 min of rest before proceeding. In the second stage of familiarisation, following the pre-acceleration phase, a load corresponding to 10% of body mass was applied to the system, once the cadence reached 120 rpm. Subsequent all-out trials were considered successful when the time to peak power ≤ 2 s was achieved in two consecutive trials (Ozkaya 2013). Participants fulfilling these criteria were deemed to be familiarised and were admitted to the all-out sprint sessions.

All-out sprint sessions

Since we know that a traditional Wingate-type 30-s sprint is characterised by a linear behaviour in V̇O2 kinetics (Beneke et al. 2002; Ozkaya et al. 2014) and is typically performed within the extreme-2 intensity exercise zone (Ozkaya et al. 2023), the longest all-out sprint test duration was set to 30-s Wingate-type task. In this phase of the study, each participant completed six all-out sprint tests (5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 s) on separate days in a randomised cross-over design. To obtain valid vLa estimations, resistance was systematically increased by 0.5% over the typical Wingate load (i.e., 10% of body mass) for each test, and all tests were performed all-out without pacing. Body mass was measured before each testing session using a calibrated digital scale (Seca 217, Hamburg, Germany) with participants wearing minimal clothing and no shoes. Accordingly, the resistance applied for 30, 25, 20, 15, 10, and 5-s sprints corresponded to 10%, 10.5%, 11%, 11.5%, 12%, and 12.5% of body mass, respectively. Participants completed a 5-min warm-up at ~100 W at their preferred cadence (70–90 rpm), followed by a 5-min rest. Each test then began with 4 min of no-resistance cycling at ~50 rpm. During the final 30 s of the baseline period, participants stopped pedalling and awaited the start command. All tests were performed in a seated position, starting from a static position with the dominant leg at the top of the first pedal stroke. A verbal countdown (“3–2–1–go”) signalled the start of each sprint, at which point participants were encouraged to produce maximal effort from the first pedal stroke. Once cadence exceeded 120 rpm, the ergometer automatically applied the pre-set resistance (i.e., 10 to 12.5% of body mass), initiating data acquisition. Participants were verbally encouraged to maintain maximal effort throughout the sprint. Power output (i.e., W and W·kg−1) and cadence (i.e., rpm) were recorded continuously. The highest instantaneous power production during the sprint was considered the Pmax, while the mean power output measured during each sprint was recorded as the Pavg. The highest 30-s moving average of V̇O2 measured during the sprints and the following 15-min recovery periods were also analysed from 5-s binned averages.

Estimation of the vLa and vLamax

Blood La values were analysed, using 20-μl blood samples, which were collected from the fingertip before, at the end, and at the 1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th and 10th min of the recovery periods, by the enzymatic amperometric method (Biosen C-line, EKF Diagnostics, GmbH, Barleben, Germany). A resting blood La sample was obtained upon arrival at the laboratory, before the warm-up, after ≥ 5 min of seated rest. The change in blood La (i.e., ∆La in mM) between the resting (Larest) and post-exercise peak La values (Lapeak) obtained from all six sprint tests were calculated. Estimation of breakpoints in the relationship between vLa and power output

Calculated vLa values (on the y-axis) were plotted against the Pavg during the sprints (on the x-axis) to derive a vLaBP for each participant. A two-segment linear regression model (SigmaPlot; Systat Software Inc., San Jose, CA) was used to mathematically determine the vLaBP. Regression analysis essentially fits two separate linear regression lines to different domains of the data defining the gradient of the first and second segment, the change in gradient from the first to second segments, and the breakpoint position (Muggeo 2003). The power output at the breakpoint position (i.e., P-vLaBP) and the exercise duration corresponding to the P-vLaBP (i.e., t-vLaBP) were then recorded for each participant individually. Breakpoint positions which were determined statistically were then visually verified by five sports physiologists, who were asked to independently determine breakpoint positions in the vLa–power output relationships (Ozkaya et al. 2025).

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 26 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was tested with the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed by Levene’s test. Sphericity was evaluated using Mauchly’s test: when assumptions were violated, degrees of freedom were corrected using Greenhouse-Geisser (ε < 0.75) or Huynh-Feldt (ε > 0.75) as appropriate. Differences in power output, V̇O2, La, and time related variables across sprint tests were then analysed using a repeated-measures ANOVA. Pairwise comparisons were conducted with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons. Significance levels were set at p ≤ 0.05. Data are presented as mean ± SD.

Results

The mean time to limit of tolerance at the end of the ramp incremental exercise test was 11.6 ± 1.7 min. The mean V̇O2max of the group was 50.2 ± 5.1 mL·min–1·kg–1 and the P-V̇O2max corresponded to 334 ± 56 W and 4.7 ± 0.4 W·kg−1. The highest Pmax (1027 ± 212 W; 14.5 ± 2.6 W·kg−1) and vLamax (0.70 ± 0.20 mM·s–1) were achieved during the 5-s all-out sprint test for each participant. Peak cadence values obtained from the six all-out efforts performed from 30 to 5 s were 134 ± 8 rpm, 138 ± 8 rpm, 136 ± 7 rpm, 132 ± 9 rpm, 137 ± 8 rpm and 137 ± 8 rpm, respectively. The two-segment linear regression model identified a distinct P-vLaBP at 795 ± 135 W and 11.2 ± 1.5 W·kg−1 (R2 = 0.98 ± 0.03; SEE% = 2.90 ± 1.78; p ≤ 0.01). This corresponded to a ∆La of 6.50 ± 1.27 mM, a vLa of 0.50 ± 0.10 mM·s–1, and a t-vLaBP of 13.3 ± 1.9 s (Table 1). Discussion

The purpose of the present study was to investigate whether individual P-vLaBP derived from the vLa–power relationship from six all-out sprint tests corresponds to the boundary which separates the extreme-3 from the extreme-2 subzone. Consistent with our hypothesis, the P-vLaBP was found to separate a range of power outputs wherein V̇O2 kinetics during exercise could be best described with a linear function from a range of power outputs wherein V̇O2 kinetics could not be modelled at all. The upper boundary of the extreme-2 intensity exercise zone may therefore be considered to represent the highest all-out effort beyond which the vLa shifts from limited to rapidly accelerating behaviour. The vLaBP corresponded to a mean power output of 795 W and 11.2 W·kg−1 (i.e., approximately 238% of P-V̇O2max and 78% of Pmax). Accordingly, the extreme-3 exercise zone includes exercise durations up to approximately 13 s.

The extreme-3 intensity exercise zone of our participants had a range of approximately 232 W and 3.3 W·kg−1 between Pmax and P-vLaBP (i.e., 1027 – 795 W; 14.5 – 11.2 W·kg−1). Within this zone, which comprises very short-term maximal efforts, participants typically prefer to hold their breath, and the peak V̇O2 is reached during the recovery period, with the recovery V̇O2 response resembling an M-shape (Lamarra 1990; Ozkaya et al. 2023). It is known that breath holding during such short-term, extreme intensity exercise reflects the Valsalva manoeuvre, which significantly increases systolic blood pressure and supports the expression of strength and power (Narloch and Brandstater 1995). If the total exercise test duration incorporates a significant period of breath holding, it is not possible to measure or model V̇O2 data during exercise (Ozkaya et al. 2023). In contrast, exercise performed at power outputs below the P-vLaBP transition (i.e., those in the 15﻿–30 s duration range), performed within the extreme-2 exercise zone, resulted in an apparently linear V̇O2 response during exercise along with greater metabolic acidosis. It has been previously suggested that the linearity in V̇O2 kinetics for extreme-2 exercise may be due to a combination of V̇O2 reflecting a very high demand but being constrained by the V̇O2max and the relatively short exercise duration (Ozkaya et al. 2023). Clearly, however, maximal exercise in this time range enables a high rate of anaerobic glycolysis, as reflected by a greater end-exercise blood La concentration.

On the other hand, a recent systematic review fitted four separate bi-exponential models to cycling data, excluding the 3- and 60-s data points, and predicted that the shortest test duration for eliciting vLamax is approximately 4.5–5 s (Langley et al. 2025). At shorter exercise durations, the time-equivalent accounting for predominantly phosphocreatine (PCr) driven ATP resynthesis with minimal glycolytic activation and negligible blood La accumulation (tPCr) is likely to have a disproportionate impact, potentially inflating variability in vLamax estimates. Indeed, unlike V̇O2max, which is typically operationalised as the highest V̇O2 value attained within a standardised time interval (i.e., 1-min), vLamax is calculated by the ∆La divided by sprint duration. As exercise intensity increases and sprint duration shortens, this ratio-based computation may produce a vLamax value that is disproportionately high and which may not accurately reflect the underlying glycolytic rate. This issue could be considered one of the most important limitations to the ability to obtain a vLamax response and to use this value as an indicator of maximal glycolytic power. Leaving these debates aside, our findings suggest that, rather than attempting to determine a single vLamax value, it may be more appropriate to frame the phenomenon as a vLa breakpoint (i.e., vLaBP), with the corresponding power output representing a transition that separates the extreme-2 and extreme-3 subzones.

A further methodological issue in the vLamax literature concerns the tPCr and the variability in how it is defined and calculated. It has been claimed that tPCr refers to the beginning of the sprint performance that is dominated by PCr breakdown without a significant activation of glycolysis or accumulation of blood La (Mader 1984, 1994; Heck et al. 2003; Yang et al. 2023). There are different assumptions when calculating the tPCr. For example, for the 100 m, 200 m, and 400 m sprints (i.e., approximately 10–12-s, 20–25-s and 45–60-s duration), it is suggested that tPCr be fixed at 3-s, 4-s, and 8-s time periods, respectively (Mader 1994; Heck et al. 2003). Meixner et al. (2024a) suggest the use of a standard tPCr of 3.5 s. In another method, the time to reach Pmax is considered to be the tPCr duration (Heck et al. 2003; Manunzio et al. 2016). On the other hand, it has also been suggested that the tPCr should be defined as the time window from the onset of exercise to the point at which power output has decreased by 3.5% after the Pmax is reached (Weber 2003; Heck et al. 2003; Langley et al. 2024). As another example, Yang et al. (2023) have recently suggested that the contribution of oxidative energy metabolism should be taken into account to reveal a more genuine vLamax. However, it is important to note that this time interval approach in which glycolytic activity does not occur is merely hypothetical assumption (Gaitanos et al. 1993; Brooks 2018), and none of these methods has yet gained widespread acceptance or evolved into a broadly endorsed consensus. In a recent review, Wackerhage et al. (2025) highlighted several conceptual and methodological limitations associated with the estimation of vLamax, suggesting that calculating a peak vLa value (i.e., vLapeak) without subtracting a tPCr may offer a more valid alternative. Indeed, in empirical settings, it has been suggested to assume “tPCr = 0” and to calculate vLamax by dividing ∆La by test duration (Quittmann 2026), given that phosphagen contribution can only be estimated with limited precision (Hirvonen et al. 1987; Gastin 2001; Quittmann 2026) and that accelerated glycolysis and lactate accumulation begin immediately at exercise onset (Gastin 2001; Brooks et al. 2022; Quittmann 2026). Accordingly, we took this approach in the present study.

From a practical perspective, the P-vLaBP could be useful in discriminating and prescribing sprint interval training (SIT) and repeated sprint training (RST). Over the past two decades, the physiological and performance benefits of SIT (i.e., 20–30-s all-out sprints, performed at approximately 150–200% of P-V̇O2max) have been consistently demonstrated (Burgomaster et al. 2005; Gibala et al. 2006). Similar improvements have been described even following very short-duration, high-intensity intermittent exercise of 8–12 s, i.e., RST (Buchheit and Laursen 2013). In the current study, the t-vLaBP, corresponding to an effort time of approximately 13 s, may be considered a boundary separating SIT and RST prescriptions. Indeed, exercise involving comparatively longer sprint bouts and yielding high end-exercise blood La appears more closely aligned with SIT, while much shorter bouts, often accompanied by breath-holding and characterised by low end-exercise blood La responses despite a high lactate accumulation rate, are consistent with RST.

In terms of limitations, it should be noted that post-exercise La was sampled at the 1st, 3rd, 5th, 7th and 10th min. Future work should sample blood more frequently (e.g., every minute) to refine breakpoint estimation. Additionally, further investigations should consider a broader range of populations, including well-trained or elite athletes, females, and older adults, and should also evaluate the validity of the approach for other exercise modalities. The reliability of vLaBP determination could also be investigated in future studies.

Conclusions

The present study identified a P-vLaBP that separated the power outputs that provide the greatest vLa from those that generate higher levels of La. This P-vLaBP boundary was found to occur at approximately 13-s of all-out effort for the physically active participants in this study. The all-out sprints performed below the P-vLaBP resulted in apparently linear V̇O2 response kinetics, while it was not possible to measure or model V̇O2 data for the all-out sprints performed above the P-vLaBP. The results of this study may be useful in differentiating extreme-2 from extreme-3 exercise and potentially in the prescription of SIT and RST.
