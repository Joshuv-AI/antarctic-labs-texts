Gut microbiota dysbiosis in the pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis and the therapeutic efficacy of fecal microbiota transplantation: a meta-analysis
Authors: Chaolun Zhu, Mengyuan Wang, Lingfei Meng, Zixu Zhao, Haitao Han, Yi Chang, Shuangxi Zhang, Yun Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the correlation between gut microbiota dysbiosis and the pathogenesis of ulcerative colitis (UC), and to provide an evidence-based basis for clinical diagnosis and treatment.MethodsRandomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included through a systematic search of the PubMed, Medline, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and EMBASE databases. The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB 2) was used to evaluate the quality of the literature. RevMan 5.3 software was employed to conduct a Meta-analysis, and the pooled effect size (OR/RR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) were calculated. The primary outcome measures included the associations between gut microbial diversity, changes in the abundance of specific microbial groups and the onset of UC.ResultsA total of 6 literatures were included, involving 171 study subjects (100 cases in the fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) group and 71 cases in the control group), all of which were randomized controlled trials. There was no significant difference in the clinical response between the FMT group (ulcerative colitis patients receiving fecal microbiota transplantation intervention) and the control group (ulcerative colitis patients receiving conventional treatment or placebo) (RD = 0.17, 95% CI [-0.05-0.40], P = 0.14); the clinical remission in the FMT group was significantly better than that in the control group (OR = 2.20, 95% CI [1.05–4.59], P = 0.04); the changes in the microbiota composition in the FMT group were significantly higher than those in the control group (MD = 0.20, 95% CI [0.11–0.28], P < 0.001); there was no significant difference in the occurrence of adverse events between the FMT group and the control group (RD=-0.00, 95% CI [-0.15-0.14], P = 0.98); there was no significant difference in endoscopic remission between the FMT group and the control group (OR = 1.53, 95% CI [0.57–4.11], P = 0.40).ConclusionThis exploratory meta-analysis suggested that gut microbiota dysbiosis may be associated with the onset of UC, manifested as reduced diversity, imbalanced microbiota structure, and changes in the abundance of specific microbial groups. However, given the limited number of included studies, these findings are hypothesis-generating and require validation in larger, well‑powered trials. Further exploration of the potential value of microbiota intervention in the treatment of UC is needed.Keywords: Ulcerative colitis, Gut microbiota, Pathogenesis, Dysbiosis, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Ulcerative Colitis (UC) was a chronic disease characterized by continuous and non-specific inflammation of the rectal and colonic mucosa [1]. Clinically, it often presents with symptoms such as recurrent abdominal pain, mucopurulent bloody stools, and diarrhea. The disease has a protracted course and is prone to recurrence, severely affecting the quality of life of patients [1]. As one of the main types of inflammatory bowel diseases (IBD), its pathogenesis is not caused by a single factor but is a complex result of the interplay among genetic susceptibility, immune dysfunction, and environmental factors [2].

Genetically, UC shows obvious family aggregation. Genome - wide association studies (GWAS) have identified multiple susceptible genes, such as NOD2, IL − 23R, and ATG16L1. These genes are mostly involved in immune signal transduction or autophagy processes and may increase disease susceptibility by influencing the recognition and response of the intestinal mucosa to microorganisms [3]. For example, genetic variations in the IL − 23R gene can lead to excessive activation of T helper 17 (Th17) cells, resulting in the massive release of pro - inflammatory factors (such as IL − 17 and IL − 22), which exacerbates intestinal inflammation.

Immune abnormalities are the core link in the pathogenesis of UC. Under normal circumstances, the intestinal mucosal immune system maintains homeostasis by tolerating symbiotic flora and eliminating pathogenic bacteria. However, in UC patients, there is an imbalance between innate and adaptive immunity: the secretion of antimicrobial peptides by intestinal epithelial cells is reduced, and the defensive ability of the mucosal barrier is decreased; antigen - presenting cells such as dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages are over - activated, inducing the differentiation of T cells into pro - inflammatory phenotypes (Th1/Th17). Meanwhile, the function of regulatory T (Treg) cells is impaired, failing to effectively suppress the excessive immune response, leading to the continuous amplification of inflammation [4–6].

Environmental factors play an important role in triggering the disease. Dietary patterns (such as high-fat and high-sugar diets), abuse of antibiotics, and mental stress may indirectly affect the occurrence of UC by altering the gut microbiota. Among them, the gut microbiota, as the “second genome”, has become a research focus in recent years regarding its association with UC. The normal gut microbiota consists of thousands of microorganisms, which maintain intestinal health through mechanisms such as metabolism (e.g., synthesis of short-chain fatty acids, SCFAs), competitive colonization, and immune regulation. In UC patients, there is a widespread dysbiosis of the microbiota: the diversity indices such as the Shannon index are significantly decreased, the ratio of Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes is imbalanced, the abundance of symbiotic bacteria such as Prevotella and Bifidobacterium is reduced, and pro-inflammatory bacteria such as Escherichia coli (E. coli) and adherent-invasive Escherichia coli (AIEC) were over-proliferated. This imbalance can directly disrupt the tight junctions of the intestinal epithelium (e.g., down-regulation of the expression of occludin and claudin proteins), increase intestinal permeability, and expose more antigens in the intestinal lumen. At the same time, the abnormal microbiota can further promote the release of pro-inflammatory factors by activating signaling pathways such as toll-like receptor 4/nuclear factor kappa B (TLR4/NF-κB), forming a vicious cycle of “microbiota dysbiosis - barrier disruption - immune activation” [7, 8].

However, there is significant heterogeneity in existing studies: the characteristics of microbiota changes in different cohorts are not completely consistent. Some studies suggest that a decrease in Clostridium is associated with UC, while others focus on the fluctuations of Bacteroidetes, differences in detection methods (16 S rRNA sequencing vs. metagenomics) may also lead to result biases. In addition, most studies are small - sample observational analyses, lacking systematic evaluations. Therefore, in this study, data from 6 randomized controlled trials were integrated through a Meta - analysis to systematically evaluate the association between gut microbiota dysbiosis and the onset of UC, especially the relationship between microbiota changes and clinical outcomes after fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) intervention, aiming to provide evidence - based support for clarifying the etiology of UC and optimizing microbiota - targeted therapies.

Data and methods

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

1) Study type: Randomized controlled trials (RCTs). 2) Study subjects: Patients diagnosed with UC by endoscopy or pathology. The FMT group consists of patients who received fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) intervention. The control group consists of patients who received conventional treatments, including 5‑aminosalicylates (5‑ASA), glucocorticoids, or placebo, either alone or in combination. 3) Exposure factors: Detection of the gut microbiota (16 S rRNA sequencing or metagenomics). 4) Outcome indicators: Microbial diversity indices (such as the Shannon index) and changes in the abundance of specific microbiota. 5) Complete data: OR/RR and 95%CI can be extracted.

Exclusion criteria

1) Reviews, conference abstracts or animal studies. 2) Patients with other intestinal diseases (such as Crohn’s disease and irritable bowel syndrome). 3) Data that cannot be converted.

Search strategy

Databases

PubMed, Medline, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, EMBASE.

Search terms

To ensure comprehensive literature retrieval, both Medical Subject Headings (MeSH terms) and their corresponding entry terms/keywords were used, covering three core themes: disease, microbiota, and mechanism.

Disease-related terms:

Disease-related terms:

MeSH terms: “Ulcerative Colitis”. Entry terms/keywords: “Colitis, Ulcerative”, “Idiopathic Ulcerative Colitis”, “UC”.

(2)Microbiota-related terms:

Microbiota-related terms:

MeSH terms: “Gut Microbiota”, “Dysbiosis”. Entry terms/keywords: “Intestinal Microbiota”, “Gut Flora”, “Intestinal Flora”, “Microbial Dysbiosis”, “Microbial Diversity”.

(3)Mechanism-related terms:

Mechanism-related terms:

MeSH terms: “Pathogenesis”. Entry terms/keywords: “Pathogenic Mechanism”, “Etiology”, “Risk Factor”.

Boolean logical operators (AND, OR) were used to combine the terms. The specific search formula was: (“Ulcerative Colitis”[MeSH Terms] OR “Colitis, Ulcerative”[Title/Abstract] OR “Idiopathic Ulcerative Colitis”[Title/Abstract] OR “UC”[Title/Abstract]) AND (“Gut Microbiota”[MeSH Terms] OR “Intestinal Microbiota”[Title/Abstract] OR “Gut Flora”[Title/Abstract] OR “Intestinal Flora”[Title/Abstract] OR “Dysbiosis”[MeSH Terms] OR “Microbial Dysbiosis”[Title/Abstract] OR “Microbial Diversity”[Title/Abstract]) AND (“Pathogenesis”[MeSH Terms] OR “Pathogenic Mechanism”[Title/Abstract] OR “Etiology”[Title/Abstract] OR “Risk Factor”[Title/Abstract]).

Search period

The search was conducted from database inception to October 31, 2024, covering all studies published up to that date. This time frame was chosen to ensure both timeliness and data relevance.

Literature screening and quality assessment

Literature was independently screened by two researchers, and disagreements were arbitrated by a third researcher. The Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB 2) was used to evaluate the quality of the literature.

Primary outcome measures

Clinical response

Defined as a reduction in disease activity scores (e.g., ≥ 30% decrease in the Mayo score) or improvement in symptoms (e.g., reduced frequency of diarrhea or hematochezia).

Clinical remission

Defined as the absence of symptoms (e.g., Mayo score ≤ 2 with no subscore > 1) or endoscopic healing (e.g., Mayo endoscopic subscore ≤ 1).

Microbiota composition changes

Assessed through microbial diversity indices (e.g., Shannon index) and the relative abundance of specific bacterial groups (e.g., Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, Prevotella).

Adverse events

Any reported short-term complications (e.g., abdominal distension, diarrhea) related to FMT or conventional treatments.

Endoscopic remission

Defined as mucosal healing confirmed by endoscopy (e.g., absence of ulcers or erosions).

Risk-of-bias assessment

Risk-of-bias assessment for the included RCTs was conducted independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool version 2 (RoB 2). Overall Bias assessment for the included RCTs was conducted independently by two reviewers using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool version 2 (RoB 2). This tool evaluates the risk of bias across five domains: (1) Randomization process; (2) Deviations from intended interventions; (3) Missing outcome data; (4) Measurement of the outcome; (5) Selection of the reported result. A judgment of “Low risk,” “Unclear risk of bias,” or “High risk” of bias was made for each domain and for each study’s Overall Bias. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consulting a third reviewer.

Statistical analysis

RevMan 5.3 software was used for the Meta - analysis to calculate the combined effect size. The I² statistic was used for heterogeneity testing (I² > 50% indicated high heterogeneity), and a random - effects model was adopted. Sub - group analyses were stratified by disease activity status (active/remission) and detection method (16 S rRNA/metagenomics).

Quality evaluation

The quality of the six included literatures was evaluated using the Cochrane risk - of - bias assessment tool, which covered seven aspects including random sequence generation (selection bias), allocation concealment (selection bias), blinding of participants and personnel (performance bias), blinding of outcome assessment (detection bias), incomplete outcome data (attrition bias), selective reporting (reporting bias), and other biases. The results showed that the proportions of low - risk and high - risk in each bias item were both low, and most were of unclear risk. Among them, other biases were all rated as “unknown risk”, and the overall risk of bias in the literature quality was mainly unclear (Figs. 2 and 3). The main potential bias risks are shown in Table 2. In terms of samples and grouping, the sample size of some studies did not meet expectations or was relatively small. For example, the trial by Crothers JW 2021 was terminated early due to recruitment issues, and Schierová D 2020 had a limited sample size. these factors may affect the generalizability of the pooled analysis results. Additionally, the baseline gender differences caused by non-random grouping in Kump P 2018 should also be taken into account when interpreting the results. In the intervention and detection phase, the unblinded design in Brezina J 2021 and Fang H 2021 may slightly affect the judgment of efficacy due to subjective expectations. Meanwhile, the detection bias in Kump P 2018 and sample storage issues in Brezina J 2021 might lead to slight fluctuations in some key data, which in turn could impact the stability of the pooled analysis results. Regarding donor-related factors, the small number of donors in Crothers JW 2021 and Brezina J 2021, the special donor source in Fang H 2021, and the short donor intervention period in Shabat CS 2022 may cause the effect differences among different studies in the pooled results to be mixed with the influence of donor variables. When interpreting the results, it is necessary to objectively distinguish the role of the intervention itself from that of donor factors.

Results of the meta-analysis

Clinical response

Two articles compared the correlation between gut microbiota dysbiosis and clinical response in patients with UC. The Q - test and I² - test indicated that P = 0.44 (Chi² = 0.59, df = 1) and I² = 0%, suggesting no obvious heterogeneity among the studies. The Mantel - Haenszel (M - H) method in the fixed - effect model was used for data analysis and calculation of the risk difference (RD). The results showed that there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups (Z = 1.49, P = 0.14). There was no significant difference in the clinical response between the FMT group and the control group, with an RD of 0.17 and a 95% confidence interval (CI) of [-0.05, 0.40] (Fig. 4).

Forest plot for comparison of clinical responses

Clinical remission

Six articles were used to compare the correlation between gut microbiota dysbiosis and clinical remission in patients with UC. The Q - test and I² - test indicated that P = 0.49 (Chi² = 4.43, df = 5) and I² = 0%, suggesting no clear heterogeneity among the studies. The Mantel - Haenszel (M - H) method in the fixed - effect model was employed for data analysis and calculation of the odds ratio (OR). The results showed that there was a statistically significant difference between the two groups (Z = 2.10, P = 0.04). The clinical remission in the FMT group was significantly better than that in the control group, with an OR of 2.20 and a 95% CI of [1.05, 4.59], P = 0.04 (Fig. 5).

Forest plot for comparison of clinical remission

Microbiota composition analysis

Two articles were used to compare the correlation between gut microbiota dysbiosis and microbiota composition in patients with UC. The Q - test and I² - test indicated that P = 0.69 (Chi² = 0.16, df = 1) and I² = 0%, suggesting no clear heterogeneity among the studies. The inverse - variance (IV) method in the fixed - effect model was used for data analysis and calculation of the mean difference (MD). The results showed that there was a statistically significant difference between the two groups (Z = 4.38, P < 0.001). The change in microbiota composition in the FMT group was significantly higher than that in the control group, with an MD of 0.20 and a 95% CI of [0.11, 0.28] (Fig. 6).

Forest plot for comparison of microbiota composition analysis

Adverse events

Three articles were used to compare the correlation between gut microbiota dysbiosis and adverse events in patients with UC. The Q - test and I² - test indicated that P = 0.62 (Chi² = 0.97, df = 2) and I² = 0%, suggesting no clear heterogeneity among the studies. The M - H method in the fixed - effect model was used for data analysis and calculation of the risk difference (RD). The results showed that there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups (Z = 0.02, P = 0.98). There was no significant difference in adverse events between the FMT group and the control group, with an RD of − 0.00 and a 95% CI of [− 0.15, 0.14] (Fig. 7).

Forest plot for comparison of adverse events

Endoscopic remission

Four articles were used to compare the correlation between gut microbiota dysbiosis and endoscopic remission in patients with UC. The Q - test and I² - test indicated that P = 0.17 (Chi² = 4.97, df = 3) and I² = 40%, suggesting no obvious heterogeneity among the studies. The M - H method in the fixed - effect model was used for data analysis and calculation of the OR. The results showed that there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups (Z = 0.84, P = 0.40). There was no significant difference in endoscopic remission between the FMT group and the control group, with an OR of 1.53 and a 95% CI of [0.57, 4.11], P = 0.40 (Fig. 8).

Forest plot for comparison of endoscopic remission

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding the included studies one by one. For clinical remission, the I² value remained below 50% throughout the sensitivity analysis, and the pooled OR was consistently greater than 1 after each exclusion, indicating that the meta-analysis results were relatively stable. In the sensitivity analysis for adverse events, although the pooled effect size showed that the intervention was ineffective (MD=-0.00), the small sample size led to poor stability of the results. After excluding individual studies one by one, the pooled effect size varied between − 0.06 and 0.03, suggesting that the conclusion was highly sensitive to the inclusion of individual studies. For endoscopic remission, the sensitivity analysis revealed that heterogeneity increased when the studies by Brezina J 2021 and Shabat CS 2022 were excluded. When the study by Fang H 2021 was excluded, the direction of the pooled OR changed, indicating that the conclusions of the meta-analysis should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

The gut microbiota, regarded as an “invisible regulator” of host health, has become a core research direction in the field of IBD in terms of its association with the pathogenesis of UC [15]. UC is a chronic disease characterized by continuous inflammation of the rectal and colonic mucosa. Its pathological process involves complex interactions among genetic susceptibility, immune imbalance, and environmental factors. Gut microbiota dysbiosis is considered a key initiating factor triggering immune abnormalities and mucosal barrier damage [16]. Under normal physiological conditions, the gut microbiota maintains gut homeostasis through competitive inhibition of pathogenic bacteria colonization, synthesis of protective metabolites such as SCFAs, and regulation of mucosal immune cell differentiation. However, UC patients generally exhibit characteristics such as reduced microbiota diversity (e.g., a significant decrease in the Shannon index), an imbalance in the ratio of Firmicutes to Bacteroidetes, a decrease in the abundance of commensal bacteria such as Prevotella, and over - proliferation of pro - inflammatory bacteria (e.g., E. coli, AIEC) [17]. These abnormal microbiota structures can exacerbate the intestinal inflammatory response and promote disease progression through mechanisms such as disrupting intestinal epithelial tight junctions (e.g., down - regulation of occludin and claudin protein expression), activating the toll - like receptor (TLR) - nuclear factor κB (NF - κB) signaling pathway, and inducing the release of pro - inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF - α, IL − 6, IL − 17) [18]. This study, through a Meta - analysis integrating the results of six randomized controlled trials, further confirmed the close association between gut microbiota dysbiosis and the onset of UC. Moreover, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), as an intervention method for reconstructing gut microbiota balance, has shown clear advantages in improving clinical remission and regulating microbiota structure, providing important evidence - based support for exploring the etiology of UC and optimizing treatment strategies.

The results of this study showed that the clinical remission rate in the FMT group was significantly higher than that in the control group (OR = 2.20, 95%CI [1.05–4.59], P = 0.04), and the change amplitude of the microbiota composition was significantly greater (MD = 0.20, 95%CI [0.11–0.28], P < 0.0001). This finding directly verified the hypothesis that “microbiota remodeling is the core mechanism of FMT in treating UC”. From the perspective of microbial ecology, the therapeutic effect of FMT may be achieved through the following pathways: First, supplementing functionally defective microbiota, such as SCFA - producing Clostridium and Eubacterium. The metabolite butyric acid can reduce the secretion of pro - inflammatory factors by inhibiting the activity of histone deacetylase (HDAC) and promote the proliferation of intestinal epithelial cells and the repair of the mucus layer. Second, correcting the microbiota metabolic imbalance. FMT can restore the levels of tryptophan metabolites (e.g., indole − 3 - propionic acid), regulate the function of DCs by activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), and induce the differentiation of Treg cells to inhibit excessive immune responses. Third, reconstructing the microbiota - host interaction network. The transplanted healthy microbiota can inhibit the adhesion and colonization of pathogenic bacteria (e.g., AIEC) by competing for nutrients and ecological niches, reduce the release of pro - inflammatory substances such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and thus reduce the inflammatory burden on the intestinal mucosa [19, 20]. In addition, the consistency between the change in microbiota composition and clinical remission (e.g., Crothers JW [11] and others observed that the recovery of microbiota diversity was synchronous with the improvement of the Mayo score after FMT) suggests that dynamic monitoring of the microbiota structure (e.g., 16 S rRNA sequencing or metagenomics analysis) can be used to predict treatment response, providing a reference for formulating individualized treatment plans.

Notably, there was no significant difference between the FMT group and the control group in clinical response (RD = 0.17, 95%CI [− 0.05–0.40], P = 0.14) and endoscopic remission (OR = 1.53, 95%CI [0.57–4.11], P = 0.40). This phenomenon may be closely related to the clinical heterogeneity of UC and the complexity of the FMT intervention protocol. Clinical response usually reflects the short - term improvement of symptoms (e.g., diarrhea, hematochezia). The non - significant difference may be related to the following factors: First, the influence of disease activity. In patients with active UC, the intestinal mucosal inflammation is severe, with a large number of neutrophil infiltrations and ulcer formations, which may destroy the colonization micro - environment of the FMT microbiota and reduce its survival and proliferation efficiency (e.g., Shabat CS [10] and others showed that the response rate of FMT in active - phase patients was significantly lower than that in remission - phase patients). Second, the heterogeneity of the FMT protocol. There were differences in the transplantation route (oral capsules vs. colonoscopy infusion), dose (single vs. multiple transplantations), and donor selection of FMT in the included studies, which may affect the microbiota delivery efficiency and colonization success rate. Third, the non - uniformity of evaluation criteria. Different definitions of clinical response used in different studies (e.g., a ≥ 30% vs. ≥50% reduction in the Mayo score) may lead to result bias [21–23]. Endoscopic remission, as a more rigorous objective indicator, may be related to the relatively short follow - up time (the follow - up period in most studies was 8–12 weeks). The histological repair and inflammation regression of the intestinal mucosa often require a longer period of microbiota regulation [24]. For example, Fang H [13] and others found that the endoscopic remission rate at 24 weeks after FMT was significantly higher than that at 8 weeks (30% vs. 10%), suggesting that a longer observation period is needed to fully evaluate the long - term efficacy of FMT.

In terms of safety, there was no significant difference in the incidence of adverse events between the FMT group and the control group (RD=-0.00, 95%CI [− 0.15 − 0.14], P = 0.98), indicating that FMT has good short - term safety in the treatment of UC. Most of the adverse events reported in existing studies are mild - to - moderate gastrointestinal reactions (e.g., abdominal distension, transient diarrhea), which may be related to the ecological niche competition and metabolic adaptation in the early stage of microbiota transplantation and usually resolve spontaneously within 1–2 weeks [25]. This result supports FMT as an alternative option for patients with ineffective conventional treatments, especially for UC patients who are resistant to 5 - aminosalicylic acid drugs or dependent on glucocorticoids [26]. However, it should be noted that the sample size included in this study was relatively small (only 171 cases), and there was a lack of long - term safety data. Potential risks such as infection transmission (e.g., Clostridium difficile, cytomegalovirus) and microbiota - disorder - related metabolic abnormalities (e.g., obesity, insulin resistance) still need to be vigilant. Donor screening is a key step in ensuring the safety of FMT. It is necessary to strictly exclude donors with a history of infectious diseases, metabolic diseases, and carriers of intestinal pathogenic bacteria. In the future, strategies such as a “standardized donor bank” or “individualized donor matching” based on the recipient’s microbiota characteristics can be explored to improve the safety and precision of treatment.

This meta-analysis has several notable strengths that enhance the reliability and clinical relevance of its findings. First, it exclusively focused on RCTs, which represent the highest level of evidence for evaluating interventions. By including only RCTs, we minimized selection bias and confounding factors, thereby strengthening the validity of our conclusions. Second, our study employed rigorous methodology, including a comprehensive search strategy across multiple databases (PubMed, Medline, Web of Science, Cochrane Library, and EMBASE), independent literature screening by two researchers, and quality assessment using the Cochrane RoB 2. These measures ensured the inclusion of high-quality studies and reduced the risk of bias.Third, our analysis specifically investigated the role of fecal FMT in UC treatment, a topic of growing interest in gastroenterology. The significant improvement in clinical remission (OR = 2.20, P = 0.04) and microbiota composition changes (MD = 0.20, P < 0.0001) observed in the FMT group highlights the potential of microbiota-targeted therapies. Moreover, the absence of significant adverse events (RD = -0.00, P = 0.98) underscores the short-term safety of FMT, supporting its feasibility as a therapeutic option for UC patients who are unresponsive to conventional treatments.Another strength lies in our subgroup analyses, which stratified outcomes by disease activity (active/remission) and microbiota detection methods (16 S rRNA sequencing/metagenomics). Although limited by the small number of included studies, these analyses provided preliminary insights into potential sources of heterogeneity and the influence of methodological differences on results. Future large-scale studies could build on these findings to optimize FMT protocols.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the evidence base is small (only six studies, 171 cases), which frames our findings as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. The limited sample size reduces statistical power, increases the risk of false positives/negatives, limits generalizability, and precludes meaningful subgroup analyses or meta-regression (e.g., by disease activity, FMT route, donor characteristics, or control type) to explore heterogeneity. Second, methodological weaknesses exist: some studies had an unclear risk of bias (e.g., inadequate reporting of random sequence generation and allocation concealment), and this review was not prospectively registered (PROSPERO). Third, control group interventions varied considerably across studies (e.g., 5-aminosalicylates, glucocorticoids, placebo), which may confound comparisons with the FMT group and bias pooled effect sizes (e.g., for clinical remission). Fourth, heterogeneity in microbiota detection methods (16 S rRNA sequencing vs. metagenomics) and inconsistent reporting of taxonomic data precluded systematic analysis of specific bacterial groups (e.g., Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, SCFA-producing genera) linked to treatment response, limiting mechanistic insights. Fifth, the lack of long-term follow-up data prevents assessment of FMT’s impact on UC recurrence rates or long-term intestinal functions (e.g., nutrient absorption, barrier integrity). Sixth, the robustness of findings varies across outcomes: clinical response and microbiota changes each derived from only two studies, and adverse events from three. These analyses are particularly underpowered and susceptible to instability (e.g., sensitivity analysis showed considerable variation for adverse events upon exclusion of individual studies). Seventh, due to the limited number of included studies (n = 6), we did not perform formal publication bias assessments using funnel plots or Egger’s tests, as these methods are unreliable with fewer than 10 studies. Consequently, all conclusions warrant caution, and future large-scale, well-powered RCTs with standardized protocols are needed to validate our findings.

Based on the above limitations, future research should address the following targeted directions. First, large-scale, multi-center randomized controlled trials with standardized FMT protocols are urgently needed. These trials should define optimal donor selection, transplantation route (e.g., oral capsules vs. colonoscopy infusion), dose, frequency, and treatment course. Second, to reduce heterogeneity, future studies should unify efficacy evaluation criteria (e.g., standardized definitions of clinical response, remission, and endoscopic healing) and microbiota detection methods (e.g., 16 S rRNA sequencing vs. metagenomics) to allow cross-study comparison and meta-analysis. Third, stratified studies based on UC phenotypes (e.g., disease activity, hormone dependence, refractory status, extra-intestinal manifesations) should be conducted to identify which patient subgroups are most likely to benefit from FMT. Fourth, long-term follow-up systems (≥ 12 months) must be established to evaluate the durability of FMT efficacy, risk of relapse, and potential long-term adverse events. Finally, integrating multi-omics approaches (metagenomics, metabolomics, transcriptomics) may help identify key functional microbiota and metabolic markers that predict treatment response.

In conclusion, this exploratory meta‑analysis of six randomized controlled trials suggests that gut microbiota dysbiosis may play an important role in UC pathogenesis, and FMT appears to improve clinical remission with favorable short‑term safety. These findings suggest that FMT may be considered as an adjunctive option for UC patients refractory to conventional treatments, particularly for achieving clinical remission, but should currently be performed only in specialized centers with rigorous donor screening. However, due to the limited number of included studies, small sample sizes, heterogeneity in FMT protocols (donor, route, dose), and lack of long‑term follow‑up data, these results are hypothesis‑generating rather than conclusive. FMT is not yet ready for widespread clinical implementation in UC, and large‑scale, well‑designed RCTs are urgently needed to validate our findings and establish standardized protocols.
