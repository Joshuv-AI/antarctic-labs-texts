Background

Mesenchymal stem/stromal cells (MSCs), isolated from various tissues and expanded in vitro, possess the capacity to differentiate into tissue-specific cells when cultured in conditioned medium [1]. When activated by inflammatory cytokines, MSCs acquire robust immunomodulatory capabilities, characterized by the upregulation of several immunosuppressive factors such as inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS; in the case of rodents), indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1; in the case of human and other mammalian species), Tumor necrosis factor- (TNF) stimulated gene-6 (TSG-6) and programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1), secretion of chemokines including C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 9 (CXCL9), CXCL10, and CXCL11, and production of growth factors such as endothelial growth factor (EGF), fibroblast growth factor (FGF), and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF). These activities enable MSCs to establish a balanced inflammatory and regenerative microenvironment in damaged tissues, even under conditions of intense inflammation. The therapeutic potential of MSCs is currently being explored in numerous degenerative and inflammatory disorders through both preclinical and clinical studies, including Crohn’s disease, graft-versus-host disease, diabetic nephropathy, and organ fibrosis [2–6]. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the immunoregulatory functions of MSCs remain poorly understood.

Poly ADP-ribose polymerase (PARP) activation is implicated in the progression of inflammatory diseases, and PARP inhibitors, commonly used as targeted antineoplastic agents in clinical practice, have also shown promise in treating various inflammatory disorders [7]. In inflammatory diseases, PARP activation in macrophages promotes their pro-inflammatory response. Correspondingly, PARP inhibitors were shown to attenuate the inflammatory response and promote tissue repair by blocking the recruitment of immune cells and reducing their secretion of inflammatory factors within the tissue microenvironment [8–11]. While PARP inhibitors are primarily used to target immune cells in the treatment of inflammatory diseases, their effects on non-immune cells such as MSCs, which possess immunomodulatory functions, remain unclear.

In this study, we investigated the inhibitory effect of PARP on the immunosuppressive function of in vitro-expanded MSCs upon inflammatory stimulation. We found that, in contrast to the negative regulation of the pro-inflammatory factors in macrophages by PARP inhibition, the transcriptional and translational levels of immunosuppressive molecules were upregulated in PARP-inhibited MSCs treated with inflammatory factors, which led to enhanced immunosuppressive abilities as well as improved therapeutic effects of MSCs in inflammatory diseases. These results suggest that PARP plays an inhibitory role in MSC-mediated immunosuppression. Mechanistically, PARP inhibition enhanced phosphorylation at the tyrosine 701 site on signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) in MSCs stimulated with IFNγ and TNFα. Collectively, our findings suggest that PARP activity regulates the plasticity of MSC-mediated immunoregulation. PARP inhibition enhances the immunomodulatory function of MSCs and improves the treatment of inflammatory diseases in a STAT1-dependent manner.

Materials and methods

Animal

C57BL/6 mice, aged 8 ~ 10 weeks, were purchased from Slack Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and housed in a specific pathogen-free facility at the Laboratory Animal Center of Soochow University. Parp1 knockout mice were purchased from Jackson Laboratory. The mice were kept in ventilated cages at a temperature of 21 to 23 °C, with a 12-hour light-dark cycle. The relative humidity in the animal facility was maintained between 40% and 60%. Irradiated food from Double Lion Experimental Animal Feed Technology Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China) and sterile water were provided ad libitum. All procedures were performed in compliance with the relevant ethical guidelines and regulations.

MSC isolation and culture

Human umbilical cord-derived or dental pulp-derived MSCs and mouse bone marrow-derived MSCs were isolated and cultured as previously described [12]. MSCs were cultured in low-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 µg/ml streptomycin (all from Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA), and 10 ng/ml human FGF (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN), and incubated at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The cells were passaged every 2 to 3 days, with the cells used in the experiment being below the 10th passage number. Details regarding the characterization of cultured MSCs are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1 of the Supplementary Information.

MSC stimulation

MSCs were maintained in 6/12-well or culture dish until cells reached 75–80% confluence. The MSCs pre-washed with PBS were treated with TNFα (10 ng/ml, R&D Systems) and IFNγ (10 ng/ml, R&D Systems) for 24 h to elicit their immunoregulatory properties. Rucaparib (working concentration1 to 20 µM), Olaparib (10 µM) and Nifuroxazide (40 µM) were from Selleck (Houston, TX). 2-NBDG was from Thermo Scientific.

Griess assay

Nitrate measurements were performed exclusively on cell culture supernatants derived from WT and PARP1−/− mouse MSCs following 24-hour stimulation with IFNγ and TNFα. Supernatants were centrifuged at 12,000–14,000 × g for 10 min to remove cellular debris. Fifty microliters (50 µL) of debris-free supernatant were aliquoted into a 96-well plate. An equal volume (50 µL) of Griess reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) was added. The reaction mixture was incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. Absorbance was measured at 540 nm, and nitrate concentrations were calculated based on the absorbance using a standard curve.

CCK8 assay

MSCs were treated with a gradient of PARP inhibitor concentrations and cultured for 48 h in 96-well plates. Subsequently, MSCs were incubated for 2 h with fresh medium containing CCK-8 reagent (New Cell &Molecular Biotech) at a 10:1 ratio.

Real time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cell lysates according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the FastPure® Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit (Fastagen, Shanghai, China). The extracted RNA was then reverse-transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript™ RT Master Mix (Takara, Dalian, China) following the manufacturer’s protocol. The total reaction volume of 10 µL consisted of 1 ng cDNA, 3 µL DNase/RNase-free water (Takara), 1 µL primers (GENEWIZ, Suzhou, China), and 5 µL SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). After adding the reaction mixture to a 384-well plate, real-time PCR was performed using the QuantStudio 6 Flex (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA). Gene transcription levels were normalized to β-actin as a housekeeping gene. Gene expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔCt method, where ΔCt(treated) = [Ct(target gene) − Ct(β-actin)] and ΔCt(control) = [Ct(target gene) − Ct(β-actin)].

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from each sample using TRIzol reagent. The RNA libraries were prepared and sequenced on an Illumina Novaseq™ 6000 platform by LC Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). Data analysis and visualization were performed using R (https://www.r-project.org/) and the OmicStudio platform of LC Bio Technology Co., Ltd. (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool). Specifically, log processing was performed on all data obtained by RNA-seq. Then, after log pretreatment these data were processed by centralization, which refers to the value of a gene minus its mean across all samples.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed with RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China), containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche, Nutley, NJ) and PMSF (Beyotime), for 30 min on ice. The cell lysates were collected and centrifuged at 12,000×g for 20 min. The supernatant was then collected and heated in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) sample buffer at 95 °C for 10 min. Protein concentration in the supernatant was measured using the BCA protein assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). Protein samples were separated on a 10%~12% SDS-polyacrylamide gel and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked for 1 h with Tris-buffered saline containing 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Amresco, Solon, OH) and Tween 20 (TBST) at room temperature with gentle shaking. The protein bands were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed extensively with TBST (three times), incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature, and washed again with TBST (three times). The membranes were exposed to chemiluminescent detection (Millipore, Temecula, CA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Measurement of PD-L1 expression on MSCs

The cells were washed with pre-cold PBS and digested with pre-heated trypsin. The cell suspension was collected and centrifuged at 800 rpm for 5 min. The cells were then resuspended in staining buffer containing the PD-L1 antibody and incubated at 4 °C for 30 min. The expression of PD-L1 was detected using a flow cytometer.

T cell proliferation assay

The proliferation of activated splenocytes and PBMCs was measured using CFSE staining and flow cytometry. Freshly isolated splenocytes and PBMCs were stained with 5 µM CFSE dye for 10 min in a 37 °C water bath, with mixing every 2 min. The reaction was terminated by adding pre-cooled FBS. After centrifugation, the cells were resuspended in RPMI 1640 medium. Splenocytes or PBMCs were then stimulated with anti-CD3(produced in the laboratory) and cocultured with MSCs for 72 h. Subsequently, the suspended cells were collected and analyzed by flow cytometry (Beckman). Analyze the data using the FlowJo_10.8.1 software. Live lymphocytes were identified within the characteristic FSClow/SSClow region. Then dual exclusion was achieved through sequential gating: FSC-H vs. FSC-A, followed by SSC-H vs. SSC-A. Activated T cell populations exhibited increased FSC/SSC profiles compared to quiescent lymphocytes, consistent with blastogenesis (Supplementary Fig. 2A, B).

Murine acute liver injury model

Male C57BL/6J mice, aged 8 ~ 10 weeks, were subjected to acute liver injury induction via intravenous injection of 15 mg/kg Concanavalin A (ConA, Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Thirty minutes after ConA treatment, activated MSCs pretreated with IFNγ/TNFα w/o PARP inhibitor were intravenously administered to the mice. Then mice were euthanized 12 h later, and serum and liver samples were collected. Specifically, anesthetic (10 mL of the anesthetic contains 2.4 mL of 50 mg/mL ketamine, 0.8 mL of 20 mg/mL mefenthermine, and 6.8 mL of normal saline) was injected intraperitoneally at a dose of 80 µL per 10 g of body weight. After collecting the whole blood from the heart, the mice were subjected to cervical dislocation and tissue collection. Serum aminotransferase levels were measured using a detection kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China).

Murine colitis model

Male C57BL/6J mice, aged 8 ~ 10 weeks, were provided drinking water containing 4% DSS (MP Biomedicals, Santa Ana, CA) ad libitum for 7 days to induce colitis. Pre-stimulated MSCs with IFNγ/TNFα w/o PARP inhibitor were intravenously administered to the mice on Day 2 of DSS treatment. The body weight of each mouse was recorded daily throughout the induction of colitis. On Day 7 of DSS treatment, the mice were euthanized, and colon samples were collected. The disease activity index was calculated based on body weight loss (grades 0–4: 0, none; 1, < 10% loss of the initial body weight; 2, 10–15% loss of the initial body weight; 3, 15–20% loss of the initial body weight; and 4, > 20% loss of the initial body weight), stool consistency (grades 0–2: 0, none; 1, mild diarrhea; and 2, moderate to severe diarrhea), rectal bleeding (grades 0–2: 0, none; 1, mild bleeding; and 2, moderate to severe bleeding), and general activity (grades 0–2: 0, normal; 1, mildly depressed; and 2, moderately to severely depressed) [13].

H&E staining and histological analysis

Colon and liver tissue samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h and subsequently dehydrated through a graded ethanol series: 75% ethanol (1 h), 85% ethanol (1 h), 95% ethanol (40 min), and 100% ethanol (30 min). The samples were then treated with xylene twice for 20 min each before being embedded in paraffin. Finally, the samples were sectioned into 5-µm slices. Histological changes were analyzed using standard hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. The severity of colitis symptoms was assessed by evaluating the extent of bowel wall thickening (grades 0–3: 0, none; 1, mucosa; 2, mucosa and submucosa; and 3, transmural), crypt damage (grades 0–3: 0, none; 1, loss of goblet cells; 2, surface epithelium intact; and 3, complete loss of crypt and epithelium), and inflammatory cell infiltration (grades 0–2: 0, none; 1, mild to moderate; and 2, severe) in H&E-stained colon sections.

Statistical analysis

The experimental results were statistically analyzed using three or more biological replicates, with data analysis and plotting performed using GraphPad Prism software (Version 8.0, GraphPad Prism Software Inc., San Diego, CA). The data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The number of samples in each treatment group is indicated as “n” in the figure legends. For comparisons between two groups, a two-tailed unpaired t-test was applied. For comparisons between multiple groups, one-way analysis or two-way of variance (ANOVA) was used. Statistical significance was determined as follows: * p < 0.05; ** p < 0.01; *** p < 0.001; **** p < 0.0001.

Results

PARP loss of function enhances the expression of chemokines and immunomodulatory genes in inflammatory cytokine-primed mouse MSCs

PARP inhibitors have been utilized in the treatment of various inflammatory diseases because PARP functions to promote pro-inflammatory response in macrophages [14, 15]. Considering that systemic application of PARP inhibitors may affect various cell types in the inflammatory microenvironment, it is important to investigate whether PARP inhibitors may influence tissue microenvironments via non-immune cells, such as MSCs. Inflammatory factor-activated MSCs are known to produce ligands for CC-chemokine receptor 5 (CCR5) and CXC-chemokine receptor 3 (CXCR3), including CC-chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5), CXCL9, CXCL10, and CXCL11, which recruit T cells to the vicinity of MSCs and thus inhibit the proliferation and activity of T cells by expressing iNOS (in murine MSCs) or IDO1 (in human MSCs) [16–20].

Low concentrations (1 ~ 10 µM) of Rucaparib had no toxic effects on in vitro-expanded mouse MSCs (Supplementary Fig. 3A, B). Consistent with previous report of the pro-inflammatory function of PARP in macrophages, we observed that PARP inhibitor Rucaparib inhibited the expression of Cxcl9 and Nos2 in macrophages (Fig. 1A). Interestingly, when MSCs were co-treated with PARP inhibitor Rucaparib and IFNγ/TNFα, the expression of Cxcl9 and Nos2 was further upregulated compared to MSCs treated with only IFNγ/TNFα. (Fig. 1B). In addition to Cxcl9 and Nos2, Cxcl11and Ho1 were also further upregulated in Parp1-deficient mouse MSCs treated with IFNγ and TNFα (Fig. 1C). Moreover, the protein level of iNOS was also higher in Parp1-deficient mouse MSCs treated with IFNγ and TNFα (Fig. 1D). Additionally, the production of NO, which is cytotoxic to activated T cells, was enhanced in IFNγ/TNFα-stimulated MSCs with PARP1 loss of function (Fig. 1E). Taken together, these results suggest that PARP1 negatively regulates the immunomodulatory function of mouse MSCs.

PARP inhibitor enhances the expression of chemokines and Immunomodulatory genes in inflammatory cytokine-primed human MSCs

We further explored the effect of PARP inhibition on the immunosuppressive function on human MSCs. Firstly, low concentrations (1 ~ 10 µM) of Rucaparib did not reduce the viability of human MSCs, as in murine MSCs (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). Furthermore, IFNγ/TNFα-primed MSCs treated with Rucaparib at 10 µM exhibited the highest expression levels of chemokines CXCL9, CXC10, CXCL11 and immunosuppressive genes IDO1, TSG-6 and PD-L1 (Supplementary Fig. 4C, D). Inflammatory factors “license” MSCs, enabling them to regulate immune cells through increasingly producing immunosuppressive factors and chemokines [21]. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on MSCs treated with IFNγ and TNFα for 24 h, in the presence or absence of Rucaparib. PARP inhibition increased the expression of genes related to cellular response to IFNγ and TNFα, as indicated by gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) (Fig. 2A, B). The gene set about chemokine activity was also enriched in MSCs activated with Rucaparib and inflammatory factors (Fig. 2C). Specifically, Rucaparib treatment positively regulated the expression of chemokines CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL11 and anti-inflammatory mediators IDO1, TSG-6 and PD-L1 (Fig. 2D). Moreover, the elevated protein level of PD-L1 in MSCs stimulated with PARP inhibitor and inflammatory factors was also detected by flow cytometry (Fig. 2E). In the MSCs from dental pulp that were treated with inflammatory factors in the presence or absence of PARP inhibitor, we also obtained consistent findings (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B). Furthermore, IDO1 level gradually increased in inflammatory cytokine-primed MSCs treated with Rucaparib over a 48-hour period, at both transcript and protein levels (Fig. 2F, G). Similarly, in MSCs stimulated with Olaparib, another PARP inhibitor, and inflammatory factors, we also observed the upregulation of IDO1, TSG-6 and PD-L1(Supplementary Fig. 6A-C). The increased expression of immunosuppressive molecules in cytokine primed-MSCs was accompanied by enhanced glycolysis [22]. In this study, we also detected increased glucose uptake (Supplementary Fig. 7). Collectively, these results suggest that inhibition of PARP activity can enhance the expression of chemokines and immunomodulatory genes in human inflammatory cytokine-primed MSCs.

PARP Inhibition promotes the immunosuppressive effects of MSCs on T cells

We next aimed to investigate whether PARP inhibition could enhance the immunoregulatory effects of MSCs on T cells. Human and murine MSCs are known to inhibit T cells proliferation via IDO1 and iNOS, respectively [16, 23, 24]. To elucidate the immunoregulatory effects of human-derived MSCs on murine T cell proliferation, we first assessed the protein levels of IDO1 in MSCs at various time points after removal of human inflammatory factors and PARP inhibitor using Western blotting. The results demonstrated that the IDO1 levels in MSCs pretreated with IFNγ and TNFα, in combination with Rucaparib, remained consistently higher than in those pretreated with IFNγ and TNFα alone (Fig. 3A, B). Subsequently, we co-cultured inflammatory factor-primed MSCs with activated splenocytes at a 1:20 ratio for 72 h. The proliferation of splenocytes was evaluated using the carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE) dilution assay. As shown in Fig. 3C, primed MSCs inhibited splenocyte proliferation, and this inhibitory effect was significantly enhanced after Rucaparib co-treatment.

In the co-culture system of MSCs and activated human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), IFNγ and TNFα secreted by activated PBMCs can re-stimulate MSCs. To simulate this scenario, MSCs were re-stimulated with IFNγ and TNFα after an initial treatment with inflammatory factors, in the presence or absence of Rucaparib. In these treatments, the highest levels of IDO1 were observed in the group initially treated with inflammatory cytokines and Rucaparib (Fig. 3D-F). Moreover, IFNγ/TNFα-primed MSCs demonstrated more pronounced suppressive effects on the activation and proliferation of T cells in PBMCs following PARP inhibition (Fig. 3G). Collectively, these data suggest that PARP inhibition can enhance the immunosuppressive effects of MSCs on T cells.

PARP Inhibition enhances the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs in inflammatory diseases

MSCs are widely utilized in the treatment of inflammatory diseases due to their potent immunoregulatory capabilities [3–5]. In this study, we employed the concanavalin A (ConA)-induced acute liver injury model and the dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) model to investigate the effects of PARP inhibition on the immunomodulatory function of MSCs in vivo. The ConA-induced liver injury model is a well-established in vivo system of hepatocyte apoptosis triggered by acute adaptive immune responses, with excessive T lymphocytes proliferation being the primary contributing factor [25]. Inflammatory cytokine-primed MSCs treated with PARP inhibitor significantly alleviated liver damage, resulting in marked reductions in serum aspartate transaminase (AST) levels and centrilobular necrosis, compared to MSCs treated with IFNγ and TNFα alone (Fig. 4A-C).

We next assessed the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs in DSS-induced IBD. Experimental mice were fed 4% DSS for 7 days, and on day 2, MSCs were administered to the diseased mice (Fig. 4D). As expected, compared to MSCs primed with IFNγ/TNFα alone, MSCs co-treated with Rucaparib significantly improved clinical parameters, including body weight and the disease activity index (Fig. 4E, F). These MSC-treated IBD mice also exhibited significantly less reduction in colon length (Fig. 4G). Furthermore, the extent of bowel wall thickening and crypt damage was notably reduced in the colons of mice treated with inflammatory factor-primed MSCs combined with PARP inhibition, as demonstrated by histological examination (Fig. 4H). In summary, these data suggest that PARP inhibition enhances the therapeutic efficacy of MSCs in inflammatory diseases.

PARP Inhibition promotes STAT1 activation to strengthen immunosuppressive function of MSCs

Inflammatory factors IFNγ and TNFα induce the expression of various immunosuppressive molecules and modulate the immunoregulatory properties of MSCs through the JAK-STAT and NF-κB signaling pathways [2, 26–29]. PARP1, a enzyme present in the nucleus of eukaryotic cells, regulates cell fate and function by acting as a co-activator or repressor of transcription factors through its catalytic product, PAR [30–33]. NF-κB is one of the target transcription factor for PARlytation in macrophages [13]. IκB is an inhibitory protein of NF-κB. The activation and degradation of IκB contribute to the nuclear translocation of p65. However, in our study, processes related to the regulation of IκB were not enriched in inflammatory cytokine-primed MSCs treated with Rucaparib (Supplementary Fig. 8A-C). Additionally, the phosphorylation level of serine 536 on P65 was not further enhanced (Supplementary Fig. 8D). Although NF-κB activation can lead to phosphorylation of STAT3 [26], no increases were observed in the phosphorylation levels of tyrosine 705 on STAT3 (Supplementary Fig. 8E).

However, genes related to the JAK-STAT signaling pathway were enriched in MSCs co-treated with the PARP inhibitor and inflammatory factors, as shown by GSEA (Fig. 5A). Moreover, Western blot analysis revealed that PARP inhibition significantly increased the phosphorylation level of tyrosine 701, but not serine 727, on STAT1 in IFNγ/TNFα-primed MSCs treated with the PARP inhibitor or in Parp1-deficient MSCs (Fig. 5B, C, and Supplementary Fig. 8F). Furthermore, we assessed the phosphorylation level of tyrosine 701 on STAT1 in the nucleus and cytoplasm of MSCs treated with inflammatory factors and the PARP inhibitor over various durations. Western blotting analysis showed that the phosphorylation of STAT1 in both the nucleus and cytoplasm of MSCs treated with the PARP inhibitor and inflammatory factors was higher at all time points compared to MSCs activated with inflammatory factors alone. Notably, MSCs co-treated with the PARP inhibitor and inflammatory factors for a short duration exhibited significantly enhanced tyrosine phosphorylation of STAT1 (Fig. 5D). To further investigate the role of STAT1 in promoting the immunomodulatory effects of inflammatory cytokine-primed MSCs following PARP inhibition, we treated MSCs with the STAT1 inhibitor Nifuroxazide or STAT1 small interfering RNA. As shown in Fig. 5E, in the presence of Nifuroxazide, the IDO1 induction by inflammatory factors and Rucaparib was greatly attenuated. Similarly, in STAT1-knockdown MSCs, the inflammatory factors and the PARP inhibitor also failed to elevate IDO1 (Fig. 5F). Collectively, these results demonstrate that PARP inhibition enhances the immunomodulatory effects of inflammatory cytokine-primed MSCs in a STAT1-dependent manner.

Discussion

PARP inhibitors have been widely used to treat various types of cancers. In addition to their utilization in cancer therapy, PARP inhibitors are also employed to modulate immune responses and promote tissue repair in inflammatory diseases [7–9, 11]. These inhibitors block the progression of inflammatory diseases by suppressing the pro-inflammatory responses of immune cells, particularly macrophages [14, 15]. Beyond their effects on macrophages and differentiation capacity of MSCs [32], PARP inhibitors are here shown to also enhance the immunosuppressive function of MSCs. Unlike their action in macrophages, where PARP inhibitors attenuate the pro-inflammatory response by preventing STAT1 phosphorylation [15], PARP inhibition in MSCs instead promotes STAT1 phosphorylation, leading to increased expression of immunosuppressive molecules. This enhances the immunoregulatory function of MSCs on T cells and augments their therapeutic efficacy in inflammatory diseases including acute liver injury induced by ConA and colitis induced by DSS. In the case of ConA-induced acute liver injury, which is characterized by three distinct phases—acute phase (0–4 h), intermediate phase (4–8 h), and effector phase (8–12 h), T cells are the main pathogenic cells. During the effector phase (8–12 h), TCR-mediated T cell activation triggers the overproduction of IFNγ and TNFα, culminating in hepatocyte apoptosis and a significant increase in transaminase secretion. As illustrated in Figs. 3 and 4, MSCs pre-stimulated with a PARP inhibitor and inflammatory factors indeed possess a stronger ability to suppress T cells.

Our findings also have implications in understanding the development of resistance to PARP inhibitors in anti-tumor treatments [34, 35]. As a class of widely used anticancer agents, PARP inhibitors face a major therapeutic challenge in acquired drug resistance. PARP inhibition was shown to stabilize FOXP3 and enhance the immunosuppressive function of regulatory T cells [31]. Our findings suggest that PARP inhibitors may also confer MSC-like tumor-associated fibroblasts increased immunosuppressive functions. Emerging evidence indicates that PARP inhibitor treatment induces the upregulation of immunosuppressive biomarkers, such as PD-L1, not only in tumor cells but also in stromal components [33]. This PD-L1 upregulation may further facilitate immune escape.

The PARP protein family plays a crucial role in DNA repair and the maintenance of genomic stability by adding single or covalently linked polymers of ADP-ribose moieties to PARP itself and nuclear target proteins, a process known as poly (ADP-ribosyl) ation (PARylation), by utilizing nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+). Of the 17 members in humans and 16 members in mice, PARP1 alone accounts for approximately 90% of all NAD+ consumption by the PARP family [36]. PARP inhibitors, which are niacinamide analogs, compete for binding to PARP [37]. The intracellular NAD+ level influences glycolysis-dependent immune regulation in inflammatory cytokine-primed MSCs. MSCs treated with inflammatory factors require glycolytic function to exert immunosuppressive effects, which consumes a large amount of NAD+. In addition, inflammatory factor-treated MSCs also activate PARP, further consuming NAD+ (data not shown). The use of PARP inhibitors preserves some NAD+, which may then be used for glycolysis. We also detected an increase in glycolytic levels [13, 22, 38]. However, although glycolysis was enhanced in IFNγ/TNFα-activated MSCs treated with PARP inhibitors, no significant decrease in intracellular NAD+ was detected (data not shown), suggesting that the NAD+ pool is spared by the inhibition of PARP and thus could sustain glycolysis more robustly.

PARP1 also regulates gene expression through the PARylation of transcription factors [39]. PARP1 interacts with NF-κB or STAT1 via its catalytic product, PAR, thereby contributing to the activation of the NF-κB or STAT1 pathway in macrophages [14, 15]. In addition to positively regulating transcription, PARP1 also negatively regulates the activities of certain transcription factors. For instance, during the differentiation of adipose precursor cells into adipocytes, PARylation of C/EBPβ by PARP1 impairs its ability to bind DNA, thereby inhibiting the adipogenic transcriptional program [32, 40]. Furthermore, the transcription factor Foxp3 can be ADP-ribosylated by PARP1, leading to its degradation in regulatory T cells [31]. In tumor cells, the expression of PD-L1 is inhibited through the PARylation of STAT3 [33]. In MSCs treated with IFNγ, TNFα and PARP inhibitor, genes related to the cellular response to these inflammatory cytokines were enriched, as shown by GSEA. TNFα can also activate the JAK-STAT signaling pathway [41]. However, we only detected the phosphorylation of the tyrosine 701 site of STAT1 in activated MSCs, which notably differed from the PARylation-induced phosphorylation of the serine 727 site of STAT1 in IFNγ-treated macrophages [14]. During the progression of inflammation, PARP1 may play distinct regulatory roles in different cell types. In inflammatory factor-primed MSCs, PARP1 may influence the activation of STAT1 through its catalytic product, PAR, thereby modulating the expression of STAT1-dependent immunosuppressive genes. These findings also provide a direction for improving the efficacy of MSCs in treating inflammatory diseases.

Conclusion

Our study reveals a boosting role of PARP inhibition in the immunosuppressive function of MSCs. We found that phosphorylation at the tyrosine 701 site of STAT1 was increased in inflammatory factor-primed MSCs treated with Rucaparib. PARP inhibition enhanced the expression of STAT1-mediated immunosuppressive genes in MSCs, thereby strengthening their regulatory function on immune cells (Fig. 6). These findings establish a link between PARP and the immunosuppressive function of MSCs, offering a novel approach to improving the therapeutic application of MSCs in autoimmune and hyperinflammatory diseases.

A schematic diagram illustrating the promotion of MSC immunosuppressive function and therapeutic efficacy by PARP inhibition. Pretreatment of MSCs with IFNγ and TNFα triggers PARP activation, leading to poly ADP-ribose (PAR) synthesis. The accumulated PAR may interact with STAT1, thereby attenuating STAT1 phosphorylation and compromising the immunosuppressive capacity of MSCs. The blue components illustrate the inhibitory cascade where cytokine-primed PARP activation in MSCs limits STAT1 phosphorylation, reduces expression of immunosuppressive molecules such as IDO1, PD-L1 and TSG-6, and diminishes therapeutic efficacy in inflammatory conditions. Conversely, the pink components indicate that pharmacological PARP inhibition facilitates STAT1 activation, enhances immunosuppressive molecule expression, and improves disease-modifying outcomes in inflammatory models through blockade of this PAR-STAT1 interaction pathway
