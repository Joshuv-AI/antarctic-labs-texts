In-Hospital and Long-Term Clinical Outcomes in Patients With Acute Myocardial Infarction Categorized as SCAI Shock Stage B
Authors: Hisashi Sato, Kenichi Sakakura, Hiroyuki Jinnouchi, Yousuke Taniguchi, Kei Yamamoto, Takunori Tsukui, Masashi Hatori, Taku Kasahara, Yusuke Watanabe, Shun Ishibashi, Masaru Seguchi, Hideo Fujita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe mortality rate for acute myocardial infarction (AMI) complicated with cardiogenic shock (CS) remains high despite advances in mechanical circulatory support (MCS). The Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) has proposed a 5-stage classification of CS, and in this study we compared the clinical outcomes of AMI patients with SCAI shock stage A with those in stage B.Methods and ResultsWe retrospectively analyzed 257 AMI patients, classified into modified stage A (n=180) and modified stage B (n=77) groups. The primary endpoint was in-hospital adverse events, defined as a composite of in-hospital death, initiation of MCS, and mechanical complications. The secondary endpoint was long-term major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including all-cause death, re-admission for heart failure, or non-fatal myocardial infarction. The median follow-up duration was 786 days. Compared with modified stage A, modified stage B group had a significantly lower left ventricular ejection fraction (44.6% vs. 54.4%, P<0.001). In-hospital adverse events were comparable between the groups (P=0.128). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed a higher incidence of MACE in modified stage B (P=0.029); however, multivariate analyses revealed no significant association between modified stage B and either in-hospital adverse events or long-term MACE.ConclusionsAs compared to SCAI shock modified stage A, modified stage B was not independently associated with in-hospital and long-term clinical outcomes after controlling confounding factors.

Central Figure

Although mechanical circulatory support (MCS) has become widely used and optimal medical therapy has advanced in recent years, cardiogenic shock (CS) remains associated with the highest in-hospital mortality rates, reportedly >30%.1–5 Among various cardiovascular diseases, acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is the most common cause of CS, accounting for 62–81% of CS cases.1,2,4 Given the persistently high mortality rate for CS, numerous studies have investigated this condition from multiple perspectives to improve patient outcomes, including the importance of the first medical contact, early identification of shock etiology and severity, and prompt therapeutic strategies.4,6–9 These efforts have highlighted the potential benefits of early MCS initiation (e.g., pre-PCI support), as well as adjunctive tools such as ultrasound for early diagnosis.6,7,10,11 However, because the definition of CS varies widely among studies, the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAI) proposed their shock stage classification,12 in which early recognition of CS and prompt MCS at SCAI shock stage C are recommended.12,13 Despite these efforts in early recognition and intervention, the mortality rate for CS remains high, prompting discussion about the potential benefits of proactive intervention for patients classified as SCAI shock stage B.14,15 However, it remains unclear whether AMI patients in SCAI shock stage B have a poorer prognosis than those in SCAI shock stage A. In this retrospective study we compared the in-hospital and long-term clinical outcomes between AMI patients with SCAI shock stage A and shock stage B.

Methods

SCAI Shock Stage Classification

The SCAI proposed their shock stage classification in 2019, and it was subsequently updated in 2022.12 Patients are divided into 5 stages (A–E), based on physical examination findings, laboratory data, vital signs and hemodynamic status. Because physical examination findings are subjective and difficult to verify retrospectively, in this study we used only objective data for SCAI shock stage classification: specifically, blood pressure, heart rate and lactate levels. Classification was based on the very first set of vital signs and lactate levels obtained upon arrival at the emergency department (ED). Moreover, all analyses in the present study were performed according to the patients’ status on arrival, even if the stage migrated within the first few hours after ED arrival.

Study Patients

We retrospectively reviewed all patients with AMI treated at Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University) between January 2015 and December 2023.

The inclusion criterion was AMI diagnosis. The exclusion criteria were: (1) cardiac arrest prior to hospital arrival, (2) not undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for the culprit lesion of AMI, (3) lactate levels not measured on arrival at hospital, (4) lactate level ≥2.0 mmol/L (lactate levels ≥2.0 mmol/L classified as SCAI shock classification stage C, D or E), and (5) experiencing ≥2 AMI during the study period, which was regarded as a clinical event.

The final study population was divided into SCAI shock stage A (modified stage A group) or SCAI shock stage B (modified stage B group) according to the vital signs on arrival. We used the term “modified”, because our classification did not incorporate physical examination findings and thus would not be identical to the original SCAI classification. In the final study population, patients who had a systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg or a pulse rate ≥100 beats/min on arrival were assigned to the modified stage B group. All remaining patients were assigned to the modified stage A group. In the definition of the SCAI shock stage, AMI patients are considered at risk for CS (≥stage A) even if the patient’s vital signs were stable on arrival.12

The primary endpoint was in-hospital adverse events, which included in-hospital death, initiation of MCS (intra-aortic balloon pumping, percutaneous left ventricular assist device, and extracorporeal membrane oxygenation), and mechanical complications (ventricular septal perforation, papillary muscle rupture, or free wall rupture). The secondary endpoints were the occurrence of long-term major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), which was defined as a composite of all-cause death, re-admission for heart failure, and non-fatal MI. Clinical events were identified from the information available in electronic medical records. Because all patients who underwent PCI for AMI at Saitama Medical Centre are scheduled to attend annual outpatient follow-up visits, corresponding documentation was available. The day of hospital admission was defined as the index day (day 1). Patients were followed up until the occurrence of long-term MACE or until the study end date (22 October 2024).

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University. The requirement for written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective study design. The data collection and storage were conducted anonymously in accordance with the guidelines of the Japan Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare.

Definitions

AMI was defined according to the universal definition.16,17 The definitions of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia were described in previous studies.18,19 Laboratory data obtained at admission were used. If arterial lactate levels were not measured, venous lactate levels were used instead.20 Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was assessed by transthoracic echocardiography performed during the hospitalization.21 The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using serum creatinine (Cr) level, age, and sex based on the following formulas: eGFR = 194 × Cr−1.094 × age−0.287 (male); eGFR = 194 × Cr−1.094 × age−0.287 × 0.739 (female).22 The first and final Thrombolysis In Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) flow grades were based on the coronary angiographic findings.23

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented as percentages for categorical variables or means±standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed continuous variables and median (quartile 1–quartile 3) for nonparametric continuous variables. Categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentages) and were compared using Fisher’s exact test. For continuous variables, the Shapiro-Wilk test was performed to determine whether the continuous variables were normally distributed or not. Nonparametric continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test and otherwise using Student’s t-test. Event-free survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method, and statistical differences between the curves were assessed using the log-lank test. For the evaluation of the association between modified stage B and in-hospital adverse events, multivariate logistic regression analysis using the force entry method was performed to find the odds ratio of modified stage B (vs. modified stage A) after confounding for age and sex. Moreover, for the evaluation of the association between modified stage B and long-term MACE, multivariate Cox hazard analysis was performed. Variables that were significantly different (P<0.05) between the modified stage A and modified stage B groups were included in the model as confounding factors. Variables with missing data were excluded from the multivariate model. Furthermore, when ≥2 similar variables were present, only 1 was selected to avoid multicollinearity. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were calculated. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using statistical software, SPSS version 24.0/Windows (SPSS, Chicago, ILL, USA).

Results

From January 2015 to December 2023, a total of 2,525 patients with AMI were admitted and after excluding 2,268 patients based on the predefined exclusion criteria, 257 patients were included in the final study population: modified stage A group (n=180) and modified stage B group (n=77). Discussion

In this study we evaluated the data for 180 patients in SCAI shock modified stage A and 77 patients in modified stage B, and compared their clinical outcomes. Over a median follow-up of 786 days, in-hospital adverse events were comparable between the 2 groups; however, patients with modified stage B shock had a significantly higher incidence of long-term MACE than those with modified stage A shock, which might, at least in part, be attributed to the distinct clinical characteristics of the patients with modified stage B shock. Nevertheless, the significance of this difference was lost after multivariate adjustment. Taken together, our findings do not provide sufficient evidence to justify early intervention solely on the basis of SCAI shock stage B.

First, it is important to highlight the differences between the present study and previous research. It is well known that patients in SCAI shock stages C, D, and E have high in-hospital mortality rates, and current guidelines recommend early recognition and prompt consideration of MCS. However, patients in stages A and B, the so-called “pre-shock” states, may also experience significant mortality rates. For example, a 30-day mortality rate of 21.4% has been reported among patients with stage B,24 and some experts have suggested that the need for MCS should be evaluated in patients with stage B.15 However, the clinical differences between stages A and B have not yet been well clarified, which motivated us to focus on a direct comparison of clinical outcomes between stage A and stage B on arrival at hospital. Several retrospective studies showed that higher in-hospital or 30-day mortality rates were associated with more advanced SCAI shock stages.12,25–29 However, the study populations and clinical settings in those studies were different from those in our study. For example, Jentzer et al. investigated patients admitted to cardiac intensive care units and reported the in-hospital mortality rate as 7.1% for patients with stage B.25 Pareek et al. revealed that the mortality rate was high in patients who experienced out-of-hospital cardiac arrest and that higher SCAI shock stages were associated with increased mortality.25,27 Those studies either included patients with cardiac arrest or focused exclusively on patients in SCAI shock stages C, D or E, which were traditionally recognized as CS. Furthermore, many of the studies included patients with cardiac conditions other than AMI. Therefore, the patient population classified as stage A was heterogeneous in previous studies. There has not been a study that conducted a direct comparison between AMI patients with stage A and those with stage B, especially in a Japanese patient population. Because medical systems differ between Japan and other countries, our study focusing on Japanese population is scientifically noteworthy. Furthermore, we did not include patients with cardiac arrest and included only patients who could undergo PCI, many of whom might experience hemodynamic improvement following PCI. As a result, the in-hospital mortality rate in the present study was 3.9% for patients with modified stage B, which was lower than reported in previous studies (ranging from 7.1% to 33.9%).25–27 Additionally, a unique strength of our study is the extended follow-up (median of 786 days), which allowed us to evaluate long-term outcomes more comprehensively than prior studies.

Second, we should discuss how to interpret our findings. Compared with the modified stage A group, patients in the modified stage B group had lower eGFR, lower LVEF, higher HbA1c, higher CRP, and higher BNP levels, which may reflect a higher burden of comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus and heart failure in the modified stage B group. Moreover, coronary artery disease in the modified stage B group appeared to be more severe and complex than in the modified stage A group, as shown by more CTO lesions in the non-culprit arteries and the requirement for larger guide-catheter sizes. This speculation might be supported by the proportion of IABP use in modified stage B patients being more than twice that in modified stage A patients (12.0% vs. 5.6%). Most MCS were introduced before PCI when needed, and the timing of PCI was significantly later in modified stage B patients than in those in modified stage A. Greater lesion complexity as well as the presence of more comorbidities in the modified stage B patients might explain the delay in PCI. It may be reasonable to pay more attention to culprit lesion complexity and clinical background when treating AMI patients in modified stage B shock.

The features of the modified stage B group may explain why the Kaplan-Meier curves demonstrated a greater incidence of long-term MACE in this group, although the significant difference disappeared after controlling for confounding factors in multivariate analysis. Thus, modified stage B alone was not identified as an independent prognostic factor, suggesting that the poor outcomes were caused by underlying clinical characteristics rather than the stage classification itself. Furthermore, the time course of clinical deterioration might play an important role. Ton et al. revealed that >50% of stage B patients experienced shock stage progression within 24 h of admission.29 Morici et al. demonstrated that the SCAI shock classification at 24 h could be an independent predictor of in-hospital death.14 In our study, the classification was based on clinical status at the time of admission. Therefore, we may have included patients who progressed to stage C at a later time as modified stage B.

The clinical implications of the present study should be noted. Because modified stage B of the SCAI shock classification was not identified as an independent predictor of long-term MACE or in-hospital death, our findings do not support the need for early intervention solely based on a modified stage B. However, our results indicated that modified stage B patients tended to have more severe comorbidities. Therefore, if patients with AMI are classified as modified stage B, their clinical background, including heart failure, should be carefully evaluated for signs of the development of more severe CS. Close monitoring is warranted for patients with modified stage B on admission, because some may require MCS after admission.

There are several study limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective observational study, there is an inherent risk of selection bias. The selection of variables for the multivariate Cox hazard model might be arbitrary in a retrospective study. In addition, because lactate levels are not routinely measured in all patients, many cases were excluded, which might introduce further selection bias. In fact, a substantial number of patients with modified SCAI stage A or B might be excluded because of the lack of data on lactate levels. Moreover, the relatively small sample size might increase the possibility of type 2 error. Second, the inclusion of the initiation of MCS as a component of the primary endpoint might be influenced by our clinical practice patterns. In other words, the initiation of MCS might not be an objective endpoint. Third, because physical examination findings were not incorporated into the classification process due to concerns about their lack of objectivity in a retrospective setting, there is a possibility of misclassification between SCAI modified stage A and modified stage B. This limitation may partly explain the comparable in-hospital mortality rates observed between the 2 groups in the present study, whereas previous reports have shown inconsistent mortality rates across SCAI stages.24,28,30 Indeed, most of the previous reports were retrospective in nature, making precise differentiation between early shock stages challenging. Furthermore, the unique patient selection process in the present study, such as the inclusion of only patients who underwent PCI and the exclusion of those with out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, might have contributed to discrepancies in reported outcomes. Fourth, lactate levels were obtained from either arterial blood or venous blood samples. van Tienhoven et al.20 reviewed the differences between sampling methods and concluded that venous lactate levels are generally higher than arterial levels. However, they also noted that a venous lactate level cutoff of 2 mmol/L served as a reasonable indicator of arterial hyperlactemia, which coincides with the threshold between SCAI shock classification stages B and C. Therefore, we considered it acceptable to substitute venous lactate levels for arterial ones in our analysis. Fifth, because serial lactate measurements were not routinely performed, the transition among SCAI shock stages over time, which would be useful for predicting patients’ prognosis,29,30 could not be assessed. Finally, eGFR was calculated from serum creatinine levels on admission, which could be influenced by hemodynamic status. Because we could not obtain true baseline serum creatinine levels (i.e., serum creatinine levels before AMI admission), there is a possibility of over-adjustment in the multivariate Cox hazard analysis, which included eGFR for adjustment.

Conclusions

As compared to SCAI modified stage A shock, patients with modified stage B shock was not independently associated with in-hospital or long-term clinical outcomes after controlling for confounding factors.
