Path Loss Dataset from Field Measurements at 3.5 GHz for the Fifth Generation of Wireless Communications in Indoor Environments
Authors: Patricia Perdomo-Reyes, Giselle M. Galvan-Tejada, Amilcar Meneses-Viveros
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wireless communications are today an important mechanism to connect people, machines and things. There are different uses of this technology like security, business, health, education, entertainment, agriculture among many others. In any case, the operational frequency and the scenario where wireless systems are deployed dictate the radio wave propagation conditions and therefore the quality of the communication signals. The path loss refers to the attenuation that wireless signals experiment between pairs of transmitter-receiver and is the basic parameter of performance of wireless communications. In this study, we reported and share different datasets of path loss derived from a campaign of field measurements conducted in three indoor environments. The operational frequency used in this campaign was 3.5 GHz, which is one of the frequency bands of interest for the fifth generation of wireless communications and beyond. The followed strategy to gather data is described in detail, including measurements parameters like equipment, antennas, dimensions and materials of each scenario and the grids used to locate the reception points.

Background & Summary

The transmission of information has recently experienced an exponential increase. This is due to the proliferation of connected devices on the Web, online platforms, and large data centers. Examples of information exchange scenarios are social networks, cloud services, streaming platforms, and daily compilations executed by companies for data analysis and decision-making. The current fifth generation of mobile networks (5G) has further encouraged this exchange, since it offers improvements in speed, latency, capacity, and connectivity1. Recently, communications have not only been oriented towards services for human beings, but also between machines and devices with sensors or actuators, forming what is well known as Internet of Things (IoT). The integration of Artificial Intelligence (AI) techniques and Wireless Sensor Networks (WSNs) is today a route towards the next sixth generation of wireless communications (6G) projected to 20302. Furthermore, much more information exchange is expected by 2040 with the proliferation of virtual and augmented reality3.

Given the importance of wireless technology, many efforts have been made for decades to understand the behavior of the communication channel given the characteristics of the medium where radio signals propagate and its influence on the performance and quality of communication systems operating wirelessly. Parameters associated with radio channel behavior include path loss, delay spread, Doppler shift, coherence bandwidth, and coherence time. From these, path loss (average attenuation that experiments a signal from a transmitter to a receiver independently of temporal dynamics of environments) is the basis to determine the coverage and the quality that a wireless device can achieve. Classic examples of path loss models are the famous Hata-Okumura model4 for outdoors used by different operators around the world during the early generations of mobile communications at the 800/900 MHz frequency band, and the Motley-Keenan multi-wall model for indoor communications operating at 900/1700 MHz5. The multi-wall model considers attenuation introduced by each wall (e.g.6,7 reported attenuation values for several materials at different frequencies) and continues being a reference model8.

Through the evolution of other wireless technologies and generations of mobile networks, the use of a variety of frequencies has been explored and implemented at different portions of the electromagnetic spectrum from UHF to the known as mmWave or even at THz. In any case, many deterministic and empirical (measurement-based) models have been proposed in the open literature for a diversity of scenarios like outdoor, indoor, urban, suburban, rural, etc. For example, measurements of received power were conducted at 2.4 GHz in9 whose results were implemented in an algorithm to select a physical layer for Bluetooth technology.

Regarding path loss modeling, two recent surveys of 5G technology operating at mmWave can be found in10,11. This frequency band has attracted a lot of attention because of the wide bandwidths providing higher data rates, which is interesting not only for applications of wireless communications, but also for broadcasting12. In contrast to this band, there is a frequency band below 6 GHz that has been widely considered for 5G indoor communications as a good trade-off between coverage and data rate: 3.5 GHz. Research on this frequency band has been reported, both for outdoors and indoors, including experimental findings, e.g.13–20 and propagation models have been derived based on linear regression analysis. Moreover, comparisons of results at this frequency band with those at 2.6 GHz21 and with upper frequencies22,23 have also been published.

Given the importance of accounting with datasets at this frequency band for indoor scenarios, we present here information of datasets generated from measurement campaigns conducted in indoor environments at 3.5 GHz which represent the key contribution of this work. It is so because, to the best of our knowledge, no references have been found in the open literature with public repositories to consult this type of information. In fact, references13–20 do not provide any dataset or repository at all. From them, only15–17 present tables with results averaged over 100 snapshots per measurement point or over very few points per scenario. Fig. 1 presents a schematic overview detailing the methodology employed in this study, which consisted of moving a receiver through a grid of points and receiving a 3.5 GHz signal (at each point) coming from a transmitter situated at a strategic location. This methodology was implemented in three scenarios for two antenna configurations each. Thus, we generated six datasets of levels of received power as a function of the distance between transmitter and receiver. In addition, we calculated the path loss for each case, so we obtained other six datasets. Methods

Experimental design

The experiment was made up by generating a dataset of levels of received power gathered through field measurements. These measurements were conducted in three indoor scenarios at the Center for Research and Advanced Studies (Cinvestav) in Mexico City, Mexico. The testbed for each scenario is organized by a transmitter located at a certain position radiating a continuous wave (CW) signal at 3.5 GHz, whereas the receiver was moving through a discrete grid of points where a lecture is recorded per point. In this way, the dataset of levels of received power is related to each distance between transmitter and receiver. Two antenna configurations were considered in each of the three scenarios. On the one hand, both transmitter and receiver antennas were set at the same height, which we reefer as Configuration 1 (C1). On the other hand, the transmitter and receiver antennas were installed at different heights, which we named Configuration 2 (C2). In other words, we conduct six measurement campaigns, two for each scenario, hence, we have six datasets. Finally, from the dataset of levels of received power, we straightforwardly determined a dataset of path losses as a function of the distances in each grid for each measurement campaign. It is worth mentioning that other authors could conduct their studies based on the data shared here for scenarios similar to those described below. This is a common practice in scientific research, as can be seen in24–26, for instance. In fact, the famous empirical Okumura-Hata model generated from experimental data in Japan was widely adopted by mobile operators in other countries27.

Experimental settings

In order to conduct the field measurement campaigns, a combination of high-precision instruments and specific devices were used to guarantee the accuracy and reliability of the collected data. The settings of these instruments and devices are exposed in what follows: Transmitter. The transmitter used to generate the CW signal during the measurements was the N5181A MXG Analog Signal Generator, which is a high-performance signal generator manufactured by Agilent Technologies (now Keysight Technologies). This generator has a wide range of working frequencies, which goes from 100 kHz up to 6 GHz with a resolution of 0.1 Hz. This transmitter was tuned at 3.5 GHz and its power was set to 10 dBm.Receiver. To capture the signal of interest, the Agilent Technologies N9343C handheld spectrum analyzer was used. This is a portable device that offers wide coverage of frequencies (1 MHz to 13.6 GHz). The spectrum analyzer was configured as explained in what follows. The central frequency was set at 3.5 GHz, where the signal of interest was expected to be received. The attenuation was set to 0 dB, the reference level to −50 dBm, and the span to 50 kHz. These values were used to guaranty that the received power levels are not affected by the background noise of the spectrum analyzer, mainly to longest distances where signal is weaker. We kept these values for almost all points of the grid in each scenario in order to have uniform settings during the measurement campaigns. It should be noted that at locations close to the transmitting base station, approximately 3 m away, where the received signal is much stronger, the reference level of the spectrum analyzer was raised to −30 dBm to ensure that the measured signal was kept within the dynamic range of the spectrum analyzer and avoid its saturation, measuring the signal with greater precision. This action did not affect the data collection. Additionally, an average of 5 samples was taken to show the value of power received by the equipment. This setting was selected to ensure accurate capture of the desired signal, minimizing interference from other signals and noise present in the environment. The use of an average of multiple samples allows obtaining a more stable and representative signal, reducing the variability that can be introduced by instantaneous fluctuations.Antennas. For both equipment, transmitter and receiver, Pasternak antennas model PE51110 were used. These devices are omnidirectional antennas with vertical polarization, high gain, robust and light. They offer a nominal gain of 5 dBi in the frequency range from 3.3 GHz to 3.8 GHz, so they were suitable for the frequency at which measurements were conducted. The characteristic impedance of the antenna is 50 Ω, matching the output impedance of the N5181A MXG Analog Signal Generator and the input impedance of the N9343C Handheld Spectrum Analyzer. As was mentioned before, two antenna configurations were considered per scenario, based on the heights for the transmitter (htx) and the receiver (hrx): for C1 the height for both transmitter and receiver antenna was of 1.5 m; whereas for C2 the transmitter antenna was installed at a height of htx = 1.925 m and the receiver antenna at a height of hrx = 0.71 m. Data acquisition assays

Once we have exposed details of each scenario and the experimental settings, let us describe the data acquisition stage. First of all, it was necessary to define the place for the transmitter, for which a coverage test was conducted in each scenario. This test consisted of installing the transmitter in candidate locations and verifying that the signal radiated by it is properly received at the most extreme possible points in the study area. A threshold of −115 dBm of received power was considered as reference, in such a way that if for a given position of the transmitter most of extreme points were able to receive the signal above this threshold, this transmitter location was used for the measurements campaigns. In a second step, it was necessary to define the reception points. For the Communications Section and SSE students building, a resolution of 1 × 1 m was used in their grid, whereas for the irregular shape of the Central Library a resolution of 1.355 × 1.355 m was used taking as a reference the flagstone size on the floor. Please note that for the irregular shape of the Central Library we followed the floor flagstone (1.355 × 1.355 m size) distribution to have a proper guide to form the corresponding grid of reception points; please also note that this resolution was similar to that used in other buildings, so it did not impact the data collection. Figures 7, 8, and 9 also show the grid (green points) on sketch’s of each scenario where measurements were planned to be conducted. Please observe that each point is identified by its coordinates given by a capital letter and a number. For example, for the Communications Section scenario the transmitter is at (E,29), and for the SSE students building the transmitter is at (N,10). In the case of the Central Library, the transmitter coordinates are (N,16) for which Fig. 10 can be used for best reference.Fig. 10Coordinates on the sketch of the third level of the Central Library.

Coordinates on the sketch of the third level of the Central Library.

Third, for each grid of points, the values of the Euclidean distance between transmitter and reception points were determined in each scenario by using simple geometry. For C1 it was determined a vector of 2D distances, whereas for C2 a vector of 3D distances was calculated given the difference of antennas’ heights.

In a fourth step, for each grid point per scenario, it was identified and counted the number and type of walls found in the propagation path (direct line between transmitter and receiver). Basically, the types of walls found were: bricks, glass, drywall and wooden. Thus, for each wall type we got a vector of integer numbers associated to each grid point.

Then, we measured the levels of received power, Prx, per scenario and per antenna configuration (C1 and C2) following the grid of each case. These levels of Prx, as a function of their corresponding distances between transmitter and receiver made up a vector.

By taking these vectors (distance, wall type, and Prx) we made up six datasets, considering three scenarios and two antenna configurations. Now, provided that some locations on the grid were unable to gather the measure, the original datasets were processed to eliminate those points. Furthermore, the original datasets were checked to clean them of outliers.

Finally, from the values of Prx in each cleaned dataset, it was determined the corresponding path loss PL in dB as a difference between the transmitter power Ptx and Prx, both in dBm (remember that the transmitter power is one experimental setting and is fixed to Ptx = 10 dBm as explained in Experimental settings Section): 1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$PL={P}_{tx}-{P}_{rx}.$$\end{document}PL=Ptx-Prx.

And so, six new datasets for PL as a function of the distance between transmitter and receiver were generated.

Data Records

The data records are identified by one of three type of datasets: (i) the raw data of received power; (ii) the cleaned data of received power; and (iii) the calculated data of path loss. The public repository that contains all datasets is available at OSF28.

This repository contains csv files (comma-separated values) whose names are given in Tables 1, 2, and 3. In general terms, the files names follow the structure VARIABLE_SCENARIO_CONFIGURATION.csv, where VARIABLE corresponds to the main parameter: received power (RD for raw data or just Prx for cleaned data) or path loss (PL); SCENARIO represents one of three possible environments: Communications Section (Comms), SSE students building (SSE), or Central Library (Library); and CONFIGURATION is given for the antenna configuration: when both antennas have the same height (C1) or when antennas have different heights (C2). Data quality

Data quality is a critical aspect of data mining and machine learning processes, since the analytical and predictive value of models depends directly on the reliability of the information used. A dataset with deficiencies—such as inconsistencies, missing values, or inaccurate records—can introduce bias, reduce algorithm performance, and even lead to erroneous conclusions. To ensure quality, it is essential to establish validation criteria from the collection stage, apply cleaning and normalization techniques, and systematically monitor key attributes such as accuracy, completeness, consistency, uniqueness, and timeliness. Likewise, verification against trusted reference sources and the incorporation of objective metrics help maintain appropriate standards that guarantee the relevance and usefulness of data throughout the project’s lifecycle.

Data quality is based on metric such as completeness, consistency, validity, uniqueness, and timeliness. The completeness of the database presented in this work is guaranteed by the data collection process described in the previous sections. Measurements were taken considering the points indicated in Figs. 7, 8, and 9. For the Communications Section scenario, 912 points had originally been considered, however, it was only possible to measure 721 points for the antenna configuration C1 and 677 for C2 because at several points, it was impossible to place the receiver because there would be furniture or some structural element that made its placement impossible. In the same way, for the SSE students building the original number of points was 140, but measurements were conducted on 107 points for both C1 and C2; finally, for Central Library scenario, 675 points had been planned to place the receiver and it was only possible to place it at 343 points for C1 and 344 points for C2.

The data has no inconsistencies and conforms to the field formats. The data in the fields Num_brick_wall, Num_wood_wall, Num_glass_wall, Num_drywall, and Num_column are non-negative integers; and the values for the Distance field are always positive.The data does not contain unnecessary duplicates, thus ensuring data uniqueness. As mentioned, the measurements were taken at 3.5 GHz, which is one of the mid-bands used in 5G technology, so the timeliness of these results is relevant.

A correlation analysis is included to show that the data do not exhibit statistically anomalous or redundant correlations between variables. This is because there are no duplicate variables, data entry errors, or unwanted dependencies.

Correlation analysis

Regarding the correlation analysis, the Spearman’s correlation coefficient was used to evaluate monotonic relationships between variables. Since it does not depend on the probabilistic distribution of the variables, it is considered a non-parametric technique, which makes it more robust in the presence of non-parametric data normal and capable of capturing non-linear relationships29. Thus, it is illustrated by Spearman correlation plots generated in R, which are organized in a matrix array of cells, where each cell (except those of the main diagonal) represents a relationship between two variables in the dataset. The cells on the main diagonal show histograms for each individual variable, so vertical axis represents the frequency or density of values of the variable on the diagonal, and the horizontal axis represents its range of values. The cells below the diagonal show scatter plots that visualize the relationship between pairs of variables, then the vertical axis shows the values of the variable on the diagonal, and the horizontal axis depicts the values of the variable to be compared with the variable on the diagonal. The cells above the diagonal show the values of Spearman correlation coefficients between the variable on the diagonal and the other variables. For the datasets reported here variables depicted in the correlation plots are those explained in Section Data Record (Distance, Num_brick_wall, Num_wood_wall, Num_glass_wall, Num_drywall, Num_column, and P_rx).

For example, Fig. 11 depicts a correlation plot obtained for the dataset Prx_Comms_C1.csv where histograms and scatter plots illustrate the shape and dispersion of the data. As can be seen, there are different correlation values among features, and there is a wide dispersion of data providing interesting information for analysis.
