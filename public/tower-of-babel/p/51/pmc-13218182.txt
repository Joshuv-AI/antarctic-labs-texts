Preferences of women experiencing infertility for in vitro fertilisation treatment in China: a discrete choice experiment
Authors: Liyong Lu, Yuyu Lv, Hongwei Nie, Shan Jiang, Shi Zhao, Ling Geng, Haipeng Wang, Shunping Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIn vitro fertilisation (IVF) is a key treatment for infertility, but uptake and continuation may be limited by treatment burden, side effects and out-of-pocket costs. Evidence on IVF treatment preferences among women in China remains limited. This study examined the preferences of Chinese women experiencing infertility for different IVF treatment attributes.MethodsA discrete choice experiment was conducted using face-to-face surveys to elicit preferences among women experiencing infertility receiving IVF treatment. Five attributes were examined: number of visits per cycle, side effects, treatment costs per cycle, information disclosure about IVF and pregnancy rate per cycle. Preference weights were estimated using the mixed logit model. Willingness to pay and relative importance of the attributes were estimated. Scenario and heterogeneity analyses were performed.Results591 participants were included in the final analysis. All five attributes significantly influenced preferences. Pregnancy rate per cycle was the most important attribute, followed by side effects, treatment costs, information disclosure and number of visits. Increasing the pregnancy rate from 15% to 60% per cycle produced the largest utility gain. Participants were willing to pay ¥56 099.89 for this improvement, ¥22 718.73 to reduce side effects from serious to mild, ¥8720.87 for always receiving information versus none and ¥6429.00 to reduce visits from 30 to 10 per cycle. Preference heterogeneity was associated with education and household income.ConclusionsChinese women undergoing infertility treatment place the greatest value on higher IVF pregnancy rates, fewer side effects and lower treatment costs. Patient-centred counselling, burden-reduction strategies and policies that reduce financial barriers, including potential insurance coverage, may improve IVF acceptability, continuation and utilisation. Tailored approaches are needed to address preference heterogeneity across patient subgroups.Keywords: Female, Humans, Public Health

Introduction

Infertility is a major global health issue. Infertility is a medical condition affecting the male or female reproductive system, defined by the inability to achieve pregnancy after 12 months or more of regular, unprotected sexual intercourse.1 It impacts approximately 15% of couples globally, translating to around 186 million individuals, and often leads to significant emotional and psychological distress due to the challenge of conceiving.2 3 The WHO estimates that approximately 17.5% of adults—around one in six people worldwide—experience infertility during their lifetime, underscoring the need to improve access to affordable, high-quality fertility care. In China, infertility has emerged as a major public health issue, with prevalence rates estimated at 15%–20% (40–50 million) among women and 10%–12% (45 million) among men.4 Moreover, available national estimates suggest that infertility prevalence in China increased from approximately 12% in 2007 to 18% by 2020, indicating a rising burden over time.5

In vitro fertilisation (IVF) is the principal treatment for infertility, facilitating conception and full-term pregnancies.6 Despite this, financial burdens, side effects and procedural inconveniences frequently result in many women either discontinuing treatment or opting against IVF altogether.7 8 Globally, only 56% of infertile couples choose IVF, with China’s uptake notably lower at 46.5%.5 Enhancing patient treatment continuation and acceptance of infertility treatments is critical for improving the likelihood of successful pregnancies and increasing fertility rates.9

Personalised treatment approaches are increasingly being adopted in infertility management.10 11 Tailoring IVF treatments to meet the specific preferences and needs of patients can improve adherence and outcomes. Understanding patient preferences can support more patient-centred IVF services, facilitate shared decision-making and help reduce avoidable treatment burden for those who pursue IVF. Reducing avoidable treatment burden and improving alignment between IVF services and patient preferences may support treatment continuation across cycles and enhance patient experience; sustained engagement with care may increase the opportunity for achieving pregnancy over time.

Recent international research has increasingly emphasised that infertility treatment choices are preference-sensitive because available IVF options differ in effectiveness, risks, treatment burden, costs and the degree of patient-centred care. A 2024 systematic review summarising evidence from discrete choice experiments (DCEs), surveys and related methods found that patients’ decision-making is most strongly driven by treatment effectiveness, followed by safety and costs, while burden (eg, visits/injections/time) and patient-centredness (eg, information provision and involvement in decisions) also influence choices.12 Stated-preference studies across countries similarly show that, although effectiveness is highly valued, patients make important trade-offs with out-of-pocket costs, treatment discomfort/inconvenience and shared decision-making. For example, a multi-country DCE spanning the USA, UK, Nordic countries, Spain and China quantified willingness-to-pay (WTP) for improvements in effectiveness and shared decision-making while demonstrating systematic cross-country differences in relative attribute importance.13 IVF-specific preference evidence further suggests that outcome measures such as live birth-related probabilities may dominate decisions, with process and quality-of-life impacts playing secondary roles.14 In addition, fertility-treatment DCE work has quantified WTP for higher success rates and for avoiding moderate to significant side effects, highlighting the policy relevance of both clinical outcomes and treatment burden.15

Although some research has examined patient preferences in infertility treatment,16,18 it has generally focused on assisted reproductive technologies (ART) in broad terms rather than on specific aspects of IVF. Additionally, with declining birth rates and rising infertility rates, China faces substantial risks to its economic and social development.19 Addressing the fertility decline is crucial for fostering population growth, yet there is a notable lack of evidence regarding IVF treatment preferences among Chinese patients. Cultural differences between China and other countries further underscore the need for dedicated research within the Chinese context, as findings from other nations may not be directly applicable. In China, lower IVF uptake may reflect a combination of demand- and supply-side factors rather than a single barrier. On the demand side, infertility-related stigma and culturally mediated expectations surrounding childbearing may shape whether couples seek care and how long they persist with treatment. On the supply side, IVF is commonly associated with substantial out-of-pocket payments and may involve intensive time commitments, including repeated clinic visits and potential travel to specialised centres. In addition, service availability and authorisation scope can vary across institutions and regions; for example, while many institutions are authorised to provide ART, only a subset were approved to deliver more advanced services such as preimplantation genetic diagnosis (PGD), which may concentrate care and increase access burdens for some patients. These China-specific social and health-system dynamics underscore the importance of eliciting IVF preferences in the local context to inform patient-centred strategies that could improve acceptability and continuity of care.

The DCE is a robust methodological tool for quantitatively assessing preferences20 and is widely used to evaluate health preferences among patients, the public and stakeholders concerning medical services or products.21 22 This study employs DCE to investigate the preferences of patients experiencing infertility for various IVF treatment options. IVF is a preference-sensitive medical decision; the goal of this study is not to promote IVF universally, but to inform patient-centred design of IVF care for individuals who are considering or undergoing treatment. Given the physiological complexities involved, women undergoing IVF often face invasive procedures and bear the primary responsibility for reproduction. IVF procedures include in vitro fertilisation-embryo transfer (IVF-ET), intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI), PGD and oocyte cytoplasmic replacement (OCR). Due to ethical concerns, OCR is not approved for clinical use in China. According to the information published by the National Health Commission of China, as of 30 June 2024, among the 622 institutions in mainland China authorised to perform human ART, only 78 were government-approved to conduct PGD. Consequently, most patients experiencing infertility in China undergo IVF-ET or ICSI. This study aims to elicit preferences from women who have undergone IVF-ET or ICSI treatments, providing empirical insights to clinicians and policymakers. These insights can inform preference-informed IVF service design and can support shared decision-making between patients and clinicians to deliver more patient-centred infertility care.

Materials and methods

This study used a DCE to evaluate the preferences of women experiencing infertility who have undergone IVF-ET or ICSI treatments. (In this study, we focus on women already undergoing IVF/ICSI and quantify how key treatment-plan attributes influence choice among alternative IVF plan profiles; we do not evaluate clinic choice or population-level initiation of IVF.) The methodology was developed following the guidelines recommended by the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research.23 Key steps included the identification of attributes and their levels, experimental design, survey planning and statistical analysis.

Identification of attributes and levels

To identify relevant attributes and levels for the DCE, we adopted a comprehensive approach involving literature review, in-depth interviews and expert consultations. An extensive literature review was conducted to identify studies related to IVF preferences. Searches included both English and Chinese language articles published up to 31 March 2023, across several databases including PubMed, Web of Science, CNKI, Wanfang and VIP. Search keywords included “discrete choice experiment”, “preference”, “assisted reproductive technology” and “in vitro fertilization”. Details of the literature review are provided in online supplemental text S1 and online supplemental figure S1 in online supplemental material.

In addition, in-depth interviews were conducted with a convenience sample of 20 patients experiencing infertility and five reproductive medicine specialists from Guangdong, Hebei, Henan and Gansu Provinces. These interviews explored factors influencing IVF treatment decisions and identified 24 potential attributes. Interviewee demographics, potential attributes, the qualitative interview guide for physicians and the qualitative interview guide for patients are detailed in online supplemental table S1, online supplemental table S2, online supplemental text S2 and S3 of online supplemental material.

Subsequent expert consultations involved two DCE specialists from academic institutions and three reproductive doctors with doctoral degrees (holding clinical medical doctorates, equivalent to an MD) from hospitals. Beginning in September 2022, two rounds of email consultations, each lasting 1 week, were conducted. The feedback from these consultations led to a refinement of attributes and levels. The final selection included five attributes: number of visits per cycle, side effects, treatment costs per cycle, information disclosure about IVF and pregnancy rate per cycle. Experimental design

To construct the choice tasks, we used Ngene V.1.2 (ChoiceMetrics, Sydney, Australia) to implement a D-efficient design. Each task presented two options differing across the five attributes, with an opt-out choice to simulate realistic clinical decision-making scenarios. The opt-out option reflects declining a presented plan profile rather than observed discontinuation, and direct causal claims about real-world discontinuation cannot be made from this design alone. Tasks were presented without labels to ensure respondents focused on the attributes. A total of 12 choice tasks were generated, divided into two blocks of six tasks each and randomly assigned to respondents. Attribute order within each task was randomised to minimise sequencing effects.24 25 Additionally, a ‘quality control’ task was included as a duplicate in the second choice set to verify respondent engagement and sincerity. Demographic information, including age, residence and occupation, was collected post-consent. A pilot study conducted in January 2022 with 32 patients experiencing infertility in Jinan City assessed the clarity and length of the questionnaire. The pilot study indicated that the questionnaire and choice tasks were understandable and feasible to complete. No changes were made to the final set of attributes/levels or the experimental design; however, minor revisions were made to the questionnaire wording and formatting (eg, simplifying phrasing and clarifying instructions) to improve readability. Survey

The survey was conducted across five hospitals authorised to perform ART in eastern (Shandong and Guangdong), central (Henan and Hebei) and western (Gansu) China, selected to reflect economic disparities, geographic diversity and implementation feasibility. Based on Orme’s formula,26 a minimum sample size of 167 respondents was determined, with a target of over 500 to ensure robust statistical estimates and facilitate heterogeneity analyses.

The study targeted women who have undergone IVF-ET or ICSI treatments. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age over 20 years, (2) female gender, (3) diagnosed with infertility, (4) having received IVF or ICSI treatment and (5) ability to understand and independently respond to the survey.

The anonymous survey was conducted from February to March 2023. To ensure data quality, electronic questionnaires were administered face-to-face. Respondents were thoroughly briefed on the survey’s purpose and content by trained researchers. Each participant required approximately 15 min to complete the questionnaire and received a ¥20 (approximately US$3) gift on completion.

Statistical analyses

Our analysis primarily employs the random utility model (RUM) framework. In this model, each individual, denoted as n, chooses among j alternatives with the goal of maximising their utility. We employed the mixed logit model (MLM), which accommodates variability in parameters across individuals.27 Treatment costs were modelled as a continuous variable using a linear trend test, while all other attributes were effect-coded. In this model, the β coefficients reflect the preference weight of each attribute relative to the overall mean, due to the effect coding of categorical attributes.28 The analysis was conducted using Stata V.15.1, which was used to fit the models, estimate coefficients and assess the impact of attributes on individual choices. An alternative-specific constant was included to represent the utility derived from choosing the opt-out alternative versus non-opt-out alternatives.

To ensure accurate and stable mixed logit estimates, we conducted a draw-sensitivity assessment by iteratively increasing the number of simulation draws from 50 to 500, 1000, 1500, 2000 and 2500. As shown in online supplemental figure S2 of online supplemental material, the preference coefficients became stable from approximately 1000 draws onward, with no substantial fluctuations thereafter. We further compared model fit across draw settings using the log-likelihood, Akaike information criterion and Bayesian information criterion; the model estimated with 2000 draws outperformed the alternatives, indicating the best balance of goodness-of-fit and parsimony. Therefore, we selected the 2000-draw mixed logit as the primary model for reporting main-effects results, and all subsequent analyses (eg, relative importance (RI) and WTP calculations) were based on this specification.

The RI of each attribute was computed by dividing the difference between the coefficients of the most and least preferred attribute levels by the sum of all attribute differences.29 Higher RI scores indicate greater importance of the attribute to respondents. Mean WTP values were calculated as the ratio of coefficients between each attribute and the cost attribute in the MLM model. WTP-space MLM estimates were used to quantify individuals’ marginal payments for changes in attribute levels within a new treatment programme. It should be noted that WTP captures marginal trade-offs within hypothetical choice tasks rather than directly predicting real-world spending under financial constraints; moreover, if cost sensitivity is attenuated by framing effects or social desirability bias, the resulting WTP estimates may be inflated. Scenario simulation analysis was performed to estimate adoption rates for different scenarios using mixed logit coefficients. Adoption rates represent the likelihood of patient acceptance for specific profiles and illustrate how optimising these profiles could enhance patient selection. The baseline scenario, characterised by the least favourable attribute levels, resulted in a zero-adoption rate. In this baseline scenario, the treatment programme featured 30 visits, serious side effects, no information disclosure about IVF, treatment costs of ¥60 000 (over US$8000) per cycle and a 15% pregnancy rate per cycle. Additionally, interaction terms involving education level, annual family income and attribute levels were analysed to explore preference heterogeneity.

Results

Patient characteristics

Out of 650 participants who completed the questionnaire, 45 were excluded for failing the consistency test and an additional 14 were excluded due to position bias (consistently favouring left, right or opt-out options). Consequently, 591 participants were included in the final analysis.

Table 3 provides an overview of the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of these participants. The mean age was 34.2 years (SD=4.6). Over 70% of participants had an education of high school or above, and nearly 80% were employed. More than 60% lived in urban areas, and approximately half reported an annual household income of ¥80 000 (approximately US$10 000). Over 60% of participants were planning to have their first child, and nearly 75% had undergone IVF-ET treatment. During the survey period, 47.4% of participants had been diagnosed with infertility for over 3 years, and 46.0% had prior experience with embryo transfers.

Model estimates and RI

Figure 1 displays the estimated coefficients for the five attributes derived from the MLM with 2000 random draws, with detailed results provided in online supplemental table S3 of online supplemental material. The coefficient for choosing the opt-out option was −9.53 (95% CI −11.47 to −7.59), indicating a strong aversion to opting out and a notable preference for undergoing IVF treatment. Participants showed significant preferences for higher pregnancy rates, milder side effects, effective information disclosure about IVF, lower treatment costs and fewer visits per cycle. For instance, increasing the pregnancy rate from 15% to 60% per cycle resulted in a 3.63 increase in marginal utility, while improving side effects from serious to mild yielded a 1.86 increase in marginal utility. The SD of preference coefficients for most attribute levels were statistically significant, reflecting considerable preference heterogeneity within the sample.

Coefficients of Mixed Logit Models and Relative Importance of Attributes.

Based on the MLM estimates, we calculated the RI of each attribute, as shown in figure 1. The pregnancy rate per cycle was identified as the most critical attribute for participant selection (44.7%). Side effects ranked second (22.9%), followed by treatment costs per cycle (15.0%) and information disclosure about IVF (12.7%). The number of visits per cycle was relatively less critical (4.9%).

WTP and scenario analysis

Table 4 presents the WTP results. Participants demonstrated the highest willingness to pay for improvements in the pregnancy rate per cycle. For example, increasing the pregnancy rate from 15% to 60% per cycle elicited a willingness to pay of ¥56 100 (over US$7700). Improving IVF side effects from serious to mild prompted a willingness to pay of ¥22 719 (over US$3000) per cycle. Participants were also willing to pay ¥8721 (about $1200) for consistent information about IVF compared with no information. To reduce the number of hospital visits by 20 per cycle, participants were willing to pay an average of ¥6429 (about $900).

Table S4 displays the results of scenario analysis. The baseline treatment scenario included 30 visits, serious side effects, no information disclosure about IVF, treatment costs of CNY 60,000 (more than USD 8,000) per cycle, and a 15% pregnancy rate per cycle. Compared to this baseline, 90.2% of patients preferred a new treatment scenario if side effects were improved from serious to mild, with all other factors held constant. Adjusting a single attribute level could increase the proportion of patients willing to undergo IVF treatment from less than 55.3% to over 98.7%. The most significant preference impact was observed with a reduction in treatment cost to CNY 20,000 (about USD 2,800), whereas the effects of reducing the number of visits and improving information disclosure about IVF were minimal.

Heterogeneity analysis

Interaction terms between individual characteristics and attribute levels were incorporated into the MLM. Results, detailed in online supplemental table S5 of online supplemental material, revealed statistically significant interactions between education level, family income and attribute levels, indicating that these factors contribute to preference heterogeneity.

Specifically, interaction analyses indicated systematic preference differences by education and household income. Women with undergraduate education or above showed stronger preferences for milder side effects and higher pregnancy rates, as reflected by significant positive interactions for mild side effects (β=0.61) and for higher pregnancy rates (45%: β=0.49; 60%: β=1.21). In contrast, women with annual household income ≥¥80 000 were less sensitive to treatment burden and cost (number of visits interaction β=0.04; cost interaction β=0.03), and placed greater value on frequent information disclosure (always inform interaction β=0.28). These findings support tailored counselling strategies, with greater emphasis on affordability for lower-income patients and enhanced transparency and outcome trade-offs for higher-education/higher-income patients. Further research is needed to explore how education and family income influence patient preferences in greater detail to better understand preference heterogeneity.

Discussion

This study employed a DCE to evaluate the preferences of women experiencing IVF treatment. Our findings underscore that the success rate of pregnancy is the foremost factor influencing treatment decisions, followed closely by concerns about side effects and treatment costs. Although factors such as information disclosure and frequency of clinic visits have a lesser impact, they still significantly influence IVF decisions. Additionally, our analysis revealed variability in preferences among different patient subgroups, indicating the need for further investigation in future research.

These findings are actionable because they indicate where improvements are most valued by patients and can guide counselling and service design. Given that the success rate was the dominant attribute, clinicians can use preference-informed counselling to clearly communicate expected success probabilities and support shared decision-making when more than one clinically appropriate protocol is available. At the same time, the high importance placed on side effects suggests that reducing symptom burden is a key determinant of treatment acceptability. Practical steps include proactive risk assessment and monitoring, early identification and manageent of symptoms, and structured patient education on what to expect and when to seek care. Integrating success-rate counselling with a clear symptom-prevention and management plan may help align IVF plans with patient priorities.

The desire to conceive is significantly shaped by cultural norms in China, where filial piety emphasises the importance of having children to honour ancestors.30 Moreover, infertility can place strain on marital relationships, as traditional beliefs associate childbearing with strengthening marital bonds.31 These cultural factors profoundly influence treatment choices among patients.

Our results suggest that women experiencing infertility are willing to invest more in IVF plans that promise higher pregnancy rates and fewer side effects. This pattern is consistent with the stated-preference literature in fertility care, where effectiveness typically dominates decisions and patients make explicit trade-offs between outcome gains, treatment burden, and costs. For example, Ryan’s early conjoint analysis in IVF demonstrated that patients place a very high value on improvements in treatment outcomes and are willing to accept changes in other aspects of care to increase the chance of success.16 Botha et al similarly showed in a societal DCE in Australia that attributes related to treatment effectiveness were prioritised over other programme features.32 IVF-specific preference studies further show that women trade off live birth-related probabilities against the inconvenience and discomfort of treatment; for instance, Musters et al quantified preferences regarding injections, costs, and live birth rates in IVF.33 Likewise, Braam et al reported that women at increased risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome place substantial value on safer and less burdensome IVF options, highlighting the importance of symptom burden and perceived safety alongside effectiveness.34 In our study, pregnancy rate per cycle had the highest RI (44.7%), followed by side effects (22.9%) and cost (15.0%), aligning with these prior findings while providing evidence from a multi-site Chinese sample.

Because patient safety is non-negotiable, clinicians should continue to adhere to best-practice risk-reduction strategies. Within clinically appropriate options, our estimates indicate that women place substantial value on protocols that reduce adverse effects and treatment burden, suggesting that shared decision-making and individualised risk mitigation should be prioritised alongside efforts to improve per-cycle pregnancy rates. Importantly, our study also extends the literature by explicitly incorporating a patient-centred process attribute—information disclosure frequency—into IVF plan choice. This is comparable to fertility-care DCE evidence showing that patient-centredness is not merely an ‘add-on’, but a valued component of care. For example, van Empel et al found that physicians tend to underestimate the importance patients place on patient-centredness in fertility care,35 and Huppelschoten et al quantified a monetary value for patient-centred care in Dutch fertility care.36 In our data, participants preferred receiving IVF-related information ‘sometimes’ or ‘always’ rather than ‘never’, indicating that transparency and communication are meaningful determinants of treatment-plan acceptability in China as well.

Additionally, our study reaffirms that reducing treatment costs enhances patient acceptance, consistent with prior evidence that affordability constraints can shape ART choices. For example, Maeda et al found that household income influences ART choices under out-of-pocket payment conditions.37 In China, where IVF expenses are largely not covered by medical insurance, financial constraints remain a major barrier to treatment continuity. Our WTP estimates quantify the magnitude of the trade-offs patients are willing to make and provide decision-relevant inputs for policy discussions aimed at reducing financial barriers.

Several limitations should be noted and can be addressed in future work. First, because participants were recruited non-randomly from five ART-authorised hospitals, generalisability may be limited; future studies should recruit from a wider range of regions and facility types and consider stratified sampling. Second, we did not elicit WTP from the general public, which is relevant for insurance coverage decisions; future research should collect parallel preferences from the public and other stakeholders and compare them with patient preferences. Third, the DCE necessarily simplified real-world decision-making and our attribute set may have omitted important factors (eg, travel time, waiting time, emotional burden and partner preferences); future studies should refine and expand attributes using additional qualitative work. Fourth, stated choices may not fully predict real behaviour; external validity could be strengthened through prospective follow-up or comparison with revealed choices. Fifth, preferences may change across IVF stages and cycles; longitudinal designs are needed to capture such dynamics. Sixth, some attributes served as proxies (eg, information frequency and visit number), and we surveyed women only; future work should better measure communication quality and convenience, include partners, and use richer methods (eg, latent class models) to explore preference heterogeneity. Finally, although anchored descriptors were provided, respondents may still differ in their perception of symptom severity. Future DCEs could further enhance interpretability by specifying clinically defined adverse events alongside severity anchors.

Conclusions

To improve patient treatment continuation and utilisation, efforts should focus on enhancing IVF pregnancy rates and minimising side effects. Integrating IVF treatment into basic medical insurance could alleviate the financial burden on patients experiencing infertility, thereby increasing their treatment continuation and utilisation rates for IVF. Understanding preference heterogeneity across demographic characteristics is essential for effectively tailoring personalised IVF treatments.
