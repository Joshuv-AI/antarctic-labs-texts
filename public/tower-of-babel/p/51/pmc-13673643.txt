Purpose in life as a predictor of capability well-being among older adults in Thailand: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Yuki Kumagai, Ishtiaq Ahmad, Chitose Shimizu, Yoshihisa Shirayama, Miyoko Okamoto, Shori Takaoka, Itsaranuwat Yongpisanphop, Motoyuki Yuasa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionContemporary discussions on the definition of health, as highlighted by the World Health Organization (WHO), emphasize the significant role of spiritual health (SH) in contributing to overall well-being, alongside physical, mental, and social dimensions. In Thailand, the rapidly ageing population has elevated the importance of improving the well-being of older adults, making it a critical national concern. In this context, SH plays a crucial role and is recognized in Thai national legislation as a key element of health. However, specific research on the relationship between SH and well-being among Thai older adults remains limited. This study aimed to investigate the current state of well-being among independent older adults in Central Thailand and to examine factors associated with SH and well-being.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted from June 1 to July 31, 2025, using a Paper-based questionnaire survey targeting 500 older adults aged 60 and over within Ratchaburi Province, central Thailand. The well-being was assessed using the ICECAP-O (ICEpop CAPability measure for Older people). Descriptive statistics (frequency, percentage) and univariate analysis (one-way ANOVA, t-test) were conducted. Multivariate linear regression analysis was performed to examine the relationships between well-being, socioeconomic characteristics, health perception, and the Purpose in Life (PIL) test. Stata/BE17.0 was used, with a significance level set at p < 0.05. This study was approved by the ethics review boards of both countries’ institutions: Juntendo University, Japan (E23-0364-M03), and Chulalongkorn University, Thailand (COA No.197/68).ResultsAmong the 471 valid responses, the median ICECAP-O tariff scores was 0.868, and participants were classified into low (10.6%), moderate (33.8%), and high (55.6%) PIL groups. In multivariate linear regression analysis, the adjusted predictive margins revealed a stepwise gradient, where ICECAP-O tariff scores for the moderate and high PIL groups were 0.098 and 0.149 points higher, respectively, compared to the low PIL group (0.735). Subjective health, economic status, and caregiving roles were also positive predictors, whereas older age was negatively associated.ConclusionGiven the critical role of spiritual health, targeted strategies to promote PIL are needed to enhance healthy aging. The findings highlight PIL as the strongest predictor of capability well-being, alongside subjective health and financial factors within the Thai cultural context.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07929-2.Keywords: Well-being, Older adults, Spiritual health, Purpose in life, Thailand

Introduction

Population aging is a defining global phenomenon of the 21st century. According to the United Nations (UN), the number of older adults aged 60 years or over is projected to double by 2050 [1, 2]. By 2030, 1 in 6 people in the world will be aged 60 years or over. The World Health Organization (WHO) projects that the proportion of the global population aged 60 years and older will nearly double from 12% to 22% between 2015 and 2050 [3].

Thailand is currently experiencing a dramatic demographic transition characterized by a rapid increase in the older population [4]. Recent public health data indicate that the number of older adults aged 60 years and over has reached approximately 14 million, constituting over 20% of the total population. This signifies that Thailand has officially entered a “complete aged society” [5]. This shift is primarily driven by a drastic decline in the total fertility rate (TFR), which has fallen to approximately 1.0 to 1.1, a level significantly below the population replacement rate [6]. The WHO South-East Asia Region forecasts that the elderly population will comprise 36% of the total by the 2050s, signifying a demographic shift toward a “super-aged society” [4]. This demographic shift poses significant challenges to the healthcare system and necessitates a comprehensive approach to maintaining the quality of life for older adults.

Traditionally, health has been defined in three dimensions: physical, mental, and social well-being. However, there is a growing global consensus regarding the inclusion of a fourth dimension as spiritual health (SH) [7]. The WHO has long discussed the integration of the spiritual dimension into the definition of health to ensure a holistic approach to human well-being. SH often conceptualized through constructs such as meaning and purpose in life, is increasingly recognized as a vital determinant of overall well-being, distinct from religious adherence [8].

In alignment with global trends, the Thai government has prioritized the promotion of holistic well-being in its national health policies. Uniquely, Thailand’s National Health Act B.E. 2550 (2007) explicitly defines health as consisting of four dimensions: physical, mental, social, and spiritual well-being [9]. Within this framework, SH is regarded as a fundamental element for achieving happiness and societal harmony. Consequently, fostering SH has become a key strategic objective in Thailand’s efforts to support its aging population.

Despite increasing policy emphasis, empirical evidence regarding the SH of Thai older adults remains limited. Although its conceptual importance is well-recognized, quantitative data characterizing the prevalence and levels of SH in this demographic are scarce [5]. Furthermore, the mechanisms through which SH, specifically its measurable core component, the Purpose in Life (PIL), is associated with the well-being of the rapidly aging population remain insufficiently clarified [10]. Consequently, further investigation into the predictors of healthy life expectancy and the specific role of PIL is imperative to develop evidence-based strategies that promote inclusive well-being among Thailand’s aging population.

To address these gaps, this study employs the ICECAP-O to conceptualize the measure of overall well-being and five attributes of capability (Attachment, Security, Role, Enjoyment and Control). Unlike traditional measures focused on “functioning” or current health states, the capability approach emphasizes an individual’s opportunity to “do and be” what they value [11]. The attributes are fundamentally linked to various sociodemographic and spiritual factors. Specifically, Attachment is formed by social and familial networks, such as co-residence with children or participation in elderly clubs. Security relates to financial stability and health-related certainty, influenced by factors like pension receipt and household debt. The role covers the ability to feel valued through social contributions, including volunteering or caregiving. Enjoyment reflects opportunities for leisure and spiritual fulfilment, while Control indicates the autonomy to make independent life choices, often constrained by physical mobility or economic dependency. This framework is particularly suited for assessing SH, as it accommodates diverse, value-based interpretations of a fulfilling life.

Building on this background, the present study seeks to answer the following research question. How PIL an indicator of SH associated with well-being among independent older adults in Thailand? We hypothesize that SH, measured by a strong sense of PIL, serves as a significant positive predictor of well-being. Accordingly, this study aims to investigate the current status of well-being among independent older adults in Central Thailand and examine factors associated with SH and well-being. In this study, while SH is recognized as a broad theoretical dimension, we specifically focus on PIL to operationally evaluate this concept.

Methodology

Study setting and participants

Paper-based questionnaires were distributed at local older adults’ community club, volunteer organizations, and health centers. Community leaders were previously informed about the inclusion criteria (60 years or older, living in the community, and capable of responding independently) and helped to invite suitable participants. Written informed consent was obtained from all respondents prior to completing the questionnaire.

A total of 500 responses were collected through this multistage purposive sampling technique (Fig. 1). This approach was employed to select participants from this region, which is characterized by its significant economic, political, and cultural integration with Bangkok [12]. Ratchaburi Province was specifically selected due to its representative demographic structure, with a focus on Wat Phleng District, which reports the highest proportion of older residents (34%) in the province [13]. Among the three sub-districts within Wat Phleng, Chom Prathat was excluded due to the high number of unregistered older residents. Consequently, the participants were recruited from the Ko San Phra and Wat Phleng sub-districts.

Eligibility criteria

Participants in this study were community-dwelling older adults aged 60 years and older residing in Ratchaburi Province. To be eligible, individuals were required to be active members of an elderly club, native Thai speakers, and capable of living independently with stable general health. Both verbal and written informed consent were obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion. Conversely, individuals diagnosed with dementia were excluded from the study. Exclusion also applied to potential participants identified by public health officers or local village health volunteers as having cognitive impairment, an inability to live independently, or unstable general health conditions.

Study instruments

Dependent variables

The dependent variable in our study is well-being, which was measured using the ICEpop CAPability measure for older people (ICECAP-O). It was developed based on Sen’s capability approach; this preference-based instrument captures broad aspects of quality of life beyond health status that are essential for older adults [16]. The scale comprises five attributes: (1) Attachment (love and friendship), (2) Security (thinking about the future without concern), (3) Role (activities that provide a sense of value), (4) Enjoyment (enjoyment and pleasure), and (5) Control (independence). Each attribute is rated on a four-level scale, ranging from full capability (level 4) to no capability (level 1). Index scores were calculated using the UK population-based tariffs established by Coast et al. [17], which utilizes Best-Worst Scaling (BWS) to reflect the preferences of the general older population. The scoring follows an additive model in which coefficients for each selected level are summed. The attributes are weighted unequally, with Attachment carrying the highest weight (coefficient of 0.2535 for the top level), followed by Control (0.2094), Role (0.1923), Security (0.1788), and Enjoyment (0.1660) [17]. Details on the preparation and processing of ICECAP-O tariff scores are provided in Supplementary Information 1. The resulting index score ranges from 0.0 (no capability) to 1.0 (full capability) and was treated as a continuous variable in the analysis [11]. As an official Thai version of the ICECAP-O was unavailable, a translation was conducted following the guidelines provided by the Bristol Medical School, University of Bristol. This process involved forward and backward translation by two independent experts. A pilot study was subsequently conducted with 33 older adults in a community in Ratchaburi with similar characteristics to the target population. While the pilot results showed a significant association between the ICECAP-O and SH (p = 0.013), the initial internal consistency was moderate (Cronbach’s α = 0.66). Consequently, the Thai version was revised based on recommendations from the ethics review committee, which reconfirmed its face and content validity. The Cronbach alpha value of this instrument in our main survey data was 0.87.

Independent variables

The independent variable in our study is SH, measured by Part A of the PIL test [18]. We consider this instrument particularly appropriate for assessing SH in the Thai older adult population, as it focuses on an existential sense of meaning and the “will to meaning” rather than specific religious doctrines, making it culturally adaptable and suitable for diverse backgrounds. This scale was developed based on Viktor Frankl’s concept of the existential vacuum and logotherapy, positing that the search for meaning is a primary human motivation [19]. The PIL is a 20-item instrument; responses for each item are recorded on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (Strongly Disagree) to 7 (Strongly Agree). The variable is constructed by summing the scores of each item, yielding a total score ranging from 20 to 140. Following established classification criteria, scores ranging from 20 to 91 were categorized as low PIL, 92 to 112 as moderate PIL, and 113 to 140 as high PIL [20]. The original PIL test was written in English, and since then, it has been translated into many languages [21]. We included authentic Thai translations of this instrument in our questionnaire, as used by a previous study (Cronbach’s α = 0.84) [22]. The Cronbach alpha value of this instrument in our survey data was 0.96.

Data on other independent variables, such as sociodemographic and economic factors, social engagement, and health-related factors were collected using the following variables: Sociodemographic factors included age, gender, education level, marital status, having children, and co-resident’s status. Economic factors included employment status, longest job, income level, economic status, household debt, and pension receipt. Health-related and social engagement factors included current illness, caregiving within the household, subjective health perception, social activities, and volunteering. Additionally, religious background specifically included faith status and religion type. The validated questionnaire CECAP-O and PIL in the Thai language was used in this study. The English version of the questionnaire is provided as a supplementary file (Supplementary Information 2). The questionnaire was organized into three separate parts, (i) sociodemographic, economic factors, social engagement and health-related items (independent variables), (ii) ICECAP-O (dependent variable) and (iii) the PIL test (independent variable). This procedural separation was used to reduce the potential bias of the common method.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized respondent characteristics using frequencies and percentages for categorical variables, and means with standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for continuous variables. Following the OECD Guidelines on Measuring Subjective Well-being [23], we prioritized reporting central tendencies that are robust against the negative skewness and ceiling effects typically observed in well-being data. To explore differences across covariates, ICECAP-O tariff scores were treated as a continuous variable to preserve the full informational value of the capability measure, with group differences evaluated using Student’s t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), as appropriate. Univariate linear regression analyses were performed to identify unadjusted associations between capability well-being and individual independent variables. Variables found to be statistically significant in univariate analyses (p < 0.20), along with theoretically essential covariates such as age and gender, were included in multivariable linear regression models (ordinary least squares; OLS). Furthermore, the assumptions of the OLS regression were checked. The normality of residuals was visually confirmed using Q-Q plots, and homoscedasticity was verified via residual versus fitted plots and the Breusch-Pagan test, indicating no significant deviations from these assumptions. Regarding economic factors, subjective economic status was prioritized as the representative variable to mitigate potential multicollinearity among income, household debt status, pension, and employment status. The variable was the largest effect size (η2 = 0.149) in the univariate analysis among economic variables. This selection reflects perceived financial security as a direct determinant of individual well-being. Although both social activities and volunteering showed significant associations in the univariate analysis regarding social engagement, social activities demonstrated a larger effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.393 vs. 0.313). Therefore, to avoid multicollinearity, social activities were selected for the final model to represent social engagement. Other non-significant variables in the univariate analysis were excluded to ensure model parsimony and prevent overfitting. Details regarding the justification for the analytical approach are provided in Supplementary Information 3. To illustrate practical significance, adjusted predictive margins were estimated to visualize the specific impact of PIL on capability well-being. Multicollinearity was further evaluated using variance inflation factors (VIF), with values < 5 indicating no significant multicollinearity. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and 95% confidence intervals were reported. All Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17 (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Ethical review

This study was approved by the ethics review boards of both institutions: Juntendo University, Japan (E23-0364-M03) and Chulalongkorn University, Thailand (COA No.197/68). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. To ensure confidentiality, all responses were anonymized and securely stored in a password-protected offline system.

Results

A total of 471 participants were included in the final analysis. The average age was 69.17 years (SD = 7.55), with a range of 60 to 97 years. As summarized in Table 1, the majority of the participants were women (60.9%), aged 60–69 years (59.2%), primary or lower secondary school graduates (69.0%), married or cohabiting (54.6%), had children (83.0%), lived with others (90.9%), and identified as Buddhist (84.7%). Regarding economic status, participants reported having no financial difficulty (46.9%), household debt (61.4%), and a monthly income below 20,000 baht (51.0%). Most were currently working (66.0%), with self-employment (29.7%) being the most common longest-held occupation. Regarding health status, the participants reported a current illness (69.6%). Subjective health perception was predominantly positive, including “good” or “very good” (59.2%) versus “poor” (2.8%). Social engagement included activity participation (59.9%) and volunteering (41.6%), and a substantial proportion (62.0%) was involved in household caregiving.

Factors associated with well-being (univariate analysis)

A univariate analysis was conducted to examine the unadjusted associations between modifying factors and well-being, with the ICECAP-O tariff scores treated as a continuous variable (Table 3). The analysis revealed that age and education level were significantly associated with capability well-being (p < 0.001). The mean ICECAP-O tariff scores was 0.874 in the 60–69 age group and 0.751 in the 80 + age group. Regarding education level, participants with a bachelor’s degree or higher had a mean score of 0.908. Compared to this group, those with primary education or lower had a mean score of 0.833 (p < 0.001). Marital status was also significantly associated with capability well-being (p = 0.029). No statistically significant differences were observed for gender, presence of children, or co-residency status.

Factors associated with capability well-being (multivariable linear regression analysis)

A multivariable linear regression analysis was conducted to identify factors independently associated with capability well-being (ICECAP-O) (Table 4). The analysis revealed that PIL had the strongest independent association with well-being compared to participants with low PIL. Those with “Moderate” (Coefficient = 0.098, p < 0.001) and “High” (Coefficient = 0.149, p < 0.001) levels exhibited significantly higher ICECAP-O tariff scores, indicating a significant linear trend across increasing PIL levels. Subjective health perception also showed a strong, significant association; participants rating their health as “poor” had substantially lower well-being score (Coefficient = − 0.269, p < 0.001) compared to those with “very good” health. Similarly, economic status was a significant predictor, with participants expressing “very worried” about their finances showing a lower score (Coefficient = − 0.128, p < 0.001) compared to those with no financial difficulties. And, caregiving was positively associated with well-being; those taking care of others had significantly higher scores (Coefficient = 0.046, p < 0.001) than non-caregivers. In contrast, Age (80 years and older) was associated with lower capability well-being (Coefficient = − 0.061, p < 0.001). Gender, Education level, Marital Status, Current illness, Faith, Religion Type, and Social Activities were not statistically significant in the adjusted model.

Relationship between PIL score and ICECAP-O tariff scores

To further quantify the independent relationship between PIL and the capability of well-being, we calculated the predictive margins of the ICECAP-O tariff scores adjusted for all covariates in the multivariable model. The adjusted mean ICECAP-O tariff scores was the lowest for participants in the Low PIL group at 0.735 (95% CI: 0.701–0.768). The score significantly increased to 0.833 (95% CI: 0.815–0.850) for the Moderate PIL group and reached the highest level of 0.884 (95% CI: 0.870–0.898) for the High PIL group. The distinct, not overlapping confidence intervals between the Low PIL group and the other groups confirm a statistically significant, stepwise gradient in capability scores associated with higher levels of PIL.

Discussion

This study investigated the status of ICECAP-O among independent older adults in Central Thailand and examined its association with SH. The results demonstrated that the participants maintained a generally high level of well-being and SH. The most significant finding was that PIL served as the strongest independent predictor of ICECAP-O tariff scores in the multivariable analysis (Coefficient = 0.149, 95% CI: 0.111 to 0.187), exhibiting a clear stepwise gradient according to the level of PIL (Table 4; Fig. 4). This result strongly supports our hypothesis that SH is a vital element for maintaining well-being in older adults. Its magnitude of association surpassed both economic and health factors. In this study, the mean ICECAP-O tariff scores was 0.861 (SD 0.131). This score is comparable to or even slightly higher than the average score reported in the general older population of the United Kingdom (0.832) [17]. The UK is a high-income country with an advanced welfare system. Therefore, it is noteworthy that Thai older adults in a developing region achieved such high capability scores. This suggests that factors other than economic wealth play a fundamental role in maintaining quality of life. Specifically, social and cultural capital appear to be key drivers in this population.

Over 80% of respondents reported the top two levels of capability (“Full capability” or “A lot”) in the “Attachment (84%)”, “Enjoyment (85%)” and “Control (83%)” domains (Fig. 2). This finding strongly reflects the cultural fabric of Thai society. Thai culture is characterized by strong intergenerational solidarity and community cohesiveness. In Thailand, older adults typically live with or near their children and are deeply integrated into village life [24]. Previous qualitative research has highlighted that “connectedness” with family and neighbors is the primary source of psychological well-being for Thai older adults [25], our study results to consistent these studies. Moreover, the Village Health Volunteer (VHV) system plays a crucial role in supporting this “Security” and “Attachment”. In Thailand, over one million VHVs provide basic health monitoring and social support to older adults in their communities [26]. The volunteers visit the older adults regularly. They check their health status, deliver essential medications and caregiving. This robust community-based network likely provides older adults with a profound sense of safety and control. It ensures them that they will not be abandoned even if their health declines. Consequently, this may contribute to the high scores (79%) in the “Security” domain of the ICECAP-O tariff scores.

SH, the mean PIL score was 112.42 (Table 2), 55.6% of participants were classified into the “High” PIL group (Fig. 3). This score is comparable to or higher than that reported in Japan (42.9%) [27], suggesting that cultural and health systems are fundamentally associated in Thailand. This high prevalence of strong life purpose is deeply rooted in the Thai cultural concept of “Tham Boon” (merit-making). Importantly, in Thai society, “Tham Boon” is not limited to religious rituals at temples. It encompasses a broader spirit of mutual aid and altruism in daily life. For instance, helping neighbors and caring for family members are also considered good acts of accumulating merit [28]. This cultural belief system reframes “caregiving” and “community service” not as burdens, but as spiritual opportunities. By supporting others in the community or caring for grandchildren, older adults feel they are actively accumulating merit for their future lives. This perspective transforms their social roles into sources of spiritual meaning. Consequently, the act of “living for others” becomes a powerful engine for self-worth [29]. This likely explains why Thai older adults maintain high SH and a strong sense of purpose, even as they age.

The predicted ICECAP-O tariff scores rose significantly from 0.735 in the Low PIL group to 0.833 in the Moderate group and 0.884 in the High PIL group (Table 5; Fig. 4). The difference of nearly 0.15 points between the Low and High PIL groups is substantial. This magnitude exceeds the change typically associated with major physical morbidities. This implies that the lack of PIL may have a stronger negative association with capability than physical illness itself. The strong link between PIL and capability can be theoretically explained through Activity Theory and the concept of Successful Aging [30]. Activity Theory posits that maintaining social roles and engagement is fundamental to life satisfaction [31]. A high sense of PIL likely acts as a motivational engine. It encourages older adults to remain socially active, participate in community events, and engage in meaningful hobbies [32]. This motivation persists despite the physical limitations of aging. Consequently, these activities are likely reflected in higher scores in the “Role (80%)” and “Enjoyment (85%)” domains of the ICECAP-O tariff scores. Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge a potential conceptual overlap between PIL and the ICECAP-O. Both instruments capture deeply psychological and evaluative dimensions of human success, such as feeling valued and having a sense of control over one’s life. This inherent theoretical overlap likely explains a portion of the strong statistical association observed in our study. Consequently, rather than a mere methodological artifact, this thoughtful connection emphasizes the importance of the discussion by the WHO regarding the inclusion of a fourth dimension as SH of overall well-being.

Moreover, from the perspective of Resilience, PIL functions as a critical psychological buffer. Older adults inevitably face declines in physical health or the loss of loved ones. In such times, a strong sense of PIL provides the mental resilience needed to adapt. It allows them to focus on “what they can still do” (capability) rather than “what they have lost” (functioning) [33, 34]. This finding is consistent with recent meta-analyses showing that purposeful living significantly reduces the risk of functional disability and mortality [35]. In the context of Thailand’s rapid aging, promoting SH is not merely a philosophical pursuit. Instead, it is a practical public health intervention to extend healthy life expectancy and capability.

This study found subjective economic status, subjective health, and caregiving roles as significant determinants of capability well-being. Regarding economic factors, subjective economic status demonstrated a stronger association with well-being than objective income. In our analysis, compared to those with “No difficulty,” participants who were “Very worried” about their finances had a significantly lower score (Coefficient = -0.128, 95% CI: -0.172 to -0.083) with p < 0.001 (Table 4). This supports previous gerontological research suggesting that perceived financial adequacy is a more accurate predictor of psychological well-being than actual wealth [36]. For older adults, the “feeling of security” that they can manage future expenses is often more important than the absolute amount of money they possess. This finding directly aligns with the “Security” attribute of the ICECAP-O measure. It suggests that social safety nets, which reduce financial anxiety may be as effective as direct cash transfers in improving quality of life.

Subjective health was also a powerful predictor. Participants who perceived their health as “Poor” had markedly lower capability score (Coefficient = -0.269, 95% CI: -0.344 to -0.193) with p < 0.001 compared to those in “Very good” health. This is consistent with established literature indicating that self-rated health captures subtle bodily changes and psychological reserves that medical diagnoses might miss [37].

Notably, participants engaging in caregiving roles exhibited significantly higher capability scores (Coefficient = 0.046, 95% CI: 0.025 to 0.067) with p < 0.001 than non-caregivers (Table 4). This result challenges the common “caregiver burden” narrative. Instead, it supports the “Positive Aspects of Caregiving (PAC)” hypothesis [38]. Recent large-scale studies have indicated that caregivers often experience lower mortality and higher self-esteem due to the sense of being needed [39]. In the context of Thailand, this can be interpreted through the cultural norm of “Bun Khun” (gratitude and reciprocity). Caring for family members is viewed as a way to repay debts of gratitude and accumulate merit (Tham Boon). Therefore, caregiving provides older adults with a meaningful “Role” and enhances their social “Attachment”. It transforms a potential burden into a source of spiritual satisfaction and capability [40].

This study has several strengths. First, it is one of the few studies in Southeast Asia to assess older adults’ well-being using the ICECAP-O measure. Unlike traditional health-related quality of life instruments (e.g., EQ-5D), which focus primarily on physical and psychological functioning, the ICECAP-O is grounded in Amartya Sen’s capability approach [41]. This allowed us to capture a broader spectrum of well-being. Specifically, we evaluated what older adults “can do” and “be,” rather than merely the absence of disease. This approach aligns with the WHO’s modern definition of Healthy Ageing, which emphasizes functional capability over mere health status [42]. Second, this study provides novel empirical evidence linking SH (PIL) directly to capability well-being. Previous research has largely focused on the impact of spirituality on mental health outcomes, such as depression or anxiety [43, 44]. In contrast, our study highlights the positive role of “PIL” as a driver of capability. The observed stepwise gradient across PIL levels underscores SH as a proactive strategy to enhance the autonomy and quality of life among older adults. Third, our findings contribute to the diversification of gerontological literature, which has been predominantly Western-centric [45]. By highlighting cultural factors such as “Tham Boon” (merit-making) and the strong community-based support system (for example, Village Health Volunteers), this study underscores the importance of cultural capital. It suggests that non-material resources, social connections and spiritual beliefs are critical assets for successful aging in the Thai context [46, 47].

However, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences. Second, a potential selection bias exists. The eligibility criteria required membership in an elderly club. Therefore, the study sample may represent a more socially active and healthier segment of the older population compared to homebound non-members. This phenomenon, often observed in gerontological studies, implies that our results might overestimate the general population’s well-being scores [48]. Additionally, some subgroup sample sizes (those with poor subjective health or specific religious types) were small, which may affect the statistical power and stability of the regression estimates.” Third, the study was conducted in a specific province (Ratchaburi). Cultural nuances regarding “Tham Boon” or family structures may differ in urban Bangkok or other rural regions. Thus, generalizability to the entire Thai population requires caution. Finally, the reliance on self-administered questionnaires may have introduced response bias, such as social desirability bias, particularly in questions regarding religious or family values.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study investigated the current states of capability well-being and its association with SH among independent older adults in Central Thailand. The findings revealed that PIL was the strongest independent predictor of the ICECAP-O tariff scores. Better subjective health, perceived financial security, and engagement in caregiving roles were also significantly associated with higher capability well-being. The results suggest that improving well-being in later life requires not only physical and economic support but also focused attention on spiritual and psychosocial dimensions. In the Thai context, where merit-making (Tham Boon) and community-based networks like Village Health Volunteers are fundamental, fostering spiritual meaning and social connectedness plays a critical role. Future research should explore culturally sensitive strategies and interventions that support PIL and social roles to enhance healthy aging in rapidly transitioning super-aged societies.
