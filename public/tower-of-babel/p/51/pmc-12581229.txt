Childhood vaccine hesitancy among academics in Türkiye
Authors: Aylin Palloş, Cevriye Yüksel Kaçan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundVaccine hesitancy has been increasing both in Türkiye and worldwide, posing a growing challenge to public health efforts.AimThis study aimed to determine the level of childhood vaccine hesitancy among academics in Türkiye.MethodsThis descriptive and cross-sectional study was conducted between June 15 and December 15, 2021. The study population included all academics employed at public universities in Türkiye, and data were collected from 245 participants. The “Sociodemographic Data Collection Form” and the “Vaccine Hesitancy Scale” were used as data collection tools.ResultsThe mean age of academics was 43.63 ± 10.68 years. Of the participants, 55.9% were female, 76.7% were married, 62% had no children, and 74.3% worked in non-health fields. Additionally, 64.9% received information or education about childhood vaccinations. The most frequently cited sources of information were healthcare professionals (48.6%) and scientific articles (26.1%). Most participants (95.5%) believed that vaccines are necessary. Factors influencing attitudes toward vaccination included past experiences with vaccines (33.5%) and concerns about the reliability of vaccine sources (29.4%). The mean total score on the Vaccine Hesitancy Scale was 37.18 ± 14.74.ConclusionsAcademics in this study exhibited a low level of vaccine hesitancy. These findings highlight the importance of addressing specific concerns, such as source reliability, even among highly educated populations.Trial registrationThis study was not registered as a clinical trial as it does not involve an interventional design.Keywords: Academics, Vaccine, Vaccine Hesitancy

Background

Vaccination is one of the most effective public health interventions, providing individual immunity, reducing community transmission, and in some cases leading to disease eradication [1, 2, 4–6, 18, 22, 50], while also alleviating the economic burden on healthcare systems [36]. Vaccination policies are implemented worldwide to maximize benefits, and strong evidence confirms that vaccination is essential for sustainable health systems [5, 15].

According to the World Health Organization’s (WHO) 2018 report, global immunization prevents two to three million deaths annually. Estimations indicate that increasing immunization coverage could prevent an additional 1.5 million deaths per year. Although global immunization rates have hovered around 85% in recent years, data from WHO and the Turkish Ministry of Health indicate an increase in the number of individuals refusing or receiving incomplete vaccinations [6, 18, 48].

As defined by WHO’s Strategic Advisory Group of Experts (SAGE) on Immunization, vaccine hesitancy refers to the delay in acceptance or refusal of vaccines despite the availability of vaccination services [51]. Vaccine opposition, on the other hand, is defined as the complete refusal of all vaccines as a conscious decision [18]. Previous studies revealed increasing trends of vaccine hesitancy both in Türkiye and globally [6, 8, 39, 41, 45]. Due to the severity of this issue, WHO included vaccine hesitancy among the top ten global health threats in 2019 [48].

Vaccine hesitancy is a complex, context-specific phenomenon that varies across time, place, and vaccine type [4, 21, 45]. The WHO highlights the need to understand hesitancy at the local level. Multiple factors—including education, profession, health and religious beliefs, income, gender, social media use, and personal experiences—have been shown to influence vaccine hesitancy [6, 7, 10, 21, 24, 28, 32, 47, 53].

Vaccine hesitancy presents serious public health challenges by reducing vaccination rates and facilitating the re-emergence of preventable diseases such as measles, diphtheria, and pertussis [15]. It weakens herd immunity, posing fatal risks for immuno-compromised individuals [27], and increases the economic burden on healthcare systems through higher hospitalization and treatment costs [36]. Moreover, it undermines public trust, fosters anti-scientific discourse, and diminishes the effectiveness of health policies [48].

Academics play a crucial role in raising scientific awareness and disseminating accurate information within society. As knowledge producers and role models, they influence students, colleagues, and the broader community, and are often regarded as trusted sources of scientific information. Their attitudes toward vaccination may therefore have a multiplier effect on public perceptions, behaviors, and even health policies. Despite their capacity to assess and interpret scientific data, instances of vaccine hesitancy have been observed among academics [27]. This paradox—where highly educated individuals with access to scientific resources still demonstrate hesitancy—highlights that education alone is insufficient to explain vaccine attitudes and that other psychosocial and contextual factors must be considered. Through their involvement in curriculum development, policy advice, and scientific communication, academics can directly shape health education and public health strategies. However, there is a paucity of research specifically focusing on academics, both in Türkiye and internationally, underscoring the importance of addressing this gap to better understand the determinants of vaccine hesitancy in this influential group.

Methods

Design

This descriptive and cross-sectional study employs a validated online survey to determine the level of childhood vaccine hesitancy and the factors influencing vaccine hesitancy among academics in Türkiye.

Participants and setting

The study population consisted of all academics employed at public universities in Türkiye between June 15, 2021, and December 15, 2021. As this study was descriptive in nature, an attempt was made to reach all academics in the population, and a separate power analysis for the sample size was not conducted. The sample was obtained through a convenience sampling method and comprised academics who were accessible and voluntarily agreed to participate (n = 245). Of the 154,096 academics invited, 245 completed the survey, yielding a response rate of approximately 0.16%. For the purposes of this study, academics were defined as full-time faculty members and research assistants, while postgraduate students were excluded.

The inclusion criteria were active employment as an academic during the study period and voluntary consent to participate. Exclusion criteria included being on leave during the study period (e.g., maternity leave, military service, annual leave, or sick leave) or unwillingness to participate.

Vaccine hesitancy was evaluated with respect to childhood vaccination in general, including those recommended for participants’ children.

Data was collected through an online survey using Google Forms. Invitations to participate were distributed via institutional e-mail lists using the official correspondence channel of the rectorate. To increase participation, a reminder message was sent 15 days after the initial distribution of the survey. Participation was entirely voluntary, and all participants provided informed consent prior to the survey. The questionnaire, designed to be completed in approximately 10–15 min, included standardized scales and demographic items. The use of an online platform was preferred for ease of access and completion, enabling participants to respond at their own pace while maintaining anonymity.

Variables and instruments

Data collection tools

The study data were collected using two instruments aiming to determine participants’ demographic characteristics, academics’ levels of vaccine hesitancy and the factors influencing vaccine hesitancy among academics in Türkiye: the Sociodemographic Data Collection Form (25 items) and the Vaccine Hesitancy Scale (21 items).

Consisting of 25 items, the Sociodemographic Data Collection Form was prepared reviewing the relevant literature [18, 25, 35, 38] to collect data on participants’ sociodemographic characteristics such as age, academic title, marital status, number of children, and evaluating knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors toward childhood vaccinations.

Vaccine hesitancy scale

Developed by Kılınçarslan et al. [25], this scale underwent validity and reliability testing. It includes both a short and a long form. The long form, which was used in this study, consists of 21 items across four subscales: “benefit and protective value of vaccine”, “vaccine repugnance”, “solutions for non-vaccination”, and “legitimization of vaccine hesitancy.” The items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly disagree, 5 = Strongly agree). Items under the subscale “benefit and protective value of vaccine” are reverse-scored. The scale has no cut-off point; higher scores suggest a higher level of vaccine hesitancy. The Cronbach’s alpha reliability coefficient of the scale was calculated to be 0.905 by Kılınçarslan et al. [25]. In the present study, Cronbach’s alpha was calculated to be 0.955, indicating a high level of reliability.

Data analysis

The data were statistically analyzed using the IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). The information gathered through data collection forms was coded and transferred to a computer. Frequency (n), percentages (%), means, standard deviations, and median with 25th and 75th percentiles were used as descriptive statistics. Normality of the data was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. The results revealed the non-normal distribution of the data, justifying the use of nonparametric tests. A Mann–Whitney U test was employed to compare quantitative, continuous data between two independent groups. A Kruskal–Wallis test compares data among three or more independent groups. A stepwise multiple linear regression analysis was conducted, including title, gender, age, field of study, education status, and marital status as independent variables in order to identify significant predictors of the Vaccine Hesitancy Scale Total Score.. The Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee of Bursa Uludağ University (Approval No: 2021–04, Date: 28 April 2021). Permission was also obtained from the authors who developed and tested the scales for validity/reliability. The academics who agreed to take part in this study were informed about the purpose, contents, duration, and benefits of this study and how their data would be used. Written consent was obtained from each participant. The principle of confidentiality was adhered to by informing academics that their personal information would not be shared with anyone and that no one except the investigator would be able to access their information. They were also informed that their information would not be used for any purpose other than for this study on Google Forms. Responses were anonymized to ensure confidentiality, and access to the data was restricted solely to the research team. This study was carried out in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Moreover, 23.7% of the academics were professors, and the majority (74.3%) worked in fields other than health sciences (Table 1).

A majority of academics (87.8%) believed that vaccines have side effects, and the most commonly mentioned adverse effects were fever (79.2%) and pain (78.0%). In total, 94.7% indicated that they had vaccinated or would vaccinate their child with all vaccines recommended by the Ministry of Health, and 76.7% had vaccinated or would vaccinate their child with all childhood vaccines, including both mandatory and optional (paid) vaccines. Futhermore, 72.7% reported having their child’s vaccinations administered or planning to do so at Family Health Centers (Table 2).

Regarding the question “Which diseases are vaccinated against?”, the most frequently cited were measles (83.3%) and polio (79.2%), while 8.6% responded that they did not know. It was determined that 23.3% did not have their children receive paid vaccines due to a lack of knowledge, and that past experiences related to vaccines (33.5%) and concerns about the reliability of vaccine sources (29.4%) were factors influencing their attitudes and acceptance of vaccination. Moreover, 46.1% of the participants believed that routine vaccinations should be postponed if the child is ill (Table 2).

The mean score obtained by academics on the Vaccine Hesitancy Scale was 37.18 ± 14.74. Even though there is no established classification for this scale, given that the maximum score is 105, the findings indicate that participants exhibited low levels of vaccine hesitancy. A higher total score on the scale indicates higher levels of vaccine hesitancy. The highest mean score among the subscales was observed in the “vaccine repugnance” subdimension (13.79 ± 5.37) (Table 3).

When comparing the variable of receiving information/education on childhood vaccines with the scale scores, significant differences were found in the subdimensions of “benefit and protective value of vaccine”, “vaccine repugnance”, and the total Vaccine Hesitancy Scale score (p < 0.05). Academics who did not receive an education had higher scores in the benefit and protective value of the vaccine (9.56 ± 5.10), vaccine repugnance (14.69 ± 5.94), and total scale score (40.73 ± 17.04) (Table 4).

Similarly, significant differences were found in the total and all subscale scores of the Vaccine Hesitancy Scale based on whether participants believed vaccines were necessary. Those who did not believe in the necessity of vaccines had significantly higher scores (p < 0.05) (Table 4).

When comparing in terms of the belief in vaccine side effects variable with the scale scores, statistically significant differences were found in the total and all subscale scores. Participants who believed vaccines have side effects scored significantly higher (p < 0.05) (Table 4).

In addition, significant differences were found between participants’ intention to vaccinate their children with all Ministry-recommended vaccines and the Vaccine Hesitancy Scale scores. Those who stated they would not vaccinate their children had significantly higher scores across the total and all subscales (p < 0.05) (Table 4).

Finally, when comparing the types of vaccines administered or intended to be administered to children, those who selected the option “other” had higher total and subscale scores on the Vaccine Hesitancy Scale (p < 0.05) (Table 4).

Stepwise linear regression analysis was conducted to identify the predictors of Vaccine Hesitancy Scale Total Score among the independent variables (title, gender, age, field of study, education status, and marital status). The final model retained three significant predictors: Title (Research Assistant), Field of Study (Health Sciences), and Education Status (Master’s Degree). The results showed that being a Research Assistant was positively associated with vaccine hesitancy (β = 9.504, p < 0.001), indicating higher hesitancy scores compared to other titles. In contrast, studying in the field of Health Sciences was negatively associated with vaccine hesitancy (β = −6.862, p = 0.001), suggesting lower hesitancy among these participants. Similarly, having a Master’s degree was associated with lower hesitancy (β = −5.124, p = 0.039). The standardized coefficients indicated that Title (β = 0.266) had the strongest effect, followed by Field of Study (β = −0.204) and Education Status (β = −0.141). Gender, age, and marital status were excluded from the model as they did not contribute significantly. These findings suggest that academic title, field of study, and education level are important factors influencing vaccine hesitancy (Table 5).

Discussion

This study aims to determine the level of childhood vaccine hesitancy among academics in Türkiye and to identify the factors influencing this hesitancy. The mean total score obtained from the Vaccine Hesitancy Scale by participating academics was 37.18 ± 14.74, indicating a low level of vaccine hesitancy among this highly educated group. Similar results were reported in a study carried out on academics [12]. However, in contrast to the results achieved in the present study, [37] reported that individuals with higher income and educational levels exhibited higher rates of vaccine hesitancy compared to other segments of society. This discrepancy suggests that neither education level nor economic status alone can fully account for vaccine hesitancy. In a study involving 1,087 parents of hospitalized children aged 0–5 across three cities in Türkiye, [17] reported that the vaccine hesitancy rate was found to be 9.8%. Similarly, [3] revealed that 21.6% of participants refused to vaccinate their children with the vaccines included in the national immunization schedule. Hasar et al. [22] found a vaccine hesitancy rate of 19.7% among their study population. Conversely, other studies carried out in Türkiye reported that over 95% of participants consider childhood vaccines necessary and that vaccine hesitancy levels are relatively low [11, 19, 52]. Notably, some studies revealed that even individuals who experience hesitation about vaccinating their children still believe in the necessity of vaccination [18, 20]. However, when evaluating the literature as a whole [13, 30, 33, 42, 46], it can be concluded that vaccine hesitancy constitutes a form of social resistance both globally and in Türkiye.

In the present study, the finding that academics working in health-related fields exhibited significantly lower vaccine hesitancy is noteworthy. This result suggests that greater access to accurate information and a tendency to make decisions based on scientific evidence may protect health professionals from adopting vaccine-hesitant attitudes. Similar findings are supported in the literature, which consistently shows that health professionals demonstrate higher vaccine acceptance compared to the general population [14, 26]. Vaccine hesitancy is often defined as the refusal or delay of vaccines despite availability and is associated with lack of information, mistrust, misinformation, and emphasis on individual freedom [31]. Academics in the health sciences are better equipped to evaluate the efficacy and societal importance of vaccines based on their professional knowledge, which may make them more resistant to anti-vaccine rhetoric. Furthermore, their strong commitment to scientific methods facilitates evidence-based decision-making in health-related matters [16] The stepwise linear regression analysis results presented in Table 5 indicate that the field of study has a significant effect on vaccine hesitancy. It was found that individuals working in the field of health sciences had significantly lower vaccine hesitancy scores. This finding supports the notion that access to scientific knowledge and professional expertise serves as a mitigating factor for vaccine hesitancy. Although the explanatory power of the model is limited (R2 = 0.100), the results demonstrate that professional factors are important determinants of vaccine hesitancy. However, it is important to acknowledge that even within health professions, some individuals may still exhibit vaccine hesitancy. This indicates that vaccine hesitancy is not solely a function of education level but is also shaped by factors such as personal values, religious beliefs, and political ideologies [24]. Another important finding of the study relates to the variable of academic title. The analysis results revealed that research assistants had significantly higher vaccine hesitancy scores compared to academics with other titles (β = 9.504, p < 0.001). The higher level of vaccine hesitancy among research assistants can be explained by several factors. First, this group generally represents younger age cohorts, which may be associated with different perceptions of health-related risks and greater susceptibility to peer influence in decision-making processes. In addition, the fact that younger academics are more active on digital platforms increases their exposure to misinformation, which in turn may trigger vaccine hesitancy.

The results achieved in this study also revealed that academics who received information or education about childhood vaccines, who believed in the necessity of vaccines, and who did not believe in the presence of side effects, had significantly lower levels of vaccine hesitancy. These findings are consistent with literature indicating that knowledge level, perceived benefits, and trust are major determinants of vaccine hesitancy [7, 28]. In a similar study, even though 12% of academics stated that vaccines should not be administered due to side effects, 90.4% reported no hesitation regarding childhood vaccinations [12]. Similarly, previous studies emphasized the role of fear of side effects as a key factor contributing to vaccine hesitancy [34, 40, 46]. Misinformation regarding side effects, particularly when disseminated through digital and social media, can significantly distort individuals’ perceptions [29]. Increasing number of studies shows that non-scientific, sensational, or manipulative content on social media platforms negatively affects public attitudes toward vaccines [29, 49]. Social media rapidly exposes users to both factual and emotional content, thus facilitating the spread of fear, doubt, and mistrust [9]. Exaggerated or false claims about vaccine side effects can distort risk perception and lead to hesitancy. Therefore, how information is presented becomes crucial in addressing knowledge gaps [7]. As emphasized by Thaler and Sunstein [44], trusted experts such as physicians and nurses play a vital role in public education. Educational strategies must be well-designed and evidence-based to effectively counter vaccine misinformation. Randomized controlled trials have also underscored the importance of education-based interventions in reducing vaccine hesitancy [23, 34].

In the present study, academics who stated that they would not vaccinate their children with all the vaccines recommended by the Ministry of Health had higher levels of vaccine hesitancy. Vaccine hesitancy is not only related to individual health beliefs but also reflects parenting values, risk perception, and trust in authorities [16]. Decisions parents make regarding their children’s health often involve heightened protective instincts and emotional investment and can thus be interpreted as a direct reflection of their trust in vaccines. Consequently, academics who refrain from administering all recommended vaccines to their children may have low confidence in scientific authorities or may have doubts about the necessity and safety of vaccines [43].

Conclusions

This study revealed that academics, particularly those working in the health sector, exhibited lower levels of vaccine hesitancy. This finding suggests that health professionals, thanks to their professional knowledge base, are more informed about the effectiveness and importance of vaccines and are more likely to rely on scientific sources when making decisions. Furthermore, academics who had received information or education about childhood vaccinations, who believed vaccines are necessary, and who did not believe in the existence of side effects, had significantly lower vaccine hesitancy scores. These results emphasize the critical role of knowledge level and perceived trust in shaping vaccine-related attitudes.

In light of these results, several recommendations can be made. First, the development of structured training modules for academic staff is recommended to increase awareness of vaccine hesitancy and public health responsibilities. Second, targeted informational campaigns should be implemented in non-health disciplines to ensure that academics from diverse fields also have access to accurate knowledge about childhood vaccination.

For future research, longitudinal study designs are recommended to capture changes in vaccine attitudes over time and to allow for stronger causal inferences. Broader and more representative academic samples from multiple universities and faculties should also be included to enhance external validity. In addition, the inclusion of parents as participants is advised to provide a more comprehensive understanding of vaccine hesitancy across different segments of society. Finally, employing mixed-method approaches (quantitative and qualitative) may yield a more holistic understanding of the individual, social, and psychosocial factors that influence vaccine hesitancy.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the use of convenience sampling restricts the generalizability of the findings, as participation was voluntary and may have attracted individuals with greater motivation or interest in the subject, thereby introducing selection bias. Second, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish causal inferences between variables, as data were collected at a single point in time. Third, reliance on self-reported measures raises the potential for bias, including social desirability and recall errors, which may have influenced the accuracy of responses. Fourth, although 154,096 academics were invited to participate, only 245 responded, yielding a response rate of approximately 0.16%. This low response rate and the lack of information about non-respondents further limit the generalizability of the findings and increase the possibility of response bias. Finally, the sample was limited to academics, which may affect the generalizability of the findings. In particular, participants’ professional backgrounds—especially in the health sector—may have influenced their vaccine attitudes in ways not representative of the wider population.
