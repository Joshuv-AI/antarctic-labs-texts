A 40-year-old man with a new diagnosis of hepatitis B
Authors: Mia J. Biondi, Kashif Parvez, Vanessa Redditt, Jordan J. Feld
Published: 2026

A 40-year-old man emigrated from India 2 years ago and has no prior medical conditions. He requested a clinic visit with his primary care provider (PCP) now that he is 40 years old. He has not been screened for diabetes or hypercholesterolemia, and his PCP considers what other routine screening should be discussed.

What are the current recommendations for hepatitis B virus screening in adult patients?

The 2025 Canadian guideline on hepatitis B virus (HBV) recommends that all adults have 1-time triple HBV serology, which includes HB surface antigen (HBsAg), HB surface antibody (HBsAb), and HB core antibody (HBcAb), irrespective of risk or clinical history.1 Risk-based screening is generally ineffective, especially when related to stigmatized activities such as sex or drug use,2 and most individuals do not have symptoms.1 The prevalence of HBV infection among the general population in Canada ranges from 0.4% to 1.0%, with estimates among immigrants from countries where HBV infection is common ranging from 1.9% to 5.6%.3 However, routine HBV screening is not part of the Canadian immigration medical examination,4,5 and as a result, people may live in Canada for decades before a diagnosis. Many individuals may have even been screened for occupational reasons but are screened only for immunity or vaccination status — that is, HBsAb only. This isolated testing can result in individuals incorrectly being identified as “not immune” when they are in fact infected, as infected individuals do not typically make HBsAb. Triple serology ensures a comprehensive understanding of the individual’s HBV status (Box 1). The results for this individual are as follows: HBsAg positive, HBcAb positive, HBsAb negative. At a follow-up clinic visit, the PCP informs him of his HBV infection. The patient describes that he was tested for HBsAb for his occupation in the past and was HBsAb negative, and that he received 3 doses of the HBV vaccine but was not tested for HBV infection (i.e., HBsAg).

What baseline investigations should be ordered for a new diagnosis of HBV?

Box 2 outlines the recommended investigations for a patient with newly diagnosed HBV infection.1,6 The 2025 guideline states that screening for hepatitis D virus (HDV) antibodies should be ordered for all patients positive for HBsAg.1 Coinfection with HDV accelerates progression to advanced liver disease7 and can be as high as 40% to 60% in some regions. Countries or regions with a high prevalence of HBV and HDV include Mongolia, Romania, Pakistan, and parts of West and Central Africa.8,9

What are the recommendations for HBV management and treatment?

The Canadian guideline recommends immediate treatment initiation if the patient is HBsAg positive and has any of the following: cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, HDV coinfection, extrahepatic manifestations, or planned immunosuppressive therapy.1 In addition, there are expanded treatment criteria that rely on laboratory investigations of alanine transaminase (ALT) and HBV DNA levels, described in Appendix 1, Supplementary Box 1 (available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.260032/tab-related-content). First-line daily oral therapies include once-daily tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF) 300 mg, tenofovir alafenamide (TAF) 25 mg, or entecavir 0.5 mg.1 Entecavir or TAF are recommended for those with risk factors for renal disease or current renal disease, because they do not affect kidney function. Patients receiving treatment should be monitored every 3 to 6 months using the recommendations described in Appendix 1, Supplementary Box 2.

How can HBV transmission be prevented?

The primary mode of HBV transmission globally continues to be vertical transmission. The virus is also transmitted sexually (vaginal and anal exposure), through sharing drug-use equipment, reusing piercing or tattooing materials, and other parenteral exposures such as unsafe medical practices.10 Hepatitis B virus is extremely infectious and can live outside the body for up to 7 days.11 Thus, it can also be transmitted horizontally through household transmission via contact with blood or items with small amounts of blood, such as sharps, nail clippers, tweezers, and toothbrushes, which is concerning for household members, especially infants and children.10 Sexual partners and all those previously or currently living with the infected individual should be tested with triple serology. If results are negative, vaccination should occur, followed by repeat testing no earlier than 4 weeks after the last dose to ensure the individuals are negative and have responded adequately to vaccination. In this case, the patient lives with his pregnant partner and his 5-year-old and 2-month-old children. His partner was tested for HBsAg earlier in this pregnancy and was negative. All 3 are screened using triple serology, found to be negative, vaccinated, and then retested.

Case revisited

This patient’s HBV DNA was 3.4 × 105 IU/mL, and his hepatitis B e-antigen and hepatitis B e-antibody were both negative. He was immune to hepatitis A virus, and negative for HIV, hepatitis C virus, and HDV. His platelets were 160 (reference 150 to 400) × 109, ALT was 70 (reference 7 to 40) IU/L, aspartate aminotransferase was 50 (reference 5 to 34) IU/L, total bilirubin was 18 (≤ 22) μmol/L, direct bilirubin was 5 (reference ≤ 7) μmol/L, albumin was 40 (reference 30 to 50) g/L, serum creatinine was 105 (reference 64 to 110) μmol/L, α-fetoprotein was 10 to 40 (reference ≤ 7) μg/mL, and no hepatocellular carcinoma was found on abdominal ultrasonography. He did not have renal disease; as such, TDF 300 mg daily was initiated. One year after treatment initiation, the patient’s HBV DNA was not detectable, and his liver test results returned to normal.
