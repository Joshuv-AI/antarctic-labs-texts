Person‐centered intervention response in primary progressive aphasia: A secondary analysis of the Communication Bridge‐2 randomized clinical trial
Authors: Emily Rogalski, Ollie Fegter, Angela C. Roberts, Matthew Bona, Emily Cummings, Marisa Esparza, Eric Polley, Alfred Rademaker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONPerson‐centered outcomes that capture clinically meaningful change are essential to non‐pharmacological intervention research for Alzheimer's disease and related dementias. Primary progressive aphasia (PPA) is a neurodegenerative syndrome characterized by progressive language impairment. The Communication Bridge‐2 (CB2) randomized controlled trial previously demonstrated the efficacy of a personalized, participation‐focused speech‐language intervention on Goal Attainment Scaling (GAS), a person‐centered communication participation outcome. This secondary responder analysis characterized meaningful participant‐ and goal‐level response following intervention and at 12 months after enrollment to inform clinical translation and future appropriate use criteria.METHODSFifty participants with mild to moderate PPA in the CB2 experimental arm, each with three individualized communication participation goals (N = 150 goals), were included. Prespecified responder thresholds were defined as GAS ≥1 following each intervention block and GAS ≥0 at 12 months, reflecting improvement or maintenance relative to baseline in a progressive condition. Descriptive analyses examined responder patterns across time points, participants, baseline severity, PPA subtypes, and goal characteristics.RESULTSAll participants met responder criteria at one or more evaluation time points. At 12 months, 79% of goals met responder criteria, and 60% of participants met responder criteria across all three goals. More than half of the goals (51.3%) met responder criteria at all three evaluation time points. Meaningful response was observed across PPA subtypes, baseline severity levels, and diverse goal characteristics. Exploratory patterns suggested higher 12‐month responder rates for goals targeting speech, familiar communication partners, and familiar communication contexts.DISCUSSIONThis secondary responder analysis demonstrates that a person‐centered, participation‐focused speech‐language intervention was associated with meaningful goal‐level improvement or maintenance across a heterogeneous PPA population, with many responses sustained over 12 months. These findings support the value of GAS as a pragmatic, person‐centered outcome for capturing meaningful response in progressive neurodegenerative disease and provide translational evidence to inform future appropriate use criteria and individualized dementia care.

BACKGROUND

Progressive language impairment with relative sparing of other cognitive domains is the hallmark feature of primary progressive aphasia (PPA), a clinical neurodegenerative dementia syndrome within the Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) spectrum. 1 , 2 Language loss in PPA negatively impacts communication and can limit participation in everyday activities, resulting in reduced quality of life and overall well‐being. 3 , 4 A major challenge in non‐pharmacological intervention research for ADRD is the identification of person‐centered outcomes that capture clinically meaningful change over time. In the absence of a definitive cure, non‐pharmacological intervention is a central component of care for PPA, commonly delivered by a speech‐language pathologist. However, until recently, speech‐language interventions for PPA were supported largely by early‐stage studies, limiting evidence‐based clinical selection and broader implementation. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8

The Communication Bridge (CB) Research Program was initiated to address this gap by developing and advancing participant‐ and clinician‐informed interventions through National Institutes of Health‐staged clinical trials. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 The CB intervention integrates speech‐language, educational, and psychosocial approaches to maximize communication participation, confidence, and self‐efficacy. The pilot trial, CB1, established the feasibility of delivering tailored speech‐language intervention via telehealth, supported by a custom web application, and demonstrated communication confidence gains at 6 months after enrollment. 11 , 13 Building on this work, the CB2 trial was an international, Phase 2, Stage 2, randomized, active controlled clinical trial evaluating the efficacy of a personalized, dyadic, participation‐focused speech‐language intervention delivered via telehealth and supported by asynchronous web‐application‐based home practice and educational content. 9 , 12 , 14 The full protocol, baseline characteristics, and primary outcomes were published previously. 9 , 12 , 14 Briefly, CB2 demonstrated efficacy of the experimental intervention for the communication participation outcome of Goal Attainment Scaling (GAS), with favorable participant‐reported impacts on communication in daily activities and emotional well‐being relative to an impairment‐based control arm. The CB2 trial represented the first large‐scale randomized controlled trial (RCT) in PPA, an important step toward developing clinically meaningful interventions for PPA and other ADRD.

Following demonstration of efficacy, secondary analyses, such as responder analysis, can help characterize the heterogeneity of meaningful response beyond average treatment effects. Responder analyses are increasingly recognized as essential for informing the clinical “who, what, and when” of intervention. By identifying who is most likely to benefit, what types of goals are most likely to respond, and when response emerges or is sustained, responder analyses can help refine appropriate use criteria and guide translation into care. A responder analysis requires defining an outcome threshold above which individual are considered responders and below which they are considered non‐responders. 15 Examining individual‐ and goal‐level response patterns provides a framework for understanding heterogeneity of intervention effects and their relevance to translational care.

Although responder analyses have been used in pharmacological trials in dementia, their application to non‐pharmacological interventions remains limited, 16 and none, to our knowledge, has been conducted in PPA or in speech‐language therapy. The purpose of this secondary analysis was to characterize participant‐ and goal‐level response patterns within the experimental arm of the CB2 RCT. The study also sought to identify features of response that may inform individualized clinical decision‐making and future appropriate use criteria for communication interventions in PPA.

METHODS

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents

The Institutional Review Boards at Northwestern University and the University of Chicago approved the CB2 trial (STU00206086). The trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03371706). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. This study represents a secondary responder analysis of the CB2 RCT. This secondary analysis follows CONSORT (CONSORT reporting guidelines) reporting guidelines where applicable.

Participants

The recruitment and enrollment procedures for the CB2 trial were published previously. 14 Briefly, 95 dyads consisting of individuals with a clinical diagnosis of mild to moderate PPA and their communication partners were enrolled across the three research subtypes: logopenic, semantic, and agrammatic. 17 Each subtype is characterized by relative strengths and weaknesses in semantics, grammar, and word finding. Communication partners were self‐identified informal caregivers (often a family member or close friend) with regular contact and ongoing supportive roles.

Because the experimental arm demonstrated superiority over the control arm on the primary GAS outcome in the parent trial, this secondary responder analysis was restricted to participants randomized to that arm to characterize participant‐ and goal‐level response patterns within the efficacious intervention condition. Because analyses were restricted to the experimental arm, findings are descriptive and do not permit causal inference regarding comparative effectiveness.

Measures

The full CB2 trial protocol, including a detailed description of the primary and secondary outcome measures and their rationale, was published previously. 12 This secondary analysis focused on GAS, a primary outcome measure in the CB2 trial and a well‐established person‐centered method used to assess progress toward individualized communication participation goals. 18 , 19 GAS has been widely applied in rehabilitation settings because it is well suited to heterogeneous populations and individualized functional targets. 20 , 21 In the CB2 trial, GAS goals were collaboratively developed before randomization with the person with PPA, their communication partner, and a trained speech‐language pathologist (SLP). Three goals were developed for each person with PPA, with the current level of functioning anchored at 0 and potential intervention outcomes ranging from −3 (decline from baseline) to +3 (improvement from baseline). GAS ratings were obtained at prespecified evaluation time points by a non‐treating SLP who was familiar with the dyad and blinded to trial arm assignment.

Analysis

Given the demonstrated efficacy of the experimental arm in the parent trial, this secondary responder analysis focused on characterizing participant‐ and goal‐level patterns of response to inform clinical application and future hypothesis‐driven research. Response was evaluated at three prespecified time points: (1) after the first block of intervention (post‐block 1), (2) after the second block of intervention (post‐block 2), and (3) 12 months after enrollment (Figure 1).

CB2 trial timeline and operationalization of response across intervention evaluation time points. Participants completed two sequential intervention blocks followed by post‐intervention evaluations (post‐Block 1 and post‐Block 2) and a 12‐month follow‐up evaluation. Participants had access to indirect asynchronous home practice exercises on a tablet application throughout the entirety of the trial. Response was defined as Goal Attainment Scaling (GAS) ≥1 at post‐Block 1 and post‐Block 2, reflecting improvement following active intervention, and GAS ≥0 at 12 months, reflecting improvement or maintenance relative to baseline. Post‐intervention Response metrics summarized combined response across post‐Block 1 and post‐Block 2, while Cumulative Response captured response across all three evaluation time points. Response was characterized at both the participant and goal levels, as indicated.

Response thresholds were defined a priori. These thresholds were selected on clinical grounds to reflect meaningful improvement following active intervention and meaningful maintenance or improvement at 12 months in the context of progressive disease. At post‐block 1 and post‐block 2, response was defined as GAS ≥ 1, reflecting improvement following intervention. At 12 months, response was defined as GAS ≥ 0, reflecting improvement or maintenance relative to baseline, an important therapeutic target in progressive neurodegenerative disease. 24 Participant‐level variables included demographic and clinical characteristics (e.g., PPA subtype, age, sex). Participant‐level analyses summarized response across each individual's three goals at each evaluation time point. Goal‐level analyses examined response across time points and goal characteristics, including communication mode, partner type, group size, and context (Table 1).

At the goal level, a binary response indicator (yes/no) was assigned for each goal at each prespecified evaluation time point, and cumulative goal‐level response was defined as the number of time points at which a goal met response criteria (range 0 to 3). At the participant level, response was summarized as the number of goals meeting response criteria at each time point (range 0 to 3). Cumulative participant‐level response was defined as the total number of responses across three goals and three time points (range 0 to 9). Additional summaries characterized post‐intervention response (post‐block 1 plus post‐block 2; range 0 to 6) and maintenance response at 12 months (range 0 to 3).

Descriptive analyses were used to characterize the distribution and timing of responses across participants and goals. Frequencies, proportions, and summary statistics were used to describe cumulative response at both the participant and goal levels, with emphasis on overall trajectories rather than between‐group comparisons. No inferential hypothesis testing was performed. No imputation was performed; analyses were conducted on complete cases. Effect sizes (Cramer's V) were calculated to characterize the strength of associations between response patterns and select goal and participant characteristics. Associations with larger effect sizes (V ≥ 0.25) were highlighted to identify potentially meaningful patterns for future investigation. 25

The CB2 parent trial protocol was published previously and is freely available as open access. 12 The statistical analysis plan for the present secondary responder analysis is available in eSAP 1.

At the goal level (N = 150), 79% of goals (n = 118) met response criteria at 12 months, and over half of goals (51.3%, n = 77) were responsive at all three evaluation time points (Figure 3). Goals that demonstrated response following both intervention blocks were highly likely to show sustained response at 12 months (77 of 78 goals), whereas goals with limited post‐intervention response showed more variable 12‐month outcomes (41 of 72 goals).

Goals represented a wide range of communication activities and contexts, as classified by communication mode, partner, and environment (Table 1). Individual goals could include multiple codes within each category. For communication mode, most goals included a speech target (n = 98, 65.3%), with additional goals involving phone‐based communication (n = 25, 16.7%) and writing or drawing (n = 24, 16.0%). The communication partners in the goals most commonly included family members (n = 75, 50.0%) and close friends (n = 57, 38.0%), with acquaintances (n = 26, 17.3%) and unfamiliar individuals (n = 24, 16.0%) being less common and similar in proportion. Goal contexts and environments were primarily situated in the home (n = 111, 74.0%), the neighborhood (n = 55, 36.7%), and the local community (n = 44, 29.3%).

Exploratory descriptive patterns were observed across goal features and participant characteristics. At the goal level, higher 12‐month response rates were observed for goals involving good friends compared with those that did not (95%, n = 54/57 vs. 69%, n = 64/93; Cramer's V = 0.31). Goals with speech as the primary mode also demonstrated higher 12‐month response (84%, n = 82/98) than those without speech (69%, n = 36/52). Goals occurring in the neighborhood had a 12‐month response of 87% (n = 48/55) versus 74% (n = 70/95) for other environments. Goals directed toward family members (85%, n = 64/75) also demonstrated higher sustained response rates at 12 months than goals directed toward non‐family members (72%, n = 54/75). These patterns are reported descriptively to guide future hypothesis‐driven analyses.

When goal‐level response was examined across participant characteristics, the 12‐month response was high across all PPA subtypes, with the strongest response observed in the agrammatic subtype (83%, n = 35/42) and similar rates in the logopenic (78%, n = 54/69) and semantic subtypes (76%, n = 29/38). Higher goal‐level response rates were observed among male participants across evaluation time points (post‐block 1: 77% vs 60%; post‐block 2: 70% vs 56%; 12‐month: 85% vs 74% for male vs female participants). Higher 12‐month GAS response was also observed among participants with less severe language impairment at baseline, as reflected by higher Western Aphasia Battery‐Aphasia Quotient scores above versus below the median (84% vs 73% GAS response, respectively). These descriptive patterns are presented to inform future investigation in adequately powered studies.

DISCUSSION

The purpose of this study was to characterize participant‐ and goal‐level response patterns within the experimental arm of the CB2 trial after two intervention blocks and at 12 months after enrollment. This secondary responder analysis demonstrated that the communication participation‐based intervention was associated with meaningful goal‐level improvement or maintenance across a heterogeneous group of individuals with PPA, spanning diverse goal types, and sustained over time.

These response patterns are particularly notable in the context of a progressive neurodegenerative disease, in which functional decline is expected and maintenance of everyday abilities represents an important therapeutic target. 24 Recent pharmacological trials in ADRD have reinforced the notion that slowed decline can be clinically meaningful, with modest reductions in cognitive and functional deterioration supporting regulatory approval of disease‐modifying therapies. 26 , 27 Within this broader treatment landscape, these findings underscore the potential for personalized behavioral interventions to achieve patient‐centered functional outcomes, including not only stability but improvement, in progressive neurodegenerative disease.

One of the most notable findings from this analysis was that all participants demonstrated response at one or more evaluation time points. In a condition in which progressive decline is the expected trajectory, the presence of either improvement or stability broadens the conceptualization of intervention success. Recent work in ADRD has emphasized the importance of outcomes that matter to persons living with dementia and their families, including functional ability and participation in everyday life. 28 , 29 , 30 Given the limited pharmacological options for improving language symptoms in PPA, 31 , 32 the CB2 response patterns highlight the potential role of personalized, participation‐focused speech‐language intervention as an important component of care for individuals with mild to moderate PPA.

At 12 months after enrollment, 79% of communication participation goals met response criteria, and over half of the goals showed sustained response across all three evaluation time points. Response was observed across all three PPA subtypes, across mild to moderate baseline severity levels, and across goals targeting different communication modes, partners, and contexts. Together, these findings support the applicability of personalized, goal‐based speech‐language intervention across a phenotypically heterogeneous mild to moderate PPA population. Response rates were highest at the 12‐month evaluation, suggesting that the benefits of the CB intervention may extend beyond the active intervention period. This longer‐term pattern may reflect ongoing use of learned communication strategies, continued practice, and sustained communication partner support. 9 , 12 The dyadic nature of the intervention may be particularly relevant in this regard, as communication partners may continue to provide scaffolding and facilitate successful communication after formal intervention sessions end. 33 , 34 These findings build on the 6‐month maintenance results from the pilot CB1 trial 11 and extend the evidence base for longer‐term benefit of speech‐language intervention in PPA.

Although descriptively higher response rates were observed in some subgroups, including the agrammatic subtype and participants with less baseline impairment, substantial response was evident across all PPA subtypes and across mild to moderate severity range. This breadth of response contrasts with the highly selective inclusion criteria typical of many ADRD clinical trials, which may enhance internal validity but limit ecological validity and restrict inclusion of atypical Alzheimer's disease syndromes such as PPA. 35 Exploratory analyses of goal characteristics also identified several patterns that may inform future research and clinical goal setting. Goals involving speech, familiar communication partners, and familiar contexts were associated with higher sustained response. These findings are consistent with prior work suggesting that communication may be facilitated when contextual supports are strong and cognitive demands are reduced, including through communication partner scaffolding and environmental cues. 33 , 34 , 36 , 37 , 38 Although preliminary, these observations provide clinically meaningful directions for tailoring communication goals and designing future interventions in PPA. Numerically higher response rates were also observed among male participants; however, this finding should be interpreted cautiously given the exploratory nature of the analysis and the likely interrelationship between participant and communication‐partner characteristics in this dyadic sample.

This study also contributes methodologically by demonstrating the feasibility and value of responder analyses in non‐pharmacological intervention research for PPA. Although responder analyses have been widely used in pharmacological trials, 15 , 39 their application to non‐pharmacological interventions remains limited. 16 By operationalizing response thresholds that capture both improvement following active intervention and maintenance as a longer‐term outcome, this approach offers a framework for characterizing individual‐level intervention effects that may complement group‐level efficacy analyses and support movement toward more individualized, precision‐oriented models of care in neurodegenerative disease. 40

These findings also have implications for clinical care and long‐term management. Clinicians can counsel individuals with mild to moderate PPA and their families that personalized, participation‐focused speech‐language intervention may yield benefits across subtypes, severity levels, and diverse communication goals. Clinicians should set expectations for variable response trajectories, recognizing that benefits may emerge after the first intervention block or over time and that maintenance of function at 12 months represents a positive outcome in progressive disease. 24 Future research examining baseline characteristics associated with response patterns may further refine clinical recommendations and support individualized intervention planning. These findings may also help inform future appropriate use criteria by identifying which participants, goals, and time points are most likely to demonstrate meaningful response.

This study has limitations. The moderate sample size limited inferential testing, and observed response patterns should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis‐generating. Findings were restricted to individuals with mild to moderate PPA who had a communication partner willing to participate, which may limit generalizability. In addition, the response thresholds were selected based on clinical reasoning related to meaningful change, and alternative threshold definitions may yield different classifications. Future studies should include individuals with more advanced disease, test specific predictors of response in adequately powered samples, and integrate richer biomarker and baseline clinical data to refine individualized intervention strategies.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that personalized, participation‐focused speech‐language intervention is associated with meaningful improvement or sustained maintenance across diverse communication goals and participant profiles in PPA. The broad pattern of response, including response at one or more evaluation time points in all participants and sustained response in a high proportion of goals at 12 months, supports the clinical and translational relevance of the CB approach and provides a foundation for future work aimed at optimizing individualized intervention delivery. As dementia care increasingly emphasizes person‐centered and precision‐based approaches, responder analyses such as this may help tailor interventions and maximize functional communication outcomes for individuals living with PPA and related ADRD syndromes.
