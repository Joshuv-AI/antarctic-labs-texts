Plasma proteomics predicts pathological complete response and reveals LIF as a potential mediator of resistance to neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy in resectable NSCLC
Authors: Shoucheng Feng, Ziqing Feng, Songzuo Xie, Yuheng Zhou, Weizhen Sun, Nengqi Lin, Zhichao Lin, Qinglin Wang, Zerui Zhao, Yaobin Lin, Hao Long
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (nICT) is increasingly used for resectable non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), yet a substantial proportion of patients fail to achieve pathological complete response (pCR). Clinically scalable, minimally invasive biomarkers that predict response and point to actionable resistance mechanisms remain needed.MethodsPretreatment plasma samples collected prior to the first dose were profiled using the Olink proximity extension assay in a Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center cohort (n=86), randomly split into a training set (n=65) and an internal validation set (n=21). Differentially abundant proteins associated with pCR were identified, and predictive models were developed using logistic regression, random forest, and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), followed by internal validation and independent external validation in the Jiangmen Central Hospital cohort (n=46). Survival associations were evaluated by Cox regression. Mechanistic analyses integrated tumor immunohistochemistry/multiplex immunofluorescence, bulk RNA sequencing with immune deconvolution, and functional validation in subcutaneous and orthotopic murine lung cancer models with pharmacologic leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) blockade.ResultsFour candidate proteins (LIF, CXCL1, CX3CL1, and NT-3) showed modest single-marker discrimination for pCR (area under the curve (AUC), 0.611–0.673). Multiprotein models improved prediction, with XGBoost achieving the highest performance (training AUC=0.95; internal validation AUC=0.845 (95% CI 0.65 to 1.00); external validation AUC=0.801 (95% CI, 0.57 to 0.93)). SHapley Additive exPlanations analysis identified LIF as the dominant feature negatively associated with pCR probability. Elevated baseline plasma LIF was associated with inferior overall survival (HR=13.003; 95% CI 1.456 to 116.135; p=0.0217) and progression-free survival (HR=3.75; 95% CI 1.362 to 10.327; p=0.0105) after nICT. High LIF tumors exhibited reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration and suppressed cytotoxic effector programs. In murine models, LIF blockade synergized with immunochemotherapy to enhance antitumor efficacy, restore CD8+ T-cell effector function, and mitigate T-cell exhaustion.ConclusionsBaseline plasma proteomics shows promise for predicting pCR to nICT in resectable NSCLC and nominates LIF as a predictive, prognostic, and therapeutic candidate mediator of resistance. These findings warrant prospective validation and further investigation of LIF-targeted combination strategies in the neoadjuvant setting.Keywords: Biomarker, Lung Cancer, Immunotherapy, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor

Introduction

Neoadjuvant immunochemotherapy (nICT) has become a standard treatment strategy for resectable and locally advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), significantly improving pathological response and survival outcomes compared with chemotherapy alone.1 2 Multiple landmark randomized trials have demonstrated that the addition of immune checkpoint inhibitors targeting the programmed cell death 1 (PD-1) or programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) axis to platinum-based chemotherapy markedly increases pathological complete response (pCR) rates and prolongs event-free survival (EFS). Importantly, accumulating evidence supports pCR as a clinically meaningful surrogate endpoint in the neoadjuvant setting, correlating with favorable long-term outcomes, including overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival, across multiple solid tumors, including NSCLC.3 4 Despite these advances, a substantial proportion of patients still fail to achieve pCR and experience early recurrence or progression, underscoring pronounced interpatient heterogeneity in treatment response and the need for robust predictive biomarkers that can also reveal actionable resistance mechanisms.

Current efforts to predict response to nICT in NSCLC have primarily focused on tumor-based biomarkers, such as PD-L1 expression, tumor mutational burden, and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes.5,7 While these tissue-derived features provide valuable insights into tumor–immune interactions, their predictive performance remains inconsistent, and real-world implementation is constrained by intratumoral heterogeneity, sampling bias, and the limited feasibility of repeated biopsies.8 In contrast, circulating biomarkers offer a minimally invasive and clinically scalable means to capture systemic immune and inflammatory states that may shape intratumoral immune set points and influence early treatment trajectories.9 Plasma proteomic profiling, particularly using proximity extension assay (PEA)-based platforms, enables high-throughput, sensitive quantification of immune-related proteins and cytokines and has shown promise in predicting response to immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) in advanced cancers.10,12 However, whether baseline plasma proteomic signatures can reliably predict pathological response and long-term outcomes specifically in the nICT setting remains incompletely defined, and clinically translatable circulating mediators that link prediction with actionable biology are still lacking.

Leukemia inhibitory factor (LIF) is a pleiotropic cytokine belonging to the interleukin (IL)-6 family and has emerged as a key regulator of tumor progression, immune modulation, and therapeutic resistance across multiple malignancies.13 14 LIF signaling has been shown to promote tumor cell survival, activate protumorigenic inflammatory pathways, and establish an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by impairing cytotoxic T cell function and promoting immune exhaustion.15 Importantly, LIF is not merely a mechanistic mediator but has become a clinically actionable target, with LIF-neutralizing antibodies and inhibitors of the LIF–LIF receptor (LIFR) axis having advanced into early-phase clinical trials in pancreatic cancer.16 These developments provide a strong rationale to test whether circulating LIF captures intrinsic resistance to nICT, predicts pathological response and clinical outcomes, and functionally contributes to treatment failure in NSCLC—questions that remain largely unexplored.

In this study, we performed comprehensive baseline plasma proteomic profiling using PEA technology in patients with NSCLC treated with nICT to identify circulating protein signatures associated with pCR. We subsequently constructed and validated plasma protein-based predictive models using multiple machine learning approaches across independent cohorts. Building on these clinical observations, we investigated the prognostic relevance of key candidate proteins and focused on elucidating the biological role of LIF through integrated analyses of patient plasma samples, tumor tissues, bulk transcriptomic profiling, and immune landscape characterization. Finally, using both subcutaneous and orthotopic murine lung cancer models, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of LIF blockade in combination with immunochemotherapy and delineated its impact on CD8+ T cell infiltration, effector function, and exhaustion. Collectively, our work supports baseline plasma proteomics may have utility for response stratification and nominates LIF as a circulating biomarker with predictive, prognostic, and therapeutic relevance in the neoadjuvant setting.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient cohorts

This retrospective multicenter cohort study was conducted to identify baseline circulating plasma proteins associated with pCR and survival outcomes in patients with resectable NSCLC treated with nICT, and to functionally investigate the role of LIF in therapeutic resistance.

Consecutive patients with clinical stage IB–III NSCLC who received standardized nICT followed by curative-intent surgery between October 2019 and April 2024 were screened. Inclusion criteria: (1) histologically confirmed NSCLC without actionable driver gene alterations; (2) clinical stage IB–III disease deemed resectable by multidisciplinary evaluation; (3) receipt of PD-1 inhibitor combined with platinum-based doublet chemotherapy; (4) availability of pretreatment plasma samples; (5) completion of surgical resection with pathological evaluation. Exclusion criteria: (1) prior systemic antitumor therapy; (2) active autoimmune disease requiring immunosuppression; (3) inadequate plasma samples or incomplete clinicopathologic data. A total of 86 eligible patients with available pretreatment plasma samples were retrospectively enrolled from Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC cohort). The SYSUCC cohort was first used as the discovery cohort for identifying candidate plasma proteins associated with pCR. After candidate protein selection, patients in the SYSUCC cohort were randomly assigned at an approximate 3:1 ratio into a training cohort (n=65) and an internal validation cohort (n=21) using a computer-generated random sequence for model construction and internal performance assessment. Because candidate protein selection was performed before this split, the internal validation cohort was not considered a fully independent validation cohort. An independent external validation cohort comprising 46 patients was obtained from Jiangmen Central Hospital (JMCH cohort). The JMCH cohort was not involved in candidate protein selection, model fitting, or parameter tuning, and was used for independent external validation of the finalized plasma protein-based predictive model. Baseline clinicopathological characteristics of all cohorts are summarized in online supplemental table S1.

nICT consisted of platinum-based doublet chemotherapy plus a PD-1 inhibitor administered every 3 weeks for four cycles, followed by surgical resection. The PD-1 inhibitors used in this study included nivolumab, pembrolizumab, sintilimab, and tislelizumab. Chemotherapy regimens were selected according to tumor histology and institutional practice. Patients with LUAD generally received platinum plus pemetrexed, whereas patients with lung squamous cell carcinoma received platinum combined with a taxane-based regimen. Postoperative/adjuvant therapy consisted primarily of monthly maintenance immunotherapy after surgery when clinically indicated. Peripheral blood samples were collected immediately prior to initiation of systemic therapy to capture baseline systemic immune status. Pathological response was independently assessed by two dedicated thoracic pathologists blinded to plasma proteomic results. pCR was defined as the absence of viable tumor cells in both the primary tumor and sampled lymph nodes (ypT0N0).

Plasma proteomic profiling

Peripheral blood was collected in EDTA tubes (BD Biosciences) and centrifuged to isolate plasma, which was stored at −80°C until analysis. Plasma proteomic profiling was performed using the Olink PEA platform with the Olink Inflammation panel (Olink Proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Quality control (QC) was performed in accordance with Olink guidelines. Briefly, four internal controls were included in each sample to monitor assay performance and sample quality. QC was conducted at both the plate and sample levels. At the plate level, the SD of internal controls was required to be <0.2 normalized protein expression (NPX). At the sample level, samples with deviation from the median of internal controls <0.3 NPX were considered to pass QC. Only plates and samples meeting these criteria were included in downstream analyses. At the protein level, proteins with a high proportion of measurements below the limit of detection (>25%) or failing predefined Olink QC criteria were excluded. Protein abundance was reported as NPX values, which are provided on a log2 scale. The complete list of proteins included in the Olink Inflammation panel is provided in online supplemental table S2.

Differential proteomic analysis and pathway enrichment

Baseline plasma protein levels were compared between pCR and non-pCR patients in the overall SYSUCC cohort as a discovery analysis for candidate protein identification. Differential protein expression was assessed using a two-sided Student’s t-test. Proteins with p value <0.05 were considered differentially expressed. P values and differences in average protein expression between groups were visualized using volcano plots. Functional enrichment analysis of upregulated proteins was conducted using the clusterProfiler package (V.3.18.1) in R.

Construction and validation of pCR prediction models

Candidate plasma proteins were first identified from differential proteomic analyses in the overall SYSUCC discovery cohort. After candidate protein selection, the SYSUCC cohort was randomly divided into a training cohort (n=65) and an internal assessment cohort (n=21). Multivariable logistic regression, random forest, and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) models were constructed in the training cohort using the preselected candidate proteins. The class distribution of pCR and non-pCR cases is provided in online supplemental table S1. Within the training cohort, model development incorporated stratified k-fold cross-validation, and hyperparameters for machine learning models were optimized using grid search with cross-validation. Model performance in the training cohort was assessed using cross-validated area under the curve (AUC) to mitigate overfitting. The final models were then applied without refitting to the internal validation cohort (n=21) and an independent external validation cohort (n=46). Because candidate protein selection was performed before the SYSUCC cohort split, the internal validation cohort was interpreted as an internal performance assessment rather than a fully independent validation cohort. By contrast, the JMCH cohort was not used for candidate protein selection, model fitting, or hyperparameter tuning and therefore served as the independent external validation cohort. Model calibration was evaluated using calibration plots, and clinical utility was assessed using decision curve analysis. Given the sample size, the models should be considered exploratory and require further validation.

Survival analysis

OS and progression-free survival (PFS) were calculated from the date of initiation of nICT to death, disease progression, or last follow-up. Patients were stratified into high-expression and low-expression groups based on median baseline plasma protein levels. Median expression was used as a predefined cut-off without outcome-driven optimization. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared by log-rank test. Univariable Cox regression analyses were performed to assess the associations between baseline plasma proteins and survival outcomes. Multivariable Cox regression analyses adjusted for the other candidate plasma proteins. Because the number of survival events, particularly OS events, was limited, full multivariable adjustment for clinicopathologic variables and postoperative therapy was not performed to avoid overfitting and unstable estimates. Therefore, the survival analyses were considered exploratory.

Plasma LIF measurement

Baseline plasma LIF concentrations were measured using a human LIF ELISA kit (Neobioscience) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and concentrations were calculated from standard curves. All samples were assayed in duplicate.

Immunohistochemistry

Pretreatment tumor biopsy specimens were formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 4 µm thickness. Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval, followed by incubation with an anti-LIF primary antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, UK). After incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) -conjugated secondary antibodies (ZSGB-BIO, Beijing, China), immunoreactivity was visualized using a chromogenic detection system. LIF expression was semi-quantitatively evaluated using an immunoreactive score (IRS) based on staining intensity and the proportion of positive tumor cells. Two pathologists blinded to clinical outcomes independently evaluated slides, and discrepancies were resolved by consensus.

Multiplex immunofluorescence

Multiplex immunofluorescence was performed using the Opal multiplex immunohistochemistry (IHC) kit (Akoya Biosciences). Primary antibodies included anti-LIF (Abcam), anti-CD8 (Cell Signaling Technology), anti-interferon-gamma (anti-IFN-γ) (Abcam), and anti-GZMB (Abcam). Nuclei were counterstained with 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Slides were scanned using a multispectral imaging system (Akoya Biosciences), and immune cell density and functional subsets were quantified.

TCGA-based immune correlation analysis

Publicly available transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC) cohorts were analyzed. The relative enrichment of immune cell populations was estimated using established computational immune deconvolution approaches based on bulk RNA sequencing data. Correlation analyses were performed to assess the associations between LIF expression and the inferred abundance of multiple immune cell subsets, as well as with cytotoxic immune markers, including CD8A, IFNG, and GZMB. Correlation coefficients were calculated using Spearman’s rank correlation, and statistical significance was determined using two-sided tests. These analyses were used to evaluate the relationship between LIF expression and cytotoxic immune activity in the tumor microenvironment.

Murine lung cancer models and treatment protocols

Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cells were used to establish subcutaneous and orthotopic lung tumor models. For the subcutaneous model, 1×106 LLC cells were injected into the right flank. When tumors reached approximately 100 mm3, mice were randomized to treatment groups (Untreated, anti-LIF, immunochemotherapy (IC), or IC+anti LIF; n=6 mice per group) using a computer-generated randomization sequence. Investigators performing tumor measurements and downstream endpoint analyses were blinded to group allocation. Anti-LIF neutralizing antibody (R&D Systems) and anti-PD-1 antibody (Bio X Cell) were administered intraperitoneally at 200 µg per mouse two times per week (days 3 and 7). Chemotherapy consisted of carboplatin (100 mg/kg) and pemetrexed (100 mg/kg), administered intraperitoneally once weekly on day 3. Combination treatment was given according to the same dosing schedule. Tumor size was measured with digital calipers, and tumor volume was calculated as (length × width2/2). Body weight was recorded at each measurement to monitor treatment-related toxicity. For the orthotopic lung model, LLC-luciferase cells (1×105) were injected intrapulmonarily and mice were randomized to the same four treatment arms (n=5 mice per group). Tumor burden was monitored by bioluminescence imaging (PerkinElmer) at indicated time points, and total flux was quantified using Living Image software. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee (ACUC) of the SYSUCC (approval number: L102042026010I). The maximal permitted tumor volume was 1500 mm³, as specified in the approved ACUC protocol. This limit was not exceeded in any experiment.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Tumors were dissociated into single-cell suspensions by enzymatic digestion and mechanical disruption. Cell viability (>85%) was confirmed prior to downstream assays. For single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), subcutaneous tumors from mice treated with IC or IC plus anti-LIF were processed (one sample per group). Single-cell libraries were prepared using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ platform (10x Genomics) and sequenced. After QC, 33,302 cells were retained (IC, 16,536; IC+anti LIF, 16,766). Raw data were processed with Cell Ranger (10x Genomics) and aligned to the mm10 reference genome. Downstream analyses were performed in Seurat (V.5.3.0). Cells were filtered using nFeature_RNA >300 and mitochondrial reads <15%, and doublets were removed using scDblFinder. Data were normalized with SCTransform and clustered based on principal components using a shared nearest neighbor graph. Cell types were annotated according to canonical markers. Module scores for cytotoxicity, proliferation, and exhaustion were computed.

Flow cytometry

For flow cytometry, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes were stained with antibodies against CD8, IFN-γ, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), GZMB, perforin, PD-1, and LAG3 (BioLegend). For intracellular cytokine staining, cells were stimulated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA)/ionomycin in the presence of brefeldin A/monensin for 4 hours prior to fixation/permeabilization. Data were acquired on a BD flow cytometer and analyzed using FlowJo. The gating strategy is provided in online supplemental figure S1.

Bulk RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from pretreatment primary tumor specimens from 25 patients using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA). RNA libraries were prepared using standard protocols and sequenced on an Illumina platform. Gene expression levels were quantified and normalized as transcripts per million. Differential expression analyses were performed between predefined groups, with multiple testing controlled by the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure; genes with false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 were considered differentially expressed. Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses were conducted to characterize biological processes associated with LIF expression. Immune cell composition was inferred from bulk transcriptomic data using CIBERSORT. In addition, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed to identify LIF-associated transcriptional programs.

Study cohorts and sample overlap

To clarify the relationships among the analytical cohorts, a patient/sample flow diagram was provided in online supplemental figure S2. Pretreatment plasma Olink proteomic profiling was performed in the SYSUCC cohort (n=86) and an independent external JMCH cohort (n=46). The SYSUCC cohort was randomly divided into a training cohort (n=65) and an internal validation cohort (n=21), while the JMCH cohort was used for external validation of the plasma protein-based predictive model. ELISA was performed in 77 patients, all of whom overlapped with the SYSUCC Olink cohort; therefore, this analysis was considered orthogonal assay-level validation rather than independent cohort-level validation. For tissue-based analyses, IHC was performed in 26 patients with available tumor specimens, all of whom overlapped with the Olink cohort. Bulk RNA-seq was further performed in 25 patients, all of whom overlapped with the IHC cohort. Accordingly, the IHC and bulk RNA-seq analyses were interpreted as tissue-level correlative analyses supporting the biological relevance of LIF and its association with the tumor immune microenvironment.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (V.4.5.1) and GraphPad Prism (V.10.1.2). Unless otherwise stated, data are presented as mean±SEM. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups, Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed data; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. For comparisons among more than two groups, one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test was used for parametric data, whereas the Kruskal-Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparisons test was used for non-parametric data.

Results

Baseline plasma proteomic profiling identifies differential immune-related proteins associated with pCR

We first investigated whether baseline systemic immune states are associated with pathological response to nICT. Pretreatment plasma samples from 86 patients (SYSUCC cohort) were subjected to Olink PEA-based proteomic profiling. Patients were stratified according to pathological response into the pCR and non-pCR groups. Differential expression analysis revealed a distinct plasma proteomic signature between the two groups (figure 1A, online supplemental table S3). Several immune-related proteins exhibited significant differences in baseline NPX levels. Notably, CX3CL1 was significantly elevated in patients who achieved pCR, whereas CXCL1, LIF, and neurotrophin-3 (NT-3) were significantly enriched in the non-pCR group, suggesting a potential association between these circulating factors and treatment resistance. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering based on differentially expressed proteins demonstrated clear segregation between pCR and non-pCR patients (figure 1B), indicating that baseline plasma proteomic profiles may reflect intrinsic biological differences relevant to treatment response. Consistent with the volcano plot results, individual comparisons confirmed significantly higher baseline levels of CXCL1, LIF, and NT-3 in non-pCR patients, while CX3CL1 was enriched in the pCR group (figure 1C). These proteins are known to be involved in chemokine signaling, inflammatory regulation, and immune cell trafficking, implying their potential roles in shaping the pretreatment immune milieu. Functional enrichment analyses further supported these observations. GO biological process analysis revealed significant enrichment of pathways related to chemokine-mediated signaling, positive regulation of MAPK cascade, leukocyte chemotaxis, and NT-associated biological processes (figure 1D). KEGG pathway analysis identified cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction, TNF signaling, MAPK signaling, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways as the most significantly enriched pathways (figure 1E).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that pretreatment plasma proteomic profiles differ markedly between pCR and non-pCR patients, with dysregulation of cytokine and chemokine signaling pathways potentially contributing to differential therapeutic responses to nICT.

Development and evaluation of plasma protein-based models for pathological complete response prediction

To further evaluate the predictive potential of baseline plasma proteins identified in figure 1, receiver operating characteristic analyses were first performed for individual candidate biomarkers. As shown in figure 2A, LIF, CX3CL1, CXCL1, and NT-3 each demonstrated modest discrimination for predicting pCR, with AUC values of 0.611, 0.638, 0.673, and 0.636, respectively, indicating limited predictive performance when used as single biomarkers. To improve prediction accuracy, multivariable pCR prediction models were subsequently constructed using logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost algorithms based on the selected plasma protein features. In the training cohort, the logistic regression model achieved an AUC of 0.782, while the XGBoost model demonstrated high discrimination with an AUC of 0.95 (figure 2B,D). For the random forest model, the out-of-bag AUC was 0.716 (figure 2C). In internal validation, model performance declined to varying extents but remained acceptable, with AUCs of 0.673 (95% CI 0.42 to 0.93) for logistic regression, 0.745 (95% CI 0.51 to 0.98) for random forest, and 0.845 (95% CI 0.65 to 1.00) for XGBoost. These results suggest that multivariable plasma protein-based models improved predictive performance compared with individual biomarkers, although the relatively high training performance of the XGBoost model indicates potential model optimism.

The robustness and generalizability of these models were further evaluated in an independent external validation cohort (JMCH cohort). As shown in figure 2E, the XGBoost model achieved the highest AUC (AUC=0.801; 95% CI 0.67 to 0.93), followed by logistic regression (AUC=0.776; 95% CI 0.64 to 0.91) and random forest (AUC=0.687; 95% CI 0.53 to 0.85). However, the difference between XGBoost and logistic regression was not statistically significant (DeLong test, p=0.758). In contrast, both XGBoost and logistic regression significantly outperformed the random forest model (p=0.041 and p=0.047, respectively) (online supplemental table S4).

To further benchmark the plasma protein-based model against routinely available clinicopathological variables, we evaluated the predictive performance of individual clinical features and a clinical-only logistic regression model. As shown in online supplemental figure S3A, single clinical variables showed limited discriminatory ability for pCR prediction, with AUC values of 0.556 for gender, 0.469 for age, 0.561 for smoking history, 0.589 for histology, 0.579 for PD-L1 expression, 0.503 for clinical tumor, node, metastases (TNM) stage, and 0.548 for interval time to surgery. These results indicate that no individual conventional clinical factor provided sufficient predictive power for identifying patients who achieved pCR after nICT.

We next constructed a clinical-only logistic regression model incorporating gender, age, PD-L1 expression, clinical TNM stage, and histology. The clinical-only model achieved an AUC of 0.734 (95% CI 0.61 to 0.86) in the training cohort; however, its performance decreased substantially in the internal and external validation cohorts, with AUCs of 0.555 (95% CI 0.29 to 0.82) and 0.586 (95% CI 0.41 to 0.76), respectively (online supplemental figure S3B). In contrast, the Olink-only logistic regression model based on CX3CL1, CXCL1, LIF, and NT-3 achieved an AUC of 0.776 in the external validation cohort. Direct comparison in the external validation cohort demonstrated that the Olink-only model outperformed the clinical-only model for pCR prediction, with a statistically significant difference in AUCs by the DeLong test (AUC, 0.776 vs 0.586; p=0.044; online supplemental table S5). These findings suggest that baseline plasma proteomic features provide additional predictive information beyond standard clinicopathological variables and may better capture the systemic immune state associated with pathological response to nICT.

To enhance model interpretability, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis was performed for the XGBoost model. The SHAP summary plot revealed differential contributions of the four plasma proteins to pCR prediction, with higher LIF levels exerting a consistently negative impact on pCR probability, whereas CX3CL1 levels were associated with a higher likelihood of achieving pCR. NT-3 showed a comparatively modest contribution to the model output (figure 2F). Model calibration was subsequently assessed to evaluate the agreement between predicted and observed pCR probabilities. Calibration plots demonstrated good concordance for all three models in the training cohort (figure 2G). Although increased deviation was observed in the internal validation cohort (figure 2H), acceptable calibration performance was maintained in the external validation cohort, particularly for the logistic regression and XGBoost models (figure 2I), supporting stable probability estimation across datasets. Additional calibration metrics, including calibration intercept, calibration slope, and Brier score, are reported in online supplemental table S6.

Finally, decision curve analysis was conducted to assess the potential clinical utility of the predictive models. In the training cohort, the XGBoost model provided the highest net benefit across a broad range of threshold probabilities compared with logistic regression and random forest models (figure 2J). Consistent trends were observed in both internal (figure 2K) and external validation cohorts (figure 2L), indicating that plasma protein-based models may provide clinically useful risk stratification for identifying patients more likely to achieve pCR after nICT.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that baseline plasma proteomic models outperform individual biomarkers and conventional clinicopathological predictors for pCR prediction. Although the XGBoost model showed the highest apparent discrimination, its external validation performance was close to that of the parsimonious logistic regression model. Therefore, the current findings support the potential value of circulating proteomic profiling for pCR prediction, while also highlighting the need for further prospective validation before clinical implementation.

Baseline plasma LIF is associated with inferior survival outcomes in patients with NSCLC treated with nICT

To further determine whether baseline plasma proteins associated with pathological response also possessed prognostic value, survival analyses were performed in patients with NSCLC receiving nICT. OS and PFS were evaluated according to pretreatment plasma protein expression levels in the SYSUCC cohort (n=86). The median follow-up time was 26 months, during which 8 deaths and 21 progression/recurrence/death events were observed. Kaplan-Meier analyses demonstrated that baseline CXCL1 expression was not significantly associated with OS or PFS (OS: p=0.65; PFS: p=0.66; figure 3A,B). In contrast, elevated baseline LIF expression was significantly associated with inferior survival outcomes. Patients with high LIF levels exhibited significantly reduced OS (p=0.037; figure 3C) and markedly shorter PFS (p=0.0095; figure 3D), indicating that circulating LIF before treatment initiation is associated with unfavorable survival outcomes following nICT. Baseline NT-3 expression showed no significant association with OS or PFS (OS: p=0.90; PFS: p=0.10; figure 3E,F), although a trend toward improved PFS was observed in patients with lower NT-3 levels. Similarly, CX3CL1 expression was not significantly correlated with either OS or PFS in this nICT cohort (OS: p=0.87; PFS: p=0.62; figure 3G,H).

Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were further performed to explore the associations between baseline plasma proteins and survival outcomes. In univariable analysis for OS, baseline LIF expression showed a trend toward increased mortality risk (HR=6.81, 95% CI 0.847 to 54.733, p=0.0712), whereas CXCL1 (HR=0.732, 95% CI 0.193 to 2.777, p=0.647), CX3CL1 (HR=1.121, 95% CI 0.300 to 4.191, p=0.865), and NT-3 (HR=0.916, 95% CI 0.243 to 3.452, p=0.897) were not significantly associated with OS (figure 3I).

Importantly, multivariable Cox regression analysis confirmed that baseline LIF remained associated with poorer OS after adjustment for the other plasma proteins (HR=13.003, 95% CI 1.456 to 116.135, p=0.0217; figure 3J). However, this estimate should be interpreted cautiously because of the limited number of death events and the wide CI. In contrast, CXCL1 (HR=0.263, 95% CI 0.058 to 1.195, p=0.0837), CX3CL1 (HR=0.869, 95% CI 0.219 to 3.456, p=0.843), and NT-3 (HR=0.878, 95% CI 0.234 to 3.301, p=0.847) were not significantly associated with OS after adjustment for the other proteins. Consistent findings were observed for PFS. In univariable Cox analysis, high baseline LIF expression was significantly associated with an increased risk of disease progression (HR=3.177, 95% CI 1.260 to 8.009, p=0.0143; figure 3K), whereas CXCL1 (HR=1.198, p=0.659), CX3CL1 (HR=0.817, p=0.621), and NT-3 (HR=0.499, p=0.109) were not significantly associated with PFS. In multivariable Cox regression adjusting for the other candidate plasma proteins, LIF remained associated with inferior PFS in patients with NSCLC treated with nICT (HR=3.75, 95% CI 1.362 to 10.327, p=0.0105; figure 3L), while the other plasma proteins remained non-significant.

Collectively, these results suggest that elevated baseline circulating LIF is associated with inferior OS and PFS in patients receiving nICT. Given the limited number of survival events and the absence of full clinicopathologic multivariable adjustment, these survival analyses should be interpreted as exploratory.

Elevated LIF is associated with poor pathological response and impaired CD8+ T cell infiltration and function

To further elucidate the biological mechanisms underlying the adverse predictive and prognostic impact of LIF, we examined the relationship between LIF expression, pathological response, and the tumor immune microenvironment in patients with NSCLC treated with nICT, using both plasma-based and tissue-based analyses. In a cohort of 77 patients with available pretreatment plasma samples, baseline plasma LIF levels measured by ELISA were significantly higher in non-pCR patients compared with those achieving pCR (figure 4A, p<0.0001). Survival analyses in this plasma cohort showed that elevated LIF levels were associated with inferior clinical outcomes. Specifically, among 77 evaluable patients, 7 OS events were observed, including 1 event in the LIF-low group and 6 events in the LIF-high group, and high plasma LIF was associated with worse OS (p=0.036). For PFS, 22 events were observed, including 6 events in the LIF-low group and 16 events in the LIF-high group, and high plasma LIF was associated with significantly shorter PFS (p=0.0026) (figure 4B). To further assess these observations at the tissue level, pretreatment tumor biopsy specimens from 26 patients with available tissue samples were analyzed by IHC. Representative images demonstrated markedly higher LIF expression in tumors from non-pCR patients compared with pCR cases (figure 4C). Consistently, tumors with high LIF expression were significantly enriched in the non-pCR group (figure 4D, p=0.0154), and LIF IRSs were significantly higher in non-pCR tumors than in pCR tumors (figure 4E, p=0.0137). Survival analyses based on tumor LIF expression in this IHC cohort showed similar trends to those observed in the plasma cohort. Among 26 evaluable patients, 3 OS events occurred, all in the LIF-high group, whereas no OS event was observed in the LIF-low group; high tumor LIF expression showed a trend toward worse OS (p=0.068). For PFS, six events were observed, including one event in the LIF-low group and five events in the LIF-high group, with high tumor LIF expression showing a trend toward shorter PFS (p=0.064) (figure 4F). Moreover, a positive correlation was observed between plasma LIF levels and tumor LIF expression assessed by IHC (R=0.58, p=0.0019, figure 4G). To explore the immunological basis of LIF-associated treatment resistance, we next examined the relationship between LIF expression and immune cell infiltration using transcriptomic data from TCGA lung cancer cohorts (LUAD and LUSC). As shown in online supplemental figure S4, TCGA-based immune deconvolution analyses revealed that LIF expression was negatively correlated with CD8+ T cell enrichment (R = −0.229, p=0.001), as well as with cytotoxic effector molecules including IFN-γ (R = −0.061, p=0.049) and GZMB (R = −0.097, p=0.024). These in silico findings suggested that elevated LIF expression is associated with an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment characterized by impaired CD8+ T cell-mediated cytotoxic activity. Guided by these observations, multiplex immunofluorescence analysis was performed to directly assess CD8+ T cell infiltration and functional status in pretreatment tumor tissues. Quantitative analyses demonstrated that tumors with high LIF expression exhibited significantly reduced CD8+ T cell density (p<0.0001), as well as markedly lower proportions of CD8+GZMB+ cells (p<0.0001) and CD8+IFN-γ+ cells (p<0.0001) compared with LIF-low tumors (figure 4H,I).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that elevated LIF, assessed by both plasma ELISA and tumor biopsy IHC, is significantly associated with poor pathological response, inferior survival outcomes, and impaired CD8+ T cell infiltration and effector function in patients with NSCLC receiving nICT.

LIF blockade enhances the antitumor efficacy of immunochemotherapy and is associated with exploratory changes in T-cell states

To investigate whether LIF blockade could enhance the antitumor activity of immunochemotherapy, the therapeutic impact of LIF blockade was evaluated in murine lung cancer models. In a subcutaneous LLC tumor model, mice were treated with vehicle control, anti-LIF antibody, IC, or IC combined with anti-LIF according to the indicated treatment schedule (figure 5A). While IC alone significantly suppressed tumor growth compared with untreated controls, the addition of anti-LIF resulted in a further and more pronounced reduction in tumor burden. Both gross tumor images and longitudinal tumor volume measurements demonstrated that IC plus anti-LIF achieved the greatest tumor growth inhibition among all treatment groups (figure 5B). Consistent results were observed in an orthotopic lung tumor model (figure 5C). Bioluminescence imaging revealed that IC plus anti-LIF markedly reduced intrapulmonary tumor burden compared with IC alone, whereas anti-LIF monotherapy exerted only modest antitumor effects (figure 5D). Together, these findings indicate that LIF blockade synergizes with immunochemotherapy to enhance antitumor efficacy in vivo. To explore potential immune changes associated with IC plus anti-LIF treatment, scRNA-seq was performed on subcutaneous tumors harvested from mice treated with IC or IC combined with anti-LIF. Unsupervised clustering identified multiple immune cell populations, including B cells, dendritic cells, myeloid cells, natural killer (NK) cells, plasma cells, and T cells (figure 5E). Given the central role of T cells in antitumor immunity, subsequent analyses focused on the T cell compartment. Subclustering of T cells revealed distinct functional states, including naïve, central memory, effector memory, tissue-resident memory, precursor exhausted, exhausted (Tex), effector, and regulatory T cells (figure 5F). Analysis of immune checkpoint and costimulatory molecule expression demonstrated state-specific regulatory landscapes across T cell subsets (figure 5G). Importantly, treatment with IC plus anti-LIF was associated with an apparent shift in T cell state composition compared with IC alone. Tumors from the IC plus anti-LIF group exhibited increased proportions of effector and memory T cell subsets, accompanied by a relative reduction in Tex (figure 5H). Consistently, functional scoring analyses suggested that T cells from the IC plus anti-LIF group may exhibit higher cytotoxicity and proliferation scores, together with reduced exhaustion scores, compared with those from the IC-treated group (figure 5I).

The single-cell findings should be considered hypothesis-generating and require validation in datasets with biological replication.

LIF blockade restores CD8+ T cell effector function and reduces exhaustion in vivo

To directly assess the impact of LIF blockade on CD8+ T cell function in vivo, tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes from subcutaneous LLC tumors were analyzed by flow cytometry and multiplex immunofluorescence. The gating strategy for identification of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells and functional subsets is shown in online supplemental figure S1.

Flow cytometric analysis revealed that combined immunochemotherapy and LIF blockade (IC+anti LIF) markedly increased the proportion of polyfunctional CD8+ T cells producing IFN-γ and TNF-α compared with untreated mice, and IC alone (figure 6A; all comparisons p<0.0001). Similarly, the frequency of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells coexpressing GZMB and perforin was significantly elevated in the IC+anti-LIF group relative to IC alone and untreated group (figure 6B; p<0.0001). In contrast, expression of the exhaustion markers PD-1 and LAG3 on tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells was significantly reduced following LIF blockade. Mice treated with IC+anti-LIF exhibited a markedly lower proportion of PD-1+LAG3+ exhausted CD8+ T cells compared with IC alone and control groups (figure 6C; p<0.0001). These findings were further corroborated by multiplex immunofluorescence analysis of tumor tissues. Representative images demonstrated substantially increased CD8+ T cell infiltration in tumors treated with IC+anti-LIF, accompanied by enhanced expression of IFN-γ and GZMB (figure 6D). Quantitative analysis confirmed that IC+anti-LIF significantly increased CD8+ T cell density, as well as the proportions of CD8+IFN-γ+ and CD8+GZMB+ effector cells, compared with IC alone and untreated group (figure 6E; all comparisons p<0.0001).

Differential expression analysis between non-pCR and pCR tumors revealed a distinct transcriptional landscape (figure 7A). Notably, LIF expression was significantly elevated in non-pCR tumors compared with pCR tumors (figure 7B), suggesting a potential association between high baseline LIF expression and suboptimal pathological response.

Correlation analyses further demonstrated that LIF expression was inversely associated with key cytotoxic effector genes, including IFNG (R = −0.42, p=0.031; figure 7C) and GZMB (R = −0.49, p=0.013; figure 7D), indicating that elevated LIF expression is linked to impaired cytotoxic immune activity at the transcriptional level. To further characterize LIF-associated immune and signaling programs, tumors were stratified into LIF-high and LIF-low groups (n=10 per group) for downstream analyses. The 10 tumors with the highest LIF expression and the 10 tumors with the lowest LIF expression were defined as the LIF-high and LIF-low groups. Differential gene expression analysis revealed widespread transcriptional differences between the two groups (figure 7E). Functional enrichment analysis demonstrated that genes differentially expressed between LIF-high and LIF-low tumors were significantly enriched in pathways related to cell adhesion molecule interactions, chemokine signaling, leukocyte transendothelial migration, and immune regulatory processes, as revealed by KEGG pathway analysis (figure 7F). Consistently, GO biological process analysis highlighted enrichment of leukocyte-mediated immunity, regulation of T cell activation, lymphocyte proliferation, and immune cell adhesion-related processes (figure 7G). Immune cell deconvolution using CIBERSORT further revealed a distinct immune infiltration landscape between LIF-high and LIF-low tumors. Specifically, LIF-high tumors exhibited reduced infiltration of cytotoxic immune populations, including CD8+ T cells and activated NK cells, together with relative enrichment of non-effector or immunoregulatory immune subsets (figure 7H,I). Finally, GSEA demonstrated that antitumor immune programs, including IFN-γ response, TNFα-NF-κB signaling, and inflammatory response pathways, were significantly enriched in LIF-low tumors compared with LIF-high tumors (figure 7J), further supporting the notion that elevated LIF expression is associated with a transcriptionally immunosuppressed tumor microenvironment.

Collectively, these bulk transcriptomic analyses provide mechanistic evidence that high baseline LIF expression is associated with suppression of cytotoxic immune programs and attenuation of inflammatory signaling pathways in NSCLC, complementing the protein-level, survival, and functional immune findings observed in patient cohorts and murine models.

Discussion

In this study, we integrated pretreatment circulating proteomics with tumor transcriptomic and functional immunology analyses to address a clinically central question in resectable NSCLC: why a substantial proportion of patients fail to achieve deep pathological response after nICT, and whether a tractable biomarker–target axis can be leveraged for both prediction and therapeutic intervention. Our key findings are threefold. First, baseline plasma proteomic profiling identified a systemic immune state associated with pCR, and enabled exploratory development of a parsimonious plasma protein-based model for pCR prediction. The model showed promising performance in the independent external cohort, although the internal performance should be interpreted cautiously because candidate protein selection was performed before splitting the SYSUCC cohort into training and internal validation subsets. Second, among the candidate circulating proteins, LIF emerged as a clinically informative marker, showing modest discrimination as a single biomarker, but contributing strongly within a multivariable model and being associated with inferior survival outcomes after nICT. Third, mechanistic analyses suggested that elevated LIF is associated with an immunosuppressive tumor ecosystem characterized by impaired CD8+ T-cell infiltration and dysfunctional effector programs, while pharmacologic LIF blockade synergized with immunochemotherapy in murine lung cancer models by restoring CD8+ T-cell polyfunctionality and reducing exhaustion. Notably, single-cell observations contributing to these interpretations are descriptive and should be considered hypothesis-generating. Together, these findings support LIF as a candidate biomarker and therapeutic target associated with resistance to nICT in resectable NSCLC.

Perioperative ICB combined with platinum-based chemotherapy has redefined the standard of care for resectable NSCLC. Randomized phase III trials have consistently demonstrated improved pathological response and EFS with neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy, and more recently, OS gains have been reported with established regimens. CheckMate 816 showed that neoadjuvant nivolumab plus chemotherapy increased pCR and improved EFS relative to chemotherapy alone, and longer follow-up has now demonstrated a statistically significant OS benefit, strengthening the clinical meaning of deep pathological responses in this setting.2 In the neoadjuvant immunotherapy context, pCR likely reflects not only tumor cytoreduction, but also the rapid establishment of durable, systemic antitumor immune memory.17 18 Similarly, perioperative pembrolizumab in KEYNOTE-671 and perioperative durvalumab in the AEGEAN phase III randomized trial of perioperative durvalumab plus platinum-based chemotherapy in resectable non-small cell lung cancer improved EFS and increased pCR rates, with KEYNOTE-671 also demonstrating an OS advantage at interim analysis.19 20

Despite these advances, clinical benefit remains heterogeneous, and there is still no widely adopted, minimally invasive biomarker that can both (1) identify patients unlikely to achieve major pathological response (MPR) with chemoimmunotherapy alone and (2) illuminate actionable resistance mechanisms. Tumor PD-L1 and mutation-based metrics provide partial information but offer limited negative predictive value in perioperative disease, while tissue-based multiomic predictors are constrained by sampling bias, intratumoral heterogeneity, and feasibility.

Our plasma proteomic results suggest that pretreatment circulating protein programs can reflect clinically meaningful “immune readiness” for effective neoadjuvant ICB. This is conceptually consistent with emerging evidence that peripheral immune composition and soluble mediator networks can shape intratumoral immune set points and influence early treatment trajectories.21 22 In routine perioperative practice, plasma-based biomarkers are particularly attractive because they circumvent both the logistical burden and biological limitations of repeated tissue sampling, which is often constrained by tumor accessibility, procedural risk, and intratumoral heterogeneity. Moreover, blood-based assays are readily standardized, minimally invasive, and easily integrated into existing perioperative workflows, making them well suited for real-world implementation and longitudinal monitoring.23 However, the predictive modeling results should be interpreted with caution. Candidate protein selection was performed in the overall SYSUCC cohort before random division into the training and internal assessment subsets; therefore, the internal assessment results may be affected by information leakage and should not be regarded as fully independent validation. The independent JMCH cohort, which was not involved in candidate protein selection, model fitting, or parameter tuning, provides the main external validation evidence for the finalized model. In addition, the relatively small training cohort and the discrepancy between training and validation performance suggest potential model optimism, particularly for more flexible machine-learning algorithms such as XGBoost. Importantly, the current data do not establish that XGBoost is clearly superior to a simpler parsimonious model. Although XGBoost achieved the highest apparent training AUC, its performance declined in the internal validation and external validation cohorts. Moreover, the external AUC of logistic regression was close to that of XGBoost, with overlapping CIs. Therefore, the apparent advantage of XGBoost should be interpreted cautiously, and the logistic regression model may represent a more interpretable and clinically practical candidate model if validated prospectively.

We further benchmarked the plasma protein model against routinely available clinicopathologic variables. The clinical-only model showed limited performance in the internal assessment and external validation cohorts, whereas the Olink-only logistic regression model achieved higher external discrimination. These findings suggest that circulating proteomic features may capture response-related biological information not fully reflected by conventional variables such as stage, histology, PD-L1 status, age, or gender. However, we did not construct a full clinical-plus-protein model or perform formal subgroup performance analyses by stage and histology. Given the modest sample size, limited number of pCR events, and imbalanced stage and histology distribution, such analyses would be prone to overfitting and unstable estimates. Therefore, the added value of plasma proteomics beyond routine clinical variables should be interpreted cautiously and requires validation in larger, clinically balanced cohorts.

Recent studies have supported plasma proteomics as a liquid-biopsy approach for predicting benefit from PD-1/PD-L1 blockade in NSCLC, although the specific proteins associated with outcome vary across cohorts. In advanced NSCLC, Mohamed et al reported that higher baseline levels of CXCL9, CXCL10, and IL-15 were associated with clinical benefit, suggesting that a pre-existing T-cell-inflamed systemic state may favor response.24 More recently, Gao et al identified an 8-protein immunosuppressive signature associated with inferior survival and resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade.25 Together with our findings, these studies support the value of plasma proteomics for immunotherapy stratification, while also indicating that the dominant biomarkers are not uniform across studies. These differences likely reflect variations in clinical setting and study design. Prior studies were conducted mainly in advanced/metastatic disease and used radiographic or survival outcomes, whereas our study focused on resectable NSCLC treated with nICT and used pCR as the primary endpoint. Differences in treatment backbone, sampling strategy, and proteomic panels may also contribute.

Among plasma candidates, LIF showed the strongest association with both pathological response and survival, and this clinical signal is biologically plausible. LIF is a pleiotropic IL-6 family cytokine signaling through the LIFR/gp130 axis, classically engaging JAK/STAT3 pathways and influencing myeloid differentiation, stromal activation, and inflammatory tone.26 27 In NSCLC, LIF overexpression has been linked to STAT3 activation and aggressive clinicopathologic features, supporting its relevance as a disease-associated cytokine rather than a bystander marker.28 Mechanistically, prior work has positioned LIF as a myeloid-centric suppressor of effective antitumor immunity. In tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), LIF signaling has been shown to regulate chemokine programs that restrict CD8+ T-cell recruitment—most notably through epigenetic repression of CXCL9—thereby limiting intratumoral T-cell trafficking.29 In a complementary translational framework, large-scale pancancer analyses demonstrated that LIF expression correlates with TAM signatures and that LIFR expression is enriched within macrophage compartments, with LIF exposure driving macrophages toward immunosuppressive states.30

Consistent with these frameworks, our tumor transcriptomic analyses indicate that LIF-high tumors exhibit an immune landscape marked by dampened cytotoxic programs and reduced CD8+ T-cell infiltration/function, providing a plausible mechanistic bridge between the blood-based biomarker and intratumoral immune remodeling. This connection is particularly consequential in the neoadjuvant setting, where effective priming, expansion, and trafficking of tumor-reactive T cells must occur rapidly within a limited preoperative window to translate into deep pathological responses.

Importantly, the role of LIF in this study should be interpreted in the context of its different analytical functions. As a single circulating biomarker, LIF showed only modest discrimination for pathological response. However, within the multivariable proteomic model, LIF emerged as a dominant feature, likely reflecting its interaction with broader systemic immune networks rather than its standalone predictive performance. In addition, LIF was associated with survival outcomes and linked to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, supporting its biological relevance. These observations are complementary rather than contradictory, highlighting the distinction between single-marker performance, multivariable model contribution, prognostic association, and mechanistic plausibility.

The translational significance of identifying LIF extends beyond risk stratification because LIF is druggable. MSC-1 (AZD0171), a humanized anti-LIF monoclonal antibody, has been evaluated in a first-in-human phase I trial in advanced solid tumors with acceptable tolerability and evidence of pharmacodynamic engagement, supporting early clinical feasibility for targeting this pathway.31 In our murine lung cancer models, LIF blockade enhanced the antitumor activity of immunochemotherapy and was associated with changes in T-cell states—characterized by increased polyfunctional CD8+ effector responses and reduced exhaustion phenotypes—supporting a mechanistically aligned combination strategy. While single-cell analyses provide a high-resolution view of T-cell states, it should be noted that these observations were derived from one tumor per group and are therefore descriptive rather than inferential. These results motivate a clinically testable hypothesis: patients with elevated baseline LIF represent a biologically defined subgroup in whom standard neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy may be insufficient, and who could benefit from adding LIF inhibition to restore CD8+ T-cell trafficking and effector function. This concept aligns biomarker selection (baseline plasma LIF) with mechanism-based combination therapy (IC+anti LIF), a strategy increasingly favored in precision immuno-oncology trial design.

Our data suggest two potential future clinical applications. First, baseline plasma protein signatures—particularly those incorporating LIF—could be used to enrich for patients likely to achieve pCR (or conversely, identify those at high risk of non-pCR) before treatment initiation. This could inform treatment intensification (eg, adding a novel immunomodulator), de-escalation, or closer surveillance. Second, for LIF-high patients, a biomarker-guided interventional approach could be considered: adding LIF blockade to nICT, with intermediate endpoints including pCR/MPR rates, (circulating tumor) ctDNA clearance, and immune remodeling on paired tissue.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, candidate protein selection was performed using the overall SYSUCC Olink cohort before random division into the training and internal assessment subsets. Therefore, although model fitting was performed in the training cohort, the internal assessment results may still be affected by information leakage and should not be interpreted as fully independent validation. The relatively small size of the training cohort may further increase the risk of model optimism, particularly for flexible machine-learning approaches. To mitigate overinterpretation, we emphasized the independent JMCH cohort as the primary external validation dataset and considered the plasma protein-based model exploratory. Prospective validation in larger, regimen-diverse, multicenter cohorts is required before clinical implementation. Second, plasma proteins can be influenced by comorbid inflammation, infections, and concomitant medications; although our findings suggest a robust signal, residual confounding is possible and should be addressed in future studies with standardized preanalytic controls and sensitivity analyses. Third, mechanistic tumor transcriptomic analyses were performed on bulk RNA sequencing, which limits cellular resolution; single-cell and/or spatial profiling would more precisely define the LIF-responsive compartments (tumor, macrophage subsets, fibroblasts, endothelial cells) and the spatial constraints on CD8+ trafficking. Fourth, the survival analyses should be interpreted cautiously. Although OS and PFS were calculated from the initiation of nICT, this retrospective cohort included only patients who completed surgery, which may introduce selection bias and does not capture patients who failed to proceed to resection because of progression, toxicity, or other adverse events. In addition, the number of survival events, particularly OS events, was limited, resulting in wide CIs and unstable HR estimates. Full multivariable adjustment for clinicopathologic variables and postoperative therapy was not performed because it would increase the risk of overfitting. Therefore, the association between baseline LIF and survival outcomes should be considered exploratory and requires validation in larger prospective cohorts enrolling patients from treatment initiation. Fifth, while our study identifies LIF as a promising candidate biomarker and therapeutic target, the evidence in human cohorts remains primarily associative. The precise cellular origin of circulating LIF—whether derived from tumor cells, surrounding stroma, immune cells, or systemic inflammatory responses—remains to be fully dissected. Furthermore, without direct perturbation experiments in patient-derived systems or genetic compartment-specific dissection in mouse models to confirm necessity and sufficiency, LIF should be considered a candidate mediator rather than a definitively proven driver of resistance in patients. In addition, although scRNA-seq provided valuable insights into T-cell state heterogeneity, this analysis was performed on one tumor per treatment group, precluding biological replication. Therefore, differences in T-cell composition and functional states should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of reproducible immune remodeling. Finally, because all patients in this study received nICT, the present analysis cannot definitively determine whether LIF specifically predicts benefit from this treatment or instead reflects more aggressive tumor biology. Thus, LIF should be interpreted as a biomarker associated with pathological response and survival outcomes in this treatment context. Future work should prioritize: (1) prospective validation of the plasma proteomic model in larger, multicenter, regimen-diverse perioperative cohorts using prespecified feature-selection and model-locking procedures. Standardized pathology reporting of pCR, MPR, and residual viable tumor percentage should also be incorporated to evaluate model performance across the full spectrum of pathological response; (2) integration with orthogonal biomarkers such as ctDNA dynamics, radiomic or metabolic imaging response, and tissue immune features to improve predictive accuracy; (3) deeper mechanistic mapping of the LIF axis using single-cell and spatial transcriptomics, multiplex proteomics, and patient-derived experimental systems to clarify the cellular sources and downstream immune effects of LIF; and (4) biomarker-driven clinical trials testing LIF inhibition in combination with chemo-immunotherapy for LIF-high resectable NSCLC, with pathological response, EFS, and immune pharmacodynamics as key translational endpoints.

Conclusion

Our study identifies a pretreatment circulating proteomic signature associated with pathological response to nICT in resectable NSCLC and highlights LIF as a clinically relevant biomarker linked to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. The plasma protein-based model showed promising predictive performance, particularly in the independent external validation cohort, and appeared to provide information beyond routinely available clinicopathological variables. Mechanistically, elevated LIF was associated with impaired CD8+ T-cell infiltration and effector programs, while preclinical models suggested that LIF blockade may enhance CD8+ T-cell function and improve the antitumor activity of immunochemotherapy. These findings support LIF as a biologically plausible biomarker–target axis and provide a rationale for future biomarker-guided patient stratification and LIF-targeted combination strategies in the neoadjuvant setting.
