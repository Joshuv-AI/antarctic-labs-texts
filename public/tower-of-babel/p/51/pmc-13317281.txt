A placebo-controlled study of the effects of Saccharomyces cerevisiae var. boulardii CNCM I-1079 probiotic on faecal quality, microbiota and immunity in weaned kittens
Authors: Virginie Gaillard, Ana Rodiles, Amélie Mugnier, Jérémy Laxalde, Diana Knight, Willy Joly, Franck Péron
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcute diarrhoea or soft stools can occur in kittens after environmental challenges. The effects of a probiotic on gastrointestinal and immune biomarkers in kittens exposed to three study events were investigated. Healthy weaned 10-week-old kittens were fed a daily dietary supplement of Saccharomyces cerevisiae var. boulardii CNCM I-1079 (SB-1079) or placebo for 7 weeks (20 kittens/group). The study events were a routine booster vaccination (Week 3), a change in housing (Week 5), and a diet transition (Week 6). Outcomes included faecal scores and percentage dry matter (DM%), total serum IgG, faecal IgA, and faecal microbiota. Statistical analyses included Linear Mixed Models and Generalized Linear Mixed Models with group, time and group × time interaction as fixed effects; longitudinal Kruskal-Wallis test corrected for false discovery rate was used for microbiota outcomes.ResultsFaecal scores were optimal throughout the whole study for 12/20 supplemented kittens vs 8/20 control kittens. Overall, group and group × time did not affect faecal quality or moisture, but in Week 5 (housing change) supplemented kittens had firmer stools (P=0.007) and lower faecal DM% (P=0.032) than placebo kittens. Total serum IgG concentration was affected by group (P=0.028), time (P<0.001) and group × time (P=0.001), and was higher in the probiotic vs placebo group (P<0.039) at Week 7, having increased 250% vs 62% from Week 0. Faecal IgA concentration tended to be higher in the probiotic vs placebo group after booster vaccination (P=0.068). Microbiota composition (weighted Unifrac beta diversity) differed between groups overall (P=0.025), at Week 4 (P=0.017), and in the differences between Week 6 and 7 (P=0.008). In supplemented kittens, relative abundances of Veillonellaceae and Actinobacteria remained stable over the first weeks, and abundance of potential pathogens in the Desulfovibrionaceae family decreased. A decrease in Shannon alpha diversity of faecal microbiota post diet transition was significant in supplemented kittens only (P = 0.008) due to a rapid increase in abundance of the Helicobacteraceae family. ConclusionsThe impacts of SB-1079 on biomarkers of gastrointestinal and immunological health suggest its potential to promote resilience of these systems in young kittens experiencing physiological challenges like new environments and diet transitions.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42523-026-00574-5.

Introduction

In kittens, as in most growing animals, soft stools and diarrhoea episodes are believed to be common, although the incidence in populations of owned cats is not known. Diarrhoea in kittens is one of the primary concerns of veterinarians treating shelter cats [1]. In UK cat rehoming centres, kittens less than 6 months old were at an increased risk of diarrhoea relative to older cats, with an odds ratio of 2.54 (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.45–4.46; P = 0.001) [2]. In a large animal shelter in the US, the foster carers of 12.8% of orphaned kittens requested veterinary consultations because of clinical diarrhoea [3]. Another study found that diarrhoea of more than 2 days duration in kittens between approximately 4.5 and 8 weeks of age was a common health issue reported by foster carers for 23.1% of kittens [4].

There are multiple potential causes of diarrhoea in kittens, commonly diet-related issues (the type, quantity and quality of food, and dietary changes) and infections, including parasites (e.g., Cytoisopra spp and Giardia spp) and viral enteropathic infections (e.g. feline enteric corona virus) [2, 5]. Causes of diarrhoea can be multifactorial, and other environmental factors such as stress affect faecal consistency [2]. The first few months of a kitten’s life are characterised by physiological and environmental challenges that are risk factors for gastrointestinal signs. Those signs include weaning, transition between different diets, separation from their dam and littermates, changes in housing or environment, and new social interactions with conspecifics, other species including humans. Diarrhoea in animals at a physiologically vulnerable age is both a health and well-being issue and it is a significant risk factor for mortality in kittens less than 8 weeks of age [6] and can be difficult for new owners to manage. Little is known about any long-term health consequences of acute diarrhoea in kittens [7]. However, data from the Bristol Cats Study showed that cats between 6 and 30 months of age with gastrointestinal signs presenting to veterinarians on two or more occasions, were significantly more likely to have had vomiting, diarrhoea or both when they were kittens of less than 16 weeks of age [8].

One strategy to ameliorate or prevent acute diarrhoea or episodes of soft stools is to improve or restore gastrointestinal health with probiotics [9, 10], which are defined broadly as ‘live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host [11]’. Several studies of probiotics in cats report improvements in faecal consistency alongside other markers of gastrointestinal health [10, 12–16]. The concept of gastrointestinal health encompasses the diet, the gastrointestinal mucosa, the microbiome and gut-associated lymphoid tissue [17, 18]. There are numerous mechanisms through which probiotics are believed to promote gastrointestinal health, including pathogen exclusion, secretion of antimicrobial proteins, maintenance of mucosal barrier function (e.g. protection of the integrity of the tight junctions of mucosal cells), maintenance of intestinal and immune homeostasis (e.g., production of short-chain fatty acids [SCFAs] [19]), trophic effects (enhancement of intestinal digestive function and nutrient uptake), immune modulation (e.g., anti-inflammatory, induction of gastrointestinal IgA production), proteolytic digestion of toxins [20], stabilisation of a healthy gut microbiota [21] and improvement of the antioxidant status of the gut [22–29].

The yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae var. boulardii (S. boulardii) is a non-pathogenic, non-colonising yeast resistant to gastric acid [25] that has been used as a probiotic for many years in human adults and infants for the management and prevention of diarrhoea [30–32]. This yeast probiotic has been shown to be safe and efficacious in preventing or limiting antibiotic-associated diarrhoea and acute gastritis [33, 34], and acute gastroenteritis in humans [35]. More specifically S. boulardii has significantly reduced the duration of diarrhoea, accelerated recovery, reduced the risk of prolonged diarrhoea in infectious gastroenteritis, and might be effective in preventing the occurrence of new episodes of diarrhoea [30, 36, 37].

Four randomised controlled studies have recently investigated the benefits of S. boulardii in cats [14, 15, 38, 39]. In healthy adult cats, a combination of the two probiotics S. boulardii and Pediococcus acidilactici did not affect the structure or diversity of the gastrointestinal microbial population but did promote the colonization of the gut with beneficial bacteria (Pediococcus and Bacillaceae), increase the concentration of SCFAs in faeces and reduce the concentrations of faecal inflammatory markers (myeloperoxidase and calprotectin) [38]. Another study in healthy cats showed Saccharomyces cerevisiae var. boulardii CNCM I-1079 (SB-1079) increased the alpha diversity of gastrointestinal microbiota, decreased faecal calprotectin and increased faecal IgA compared with placebo Mugnier, 2025 #217}. A different strain of S. boulardii also increased faecal IgA in healthy adult cats as well as improving faecal quality [14]. In kittens aged 6 months with mild diarrhoea, a supplement of either enzymolysis seaweed or S. boulardii significantly improved faecal score after 28 days compared with placebo, and S. boulardii reduced the relative abundance of potential harmful bacteria, namely Desulfovibrionaceae and Erysipelatoclostridium compared with enzymolysis seaweed, and Sutterella compared with control or seaweed [15].

S. boulardii has also been studied as a probiotic in dogs. One of these was a randomised controlled trial in which S. boulardii reduced the signs and clinical activity of chronic enteropathy compared with placebo and appeared to achieve better control of gastrointestinal signs than standard therapy alone [40]. Another randomised controlled trial in healthy dogs showed limited effects of a synbiotic combination of S. boulardii and beta-glucan, taken with a bacterial probiotic, on antibiotic-associated gastrointestinal signs [41]. When S. boulardii was administered to bitches during pregnancy and lactation, numerous positive outcomes were observed in the dams, including stabilisation of the gut microbiota at whelping (avoidance of a significant change in alpha diversity), a trend towards higher faecal IgA concentrations, and fewer puppies in the lowest quartile of birth weight compared with unsupplemented control dams [42]. The puppies born to dams receiving S. boulardii had a higher growth rate between day 21 and 56 compared with the control group [42]. Both puppy groups had antigen-specific immune responses to a rabies vaccination but the post vaccination blood IL-8:IL-10 ratio was lower in puppies born to supplemented dams, suggesting that these puppies had a less acute inflammatory response to the vaccine than puppies from unsupplemented dams [42].

This study was intended to elucidate the gastrointestinal and immunological effects of supplementation with SB-1079 on young kittens during a developmental period that frequently coincides with environmental changes as they are rehomed from breeders. The primary hypothesis tested was that SB-1079 supplementation of kittens for a total of 7 weeks from the age of 10 weeks, reduces the severity of soft stools and diarrhoea episodes after exposure to routine events such as a booster vaccination, a change in accommodation and a dietary transition. Secondary objectives were to investigate the effects of supplementation on the gut microbiome and on immunological indicators of a systemic response to vaccination.

Materials and methods

Animals, housing, and husbandry

For practical reasons the study was conducted with two cohorts of 20 kittens, with identical protocols followed between August and October 2021, and then March and May 2022 by the contract-research organization Isoquimen S.L. (Barcelona, Spain). European Shorthair kittens were bred and weaned in specific pathogen free (SPF) conditions and supplied by Isoquimen S.L. (Barcelona, Spain).

To be eligible for the study, kittens had to be assessed as healthy at 9–10 weeks of age (based on a veterinary exam in Week 0 of the study), to be 10 weeks of age at the commencement of study treatment (Week 1), and to have a mean faecal score between 1.5 and 4.5 on the 3 consecutive days of collection (baseline evaluation) in Week 0. All faecal scoring in the study was on scale ranging from 1 (hard dry stool) to 5 (liquid stool); a faecal score of 2.5 was considered optimum (firm, fully formed stool) [43]. Non-inclusion criteria were any pathology diagnosed before or during the baseline veterinary evaluation or a mean faecal score of 1 or 5 on the three faecal collection days in Week 0. Kittens had to be withdrawn from the study if any of the following occurred: diagnosis of a symptomatic infection, clinical signs of disease, a veterinary diagnosis of pathology or a condition requiring medical treatment or a therapeutic diet, failure to gain or maintain weight, an episode of severe diarrhoea (score 5) of more than 2 days’ duration, or repeated refusals to ingest the test substances or food colouring agent.

The treatment groups were housed in two separate, identical rooms (no outdoor access) that were each divided into sub-rooms by mesh to accommodate five pairs of kittens. Kittens were acclimatized to the accommodation for at least one week before Week 0. Kittens were swapped between the two rooms at the beginning of Week 5. The rooms were maintained at a temperature of 17.1–24.0 °C and a humidity of 26%–90%, and there was a 12/12-hour light/ dark photoperiod cycle.

Supplements and feeding

All dry food amounts were allocated according to the on-pack feeding guidelines. Kittens were fed Royal Canin Mother & Babycat Dry (Royal Canin, Aimargues, France) (Additional file 1: Table S1) as a daily ration of 55 g. In Week 6 the diet was transitioned to Royal Canin Kitten Dry (Additional file 1: Table S2) by substituting this for 25%, 50%, 75% and then 100% of the Royal Canin Mother & Babycat Dry ration over 4 days, which follows standard recommendations for changes in diets. After this transition, kittens were fed 66 g of Royal Canin Kitten Dry daily for the rest of the study. Prior to starting the study, each kitten had a microchip placed subcutaneously for unrestricted access to its own feeding station (SureFeed Microchip Connect, Sure Petcare, Cambridge, UK) equipped with one bowl for dry food and one bowl for wet food. The daily food ration was provided in the same bowl every morning after any kibbles remaining from the previous day had been removed for weighing. Clean water was always available from a single dispenser in every sub-room.

Each morning, kittens received either a probiotic or placebo supplement mixed with 10 g of Royal Canin Mother and BabyCat Mousse (Royal Canin, Aimargues, France) (Additional file 1: Table S3). The probiotic consisted of SB-1079 (Levucell SBⓇ, Lallemand SAS, Blagnac, France), administered at a daily dose of 1 × 108 CFU per animal, with maltodextrin (Agenamalt, Agrana, Vienna, Austria) as the excipient. The placebo consisted of maltodextrin alone.

To enable individual faecal identification within each pair of kittens housed in the same sub-room, a food-grade blue colourant (E133, Brilliant Blue FCF; Scrapcooking, Fondettes, France), considered safe for cats and dogs by the European Food Safety Authority [44], was administered to one randomly selected kitten per pair during faecal collection periods. The colourant was mixed with Royal Canin Mother and BabyCat Mousse and provided at a dose of 6 mg/kg body weight. Preliminary testing confirmed that the colourant enabled reliable stool identification without impacting stool quality or faecal IgA measurements. Treatment allocation was blinded and defined at the room level; the colorant was used solely for faecal identification.

Overall study design and procedures

After baseline evaluations, those kittens meeting the eligibility criteria in each cohort were assigned 1:1 to the probiotic or placebo group. Kittens were ranked by birth date and assigned alternately to one of the two groups to ensure a homogenous distribution of age and litters between groups. Changes in assignments were made to balance the groups with respect to birth weight and sex. All study personnel were blinded to treatment group. Probiotic or placebo supplement was fed for 7 weeks to kittens from the age of 10 weeks to 17 weeks (Fig. 1).

Kittens were administered a subcutaneous rhinotracheitis virus/calicivirus/panleukopenia virus/chlamydia/feline leukemia virus vaccine (RCPCh-FeLV, PurevaxⓇ, Boehringer Ingelheim Vetmedica GmbH, Germany) 1 week before the study’s baseline (i.e., Week − 1), and boosted 4 weeks later at the beginning of study Week 3 (i.e., at 8 and 12 weeks of age). This is an adjuvant-free vaccine containing attenuated feline rhinotracheitis herpesvirus (FHV F2 strain), inactivated feline calicivirus (FCV 431 and G1 strains) antigens, attenuated Chlamydophila felis (905 strain), attenuated feline panleukopenia virus (PLI IV), and FeLV recombinant canarypox virus (vCP97). The booster vaccination was one of three early-life events investigated, the others being a change in housing at the beginning of Week 5 and a diet transition over four days in Week 6 (see supplements and feeding section above).

Kittens had a veterinary examination at baseline (Week 0) and the end of Week 7, and clinical signs (e.g., vomiting, diarrhoea, inappetence), bodyweight and food intake were monitored daily from Week 0 to the end of the study (Fig. 1). A blood sample of 1 ml was taken by jugular venipuncture at Week 0, Week 3 (before the booster vaccination) and at Week 7. Stools scored for quality [43], and collected for 3 days in Week 0 then every morning from Tuesday to Friday, from Week 1 to Week 7. There were rarely additional faeces produced during the rest of the day and only faeces voided overnight were analysed. Faecal collections were stored for individual kittens at -20 °C prior to measurement of dry matter and IgA concentration. A rectal swab was collected from each kitten at Weeks 0, 2, 4, and 6 (before the diet transition), and at the end of Week 7; swabs were frozen at -80 °C for later microbiota analyses.

Faecal, immunological and other health outcomes

Weekly faecal consistency scores and weekly faecal moisture percentages were the mean of values for four consecutive days each week. The faecal scoring scale was from 1 (hard, dry and crumbly stool) to 5 points (watery diarrhoea) in half point increments [43], and weekly scores were categorized as too firm (score 1.00 to 1.75), acceptable (> 1.75 to < 2.25, or > 2.75 to ≤ 3.25), optimal (≥ 2.25 to ≤ 2.75), or too soft (> 3.25 to 5.00). Faecal dry matter was measured using a freeze-dried method (University of Bologna, Italy, internal protocol).

Non-antigen-specific immune defences in the gastrointestinal tract were evaluated through measurement of faecal IgA concentration in three faecal collections per week (Wednesday, Thursday, Friday) using a feline enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA kit E-20 A, CliniSciences, France; University of Bologna, Italy). Immunological response to booster vaccination was evaluated by measuring circulating immunoglobulin concentrations at Week 0, at Week 3 prior to booster vaccination and 32 days later (Week 7). Total serum IgG concentration (Cat IgG ELISA kit ab190523, Abcam, UK; Servei d’anàlisi de fàrmacs Universitat Autónoma de Barcelona, Spain) and serum IgG specific for Chlamydophila virus, Panleucopenia virus, Herpes virus, and Calicivirus (ELISA kit Feline Chlamydia ab EIA F1009-AB01, ELISA kit Feline Parvo Virus ab ElA F1004-AB01, ELISA kit Feline Herpes Virus antibody EIA F1007-AB02, ELISA kit Feline Calici Virus EIA F1008-AB02, EVL, The Netherlands; VetAgroSup, France) were measured by ELISAs.

Standard haematological and serum biochemical variables, as well as fructosamine and ammonia, were measured at Week 0 just before vaccination and at the end of Week 7 as indicators of overall health status.

Bodyweight was measured in Week 0 and daily from Week 0 to Week 7. Adverse events of any kind were recorded in the daily clinical observations.

Microbiota outcomes

DNA was extracted from faecal swab samples using Quick-DNA Faecal/Soil Microbe Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, United States) following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA was amplified by PCR using universal primers of the 16S rRNA V3-V4 region (341F: 3’-CCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-5’; 806R: 3’-GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT-5’). DNA concentration was quantified using a fluorometric method with Quant-iT™ PicoGreen® dsDNA assay kit (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA). Sequencing was performed on Illumina MiSeq sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at the GeT-PlaGe facility (INRAE, Genotoul, Toulouse, France). Paired-end samples of 250 base pairs sequences were generated. Fastq files were imported, demultiplexed, quality filtered and dereplicated with DADA2 [45] for the identification of amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). De novo alignment and phylogeny construction were done with MAFFT and FastTree2 [46, 47]. Full community sampling depth was with rarefaction curves, 15,148 sequences sampling depth was chosen for alpha and beta diversity, with Shannon index and weighted Unifrac distance used to assess diversity differences between the two treatment groups. Taxonomy was assigned to the resulting 16 S rRNA marker genes against Greengenes [48]. Differential analysis of the taxonomy were done with paired-method longitudinal analysis, which evaluates the magnitude of change within individual subjects following Bokulich et al. [49].

Statistical analyses

The temporally separated cohorts were combined for statistical analyses. Linear Mixed Models were applied for the analysis of quantitative parameters, with Group, Time, and Group by Time interaction as fixed effects, and Animal as a random effect. Tukey HSD was used for P-value correction for multiple pairwise comparisons. The data were log or rank transformed when appropriate to meet classical statistical assumptions of the model (normally distributed residuals and homoscedasticity). Pairwise contrasts were calculated within Time between Groups and within Group between Time in order to investigate effects of the discrete study events at their fixed timepoints as distinct from any overall effect over the entire study duration. Generalized linear mixed models were used to analyse discrete parameters with the same structure described above. Statistical analyses were done in R (R Core Team 2021) [50]. The dplyr package was used for data manipulation [51] and ggplot2 for data visualization [52]. Linear mixed models were calculated using the lmer function from the lme4 package [53]; the emmeans package [54] was used to calculate marginal means, pairwise contrasts and effect sizes. The 16 S rRNA microbiome data were analysed with QIIME2 (version 2020.2). Cross-sectional analyses were performed by week for beta diversity (weighted Unifrac distances with data ellipses at 0.75 standard deviation [SD] using ggplot2 [55]) with permdisp and PERMANOVA with 999 permutations and for alpha diversity (Shannon index) with Kruskal-Wallis, both corrected for False Discovery Rate (FDR) with Benjamini-Hochberg (BH). Longitudinal analyses of the effect of study treatment and of changes within treatment group were performed for beta and alpha diversities and bacterial taxa by Kruskal-Wallis corrected for FDR with BH, followed by pairwise Wilcoxon tests also corrected for FDR with BH. The Spearman method was selected to correlate Shannon index with relevant taxa and log ratios [56].

Descriptive statistics are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean, except for the microbiota data, which are mean ± SD. For all the analyses, P-value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant and P < 0.10 was taken to indicate a biological trend.

Results

Study population and safety findings

The study population of 40 kittens comprised 12 male kittens and 8 female kittens in each treatment group, recruited from 14 litters in total, with a mean of 2.9 ± 0.4 kittens per litter (range 1–5). Mean body weight at Week 0 was similar between the groups: 1.26 ± 0.05 kg in the probiotic group and 1.28 ± 0.04 kg in the placebo group (P = 0.741). All kittens completed the study. There were no deaths, no study withdrawals and no adverse events recorded during the study. There were no safety concerns from the biochemistry analytes and haematological variables.

Bodyweight and food intake

The body weight of kittens in both treatment groups increased steadily, reaching a mean of 2.21 ± 0.08 kg in the probiotic group and 2.25 ± 0.07 kg in the placebo group at the end of the study. There was no group × time interaction for body weight (P = 0.520).

Food intake increased during the study (overall P < 0.001). There was a group × time interaction (P = 0.010) and the placebo group consumed more food than the test group in Week 1 (P < 0.001) and Week 2 (P < 0.01).

Faecal consistency

From Week 1–7 it was possible to collect and score morning faeces on 93.0% (519/560) and on 96.1% (538/560) of the total scheduled collections across all probiotic kittens and all placebo kittens respectively. There were seldom new stools for any kitten in the afternoon collection and therefore faecal scores are reported only for the morning observations.

Faecal scores at baseline were similar for probiotic and placebo groups: 2.51 ± 0.02 versus 2.63 ± 0.10 (P = 0.122). Mean weekly faecal scores were acceptable or optimal throughout the study, ranging from 2.3 to 2.6 in the placebo group and 2.2 to 2.5 in the probiotic group (Fig. 3A). Across all observations in the study, 12 of 20 kittens in the probiotic group and 8 of 20 kittens in the placebo group had all their faeces scored as optimal (between 2.25 and 2.75). Although there was no overall group or group × time interaction for faecal score (P = 0.378), pairwise group-within-time comparisons were undertaken to determine whether there was an effect of the discrete study interventions at fixed timepoints. Mean faecal score in the probiotic group was 7%, 6%, and 8% lower (more firm stools) than that in the placebo group in Week 1 (P = 0.023), Week 2 (P = 0.035), and Week 5 (P = 0.007), the latter corresponding to the week of the room swap. The largest decrease in mean faecal score during this time was observed in the probiotic group between Week 4 and Week 5 (P = 0.004).

Across the study, weekly faecal moisture values ranged from 59.4 ± 0.5% to 62.5 ± 0.8% in the probiotic group and from 60.0 ± 0.39% to 62.0 ± 0.7% in the placebo group. Overall, there was no significant effect of group, or interaction between group and time for faecal moisture. Mean faecal moisture increased from Week 3 to Week 4 and decreased from Week 4 to Week 5 (Fig. 3B) in both groups, but the changes were only statistically significant in the probiotic group. Between-group within-time analyses showed that when faecal moisture content peaked at Week 4 the values were not significantly different between groups. In contrast, relatively larger decreases between Week 4 and Week 5 in mean faecal moisture in the probiotic group compared with the placebo group resulted in a value that was 3% lower in the probiotic group than in the placebo group (P = 0.032) (Fig. 3B).

Immunological outcomes

Total serum IgG at baseline, Week 3, and Week 7 was 3.25 ± 0.43 mg/ml, 13.02 ± 2.09 mg/ml, and 11.36 ± 1.27 mg/ml, respectively in the probiotic group, and 4.73 ± 0.56 mg/ml, 12.03 ± 1.48 mg/ml and 7.66 ± 0.48 mg/ml, respectively in the placebo group. There was a significant effect of group (P = 0.028) and time (P < 0.001) on the concentration of total serum IgG and a group × time interaction (P = 0.001) (Fig. 4A). Total serum IgG in the probiotic group increased by 250% from Week 0 to Week 7 (P < 0.001) and by 62% in the placebo group (P = 0.001), and at Week 7 was significantly higher in the probiotic group than the placebo group (P = 0.039). IgG antigen-specific titres increased between Week 3 just before the booster vaccination and Week 7 in both treatment groups. For the probiotic group versus placebo group the changes were from 84.5 ± 14.1 to 129.2 ± 22.4 versus from 78.9 ± 10.8 to 168.2 ± 26.1 for Chlamydophila virus, from 993.0 ± 100.7 to 1264.1 ± 118.9 versus from 902.7 ± 97.6 to 1359.7 ± 122.0 for Panleukopenia virus, from 60.8 ± 8.3 to 257.6 ± 19.3 versus from 51.6 ± 1.1 to 245.1 ± 35.0 for Herpes virus, and from 199.7 ± 32.1 to 406.9 ± 56.3 versus from 150.6 ± 26.0 to 444.0 ± 68.9 for Calicivirus. There were no significant group effects or group × time interactions for any of the antigen-specific antibody responses to booster vaccination.

The weekly faecal IgA concentration ranged from 2.17 ± 0.17 to 2.74 ± 0.28 ng IgA/g faecal dry matter in the probiotic group and from 2.06 ± 0.25 to 2.86 ± 0.23 ng IgA/g faecal dry matter in the placebo group (Fig. 3B). Overall, there was an effect of time but no effect of group, or group × time interaction for faecal IgA concentrations. In pairwise comparisons, the difference between treatment groups was close to statistical significance at Week 4, with the mean faecal IgA being 30% higher in the probiotic group compared with the placebo group (P = 0.068).

Microbiota outcomes

A total of 7,910,443 sequences were obtained from 199 samples after high-throughput sequencing. After de-noising and chimera filtering, 4,897,977 sequences corresponding to 1051 ASVs of 411 ± 20 base pairs were retained for down-stream analysis. Rarefactions curves showed a plateau in observed ASVs and a Goods coverage close to 1 from 5000 sequences, indicating that the microbiota was fully sampled (Additional file 1: Figure aS1).

The weighted Unifrac distances of beta diversity of the microbiota are shown separately per group in Fig. 6. The composition of the microbiota was significantly different between treatment groups both overall (excluding week 0; P = 0.025) and at Week 4 (i.e. the week after the booster vaccination; P = 0.017; supplementary Figure S2A). The longitudinal comparison of microbiota differences between Week 6 and Week 7 (i.e. the week after the diet change) was also significantly different between treatment groups (P = 0.008; additional file 1: Figure S2B). The Shannon index measuring alpha diversity decreased significantly after the diet transition i.e., from Week 6 to Week 7 in the placebo group (P = 0.008; Fig. 5) and a trend was found in the probiotic group (P = 0.08). There were significant positive and negative associations of Shannon index with specific taxa and log taxa ratios (Table 1, Additional file 1: Tables S4 and S5). The strongest positive correlations were for family Ruminococcaceae (ρ = 0.79), Blautia (ρ = 0.79), family Clostridiaceae (ρ = 0.78) and Phascolarctobacterium (ρ = 0.75) (P < 0.001 for each); the strongest negative correlations were for family Tissierellaceae (ρ = -0.49), Finegoldia (ρ = -0.4459), and Anaerococcus (ρ = -0.43) (P < 0.001 for each). Among the significant correlations between taxa log ratios and Shannon index, the greatest positive correlation was for Prevotella:Bacteroides (ρ = 0.45, P < 0.001) and the greatest negative correlation was for Firmicutes: Bacteroidetes (ρ = -0.54, P < 0.001).

The phyla of faecal microbiota of the kittens comprised 62.8% Firmicutes, 21.5% Bacteroidetes, 13% Proteobacteria, 1.7% Actinobacteria and 1% Fusobacteria (Fig. 2A). The main families were 28.3% Tissierellaceae, 12.3% Lachnospiraceae, 9.8% Bacteroidaceae, 7.7% Veillonellaceae, 6.1% Helicobacteraceae, 5.6% Streptococcaceae, 5.3% Prevotellaceae, 4,4% Porphyromonadaceae, 2.5% Clostridiaceae, 2.5% Enterobacteriaceae, 2.5% Campylobacteriaceae and 2% Ruminococcaceae (Fig. 2B).

Longitudinal differential pairwise analysis of the relative abundance at the family, phylum and genus levels are shown in Table 1 and in Additional file 1: Tables S4 and S5, respectively. In all cases, significant differences between treatments are marked with * (P < 0.05), and trends are marked with # (0.05 < P < 0.1). In the first two weeks of the study, all kittens had an increase in the relative abundance of Porphyromonadaceae (Porphyromonas), a decrease in Enterococcaceae (Enterococcus) and a trend towards a decrease in Paraprevotellaceae ([Prevotella]). There was a trend towards differences between groups in the treatment responses of Pasteurellaceae# and Oscillospira#. The placebo group had decreases in the relative abundances of Actinobacteria, Coriobacteriaceae (Collinsella), Lactobacillales#, and Dialister, trends towards decreases in Bifidobacteriaceae (Bifidobacterium) and Veillonellaceae (Megamonas*), and increases in Faecalibacterium and Parabacteroides*. The probiotic group had a decrease in Peptostreptococcaceae (Peptostreptococcus) abundance and a trend towards a decrease in Desulfovibrionaceae# and Erysipelotrichaceae# (Catenibacterium*, Eubacterium). From Week 2 to Week 4 post vaccination, all kittens had a trend for increases in the relative abundance of Fusobacterium. There was a trend towards differences between groups in the treatment responses of Alcaligenaceaebacter# and Finegoldia* The placebo group had an increase in the relative abundance of Helicobacteraceae (Helicobacter), a trend for an increase in Bacteroidaceae (Bacteroides), Lachnospiraceae (an unidentified genus from Lachnospiraceae*) and Succinivibrionaceae, Butyriciccocus, decreases in Enterococcaceae (Enterococcus) and Tissierellaceae (Anaerococcus*) and a trend for decreases in Firmicutes and Peptostreptococcus*. In the probiotic group there were increases in the relative abundances of Bacteroides fragilis*, Bilophila#, Corynebacteriaceae (Corynebacterium*), Enterobacteriaceae* (unidentified genus from Enterobacteriaceae*), Lactobacillales*, Lactobacillaceae (Pediococcus), Staphylococcaceae, Streptococcaceae* (Streptococcus), Veillonella, and the log ratio of Pediococcus/Collinsella, and trends for increases in Fusobacteria, Enterococcaceae* (Enterococcus*), Anaerococcus and Collinsella#. In addition, the probiotic group had decreases in the relative abundances of Bifidobacteriaceae, Clostridiaceae (Clostridium*, C. hiranonis*) and Veillonellaceae# (Megamonas*, Phascolarctobacterium) and trends for decreases in Coriobacteriaceae (Slackia, Collinsella), Erysipelotrichaceae# (Catenibacterium*), Lachnospiraceae* (Blautia*, Coprococcus, Dorea), and Ruminococcaceae (Gemmiger, Oscillispora). From Week 4 to Week 6 all kittens had an increase in relative abundance of Lactobacillus and trends for decreases in Mogibacteriaceae (an unidentified genus from Mogibacteriaceae) and Peptococcaceae (Peptococcus). Propionibacteriaceae# (Propionibacterium#), Clostridium* (Lachnospiraceae) and unidentifed genus from Clostridiales# and Lachnospiraceae#, Pediococcus* and Slackia* and Sutterella# tended to show differential treatment responses. In the placebo group there were increases in the relative abundances of Lactobacillales# (Lactobacillaceae, Streptococcus equi) and Enterobacteriaceae (an unidentified genus from Enterobacteriaceae), trends for increases in Peptostreptococcaceae* (Peptostreptococcus*) and Odoribacteraceae (Odoribacter), and trends for decreases in Succinivibrionaceae* (Anaerobiospirillum*) and Lachnospira, and an unidentified genus from Ruminococcaceae#. The probiotic group had trends for increases in Proteobactoeria, Campylobacteraceae (Campylobacter), Lactobacillus and Ruminococcus*, had a decrease in Porphyromonadaceae (an unidentified genus from Mogibacteriaceae), and trends for decreases in Dialister, Eubacterium, Oscillospira, and the log ratio Pediococcus/Collinsella#.

Between Week 6 and Week 7 all kittens tended to have an increase in the relative abundance of an unidentified genus of Fusobacteriaceae, a decrease in Fusobacterium and trends for decreases in Coprococcus, Lactobacillus and Odoribacteraceae (Odoribacter). Propionibacteriaceae# (Propionibacterium#) and Slackia# tended to show differential treatment responses. The placebo group had an increase in the relative abundance of Helicobacteraceae (Helicobacter), a trend for an increase in Staphylococcaceae (Staphylococcus), decreases in Clostridiaceae# (Clostridium hiranonis#), Campylobacteraceae (Campylobacter) and Succinivibrionaceae (Anaerobiospirillum), and a trend for a decrease Paraprevotellaceae ([Prevotella]). The probiotic group had an increase in Actinomycetaceae (Actinomyces), a trend for an increase in Helicobacteraceae (Helicobacter), decreases in Paraprevotellaceae ([Prevotella]), Parabacteroides, Peptostreptococcaceae (Peptostreptococcus), and trends for decreases in Campylobacteraceae (Campylobacter), an unidentified genus from Clostridiales, Lachnospiraceae, Ruminococcaceae, and S24-7.

In general, the probiotic group maintained a stable Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio over the trial, as well as stable relative abundances of the Actinobacteria (Bifidobacterium) and Veillonellaceae families in the first 2 weeks. In the week after vaccination, the probiotic group had a decrease in C. hiranonis but an increase in Enterobacteriaceae (also Veillonella and the ratio of Pediococcus/Collinsella), while the placebo group experienced the same changes 2 or 3 weeks later. The probiotic treatment resulted in a gradual transition to increased Helicobacter abundance from Week 0 to Week 7, whereas the placebo group experienced a more pronounced increase between Week 6 and Week 7.

Discussion

This study tested the ability of SB-1079 to act as gut stabiliser and promote resilience of the gastrointestinal microbiota in healthy kittens exposed to environmental and nutritional changes commonly experienced between 10 and 17 weeks of age. Supplementation was associated with higher total serum IgG concentration at Week 7 compared with placebo, with modulations in faecal consistency/moisture content, and with a smooth transition towards a healthy gastrointestinal microbiome.

As expected, both treatment groups mounted antigen-specific humoral responses to the vaccine. Total serum IgG concentration increased significantly in both treatment groups in the 4 weeks after the primary vaccination to levels not significantly different from each other. This is consistent with the increase in serum IgG typical even in unvaccinated kittens as their immune system develops and de novo synthesis outpaces the decline of IgG acquired from colostrum [57]. In a prospective clinical trial, total serum IgG reached a nadir at 4–6 weeks of age [58] and other study found that the serum IgG concentration of kittens reaches 80% of adult values by 90 days of age [57]. Four weeks after the vaccination booster, the concentration of total IgG had significantly declined in the placebo group but remained stable in the probiotic group relative to the concentration 4 weeks after the initial vaccination and was significantly higher than in the placebo group. Antigen-specific IgG increased between Week 0 and Week 3 and between Week 3 and Week 7 in both treatment groups (data not shown) but there were no differences between groups in the magnitude of antigen-specific IgG response to the vaccine at any time. This may be explained by the strength of antigen-driven adaptive immunity in healthy, well-nourished kittens, which makes dietary modification difficult. Taken together, these data may suggest an effect of probiotic in sustaining IgG levels either through maintaining IgG production or reducing the rate of degradation. The reason for the decline of total IgG in the control group is unclear; nevertheless SB-1079 supplementation appeared to protect kittens from whatever the causative factors were. If the trial had been longer, the persistence or lack of persistence of this difference in the concentration of total IgG may have provided additional insights.

This study used faecal IgA as a biomarker of the activity of gastrointestinal-associated lymphoid tissue and of the innate immune response [59–62]. Secretory IgA is the predominant gastrointestinal immunoglobulin and, in addition to neutralizing specific pathogens, it helps to maintain gut homeostasis, promoting an anti-inflammatory environment and a healthy microbiota [63, 64]. Physiological stress can affect secretory IgA concentration via neuroendocrine pathways between the brain and the gut [65]. Healthy puppies transported from breeding kennels to a distributer had acute increases in faecal concentrations of both glucocorticoid metabolites and IgA [62]. In cats admitted to an animal shelter, those with well-adjusted behaviours had higher concentrations of faecal IgA compared with those displaying anxiety and aggression [60]. Overall, evidence from the literature indicates a positive association between mucosal immune function and a variety of coping mechanisms to acute stress, not necessarily corresponding to enhanced immune protection. In this study, although differences in faecal IgA concentration between groups were greatest in the week following vaccination and favoured the SB-1079 group, this was not statistically significant. Therefore a beneficial effect of SB-1079 in enhancing gastrointestinal immune activity was not proven but a small effect cannot be ruled out. Adult healthy cats supplemented with SB-1079 showed a significant increase in faecal IgA compared with control in a study that did not administer any immune challenge in the form of a vaccine [39]. The current study may not have had sufficient power to show a significant effect because the powering was based on data from puppies. It is also noted that the intensity of the study events was only modest and effects may have been masked by increases in IgA levels that occur naturally as animals mature [66].

Over the 7 weeks, mean faecal consistency in healthy kittens remained acceptable or optimal, with average faecal scores in a narrow range between 2.2 and 2.6 on a 1–5 faecal scoring system. Although some kittens experienced episodes of soft stools, the signs of gastrointestinal disturbance were minor, so there was little opportunity for SB-1079 supplementation to improve gastrointestinal health parameters. Nevertheless, there was a significant increase in stool firmness directly after the room swap in the probiotic group that was corroborated by a significant decrease in the percentage of moisture content. This decrease in faecal moisture had been preceded by a significant increase in the probiotic group in the week between vaccination and room change and might therefore be interpreted as a rapid ‘reset’ to an underlying norm for the group. Stabilisation or reduction of faecal moisture by a yeast-based probiotic has been demonstrated in other dog or cat studies but these have had very different designs. In bitches undergoing the physiological stress of pregnancy, whelping, and lactation, the test group supplemented with SB-1079 had no changes in faecal dry matter, while the unsupplemented control group showed a small reduction (2%) [42]. In other studies of healthy adult dogs and cats, supplementation of a different strain of Saccharomyces, S. cerevisiae MUCL 3988, has increased the dry matter percentage of faeces [67, 68]. When a fermentation product of S. cerevisiae containing bioactive fermentation metabolites was fed to adult dogs, it stabilised indicators of a stress response to a controlled 45-minute van journey, namely faecal dry matter, cyclooxygenase-2 and malondialdehyde gene expression [69]. Overall, across the full duration of the study, our results did not detect an effect of probiotic on faecal scores or moisture. However, there were indications that SB-1079 could modulate these variables at discrete timepoints. Numerically 50% more of the supplemented kittens were characterised as having exclusively optimal stool quality throughout the 7 weeks when compared to control kittens. Although this maintenance of ideal faecal scores was not a statistically analysed outcome, it is an observation of biological interest given that even healthy kittens can experience episodes of soft stools or diarrhoea over a period as long as 7 weeks, whether or not this can be linked to a specific event.

Regarding faecal microbiota composition, the predominant phyla identified in this study were consistent with those found in healthy kittens of 8 [70] or approximately 9 weeks of age [71], as well as with a similar core microbiome of older healthy cats [72]. The global effect of SB-1079 was evidenced by differences in beta diversity between the treatment groups, signifying different profiles of the bacterial population, particularly after vaccination and after diet change. In neonatal rats, administration of SB-1079 promoted faster maturation of the microbiota by accelerating oxygen consumption in the lumen of the intestine [73]. In the first 2 weeks of supplementation after the initial vaccination at Week − 1, SB-1079 promoted the maintenance of beneficial gut microbiota from the Veillonellaceae family (including Megamonas, Dialister, Megasphera, Phascolarctobacterium and Veillonella) and a constant level of Actinobacteria, in particular Bifidobacterium, all of which are positively correlated with good kitten health [74]. SB-1079 also reduced the relative abundance of potential pathogens in the Desulfovibrionaceae family (Bilophila) that produces toxic sulphides and can provoke inflammatory bowel disease in cats [75].

Both treatment groups showed a downward trajectory in microbiota alpha diversity after Week 4 (placebo group) or Week 2 (probiotic group). This appeared to be gradual for the probiotic group compared with a sharp and statistically significant decline in the placebo group after the diet transition. An abrupt diet transition in adult dogs can result in changes in faecal metabolites in 2 days and microbial diversity and composition in 6 days [76, 77]. In calves, supplementation with SB-1079 from birth to 1 week of age increased the alpha diversity and stability of the microbiota, and this was associated with an increase in intestinal IgA production [78, 79]. In the study reported here, SB-1079 seemed to have a ‘gut stabilising’ effect, preserving the diversity (and composition) of the microbiota during the diet transition; a similar effect has been demonstrated in dogs and pigs [42, 80]. The stabilisation of the Shannon index and the smooth change in the structure of the bacterial population from Week 0 to Week 7 shownin the principal coordinates analysis, was accompanied by a gradual only increase in the relative abundance of Helicobacter in the probiotic group, which increased 2.2 fold during this period in contrast to a 5.5-fold increase in the placebo group, whilst the relative abundance of C. hiranonis was maintained. Helicobacter was negatively correlated with the alpha diversity and is well known to produce gastritis in cats [81]. C. hiranonis was positively correlated with alpha diversity. In dogs, SB-1079 supplementation has promoted C. hiranonis [42]. A reduction of C. hiranonis has been described in cats and dogs with chronic enteropathology [82, 83], and recent evidence supports the role of this bacterium as a bile acid converter in cats [84].

High and stable diversity of gastrointestinal microbiota is indicative of a healthy gastrointestinal tract [85], which in turn is important for energy homeostasis and metabolic health [86]. The diversity of the microbiota and relative abundance of bacterial species are influenced by age, diet, health status and other environmental factors [72, 87–93]. In particular, as in other species, cats with acute diarrhoea typically display varying degrees of dysbiosis (perturbations in the composition of the gastrointestinal microbiota that lead to functional changes in the metabolome), characterized by reductions in the diversity of their gastrointestinal microbiota [94, 95]. Kittens in both treatment groups generally did not develop diarrhoea and it is unknown whether the observed stabilisation of microbiota diversity by SB-1079 would translate to a clinical protective benefit in the context of more radical or abrupt changes in diet demonstrably capable of inducing adverse effects.

The associations between alpha diversity (the Shannon index) and particular bacterial genera highlight the importance of maintaining diversity in the microbiota. Most of the genera positively associated with diversity were obligate anaerobes, and those with a negative association were mainly facultative anaerobes, with the exception of Finegoldia, Anaerococcus and Peptoniphilus, which are obligate anaerobes [96–100]. This is significant because gut dysbiosis is typically characterized by both low diversity and a shift from obligate anaerobes towards facultative anaerobes [101, 102]. During the establishment of a healthy gastrointestinal microbiota in the young growing animal, or the re-building of a healthy microbiota in an adult after severe dysbiosis, the environment in the gastrointestinal tract shifts from aerobic to anaerobic with a corresponding transition from facultative anaerobic to obligate anaerobic microbial communities [17, 101–103]. When there is homeostasis in a healthy gastrointestinal tract, the environment remains anaerobic with obligate anaerobes outcompeting facultative anaerobes [17]. The association found in the study between microbiota diversity in the gastrointestinal microbiota and strict anaerobes supports the concept of a healthy anaerobiosis in the maturing microbiota of growing kittens.

Establishing a healthy gut microbiome before an animal reaches maturity is important for long-term health, at least in part because gut microbes are critical in the development and education of the immune system, promoting for example, the development of gut-associated lymphoid structures and tolerance to microbial antigens [104, 105]. Early exposure of children to antibiotics, a common cause of dysbiosis, is associated with a range of chronic immune-mediated conditions, including asthma, eczema and inflammatory bowel disease [104]. In dogs and cats, there are associations between the gut microbiota and chronic diseases [104, 106–108] but little is known about enduring effects of dysbiosis early in life. Although we observed potentially beneficial shifts in the composition of gut microbiota with SB-1079 supplementation, the study was not designed to investigate any long-term health consequences of these.

It is important to note study strengths and limitations other than those mentioned above. The study was designed to reproduce common early life events relevant to owned kittens in the real-world setting including changes in feeding and environment, and vaccination. However, the SPF status of study cats differed from the likely immunological context of household kittens. It removed the possible interference of maternally derived passive immunity with vaccine response and avoided potential differences between kittens in early exposure to pathogens. The translatability of studies in SPF animals to animals in more natural or conventional human settings has been questioned with respect to both immunological and microbiological outcomes [109]. For example, adult SPF mice were shown to have an immature immune system compared with pet-store mice and lacked effector-differentiated and mucosally distributed memory T cells [110]. In the absence of comparative data between SPF and healthy pet kittens on the development of the immune system and the gut microbiome, it is unknown if SPF status reduces the generalisability of our data.

Benefits of the probiotic were observed in healthy kittens, which is arguably more difficult to demonstrate than benefits in kittens with poor health or than amelioration of negative outcomes from more disruptive early-life events than those tested here. During their progression through postnatal development, kittens are vulnerable to environmental risk factors that have potentially long-term as well as acute health consequences [7]. This study does not address long-term health outcomes, but interventions that promote physiological resilience and the establishment of a healthy microbiome in young animals are of special interest for preventative husbandry practices. The necessity of controlled experimental conditions with sequential rather than concurrent environmental changes is likely to have underestimated the challenges that many owned kittens experience in early life. Typically, owned kittens are vaccinated close to the time of rehoming, and this event encompasses a multitude of stresses not present in the study e.g., travel to the veterinary clinic, exposure to a totally different sensory environment (sights, sounds, smells, feels) without their litter mates, socialization with different and potentially more people with less predictive behaviours, potentially a change in post-weaning diet. The severity of the discrete events in the study was also lower than would be likely in and uncontrolled setting. The room change in the study was a swap between almost identical rooms, and the diet transition was managed over 4 days in accordance with best practice, which is not always the case for privately owned animals. Vaccination and the room swap were conducted at the beginning of the week, giving the maximum opportunity to observe immediate effects. Nevertheless, the lack of faecal collections and scoring during the weekends could have missed short-lived gastrointestinal signs. The mechanisms by which SB-1079 influenced the study endpoints were not investigated. Effects mediated through the adaptive immune system, the gut microbiota, or the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis are likely to be observed at different times after an environmental challenge. The schedule of events in the study followed the typical timing of equivalent experiences in owned kittens and the outcomes could not necessarily be attributed to a specific event. In the future data on cortisol levels may help to interpret the response to environmental perturbations; currently it is difficult to measure this biomarker in kittens without the procedure itself affecting the results.

Conclusions

SB-1079 supplementation of healthy kittens was associated with the maintenance of microbiota diversity and composition after diet transition, and shifts in the relative abundance of species associated with good health. Diversity in the gastrointestinal microbiota was associated with strict anaerobes, supporting the concept of a healthy anaerobiosis in the maturing microbiota of growing kittens. By the end of the study, when kittens were 17 weeks of age, SB-1079 supplementation was associated with higher concentrations of total serum IgG compared with no supplementation, suggesting a potentially beneficial effect on the developing humoral immune system. More kittens in the probiotic group than the control group maintained optimal faecal scores throughout the whole study, but diarrhoea was not evident in either group. In conclusion, SB-1079 supplementation of healthy kittens in a controlled environment simulating early-life events modulated biomarkers of gastrointestinal and immunological health in directions hypothesised to promote physiological resilience in the real-world where environmental challenges are more complex and variable.
