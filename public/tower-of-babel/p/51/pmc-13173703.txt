Metabolic engineering of Bacillus subtilis for high-yield surfactin production
Authors: Yong Wang, Hanqing Li, Yue Mao, Pengfei Li, Haizhen Wu, Jiang Ye, Huizhan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The industrial application of surfactin has been constrained by the low production capacity of Bacillus subtilis. This study developed a high-yield, high-tolerance surfactin-producing strain through a multi-level metabolic engineering strategy. First, we constructed an enhanced P43 promoter (P43LN) by extending its upstream region from 260 to 520 bp and pairing it with a cognate 5′ UTR, which exhibited more than threefold higher activity than the commonly used core P43 promoter, providing a versatile tool for stable and high-level expression of key genes. Based on this, the native srfA promoter in the oilfield-derived B. subtilis TD7 was replaced with the P43 promoter, yielding the engineered strain TP1. Subsequently, systematic knockout of competing antimicrobial peptide synthesis gene clusters (pps, bac, and pks) increased surfactin titer to 2.78 g/L. Further synergistic enhancement of NADPH regeneration (via zwf overexpression) and cellular tolerance (via efflux pump gene yerP overexpression) resulted in the final strain TS8, which achieved a shake-flask surfactin titer of 3.51 g/L, representing a 102.7% increase in per-OD600 productivity. This work successfully integrates promoter engineering, metabolic flux redistribution, and tolerance modules, not only significantly improving surfactin production but also providing a powerful expression and systematic optimization toolkit for B. subtilis, laying a technical foundation for the efficient biomanufacturing of complex natural products.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13068-026-02762-6.

Introduction

Biosurfactants are a class of amphiphilic microbial secondary metabolites produced under specific culture conditions [1]. Characterized by their biodegradability and low ecotoxicity, they exert essential surfactant functions, including emulsification, dispersion, wetting, foaming, and reduction of surface and interfacial tension. Importantly, they outperform synthetic surfactants in several aspects: biocompatibility, ecological safety, functional versatility under extreme conditions (e.g., high salinity, temperature, or pH), and inherent bioactivity [1, 2]. Their minimal environmental footprint is consistent with the principles of the circular bioeconomy, facilitating their diverse applications in environmental remediation (e.g., hydrocarbon degradation, heavy metal sequestration), pharmaceuticals (e.g., antimicrobial, anticancer agents), personal care (e.g., emulsifiers, cleansers), agriculture (e.g., biopesticide adjuvants), and petroleum recovery [3].

Surfactin, a model cyclic lipopeptide biosurfactant produced by Bacillus subtilis, exemplifies these traits. Its structure comprises a heptapeptide ring (L-Glu1-L-Leu2-D-Leu3-L-Val4-L-Asp5-D-Leu6-L-Leu7) linked to a β-hydroxy fatty acid chain (C12–C16), with conserved Glu1/Asp5 facilitating cyclization while hydrophobic residues (Leu2, 3, 6, 7, Val4) mediate membrane interaction [4]. This unique architecture confers exceptional surface activity, reducing water surface tension to ~ 27 mN/m at concentrations < 0.005 wt% [5] as well as membranolytic properties through insertion of the fatty acid chain into lipid bilayers, leading to disrupting of cell membranes and viral envelopes. Selective targeting of phospholipids further underpins its cancer-specific cytotoxicity [6–8]. These attributes make surfactin highly valuable for microbial enhanced oil recovery (MEOR), bioremediation, therapeutics, and sustainable agriculture. However, wild-type strains typically yield only 100–600 mg/L, necessitating engineered hyperproduction for industrial adoption [9].

The biosynthesis of surfactin involves coordinated precursor synthesis and assembly via a nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) megacomplex [10]. Key steps include the following: (i) generation of amino acid and β-hydroxy fatty acid precursors through primary metabolism; (ii) NRPS-mediated assembly, in which SrfA (a 1.2 MDa multienzyme complex) sequentially activates, modifies, and couples substrates via thiotemplated catalysis; and (iii) macrocyclization and release facilitated by the thioesterase domain [9, 10]. Final export is mediated by proton-motive-force-driven transporters such as YerP [11]. Recent studies emphasize the importance of metabolic flux partitioning between biomass formation and surfactin synthesis, particularly under nutrient limitation (e.g., nitrogen starvation) [12, 13]. Notably, the availability of β-hydroxy fatty acids often limits the production of surfactin, as their synthesis competes with membrane lipid biogenesis. Moreover, cellular tolerance mechanisms—including efflux pumps and membrane remodeling—significantly influence final titers by alleviating self-toxicity.

Building on this knowledge, current genetic engineering strategies focus on the following: (i) enhancing NRPS expression via promoter engineering (e.g., replacing native srfA promoter with strong constitutive or inducible variants such as P43, Phag, or quorum sensing-responsive elements) [14–18]; (ii) redirecting carbon flux toward precursors by overexpressing rate-limiting enzymes (e.g., AccA for malonyl-CoA, thioesterase for fatty acyl-ACP release) [19–21] and deleting competing pathways (e.g., biofilm synthesis, fengycin synthesis) [22]; (iii) improving cellular tolerance through overexpression of efflux transporters (YerP, YfiS) and manipulation of stress-responsive regulators [11, 23]; and (iv) implementing dynamic pathway control using metabolite-responsive biosensors [24, 25]. Combinatorial application of these strategies, supported by systems metabolic engineering tools such as CRISPR-Cas9 and RNA-seq-guided target identification, has enabled laboratory-scale surfactin titers beyond 5 g/L [22].

In our previous work, engineering the oilfield isolate B. subtilis TD7 by replacing the native srfA promoter with P43 led to strain TP1, which achieved a 245% increase in surfactin production (2.14 g/L) [14]. However, further efforts to overexpress genes in the β-hydroxy fatty acid pathway, either singly or in combination, resulted in marginal gains (< 20%), indicating that conventional precursor engineering alone is insufficient to overcome the existing production ceiling [19]. Therefore, this study transitions from a single-pathway approach to a systems-level strategy. Using TP1 as the parent strain, we simultaneously address two central challenges: redistributing central metabolic flux to optimize precursor synthesis and enhancing cellular tolerance through efflux pump engineering. This integrated methodology aims to develop a robust chassis suitable for MEOR, thereby establishing a foundational platform for scalable surfactin biomanufacturing.

Materials and methods

Microorganism, media, and fermentation conditions

Luria–Bertani (LB) media (10 g/L tryptone, 10 g/L NaCl, and 5 g/L yeast extract) was used for growth of both B. subtilis and E. coli colonies. The composition of the fermentation medium for surfactin activator production by original and engineered strains was as follows: yeast extract 1 g/L, sucrose 70 g/L, NaNO3 25 g/L, Na2HPO4∙12H2O 1 g/L, KH2PO4 0.333 g/L, MgSO4∙7H2O 0.15 g/L, FeSO4∙7H2O 6 mg/L, MnSO4∙H2O 6 mg/L, and CaCl2 7.5 mg/L (pH 7.0).

The strain was activated on solid LB medium plates and incubated at 37 °C for 12 h. A single colony was picked and inoculated into 30 mL of liquid LB medium, followed by incubation at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 14 h. The seed culture was inoculated into 100 mL of fermentation medium at an inoculum size of 2% (v/v) and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 96 h.

Strain construction

The host strain for genetic engineering in this study, B. subtilis TD1, and the P43 promoter (along with its derivatives) derived from B. subtilis 168 were preserved in our laboratory collection. The ansZ and zwf genes used in this study were obtained from Bacillus subtilis TP1. A codon-optimized irrE gene for expression in B. subtilis was synthesized by Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China), and its nucleotide sequence is provided in Table S1. E. coli DH5α served as the host for all plasmid construction and propagation. Gene expression vectors were derived from the pE-P43 backbone, while plasmids used for promoter replacement, gene knockout, and gene replacement were constructed using pJOE8999 as the backbone. Strains and plasmids

Transformation of B. subtilis was performed using a modified Spizizen method. Specifically, B. subtilis TP1 was streaked onto LB agar plates and incubated at 37 °C for 14 h. A single colony was then inoculated into 5 mL of GM1 liquid medium and incubated at 37 °C with shaking (200 rpm) for 20 h. A 10% (v/v) inoculum from this culture was transferred into fresh 5 mL GM1 medium and incubated under the same conditions until the late-logarithmic growth phase was reached. Subsequently, 1.5 mL of this late-log culture was added to 5 mL of GM2 liquid medium and shaken (200 rpm) at 37 °C for 1.5 h to induce competence. Aliquots (1 mL) of the competent cell suspension were dispensed into sterile microcentrifuge tubes, mixed with 300 ng of recombinant plasmid DNA, and incubated statically at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by incubation with shaking (200 rpm) at 37 °C for an additional hour. Finally, the transformation mixtures were spread onto LB agar plates supplemented with the appropriate antibiotic for selection.

GM1 medium was prepared by combining the following filter-sterilized components: 500 µL of 1.25 M sucrose, 500 µL of 10 × T-Base solution, 100 µL of 40% (w/v) glucose, 100 µL of 50 g/L yeast extract, 100 µL of 20 g/L acid hydrolysate of casein, 50 µL of 5 g/L L-tryptophan, and 5 µL of 20% (w/v) MgSO4·7H2O. The mixture was brought to a final volume of 5 mL with sterile double-distilled water (3645 µL). GM2 Medium consisted of 500 µL of 1.25 M sucrose, 500 µL of 10 × T-Base solution, 100 µL of 40% (w/v) glucose, 50 µL of 20 g/L acid hydrolysate of casein, 5 µL of 5 g/L L-tryptophan, 35 µL of 20% (w/v) MgSO4·7H2O, 50 µL of 50 mM CaCl2, and 3810 µL of sterile double-distilled water (final volume: 5 mL). The 10 × T-Base solution contained 60 g/L K2HPO4, 20 g/L (NH4)2SO4, 183 g/L KH2PO4, and 12 g/L trisodium citrate (C6H5Na3O7). All stock solutions were prepared in double-distilled water, sterilized independently by autoclaving at 115 °C for 15 min, and mixed aseptically according to the specified ratios prior to use.

Determination of surfactin production

After 96 h of fermentation, 1 mL of the culture broth was transferred to a 4 mL microcentrifuge tube. Acid precipitation was performed by adding 6 M HCl to adjust the pH below 2, followed by incubation at 4 °C for 6–8 h. The acidified sample was sequentially extracted three times with ethyl acetate (2 mL, 1 mL, and 1 mL). The organic phases from each extraction were combined into a new 4 mL microcentrifuge tube, and the ethyl acetate was evaporated by heating at 70 °C in a metal bath. The dried crude product was stored at ambient temperature in sealed, labeled microcentrifuge tubes. For analytical purposes, the crude product was dissolved in 1 mL methanol via ultrasonication for 15 min and filtered through a 0.22 μm organic-phase membrane. Quantitative analysis of surfactin was performed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using a Yteler SinoChrom ODS-BP C18 reversed-phase column (5 μm, 4.6 mm × 250 mm). Detection was carried out at a UV wavelength of 205 nm, with the column oven maintained at 30 °C. The injection volume was 25 μL, and separation employed a mobile phase of acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid (Phase A) and water with 0.1% formic acid (Phase B) under a low-pressure gradient program: 0–2 min, 80% Phase A; 2–22 min, linear gradient from 80 to 100% Phase A; 22–35 min, 100% Phase A; 35–40 min, linear gradient back to 80% Phase A; and 40–45 min, 80% Phase A. The flow rate was maintained constant at 1 mL/min throughout the entire run [14].

RNA extraction and gene expression analysis

Fermentation broths were sampled at 24 h and 48 h post-inoculation. A 2 mL aliquot was centrifuged at 4 °C and 13,000 rpm for 5 min. The cell pellet was washed twice with sterile water, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and mechanically ground using a mortar and pestle. Total RNA was isolated from the lysate using the TransZol Up Plus RNA Kit (TransGen Biotech) following the manufacturer's protocol. Genomic DNA contamination was eliminated by incubating 15.8 μL of total RNA with 2 μL of 10 × DNase I Buffer, 2 μL of Recombinant RNase Inhibitor, and 0.2 μL of Recombinant DNase I (RNase-free) at 37 °C for 2.5 h.

For reverse transcription, a mixture containing X μL of total RNA (≈800 ng), 0.5 μL of Random Primer pd(N)9, and DEPC-treated water (to a total volume of 6 μL) was denatured at 72 °C for 10 min, immediately cooled on ice for 2 min, and briefly centrifuged (5 s). Subsequently, 0.5 μL of dNTP Mixture (10 mM), 0.25 μL of Recombinant RNase Inhibitor, 2 μL of 5 × Reverse Transcriptase M-MLV Buffer, 1 μL of DEPC-treated water, and 0.25 μL of Reverse Transcriptase M-MLV (RNase H⁻) were added. The reaction proceeded at 30°C for 10 min (primer extension), 42 °C for 60 min (cDNA synthesis), and 70 °C for 15 min (enzyme inactivation). The cDNA products were stored at − 80 °C. All enzymes and reagents for DNase treatment and reverse transcription were purchased from TaKaRa Bio Inc.

Semi-quantitative RT-PCR for target genes was performed using the T100™ Thermal Cycler system (Bio-Rad, USA). The reaction mixture (10 μL total volume) consisted of 0.5 μL forward primer, 0.5 μL reverse primer, 1 μL cDNA template, 3 μL nuclease-free water, and 5 μL 2 × Hieff™ PCR Master Mix (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; 32 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 30 s, annealing at X °C (where X = primer Tm -2 °C) for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 20 s; and followed by a final extension at 72°C for 10 min.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted using the SLAN-96S Real-Time PCR System (Hongshi Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). The reaction mixture (20 μL total volume) included 0.4 μL forward primer, 0.4 μL reverse primer, X μL cDNA template (adjusting volume to maintain constant input RNA), 9.2 − X μL nuclease-free water, and 10 μL Hieff UNICON™ Universal Blue qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min; 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. The 16S rRNA gene was used as an endogenous reference for normalization, and relative transcript levels of target genes at different time points were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot

A 2 mL aliquot of the fermentation broth was centrifuged at 13,000 rpm and 4 °C for 5 min. The cell pellet was washed twice with sterile water, followed by two additional washes with PBS I solution (137 mM NaCl, 2.7 mM KCl, 2 mM KH2PO4, 10 mM Na2HPO4, pH adjusted to 7.4 with HCl). The washed pellet was resuspended in PBS I solution to achieve an optical density of 10 OD600/mL. Cell lysis was performed via ultrasonication on ice, and the resulting lysate was subjected to Western Blot analysis for target protein detection.

Results and discussion

Modulation of amino acid precursor supply

Surfactin biosynthesis requires branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs: Leu, Val) and acidic amino acids (Glu, Asp) for peptide ring assembly. To augment the supply of the direct precursor L-aspartate (Asp), we targeted the ansZ gene, which encodes L-asparaginase—an enzyme that catalyzes the production of Asp from L-asparagine. We employed the strong constitutive P43 promoter, derived from the cdd gene of B. subtilis 168 [19, 22], for its overexpression. However, initial Western blot analysis failed to detect a clear signal for the AnsZ protein (1-min exposure), indicating low expression levels (Fig. 1B). To test this, we extended the P43 promoter sequence from 260 to 520 bp upstream of the transcriptional start site (TSS). Recognizing that translation initiation is critically modulated by the 5′ untranslated region (UTR), we also engineered a series of promoter-5′ UTR hybrid constructs. We systematically designed and evaluated four variants: P43 (260-bp promoter + native cdd 5′ UTR), P43N (260-bp promoter + cognate ansZ 5′ UTR), P43L (520-bp promoter + native cdd 5′ UTR), and P43LN (520-bp promoter + cognate ansZ 5′ UTR) (Fig. 1A). Furthermore, Western blot analysis revealed that replacing the native cdd 5′ UTR with the cognate ansZ 5′ UTR substantially increased translation efficiency (Fig. 1B). Quantitative RT-PCR confirmed that the 520-bp extension significantly enhanced ansZ transcription (Fig. 1C). This synergy underscores the importance of coordinated transcriptional and translational optimization for maximizing heterologous gene expression in Bacillus. The enhanced performance of P43LN can be mechanistically explained by two cooperative effects. First, the 520-bp upstream extension likely retains additional upstream activating sequences or transcription factor binding elements that strengthen RNA polymerase recruitment and promoter clearance. Second, pairing the extended promoter with a cognate 5′ UTR optimizes ribosome binding site accessibility, mRNA secondary structure, and translation initiation efficiency. The integration of these transcriptional and translational enhancements results in a substantially elevated protein output compared to the conventional core P43 promoter.

Despite successfully establishing this optimized expression system, its application for metabolic engineering yielded an unexpected outcome. Overexpressing ansZ in TP1 under the control of the optimized P43LN promoter decreased surfactin titer by 7.8% and yield per OD600 by 11.0%, without affecting cellular growth (Fig. 1D, F).

Second, to augment the intracellular pool of BCAAs, we adopted a contrasting strategy: deleting bcaP (also known as yhdG), which encodes a BCAA permease responsible for chain amino acid uptake. Notably, although bcaP primarily facilitates BCAA import, its deletion in Bacillus licheniformis has been shown to significantly elevate intracellular amino acid levels and enhance bacitracin production, whereas its overexpression reduces yield—a precedent supporting our knockout strategy [27]. The divergent outcomes from overexpressing ansZ and deleting bcaP indicate that simply enhancing the supply of a single precursor amino acid through targeted genetic modification can have limited or even counterproductive effects on surfactin synthesis. These findings highlight the inherent complexity of the cellular amino acid supply network, where tightly interconnected biosynthetic pathways, feedback inhibition mechanisms, and precursor channeling collectively constrain the outcomes of metabolic engineering strategies.

Improvement of cellular tolerance via efflux engineering

YerP is a transmembrane transporter responsible for surfactin efflux and contributes to bacterial self-resistance against surfactin. To alleviate surfactin-induced cytotoxicity and potential feedback inhibition, we targeted yerP for overexpression. Initial attempts to construct plasmids carrying the yerP open reading frame under the control of P43 promoter in E. coli were unsuccessful. Therefore, we employed an in situ replacement strategy in TP1. We first replaced the native yerP promoter with the P43 promoter, generating strain OP43yerP (Fig. 3A). However, this modification did not improve surfactin production (Fig. S1A). This finding further corroborates that the optimized P43L and P43LN promoters possess superior transcriptional activity.

Knockout of the biofilm synthesis pathway

The formation of the biofilm, which is composed of polysaccharides, proteins, and nucleic acids, inevitably competes with surfactin synthesis for carbon sources, amino acids, NADPH, and ATP [28]; therefore, knocking out the biofilm is beneficial for increasing surfactin production [22]. In B. subtilis, biofilm matrix synthesis is primarily governed by the eps gene cluster and the tasA-sipW-yqxM operon [22, 28]. To redirect metabolic flux toward surfactin production, we aimed to reduce the diversion of precursors and energy into biofilm formation. To this end, we constructed two mutant derivatives of the parent strain TP1: ∆eps (with a deletion of the eps operon) and TS1 (with a combined deletion of both the eps operon and the tasA-sipW-yqxM operon).

Fermentation profiles and surfactin production of TP1, ∆eps, and TS1 were analyzed. As shown in Fig. 4A, all three strains exhibited nearly identical growth curves, indicating that the deletions did not impair cellular growth. Surfactin production was evaluated at 96 h. Compared to the TP1 (2.21 g/L, yielding 0.317 g/OD600), the production and specific productivity of ∆eps increased to 2.30 g/L and 0.353 g/OD600, representing improvements of 4.1% and 11.4%, respectively. The double mutant TS1 showed a more pronounced enhancement, achieving a titer of 2.56 g/L and a specific productivity of 0.394 g/OD600, corresponding to increases of 15.8% and 24.3% over TP1 (Fig. 4B). Knockout of antimicrobial peptide synthesis pathways

B. subtilis produces a diverse array of antimicrobial substances, which are primarily classified into two major categories: those synthesized via nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) or polyketide synthase (PKS) pathways—such as surfactin, iturin, fengycin, bacillaene, and bacilysin—and those produced through ribosomal pathways—including subtilin and subtilosin A. The biosynthesis of these antimicrobial compounds inherently competes for shared precursors, such as amino acids, fatty acids, and cellular energy (ATP).

Analysis of the TP1 genome sequence revealed five additional antimicrobial peptide synthesis gene clusters besides the surfactin operon: the pps cluster (for fengycin), the bac operon (for bacilysin), the pks cluster (for bacillaene), the sbo-alb cluster (for subtilosin A), and the dhb cluster (for bacillibactin). To reduce metabolic diversion toward these competing pathways, we attempted to disrupt these gene clusters in strain TP1, aiming to construct a high-yield surfactin producer. However, transformation attempts with constructs targeting the dhb or sbo-alb clusters did not yield viable transformants, suggesting these clusters may be essential under the experimental conditions or that their disruption is lethal.

In contrast, successful disruption of the nonessential lipopeptide synthesis clusters—pps (Δpps), pks (Δpks), and bac (Δbac)—effectively enhanced surfactin production. As shown in Fig. 5, these knockouts increased surfactin titer by 13.5%, 33.3%, and 33.3%, respectively, with the corresponding per-OD600 productivity rising by 22.2%, 40.8%, and 40.1%. These improvements are attributed to the redirection of metabolic resources (including amino acids, fatty acids, and ATP) from the deleted competing pathways toward surfactin biosynthesis. Enhancement of NADPH regeneration

Amino acid and fatty acid biosynthesis requires substantial reducing power in the form of NADPH. The pentose phosphate pathway (PPP) serves as a major cellular source of NADPH. Therefore, reinforcing this pathway is expected to favor the synthesis of amino acids and fatty acids, thereby increasing the precursors available for surfactin production. To augment the intracellular NADPH supply, we overexpressed the zwf gene, which encodes glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PDH), the key and rate-limiting enzyme of the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway [29].

However, previous transcriptomic analyses demonstrated that while the expression levels of zwf and its downstream gene gndA were moderately upregulated in the high-yield strain Bacillus amyloliquefaciens MT45 relative to the low-yield strain DSM7T, these differences did not reach statistical significance. This observation led researchers to propose that NADPH availability may not act as a critical rate-limiting factor governing surfactin biosynthesis [30].

To directly investigate the effect of NADPH supply on surfactin biosynthesis, the zwf gene was overexpressed in strain TP1 in this study. In the design of the expression construct, since the transcriptional start site (TSS) for zwf in B. subtilis was not clearly defined, we designated a 40-bp sequence upstream of its start codon as the 5′ UTR. We selected the P43L and P43LN promoters (derived from the P43L promoter variant with a 520-bp upstream region and the native zwf 5′ UTR) to drive expression.

When zwf was overexpressed in the base strain TP1 under the control of the P43L or P43LN promoter, surfactin titers increased by 26.2% and 23.5%, respectively, without compromising cellular growth (Fig. 6A, B). Integrated metabolic engineering and multipathway optimization

Based on the results from the individual strategies described above, we designed and implemented a series of multipathway engineering approaches. These aimed to synergistically address multiple bottlenecks, including precursor supply, metabolic competition, and cellular tolerance, to ultimately construct a high-yield surfactin-producing B. subtilis strain.

These results indicate that the engineering of TS8 successfully enhanced surfactin production without altering its isomer profile.

IrrE is a genus-specific regulatory factor in Deinococcus that positively controls the expression of various stress-related proteins [34]. It has been successfully heterologously expressed in E. coli, yeast, Pseudomonas putida, and B. subtilis, enhancing stress resistance in these hosts [34–36]. During microbial fermentation, high osmotic pressure from concentrated substrates or products, along with the accumulation of metabolites or byproducts, creates an environment detrimental to microbial growth and metabolite biosynthesis. This ultimately limits final product concentration and productivity. Consequently, employing IrrE as a heterologous stress-tolerance element has emerged as a prominent research direction to engineer robust microbial cell factories.

To enhance the overall robustness of our engineered strain under fermentation conditions, We replaced the pks gene cluster with a irrE, which is driven by the P43L promoter, to generate strain TS9. We verified the successful heterologous expression of irrE in B. subtilis by Western blot analysis (Fig. S2). Although the total surfactin titer did not change significantly, the per-OD600 yield increased markedly (Fig. 10A, B). This indicates that IrrE-mediated stress tolerance improved the metabolic efficiency of cells under fermentation pressure.

Conclusion

In this study, we have successfully engineered B. subtilis for significantly enhanced surfactin production through a systemic, multi-tiered metabolic engineering strategy. By sequentially addressing the key bottlenecks of precursor supply, metabolic competition, cellular tolerance, and global regulation, we achieved a greater than 100% increase in specific productivity in the final strain, TS8. This was accomplished by integrating targeted knockouts of competing pathways (eps, tasA-sipW-yqxM, pps, bac, pks), fine-tuning of amino acid flux (bcaP deletion), reinforcement of NADPH regeneration (zwf overexpression), and enhancement of product secretion and stress resilience (yerP).

A cornerstone of this success was the development and optimization of a versatile expression tool based on the P43 promoter. We demonstrated that extending its upstream region to 520 bp and pairing it with cognate 5′ UTRs were critical for achieving high-level, stable expression of diverse target genes (ansZ, yerP, zwf, irrE) directly on the chromosome. This optimized “gene replacement” strategy proved to be a robust platform, enabling simultaneous knockout and gain-of-function without plasmid-associated instability. The modularity and strength of this engineered P43 system were instrumental in constructing the complex, multi-modification genotypes required for systemic pathway optimization.

Collectively, our work not only establishes a high-yield surfactin producer but also provides a validated metabolic engineering blueprint and a potent genetic toolbox for B. subtilis. It highlights that maximizing the yield of complex natural products like surfactin necessitates a shift from single-gene edits to implement coordinated multipathway interventions, underpinned by precisely tuned expression regulation.
