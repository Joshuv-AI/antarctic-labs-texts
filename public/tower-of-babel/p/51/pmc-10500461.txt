Untargeted polysulfide omics analysis of alternations in polysulfide production during the germination of broccoli sprouts
Authors: Shingo Kasamatsu, Takuma Owaki, Somei Komae, Ayaka Kinno, Tomoaki Ida, Takaaki Akaike, Hideshi Ihara
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigher consumption of broccoli (Brassica oleracea var. italica) is associated with a reduced risk of cardiometabolic diseases, neurological disorders, diabetes, and cancer. Broccoli is rich in various phytochemicals, including glucosinolates, and isothiocyanates. Moreover, it has recently reported the endogenous production of polysulfides, such as cysteine hydropersulfide (CysS2H) and glutathione hydropersulfide (GS2H), in mammals including humans, and that these bioactive substances function as potent antioxidants and important regulators of redox signaling in vivo. However, few studies have focused on the endogenous polysulfide content of broccoli and the impact of germination on the polysulfide content and composition in broccoli. In this study, we investigated the alternations in polysulfide biosynthesis in broccoli during germination by performing untargeted polysulfide omics analysis and quantitative targeted polysulfide metabolomics through liquid chromatography–electrospray ionization–tandem mass spectrometry. We also performed 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl radical–scavenging assay to determine the antioxidant properties of the polysulfides. The results revealed that the total polysulfide content of broccoli sprouts significantly increased during germination and growth; CysS2H and cysteine hydrotrisulfide were the predominant organic polysulfide metabolites. Furthermore, we determined that novel sulforaphane (SFN) derivatives conjugated with CysS2H and GS2H were endogenously produced in the broccoli sprouts, and the novel SFN conjugated with CysS2H exhibited a greater radical scavenging capacity than SFN and cysteine. These results suggest that the abundance of polysulfides in broccoli sprouts contribute to their health-promoting properties. Our findings have important biological implications for the development of novel pharmacological targets for the health-promoting effects of broccoli sprouts in humans.Keywords: Polysulfide, Broccoli sprouts, Cysteine hydrotrisulfide, Sulforaphane, Antioxidant activity

Introduction

Cruciferous vegetables, including broccoli (Brassica oleracea var. italica), cabbage (B. oleracea var. capitata), cauliflower (B. oleracea var. botrytis), white radish (daikon; Raphanus sativus var. longipinnatus), kale (B. oleracea var. acephala), and watercress (Nasturtium officinale), are consumed worldwide [1]. For instance, broccoli is among the most widely consumed vegetables in the United States. Several epidemiological studies revealed that a higher intake of cruciferous vegetables is associated with a reduced risk of cardiometabolic diseases, neurological disorders, diabetes, and cancer [2]. The Brassicaceae family contains various phytochemical compounds—including phenolics, carotenoids, glucosinolates—and their breakdown products, such as isothiocyanates and indoles [3]. The secondary metabolites, especially glucosinolates, synthesized by different Brassicaceae species render them a unique and distinctive taste and provide them with various medicinal properties, thereby distinguishing the Brassicaceae family from other plant families. Glucoraphanin is the primary glucosinolate in broccoli; it gives rise to sulforaphane (SFN, 1-isothiocyanato-4-[methylsulfinyl]butane), a bioactive isothiocyanate, via hydrolysis mediated by β-thioglucosidase (myrosinase) [4]. Recently, broccoli sprouts have received enormous attention because their bioactive components differ from those of mature broccoli and are reportedly more effective than those of mature broccoli in preventing oxidative stress–associated diseases [5]. In fact, a study by Fahey et al. revealed that the glucoraphanin content of broccoli sprout and mature broccoli extracts was 16.6 and 1.08 μmol/g fresh weight, respectively [4]. They also reported that the total amount of glucosinolates in broccoli sprouts was 10-fold higher than that in mature broccoli [4]. In contrast, Branca et al. reported that the glucosinolate content of broccoli seeds was higher than that of broccoli sprouts [6]. Therefore, the bioactive constituents of broccoli sprouts may change dramatically during germination.

Recent animal studies highlighted the biological importance of polysulfides (RSnR, n > 2, R = hydrogen or alkyl). For example, it has been demonstrated that hydropolysulfides (RSnH, n > 2, R = alkyl) including cysteine and glutathione hydropolysulfides (CysSnH and GSnH), such as cysteine hydropersulfide (CysS2H) and glutathione hydropersulfide (GS2H), are endogenously generated by enzymatic reactions and/or chemical sulfur transfer reactions in vivo [7]. Our recent study reports that not only bacterial (Escherichia coli) but also mammalian (human and mouse) recombinant cysteinyl-tRNA synthetases can catalyze the formation of CysS2H from cysteine (CysSH) that was used as a substrate [8]. Furthermore, these bioactive substances can function as potent antioxidants and play important roles in multiple cellular processes, such as the regulation of mitochondrial biogenesis and metabolism [8,9]. A significant decrease in endogenous production of polysulfides, including CysS2H and GS2H, has been reported in human patients with cardiovascular disease and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease [10,11], indicating their potential role in human health and disease prevention. Several volatile polysulfides, such as diallyl trisulfide, diallyl tetrasulfide, and dimethyl trisulfide, are a family of lipophilic polysulfides that have been identified in essential oils derived from garlic (Allium sativum) and onions (A. cepa), and possess anti-inflammatory, cardioprotective, and cancer-preventive properties [12]. These volatile polysulfides originate from (+)-S-alk(en)yl-l-cysteine sulfoxides, such as (+)-S-methyl-l-cysteine sulfoxide (SMCSO, methiin) and S-allyl-l-cysteine sulfoxide (alliin) [12]. Previous studies have reported that Brassica vegetables, including broccoli and white cabbage, also contain SMCSO [[13], [14], [15]]; Coode-Bate et al. demonstrated that the SMCSO content of broccoli was higher than its glucosinolate content [16]. Despite the higher concentration of SMCSO than glucosinolates in Brassicaceae vegetables, this compound has gained little attention regarding its potential health-promoting properties because of the complexity of SMCSO catabolism. Although dimethyl trisulfide is also found in mature broccoli and broccoli sprouts [17,18], few studies have focused on the content and composition of polysulfides in broccoli and the effects of germination on endogenous polysulfide production.

In this study, we aim to investigate the endogenous polysulfide content of broccoli and the impact of germination on the polysulfide content and composition in broccoli. We first measured the total polysulfide content (TPsC) which is the total number of sulfur atom liberated from polysulfides and total sulfur content (TSC) of broccoli sprouts and mature broccoli, and analyzed their polysulfide profiles (% of TPsC to TSC). Then, we developed a technology for comprehensive detection of hydropolysulfides in broccoli sprouts, namely untargeted polysulfide omics analysis, using a novel alkylating agent, N-iodoacetyl l-tyrosine methyl ester (TME-IAM [19]), which can derivatize hydropolysulfides into a stable adduct with minimal artificial decomposition. Our findings have important biological implications for the development of novel pharmacological targets for the health-promoting effects of broccoli sprouts on humans.

Materials and methods

Materials

CysSH (98%), cystine (95%), glutathione (GSH, 97%), and glutathione disulfide (GS2G, 95%) were purchased from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemicals (Osaka, Japan). N-Ethylmaleimide (NEM, >99%) and dithiothreitol (DTT, >99%) were purchased from Nacalai Tesque (Kyoto, Japan). dl-SFN (>95%) and 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) were obtained from Tokyo Chemical Industry (Tokyo, Japan). Trolox (6-hydroxy-2,5,7,8-tetramethylchroman-2-carboxylic acid) and sodium disulfide (Na2S2) were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany) and Dojindo Laboratories (Kumamoto, Japan), respectively. An authentic stable isotope–labeled standard of the bis-S-NEM-adduct (NEM-34S-NEM-d10) was prepared using stable isotope–labeled NEM (NEM-d5) and sodium sulfide (Na234S), as previously reported [20]. TME-IAM and its stable isotope–labeled adduct standards were synthesized as described in our previous study [19]. Stable isotope–labeled TME-[13C2]IAM was also synthesized using [13C2]iodoacetic acid (purity 99%; Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, MA) instead of the normal iodoacetic acid according to a previously published procedure [19]. Broccoli seeds were purchased from Nakahara Seed (Fukuoka, Japan). All the other chemicals and reagents were purchased from general chemical suppliers and were of the highest commercially available grade.

Vegetables

Domestically cultivated mature vegetables (broccoli, cabbage, and garlic) and sprouts [white radish, red radish (R. sativus var. sativus), red cabbage (B. oleracea var. capitata f. rubra), watercress, mustard (B. juncea), Welsh onion (Allium fistulosum), water spinach (Ipomoea aquatica), alfalfa (Medicago sativa), okra (Abelmoschus esculentus), and broccoli] were purchased from local supermarkets, powdered in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until required for analysis.

For broccoli seed germination and growth experiments, broccoli seeds (approximately 1 g) were soaked in ultrapure water at room temperature (24–25 °C) for 24 h in the dark (Day 1). The seeds were then placed in a 12.5 cm-diameter tray (Kitchen farm-120; W125 × D125 × H77 mm; Yamato Plastic Co., Nara, Japan). The tray was stacked on another tray filled with ultrapure water to maintain saturated humidity at room temperature (24–25 °C). The height of the water in the bottom tray was approximately 15 cm; at this level, only the sprout roots were submerged in water. The sprouts were harvested every 24 h (Days 2–5), powdered in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C until required for analysis. Representative images of the broccoli seeds and sprouts are shown in Fig. S1.

To determine the water content of the vegetables, fresh vegetable samples (approximately 1 g) were placed in a unibloc moisture analyzer (MOC63u; Shimadzu Co., Kyoto, Japan), the dry weight was measured, and the water content (%) was calculated using the formula shown below.

Quantification of the TSC through acid-circulating decomposition and inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy

To quantify the TSC, all sulfur-containing molecules were converted into sulfate ions using an acid-circulating decomposition system (ECOPRE system; ACTAC, Tokyo, Japan), and the concentration of the sulfate ions was measured using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) [21]. Approximately 100 mg of each vegetable sample was mixed with a 15.8 M aqueous solution of HNO3 (10 mL) in a digestion vessel (ACTAC); then, the digestion vessel was combined with a condensation vessel (ACTAC) containing 5% (v/v) HNO3 (5 mL). Afterwards, digestion was performed via multiple heating/cooling cycles on a graphite hot plate (AS ONE Co., Osaka, Japan), as described in Fig. S2. After the samples were cooled to 25 °C, they were collected, diluted to 30 mL using 1 M HNO3 solution, and then further diluted to concentrations of 1–10 ppm using 1 M HNO3 solution. Ultrapure water without vegetables was utilized as a blank sample; the signals detected from the blank samples were subtracted from the signals obtained from the individual samples. The sulfate ion concentrations in the vegetable samples were measured using ICP-OES (ICPE-9000, Shimadzu Co., Kyoto, Japan) and quantified using a standard curve obtained from a sulfate ion standard (KANTO CHEMICAL Co., Tokyo, Japan). The ICP-OES measurement parameters were as follows: argon gas pressure, 450 ± 10 kPa; radiofrequency power, 1.2 kW; plasma gas flow rate, 14 L/min; carrier gas flow rate, 0.7 L/min; exposure time, 30 s; and observation wavelengths, 180.731, 182.037, and 182.625 nm.

Quantification of the TPsC using an alkaline/reductive sulfur elimination protocol

The TPsC was quantified using an alkaline/reductive sulfur elimination protocol [21]. The vegetable specimens were treated with the reductant DTT at alkaline pH to decompose the polysulfides, and the liberated sulfur atom(s) were captured by the alkylating agent NEM to form a stable bis-S-NEM-adduct (NEM-S-NEM), which was then quantitatively detected by employing liquid chromatography–electrospray ionization–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–ESI–MS/MS) combined with the stable isotope–labeled standard dilution method. An ULTRA-TURRAX® homogenizer (T10 basic; IKA, Osaka, Japan) was used to homogenize approximately 100 mg of the vegetable samples with a 20-fold volume of 100 mM N-cyclohexyl-3-aminopropanesulfonic acid–NaOH buffer (pH 11.0) containing 10 mM DTT and 25% (v/v) ethanol. Afterwards, sonication was performed for 15 s (repeated thrice with a 30 s interval), and the homogenates were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h in a heating block incubator (NICHIHIRO, Saitama, Japan). The reaction mixtures (10 μL) were then added to 200 mM tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane (Tris)-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) containing 20 mM NEM and 80% (v/v) methanol (90 μL); then, the resulting mixtures were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h in a heatblock incubator. After centrifugation at 15,000 g and 4 °C for 15 min, the resulting supernatant (10 μL) was diluted 10 times with 0.1% (v/v) formic acid (FA) solution containing 0.1 μM of NEM-34S-NEM-d10, the authentic isotope-labeled standard. The obtained NEM adducts were detected by performing LC–ESI–MS/MS analysis using the modified stable isotope–labeled standard dilution method described in our previous study [20], as described in Section 2.5. Buffer blanks without vegetables were prepared, and the signals detected from the buffer blank samples were subtracted from the signals obtained from the individual samples.

LC–ESI–MS/MS

LC–ESI–MS/MS analyses were performed using a Xevo TQD Triple Quadrupole Mass Spectrometer (Waters Co., Milford, MA) coupled to a Shimadzu LC-20A system (Shimadzu Co.) equipped with an autosampler (SIL-20A), a communications bus module (CBM-20A), an online degasser (DGU-20A5), two liquid chromatographs (LC-20AD), and a column oven (CTO-20A). The samples were evaluated using the Shimadzu LC-20A system on a Mightysil-C18 column (50 mm × 2.0 mm inner diameter; KANTO CHEMICAL Co.) and then eluted using methanol as the mobile phase with a linear gradient (for detection of NEM-S-NEM, 1% B at 0–1 min, 99% B at 4–5 min, 1% B at 5.1–8 min; for untargeted polysulfide omics analysis, 1% B at 0 min, 99% B at 10–12 min, 1% B at 13–18 min; for quantitative targeted polysulfide metabolomics and detection of SFN derivatives and oxidized glutathione polysulfides [GSnG], 1% B at 0–1 min, 99% B at 7–10 min, 1% B at 10.1–15 min) in the presence of 0.1% FA at a flow rate of 0.6 mL/min at 40 °C. The mass spectrometer was operated in the positive mode with the capillary voltage and desolvation gas (nitrogen) set to 1000 V and 1000 L/h, respectively, at 500 °C. The respective adducts were detected in the multiple reaction monitoring mode using the parameters shown in Table S1.

Untargeted polysulfide omics analysis

A POLYTRON® homogenizer (DIAX-100; Heidolph Instruments, Schwabach, Germany) was used to homogenize approximately 1 g of broccoli seeds and sprouts at Days 2, and 5 in 5 mL of 50 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 6.5) containing normal TME-IAM (light, 0.8 mM), stable isotope–labeled TME-[13C2]IAM (heavy, 0.2 mM), and 70% methanol. The homogenates were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min; the reaction was terminated by adding 0.5 mL of 10% FA, followed by centrifugation at 20,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants were collected, diluted with five-fold volume of 0.1% FA, and subjected into a reverse-phase solid phase extraction using an open column of Wakogel® 100C18 (Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemicals) preequilibrated with 0.1% FA. The column was washed with five column volumes (CVs) of 0.1% FA, then washed with five CVs of ultrapure water. TME-AM-adducts were eluted with three CVs of methanol containing 0.01% FA, and the eluates were concentrated by centrifugation (175 g, at 23 °C, for approximately 2.5 h) in vacuo until the sample volume became approximately 0.5–0.6 mL. An aliquot of the concentrate was incubated with 100 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) in the presence or absence of 1% (v/v) 2-mercaptoethanol at 37 °C for 30 min. After filtration using a centrifugation filter (Cosmospin filter; Nacalai Tesque), the flow-through was subjected to LC–ESI–MS/MS for untargeted polysulfide omics analysis. We considered polysulfide candidates whose intensity was greater than one-tenth that of GSH as significant signals.

Quantitative targeted polysulfide metabolomics

CysSnH, GSnH, and hydrogen polysulfides (H2Sn) (n = 1–3, respectively) were quantified through a previously reported procedure [19] with slight modifications. A POLYTRON® homogenizer (DIAX-100) was used to homogenize 40–65 mg of broccoli seeds, germinated seeds (Day 1), and sprouts (Days 2–5) in a 20-fold volume of 50 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 6.5) containing 1 mM normal TME-IAM and 70% methanol. The homogenates were incubated at 37 °C for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 20,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatants (100 μL) were collected, acidified with 10 μL of 10% FA, and mixed with 100 nM stable isotope–labeled TME-AM-adduct standards in 0.1% FA prior to LC–ESI–MS/MS analysis. The obtained TME-AM-adducts were quantified through LC–ESI–MS/MS analysis using the stable isotope–labeled standard dilution method. The polysulfide concentrations were normalized to the dry weights of the samples.

Synthesis and detection of SFN derivatives

To examine the possibility of forming SFN conjugates with CysSnH and GSnH, 1.43 mM CysSH or GSH were incubated in 85.7 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) containing 4.29 mM Na2S2 at 37 °C for 15 min in the dark; the generated CysSnH and GSnH were treated with 3 mM SFN at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. The obtained mixture was diluted 20-fold with 0.1% FA to terminate the reaction; afterwards, it was subjected to LC–ESI–MS/MS analysis.

For the preparation of authentic SFN derivatives, 1.96 mM GSH or CysSH were incubated in 117 mM Tris-HCl buffer (pH 7.4) containing 5.88 mM Na2S2 at 37 °C for 15 min in the dark; the mixtures (85 μL) were combined with 15 μL of 33.3 mM SFN at 37 °C for 1 h in the dark. The mixtures were diluted 10-fold with 0.1% FA to terminate the reaction, and the synthesized SFN derivatives were separated using reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). HPLC was performed using PU-2089 Plus (JASCO International Co.,Tokyo, Japan) with an ultraviolet detector (UV-4075; JASCO International Co.) set to 250 nm. The reaction mixture was injected into a C18 reverse-phase column (COSMOSIL 5C18-AR-II 10.0 mm × 150 mm; Nacalai Tesque) with a linear gradient of solvent A (water containing 0.1% FA) and solvent B (100% methanol) (gradient = 0% B at 0–4 min, 50% B at 45 min, and 100% B at 46 min) at a flow rate of 3 mL/min. After concentrating the eluate by centrifugation (175 g at 23 °C) in vacuo, the samples were further purified using a C18 reverse-phase column (Mightysil RP-18 GP 6.0 mm × 150 mm; KANTO CHEMICAL Co.) with a linear gradient of solvent A (water containing 0.1% FA) and solvent B (100% methanol) (gradient = 0% B at 0–3 min, 50% B at 15 min, 100% B at 15.1 min) at a flow rate of 3 mL/min. The fractions containing the SFN derivatives were concentrated by centrifugation (175 g at 23 °C) in vacuo. After evaluating the purity of the isolated SFN derivatives through LC–ESI–MS/MS analysis, the concentrations of the purified SFN derivatives with CysSnH and GSnH were determined through HPLC analysis, by measuring the absorbance at 250 nm and calculating from the standard curve of authentic CysSH conjugate of SFN (SFN-SCys) and GSH conjugate of SFN (SFN-SG, Toronto Research Chemicals, Ontario, Canada; 95%), respectively.

To detect the endogenous production of SFN derivatives and oxidized GSnG (n = 3–5), a POLYTRON® homogenizer (DIAX-100) was used to homogenize the broccoli seeds and sprouts (approximately 350 mg) in a 10-fold volume of 50 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 6.5) containing 0.1 mM normal TME-IAM and 70% methanol. After centrifugation at 20,000 g for 15 min at 4 °C, the resulting supernatants (700 μL) were concentrated by centrifugation (175 g at 23 °C) in vacuo until the sample volume became approximately 100 μL. After filtration using a centrifugation filter (Cosmospin), the flow-through was subjected to LC–ESI–MS/MS to detect the endogenous production of SFN derivatives and GSnG.

DPPH radical–scavenging assay

The DPPH radical–scavenging assay was performed as previously described [22] with slight modifications. The DPPH stock solution was prepared in ethanol. In a 96-well plate, 100 μM DPPH was incubated in 12 mM sodium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) in the presence or absence of analytes (final concentration: 25–1000 μM) at 25 °C for 20 min. The absorbance at 517 nm was measured using an Infinite® 200 PRO microplate reader (Tecan Trading, Männedorf, Switzerland). Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) of at least three independent experiments. All analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Comparison of the polysulfide profile between mature broccoli and broccoli sprouts

We first measured the TSC and TPsC of the mature broccoli, broccoli sprouts, cabbage, and garlic samples to analyze their polysulfide profiles (% of TPsC to TSC) (Fig. 1). As shown in Fig. 1A, the TSC of the mature broccoli, cabbage, and garlic samples did not significantly differ, which is consistent with previous reports [21,23,24]. In contrast, the TSC of the broccoli sprouts was significantly higher than that of mature broccoli; in fact, the broccoli sprouts had the highest TPsC (Fig. 1B). The TPsC of these vegetables accounted for 14.6%, 4.2%, 3.9%, and 2.4% of the TSC of the broccoli sprouts, mature broccoli, garlic, and cabbage specimens, respectively (Fig. 1C). This is the first evidence that broccoli sprouts are more abundant in polysulfides than mature broccoli. Furthermore, we compared the polysulfide profile of the broccoli sprouts with those of the other nine vegetable sprouts: white radish, red cabbage, red radish, watercress, mustard, Welsh onion, water spinach, alfalfa, and okra. Quantitative analysis revealed that among the vegetable sprouts, the broccoli sprouts had the highest TPsC, followed by the red cabbage (8.2%) and mustard (4.9%) sprouts (Fig. S3). The content and composition of glucosinolates in plants may be affected by the nutritional status of sulfur in the soil used for plant cultivation [25]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the supplemental sulfur fertilization of Brassica vegetables in greenhouse and field experiments affects glucosinolate content ranging from −25% to more than +1200% [[25], [26], [27]]. Thus, to carefully consider the effects of germination and growth on the polysulfide profile of broccoli, the alternations in the polysulfide profile of broccoli during germination and growth should be investigated.

Alternations in polysulfide profile during the germination and growth of broccoli sprouts

For the germination experiments, the broccoli seeds were soaked in ultrapure water for 24 h (Day 1) and then placed in a cultivation tray containing ultrapure water for additional 24–96 h in the dark (Days 2–5). As shown in Fig. S1, the broccoli seeds germinated on Day 1, and the hypocotyls were elongated in a cultivation time–dependent manner until Day 5. Approximately 80% of the seeds consistently germinated on Day 1. We compared the polysulfide profiles of the seeds, germinated seeds, and sprouts of broccoli (Fig. 2). As shown in Fig. 2A, there was no significant change in TSC during germination. In contrast, the TPsC of the broccoli seeds dramatically increased after soaking them in ultrapure water for 24 h (Day 1), and the TPsC elevated in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 2B). Calculation of % TPsC to TSC revealed that TPsC accounted for 15.5% of the TSC of the broccoli sprouts on Day 5 (Fig. 2C). Although it has been reported that the glucosinolate content and composition in broccoli sprouts decreased and changed compared with those in the seeds [28], this is the first evidence that the polysulfide content of broccoli significantly increased during germination and growth. Friedrich et al. reported that volatile polysulfides, such as dimethyl trisulfide and dimethyl tetrasulfide, can be generated in B. oleracea vegetables by C–S lyases using SMCSO as a substrate [29]. Ukai et al. also reported that cystine lyase purified from broccoli inflorescences can produce CysS2H (referred to as thiocysteine in the paper) by β-elimination of l-cystine and SMCSO [30]. However, the identity of the polysulfides endogenously produced in broccoli sprouts remain unclear.

Untargeted polysulfide omics analysis of broccoli sprouts

We developed a technology for the analysis of untargeted polysulfide omics, by which hydropolysulfides can be comprehensively detected through LC–ESI–MS/MS analysis. We have previously demonstrated that chemicals containing a hydroxyphenyl or hydroxyl group can stabilize polysulfide structures [8,31]; we successfully synthesized a novel alkylating agent, TME-IAM, which can derivatize hydropolysulfides to form a stable adduct via its iodoacetyl group and stabilize the polysulfide structure in the adduct via its hydroxyphenyl group [19]. By carefully considering the MS/MS fragmentation patterns of various TME-AM-adduct standards, we found that a common fragment ion at m/z 136 derived from the hydroxyphenyl group of TME-AM moiety was formed by collision-induced dissociation [19]. Based on this property of TME-AM-adducts, we developed a technology for untargeted polysulfide omics analysis using two types of TME-IAM (normal and stable isotope–labeled) at different concentrations (ratio of normal to stable isotope–labeled TME-IAM is 4:1) (Fig. 3A). Through this technology, hydropolysulfides can be derivatized by TME-IAM to form a stable adduct, and the relative signal intensity of the hydropolysulfide adducts of the normal TME-IAM (light) to that of the stable isotope–labeled one (heavy) is in the range of two to four. However, TME-IAM can react not only with hydropolysulfides but also with thiols; hence, it is necessary to distinguish between the signals derived from hydropolysulfides and those from thiols. The TME-AM-adducts of hydropolysulfides are susceptible to reductants because they contain a polysulfide structure, whereas the TME-AM-adducts of thiols are resistant to reductants because they lack a polysulfide structure. Hence, treatment with the reductant 2-mercaptoethanol enabled us to distinguish between the hydropolysulfide and thiol signals. In fact, a preliminary experiment with GSH and glutathione hydrotrisulfide (GS3H) standards showed that both GSH and GS3H were derivatized by the two types of TME-IAM, and the relative signal intensities of the respective adducts were approximately consistent with the ratio of the concentration of the normal TME-IAM to that of the stable isotope–labeled one (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, the GS-adduct remained intact after treatment with 2-mercaptoethanol, whereas the GS3-adduct disappeared completely after incubation with the reductant (Fig. 3B). These results suggest that this novel technology can be a useful tool for the comprehensive analysis of hydropolysulfides in biological samples.

A representative result of the untargeted polysulfide omics analysis of the broccoli seeds and sprouts (Days 2 and 5) is shown in Fig. S4 and Fig. 4, respectively. The number of signals identified as polysulfide candidates increased in this order: seeds < Day-2 sprouts < Day-5 sprouts. Thirty-seven types of signals, including CysS2H, cysteine hydrotrisulfide (CysS3H), cysteine hydrotetrasulfide, GS2H, GS3H, and glutathione hydrotetrasulfide, were detected as hydropolysulfide candidates in the broccoli sprouts (Day 5) (Fig. 4). Intriguingly, the signal intensities of CysS2H, CysS3H, GS2H, and GS3H were 55-, 107-, 4-, and 12-fold higher than those of the corresponding thiols (i.e., CysSH and GSH), respectively (data not shown). Previous studies in animals (humans and mice) have shown that GSH is the predominant thiol in vivo, and the GS2H levels are approximately 100-fold lower than those of GSH [[8], [9], [10],32,33]. These results suggest that polysulfide biosynthesis may be highly activated during the germination and growth of broccoli sprouts, implying that polysulfides play important physiological roles in seed germination and growth.

Quantification of endogenous CysSnH, GSnH, and H2Sn

To carefully evaluate the impact of germination on the endogenous production of polysulfides such as CysS2H and GS2H, we performed quantitative targeted polysulfide metabolomic analysis through LC–ESI–MS/MS with a stable isotope dilution method (Fig. 5), by which CysSnH, GSnH, and H2Sn (n = 1–3, respectively) can be specifically and quantitatively detected. Quantitative analyses revealed significant increase in CysSH, CysS2H, CysS3H, GS2H, GS3H, hydrogen sulfide (H2S), hydrogen disulfide (H2S2), and hydrogen trisulfide (H2S3) in the broccoli sprouts on Day 5, compared with those in the seeds; H2S3 was the most abundant polysulfide among the nine types of polysulfides and thiols analyzed, and the major organic polysulfide metabolites in the broccoli sprouts were CysS2H and CysS3H (Fig. 5B, C, and I). In contrast, there was no significant alternation in the GSH content during germination and growth of broccoli sprouts (Fig. 5D). It has been demonstrated that GS2H is the predominant polysulfide in animal tissues and cells [8,9,19]. In contrast, our results indicate, for the first time, that CysS2H and CysS3H are the predominant organic polysulfide metabolites in broccoli sprouts. Our previous study demonstrated that CysS2H and CysS3H can be generated by cysteinyl-tRNA synthetase using CysSH [8]. Furthermore, Ukai et al. reported that cystine lyase purified from broccoli inflorescences can produce CysS2H by β-elimination of l-cystine and SMCSO [30]. Thus, these enzymes may be highly activated during the germination and growth of broccoli sprouts, potentially resulting in a significant increase in CysS2H and CysS3H production.

It is well established that GSnG (n = 3–5) is endogenously produced in various living organisms, including humans, mice, and yeast [9,[34], [35], [36], [37]]; however, little is known about this process in plants. To detect the endogenous production of GSnG, the broccoli seeds and sprouts were homogenized in the presence of TME-IAM, which can convert hydropolysulfides into a stable adduct without artificial decomposition due to its iodoacetyl group and can stabilize the polysulfide structure by inhibiting hydrolysis due to its hydroxyphenyl group, resulting in the minimized artificial formation of the oxidized derivatives of polysulfides during sample preparation [19]. As shown in Fig. 6, the endogenous production of GSnG (n = 3–5) was detected in the broccoli seeds and sprouts, and the GSnG levels were significantly higher in the broccoli sprouts than in the seeds. Representative MS/MS chromatograms of endogenous and authentic GSnG are shown in Fig. S5. This is the first evidence of the endogenous production of various GSnG types in broccoli seeds and sprouts. A recent study by Barayeu et al. demonstrated that oxidized glutathione trisulfide (GS3G) functions as a superior radical scavenger in the presence of GSH to form GS2H [38]. Furthermore, Xu et al. revealed that H2S2 released from an O-silyl-mercaptan-based H2S2 donor could reduce the authentic radical DPPH more effectively than H2S released from an O-silyl-mercaptan-based H2S donor [39]. Previous studies have reported that sodium polysulfides (NaSn, n = 2–4) exert potent neuroprotective effects against oxidative stress–induced toxicity in 1-methyl-4-phenylpyridinium ion–treated midbrain dopaminergic neurons, methylmercury-treated cerebellar granule neurons, and methylglyoxal- or tert-butyl hydroperoxide–treated neuroblastoma cells [[40], [41], [42], [43]]. Therefore, the abundance of GSnH, CysSnH, H2Sn, and GSnG in broccoli sprouts may contribute to a broad spectrum of bioactivities, including antioxidant activity.

Accumulating evidence has indicated that numerous proteins are endogenously polysulfidated in both prokaryotes and eukaryotes, including mammals and plants [[44], [45], [46], [47], [48], [49], [50], [51]]. In our previous study, we developed a simple, reliable, and reproducible assay, namely the polyethylene glycol–conjugated maleimide-labeling gel shift assay, to detect protein polysulfides [8,52]. By the initial step with weak electrophiles such as 8-nitroguanosine 3′,5′-cyclic monophosphate to alkylate both protein thiol and polysulfide groups while minimizing artificial decomposition of polysulfides, biotin-polyethylene glycol36-maleimide (BPM) can react with the polysulfidated cysteine residues that still contained sulfane sulfur atom(s), resulting in bands being shifted up on sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE). Therefore, the assay enabled us to clarify the degree of protein polysulfidation as a change in SDS–PAGE band mobility, indicating that this assay is suitable for examining the degree of protein polysulfidation of a respective protein; however, it is not appropriate for the comprehensive analysis of protein polysulfidation status. Thus, to detect the alternations in the polysulfidated protein status in the broccoli seeds and sprouts, we utilized biotin-maleimide instead of BPM to minimize changes in SDS–PAGE band mobility (Fig. S6). As shown in Figs. S6A and a strong signal was detected at approximately 50 kDa in the seeds and germinated seeds (Day 1), whereas only a modest signal was observed in the broccoli sprouts (Days 2–5). In contrast, SDS–PAGE followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining showed that the protein expression patterns were dramatically altered during germination and growth (Fig. S6B). These results suggest that a polysulfide-rich protein may be utilized as a potential source for the biosynthesis of proteins and polysulfides.

Identification of the SFN conjugates of CysSnH and GSnH and their potent radical-scavenging capacities

It has been recognized that mature broccoli and broccoli sprouts are rich in SFN, which is generated via myrosinase-mediated hydrolysis of glucoraphanin. The central carbon atom of the isothiocyanate group of SFN is highly electrophilic and easily reacts with GSH and CysSH to form SFN-SG and SFN-SCys conjugates, respectively [53]. Benchoam et al. reported that the pKa of GS2H is 5.45, which is 3.49 units lower than that of GSH [54]. They also reported that GS2H can react with electrophiles, including peroxynitrite and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2), faster than GSH because of the increased availability of the anion and, depending on the electrophile, increased reactivity [54]. Therefore, we predicted that a reaction between SFN and GSnH or CysSnH may occur to form the GSnH- or CysSnH-conjugates of SFN (SFN-SnG or SFN-SnCys, respectively) in broccoli sprouts. To test this prediction, we first performed an in vitro experiment with SFN and authentic GSnH or CysSnH; then, we performed LC–ESI–MS/MS to analyze the reaction products in the mixture. As shown in Fig. S7, SFN-SnCys (n = 1–5) and SFN-SnG (n = 1–4) were successfully generated in vitro. We then analyzed the endogenous formation of these SFN derivatives in the broccoli seeds and sprouts (Fig. 7). Fig. S8 shows the representative MS/MS spectra of the endogenous SFN-SnCys (n = 1–5) and SFN-SnG (n = 1–4) detected in the broccoli sprouts (Day 5), which are consistent with the authentic standards (data not shown). Through LC–ESI–MS/MS analysis, the endogenous production of SFN-SnCys (n = 1–5) and SFN-SnG (n = 1–4) was detected in the broccoli sprouts, and a time-dependent increase in SFN-SnCys (n = 2–5) was observed (Fig. 7G–J), which is correlated with the time profile of SFN signals and CysSH content (Fig. S9). To detect the endogenous production of SFN-SnCys and SFN-SnG, the broccoli seeds and sprouts were homogenized in the presence of TME-IAM, which minimized the artificial formation of SFN-SnCys and SFN-SnG by trapping hydropolysulfides to form a stable TME-IAM adduct. Indeed, we confirmed that SFN did not interfere the reactivity of TME-IAM to CysSnH (Fig. S10), suggesting that TME-IAM enables us to precisely detect endogenous production of both oxidized polysulfides, such as SFN derivatives and GSnG, and hydropolysulfides with minimal artificial chnages. Hence, we believe that the SFN-SnCys and SFN-SnG detected in the broccoli seeds and sprouts were most likely generated endogenously.

SFN is known to function as a potent inducer of Phase II detoxification enzymes such as NAD(P)H:quinone oxidoreductase 1 and glutathione S-transferase by activating the Keap1/Nrf2 pathway [55]. Moreover, SFN can directly scavenge radicals; however, a recent study that employed the DPPH radical-scavenging assay reported that the radical-scavenging capacity of SFN was remarkably weaker than that of ascorbic acid [56]. The free radical– scavenging activities of broccoli seed extracts did not correlate with the SFN content of the seeds, indicating that SFN is not the primary antioxidant component of broccoli seeds [56]. In contrast, it has been demonstrated that GS2H has a greater H2O2-scavenging capacity than GSH [9]. Thus, we speculated that SFN conjugates with GSnH or CysSnH might exhibit greater radical-scavenging capacity than SFN. To test this hypothesis, we performed a DPPH radical–scavenging assay using authentic SFN-SnG and SFN-SnCys standards. We confirmed that various SFN-SnG (n = 1–4) and SFN-SnCys (n = 1–5) types were generated by the chemical reaction between SFN and authentic GSnH or CysSnH (Figs. S7 and S8); however, we only succeeded in isolating SFN-SnG and SFN-SnCys (n = 1 or 2, respectively) because the SFN derivatives containing a longer polysulfide chain (n > 3) easily degraded during purification by HPLC. As shown in Fig. S11, the isolated SFN-SG, SFN-S2G, SFN-SCys, and SFN-S2Cys were detected as protonated molecules at m/z 485.1193, 517.0914, 299.0552, and 331.0270, respectively, using high-resolution mass spectrometry. Product ion assignments also revealed that these novel SFN derivatives were successfully synthesized (Fig. S11). As shown in Fig. 8, by conducting DPPH radical-scavenging assay, a significant radical-scavenging capacity of SFN was not observed at the range of the examined concentration (100–1000 μM), consistent with a previous study [56]. In contrast, the order of the radical-scavenging capacity of the SFN conjugates with GSnH was SFN-S2G ≥ GSH > SFN-SG » SFN = oxidized glutathione disulfide. Similarly, the order of the radical-scavenging capacity of the SFN conjugates with CysSnH was SFN-S2Cys > SFN-SCys ≒ CysSH » SFN = cystine. These results suggest that the novel SFN derivatives can function as potent antioxidants. Furthermore, it was reported that treatment with SFN potently induced the expression of uridine diphosphate glucuronosyltransferase 1A1 and glutathione S-transferase A1 in HePG2 cells [57]. Similarly, SFN-SG can also induce Phase II detoxication enzymes [57], indicating that conjugation with GSH to form dithiocarbamate does not significantly modify the activity of SFN. Therefore, novel SFN conjugates with GSnH or CysSnH may perform multiple biological functions originating from the potential crosstalk between SFN and GSnH or CysSnH in vivo. To carefully consider pharmacological potentials of the novel SFN derivatives, further studies are required to investigate the effects of these substances on cellular antioxidant responses in vivo.

Conclusions

In this study, we investigated the alternation in polysulfide biosynthesis during the germination and growth of broccoli sprouts incubated in ultrapure water, without any chemicals such as exogenous sulfur-containing molecules. LC–ESI–MS/MS analysis revealed that the TPsC significantly increased in the broccoli sprouts; however, there was no significant difference between the TSC of the seeds and sprouts. Through the untargeted polysulfide omics analysis developed in this study, we found that polysulfide biosynthesis was dramatically activated during broccoli germination and growth, and 37 types of signals, including CysSnH (n = 2–4) and GSnH (n = 2–4), were detected as hydropolysulfide candidates in the broccoli sprouts on Day 5. By conducting quantitative targeted polysulfide metabolomics, it was confirmed that the CysSnH (n = 2, 3), GSnH (n = 2, 3), and H2Sn (n = 2, 3) content of the broccoli sprouts were significantly elevated. GSnG (n = 3–5), SFN-SnCys (n = 1–5), and SFN-SnG (n = 1–4) were endogenously produced in the broccoli sprouts. Furthermore, the DPPH radical–scavenging assay revealed that SFN-S2Cys and SFN-S2G, which are examples of SFN-SnCys, and SFN-SnG, respectively, exhibited greater radical-scavenging capacities than their precursors: SFN, CysSH, and GSH. These results suggest that the abundance of various polysulfides in broccoli sprouts may contribute to their health-promoting properties. However, the detailed mechanisms underlying polysulfide production and the biological importance of bioactive substances in seed germination and sprout growth remain unclear. Therefore, the biological importance of elevated endogenous polysulfide production during germination and growth should be clarified in future research. Hence, in the near future, we will investigate the detailed polysulfide production mechanism in broccoli sprouts and examine the physiological functions of endogenous polysulfides in seed germination and sprout growth. Our findings have important biological implications for the development of novel pharmacological targets for the health-promoting effects of broccoli sprouts in humans.
