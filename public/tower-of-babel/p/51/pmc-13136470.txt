Severity of perceived stress among primary family caregivers of patients admitted to medical and surgical intensive care units (ICUs) at tertiary care hospitals in Pakistan
Authors: Kartik Kumar Rathi, Maham Irfan, Inshal Arshad Malik, Umair Saleem, Zunairah Rais, Versha Kumari, Dahir Ashfaq, Amanullah Khoja, Nimrata Kumari, Muhammad Mehdi Taqvi, Hafsa Tariq, Umme Abiha Rizvi, Subata Khan, Ahmed Ali Shah, Nand Lal Rathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrimary caregivers of critically ill ICU patients experience substantial psychological stress, yet this burden remains understudied in lower middle-income countries such as Pakistan. This study assessed the magnitude and predictors of perceived stress among primary family caregivers of patients admitted to medical and surgical ICUs at three tertiary care hospitals in Karachi.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 161 primary caregivers at Jinnah Postgraduate Medical Center, Civil Hospital Karachi, and Liaquat National Hospital. Data were collected through interviews using the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10; range 0–40). Ethical approval and informed consent were obtained. Univariable multinomial logistic regression identified factors associated with stress levels, followed by multivariable multinomial logistic regression including significant or clinically relevant predictors.ResultsOverall, 65.8% experienced moderate stress (n = 106), 24.8% (n = 40) low stress, and 9.3% (n = 15) high stress. Female caregivers experienced significantly higher stress than males (p = 0.03), and those with comorbidities had elevated stress (40.0%, p = 0.02). Caregivers of medical ICU patients reported greater stress than those in surgical ICUs (p < 0.001). Sepsis, severe infection, and neurological diagnoses predicted moderate stress. Previous hospitalizations and ICU admissions were also associated with higher caregiver stress. Over half reported unaddressed psychological distress (51.2%, n = 82), and 65.4% (n = 104) had unmet mental health needs. After adjustment, medical ICU admission, caregiver comorbidities, and prior ICU admission independently predicted higher stress.ConclusionsCaregivers of critically ill patients experience clinically significant stress, particularly those caring for medical ICU patients and those with comorbidities. High level of unmet psychological needs highlights the urgent requirement for improved communication, targeted interventions, and the integration of psychological support in routine ICU protocols. This could help reduce caregiver burden and enhance patient care in low-resource settings.Keywords: Primary family caregivers, Perceived Stress, Intensive care unit, Mental health, Psychological stress, Caregiver stress

Background

Stress is a physiological response of the human body that arises when there is a real or perceived gap between the challenges posed by the environment and an individual’s ability to respond to those challenges [1, 2]. It enhances an individual’s ability to effectively manage and adapt to the difficulties of daily life, thus ultimately contributing to improved resilience, and overall survival [3, 4]. While certain stressors may objectively be insignificant, the impact that they have is under the subjective evaluation of every human being. This is known as perceived stress, and it refers to the degree to which an individual considers certain situations to be stressful regardless of the actual severity of the precipitant [5, 6]. This perception is often linked to feelings of uncertainty and is significantly shaped by an individual’s confidence in their ability to cope with life’s challenges [7]. Certain factors such as one’s literary background, prior life experiences, financial burden and socioeconomic status, as well as their ability to communicate with healthcare professionals can make them react more acutely and perceive stress differently than others.

In the Intensive Care Unit (ICU), the physical and psychological well-being of critically ill patients is instinctively accorded precedence. However, the emotional toll that it has on their primary caregivers is often overlooked. These individuals, usually immediate family members, play a critical role in the provision of care to patients, and are also integral to the decision-making process [8, 9]. Therefore, they are invariably exposed to tremendous amounts of mental strain both during and after ICU hospitalization [10]. It was not until after 1990 that it was demonstrated that family members of ICU patients could also experience a range of psychological issues. This was on account of one of the pioneering studies revealing that over 50% of family members of critically ill patients with traumatic brain injury (TBI) exhibited symptoms of anxiety, depression, hypochondriasis, and even suicidal tendencies. This was subsequently supported by studies that followed, which consistently demonstrated significant psychological distress among primary family caregivers of critically ill patient [11–16]. This is particularly more prevalent in low-resource setting countries such as Pakistan where financial restrictions, cultural anticipations, and lack of adequate psychological support put considerable mental strain on caregivers.

Various factors contribute to heightened stress levels among caregivers, including individual characteristics such as age, gender, educational background which often correlates to the level of understanding about patient’s condition, and the nature of their relationship with the patient. Patient-related factors, such as the type of ICU admission, also influence caregiver stress [16]. Lack of consistent communication and interaction with healthcare professionals has also been found to exacerbate stress among caregivers [17, 18]. Cultural prospects, being one of the cardinal features of Pakistan’s family dynamic system often put additional stress on caregivers. Expecting family members especially females to be present round the clock, financial responsibilities, large family involvement and having the burden to take primary decisions of the patient further intensifies existing problems. These challenges are particularly pronounced in public-sector tertiary care hospitals in Southeast Asia, where patient influx is high, and resources are extremely limited.

High levels of stress among primary caregivers can compromise the quality of care and emotional support they offer to their loved ones admitted into the ICU, which is essential for effective patient management during hospitalization [19].Chronic stress underlies a multitude of psychiatric and non-psychiatric health issues [20, 21], with studies highlighting increased rates of depression, anxiety and even post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) among caregivers with a prevalence of up to 16%, 42% and 35%, respectively [12, 22, 23]. Additionally, prolonged exposure to stress may also contribute to the development of cardiovascular diseases, hypertension, stroke, gastrointestinal disturbances, peptic ulcers, headaches, and musculoskeletal pain among caregivers [16]. This situation is compounded in low-resource countries like Pakistan, where families struggle to cope financially, healthcare systems are overburdened, and access to mental health support remains both stigmatized as well as scarce and is often viewed more as a luxury rather than a fundamental necessity [24].

As discussed above, it is well established that family caregivers play an integral role in supporting critically ill patients, however, there is a significant gap in research assessing the burden they bear, especially in high-stakes circumstances such as an ICU environment. Most existing literature originates from high-income countries, limiting our ability to extrapolate the findings to populations in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [25]. Our study highlights important aspects such as caregiver stress levels, gender differences, ICU type (medical/surgical), and mental health support. These factors could facilitate better healthcare policies in Pakistan in terms of ICU care and provide improved psychological support to caregivers.

By identifying the magnitude and correlates of perceived stress among primary family caregivers of patients admitted to medical and surgical ICUs at three largest tertiary care hospitals of Pakistan: Jinnah Postgraduate Medical Centre (JPMC), Civil Hospital Karachi (CHK), and Liaquat National Hospital (LNH) - this study aims to (1) quantify the level of perceived stress among primary family caregivers of patients admitted to medical and surgical ICUs, and (2) identify caregiver-, patient-, and hospital-related factors associated with moderate and high perceived stress. The findings of this study are intended to provide evidence that may inform the development of mental health interventions and subsequently designing structured programs for high-risk caregivers in ICU settings by healthcare policy makers.

Methods

Study design and setting

This investigation employed a multicenter, cross-sectional analytical design to quantify levels of perceived stress among family caregivers of critically ill patients and to examine caregiver, patient, and context-level correlates of stress. The methodological framework adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines for cross-sectional research.

The study was conducted across the intensive care units (ICUs) of three major tertiary-care hospitals in Karachi, Pakistan: Jinnah Postgraduate Medical Centre (JPMC), Liaquat National Hospital (LNH), Civil Hospital Karachi (CHK).

These institutions serve as high-volume referral centers providing medical and surgical critical care services. The ICUs operate under 24-hour admission cycles and maintain a continuous influx of emergency and postoperative cases, offering substantial heterogeneity in case-mix and caregiver demographics. Data collection occurred over a six-month period from February to July 2025.

Study population

The source population comprised family caregivers of patients admitted to medical or surgical ICUs. For the purposes of this study, a primary caregiver was defined as the individual aged 18 years or older, identified as a parent, spouse, sibling, any first-degree relative or a friend of an ICU patient, who was able to communicate in English, Urdu or Sindhi, was present at the hospital during data collection, and who provided informed verbal consent. Caregivers under 18 years were excluded, as they may not be able to provide reliable responses or fully comprehend the questionnaire. Additional exclusions included self-reported severe psychiatric illness, cognitive impairment, or inability to comprehend the questionnaire. Information regarding previously diagnosed psychiatric illness was obtained through self-reporting by the caregiver during the interview which was based on their prior medical diagnosis, and no formal psychiatric screening tool was administered.

Sampling method

A consecutive (convenience) sampling approach was used. At each hospital, trained data collectors approached all potential caregivers who were present in the ICU waiting area during data collection shifts. Eligibility was assessed, and all consenting individuals were enrolled until the required sample size was achieved. A total of 161 caregivers were included.

Caregivers were approached in the ICU waiting areas as this was the only feasible and standardized location where primary caregivers were consistently present and accessible during the patient’s ICU stay across all participating hospitals.

Sample size

Formal A Priori sample size or power calculation was not performed. The final sample size was determined by feasibility and the number of eligible primary caregivers available during the study period across the participating hospitals. All eligible caregivers approached during the data collection period were invited to participate until the end of the study period, resulting in a final sample size of 161 participants.

Data collection

A total of four trained data collectors (H.T., S.U.A.R., S.K., and S.A.A.S.) were involved in data collection across the three participating hospitals, with one to two data collectors assigned per site depending on shift availability. All received structured guidance on questionnaire administration, confidentiality, and managing distressed participants. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire administered verbally to caregivers. Interviewers were trained to read each question clearly in the participant’s preferred language (Urdu, Sindhi, or English), to provide neutral clarification when required, and to confirm participant understanding before recording responses. When caregivers appeared emotionally distressed, interviews were paused or discontinued at the participant’s request to ensure comfort and voluntary participation. To promote consistency across data collectors, all interviewers were provided with a standardized interview guide and were instructed to follow identical question wording and response options. No formal inter-rater reliability assessment was conducted. The study instruments are as follows:

2.Caregiving Experience Indicators:

Caregiving Experience Indicators:

Standardized Likert-scale items scored on a scale of 0–5 assessed caregivers’ quality of communication with healthcare staff, perceived involvement in decision-making, informational sufficiency, emotional burden, sleep disruption, and exposure to distressing ICU events (e.g., observing resuscitation of other patients).

For caregiving experience items, no formal pilot study was conducted; however, the questionnaire was informally pre-tested during the initial phase of data collection to ensure comprehensibility, and no major difficulties or misunderstandings were reported by participants.

3.Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10):

Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10):

Caregiver stress was measured using the 10-item Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) [5]. The validated Urdu version was used [28]. No further cultural adaptations were made, but the items were reviewed by the research team to ensure appropriateness and clarity for caregivers in the local context. Items are scored 0–3 on a Likert scale, yielding a total score of 0–40. Higher scores reflect greater stress. Consistent with established conventions: Low stress: 0–13. Moderate stress: 14–26. High stress: 27–40.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). For descriptive statistics, continuous variables were summarized as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) owing to non-normal distributions, and categorical variables as frequencies and percentages. Differences in caregiver stress across categorical variables were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Differences in continuous variables were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-parametric distributions. For all variables, missing data were minimal (< 5%) and analyses were conducted using available-case analysis. Percentages are reported based on non-missing data for each variable.

A multinomial logistic regression model was constructed to identify predictors of moderate and high stress (reference category = low stress). Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were computed. Multicollinearity among predictors was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), and all included variables had acceptable VIF values (GVIF^(1/(2*Df)) < 2), indicating no significant multicollinearity. Predictors with stable and interpretable estimates were included in the final multivariable model. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Jinnah Postgraduate Medical Centre (JPMC; Reference No. F.2–81/2025-GENL/189/JPMC) as the primary institution, and administrative ethical clearance was obtained from Liaquat National Hospital and Civil Hospital Karachi. All participants provided verbal informed consent, and confidentiality of data was strictly maintained.

Results

Characteristics of patients and primary family caregivers

Patient characteristics

A total of 161 patients were included, with a median age of 40 years (IQR: 26–54). Females constituted 55.3% (n = 89) of the sample, while males accounted for 44.7% (n = 72). Most patients resided in urban areas (78.3%, n = 126) and were married (78.3%, n = 126). Homemakers represented the largest occupational group (45.3%, n = 73), followed by employed individuals (29.2%, n = 47).

Regarding household responsibilities, 41.8% (n = 66) reported having no major responsibilities, whereas 20.9% (n = 33) were the primary breadwinners. Approximately half of the families reported a monthly household income of PKR 10,000–50,000 (USD 35–177) (48.7%, n = 77).

Clinical and admission characteristics

Most patients were admitted to medical ICUs (74.8%, n = 119). The largest proportion of admissions occurred at JPMC (42.9%, n = 69), followed by CHK (29.8%, n = 48) and LNH (27.3%, n = 44). The most frequent reasons for ICU admission were gastrointestinal/hepatobiliary conditions (20.1%, n = 32), obstetric/gynecologic conditions (13.2%, n = 21), and sepsis or infection (12.6%, n = 20).

The majority of patients had an illness duration of less than six months (73.1%, n = 117). More than half had no previous hospital admissions (54.7%, n = 88), and most had no prior ICU admission (79.1%, n = 125). Central venous pressure (CVP) lines were used in 46.6% (n = 68) of patients, and mechanical ventilation was required in 43.9% (n = 69). Most patients had not received blood transfusions in the past (46.0%, n = 74).

Primary family caregiver characteristics

The primary family caregivers had a median age of 35 years (IQR: 26–44). Most caregivers were male (64.0%, n = 103), resided in urban areas (82.0%, n = 132), and spoke Urdu (45.3%, n = 73).

In terms of relationship to the patient, caregivers were most commonly siblings (brothers: 16.1%, n = 26; sisters: 9.9%, n = 16), followed by sons (13.0%, n = 21) and husbands (9.3%, n = 15). The majority were married (78.3%, n = 126) and employed (59.0%, n = 95). At least one comorbidity was reported by 13.9% (n = 22) of caregivers, while two comorbidities were reported by 6.3% (n = 10).

Most caregivers reported having additional support during the ICU stay (77.6%, n = 125); however, the majority had no prior experience caring for ICU patients (81.4%, n = 131). Regarding duration of stay with the patient, 41.5% (n = 66) stayed for less than two days, and 29.6% (n = 47) stayed for three to four days. Caregivers who remained with the patient around the clock accounted for 78.1% (n = 125). The most commonly reported concern among caregivers was related to patient outcomes (80.1%, n = 129).

Regarding emotional burden and fatigue, more than half of caregivers reported never feeling burdened while caring for the patient (55.9%, n = 90), whereas 5.0% (n = 8) reported sometimes feeling burdened. Sleep disturbance and fatigue were common, with 34.6% (n = 55) reporting experiencing these very often and 10.1% (n = 16) reporting them sometimes. Witnessing the suffering of other ICU patients was distressing for many caregivers, with 50.0% (n = 80) reporting that this experience was often or very often upsetting.

With respect to communication and information exchange, 9.4% (n = 15) of caregivers reported very often feeling that communication between wards and specialties was poorly coordinated and wished it had been better. In addition, 40.4% (n = 65) reported sometimes to very often feeling that their knowledge of the patient’s condition was insufficient. Only 37.8% (n = 60) reported that the treating team often or very often communicated the evolving clinical situation, while 43.4% (n = 69) reported often or very often receiving clear explanations for medical decisions.

Regarding involvement in decision-making, 21.7% (n = 35) of caregivers reported being very often satisfied with their involvement, whereas 18.6% (n = 30) reported being rarely satisfied with the way the healthcare team involved them. Feeling pressured to consent to treatment was uncommon, with 49.7% (n = 79) reporting that they never felt pressured and 5.7% (n = 9) reporting that they sometimes felt pressured. With respect to communication of bad news, 28.3% (n = 45) of caregivers reported being rarely satisfied with how bad news was conveyed, whereas 20.1% (n = 32) reported being very often satisfied.

Regarding the ICU environment and visiting experience, dissatisfaction with the waiting area was common: 31.1% (n = 50) reported never being satisfied and 21.7% (n = 35) reported being rarely satisfied. Similarly, 49.1% (n = 63) reported being never or rarely satisfied with visits to the patient, whereas 21.7% (n = 35) and 18.0% (n = 29) reported being often and very often satisfied, respectively.

Finally, mental health support for caregivers was largely absent. More than half of caregivers (51.2%, n = 82) reported that their uncertainty and stress were never addressed by psychiatric social workers as part of routine care, and 65.4% (n = 104) reported that their mental health needs were never explored.

Differences by perceived stress level

Based on Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10) categorical classification, 65.8% (n = 106) of caregivers reported moderate perceived stress, 24.8% (n = 40) reported low perceived stress, and 9.3% (n = 15) reported high perceived stress (Table 4). Statistically significant associations were observed between perceived stress level and caregiver gender (p = 0.03), the presence of comorbidities (p = 0.02), and the number of comorbidities (p = 0.01) (Table 6).

Characteristics of primary family caregivers (N = 161)

Female caregivers were more likely to report higher perceived stress and constituted 60.0% of the high-stress group, compared with 40.0% male caregivers (p = 0.03). Caregivers with comorbid conditions were also more likely to report higher stress levels (40.0%, p = 0.02), and caregivers with two comorbidities reported higher perceived stress compared with those with a single comorbidity (p = 0.01).

No statistically significant associations were observed between perceived stress level and caregiver age, relationship to the patient, marital status, mother tongue, place of residence (urban vs. rural), occupation, availability of support, or prior ICU experience (all p > 0.05). Similarly, neither the number of days spent with the patient nor the duration of daily caregiving in the ICU was significantly associated with perceived stress level (all p > 0.05).

Patient-related clinical factors showed significant associations with caregiver perceived stress. The patient’s primary diagnosis was significantly associated with caregiver stress level (p < 0.001). A greater proportion of caregivers of patients admitted for trauma and respiratory conditions reported high perceived stress (21.4%), compared with those caring for patients with gastrointestinal/hepatobiliary and obstetric/gynecologic conditions (14.3%) each. Caregiver perceived stress also differed by hospital site. High stress was more frequently reported among caregivers of patients admitted to CHK (60%), whereas caregivers of patients admitted to LNH more commonly reported moderate stress (40.6%) (p < 0.001).

Caregivers reporting high (46.7%) or moderate (50.0%) perceived stress were more likely to be caring for patients without a history of blood transfusion (p = 0.02). In contrast, the absence of a previous hospital admission was more common among caregivers reporting low perceived stress (75.0%), while a history of prior admission was more frequently associated with moderate and high perceived stress levels (p = 0.02).

Type of ICU admission was also significantly associated with caregiver perceived stress (p < 0.001), with higher stress levels reported among caregivers of patients admitted to medical ICUs compared with surgical ICUs. These findings are summarized in Table 5.

Patient and admission-related predictors

Compared with caregivers of patients admitted for cardiovascular conditions, caregivers of patients admitted with sepsis or severe infection had significantly higher odds of reporting moderate perceived stress (OR = 28.50; 95% CI: 1.93–420.46; p = 0.01). Similarly, neurological diagnoses were associated with higher odds of moderate stress compared with low stress (OR = 16.50; 95% CI: 1.09–250.10; p = 0.04).

Hospital site was also a significant predictor. Compared with JPMC, caregivers of patients admitted to LNH had substantially higher odds of reporting moderate perceived stress (OR = 30.15; 95% CI: 3.91–232.63; p < 0.001).

Increasing patient age was significantly associated with higher odds of moderate perceived stress (OR = 1.03; 95% CI: 1.00–1.05; p = 0.03). Admission to a medical ICU, compared with a surgical ICU, was significantly associated with both moderate perceived stress (OR = 5.09; 95% CI: 2.27–11.41; p < 0.001) and high perceived stress (OR = 6.84; 95% CI: 1.36–34.43; p = 0.02).

A history of previous hospital admissions (< 2 admissions) was associated with higher odds of moderate perceived stress (OR = 2.65; 95% CI: 1.09–6.44; p = 0.03), whereas a history of previous ICU admissions (< 2 admissions) was associated with higher odds of high perceived stress (OR = 6.16; 95% CI: 1.25–30.31; p = 0.03).

Caregiver-related predictors

Among caregiver characteristics, the presence of comorbidities was significantly associated with higher perceived stress (OR = 8.00; 95% CI: 1.67–38.32; p = 0.01). Caregivers who did not remain with the patient around the clock were also more likely to report moderate perceived stress (OR = 3.27; 95% CI: 1.07–10.03; p = 0.04).

In contrast, caregivers who reported being primarily concerned about the length of hospital stay, rather than patient outcomes, were less likely to report moderate perceived stress (OR = 0.20; 95% CI: 0.04–0.89; p = 0.03).

Discussion

Caregiver stress in intensive care units (ICUs) is a well-recognized phenomenon worldwide, with studies showing high levels of psychological strain due to the unpredictable course of critical illnesses, patient acuity, and the demanding responsibilities placed on family caregivers [29, 30]. Multiple factors—including patient characteristics, ICU type, caregiver demographics, and availability of healthcare resources have been shown to influence stress levels. Despite these challenges, research examining ICU caregiver stress in these contexts remains severely limited.

In accordance with the existing literature, the caregivers included in our study sample experienced moderate to high levels of stress, reflecting the vigorous demands of caregiving in critical care environments [29, 30]. In low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) such as Pakistan, professional caregiving resources are often limited, and responsibility of caregiving often falls disparately on primary family caregivers, who are usually thrust into this role abruptly, and with little to no preparation or support [31, 32]. The transition from their everyday lives into the unfamiliar and unpredictable ICU setting with the expectation of taking serious yet pivotal decisions, sometimes with inadequate information may contribute to feelings of severe emotional distress [33–35].

Medical illnesses often follow a chronic and uncertain course, necessitating prolonged care and monitoring [36]. These factors may partly explain the increased psychological stress observed amongst caregivers in medical ICUs in our study. Medical patients often experience unpredictable complications, fluctuating conditions, and extended hospital stays, which may heighten caregiver anxiety and perceived responsibility. In contrast, surgical admissions may be associated with a shorter length of stay, with predictable treatment course and outcomes, which could explain the relatively lower stress experienced by caregivers in surgical ICUs [37].

Compared to JPMC, a public-sector hospital, admission to LNH, a private facility, was linked to greater odds of moderate stress. This may be partly explained by the greater financial burden in private hospitals, where treatment costs are borne entirely by the family. Financial strain can exacerbate stress as caregivers are forced to balance urgent healthcare decisions with limited resources. In addition, private hospital caregivers often receive more structured guidance and frequent updates from medical teams. While these interactions are intended to support patient care, they may paradoxically increase stress by heightening expectations for decision-making and close oversight of treatment [17, 18, 38, 39]. In contrast, public hospital caregivers may have less direct engagement with staff and receive fewer updates, which can also create uncertainty along with a myriad of other challenges. However, in public-sector settings, appropriate treatment is available free of cost or at subsidized rates, alleviating one worry amid the many added responsibilities shouldered by the primary family caregivers [39].

Medical conditions like sepsis or severe infections, and neurological conditions were significantly associated with moderate perceived stress. This can be explained by the severity, perceived permanence, and pervasive lay beliefs associated with the illness and its prognosis. Sepsis is a rapidly progressive condition with highly variable outcomes [40, 41], which may heighten caregivers’ anxiety, anticipatory grief, and feelings of helplessness, as they worry about survival and sudden deterioration. With high ICU mortality, sepsis necessitates prompt and intensive interventions [42, 43], which can further amplify caregiver stress due to the constant vigilance required and the emotional burden of making or supporting urgent decisions. Neurological conditions, on the other hand, can be severely debilitating and may result in permanent incapacity [44]. Caregivers may experience emotional exhaustion, fear of long-term dependency, sleep disturbances, and concern about the patient’s future quality of life, which can contribute to chronic psychological strain. Similar patterns have been reported in other LMICs, where caregivers of critically ill patients face elevated stress due to severe illnesses, limited hospital resources, financial pressures, and lack of structured psychosocial support [45, 46].

Increased psychological stress among caregivers of older patients may be explained by pre-existing comorbidities in this group, the supposed certainty of their decline due to poorer perceived recovery potential, or their diminished ability to care for themselves, which results in higher levels of dependency [47, 48]. Such factors may contribute to anticipatory grief among their caregivers as the act of providing care is often accompanied by an underlying awareness that, particularly in older patients, the trajectory of illness is likely to culminate in an unfavorable outcome [49, 50].

The higher stress levels among female caregivers may be a result of societal expectations that women should take on domestic duties in addition to offering emotional and physical support. At the same time, stress in male caregivers can be underreported due to cultural norms relating to masculinity. This can be attributed to the belief that expressing distress is a sign of weakness and frailty that may prevent male caregivers from publicly voicing their suffering or seeking help [51]. These findings align with studies from both LMICs and high-income countries, which consistently report higher psychological burden among female caregivers due to social roles and caregiving responsibilities, whereas male caregivers often underreport stress [52].

A history of previous hospitalizations, particularly ICU admissions, was associated with greater mental strain among caregivers. This may reflect perceptions of progressive or recurrent illness or concerns about treatment failure [53]. Moreover, given that prior hospitalizations are rarely inexpensive, subsequent admissions likely exacerbated their financial constraints as well. Caregivers for patients who had previously received transfusions were already familiarized with these interventions and might have had greater exposure to the healthcare system, which could have led to a sense of certainty and readiness, leading to lower odds of moderate stress.

Caregivers’ own health challenges may limit their physical and emotional availability for their loved ones, often evoking feelings of guilt, and further compounding their stress [54]. Our findings corroborated prior evidence that caregivers with comorbidities experienced greater stress due to the twofold burden these individuals face between managing their own health, and navigating the pressures of caring for a critically ill loved one [55].

The presence of caregivers around the clock also provided a greater sense of control and awareness despite the increased sleep deprivation, fatigue, and burnout that was seen in this group, as evidenced in our results. On the other hand, caregivers that did not stay with the patients throughout the day were more likely to experience moderate stress. This could be potentially traced back to helplessness and guilt that the caregivers might have felt in being away from the patient because constant bedside presence may be related with responsibility and devotion [56–58], thereby amplifying feelings of shame or inadequacy in those physically unable to remain in the ICU full-time.

Interestingly, caregivers who stayed for shorter periods (< 2 days) reported lower stress in our results. Although this trend was not statistically significant, it may reflect reduced caregiving responsibilities and exposure to the ICU environment, with limited disruption in their daily activities, which likely resulted in a lower emotional and physical toll and could contribute to lower perceived stress levels in this group.

It was also seen in our findings that the caregivers who directed their attention towards more practical elements such as the length of hospital stay, and other logistics had significantly lower odds of experiencing moderate stress. This could be connected to their focus on aspects they could actively manage, rather than factors like patient outcomes which were out of their hands. This likely offered a sense of control and security, thereby potentially alleviating further psychological distress.

Many of the caregivers felt misinformed or alienated by the caregiving medical teams, which is known to increase caregiver stress [17, 18]. This often occurs when communication regarding the patient’s condition, treatment plan, and prognosis is inadequate at the hands of physicians, leaving caregivers anxious, isolated, and gives rise to the sense of mistrust, particularly when excluded from decision-making or denied opportunities to express concerns [59]. Furthermore, despite obvious signs of emotional distress, mental health issues were rarely formally addressed. This disparity reflects the persistent undervaluation of family-centered care, which remains evident in many ICU settings [60, 61].

While our study identified several factors associated with higher perceived stress among caregivers, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to establish cause-and-effect relationships. Therefore, the observed associations should be interpreted as correlational rather than causal. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to better understand the temporal relationships and potential causative pathways between caregiver, patient, and ICU-related factors and stress levels.

In integrating our findings, we observed that caregiver stress is a complex and multifactorial issue that is influenced by both patient and caregiver characteristics. These associations can help direct our attention toward areas where targeted support interventions could be preferentially introduced, and be most effective, offering consideration and assistance to patients as well as their caregivers.

Public health and policy implications

At a policy level, caregiver mental health should be formally integrated into national critical care and hospital service delivery frameworks. Public and private hospitals should be mandated to implement routine psychological screening of primary caregivers at ICU admission and establish clear referral pathways to in-hospital and community-based mental health services. National and provincial health authorities should develop standardized and culturally appropriate caregiver education as well as communication protocols to reduce uncertainty and emotional distress. Workforce policies should support the placement of trained counsellors, social workers, or liaison nurses within ICU teams to provide structured psychosocial support. Strengthening linkages between tertiary hospitals and primary and community mental health services would further ensure continuity of care for caregivers after patient discharge, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, we employed a convenience sampling approach, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, the study was conducted in the ICUs of three tertiary-care hospitals in Karachi, which may not represent the experiences of caregivers in other regions or smaller healthcare settings. Nevertheless, these hospitals serve a large and diverse patient population, including individuals from both urban Karachi and surrounding rural areas, which may partially enhance the generalizability of our findings. Recruiting caregivers from ICU waiting areas may also have introduced selection bias, as those present may differ in stress levels or caregiving roles from caregivers not available during data collection.

Second, the use of self-reported measures, including the Perceived Stress Scale (PSS-10), may introduce response bias. The caregiving experience indicators were not formally piloted or psychometrically validated in the local context, and inter-rater reliability between data collectors was not assessed, which may have introduced minor variability in questionnaire administration. The identification of severe psychiatric illness relied on caregiver self-report and may be affected by underreporting due to stigma or limited awareness, as no formal psychiatric screening tool was employed. Verbal administration of questionnaires may have further introduced interviewer or social desirability bias, particularly among emotionally distressed caregivers.

Third, several categorical variables included in the study (e.g., frequency of hospital admissions, duration of illness, history of ICU admission, and socioeconomic indicators) were not derived from previously validated instruments. These categories were developed based on clinical experience, routine ICU practice, and contextual realities of the local healthcare system in Pakistan. Broad categories were intentionally used to enhance participant comprehension, minimize respondent burden, and reduce potential non-response bias for sensitive variables. While this approach was necessary given the exploratory nature of the study and resource-limited setting, it may have limited the precision of analyses and should be considered when interpreting associations. Similarly, while marital status was recorded, the category of ‘divorced’ was not included due to sociocultural sensitivities in Pakistan, to protect participant comfort and reduce potential non-response bias. This may have limited insights into the analysis of marital status as a predictor of caregiver stress. Future research should aim to validate these classifications and develop standardized tools tailored to similar healthcare contexts.

Fourth, the cross-sectional design does not allow causal inferences between caregiver characteristics, patient factors, and perceived stress. While the study recorded the presence and number of caregiver comorbidities, it did not differentiate between physical and mental health conditions. Future research could explore whether specific types of comorbidities more strongly predict perceived stress, providing more actionable insights for targeted interventions. In addition, the relatively small number of caregivers in the high stress category limited the stability and precision of the multinomial regression estimates, as reflected by wide confidence intervals for several predictors. These findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory.

Despite these limitations, the study provides important insights into caregiver stress in resource-limited ICU settings in Pakistan and highlights areas for targeted support and policy-level interventions.

Conclusion

Although the health of patients admitted to medical and surgical ICUs typically receives utmost priority, our findings show that primary caregivers experience substantial stress, which remains a neglected yet critical component of comprehensive patient care. Caregiver stress was found to be a multifactorial issue influenced by patient-related factors, including the type of ICU (medical versus surgical) and the severity and nature of the patient’s diagnosis, as well as caregiver-related factors such as gender and the caregiver’s own health status. In addition, healthcare system–related factors, including hospital setting (private versus public), financial strain, and gaps in communication from healthcare providers, were also significantly associated with caregiver stress. This study highlights the need to address the psychological well-being of caregivers alongside patient care. Hospitals should encourage effective and structured communication, provide financial counselling, and offer targeted support groups for caregivers. Such measures may improve caregiver well-being and contribute to more holistic and effective patient care, ultimately supporting better long-term patient outcomes.
