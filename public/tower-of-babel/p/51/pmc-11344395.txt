Comparison of clinical efficacy between autologous partially demineralized dentin matrix and deproteinized bovine bone mineral for bone augmentation in orthodontic patients with alveolar bone deficiency: a randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: Leping Ouyang, Jingwei Li, Yuhang Dong, Jingyu Li, Feifan Jin, Ying Luo, Renfei Wang, Shuhua Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt is common to see patients who need orthodontic treatment but with insufficient alveolar bone volume. However, safe and effective tooth movement requires sufficient alveolar bone width and height. The aim of this study is to compare the bone augmentation efficacy of Autologous Partially Demineralized Dentin Matrix (APDDM) and Deproteinized Bovine Bone Mineral (DBBM) in orthodontic patients with insufficient bone by using a randomized controlled clinical trial approach.Materials and methodsTwenty-seven orthodontic patients involving 40 posterior teeth alveolar sites (n = 40) with insufficient alveolar bone volume were randomly divided into a control group (n = 20) and an experimental group (n = 20). The patients in the experimental group were treated with APDDM, and those in the control group were treated with DBBM. After surgery, the adjacent teeth are moved toward the bone grafting sites according to the orthodontic treatment plan. Patients completed a postoperative response questionnaire by the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) score to indicate pain and swelling in the bone grafted area at the time of suture removal; and CBCT scans were conducted before surgery, 6 months and 2 years after surgery to assess changes in buccal and central alveolar heights, as well as widths at the alveolar ridge apex and 3 mm, 5 mm below the apex, respectively. The CBCT image sequences were imported into Mimics 21.0 software in DICOM format. The data of the patients in both groups were collected and analyzed by SPSS 25.0.ResultsThe VAS scores were significantly lower in the APDDM group than in the DBBM group (p < 0.05). Significant increases were observed in alveolar bone height and width at 6 months and 2 years postoperative (p < 0.05); At 2 years, the APDDM group exhibited a reduction in buccal crest height and in 3 mm, 5 mm width below alveolar ridge apex, relative to 6 months (p < 0.05), while the DBBM group showed a decrease only in the central height of the alveolar bone (p < 0.05). There was a significant bone augmentation increase found only 3 mm below the alveolar ridge apex in the APDDM group compared with the DBBM group among all 6 months group comparison (p < 0.05). At 2 years, the augmentation effects were similar across both groups (p > 0.05).ConclusionRadiomics analysis indicates that APDDM serves as a viable bone augmentation material for orthodontic patients with insufficient alveolar bone volume, achieving comparable clinical efficacy to DBBM. Additionally, APDDM is associated with a milder postoperative response than DBBM.The registration number (TRN)ChiCTR2400084607.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-04730-8.

Introduction

Clinically, sufficient alveolar bone volume is essential for successful tooth movement in orthodontic treatment [1]. Research has demonstrated that orthodontic tooth movement into areas of alveolar bone deficiency may lead to various adverse effects, including periodontal tissue damage, bone fenestration, dehiscence, reduced speed of tooth movement, root resorption, and even tooth loss [2]. Fenestration and dehiscence have been reported in up to 94% of patients with bimaxillary protrusion not undergoing orthodontic treatment [3], and in up to 61.6% of patients with posterior crossbite [4]. Alveolar bone atrophy or loss can result from periodontal disease, trauma, infection of extraction sockets, or post-extraction resorption [5]. Thus, to improve the safety, effectiveness, and stability of tooth movement during orthodontic treatment, pre-movement reconstruction of the alveolar ridge with insufficient height and width is crucial [6].

Methods to address bone deficiency encompass surgical techniques and bone grafting materials. Effective bone augmentation strategies for orthodontic patients include Periodontally Accelerated Osteogenic Orthodontics (PAOO), Guided Bone Regeneration (GBR), and site preservation [7]. The invasive nature of PAOO may lead to postoperative issues such as gingival recession, necrosis, or exposure of the bone graft material. The incidence of wound dehiscence after GBR, which can result in infection and graft loss, is documented to be 9.9% [8]. In contrast, site preservation commonly results in postoperative swelling and infection [9], potentially decelerating orthodontic tooth movement [10]. Ideal bone grafting materials should exhibit osteoinductivity, and osteoconductivity, be immune rejection-free, possess controlled biodegradation, and have adequate mechanical strength to ensure stability in the implant area [11]. Despite their utilization, current bone grafting materials present limitations. Autogenous bone grafts face challenges such as inevitable resorption, limited tissue availability, and the necessity for a second surgery, impacting their therapeutic efficacy [12–14]. Deproteinized Bovine Bone Mineral (DBBM) is recognized for its high biocompatibility and osteoconductivity but suffers from poor biodegradability and potential disease transmission risks [15–17]. Synthetic materials, while cost-effective and devoid of biological risk, lack osteoinductive and osteogenic capabilities, restricting their application to forming functional bone [5, 18, 19]. Given the limitations of existing bone grafting materials, exploring Autologous Partially Demineralized Dentin Matrix (APDDM) as an innovative alternative remains a focal point of ongoing research.

APDDM is derived from teeth unsuitable for retention, such as those affected by periodontal disease, orthodontic removals, and third molars [20]. It is characterized as an acid-insoluble, bio-absorbable, and osteoinductive matrix conducive to bone regeneration [21]. Previous research has demonstrated that APDDM not only contains a mineral phase but also includes a collagen matrix and osteoinductive growth factors, rendering it an optimal bone substitute [22]. Its composition mirrors that of bone tissue, comprising 18% collagen, 2% non-collagen proteins, 70% hydroxyapatite, and 10% body fluids [23–25]. Significantly, Type I collagen serves as a scaffold for mineral deposition [26, 27], supports cell adhesion, and facilitates differentiation [28]. Bone Morphogenetic Protein 2 (BMP-2) exerts influential effects on undifferentiated mesenchymal and osteoblastic cells, promoting effective osteogenesis. Recent case reports have highlighted the successful application of APDDM in various bone augmentation procedures, demonstrating notable clinical efficacy [29–32]. This study aims to minimize trauma from bone grafting in orthodontic patients to mitigate the impact on orthodontic treatment, eschewing invasive procedures in later stages. It emphasizes imaging techniques to assess new bone formation and the influence of orthodontic tooth movement in the grafted area. CBCT, superior to traditional two-dimensional imaging, significantly enhances diagnostic efficiency and accuracy. Within oral implantology, CBCT offers precise three-dimensional representations of jawbone quality and volume, alongside mandibular neural canal and maxillary sinus tissue structures [33]. Given the paucity of research on APDDM in orthodontics, this study hypothesizes that APDDM can more effectively address the challenge of inadequate buccal ridge bone in orthodontic patients, as evidenced by follow-up clinical case observations.

Materials and methods

Ethics and sample selection

Twenty-seven orthodontic patients with insufficient alveolar bone volume were selected from July 2020 to December 2022 at the Stomatology Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medicine University and Hangzhou Joying Clinic Dental, This study, conducted by the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Stomatology Hospital of Zhejiang Chinese Medicine University (File Number: ZCMUHSIRB-2024040905), and was registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2400084607). Written informed consent was obtained from each subject before participation in the study. This experiment followed The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) reporting guidelines. All participants met the established criteria.

Among the cases who visited the hospital, a total of 44 orthodontic patients with 64 alveolar sites were qualified to be included in this study. Only 27 patients were operated in the study and 40 alveolar sites with insufficient bone were randomly allocated to two groups. The experimental group includes 20 alveolar sites treated with APDDM, while the controlled group includes the remaining alveolar sites treated with DBBM (Bio-Oss, large particles, 1–2 mm particle size) (Geistlich AG, Wolhusen, Switzerland). After surgery, the same professional orthodontist applies precisely controlled orthodontic forces and directions according to the orthodontic treatment plan to move the adjacent teeth toward the bone grafting sites.

Sample size determination and randomization

The sample size was calculated with G*Power v.3.1.3 (Heinrich-Heine Universität, Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on the mean and standard deviation (SD) of vertical bone changes reported in previous studies − 2.0 ± 2.4 mm for the β-TCP group and 1.2 ± 2.9 mm for the DBBM-C/PG group [34]. An alpha value of 0.05 and a statistical power of 90% were established. Based on these parameters, the sample size was estimated to be 18 sites per group. To account for potential dropouts, the sample size was adjusted to 20 sites per group.

The DBBM group and the APDDM group were written on sheets of paper and enclosed in opaque, sealed envelopes. The envelopes were randomly and equally distributed to patients, bone graft material was then placed at the affected site by a single highly qualified surgeon based on the contents of the envelope. The randomization process and participants’ assignment to interventions were achieved by an independent investigator. It should be noted that blinding was not implemented for the operator or the patient in this experiment.

Preparation of the APDDM

Teeth selected for extraction are based on the orthodontic treatment plan. Surface tartar, enamel, and soft tissues, including periodontal fibers and pulp, are removed using a high-speed turbine. If caries were found, all decayed tissue was removed before the healthy dentin was treated and dried with an air gun. The BonMaker® Auto-Tooth bone graft system (BMK-AUTO-02) is used to grind the tooth and obtain autogenous dentin particles for treatment. Three types of sterilizing solutions are introduced into the device’s reagent tank respectively, resulting in the production of APDDM. Approximately 1 ml of blood is drawn from the patient’s alveolar ridge and combined with the APDDM (Fig. 1A-D).

Surgical method

All patients received prophylactic antibiotic therapy (i.e., 250 mg cefuroxime or 250 mg azithromycin if allergic to penicillin)30 min before tooth extraction. An analgesic (300 mg of Saridon) was administered as needed. Before surgery, patients were advised to rinse their mouths with a 0.2% chlorhexidine solution for 1 min. A single highly qualified surgeon performed all surgical procedures. Local anesthesia was administered using Articaine (Ultracain DS 1:200,000; Sanofi Aventis, Frankfurt, Germany). The orthodontic teeth were removed minimally invasively before the chairside preparation of APDDM. During surgery, after making an incision and lifting the flap in the area of bone deficiency, several trophoblastic holes were created in the lateral labial bone plate. Subsequently, the area was augmented with APDDM (particle size: 250 to 1000 μm in diameter). A collagen membrane (Bio-Gide®) was cut to fit and positioned 2.0 to 3.0 mm below the gingival margin, extending from the edge of the bone graft particles, and secured with staples on the buccal side and sutures on the lingual side. After relaxing the soft tissue flap, the wound was closed with interrupted sutures and the sutures were removed two weeks postoperative (Fig. 2A-F). After surgery, all patients were prescribed oral antibiotics three times daily for three days and received injections as required for managing discomfort and inflammation. Throughout the study, patients received guidance on oral hygiene and care for periodontal health as needed.

Observation indicator

Clinical observation

Patient satisfaction was assessed through a questionnaire utilizing the Visual Analog Scales (VAS, 0–10), which were regarded as secondary outcomes, concentrating on pain, swelling, and surgical incision healing. All patients completed the questionnaire upon suture removal. The questionnaire is presented in the supplementary material.

Radiographic assessment

Cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans (ORTHOPANTOMOGRAPH OP 3D, Prokavo, Tuusula, Finland) were conducted before surgery, 6 months postoperative and 2 years postoperative. Three sets of DICOM data were generated and imported into volume-imaging software (Mimics 21.0, Materialise) for three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction and image analysis. The preoperative 3D model was aligned with the 6-month and 2-year postoperative 3D models using the STL Registration feature of Mimics software, enabling the creation of a composite 3D image from two CT scans. If necessary, the measurement area was manually calibrated to enhance measurement accuracy (Fig. 3A-C). The apices of the alveolar ridge of the teeth adjacent to the missing tooth area were connected, their midpoints identified, and the coronal surfaces were selected. Measurements were then conducted using consistent reference points and lines within the bone tissue viewport interface.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS 25.0 statistical software, with results presented as mean ± standard deviation (Mean ± SD, mm). Vertical and horizontal bone gains were evaluated across various follow-up intervals and bone grafting materials. For data adhering to a normal distribution, a paired t-test was employed within groups, while an independent samples t-test was utilized for intergroup comparisons. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using The Wilcoxon signed-rank test within groups and the Mann-Whitney U test between groups to assess differences. A p-value of < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Similarly, alveolar width enhancements were noted by 3.61 ± 2.06 mm at the ridge apex, 3.18 ± 1.94 mm at 3 mm below the alveolar ridge apex, and 2.34 ± 1.92 mm at 5 mm below the alveolar ridge apex. Width increases were observed by 2.64 ± 2.04 mm at the ridge apex, 2.47 ± 1.28 mm at 3 mm below the alveolar ridge apex, and 1.93 ± 1.33 mm at 5 mm below the alveolar ridge apex. No statistical difference was observed between the groups (p > 0.05) (Fig. 12).

Discussion

In this study, we observed optimistic new bone formation via imaging at 6 months and 2 years postoperative, confirming the efficacy of APDDM and DBBM in orthodontic patients with insufficient bone volume. The bone volume at BH, CH, HW0, HW3, and HW5 were significantly greater than preoperative alveolar bone volume at both time points. Previous studies have corroborated the bone augmentation effects of DBBM, aligning with the results of this experiment [35, 36]. APDDM’s physicochemical properties resemble those of autologous bone, rendering it a superior graft material with osteoinductive, osteoconductive, and osteogenic properties [37]. The inorganic components of dentin, predominantly hydroxyapatite, are instrumental in sustaining graft volume and facilitating osteoconduction [38]. The organic matrix, comprising type I collagen fibers and other non-collagenous proteins, such as bone morphogenetic proteins, osteocalcin, and vascular endothelial growth factor, which plays an osteoinductive role in promoting new bone formation [11, 37]. Furthermore, type I collagen fibers serve as a scaffold for bone production and cell adhesion. Dentin also harbors an array of growth factors, including transforming growth factor (TGF-β), fibroblast growth factor, and insulin-like growth factor (IGF-1), which are integral to inducing new bone formation and advancing bone remodeling [25].

Additionally, we observed a general decline in alveolar bone volume at 2 years postoperative compared to 6 months postoperative. Nonetheless, there was a significant increase in the height and width of alveolar bone relative to the preoperative baseline. Furthermore, the height of the buccal crest and width at 3 mm and 5 mm below the alveolar ridge apex were notably reduced in the APDDM group, whereas the central height decreased significantly in the DBBM group, which was a statistically significant difference. This is likely attributable to orthodontic tooth movement and the influence of APDDM on neoplastic alveolar bone remodeling. Previous studies found that the pressure exerted during orthodontic mobilization may cause the bone structure in the grafted region to become unstable, thereby accelerating bone resorption [39]. KIM et al. [40, 41] demonstrated that tissue resorption and remodeling occur 3–4 months following autogenous tooth bone grafts. Histological samples indicated that the majority of the graft material had been resorbed, with new bone formation around it. Six months subsequent to grafting, the volume of new bone increased, and trabecular connections with the adjacent bone developed, resulting in a more stable structure. Orthodontic tooth movement is typically accompanied by alveolar bone remodeling, characterized by resorption on the pressure side and augmentation on the tension side. Xu et al. [42] observed that during orthodontic tooth movement, alterations in the alveolar bone were primarily due to resorption, with limited augmentation. Concurrently, inadequate oral hygiene during orthodontic treatment has been associated with the development of subgingival bone pockets and attachment loss, potentially exacerbating alveolar bone resorption. In the APDDM group, there was a greater reduction in bone dimensions compared to the DBBM group, which may be attributed to the demineralization process enhancing the release of growth factors and reducing material crystallinity, thus aiding biodegradation [43]. In contrast, Bio-Oss bone powder is characterized by a prolonged degradation cycle [44]. Based on previous research, the implanted Bio-Oss did not degrade even 11 years after maxillary sinus lift surgery [45]. The autogenous dental material used in this study underwent partial demineralization, a process recognized for increasing osteogenic potential without altering the material’s structure or its intrinsic growth factors [43]. Consequently, this material’s biodegradation and new bone formation rates were superior to those of Bio-Oss. However, the slower degradation rate of Bio-Oss contributes to its effectiveness in maintaining the space and stability of the grafted site in comparison with APDDM. Additionally, the degree of hydroxyapatite (HA) crystallization and its quantity in APDDM differ substantially between different parts of the tooth. The crown has a higher content of HA with superior crystalline properties, whereas the root contains a lower concentration of HA and more organic material, which may explain the increased resorption observed with APDDM [25].

However, we observed that at 6 months and 2 years postoperative, the BH, CH, HW0, HW3, and HW5 increased more in the APDDM group than in the DBBM group. Notably, the expansion in width at 3 mm below the alveolar ridge apex was greater in the APDDM group at 6 months postoperative, compared to preoperative, with the difference being statistically significant. These outcomes are consistent with those reported by Jun et al. [46], who observed that autogenous dentin particles yielded a higher trabecular and bone-like tissue thickness compared to Bio-Oss in sinus implant procedures. In a related prospective study, Kim ES et al. [47] utilized autogenous demineralized tooth blocks for maxillary sinus elevations with concurrent implant placements and reported superior osteogenesis relative to Bio-Oss. However, the augmentation observed in width at 3 mm below the alveolar ridge apex at 6 months postoperative may not solely reflect alveolar bone deposition but could also be related to delayed alveolar bone remodeling in this area following tooth movement.

In this study, we documented and compared the postoperative response, including pain, swelling, and wound healing, between the APDDM and DBBM groups. The APDDM group exhibited more favorable outcomes, which might be attributable to the potential of APDDM to elicit a reduced inflammatory response. In contrast, xenogeneic bone is recognized by the body as foreign when grafted into bone defects, potentially initiating an immune and inflammatory reaction [48, 49]. After bone graft material implantation in defect sites, a temporary increase in volume occurs, driven by the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-1 (IL-1) from immune cells [50]. Additionally, since both teeth and alveolar bone originate from embryonic neural crest cells [51], bone reconstruction using APDDM may result in minor postoperative reactions and fewer complications. The psychological benefit of patients perceiving the autogenous dental implant materials as a part of themselves could also positively influence postoperative outcomes.

Compared to the study by Shuai Liu et al. [32], more accurate and reliable conclusions may be obtained owing to a larger sample size in the APDDM group. Furthermore, by comparing the bone augmentation capabilities of APDDM with DBBM — currently the most extensively utilized material in clinical practice — the bone augmentation efficacy of APDDM can be assessed. However, this study presents certain limitations when compared with previous studies, for example, the absence of histo-cytological analysis of new bone formed precludes the verification of its association between APDDM and new bone from a histological perspective. The osteogenic effect evaluation in this study was solely based on comparative imaging measurements at the grafted site at distinct time points. Moreover, additional animal studies are imperative to further explicate the interplay between APDDM alterations and root changes during tooth movement.

Conclusion

The analysis of imaging data 6 months and 2 years postoperative suggests that APDDM is a safe and effective novel bone grafting material, achieving clinical bone augmentation outcomes comparable to those with DBBM in orthodontic patients requiring bone augmentation.
