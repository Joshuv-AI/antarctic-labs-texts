Extracellular vesicle R-Ras is a potential biomarker for human peripheral artery disease
Authors: Xiaochao Wei, Mohamed Zaghloul, Shahab Hafezi, Fumihiko Urano, Mohamed A. Zayed, Clay F. Semenkovich
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryHuman peripheral artery disease (PAD) is common and associated with amputation, heart attack, stroke, and death. Treatment options are limited by inadequate understanding of disease pathophysiology and lack of early detection strategies. R-Ras, which regulates vascular integrity, undergoes reversible palmitoylation. In mice, disrupting this process by inhibiting the enzyme acyl-protein thioesterase 1 (APT1) impairs vascular function and promotes experimental PAD. In arteries from humans with PAD we found increased palmitoylated R-Ras, which correlated with age and hypertension. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) isolated from APT1-knockdown endothelial cells were enriched in R-Ras protein, suggesting that palmitoylation promotes incorporation of R-Ras into EVs. PAD patients compared to subjects without PAD had increased serum EV R-Ras content that was positively associated with age, smoking, hypertension, and PAD severity. These findings suggest that altered R-Ras palmitoylation impacts human PAD and that EV-associated R-Ras may be an accessible biomarker for human PAD.

Introduction

Peripheral artery disease (PAD) affects more than 200 million people globally and restricts blood flow leading to limited mobility, chronic limb-threatening ischemia (CLTI), and major adverse limb events such as amputation.1,2,3 PAD incidence is rising with aging populations and the increasing prevalence of metabolic risk factors such as diabetes and associated risk factors. About 10%–15% of people with PAD progress to CLTI, leading to amputation in as many as 30% within two years without revascularization.3,4 Moreover, PAD carries considerable risk of major adverse cardiovascular events in addition to limb loss. Mortality in CLTI is alarmingly high with survival rates worse than many cancers. The growing burden of PAD strains healthcare systems and particularly affects those from lower socio-economic backgrounds.1,2,3,4

While PAD shares some features with obstructive vascular diseases of the heart and brain, it is underdiagnosed due to limited awareness, asymptomatic presentation, and the lack of suitable screening approaches. Diagnosis is often delayed until disease is advanced and difficult to treat. Despite interventions to promote exercise, stop smoking, decrease thrombosis, treat hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes, and achieve revascularization,1,3,5 PAD remains a catastrophic form of atherosclerosis.6

Conceptually, regenerative vascular therapy represents a promising option by promoting the growth of new blood vessels. However, mixed outcomes of preclinical angiogenic approaches highlight the need for an improved understanding of vascular biology to improve therapies for acute limb ischemia and CLTI. Recently, the Glucagon-Like Peptide (GLP)-1 receptor agonist semaglutide was reported to improve walking function in people with type 2 diabetes and PAD,7 an outcome that appeared to be clinically meaningful. If GLP-1 agonists represent an effective therapy for PAD, the availability of biomarkers could facilitate the identification of asymptomatic disease allowing intervention at early stages of vascular compromise.

Complex interactions between environmental, genetic, and post-translational modifications may contribute to the heterogeneous pathogenesis of PAD. One such modification is protein S-palmitoylation (or S-acylation), a reversible post-translational lipid modification of the thioester bond at cysteine residues important in metabolism and signaling.7 R-Ras, a small GTPase modified by palmitoylation, regulates vascular growth and maintains blood vessel integrity.8 Genetic variants at the R-Ras locus in humans are associated with hypertension and kidney disease,9,10 frequent PAD comorbidities. Genetic ablation of R-Ras in mice impairs reparative perfusion in ischemic hindlimbs.11 Palmitoylation is carried out by a large family of DHHC acyl transferases and its reversal (required for trafficking of proteins to and from membranes to transmit metabolic signals) is mediated in part by acyl-protein thioesterase 1 (APT1), which removes palmitate from proteins. APT1-dependent depalmitoylation of R-Ras is required for vessel maturation and ischemia-induced vascular remodeling in mice.12 We studied tissues and serum from humans with different degrees of ischemia to test the hypothesis that increased vascular R-Ras palmitoylation, an indicator of compromised depalmitoylation cycling, could reflect PAD severity.

Arterial segments with minimal or absent disease from PAD patients and visually normal arterial segments from controls were processed for protein isolation and an acyl-RAC procedure13 to isolate palmitoylated proteins. Semi-quantitative Western blotting is used to detect R-Ras protein in total isolates and palmitoylated fractions (representative blots in Figure S1). Age and hypertension in PAD are associated with R-Ras palmitoylation

To pursue potential relationships between R-Ras palmitoylation and vascular risk factors, nonparametric Spearman correlations were generated within the PAD cohort. Correlation coefficient r values are presented in Figure S2 and P values are shown as a heatmap in Figure 1E. Age, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and coronary artery disease were associated with palmitoylated R-Ras. Simple linear regression analysis between age and R-Ras palmitoylation demonstrated significance of this association in PAD patients (Pearson r = 0.35, p = 0.0469, Figure 1F). This correlation was also significant when donors without PAD were included (Pearson r = 0.61, p < 0.0001, Figure 1G). Palmitoylated R-Ras in vascular tissue was 1.7-fold greater in PAD patients with hypertension compared to those without hypertension (p = 0.0005, Figure 1H). Palmitoylated R-Ras was numerically but not statistically greater in patients with PAD based on presence of diabetes (Figure 1I) or coronary artery disease (Figure 1J).

R-Ras EV association is increased in human endothelial cells deficient in APT1

EVs, a spectrum of lipid bilayer particles found in the circulation, may reflect disease.14,15 Since R-Ras is palmitoylated and found in EVs,16,17 greater intracellular palmitoylation of R-Ras would be expected to increase its membrane affinity and promote its presence in EVs. To test this possibility, we knocked down the depalmitoylase enzyme APT-1 using shRNA in cultured human adipose microvascular cells (HAMECs) and isolated EVs from culture media (Figure 2A) using a column-based method.18 APT1 knockdown in HAMECs was validated at the protein level by Western blotting (Figure 2B). EVs from APT1 deficient endothelial cells had increased R-Ras content compared to control cells (treated with scrambled shRNA) as determined by Western blotting (Figure 2C) and ELISA (Figure 2D). To validate this finding with an independent approach, EVs were isolated using serum from 15 TOS patients without PAD (12 new subjects not represented in Figure 3B), 15 patients with PAD and CLTI, and 15 patients with PAD without CLTI (Table 3) and assayed for R-Ras by ELISA. There was no difference in total serum R-Ras protein content between patients with and without PAD (Figure 3C). Compared to patients without PAD, EV-associated R-Ras was increased in patients with PAD without CLTI (p = 0.0038) and in patients with PAD with CLTI (p < 0.0001) (Figure 3D). To explore potential relationships between EV-associated R-Ras and clinical characteristics, nonparametric Spearman correlations were generated within the PAD cohort. Correlation coefficient r values are presented in Figure S3 and p values are shown as a heatmap in Figure 3E. Age, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, insulin use, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, statin use, and PAD extent by Rutherford classification were associated with EV-associated R-Ras.

Age and EV-associated R-Ras were associated in all 45 patients with and without PAD (Pearson R = 0.30 and p = 0.046, Figure 3F). There was no significant correlation between total R-Ras and EV-associated R-Ras in all 45 patients with and without PAD (Pearson R = 0.19 and p = 0.21, Figure S4). For all 45 patients with and without PAD, EV-associated R-Ras was significantly increased based on smoking status (p < 0.0001, Figure 3G) and hypertension (p < 0.0001, Figure 3H). Among PAD patients, there were no differences in total serum R-Ras based on PAD extent (Figure 3I), but those in Rutherford category 3 (p = 0.0416) and 5 (p = 0.0133, adjusted each p value by ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test) had significantly higher levels of EV-associated R-Ras as compared to Rutherford category 0 (Figure 3J).

Discussion

R-Ras is a member of the Ras superfamily, monomeric G proteins critical for cell signaling. The Ras family (one of several members of the Ras superfamily) includes H-Ras, K-Ras, and N-Ras, each involved in cell proliferation and oncogenesis. The physiologic role of R-Ras is less well understood. Abundantly expressed in the endothelium, R-Ras participates in vascular matrix remodeling, intercellular junction organization, as well as mural cell recruitment, and dysregulated R-Ras signaling has been implicated in vascular disorders.8,11,21,22 In preclinical studies,12 we identified a mechanistic link between abnormal vascular remodeling and increased R-Ras palmitoylation caused by deficiency of APT1, a major depalmitoylase. R-Ras is palmitoylated, APT1 enzyme activity is decreased in the setting of hyperglycemia, and APT1 removes palmitate from R-Ras to sustain normal trafficking and signaling in endothelial cells, suggesting a role for palmitoylation cycling in vascular biology. Studying APT1 directly in humans is technically difficult because its enzymatic activity cannot be reliably measured in frozen tissues. Moreover, studies in model systems indicate that APT1 mRNA expression is unchanged under disease conditions, implicating post-translational mechanisms.12 In contrast, R-Ras protein and R-Ras palmitoylation status, directly influenced by APT1 activity, can be examined in human specimens. Importantly, R-Ras palmitoylation recycling has not yet been examined in human PAD.

In the current study, vascular tissue R-Ras palmitoylation is shown to be increased in patients undergoing amputation for extensive PAD as compared to donor control subjects. R-Ras palmitoylation was the hightest in patients with diabetes. Age and hypertension, two risk factors for PAD, were correlated with increased vascular R-Ras palmitoylation in human PAD. Deficiency of APT1 in cultured human endothelial cells promoted the release of EV-associated R-Ras. R-Ras protein was more readily detected in human EVs from patients with PAD compared to patients without PAD. This finding was supported by independent cohort studies in which PAD patients were compared with two distinct groups of non-PAD controls. One group consisted of patients with TOS, characterized by compression of neurovascular structures often triggered by repetitive overhead limb movement. TOS differs substantially from PAD in terms of anatomy, demographics, and risk factors. In quantitative assays, EV-associated R-Ras, but not total serum R-Ras, was correlated with age, smoking, hypertension, and PAD severity. There was a large spread of values in PAD patients. While the lack of significance between Rutherford category 0 and 4 in Figure 3 is likely due to small sample size, assay variance suggests that EV-associated R-Ras may not always be indicative of PAD status. However, EV-associated R-Ras was also significantly higher in a different group of PAD patients compared with a second control group of healthy subjects without TOS. One interpretation of our findings is that aberrant R-Ras palmitoylation cycling may participate in the progression of human PAD. The results also suggest that a high throughput diagnostic assay for EV-associated R-Ras could be useful for early detection of PAD in humans. While the scale and design of our study was limited to PAD, it is possible that R-Ras palmitoylation contributes to vascular abnormalities beyond PAD. Impaired R-Ras palmitoylation turnover appears to disrupt vascular maturation,12 a process associated with other vascular disorders.

Mechanisms driving increased R-Ras palmitoylation may involve both APT1-dependent and APT1-independent pathways. While previous preclinical studies suggest that inefficient depalmitoylation due to dysfunctional APT1 activity models PAD,12 alternative mechanisms, such as those mediated by DHHC acyl transferases, may also be involved.

Stimulating angiogenesis to treat PAD has been disappointing in part because regenerated vascular networks may be unable to deliver oxygen effectively.23 Some pro-angiogenic approaches have even shown adverse effects in animal models of PAD.24,25 Ischemic muscles from patients with PAD have capillaries that are paradoxically enlarged,25,26 suggesting that structural adaptations of vessels may be immature and poorly organized, exacerbating pathological tissue remodeling marked by vascular inflammation and oxidative stress.1,3,24,25 Our finding of increased R-Ras palmitoylation in human PAD suggests impaired palmitoylation cycling and decreased R-Ras function. Since R-Ras normally inhibits vascular cell proliferation and promotes vessel maturation through fibronectin remodeling in endothelium,27,28 restoring R-Ras palmitoylation dynamics could improve vessel function in PAD.

Age is associated with R-Ras palmitoylation in human lower limb arteries, suggesting that R-Ras palmitoylation cycling driven by APT1 is diminished with aging. Palmitoylation has been implicated in Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s diseases,29 palmitoylated NMDA receptors accumulate in the frontal cortex of old compared to young mice,30 and chemical inhibition of palmitoylation ameliorates a senescent phenotype in cultured human vascular smooth muscle cells.31 Hypertension is also associated with R-Ras palmitoylation in human PAD. Palmitoylated proteins such as endothelial nitric oxide synthase, Gαq, and the norepinephrine transporter32,33,34 regulate blood pressure. R-Ras may affect hypertension in humans and older mice.35 Since both aging and hypertension are common risk factors for PAD, it is unclear to what extent the association of R-Ras palmitoylation with PAD is specifically mediated by these predisposing conditions. Increased R-Ras palmitoylation in PAD was compared to young organ donors and TOS patients, but the same effect in PAD patients was also seen in comparison to an older healthy control group (Table 4, Figure 4). Additional studies will be required to define any independent association of R-Ras palmitoylation with PAD.

The current diagnosis of PAD relies on self-reported symptoms, imaging modalities, and the ankle-brachial index of blood pressure (ABI), all with limited utility for early detection of disease. ABI can be inaccurate in the settings of diabetes, arterial calcification, or severe hypertension. C-reactive protein, fibrinogen, and D-dimer can predict cardiovascular outcomes in PAD.36 Our data suggest that EV-associated R-Ras, but not total serum R-Ras, represents a biomarker of PAD that is associated with disease severity. EVs have a complex cargo and are released by all cells.37 Potential contributors of EV-associated R-Ras in PAD include enhanced R-Ras tethering to EV membranes due to palmitoylation or a response by endothelial cells to vascular damage unrelated to palmitoylation. It is possible that R-Ras in circulating EVs could influence PAD pathogenesis. Intercellular transfer of small GTPases may promote inflammatory signaling, and palmitoylated R-Ras localized to the plasma membrane could compromise cell-matrix interactions and vessel stability.

In short, our study reveals that vascular R-Ras palmitoylation is elevated in severe PAD patients, likely reflecting decreased R-Ras signaling that might be leveraged for new therapies in PAD. R-Ras palmitoylation, reflected as EV-associated R-Ras, may serve as a biomarker for PAD. Collectively, these findings support the notion that impaired post-translational dynamics of protein S-palmitoylation contribute to human PAD.

Limitations of the study

Control samples for vascular R-Ras palmitoylation studies were not age matched to patients with PAD. The healthy limb arteries studied in the control group were obtained from organ donors, mostly young males who died accidentally, preventing matching with PAD patients. Some controls for serum studies consisted of young, healthy subjects undergoing surgical treatment of TOS, which often occurs in vigorously active patients who may be less likely to develop PAD, thus biasing results. The increase in EV-associated R-Ras in PAD patients was determined in a small sample size but was confirmed in a different cohort with different controls who did not have TOS and were older than the patients with TOS. The mechanism for increased R-Ras in EVs is presumably palmitoylation but that has not been established.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human subjects

Arterial specimens used in this study were obtained from an IRB-approved vascular tissue biobank,38 and all related studies were approved by the Washington University Human Research Protection Office (HRPO #201309043, #201705051, #201107067). Normal arteries were obtained from 12 organ donors (Mid-America Transplant) and 33 PAD specimens were obtained from limbs of patients who provided written informed consent to harvest arterial segments form the already amputated limb. In the operating room, minimally diseased (patent) popliteal and tibial arterial segments were removed en bloc from the discarded amputated lower extremity and immediately dissected from surrounding adipose and adventitial tissue. Frozen tissues from deidentified samples were used for evaluating R-Ras palmitoylation. As most donors were male (9 males, 2 females, and 1 unknown), we selected the PAD cohort (26 males out of 33) to match the gender distribution in this assay. Serum samples were obtained from randomly selected patients with various vascular abnormalities including 23 thoracic outlet syndrome patients and 55 PAD patients with or without CLTI. One non-PAD control group included 7 healthy control participants from a study of Wolfram Syndrome. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians/next of kin. Some participants were identified as carriers of Wolfram syndrome.39 Investigators were masked with respect to any information about patient samples at the time of analyses.

Primary human endothelial cell culture

HAMECs (Human Adipose Microvascular Endothelial Cells) were obtained from ScienCell. Cells were grown in EGM-2 MV media (containing 5 mM D-glucose) at 37°C and used with passage number <10. These cells were originally validated by the source company with expression of endothelial markers including vWF, Factor VIII, and CD31. A PCR-based universal mycoplasma detection kit (ATCC) was used to monitor contamination.

Method details

Acyl-RAC assay for protein palmitoylation

Protein harvested from arterial tissue was subjected to an acyl-RAC procedure to purify palmitoylated protein as described.40 Frozen tissues were homogenized in lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM Tris, 5 mM EDTA, 2% Triton X-100, 0.2 mM HDSF, pH 7.4, and protease inhibitor cocktail from Roche). Following sonication on ice, protein concentrations were determined by BCA protein assay kits (Thermo), and the same amounts of protein lysates were diluted to 2.5% SDS with 0.1% MMTS (S-methyl methanethiosulfonate), and incubated at 37°C for 20 min. Proteins were then precipitated with ice-cold acetone (1:3 v/v protein solution: acetone) at −20°C for 30 min. Protein precipitates were obtained by centrifuging at 12000 rpm for 10 min, washed 3 times with cold 70% acetone, and air dried. Pellets were then resuspended with binding buffer (100 mM HEPES, 1 mM EDTA, 1% SDS, pH = 7.4, and protease inhibitor cocktail from Roche) by vortexing for 20 min at room temperature. Solubilized fractions were obtained by centrifuging at 14000 rpm for 5 min, and supernatants were then aliquoted into 2 tubes containing thiopropyl Sepharose beads (Nanocs), with or without freshly-prepared HA (hydroxylamine 0.8 M, pH = 7.4), and rotated at room temperature for 2 h. Beads were washed 5 times with binding buffer, and enriched proteins were eluted with binding buffer containing 50 mM DTT (20 min incubation at room temperature). Both total and palmitoylated proteins were analyzed by Western blotting using Licor infrared fluorescence imaging. For measuring R-Ras palmitoylation in patient specimens, the ratios of palmitoylated to total R-Ras were calculated. To minimize data variations derived from different gels/blots, we loaded a known series of standards, diluted from the sample mixtures, onto each gel. We calculated the abundance of the target protein in arbitrary units based on the linear curves generated from the intensities of the standards. The levels of palmitoylation were calculated as the percentage of palmitoylated R-Ras relative to the total R-Ras protein and inter-experimental variations were corrected by duplicate analyses in different gels.

Gene knockdown of APT1

For APT1 knockdown (KD) in HAMECs, shRNAs were transduced by lentivirus as described.13 MISSION shRNA Bacterial Glycerol Stock for human APT1/LYPLA1 (in pLKO.1 vector) and control (scrambled sequence) were obtained from Sigma. Virus was generated in 293T cells by transfection of vectors with packaging helper plasmids psPAX2 and pMD2.G. HAMECs were infected for 24 h with virus collected from filtered supernatants of 293T cells 48 h after transfection in the presence of polybrene (10 μg/mL). Cells were selected with puromycin (2 μg/mL) for 24–48 h and studied at least 5 days after infection.

Isolation of EVs from conditioned media of human endothelial cells

After knocking down APT1 expression in HAMECs, EVs were isolated from conditioned media using a size exclusion chromatography (SEC)-based method.18 24 to 72 h conditioned media were centrifuged at 2,000 g × 5 min at 4°C to remove cell debris followed by concentration using 10 kD centrifuge filters (Sartorius) at 2,000g. The concentrates (reduced and adjusted volume to 1 mL) were loaded into each prepacked 10 mL CL-2B (Sigma) column followed by PBS wash. 10 fractions (1 mL each) of flow through were collected. Fractions 4–6 were combined, then concentrated again and stored at −80°C for further analysis.

Isolation of EVs from patient serum

For isolating serum derived EVs, we used a standard ultracentrifugation method.19 In brief, thawed serum samples were diluted by PBS before serial centrifugations at 2,000 g × 30 min, 12,000 g × 45 min, and 110,000 g × 2 h at 4°C. The supernatants were stored at −80°C until needed for total R-Ras quantitation. The pellet was resuspended in PBS and filtered through 0.45 μm filters and centrifuged again at 110,000 g × 70 min at 4°C. After another PBS wash followed by ultracentrifugation, the resultant pellets were incubated in PBS and saved at −80°C for detection of EV-associated R-Ras. R-Ras was first assayed by Western blot as for palmitoylation assays. Subsequently, a human R-Ras ELISA kit (Abbexa) was used for both total and EV-associated fractions according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10 software (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA) and p < 0.05 was considered significant. Comparison data are represented by scatter dot plot (with mean ± SEM) or stacked bar graph (with numeric values of each category). Correlation data are represented by scatterplot (with simple linear regression) or heatmap plots. For patient samples, total and palmitoylated R-Ras were analyzed by unpaired t test with Welch’s correction and ordinary one-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons. Multivariate correlations were analyzed by nonparametric Spearman correlation. For selected parameters, unpaired t tests with Welch’s correction or Pearson correlation were used. Serum total and EV-associated R-Ras measured by ELISA were analyzed by ordinary one-way ANOVA. Fisher’s exact test was used for contingency table analysis of EV R-Ras positive detection derived from Western blotting. For cell studies, EV-associated R-Ras measured by Western blotting and ELISA were both analyzed by ratio paired t test.
