Background

Echinococcosis, a parasitic infection caused by Echinococcus species, represents a significant global public health challenge. The disease primarily manifests as hydatid cysts in the liver and lungs [1], although it may also involve the abdominopelvic cavities following the extrusion, intraperitoneal fissuration, or rupture of hepatic cysts. Primary peritoneal involvement remains exceptionally rare with an incidence of 2% [2, 3]. This variability in clinical presentation can complicate diagnosis and may lead to severe complications if treatment is delayed. Due to globalization and increased human mobility, clinicians in nonendemic regions are now more likely to encounter atypical presentations of echinococcosis. Consequently, it is essential for healthcare providers to be familiar with both classical and unconventional manifestations of the disease. In this report, we describe a rare case of a primary meso-sigmoidal hydatid cyst in a 64-year-old female, underscoring the necessity of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis in patients with unusual abdominal presentations [4, 5].

Case presentation

A 64-year-old woman from an urban area in Tunisia, a country where echinococcosis is endemic with an incidence of 12.7 cases per 100,000 inhabitants [6]. She presented to our surgical outpatient department with intermittent, colicky pain localized to the left lower quadrant, persisting for three months. She denied any changes in bowel habits, vomiting, weight loss, asthenia, or night sweats. Her family history was noncontributory, and she was a non-smoker with a background of insulin-managed diabetes mellitus. Notably, she reported no direct contact with animals.

Physical examination revealed a soft, non-tender abdomen without palpable masses or hepatosplenomegaly. Routine hematological investigations were within normal limits, with no evidence of neutrophilia or eosinophilia. A serological assay using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) was positive for echinococcosis.

An abdominal computed tomography (CT) scan demonstrated a well-defined cystic mass with a poorly defined septations measuring 10 × 7.4 × 6.6 cm in the left iliac fossa, in close proximity to small bowel loops and the left ureter. These findings were consistent with a hydatid cyst classified as Gharbi type II and WHO CE3a (Fig. 1).

A computed tomography scan of the abdomen in axial plane showing a well-defined cystic mass (yellow arrow) adjacent to small bowel loops, sigmoid colon, left ureter and the left psoas muscle

After eliminating other organs involvement, specifically the liver and the lung, the patient underwent a midline laparotomy. Intraoperative findings revealed a cyst embedded within the meso-sigmoid, without invasion of the adjacent colon (Fig. 2). The cyst was closely associated with the genital vessels and had displaced the left ureter medially. Both the liver and spleen appeared normal, and no additional peritoneal or visceral cysts were identified.

An intraoperative view of the hydatid cyst attached to the mesocolon after dissecting it of the sigmoid (green star)

After isolating the viscera with hypertonic saline-soaked towels, a meticulous total excision of the cyst was performed without spillage, thereby minimizing the risk of peritoneal contamination. The mesocolic defect was then sutured. The excised specimen, measuring 6 × 6 cm, exhibited a hard, pearly white wall, and its opening revealed creamy membranes and hydatid material (Fig. 3). The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful, and she was discharged on postoperative day six. Histopathological examination confirmed the diagnosis by demonstrating characteristic lamellar membranes and vesicles (Fig. 4).

Discussion and conclusion

Hydatid disease results from the ingestion of Echinococcus granulosus eggs, with the resultant larvae typically localizing in the liver and lungs as they traverse the portal and systemic circulations. In rare cases, however, the larvae may bypass these primary filters via alternative routes, such as lymphatic dissemination, ultimately seeding the mesocolon.

The serological diagnosis of echinococcosis typically involves a two-step process. An initial screening is performed with high-sensitivity tests, such as ELISA or indirect immunofluorescence (IIF), which, while sensitive, may lack specificity. Positive results are then confirmed with secondary tests—such as immunoelectrophoresis or Western Blot—that offer higher specificity despite lower sensitivity [7]. In our case, the positive ELISA result was crucial, particularly given the patient’s atypical presentation, as imaging findings alone might not have provided a definitive diagnosis. The combination of serological testing with detailed imaging allowed for accurate diagnosis and informed surgical planning.

Preoperative diagnosis of hydatid cysts is challenging because symptoms are often nonspecific and may result from the mass effect on adjacent structures [8]. Clinically, patients may present with vague abdominal discomfort, localized pain, or symptoms related to compression of nearby structures, such as constipation, urinary symptoms, or vascular compromise. Constitutional symptoms such as fever, weight loss, or night sweats are typically absent (8). In our patient, intermittent colicky pain was the sole symptom, which could easily be misinterpreted. Large mesocolic hydatid cysts can compromise sigmoid colon vascularization, potentially leading to ischemic colitis or bowel obstruction, as reported by Astarcıogˇlu et al. [9]. Additionally, pressure on the urinary tract may result in complications such as pyelonephritis or acute urinary retention, as described by Kc M et al. [10]. CT imaging plays a vital role by not only confirming the cystic nature of the lesion—with reported high sensitivity and specificity—but also by delineating its anatomical relationships. CT features suggestive of hydatid disease include a well-defined, unilocular or multilocular cyst with internal septations, calcified walls, and occasionally, daughter cysts or membrane detachment.

The cornerstone of treatment for hydatid cysts, particularly those in challenging locations like the mesocolon, is complete surgical excision. The primary objective is to remove the cyst intact to prevent intraoperative spillage, which could precipitate anaphylactic reactions or secondary peritoneal echinococcosis. In the present case, careful isolation of the operative field using hypertonic saline-soaked towels, combined with meticulous dissection, enabled the safe removal of the cyst without compromising adjacent structures. Preoperative strategies, including the preparation of blood reserves and the consideration of alternative surgical procedures such as bowel resection, are essential when dealing with cysts that are intimately associated with critical anatomical landmarks.

This case of a primary meso-sigmoidal hydatid cyst illustrates the diverse clinical spectrum of echinococcosis and reinforces the need for heightened clinical awareness, especially in nonendemic regions. The integration of serological tests with advanced CT imaging not only enhances diagnostic accuracy but also informs surgical strategy by clarifying anatomical relationships. Recognizing such atypical presentations is imperative for prompt diagnosis and management, ultimately improving patient outcomes.
