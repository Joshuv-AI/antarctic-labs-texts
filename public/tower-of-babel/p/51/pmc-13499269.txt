The second unprovoked seizure in a cohort of treated patients with a first unprovoked seizure
Authors: Revital Gandelman‐Marton, Jacques Theitler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the rate of a second unprovoked seizure in antiseizure medications (ASMs)‐treated patients following a first unprovoked seizure and to identify potential risk factors associated with its occurrence.MethodsA retrospective review of the computerized database and the medical records of all the patients who presented to our outpatient epilepsy clinic following admission and ASM initiation due to a first unprovoked seizure during a decade (2012–2021).ResultsThe study group included 60 patients aged 18–84 years (mean: 40.8 years), 35 (58.3%) men. A second unprovoked seizure occurred in 43% of the patients at last observation. Seventy‐five percent of the recurrences occurred at 3 years. Patients with a second unprovoked seizure were younger (32.9 vs. 46.9 years) (p = 0.010) and more likely to be women (64% vs. 28.6%) (p = 0.009) compared to patients without recurrent seizures.SignificanceWomen and younger patients may deserve closer monitoring following a first unprovoked seizure. Further studies are needed to assess the efficacy of specific treatment strategies among all people with a first unprovoked seizure, with emphasis on younger adults and especially women.

INTRODUCTION

A single seizure is a traumatic physical and psychological event with major social consequences. 1 Recurrent seizures can adversely affect the occurrence of comorbid illnesses, psychological function, social stigma, and quality of life and may lead to severe morbidity, major social consequences, and increased risk of mortality. 2 , 3

Studies that evaluated the rate of seizure recurrence following a first unprovoked seizure varied considerably regarding age, seizure type, and antiseizure medication (ASM) treatment status. In untreated adolescents and adults with a first tonic–clonic seizure, the risk of recurrence at 1, 2, 3, and 4 years was 30%, 37%, 42%, and 47%, respectively, 4 and according to data from 58 studies that included mixed cohorts of treated and untreated adults with a first unprovoked seizure, seizure recurrence rates at 2 years range from 21% to 45%. 1 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Immediate ASM treatment can reduce 2‐year relapse rates by 23%–46% compared to delayed treatment and increases the probability of an immediate 5‐year remission. 1 , 9

Certain clinical variables, such as the presence of a prior brain insult, an epileptogenic lesion on brain imaging, epileptiform discharges on EEG, and a nocturnal seizure, were associated with increased risk of seizure recurrence in children and adults with a single unprovoked seizure. 1 Younger age, secondarily generalized seizures, remote etiologic factors, and an EEG with epileptiform abnormalities were significant predictors of relapse in children and adults with a single tonic–clonic seizure who received immediate ASM treatment 10 and abnormal brain CT scan was associated with increased risk of recurrence in untreated adolescents and adults with a first tonic–clonic seizure. 4

In this study, we aimed to evaluate the rate of a second unprovoked seizure in ASM‐treated patients following a first unprovoked seizure, and to identify potential risk factors associated with its occurrence. The identification of clinical variables that are associated with increased risk of a second unprovoked seizure among patients who have already initiated immediate ASM treatment may prompt the use of different treatment strategies in this population.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

This was a retrospective study of people who presented to our adult outpatient epilepsy clinic following admission and ASM initiation due to a first unprovoked seizure over a 10‐year period (2012–2021). At our department, an ASM is initiated following a first unprovoked seizure according to current guidelines, 1 and a recommendation to visit the epilepsy clinic is included in the discharge summaries of all the patients. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee at Shamir‐Assaf Harofeh Medical Center, an 890‐bed university‐affiliated community hospital that serves a population of 1 500 000 in the central part of Israel. Patient consent was waived due to the retrospective study design.

The outpatient domain in the computerized medical information management system was searched according to the following inclusion criteria: patients presenting to their first visit following admission and a discharge diagnosis of a first unprovoked seizure. Exclusion criteria included the following: admission due to provoked seizures (seizures due to identifiable causes such as metabolic factors), acute symptomatic seizures (seizures due to an acute brain process such as stroke), nonepileptic or unclassified events, or breakthrough seizures in patients with a prior diagnosis of epilepsy. In patients presenting to their first visit at the epilepsy clinic following admission due to a first unprovoked seizure, those who did not continue follow‐up at the epilepsy clinic, did not receive ASM treatment at first clinic visit, or were diagnosed with previously unrecognized or undiagnosed unprovoked seizures were excluded from the study.

The visit summaries were reviewed for the following data: age, gender, seizure type, diagnosis at first and last clinic visits, findings of brain CT scan and routine EEG performed during admission, findings of brain MRI (performed after admission), the presence of prior unrecognized/undiagnosed unprovoked seizures, dates of admission and clinic visit, follow‐up duration, the date of the second unprovoked seizure after admission and during follow‐up, and number, type and doses of ASMs at first visit, during follow‐up and at last clinic visit.

Seizure types were classified based on criteria of the International League Against Epilepsy, that is, the diagnosis of generalized or focal epilepsy is made on clinical grounds, supported by the finding of typical interictal EEG discharges. 11 , 12 , 13 Patients were diagnosed with a single seizure when epilepsy type could not be determined following a first unprovoked seizure based on data available at first clinic visit. Potentially epileptogenic imaging abnormalities included cortical/subcortical lesions such as post‐traumatic or poststroke encephalomalacia, vascular malformations, abscesses, tumors, and malformations of cortical development. Since second and third generation ASMs have better tolerability and fewer pharmacological interactions compared to first‐generation ASMs and these may influence the effectiveness of treatment, ASM type was classified according to ASM generation and included the following: first generation (carbamazepine, phenytoin, primidone, valproic acid), second generation (gabapentin, lamotrigine, levetiracetam), third generation (lacosamide), polytherapy including a first generation ASM, and polytherapy of second and third generation ASMs. Defined ASM daily doses (DDD) were obtained from the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical/DDD Index at the World Health Organization (WHO) Collaborating Centre for Drug Statistics Methodology site. 14 The prescribed daily dose (PDD)/DDD ratio was calculated for each ASM at the first and last visits.

Statistical analysis was done by SPSS version 27.0, Chicago, IL. Categorical variables were analyzed using Pearson χ 2 test and Fisher's exact test, and t‐test was used to analyze continuous variables. Multivariate analysis of the likelihood of a second unprovoked seizure was tested by logistic regression. Statistical significance was determined at p < 0.05.

RESULTS

One hundred thirty‐three patients with a first unprovoked seizure were identified. Of these, 49 patients attended a single visit and did not continue follow‐up at the epilepsy clinic, 18 patients were diagnosed with previously unrecognized or undiagnosed unprovoked seizures, and six patients did not receive ASM treatment at first clinic visit (misdiagnosis [3], a single seizure without risk factors for further seizures [1], the recommended ASM was not approved by the health insurance [1], adverse events with three different ASMs [1]), and were excluded from the study (Figure 1). There were no significant demographic or clinical differences between the patients who attended a single visit and did not continue follow‐up at the epilepsy clinic and those who continued follow‐up and were included in the study group.

Flow diagram illustrating patients' inclusion in the study.

The study group included 60 patients aged 18–84 years (mean: 40.8 years), 35 (58.3%) men, who presented to their first visit at the epilepsy clinic with ASM treatment following admission due to a first unprovoked seizure (Table 1). Patients had their first clinic visit 10. ± 7.6 weeks after discharge (range: 1.5–44 weeks), 14 (23.3%) patients within 1 month following admission. Seven (11.7%) patients had recurrent seizures that occurred 3 days to 2.7 months (0.94 ± 0.97 months) after admission and before attending the first clinic visit. Epilepsy type was determined in 46 (76.7%) patients: 28 patients had an EEG with interictal epileptiform discharges during admission and 6 patients after discharge and before the first visit at the epilepsy clinic, and 12 patients had an EEG with focal slowing—11 with supportive imaging and one patient had a focal seizure after discharge and before the first visit at the epilepsy clinic. Fifty‐six (93.3%) patients were treated with ASM monotherapy, and 40 (66.7%) were treated with second and/or third generation ASMs. The most common monotherapies at first visit were levetiracetam, phenytoin and carbamazepine, and the most common drug combination was phenytoin and lamotrigine (Table 2). Brain CT scan was obtained during admission in all the patients and routine EEG in 58 (96.7%) patients. Potentially epileptogenic imaging abnormalities, epileptiform discharges on routine EEG, or both were identified in 10 (16.7%), 22 (37.9%), and 6 (10.3%) patients, respectively. Brain MRI was obtained following admission in 57 patients, with potentially epileptogenic abnormalities in 15 patients (encephalomalacia—post‐traumatic or following tumor resection [n = 5], cavernoma [n = 4], malformations of cortical development [n = 2], abscess [n = 1], mesial temporal sclerosis [n = 1], meningioma [n = 1], amyloid angiopathy [n = 1]).

A second unprovoked seizure occurred in 26 (43.3%) patients, in 19 patients during a 2.5 ± 2.4 year follow‐up (range: 0.2–10.8 years) in our epilepsy clinic, and in seven patients both after admission and before the first clinic visit and subsequently during follow‐up. The second unprovoked seizure occurred 3 days to 8.4 years (23.5 ± 28.6 months) following the first unprovoked seizure (the exact date of recurrence was not specified in two patients), and 37.5%, 45.8%, 54.2%, and 75% of the recurrences occurred 3, 6, 12, and 36 months following the first unprovoked seizure (Figure 2). Patients with a second unprovoked seizure were younger (32.9 vs. 46.9 years) (p = 0.01) and more likely women (61.5% vs. 26.5%) (p = 0.009) compared to patients without a second unprovoked seizure (Table 3). The likelihood of a second unprovoked seizure at last observation was increased by female sex (odds ratio [OR]: 4.19, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.32–13.31) (p = 0.015) and inversely related to age (OR: 0.96, 95% CI: 0.94–1.0) (p = 0.026). The most common monotherapies at last visit were levetiracetam and lamotrigine, and the most common drug combination was levetiracetam and lacosamide (Table 2). PDD/DDD ratio at first and last observation did not significantly differ between patients with or without a second unprovoked seizure.

DISCUSSION

In this cohort of ASM‐treated patients following a first unprovoked seizure, a second unprovoked seizure occurred in 43% of the patients at last observation, and 75% of the recurrences occurred at 3 years. Patients with a second unprovoked seizure were younger compared to patients without recurrent seizures and more likely to be women. The rate of seizure recurrence following a first unprovoked seizure varies considerably among studies. A seizure recurred at 6 months, 2 years, and 5 years in 18%, 32%, and 42% of children (>1 month) and adults with a single seizure that were randomized to immediate ASM treatment with carbamazepine, valproic acid, phenytoin, or lamotrigine. 5 In children (>2 years) and adults with a single seizure that were randomized to immediate ASM treatment with carbamazepine, phenytoin, phenobarbital, or valproic acid, 24% had seizure recurrence in the subsequent 2 years, 10 and in adolescents (≥16 years) and adults with a first seizure who received immediate treatment with valproic acid, seizure recurrence at 12 months occurred in 4.3% of the patients. 15

The presence of abnormal neurologic status, epileptiform abnormalities on the EEG and nocturnal seizures were most frequently reported to be predictive of recurrent unprovoked seizures in mixed cohorts of treated and untreated children and adults. 1 , 16 In children (>2 years) and adults with a single tonic clonic seizure who received immediate ASM treatment, secondarily generalized seizures, remote etiologic factors, an EEG with epileptiform abnormalities and age (<16 years) were significant predictors of relapse. 10 Age and gender were not consistently associated with increased risk of seizure recurrence. 6 , 16 , 17 However, in a study of treated and untreated adolescents and adults with a first unprovoked seizure, recurrence rate was nonsignificantly higher in younger individuals (16–20 vs. ≥21 years) and in males, 18 and in another study that included patients with unprovoked as well as acute symptomatic and provoked first seizures, higher risk of recurrence was associated with age <16 years. 19 The association between younger age and a higher risk of seizure recurrence may be related to decreased performance of safety, wellness and treatment behaviors by younger people with epilepsy. 20 ASM treatment significantly decreased recurrence risk in males in a prospective study of children (≥3.9 years) and adults with a single generalized tonic–clonic seizure and generalized spike–wave discharges on the EEG, and the nonsignificant decrease in the female group was attributed to small sample size. 21 Although under‐treatment is more likely in women of childbearing age due to concerns regarding teratogenic effects of ASMs, 22 ASM number, dose and type did not significantly differ among women and men in our study.

Epilepsy clinic attendance following a first seizure was previously associated with reduced subsequent emergency presentations, hospitalizations, seizure‐related presentations, and mortality, and since the risk of recurrent seizure following a single unprovoked seizure in adults is highest especially during the first 3–6 months, 19 , 23 a neurologist review is recommended within 2 weeks in patients with a first seizure. 24 Recurrent seizures occurred following admission and before the first clinic visit in 11% of our patients, and the mean admission‐clinic interval was 10 weeks. Similar wait times for first assessment by an epilepsy specialist were reported by Rizvi et al. in Canada, 25 and according to McIntosh et al., 26 8% of the patients had a seizure during a 3‐week interval between the seizure that prompted the first seizure clinic visit and the clinic appointment.

Levetiracetam was the most common monotherapy among our patients both at first and last visits. This finding is consistent with other studies that reported a gradual increase of levetiracetam use over the past two decades. 27 , 28 , 29

This retrospective study has several limitations including the size of our study group and its heterogeneity regarding age, epilepsy type, and etiology, and follow‐up duration. In addition, this was a single‐center study, and further studies are needed to examine the external validity of our results.

Although a timely evaluation is recommended in all individuals following a first unprovoked seizure, our findings suggest that women and younger patients may deserve closer monitoring. Further studies are needed to assess whether intensified educational efforts regarding adherence to ASMs, specific ASM types or combinations, and higher ASM target doses or initial polytherapy may be more effective in decreasing the rate of seizure recurrence among all the people with a first unprovoked seizure, with emphasis on younger adults and especially women.
