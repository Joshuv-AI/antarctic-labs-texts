Impacts of land use/land cover change on ecosystem service values in the Dinki Watershed, central highlands of Ethiopia
Authors: Yared Worku, Mengistu Asmamaw, Argaw Ambelu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examined land use and land cover (LULC) dynamics in the Dinki watershed, central highlands of Ethiopia, and their impacts on ecosystem service values (ESVs) across three time periods (1994, 2014, 2023). Multi temporal satellite imagery was classified to detect changes, complemented by community perspectives gathered through interviews and focus group discussions. Ecosystem service values were sourced from global databases and adjusted for the Ethiopian highlands using biome-specific coefficients, cross-checked with local experts and community input. Six LULC categories were identified: cropland, built up areas, dense forest, grazing land, sparse vegetation/ slight vegetation, and water bodies from the total area of 160.91 km2. Classification accuracy improved from 86.1% (1994) to 91.67% (2014) and 94.4% (2023), with strong agreement indicated by Kappa values of 0.83, 0.90, and 0.93. The LULC analysis showed notable changes across the watershed, where increases in water bodies and sparse vegetation contributed to overall ecosystem service value (ESV) gains. These changes significantly influenced ESVs, with water bodies and sparse vegetation driving the net increase. Consequently, total ESVs rose from US$200.32 thousand per year in 1994 to US$310.33 thousand per year in 2023, despite losses from cropland and forest ecosystems. Regulating services contributed the largest share, followed by provisioning, supporting, and cultural services. The findings demonstrate that LULC dynamics simultaneously enhance and undermine ecosystem functions. Integrated watershed management combining sustainable agriculture, reforestation, livelihood diversification, and community participation will be essential to balance ecological integrity with socioeconomic development in the Dinki watershed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-44717-9.

Introduction

Land use and land cover (LULC) refers to human-induced transformations of the landscape, including agricultural expansion, settlement development, and resource extraction1. LULC change is a major driver of environmental and socio-ecological transformations, affecting biodiversity, climate regulation, water quality, and the provision of ecosystem services, though its magnitude and impacts remain debated2–4. Global assessments reveal substantial anthropogenic alteration of landscapes; yet, estimates of LULC change vary widely due to differences in methods and definitions. For instance, Hu et al.2 reported a global forest loss of 24.5 million hectares (Mha) between 1992 and 2018, while Kayitesi et al.5 found 17% of the Earth’s terrestrial surface changes at least once between 1960 and 2019, resulting in a net forest loss of 0.8 million km2 and an increase of 1.0 million km2 in agricultural land. Such discrepancies highlight the need for harmonized frameworks integrating biophysical, socio-economic, and policy dimensions.

LULC dynamics significantly affect ecosystem service values (ESV) at multiple scales by altering regulating, provisioning, and supporting functions. Globally, ESVs declined from US$145 trillion in 2007 to US$125 trillion in 2011, largely due to forest loss and land degradation4,6–8. The conversion of natural vegetation, particularly forests, has reduced carbon sequestration, hydrological regulation, and biodiversity support, while increasing soil erosion, flooding risk, and hydrological instability9–15.

In Ethiopia, particularly in the highlands, LULC is closely linked to land degradation through the conversion of natural vegetation into agricultural land. Deforestation and the expansion of intensive agriculture have contributed to soil erosion, reduced ecosystem resilience, and declining ecosystem service provision14,16–21. These changes also exacerbate greenhouse gas emissions, biodiversity loss, water pollution, and altered streamflow regimes, affecting local livelihoods and community well-being5,15,21–25. Therefore, a targeted assessment specific to the highland watershed, integrating the biophysical and socio-economic drivers, is critical to inform sustainable watershed management and policy interventions.

Although GIS and remote sensing studies mapped LULC changes across Ethiopia3,13,26,27, these approaches often overlook local knowledge, perceptions, and socio-ecological implications. Moreover, inconsistencies in land classification, limited temporal coverage, and insufficient integration of socio-economic drivers constrain the capacity to link LULC dynamics to changes in ecosystem service values13,19,20. These gaps are particularly evident in the Dinki Watershed, a socio-ecologically important highland landscape where competing land-use demands, rapid population growth, and environmental pressures intersect. In the Dinki Watershed, deforestation and agricultural expansion into marginal lands have led to pronounced environmental degradation, yet the effects of long-term LULC dynamics on ecosystem service values and community well-being remain poorly understood28.

Despite its importance, little is known about how long-term LULC dynamics in the Dinki Watershed influence ecosystem service provision and associated community well-being. Addressing this knowledge gap is critical for informing sustainable land management strategies and policy interventions in the Ethiopian highlands29,30. Therefore, this study was designed to assess the dynamics of land use and land cover in the Dinki Watershed and evaluate their impacts on ecosystem service values. We hypothesize that LULC dynamics significantly influence ecosystem service provision and valuation in the Dinki watershed. The research questions were: (i) what are the long-term spatio-temporal dynamics of land use and land cover in the Dinki Watershed between 1994 and 2023? (ii) how have changes in land use and land cover influenced the provision and overall value of ecosystem services in the watershed? and (iii) which LULC types and ecosystem services contribute most to overall ESV change? The findings of this study provide evidence-based insights that can support policymakers and land managers in designing sustainable land use planning and ecosystem conservation strategies in the region.

Materials and methods

Study area

The Dinki Watershed is situated between 9° 32′ 30" and 9° 42′ 30" N latitude and 39° 41′ 0" and 39° 53′ 0" E longitude in the central highlands of Ethiopia, with Gorobela, the district administrative center, situated 42 km east of Debre Berhan and 172 km north of Addis Ababa. The watershed encompasses an area of 160.91 km2, with elevations ranging between 1, 257 and 3,181 m above sea level (Fig. 1). The watershed is topographically complex, with hills and mountains covering 75% of the area, rocky terrains covering 17%, and flat landscapes covering 8%. Long-term meteorological records indicate a mean annual rainfall of 1,179 mm over the 30-year period (1987–2016). In contrast, annual rainfall from 201628 to 2023 ranged between 800 and 1,000 mm.Fig. 1Dinki watershed is located in Amhara region, North Shewa Zone with covering of hills and Mountains. Land use in the watershed is dominated by cropland and grazing on gentle slopes, with natural forests and shrub lands confined to steeper terrain. Tree plantations, mainly eucalyptus, are widespread and integrated into small woodlots and homesteads. Soil and water conservation measures are widely practiced, especially in the Dens sub-catchment, alongside mixed farming, livestock rearing, and small-scale forestry. These interacting biophysical and socio-economic conditions make the Dinki Watershed well suited for analyzing LULC dynamics, ecosystem service values, and human environment interactions.

Data acquisition

To assess land use and land cover dynamics in the Dinki Watershed between 1994–2014 and 2014–2023, multi-temporal Landsat satellite imagery was employed. Landsat 5 Thematic Mapper ™ data were used for 1994, Landsat 7 Enhanced Thematic Mapper Plus (ETM +) for 2014, and Landsat 8 Operational Land Imager (OLI) for 2023. All satellite images were acquired from the United States Geological Survey (USGS) Earth Explorer platform (Table 1). All Landsat images were obtained as USGS Collection 2 Level-2 surface reflectance products at 30 m resolution. Atmospheric correction was applied using LEDAPS for Landsat 5 TM and 7 ETM + , and LaSRC for Landsat 8 OLI. Cloud and cloud-shadow pixels were masked using QA bands. All images were acquired during the dry season (December-February) under similar solar illumination, minimizing phonological and illumination differences and ensuring spectral consistency across 1994, 2014, and 2023 datasets. The Water bodies for 1994, 2014, and 2023 were extracted from Landsat imagery (TM, ETM + , OLI) using the Modified Normalized Difference Water Index (MNDWI) with a threshold > 0.0. Headwater streams and ponds < 0.05 km2 were excluded. Topographic and shadow effects were corrected using a 30 m SRTM DEM, and outputs were refined with filtering and manual editing. Validation used high-resolution imagery and GPS points for 2014 and 2023. Image pre-processing and classification

Image classification assigns each pixel in a satellite image to a specific LULC category based on spectral characteristics. Accurate classification requires pre-processing to ensure data quality and comparability over time, including selection of classification methods, training sample identification, feature extraction, algorithm choice, post-classification refinement, and accuracy assessment31. Multi-temporal Landsat images used in this study were obtained from USGS Earth Explorer, providing standard products corrected for geometric and radiometric distortions. All images were verified and Orthorectified to minimize positional errors, while preserving the original 30 m spatial resolution, pixel values, and spectral properties, ensuring reliable multi-temporal LULC comparison. Following this the image classification was performed using a supervised, pixel-based approach, a reliable method for LULC mapping. Ground truth data were collected via GPS field surveys and supplemented with high-resolution Google Earth imagery and historical Landsat images. Representative polygons were digitized for each LULC class to ensure accurate spectral signature extraction.

Both maximum likelihood (ML) and support vector machine (SVM) classifiers were tested, and SVM consistently outperformed ML based on preliminary accuracy assessments. SVM was therefore used for all LULC maps (1994, 2014, 2023), with post-classification filtering applied to reduce the salt-and-pepper effect. Based on the prior land cover information, field observations, and regional ecological characteristics, six major LULC classes were identified and analyzed, including: (i) agricultural land, (ii) sparse/slight vegetation, (iii) water bodies, (iv) dense forest, (v) grazing land/grassland, and (vi) built-up areas. LULC change analysis

Following supervised classification, post-processing was applied to enhance map accuracy. A majority filter reduced fragmented artifacts, producing more coherent LULC patterns. The final maps were validated using Ground Control Points (GCPs) to ensure spatial reliability. LULC change analysis employed pairwise comparisons and weighted overlay in ArcGIS 10.5. For each class, areal extent (ha) and percentage change were calculated for 1994–2014 and 2014–2023. Absolute change was defined as the area difference between time points, with percentage change expressed relative to the total watershed area. Area coverage, annual change rate, overall change rate, and relative change were also computed and summarized for each LULC class. The annual rate of change was calculated using the following equation:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Rata of change= \frac{A2-A1}{T2-T1}$$\end{document}where A1 and A2 are the values at time T1 and T2, respectively. The rate of change is expressed in km2 per year.

This post-classification analysis provides a quantitative assessment of LULC dynamics over time, allowing for the identification of trends, rates, and patterns of land cover transformation within the Dinki watershed.

Ecosystem service valuation (ESVs)

The impacts of land use and land cover changes on ecosystem service value (ESV) dynamics were evaluated using an integrated valuation approach based on updated ecosystem service value coefficients. For this purpose, conservative estimation coefficients specifically developed for Ethiopian conditions by15,34 were applied and cross-checked with local experts and community input. These coefficients are grounded in a detailed understanding of Ethiopia’s landscape characteristics and draw upon supplementary valuation studies, with primary reference to the Economics of Ecosystems and Biodiversity (TEEB) valuation database. The classified LULC outputs for 1994, 2014, and 2023 were used to quantify the ESVs of different land cover types within the Dinki Watershed. Each LULC class was systematically matched to corresponding biomes defined in the global ecosystem services and natural capital framework established by Costanza et al.35, Costanza et al.36 and Costanza37. Furthermore, the biome equivalence was established based on vegetation structure, dominant land cover characteristics, and ecological functioning rather than strict global biome definitions. Dense forest areas in the watershed exhibit closed-canopy woody vegetation and were therefore matched with tropical forest biomes. Slight vegetation areas are dominated by shrubs and sparse woody cover and were classified as shrub land. Grazing lands are primarily herbaceous and were matched with grassland biomes, while water bodies correspond to lake and river systems. Individual ecosystem value determination

To calculate the total ecosystem service value (ESV), the values of 17 individual ecosystem services were first estimated using Eq. 7. For each land use and land cover class, the ESV was computed by multiplying the corresponding value coefficient by the total area (km2) of that LULC type. The total ESV of the watershed for each reference year was then obtained by summing the ESVs of all LULC classes. In this study, valuation coefficients developed by Costanza et al.35 were adopted, as they provide a comprehensive framework for 16 major biomes and 17 ecosystem services. The modified coefficients used in this analysis, which account for local conditions, are presented in supplementary material Table 1. The coefficient of sensitivity (CS) is derived from the economic concept of elasticity and reflects the influence of the assigned value coefficient on the estimated ecosystem service value. When CS < 1, the estimated ecosystem value is considered inelastic with respect to the coefficient, indicating that the results are relatively robust and reliable15. Conversely, when CS > 1, the ESV is elastic, demonstrating higher sensitivity to changes in the assigned value coefficient. In such cases, a precise definition of the value coefficient becomes critical, as the reliability of the estimated ESV is directly affected. The importance of accurate coefficient estimation increases proportionally with higher CS values15,39.

Considering the differences between local and global conditions40, this study employed a combination of global ecosystem service value coefficients35,37,41, and context-specific coefficients adapted for Ethiopia by Kindu et al.34, to quantify ESVs in relation to LULC changes. The application of these coefficients followed the methodologies outlined by Kindu et al.34, Gashaw et al.14, Tesfaye et al.15, and Tadesse et al.40 (Supplementary Material Table 1). The applicability of these coefficients was cross-checked through expert consultation, previously applied in a similar agro-ecology (Chacha Watershed;15), and validated against community perceptions collected via key informant interviews and focus group discussions. Final ecosystem service value estimates primarily used Ethiopia-specific coefficients developed for comparable watershed ecosystems15,34, with global coefficients from Costanza et al.35 applied only where local values were unavailable (see Supplementary Table 1). Moreover, higher ESV coefficients were assigned to water bodies because rivers and small streams in the watershed provide multiple high-value services, including water supply, flow regulation, habitat support, and cultural services. These coefficients were adopted from globally established valuations35 and contextually applied in Ethiopian watershed studies15,34.

As noted by Costanza et al.35, ecosystem service value (ESV) coefficients are not assigned for urban areas. In developed countries, urban areas may have high ESVs due to recreational, cultural, and tourism services, however, built-up areas in the Dinki Watershed provide negligible ecosystem services, lacking organized green spaces, recreational infrastructure, or basic urban amenities. Applying the global urban coefficient (US$6,661 ha⁻1 yr⁻1;15,41) would substantially overestimate ESVs in this rural context. This may be due the developed countries often exhibit relatively high ecosystem service values due to recreational, cultural, and tourism functions. Therefore, built-up land for Dinki watershed was assigned an ESV of zero (which was adopted from Tesfaye et al.15), which had minimal impact on total ESV estimates or observed temporal trends. As there are no context-specific categories for built-up areas in Ethiopia, the built-up LULC categories were excluded from ESV calculations in this study. Socioeconomic data

Qualitative data from key informant interviews (KIIs), focus group discussions (FGDs), and field observations were used to identify and interpret the drivers of land use and land cover (LULC) transitions in the watershed, providing local context for remotely sensed land cover changes. These qualitative insights supported the interpretation of observed LULC patterns and ecosystem service dynamics. KIIs and FGDs were conducted across three agro-ecological zones (upper, middle, and lower watershed) using structured and semi-structured guides. Twenty-one purposively selected key informants and three FGDs comprising diverse community representatives participated in the study. Field observations complemented interview data by documenting land use practices and resource management conditions. The qualitative findings from key informant interviews (KIIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs) were explicitly used to identify and interpret the drivers of LULC transitions observed in the watershed, providing local context for land cover change patterns derived from remote sensing. All qualitative data were transcribed, coded, and thematically analyzed using QDA Miner Lite, following established procedures. Ethical statement

Participation in interviews and focus group discussion was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained orally from all participants prior to data collection, as written consent is not mandatory for interviews and focus group discussion. However, all study producers were conducted in accordance with relevant ethical guidelines and regulations. The study protocol, including consent procedures, was reviewed and approved by the host institution, the department of Fisheries and aquatic sciences, prior to the commencement of data collection. Results

Accuracy assessment

Accuracy assessment was conducted using an error (confusion) matrix to evaluate the reliability of the image classifications. Reference sample sizes per class were selected using stratified random sampling proportional to class area (n = 15–20 per class), ensuring independence from training samples. Overall classification accuracy increased progressively from 86.1% in 1994 to 91.67% in 2014 and further to 94.4% in 2023, indicating a consistent improvement in classification performance over time. Correspondingly, the Kappa coefficients rose from 0.83 (1994) to 0.90 (2014) and 0.93 (2023), all exceeding the 0.80 threshold and demonstrating strong agreement between the classified maps and reference data (Table 5). For 2023, the Maximum Likelihood (ML) classifier achieved 88.5% overall accuracy (Kappa = 0.86), whereas the Support Vector Machine (SVM) outperformed ML with 92.3% overall accuracy (Kappa = 0.91) and was therefore used for all maps, applying the same training and validation procedures consistently across years. Trends in built-up land in the Dinki Watershed (1994–2023)

The LULC analysis indicates that built-up land showed a declining trend over the study period. Built-up areas decreased from 13.38% (21.53 km2) in 1994 to 9.77% (15.72 km2) in 2014, and further to 7.13% (11.48 km2) in 2023 (Fig. 5; Table 6). Overall, built-up land declined by 10.05 km2 between 1994 and 2023, with an average annual decrease of about 0.35 km2 due to consolidation of scattered houses into village centers (Zego). This trend likely reflects the predominantly rural nature of the watershed, where settlements are small and dispersed, and some previously mixed pixels were reclassified into agricultural or vegetation classes as classification accuracy improved. Similar patterns have been reported in rural Ethiopian highland watersheds where land use changes are mainly driven by agricultural and vegetation dynamics rather than urban expansion.

Dynamics of slight vegetation in the Dinki Watershed (1994–2023)

Following agricultural land, slight vegetation was the second largest land cover type in the Dinki Watershed throughout the study period. Although its coverage remained smaller than cropland, sparse vegetation exhibited a consistent and substantial increase over time. The largest extent was recorded in 2023, covering 28.70% (46.18 km2) of the watershed, followed by 25% (40.18 km2) in 2014, with the smallest coverage observed in 1994 at 16% (26.09 km2). Overall, sparse vegetation expanded considerably over the three decades, with an average annual increase of 0.67 km2 (Table 6; Fig. 3).

Dynamics of dense forest and grazing land, the Dinki Watershed (1994–2023)

Dense forest cover declined steadily across the study period, decreasing from about 12% (19 km2) in 1994 to ~ 6% (≈9.5 km2) in 2014 and remaining similarly low in 2023 (Table 6). In contrast, grazing land expanded markedly, reaching its maximum extent in 2023 (26.78%, 43.10 km2) after minimal coverage in 2014 (0.27%, 0.44 km2). Grazing land showed the largest net increase among all LULC classes, expanding at an average rate of 1.03 km2 yr⁻1 between 1994 and 2023 (Table 6; Fig. 3). The exceptionally low grazing-land extent in 2014 reflects its highly fragmented and degraded condition, which also contributed to reduced class-level accuracy (Table 5) due to partial spectral overlap with slight vegetation and cropland, whereas the substantial expansion and improved class separability observed in 2023 confirm a genuine land-use transition rather than classification confusion (Table 5).

Dynamics of water bodies in Dinki Watershed (1994–2023)

Water bodies, including headwater streams, occupied a substantial portion of the Dinki Watershed, particularly in later periods. In 2014, they were the second largest LULC type, and by 2023 ranked just after grazing land, reaching a maximum coverage of 18.88% (29.25 km2). From 1994 to 2023, water bodies increased at an average rate of 0.42 km2 yr⁻1 (Table 6; Fig. 3), likely due to farmers blocking streams in the lower and middle catchments for small-scale irrigation, combined with natural recovery and seasonal rainfall variability.

Land use and land cover change matrix of the Dinki Watershed (1994–2014)

The LULC matrix for 1994–2014 reveals substantial transitions among different land cover classes (Supplementary Material Tables 1, 6; Fig. 5). The sparse vegetation and water bodies experienced more pronounced expansion, increasing by 14.09 km2 and 10.78 km2, respectively, likely reflecting the effects of soil and water conservation and restoration initiatives that contributed to increased vegetation cover and thereby water retention despite increased agricultural lands. Between 1994 and 2014, agricultural land and dense forest were partly converted into sparse vegetation and water bodies, while some dense forest and sparse vegetation became cropland and built-up areas. These patterns underscore the dynamic nature of land use in the watershed and highlight the need for targeted policies that balance socio-economic development with ecological conservation.

Status and dynamics of ecosystem services in Dinki Watershed (1994–2023)

Estimated ecosystem service values

The estimation of ecosystem service values (ESVs) for each LULC category was conducted using Eq. 6 (Table 7). In 1994, the dominant contributors to ecosystem services were water bodies (US$134.15 thousand), sparse vegetation (US$25.74 thousand), dense forest (US$18.68 thousand), and agricultural land (US$18.17 thousand). Across all LULC types, ESVs ranged from US$3.56 thousand for grazing land, the lowest, to US$134.15 thousand for water bodies, the highest (Table 7). Water bodies consistently contributed the highest ecosystem service value (ESV), increasing from US$134.17 thousand in 1994 to US$237.00 thousand in 2023 and accounting for the largest share of total ESV throughout 1994–2023 (66.98% to 76.37%). Sparse vegetation showed a moderate increase in ESV and remained the second-largest contributor, followed by grazing land. In contrast, agricultural land contributed comparatively low ESVs across all periods.

Dynamics of individual and total ecosystem services in Dinki Watershed (1994–2023)

The analysis of ecosystem service value functions (ESVf) indicated an overall increasing trend across the study period. Regulating services particularly water regulation and water supply were dominant, reaching maximum values of US$399.33 thousand and US$156.33 thousand, respectively, between 1994 and 2023. In contrast, provisioning services such as food production, raw materials, and soil formation showed declining trends, while cultural services consistently recorded the lowest values. Overall, the Dinki Watershed provided a total ecosystem service value of US$800.11 thousand, with the highest annual value observed in 2023 (US$310.33 thousand). Among the major categories, regulating services contributed the largest share (US$509.14 thousand), followed by provisioning services (US$228.88 thousand), while supporting services (US$51.70 thousand) and cultural services (US$6.07 thousand) contributed relatively smaller shares (Table 8). The increase in regulating services is largely associated with the expansion of slight vegetation and improved watershed management practices that enhanced ecosystem regulation functions in the watershed.

Dynamics of ecosystem service values in the Dinki Watershed (1994–2023)

The analysis of ecosystem service value (ESVs) revealed a substantial overall increase across the three assessment periods: 1994–2014, 2014–2023, and the entire study period (1994–2023). In the first period (1994–2014), the total ESV increased by approximately US$89.14 thousand, accounting for 40.51% of the 1994 baseline value. However, agricultural land, dense forest, and grazing land showed declines in ESVs of US$0.57, 9.28, and 3.43 thousand, respectively. In the second period (2014–2023), the total ESV rose by an additional US$20.87 thousand, equivalent to a 9.49% increase. Cumulatively, over the entire study period (1994–2023), the ESV increased by US$119.01 thousand, reflecting a 50% gain relative to the 1994 baseline, about 10% higher than the increase observed in the first period.

Except for agricultural land, dense forest, and built-up land, all other land-use categories exhibited notable increases in ESVs throughout the study period (Fig. 7). Grazing land in particular recorded a significant rise of approximately US$19.82 thousand. Discussion

The results show substantial land use and land cover (LULC) changes in the Dinki Watershed from 1994 to 2023, indicating significant ecological transformation. Agricultural land, slight vegetation, and water bodies remained the dominant landscape components throughout the study period. Similar trends have been reported in the Blue Nile headwaters, where grazing land and sparse vegetation expanded due to livestock pressure, deforestation, and climate variability Ewunetu et al.44. Although built-up areas declined there due to settlement concentration along road networks. In the Dinki Watershed, agricultural land dominated in 1994 and 2014 (40–42%) (Table 6) but declined by 2023. While agricultural land expansion has been widely reported in other parts of Ethiopia44, Mehari et al., 2023), the decline in Dinki may reflect local land use transitions, including the relocation of scattered households to nearby village centers such as Zego, along with natural regeneration and conversion of some agricultural land into slight vegetation due to land management changes. Similar cropland reductions associated with LULC transitions have also been reported in Ethiopian highland watersheds15,45,46.

In the Dinki Watershed, cropland decline was primarily associated with the expansion of sparse vegetation, water bodies, and grazing land (Table 6). The establishment of man-made plantation forests has also contributed substantially to cropland reduction. Similar dynamics have been observed elsewhere in Ethiopia, including the replacement of cropland with Acacia decurrens plantations in Fagita-Lekoma District47, and the Chacha Watershed15, as well as with Eucalyptus plantations in the Blue Nile highlands48. Furthermore, the widening of riverbeds and gully expansion, corresponding to a 7.8% increase in water bodies between 1994 and 2023, have exacerbated the degradation and loss of cultivated land, corroborating earlier studies in other parts of Ethiopia49–51.

Key informants and focus group discussion participants emphasized that the expansion of agricultural land between 1994 and 2014 was primarily driven by land scarcity, particularly in marginal areas, and facilitated through traditional practices such as shifting cultivation, whereby forested areas are temporarily cleared and subsequently abandoned once soil fertility declines. In the period 2014–2023, however, declining land productivity, attributed to limited use of external inputs, the expansion of water bodies, and riverbed alterations, and generally poor resource management, compelled farmers to further expand cropland in an effort to compensate for yield losses. These insights underscore the interplay of biophysical processes and socioeconomic pressures in shaping LULC dynamics within the watershed.

Sparse vegetation remained the second most extensive land cover across the study period, with its expansion likely driven by abandonment of marginal croplands, natural regeneration, and reforestation and land conservation initiatives (Table 6; Fig. 5). Similar patterns have been documented elsewhere in Ethiopia. For instance, Taye et al.52 reported an increase in bushlands from 51.35 to 54% in the Derashe District between 1988 and 1998. Within parts of the Dinki Watershed, the expansion of open bushlands into adjacent forest areas was also observed, indicating processes reported in the Fagita-Lekoma District, where plantation forests expanded at the expense of natural forests and bare lands53. Nonetheless, unmanaged grazing remains a major driver of land degradation in Ethiopia, particularly contributing to forest degradation9,12.

Key informants reported that areas of the Ankober district, including villages within the Dinki Watershed, formerly dominated by sparse vegetation, are now showing ecological recovery. This regeneration reflects the expansion of sparse vegetation into previously degraded lands, a trend also supported by focus group discussions. Over the past three decades, gains in sparse vegetation contrast with the dramatic reduction in forest cover, which declined from 14.5% in 1994/1984 to 4.8% in 2022, highlighting significant ecological and socioeconomic pressures. Similar forest loss patterns have been reported in central Ethiopia (East Shewa), Bale-ecoregion (Agarfa District), and Southwestern Ethiopia (Yayo Bioreserve) due to agricultural expansion, fuelwood extraction, and settlement growth24,54,55. Comparable trends are observed in East Africa, including the Mau River Basin and highlands of Kenya, driven by smallholder encroachment, charcoal production, and land tenure pressures56–58). These findings indicate that the Dinki Watershed reflects broader regional land cover dynamics shaped by intertwined ecological and socioeconomic processes.

Dense forest in the Dinki Watershed steadily declined between 1994 and 2023, mainly due to conversion to cropland and grazing land, especially during 1994–2014 when weak law enforcement and settlement expansion accelerated deforestation. Similar forest-to-farmland transitions have been reported in the Wof-Washa landscape and other Ethiopian highlands59,60. Urban expansion around Gorobela, population growth in Ankober District75, and increased demand for housing, grazing, and fuel wood further intensified forest loss. Low-productivity lands were often converted into plantations rather than restored, a trend observed elsewhere in Ethiopia15,53 and internationally22. Key informants and FGD participants confirmed that demand for forest resources has been a major driver of forest conversion in the watershed.

Grazing land expanded markedly in the Dinki Watershed between 1994 and 2023, primarily through the conversion of previously degraded cropland and bare lands. The extremely low grazing land extent observed in 2014 (0.44 km2) reflects the dominance of bare and sparsely vegetated areas at that time, which were later rehabilitated through watershed restoration programs and natural vegetation regeneration. As these degraded lands recovered, many areas became suitable for livestock grazing and were subsequently used as grazing land by local communities. Key informant interviews and focus group discussions confirmed that environmental rehabilitation initiatives, reduced cultivation pressure, and the migration of livestock-owning households toward nearby settlements (e.g., Zego) contributed to the expansion of grazing areas. Similar land-use transitions from degraded or abandoned croplands to grazing land have been documented in several Ethiopian highland watershed29,30,55,61.

Similar to sparse vegetation, water bodies mainly headwater streams expanded in the Dinki Watershed over the study period, showing a net increase of 7.89%. This change is likely linked to land rehabilitation and reforestation initiatives, together with climatic variability that enhanced rainfall and altered hydrological responses and the farmers blocking streams for small-scale irrigation. Comparable hydrological sensitivities to LULC change have been reported in other Ethiopian catchments, including the Lake Tana sub-basin and the Akaki catchment, where land cover transitions affected stream flow, runoff, and infiltration processes62,63. Insights from key informant interviews and focus group discussions further emphasized strong cultural linkages between forest cover, rainfall generation, and stream persistence, reflecting local perceptions of environmental change64.

The total Ecosystem service value (ESV) assessment revealed pronounced shifts over the study period. Water bodies were the dominant contributors due to their high regulating and provisioning service coefficients, while sparse vegetation and grazing land contributed steadily. Agricultural land showed a temporary rise before declining, and dense forests gradually decreased20. The observed increase in ESV also reflects local soil and water conservation practices and aligns with trends reported in other Ethiopian watersheds Bilate Catchment (Kuma et al.18), Gena District40, and other like15,21,34,65–68.

Participants reported that Green Legacy activities, initiated in 2019 and implemented around Gedamoch, Dense, Zego, and Dinki areas, have increased attention toward ecosystem restoration and service enhancement rather than degradation alone. These perceptions suggest that recent increases in vegetation-related ecosystem services observed between 2014 and 2023 may partly reflect the influence of post-2019 restoration efforts, while changes prior to 2014 are likely driven by longer-term land-use dynamics and other socio-environmental factors.

Among the 17 assessed ecosystem service indicators, water regulation, water supply, and water treatment were the dominant functions. Overall, ESVs of major service categories ranked as regulating > provisioning > supporting > cultural (Table 8). Regulating services showed the largest gain over the study period, primarily due to the expansion of water bodies and sparse vegetation. Provisioning, supporting, and cultural services increased modestly, reflecting improvements in water supply and land management. Contributions of water bodies, sparse vegetation, and grazing land to total ESVs underscore the important role of LULC changes in enhancing ecosystem services15,65. However, uncertainties in ESV coefficients and potential classification errors may affect the absolute estimates. Across all assessment periods, water bodies exhibited the highest coefficient of sensitivity, whereas grazing land consistently recorded the lowest values. CS analysis indicated that water bodies and sparse vegetation had the largest influence on total ESV, while grazing land had the least. Values of CS < 1 suggest that changes in these coefficients would have relatively limited effects on total ESV, highlighting the relative influence of each LULC type rather than absolute stability of the valuation.

Key informant interviews and focus group discussions further identified several major driving forces of LULC changes in the watershed, including traditional agricultural practices, deforestation, overgrazing, population pressure, and local economic activities. These drivers were reported to exacerbate soil erosion and biodiversity loss, consistent with findings from earlier studies64,69. Similarly, Yesuph and Degnew70 reported that such factors are common drivers of LUC change in comparable contexts. Understanding these dynamics is therefore essential for designing context-specific interventions to mitigate land degradation and foster sustainable land management practices9,29,30,66. Local knowledge systems have played a pivotal role in improving watershed conditions in the Dinki watershed, complemented by the coordinated efforts of government institutions, non-governmental organizations, and local communities practices71. The adoption of advanced agricultural technologies and improved land use practices has further enhanced productivity while maintaining ecological balance. Recent studies highlight the critical importance of integrating local knowledge systems with modern conservation approaches to achieve sustainable watershed management29,30,61,64.

Conclusion

Land use and land cover change and its impacts on ecosystem services remain a critical challenge in the Dinki Watershed. Over the past three decades (1994–2023), this study identified six major LULC classes and revealed notable shifts in their distribution. Between 1994 and 2014, agricultural land expanded at the expense of dense forests and grazing lands, driven largely by community demand for farmland. However, from 2014 to 2023, this trend reversed, with cropland declining while grazing land and settlements expanded. Overall, sparse vegetation, water bodies, and grazing lands exhibited increasing trends, whereas dense forests, croplands, and built-up areas declined.

These LULC dynamics had significant implications for ecosystem service values (ESVs). In particular, increases in sparse vegetation and water bodies contributed to a net positive change in total ESVs, partially offsetting the losses from cropland and dense forests. To ensure sustainable watershed management, it is recommended to adopt integrated land use strategies that promote sustainable agriculture, reforestation, and livelihood diversification, while simultaneously enhancing community participation and policy coordination. Such interventions are crucial for restoring degraded ecosystems, strengthening resilience, and safeguarding both ecological integrity and socioeconomic benefits for future generations.
