A LiDAR-based machine vision dataset for online volume measurement of sweetpotatoes
Authors: Jiaming Zhang, Yuzhen Lu, Zhikun Kong, Jiajun Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Volume is an important shape descriptor in postharvest quality evaluation and breeding programs of sweetpotatoes and is also valuable for other agricultural engineering applications. Traditional volume measurement methods based on water displacement are, however, laborious, destructive, and unsuitable for high-throughput online scenarios. To address this gap, this dataset was developed to support the advancement of non-destructive, automated online volume estimation using a LiDAR (light detection and ranging)-based three-dimensional (3-D) machine vision system. A total of 200 sweetpotato storage roots of the cultivar “Beauregard” were collected for constructing a 3-D multi-view imagery dataset. Each sample was imaged online using a short-range LiDAR camera (Intel RealSense™ L515) while traveling on a custom-built roller conveyor system that enables simultaneous translation and rotation for full-surface coverage. The curated dataset comprises raw color images (1280 × 720 pixels, .png format) and corresponding raw and segmented point clouds (1280 × 720 pixels, .laz format) for individual samples, alongside the reference volume measurements obtained using the standard water displacement method. In addition, to illustrate the modeling pipeline for volume prediction, the dataset provides the extracted geometric features derived from the segmented two-dimensional (2-D) masks and point clouds, and volume prediction results obtained through regression modeling. As the first publicly available LiDAR-based dataset for sweetpotato volume estimation, this dataset provides a valuable resource for developing and validating image processing pipelines, optimizing machine learning models, and advancing 3-D vision technologies for non-destructive, rapid measurement of the volume of irregularly shaped agricultural products.

Background

Sweetpotato is the seventh most important food crop worldwide. As a high-value vegetable crop, approximately 157,500 acres of sweetpotatoes were planted in the United States in 2025, generating a farmgate value approaching $800 million [1]. Sweetpotato volume is a key metric of root size and an essential parameter in postharvest grading. It is also an important attribute for shape characterization and is useful for sweetpotato phenotyping and breeding programs, as well as other agricultural engineering applications (e.g., postharvest handling machinery design). Traditionally, the volume of horticultural products is measured manually using water displacement methods [2,3], which are tedious, time-consuming, and destructive, making the methods unsuitable for automated, online, high-throughput applications, particularly for postharvest grading and phenotyping of large quantities of storage roots. Moreover, developing AI-driven automated systems from such data can shift workers from repetitive manual tasks to innovative roles, thereby reducing errors and expenses [14]. Previous imaging-based approaches for food volume estimation required manual sample positioning to obtain multi-view images [4,5]. The emergence of short-range LiDAR-based 3-D imaging technology, which performs range measurements using time-of-flight (ToF) laser pulses, creates opportunities for rapid, nondestructive shape measurement and volume estimation of agricultural products. Although LiDAR technology has been increasingly applied in production agriculture and plant phenotyping applications [6,7], its use in postharvest measurement and quality assessment of agricultural and food products remains limited. Acquired using a short-range LiDAR camera-based machine vision system, the present dataset addresses this gap by supporting the development of automated, online volume estimation methodologies for sweetpotatoes. Furthermore, it provides a reusable resource for advancing 3-D imaging and machine learning approaches for postharvest shape measurement and quality assessment of horticultural products and related applications.

Data Description

Upon extracting the dataset (“SP_3D_Dataset.zip”), the directory is organized into three primary functional folders: “raw_PointClouds”, which contains the raw point cloud data captured by camera; “segmented_PointClouds”, which contains the processed point clouds after segmentation and filtering; “selected_Images”, which stores the synchronized RGB (red-green-blue) frames, and a ground-truth volume measurement file (sweetpotato_volume_ground-truth.xlsx), as shown in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Folder structure of the dataset.Fig 1 dummy alt text

Folder structure of the dataset.

The dataset follows a consistent hierarchical structure across all directories. Each primary folder contains 200 subfolders, corresponding to specific sweetpotato samples labeled from SampleID_001 to SampleID_200. Within these subfolders, data files are stored using a standardized naming convention: SampleID_[ID]_FrameID_[ID].[ext], where [ext] corresponds to the data type (e.g., .png for color images in the selected_Images directory, and .laz for point cloud data in the point cloud directories). A Python script is provided as supplementary material to traverse the directory structure and load the synchronized 2-D/3-D data. To ensure data relevance, the raw sequences were filtered to remove frames containing only the background. The dataset retains only the frames where the sweetpotato is visible, starting from its first appearance in the field of view until it completely exits. The original frame indices from the raw recording sequence were preserved in the filenames to maintain temporal consistency. As a result of this selection process, >95 % of the samples contain at least 20 valid frames, as illustrated in Fig. 2, which is sufficient to capture the full surfaces of each sweetpotato.Fig. 2Distribution of valid frames per sample.Fig 2 dummy alt text

Distribution of valid frames per sample.

The full dataset (unzipped) occupies approximately 15.5 GB of storage space. The root directory also contains a ground-truth file, sweetpotato_volume_ground-truth.xlsx, which provides the reference physical measurements (weight and volume) for individual sweetpotato samples, which can be used alongside the image data for algorithm development and validation. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Sample preparation

A set of 200 sweetpotatoes used in this study were storage roots of the cultivar “Beauregard”, one of the dominant varieties in the U.S. sweetpotato producing regions. This cultivar is characterized by smooth, rosy outer skin and deep orange flesh that is moist and sweet, and is known for its resistance to diseases. The storage roots were mechanically harvested from a research farm in Mississippi, USA. Damaged or bruised samples were discarded prior to data collection, and the remaining samples were washed, air-dried, uniquely numbered, and stored at ambient temperature prior to imaging and reference measurement. Additional details regarding sample preparation and handling are provided in the related research article [8].

Imaging system

A custom-assembled roller-conveyor-based machine vision system, as shown in Fig. 3, was used for sweetpotato imaging. This conveyor was designed to enable simultaneous translation and rotation, allowing full-surface imaging of samples for automated sweetpotato quality and sorting [9,10]. The vision system integrates three core modules, i.e., a roller conveyor for sample handling, a LiDAR sensor for acquiring color and depth images, and a control unit for synchronized data acquisition.Fig. 3Schematic (left) and photograph (right) of the machine vision system used for online sweetpotato volume estimation.Fig 3 dummy alt text

Schematic (left) and photograph (right) of the machine vision system used for online sweetpotato volume estimation.

The custom roller conveyor (1.2 m × 0.6 m × 0.9 m frame) consists of a closed-loop array of parallel aluminium tubes (32 mm outer diameter, 550 mm length) spaced at 6 mm intervals, each fitted with a steel spur gear (34 mm outer diameter, 20 pitch, 25 teeth) and insulated with 9 mm-thick rubber foam. The transmission of the conveyor is achieved through a chain-gear-rack mechanism that enables simultaneous sample translation and sample rotation, driven by a 90 V DC gearmotor (McMaster-Carr, Aurora, OH, USA). The roller conveyor eliminates the need for manual positioning for imaging different sample surfaces in the prior volume measurement studies [4,5,11] and provides continuous rotation to ensure comprehensive surface coverage, which is critical for accurate 3-D shape characterization. More details on the roller conveyor design are given elsewhere [8]. The conveyor speed is monitored in real time using a magnetic Hall effect sensor interfaced with an Arduino microprocessor.

A LiDAR imaging sensor (L515, RealSense™, Intel, Santa Clara, CA, USA) was selected for sweetpotato imaging, due to its suitability for short-range depth sensing (≥0.25 m), balanced depth accuracy and spatial resolution, and cost-effectiveness [12]. The camera was mounted 0.43 m above the conveyor to capture the full width of the roller bed. Operating on the ToF principle, it emits 860 nm infrared laser pulses and uses internal scanning mechanisms to acquire 24-bit RGB images (1280 × 720 pixels, .png) and 16-bit depth (point cloud) images (1280 × 720 pixels, .ply). Automated data acquisition was implemented using a custom Python script based on the camera software development kit (RealSense SDK 2.0, https://github.com/IntelRealSense/librealsense), enabling synchronized capture of color and depth images at 30 frames per second (fps) under ambient indoor light conditions. Additional details on the camera parameter settings are given elsewhere [13].

Sweetpotatoes were manually loaded one at a time at the initiation point of the roller conveyor to ensure that no more than one sample was present in the imaging scene at any given time. This controlled loading strategy eliminated inter-object occlusion and ensured unambiguous instance segmentation. The conveyor translation speed was set to 10 mm/s during imaging. With rollers of approximately 51 mm outer diameter, the corresponding rotational speed of the rollers was about 0.63 rad/s, equivalent to 6.0 revolutions per minute. At this speed, each sample translated only approximately 0.33 mm between consecutive frames. This small inter-frame sample displacement ensured negligible motion-induced image artifacts (e.g., motion blur or depth smearing), dense spatial sampling of surface geometry, and high-fidelity depth acquisition during the microsecond-level integration window of the ToF LiDAR pulses.

Reference volume measurement

Reference volumes were determined using the water displacement method [2,3]. Each sample was first weighed in air using a digital scale with a resolution of ±0.01 g and then fully submerged in a water-filled container using a vertically adjustable sinker rod mounted on a stand to ensure complete immersion. The container with the submerged samples was weighed again, and the sample volume was calculated from the resulting weight difference of displaced water. Data processing

To prepare the dataset for volume estimation, preprocessing of the acquired color images (data in the “selected_Images” folder) is needed to segment the sweetpotato from the background. As illustrated in Fig. 5, the binary mask was generated using hue-saturation-value thresholding (Tmin=[7,40,120],Tmax=[20,165,219]), followed by flood-filling and morphological operations. This process generates a refined mask that isolates the sample region, serving as a prerequisite for both 2-D geometric feature extraction and the further extraction of the 3-D region of interest described below.Fig. 5Data preprocessing for sweetpotato segmentation. (a) an example original color image, (b) an initial binary mask by thresholding in hue-saturation-value space, and (c) the refined mask through morphological processing.Fig 5 dummy alt text

Data preprocessing for sweetpotato segmentation. (a) an example original color image, (b) an initial binary mask by thresholding in hue-saturation-value space, and (c) the refined mask through morphological processing.

Point clouds were generated by combining spatial coordinates derived from the registered RGB-depth data. For each pixel, 3-D coordinates (X, Y, Z) were computed: X and Y coordinates were derived from the pixel’s position in the image frame, while Z (depth) values were extracted directly from the depth data. These initial point clouds, representing the raw captured by the camera without processing, are stored in the “raw_PointClouds” folder. To extract the sweetpotato data for volume estimation, the 2-D binary masks generated in the previous step were mapped onto the point cloud data. In this process, only the 3-D points corresponding to the foreground pixels (i.e., the sweetpotato) were retained, while all background points were discarded. This extraction process is visually demonstrated in Fig. 6.Fig. 6Illustration of the extraction of the three-dimensional region of interest.Fig 6 dummy alt text

Illustration of the extraction of the three-dimensional region of interest.

It is important to note that the generated point clouds still contained noise artifacts arising from multiple sources, including ambient light reflections and sensor noise. To mitigate these, a statistical outlier removal algorithm was applied. The algorithm calculated the average distance from each point to its 50 nearest neighbours; points whose average distances exceeded a threshold (0.02 times the standard deviation of all average distances) were classified as outliers and removed. This denoising procedure effectively eliminated spurious points while preserving the fine geometric details of the sweetpotato surface. The resulting cleaned point clouds of sweetpotatoes constitute the data in the “segmented_PointClouds” folder of this dataset. Further details regarding point cloud processing (e.g., surface mesh construction) can be found in the related research article [8].

Both RGB images and cleaned point clouds allow researchers to implement the extraction of geometric features for shape analysis and volume prediction modeling. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the provided data support the calculation of various shape descriptors. For example, the 2-D binary masks derived from the images can be used to calculate area, perimeter, radial distances, and major/minor axes, using the segmentation workflow described above, while the 3-D point clouds enable the determination of volumetric and surface attributes, including projected volume and total surface area, through algorithms such as Alpha Shape.Fig. 7Illustration of feature extraction from the two-dimensional mask and the point clouds of a segmented sweetpotato.Fig 7 dummy alt text

Illustration of feature extraction from the two-dimensional mask and the point clouds of a segmented sweetpotato.

It should be noted that utilizing this dataset for volume measurement does not necessitate reconstructing a complete 3-D shape model of the sweetpotato. Instead, as illustrated in Fig. 8, the feature extraction can be operated for individual frames that only capture partial surface geometry. The geometric features are independently extracted from every single frame (Frame 1 to Frame n) as the sweetpotato translates and rotates on the conveyor. Rather than stitching the 3-D point clouds from individual frames, a regression model that aggregates the frame-level multi-view geometric features can be built to directly predict the sweetpotato volume as done in [8] and illustrated below.Fig. 8Geometric feature extraction independently from individual frames (Frame 1 to Frame n), followed by frame-level feature aggregation for a machine learning model for volume prediction.Fig 8 dummy alt text

Regression modeling

To establish a performance benchmark and confirm the validity of the extracted geometric features for volume estimation, a baseline regression modeling workflow was implemented, following the procedure described in [8]. The dataset of 200 sweetpotato samples was partitioned into a training set comprising the first 134 samples and a testing set comprising the remaining 66 samples, with each sample represented by six sampled frames from the multi-view scanning sequence. Eight geometric features were extracted per frame, including five features from the 2-D sweetpotato mask and three features from the segmented point clouds, as done in [8].

Two linear modeling strategies were evaluated: (1) multiple linear regression (MLR), and (2) principal component regression (PCR), implemented as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) followed by MLR (PCA+MLR), to address potential multicollinearity among the geometric features. For the PCR approach, the first five principal components were retained for modeling. Since each sweetpotato sample comprises six frames in the scanning sequence, the model was designed to perform predictions at the frame level. During testing, the six frame-based volume predictions corresponding to the same sample were averaged to produce the final sample-level volume estimate. Detailed implementation information is provided in the “Demo” folder of the published dataset [13].

Both models achieved competitive prediction performance, with the PCR (i.e., PCA+MLR) model demonstrating superior accuracy, achieving an RMSE (root-mean-square error) of 26.30 cm3 (R2=0.958) on the testing set. The results are comparable to those reported in the related study [8]. Fig. 9 illustrates the scatter plots of the measured ground-truth volumes versus the model-predicted values, confirming the validity of the dataset and the modeling methodology. Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged when utilizing this dataset. First, the sample size is restricted to 200 “Beauregard” sweetpotatoes, which may restrict the generalizability of trained models to other varieties with different shape characteristics. Second, the roller conveyor speed (approximately 10 mm/s) used for sweetpotato imaging is relatively slow for high-throughput processing requirements, although the speed choice was to prioritize acquiring high-fidelity imagery with minimal motion blur and depth distortion. Further evaluation of imaging performance under higher-speed conditions remains an important direction for future dedicated investigation. Third, every image of the dataset contains only a single sweetpotato, whereas real-world processing lines or phenotyping tasks often involve multiple sweetpotatoes scanned simultaneously. Fourth, inherent characteristics of the consumer-level LiDAR sensor (RealSense™ L515) used for close-range imaging still exhibit inherent limitations, including the “flying pixel” effect at object boundaries and sensitivity to surface reflections, which may introduce depth artifacts. However, as demonstrated in our prior validations with deep neural network-based modeling [8], the remaining error margin after filtering is minimal (an RMSE of 9.5 cm3 or approximately 2 % error), indicating that these artifacts do not substantially impact the volume prediction. Although basic filtering was applied to the point clouds, these sensor-specific limitations highlight the need for either advanced point cloud restoration methods or the use of higher-precision 3-D sensors for applications requiring sub-millimetre depth accuracy. Finally, imaging was conducted under controlled ambient indoor light conditions, and the dataset’s performance in complex outdoor environments remains untested, potentially limiting its applicability in field-based imaging scenarios.
