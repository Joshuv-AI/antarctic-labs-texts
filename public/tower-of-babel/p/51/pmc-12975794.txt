Can the size of adrenal tumor impact hormonal secretion? A retrospective analysis of 170 adrenalectomies in a single center
Authors: Romina Frey, Alberto Posabella, Sara Hosner, Antonio Fiore, Alexander Wilhelm, Sophie Eschlböck, Raoul Droeser, Tarik Delko, Ioannis I. Lazaridis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAdrenal tumors are a heterogeneous group of neoplasms that may be hormonally active or inactive. Tumor size is a key factor in evaluating and managing these lesions. Understanding how size impacts hormonal activity, surgical outcomes, and complications can help guide treatment and follow-up. This study investigates the relationship between tumor size, hormonal secretion, and clinical outcomes in adrenal tumors.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed data from patients who underwent adrenalectomy between 2007 and 2023 at the University Hospital of Basel. Tumors were categorized by pathology and stratified into three size groups based on histology results.ResultsA total of 170 patients who underwent adrenalectomy for adrenal tumors were analyzed. The most common adrenal pathologies were Conn`s syndrome followed by pheochromocytoma, Cushing syndrome and incidentalomas. Hormone-producing tumors were present in 61.2% of cases.Larger tumors (> 4 cm) were associated with longer operative times (p = 0.03). When tumor size was analyzed in relation to hormone production, small functional tumors (< 2 cm) were most commonly linked to Conn’s syndrome. Cushing syndrome was most prevalent among patients with tumors measuring between 2.1 and 4 cm. There were no significant differences in perioperative complication rates across the tumor size groups.ConclusionOur results showed that tumor size was associated with both operative complexity and functional status. Larger tumors were linked to longer operative times. Smaller tumors were predominantly associated with Conn`s syndrome, while tumors of intermediate size were most often related to Cushing syndrome. These findings highlight the relevance of tumor size in preoperative planning and functional assessment.

Introduction

Adrenal tumors include a wide range of pathologies, including pheochromocytomas, aldosterone-producing adenomas (Conn`s syndrome), cortisol-secreting adenomas (Cushing syndrome), adrenocortical carcinomas, adrenal metastases, and incidentalomas. These neoplasms can be either hormonally active or inactive, with clinical manifestations largely determined by the type and amount of hormone secreted. The management of adrenal tumors is complex and requires a multidisciplinary approach involving endocrinologists, surgeons, radiologists, and pathologists [1, 2].

Among the various parameters considered in the evaluation and management of adrenal masses, tumor size is particularly critical [1–4]. It significantly influences diagnostic decisions, treatment strategies and patient outcomes [1–4]. Larger tumors are generally associated with a higher risk of malignancy and may exhibit different hormonal profiles compared to smaller lesions [1, 2, 5–7]. For instance, in pheochromocytomas, levels of plasma or urinary metanephrines correlate with tumor diameter, suggesting that larger tumors may secrete greater amounts of catecholamines [8, 9].

Understanding the relationship between tumor size and pathology-specific outcomes is essential for optimizing clinical management. Tumor size may impact hormonal activity, perioperative and postoperative complication rates, and long-term outcomes, thereby influencing surgical planning and follow-up strategies [3, 4, 10, 11]. Larger tumors may present technical challenges during adrenalectomy and carry a higher perioperative risk. Furthermore, the increased potential for malignancy necessitates thorough pathological evaluation and may dictate the extent of surgical resection [12].

Despite advances in imaging and hormonal testing, significant variability in hormone production among tumors of similar size—particularly the presence of hormonal hypersecretion in small tumors—suggests that other factors also contribute to clinical behavior [11, 13]. Therefore, comprehensive studies investigating the interplay between tumor size, hormonal secretion, and clinical outcomes are crucial. Such insights could support a more personalized approach to management, improving prognostication and therapeutic strategies for patients with adrenal tumors [14].

This study aims to clarify the relationship between tumor size, hormone secretion, and clinical outcomes in adrenal tumors, ultimately guiding clinical decision-making and optimizing care for patients undergoing adrenalectomy.

Methods

We retrospectively reviewed demographic, biochemical, perioperative and postoperative data from all consecutive patients who underwent total adrenalectomy between 2007 and 2023 at the University Hospital of Basel in Basel, Switzerland. Patients were categorized based on tumor pathology [pheochromocytoma, Cushing syndrome, Conn`s syndrome, adrenal metastases, incidentalomas, adrenal carcinoma, and myelolipomas] and stratified into three different groups based on maximal diameter according to the histopathological examination [Group 1: 0.1–2 cm, Group 2: 2.1–4 cm, Group 3: >4 cm].

Collected data include baseline demographic characteristics [sex, age, Body Mass Index - BMI, ASA classification, comorbidities], preoperative hormonal markers, perioperative parameters [surgical approach (transabdominal/retroperitoneal, open/minimal invasive), operative time, blood loss, intraoperative complications [Classintra Classification], and postoperative outcomes [complications, recurrence, adrenal insufficiency, and follow-up hormonal markers].

Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation [SD]. Differences between groups were analyzed using an independent t- .test and one-way ANOVA, with statistical significance set at p value < 0.05. Pearson correlation coefficients were computed to explore relationships between variables.

Perioperative Management: All patients with pheochromocytoma in our cohort received preoperative alpha-adrenergic blockade according to our institutional protocol. Beta-blockade was added only when indicated (e.g., persistent tachycardia or arrhythmia) and always after adequate alpha-blockade was established.

Intraoperative workflow: In all pheochromocytomas, the adrenal vein was deliberately identified early, carefully mobilized, and clipped or ligated as an initial key step whenever anatomically feasible, before relevant tumor manipulation. Throughout the dissection, a strict “no-touch” principle was applied. We minimized direct grasping of the tumor, used traction on surrounding fat and adrenal tissue rather than on the lesion, avoided excessive compression and maintained low manipulation until venous control was achieved.

Throughout the study period preoperative medical preparation strategy remained consistent. Decision making regarding surgical approach was influenced by tumor characteristics and perceived. Laparoscopic transperitoneal adrenalectomy was our preferred approach when feasible, while open adrenalectomy was selected for cases with larger tumor size and or anticipated technical complexity.

Approval for this study was obtained from the research ethics committee of nordwest- and central Switzerland [EZNK: Nr. 2022 − 01944].

Results

Baseline characteristics and perioperative outcomes

A total of 170 patients who underwent adrenalectomy for adrenal tumors were included. The mean age was 55 (SD13) years with a prevalence of female patients of 52.4% as shown in Table 1. The most prevalent preoperative comorbidity was hypertension, which was present in 89 patients (52.4%), followed by type 2 diabetes mellitus (n = 61, 35.9%). Hormonal activity including levels of free metanephrine/normetanephrine, cortisol, aldosterone and aldosterone-to-renin ratio are summarized in Table 2. Tumors were primarily localized unilaterally (n = 97, 57.1% left, n = 66, 38.8% right), while 4.1% were bilateral. The most common adrenal pathologies were Conn`s syndrome (n = 43, 25.3%), followed by pheochromocytoma (n = 29, 17%), Cushing syndrome (n = 26, 15.3%) and incidentalomas (n = 15, 8.8%). Hormone-producing tumors were present in 61.2% of cases. All diagnoses and their frequency are shown in Table 3.

Etiology

The majority of the cases were performed in transabdominal laparoscopic technique (n = 149, 87.6%), while open adrenalectomy was performed in 16 patients (9.5%) and retroperitoneal approach only in 5 patients (2.9%). Intraoperative surgical complications occurred in 11 patients (6.5%), including 6 vascular (3.5%), 1 hepatic (0.6%), and 3 splenic lesions (1.8%). Postoperative complications were seen in 20 patients (11.8%), including 2 patients with bleeding (1.2%), 2 patients with wound infection (1.2%), 2 with other infections (1.2%) and one patient with postoperative ileus (0.6%). Reoperation during the same hospital stay was performed in 2 patients (1.2%), while readmission within 30 days occurred in 1 patient (0.6%). Recurrence was observed in 9 (5.3%) patients, with 2 patients (1.2%) requiring reoperation. Steroid prophylaxis was administered in 29 (17%) patients, mainly with intravenous hydrocortisone (13.5%). Postoperative steroid prophylaxis was used in 54 patients (31.8%) for a median duration of 5 days. Adrenal failure was diagnosed in 7 patients (4.1%). Comparisons based on tumor volume

Patients with larger tumors (Group 3, > 4.1 cm, n = 60) had significantly longer operation time (119.7 ± 49.8 min, p = 0.03) than those with smaller tumors (Group 1, 0.1–2 cm, n = 52) (Table 5; Fig. 1). There was a tendency for greater intraoperative blood loss (p = 0.14) and a higher conversion rate to open surgery for larger tumors (5% conversion rate in larger tumors, no conversion in the smallest tumors, p = 0.14), but these differences were not statistically significant. Intraoperative hypertensive crises occurred in 6.6% of patients with tumors > 4.1 cm, compared to 7% in 2.1–4 cm tumors and none in 0.1–2 cm tumors (p = 0.16).

Discussion

Our data demonstrate that patients with adrenal tumors smaller than 2 cm had a higher incidence of Conn`s syndrome and elevated preoperative aldosterone levels compared with those whose tumors were larger than 2 cm. Conversely, tumors greater than 4 cm were more often pheochromocytomas, although this difference did not reach statistical significance. There was also a non-significant trend toward higher secretion of catecholamine metabolites (free normetanephrines, metanephrines, and methoxytyramines) in these larger lesions. Cushing syndrome was most prevalent in the 2.1–4 cm subgroup, which also exhibited the highest preoperative cortisol levels. Additionally, in our cohort, tumors exceeding 2 cm were associated with significantly longer operative times compared with smaller tumors. Similarly, in a retrospective study of 42 patients, Giacchetti et al. [15] demonstrated that patients with small adenomas (diameter < 20 mm) exhibited higher post-saline aldosterone values and lower serum potassium levels compared to patients with adenomas > 20 mm. Moreover, in a retrospective study of 40 patients, Ono et al. [16] showed that small adenomas could produce sufficient aldosterone to cause clinically overt primary aldosteronism due to significantly higher aldosterone synthase expression per tumor area. Although the tumor area of larger adenomas was nine times greater than that of smaller adenomas, plasma aldosterone concentrations were only 2.0–2.5 times higher in patients with larger aldosterone-producing adenomas. This suggests that aldosterone production per cell was much greater in smaller adenomas. Contradictory results were shown by Karashima et al. [6]. They compared in a retrospective study with 55 patients the clinical characteristics of micro- and macroadenomas and found no difference in the amount of aldosterone production. Nevertheless, patients with microadenomas had significantly higher diastolic blood pressure values than those with macroadenomas. Also in a retrospective cross-sectional study including 640 patients from 6 German centers, Born-Frontsberg et al. [17] found no significant difference in hormone production between patients with Conn adenoma with tumors < 20 mm and > 20 mm.

Several other studies show a positive correlation between tumor size and the level of catecholamine metabolites in plasma [18–21].

Eisenhofer G. et al. conducted a prospective, multicenter study [8] and a literature review [22] in which they interpreted the positive correlation between tumor size and level of catecholamine metabolites through the continuous production of metabolites in the tumor cell cytoplasm, which depends on passive leakage of catecholamines from vesicular stores, the size of which relates to tumor burden. Due to the metabolism of catecholamines within tumors and the independence of this process from catecholamine release, urinary excretion or plasma concentrations of metanephrines show strong positive correlations with tumor size.

In our study, comparing patients with pheochromocytoma, Cushing and Conn`s syndrome, intraoperative hypertensive crisis occurred only in patients with pheochromocytoma and more often in patients with larger tumors. Furthermore, hypotensive crisis occurred predominantly in patients with pheochromocytoma. The cause and treatment of hypotensive and hypertensive crises in pheochromocytoma are well known and established. It has been argued that catecholamines (noradrenaline is the most common) released from the tumor cause vasoconstriction, hence patients with pheochromocytoma usually have contracted intravascular volume despite being hypertensive. Peritoneal insufflation during laparoscopic resection and tumor manipulation during surgery can cause an abrupt release of catecholamines, resulting in severe hypertension and tachycardia [23].On the other hand, a precipitous drop in blood pressure may occur after taking down the adrenal tumors` blood supply. This occurs due to the sudden drop in endogenous catecholamine levels in the setting of chronic downregulation of alpha-adrenergic receptors in patients with pheochromocytoma [23].

Aydin et al. included 298 patients with adrenal tumors. In the subgroup analysis a positive correlation was found between mass size and blood loss in patients with pheochromocytoma [4].

Our data are in accordance with the results of the study of Kai Duan et al. [24], who showed that adrenocortical adenomas in Cushing Syndrome typically range from 1.5 cm to 6 cm in size. However, the relationship between tumor size and cortisol production in subclinical Cushing syndrome (sCS) is controversial. Both Kai Duan et al. [24] and Davide Calebiro et al. [25] hypothesized in a review article that in cortisol-producing adrenocortical adenomas, the amount of hormone production does not independently correlate with the tumor size. Regarding subclinical Cushing syndrome, Yener et al. conducted a prospective study with 150 patients and found that larger tumor size at diagnosis and longer follow-up duration were associated with its development [13]. Additionally, sCSwas more frequently observed in growing tumors, suggesting that glucocorticoid hypersecretion might be linked to an increase in tumor mass [13]. Similarly, Vassilatou et al. reported in a prospective, long- term follow- up study of 77 patients that some adrenal incidentalomas exhibit a growth tendency over time, occasionally leading to overt hyperfunction [26]. On the other hand, in a systematic review and meta-analysis Paschou et al. indicated that tumor size cannot be the only causative factor for the difference in hormone production; they suggested that the bilateral or unilateral occurrence of adrenal tumors has a greater influence on the level of hormone production rather than the tumor volume [7].

In our data, no correlation between adrenocortical carcinoma and tumor size was shown. It is widely agreed that a tumor size greater than 4 cm, or greater than 5 cm, is associated with an increased risk of malignancy [2, 20]. Grumbach et al. and Rockall et al. noted that adrenocortical carcinomas (ACCs) are generally larger than adenomas, with approximately 25% of masses greater than 6 cm in diameter being ACCs [27, 28]. However, a meta-analysis from Seccia et al. [29] revealed that even very small tumors could be malignant, as 9% of all cases were smaller than 3 cm.

This study has certain limitations, which must be addressed. These include the potential for bias inherent to its retrospective, single-center design. Furthermore, variations in patient characteristics may limit the generalizability of our findings to broader populations. The relatively small number of patients in certain subgroups, such as those with pheochromocytomas or adrenal carcinomas, may have reduced the statistical power to detect significant differences, particularly regarding hormonal activity and perioperative outcomes. However, our study highlights the need for further analysis and expanded databases, particularly for rare adrenal neoplasms. At our institution, preoperative investigations have consistently been performed in accordance with guidelines. As our study is a retrospective analysis spanning 2007 to 2023, access to all preoperative hormonal test results—especially for patients classified as non-hormone-producing—was unfortunately not uniformly available. Another limitation lies in the comparison of outcomes between laparoscopic and open surgical techniques due to selection bias. A primary open approach was particularly chosen for large tumors or cases anticipating a complex intraoperative setting. Although perioperative management and the principles of intraoperative strategy remained consistent throughout the study period, variations in surgical techniques over the 16-year period may have introduced variability in outcomes.Finally, as a retrospective analysis, the study cannot establish causality, and unmeasured confounders may have influenced the associations observed. Nevertheless, this cohort of 170 patients provides valuable insight into the relationship between adrenal tumor size, hormonal activity, and perioperative outcomes.

Conclusion

Our study found that adrenal tumors smaller than 2 cm were significantly more likely to present as Conn`s syndrome, whereas tumors larger than 4 cm were more frequently pheochromocytomas, although this association did not reach statistical significance. Tumors of intermediate size (2.1–4 cm) were most associated with Cushing syndrome. Additionally, tumors exceeding 2 cm were linked to longer operative times, underscoring the impact of tumor size on surgical complexity. These findings suggest that maximal tumor size can serve as a useful initial parameter in the evaluation of adrenal masses. Validation in larger, multicenter cohorts is needed, but integrating tumor size into diagnostic algorithms could improve accuracy and help tailor individual treatment plans for patients with adrenal lesions.
