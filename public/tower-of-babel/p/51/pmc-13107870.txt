A social return on investment analysis of the ENJOY Seniors Exercise Park IMP-ACT project: improving older people’s health through physical activity
Authors: Natasha K Brusco, Marcia Fearn, Keith D Hill, Bronwyn Dreher, Adrian Bauman, Andrew Gilbert, Sze-Ee Soh, Elissa Burton, Rachel Muoio, Pazit Levinger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Community dwelling older adults can improve physical and brain health through physical activity, however there is a scarcity of evidence that has evaluated the value for money of physical activity interventions for this cohort of people [1, 2]. Based on the scarce literature available, recent reviews have consistently reported that physical activity interventions for older people were generally more effective, and more costly, than no intervention [2, 3].

Seniors Exercise Parks are age-friendly active spaces. They involve outdoor exercise equipment that is available in public spaces and has been specifically designed for older adults [4, 5]. The Seniors Exercise Park physical activity program has demonstrated improvements in physical activity and physical function, and in turn, reduced falls and improved balance, exercise tolerance, socialisation and wellbeing [4], [6–10]. Seniors Exercise Parks are different to other outdoors exercise stations often seen in community parks, as they uniquely include a number of exercise stations targeting balance training, that is essential for fall prevention (an important additional target outcome of exercise programs for older people). [10] The initial and only economic evaluation of the Seniors Exercise Parks in Australia was a pre-post study of 50 participants, that took a societal perspective over a 12-month time horizon, and reported that post-intervention there was a reduction in health and social care cost without compromise to quality of life or falls. [11] These findings indicate that Seniors Exercise Parks are likely to be cost-effective compared to no intervention and warrant further economic evaluation. However, to date only a small but growing number of Seniors Exercise Parks are available in community parks in Australia (estimated at approximately 20–25 Seniors Exercise Parks, most in the state of Victoria, and most associated with our research programs) [12].

Social return on investment (SROI) analysis is a type of economic evaluation, derived from the cost-benefit analysis [13]. A SROI measures how an intervention has added value to society [13–15]. A SROI is based on seven principles and these include the involvement of stakeholders, understanding what changes, valuing things that matter, only including what is material, not over-claiming, being transparent, and verifying the result [15]. A recent consensus statement was published specific to SROI analyses in physical activity and sport interventions, and this informed the current project [16].

Implementation of a Seniors Exercise Park requires both creating (infrastructure/capital work) and activating (i.e., running community programs that require leader training, community engagement, resource development and marketing) the park. In the current project, we conducted an economic evaluation to determine the SROI across six Seniors Exercise Parks in Victoria, Australia, where the creation and activation costs, were funded by the local governments as well as supported by research funding [17]. It was hypothesised that the reduction in social and health care costs observed in the initial economic evaluation of the Seniors Exercise Park, would be replicated in the current project and demonstrate a positive SROI. The objective of the current project was to establish the cost of implementation, as well as the SROI, when scaling the Seniors Exercise Park across multiple local government areas.

Methods

This economic evaluation was conducted as part of a larger hybrid type II implementation study, and has been reported according to Consolidated Health Economics Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) [18]. The assumptions that have informed the economic modelling in this SROI have been detailed in Supplementary File 1. Participants provided verbal informed consent. The project was approved by the Monash University Human Research Ethics Committee, Melbourne Australia (Project ID: 35502), and the project was prospectively registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (trial number ACTRN12622001256763; date registered 20/09/2022).

Participants

There were two groups of participants that informed the SROI analysis. The first were community dwelling older adults (aged 60 years and over) who attended the Seniors Exercise Park space at the local parks and completed on-site intercept surveys in their visit that include sets of questions and questionnaires (who did and did not participate or use the Seniors Exercise Park equipment or programs). As we wanted to understand the impact on the local community who were within the area of the park, we deliberately did not separate out those who did and those who did not participate in or use the Seniors Exercise Park equipment or programs. The second were local government council stakeholders who provided data on the Seniors Exercise Park implementation costs.

Setting and location

Six Seniors Exercise Parks, across five local government councils, were included in the study [6]. Supplementary File 2 provides more details as well as the timeline for the six participating Seniors Exercise Park programs.

Intervention implementation

Seniors Exercise Park physical activity programs includes multiple equipment stations that target static and dynamic balance, as well as specific function and movement, and have been well described elsewhere [4–6, 10]. Seniors Exercise Park implementation included both creating and activating the spaces that are available in public parks and have been specifically designed for older adults. Activating the parks used the TERM framework and this included ‘T’ for Training allied health professionals (i.e., accredited exercise physiologist or physiotherapist, who provided extra support for park users), Volunteer Champions (community volunteers trained to support use of Seniors Exercise Parks by other older people), and up to 12 inductions sessions with qualified research staff to support community members at the park; ‘E’ for Engagement with the community (i.e., a Community of Practice committee, peer-led sessions and local events); ‘R’ for Resource development (mix of on-site information such as instructional signage, printed flyers, on-site QR codes, and the “My ENJOY Health®” mobile application); and ‘M’ for Marketing and communication (marketing and promotion efforts by all local governments using social media and other platforms, (frequency and methods varied)) [19].

In the current economic evaluation, there are three key stages to the intervention: Baseline period for creation (3 months): In addition to the cost of capital works, for the installation of the equipment and relevant infrastructure work required for the space, the baseline period included the preparation of resources pertaining to TERM. Intervention period for activation (9 months): The intervention period included actual delivery of the intervention pertaining to TERM, for the initial activation of the Seniors Exercise Park. Maintenance period for ongoing activation (3 months): The maintenance period included ongoing delivery of the intervention pertaining to TERM, however this is where the research team withdraw from any active participation/training as it was all run and managed by councils, and this was to achieve ongoing activation of the Seniors Exercise Park.

Outcomes

In the current economic evaluation, “cost of implementation” refers to all creation and activation costs related to the Senior Exercise Parks across the baseline, intervention and maintenance periods (detailed in the section above). “Social-return” includes the monetary value created by the Seniors Exercise Parks during activation of the space via its impact on participant quality of life, leisure and employment opportunity, and private and government funded social and health care utilisation (via data collected during the intercept surveys).

Data collection and management

The six Seniors Exercise Parks were represented by five local government councils (i.e. municipalities). Information about the cost investment of participating councils was collected via a bespoke Excel template that was sent to the relevant local government staff, which included the positive ageing team (or equivalent), open space and marketing representatives for each Seniors Exercise Park, at various stages during the project. Local government representatives could elect to self-complete the Excel template or meet with the health economist and complete the template via interview. The data from the older adults who visited the Seniors Exercise Park spaces (and/or participated in Seniors Exercise Park programs) was collected via cross-sectional intercept surveys, which involved a research assistant approaching park participants in-person and asking a number of questions.

Data analysis

The SROI was blended with a traditional cost-benefit analysis. Costs included capital works, for the installation of the equipment and relevant infrastructure work required for the space, and Training, Engagement, Resource Development and Marketing resources required to activate the Seniors Exercise Park (including the cost of training for seniors/volunteer champions and allied health professionals, and delivery of come and try sessions), as well as ongoing running and maintenance costs. The cost per participant was based on the total investment cost divided by the number of participants who engaged in Seniors Exercise Park activation activities. The return (benefit) was modelled based on the average difference in reported benefits for participants undertaking intercept surveys prior to, and following, activation of the Seniors Exercise Parks; benefits included the impact on quality of life, leisure and employment opportunity, and private and government funded social and health care utilisation (collected as part of the intercept surveys). Utilisation data and cost data were compared between the baseline survey and the maintenance period survey, using an independent group t-test with the mean difference (95% Confidence Interval (CI)) reported. Data were not adjusted for clustering by site.

Perspective

Investment costs took a local government (municipality) perspective, while the social return took a broader social perspective.

Time horizon

There were three continuous time periods, baseline (pre-intervention; 3 months), intervention (9 months) and maintenance (3 months). Cost of implementation included all three time periods (combined 15 months). Social return was based on the comparison of data in the baseline (3 months) time period and the maintenance time period (3 months). Doing so minimises seasonal effects as it is the same 3-month period, 12 months apart.

Discount rate

Due to the combined 15-month time period, a discount rate was not applied.

Currency, price date and conversion

All costs and benefits were costed at market rate and reported in AUD$2024. Where market rates were not available, economic modelling from previous related data sets and the literature were used. Costs reported prior to the 2024 year were inflated using the Reserve Bank of Australia inflation calculator (https://www.rba.gov.au/calculator/).

Rationale and description of model

The SROI model establishes (a) the cost of implementation of Seniors Exercise Parks over a 15-month period (both creating and activating the park); (b) the social return is based on changes to quality of life, work and activity participation, as well as health and social care utilisation. The model involves applying weights to the social return values [14].

These weights include:

Dead weight, which is an estimation of the value that would have been created if the activities from the program did not happen,

Characterising heterogeneity, distributional effects, and uncertainty

We did not characterise heterogeneity or explore distributional effects with sub-groups or individuals. To characterise uncertainty, we applied weights to the social return values, and these have been described in Table 1.

Consumer engagement

The seven principles of a SROI were used to guide consumer/stakeholder engagement [15]. This included: the involvement of local government council stakeholders; reviewing all previous Senior Exercise Park literature to understand what changes; working with stakeholders to value things that matter and only including what is material; using a published protocol [6] to ensure the SROI is transparent and does not over-claim; as well as verifying the results with the local government council stakeholders.

Results

Participants

Characteristics of the participants who took part in the intercept surveys are presented in Table 4. During the baseline period there were 48 survey participants (n = 29 (60%) who had engaged with the park prior to activation activities), during the intervention period there were 190 survey participants (n = 144 (76%) who had engaged with the park activation activities), and during the maintenance period there were 63 survey participants (n = 43 (68%) who had engaged with the park activation activities). Six-hundred and twenty-seven participants took part in an organised exercise session at the Seniors Exercise Park (e.g., used the Seniors Exercise Equipment for exercise as part of the weekly come and try sessions) during the 9-month intervention period, and 256 participants took part in an organised exercise session at the Seniors Exercise Park during the 3-month maintenance period (combined total n = 883). Across the 15 months, over 55% of intercept survey participants were aged ≥ 70 years, ≥ 60% were female, and ≥ 75% lived locally. Observed differences between the Baseline and Maintenance participants included more Maintenance participants aged 80 + years (Baseline 6% versus Maintenance 19%), greater female representation (Baseline 58% versus Maintenance 73%), and greater local residence (Baseline 71% versus Maintenance 83%).

Main results

Over 15 months the cost of implementation for the six Seniors Exercise Parks was $1,191,704, representing an average cost of $198,617 (Standard Deviation (SD) $86,551) per park (Table 5). The total investment cost ($1,191,704) was then divided by the total number of older people who participated in an organised exercise session at the Seniors Exercise Park over 12 months (n = 883) to give an average investment cost of $1,350 per person. The social return compared participants in the maintenance period (n = 63) to participants in the baseline period (n = 48) and was based on a 3-month period of recall that related to quality of life ($172 value that favoured maintenance participants), leisure activities ($119 value that favoured baseline participants), work ($323 value that favoured maintenance participants), health care ($408 value that favoured maintenance participants) and social care utilisation ($276 value that favoured maintenance participants). A detailed breakdown of the social return quantities and values can be found in Tables 6 and 7. The combined 3-month social return was $1,061 (95% CI −2,135 to 4,256) per participant in the maintenance group when compared to the baseline group, and this was reduced to $848 (95% CI −1,571 to 3,268) when the 20% discount was applied for dead weight, displacement and attribution. When extrapolated to 12-months, the $848 social return increased to $3,394 (95% CI −6,285 to 13,073). This indicates that over the first 12-months of Seniors Exercise Park programs, the per participant social return was $3,394 (95% CI −6,285 to 13,073) and the investment was $1,350, indicating a SROI of $2.50 for every $1.00 spent. As the CIs were large and crossed zero, a negative SROI was possible.

Effect of uncertainty

Applying the 20% discount rate for dead weight (5%), displacement (5%) and attribution (10%) did not alter the direction of the SROI findings, only the magnitude of the findings. Without the 20% discount the extrapolated 12-month social return was $4,242 (95% CI −8,539 to 17,023), indicating a SROI of $3 for every $1 spent.

Effect of consumer engagement

Engagement of local government council stakeholders in the design of the SROI analysis resulted in the inclusion of senior council worker support for the overall governance of the Seniors Exercise Park programs.

Discussion

Over 15-months the cost of implementation for six Seniors Exercise Parks was $1.2 M, representing an average cost of $ 200 K per park. Based on the 883 observed park users who took part in an organised exercise session at the Seniors Exercise Parks, the average investment cost per person was $1.4 K. The social return for participants in the maintenance period, compared to participants in the baseline period, was $3.4 K over 12-months. This indicates that over the first 12-months of Seniors Exercise Park programs, the per participant social return was $2.50 for every $1.00 spent, however the positive SROI needs to be interpreted carefully as the CIs were large and crossed zero, indicating that a negative SROI was possible. The SROI is expected to be far greater in subsequent years, as the capital costs for the purchase and installation of the Seniors Exercise Parks would be removed. Of note, while the SROI favoured the maintenance participants for quality of life, work, health care and social care, the SROI favoured the baseline participants for leisure activities.

These findings are consistent with the initial economic evaluation of the Seniors Exercise Park program which reported a post-intervention reduction in health and social care cost without compromise to quality of life or falls, indicating that Seniors Exercise Parks are likely to be cost-effective [11]. In the current evaluation, most park visitors reported good health and were quite active, and this was seen in the quality of life and the physical activity measures across the three timepoints. However, we believe a key to the importance of the Seniors Exercise Park work is the maintenance of quality of life and physical activity, that is, keeping older people engaged in physical activity in the longer term to maintain, and potentially improve, health outcomes. One study has also shown feasibility and effectiveness of use of Seniors Exercise Parks by older people with mild balance dysfunction [21].

While the current project is the first SROI for a Seniors Exercise Park, previous studies have examined the SROI for general outdoor exercise equipment, which is a growing industry with a predicted value of $1.8 billion (USD) globally by 2023 [22]. In the UK, a SROI was completed to understand the value of sports and recreation facilities for both general and targeted participation [23]. This project reported that for every pound (GBP) invested in the sports and recreation facilities, 1.2 pounds of social value was created. In New Zealand, a SROI for recreational physical activity was completed and they defined recreational physical activity as the environment and programs that enable play, active recreation and sport. This project reported that for every dollar (NZD) invested in recreational physical activity, 2.1 dollars of social value was created. In Australia, a SROI for active recreation was completed and they defined active recreation physical activity as being for the purpose of relaxation, health and quality of life, or enjoyment, but excluded organised competitive sport [24]. This project reported that for every dollar (AUD) invested in active recreation, a dollar of value was created across direct and indirect benefits, with the authors suggesting this is a conservative estimate of the value created. The current project exceeded these SROI values where the social return was $2.50 (AUD) for every $1.00 spent.

Based on the growing evidence now supporting Seniors Exercise Parks, including this SROI evaluation, it is surprising that there remains quite limited uptake to installing this valuable exercise equipment more widely in community parks in Australia, and internationally. There is a growing case that parks should consider providing a Seniors Exercise Park for this growing and important demographic within each local community, to provide much greater access to these exercise opportunities. Findings from this study could potentially address some of the barriers to Seniors Exercise Park uptake, by providing local government councils with sound reference points pertaining to the cost of implementing and activating a Seniors Exercise Park.[20]

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this SROI evaluation are the inclusion of six Seniors Exercise Parks across multiple local government areas which improve the generalisability of the findings, in addition to the pragmatic approach to the SROI analysis that has ensured the results reflect real-world conditions.

Limitations of this SROI evaluation are the relatively small sample size, limited time horizon, and the lack of a control group or follow up for the participants following the intercept survey. Future research could address these limitations by considering a longitudinal study to understand long term outcomes and adherence to exercise participation at the Senior Exercise Park.

Conclusion

From a broad societal perspective, scaling the Seniors Exercise Parks program across multiple local government areas may deliver a strong return on investment. This evaluation shows that even within the first year, the program generated a substantial social value, returning $2.50 for every $1.00 spent, with even greater returns expected as capital costs diminish. Beyond economic benefits, the program helps maintain physical activity and quality of life among older adults, supporting healthier ageing and reduced demand on health and social care services. These findings reinforce the value of Seniors Exercise Parks as an evidence-informed component of age-friendly community planning.
