Severe Traumatic Cerebral Fat Embolism Following Pelvic Fracture: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Jung Woo Hyung, Maru Kim, Tae-Kyu Lee, Min Ho Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cerebral fat embolism (CFE) is a rare but severe complication of trauma, most often linked to long bone or pelvic fractures. We report a 71-year-old woman who developed severe CFE after a pelvic fracture, resulting in profound coma despite early supportive treatment. Brain MRI showed the characteristic “starfield” pattern confirming the diagnosis. A systematic literature review identified 12 cases of severe CFE (Glasgow Coma Scale [GCS] ≤9) reported since 2010, including the present case. Initial GCS ranged from 3 to 9, with lower scores generally predicting poorer outcomes. Although some patients recovered after deep coma, no consistent prognostic factor—such as GCS score, corticosteroid therapy, or timing of orthopedic surgery—was identified. However, early diagnosis, aggressive supportive management, and timely fracture fixation were repeatedly emphasized as essential to improving outcomes. Multidisciplinary care involving neurologists, intensivists, and orthopedic surgeons was critical. Severe CFE remains an uncommon but often devastating condition with high morbidity. Prognosis is variable but tends to correlate with initial neurological status. Greater awareness and early intervention may enhance survival and neurological recovery, while further studies are needed to clarify prognostic determinants and establish standardized management strategies.

INTRODUCTION

Cerebral fat embolism is a rare but serious complication that can occur following traumatic injuries, particularly in long bone and pelvic fractures. The incidence of cerebral fat embolism has been reported to vary widely from 0.9% to 11% depending on diagnostic criteria and detection methods.816) Cerebral fat embolism represents a subset of fat embolism syndrome where fat emboli reach the cerebral circulation, leading to neurological symptoms. The pathophysiology of cerebral fat embolism is not fully understood, but two main theories have been proposed: 1) the mechanical theory, suggesting that fat droplets enter the bloodstream directly from the bone marrow due to increased intramedullary pressure; and 2) the biochemical theory, which posits that free fatty acids released from fat emboli cause endothelial damage and subsequent cerebral injury.9) Despite its rarity, cerebral fat embolism can lead to significant morbidity and mortality, making early recognition and management crucial.

We report the case of a patient who developed severe cerebral fat embolism after pelvic fracture. Despite early supportive care, neurological recovery was not achieved. This case highlights the need for prompt diagnosis and multidisciplinary management in traumatic cerebral fat embolism.

CASE REPORT

A 71-year-old female patient was transferred to our regional trauma center following a pedestrian-traffic accident. The patient had a history of right pontine infarction in 2010 but had remained neurologically intact since then, maintaining a modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score of 0 under aspirin monotherapy.

According to witnesses, the patient was struck by a vehicle while walking and was subsequently run over by the front wheel of the car, which was estimated to be moving at approximately 10 km/hr. At the time of transport by emergency medical services, the patient complained only of left shoulder pain. However, upon arrival at emergency room of traumatic center, her GCS score was 6 (E1V1M4) indicating a stuporous mental state. Her pupils were 3 mm in size with prompt light reflexes, bilaterally. On physical examination, the patient showed mild swelling over the left parietal region and abrasions on the right forearm, right elbow, and both knees.

The brain computed tomography (CT) showed no evidence of acute hemorrhage or skull fracture aside from scalp swelling. Chest and abdominal CT revealed multiple fractures, including right superior and inferior pubic ramus fracture, right scapular body fracture, L2 compression fracture, L4 spinous process fracture, and left 4th–6th rib fractures.

Given the patient’s low GCS score of 6, diffuse axonal injury was suspected. Thus, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) with diffusion-weighted imaging was performed. MRI revealed numerous small nodular restricted diffusion lesions in the bilateral cerebral and cerebellar hemispheres, basal ganglia, and thalami, which also demonstrated multifocal high signal intensities on T2- and T2-fluid-attenuated inversion recovery-weighted images. However, there was no evidence of bleeding on susceptibility-weighted imaging (FIGURE 1). Based on these findings, the neuroradiologist diagnosed post-traumatic cerebral fat embolism, presumed to be secondary to pelvic fracture (FIGURE 2). Dexamethasone and mannitol were prescribed to control brain edema. Despite ongoing supportive care, there was no improvement. Pelvic and scapular fractures were managed conservatively in consultation with orthopedic trauma specialists. With no further neurological deterioration, the patient was transferred to a long-term care facility.

DISCUSSION

Cerebral fat embolism is an uncommon manifestation of fat embolism syndrome, with reported incidences ranging from 0.9% to 11% among trauma patients.816) The variability in incidence is attributed to differences in diagnostic criteria and the sensitivity of detection methods. The classic triad of fat embolism syndrome includes respiratory distress, neurological impairment, and petechial rash. In cerebral fat embolism, neurological symptoms predominate and can range from mild confusion to coma. These symptoms typically develop within 12 to 72 hours post-injury. Seizures and focal neurological deficits may also occur. Diagnosis of cerebral fat embolism is primarily clinical but is supported by imaging studies. MRI is particularly useful, often revealing a “starfield” pattern on diffusion-weighted imaging that is indicative of multiple small infarcts.812) Transcranial Doppler ultrasonography and fundoscopy can also aid in diagnosis by detecting microembolic signals and retinal emboli, respectively.34) Management of cerebral fat embolism is largely supportive, focusing on maintaining adequate oxygenation and hemodynamic stability. The use of corticosteroids remains controversial, with some studies suggesting potential benefits in reducing inflammation and capillary permeability. Early surgical stabilization of fractures, ideally within 24 hours, may reduce the risk of fat embolism.614)

With the addition of the case described in this report, 12 patients in total were included in this review. The initial GCS scores among these patients ranged from 3 to 9, with a median value of 5. When grouped by initial GCS, 5 patients had scores of 5 or below, while seven patients had scores above 5. Steroid therapy was administered in 2 cases, and orthopedic surgery was performed in 8 cases. Outcomes were assessed using the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS). Eight patients achieved a favorable neurological outcome (GOS 4–5), while four patients had unfavorable outcomes (GOS 1–3). Statistical analysis was conducted to evaluate the relationship between clinical variables—initial GCS, steroid use, and orthopedic surgery—and patient outcomes. None of these variables showed a statistically significant association with favorable outcomes. However, there was a noticeable trend indicating that patients with lower initial GCS scores tended to have poorer outcomes, although this trend did not reach statistical significance due to the limited sample size (TABLE 2). These findings suggest that while initial GCS may be associated with prognosis, no single variable could be identified as a definitive predictor of outcome in this case series. Further studies with larger patient populations are needed to clarify prognostic factors in traumatic fat embolism syndrome.

The reviewed cases underscore the importance of early recognition and aggressive supportive management. Supportive care, including early intubation, mechanical ventilation, and careful hemodynamic monitoring, remains the cornerstone of treatment, as no specific pharmacological therapy has been proven effective. Early orthopedic intervention, such as prompt fracture stabilization, is also recommended to reduce the risk of further fat embolization and improve outcomes. Some reports have described favorable outcomes even in cases of cerebral fat embolism presenting with extremely severe neurological impairment, such as a GCS score of 3.1517) However, our review indicates that patients with lower GCS scores tend to have poorer prognoses. Once neurological deterioration occurs, recovery appears to be challenging, suggesting that severe impairment is often associated with limited reversibility. Therefore, prompt and proactive orthopedic surgical intervention is a critical treatment strategy for preventing clinical deterioration. To achieve optimal outcomes, a multidisciplinary approach involving close collaboration among orthopedic surgeons, intensivists, and other relevant specialists is essential.

Future research should focus on identifying early predictors of poor outcomes and developing novel therapeutic strategies to mitigate cerebral injuries in fat embolism syndrome.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, severe cerebral fat embolism after trauma is rare but often leads to significant neurological impairment and poor prognosis. Our review, including this case, shows variable outcomes: some patients recover even after severe neurological compromise, but lower initial GCS scores generally predict worse outcomes. Early recognition, aggressive supportive care, and prompt orthopedic intervention are crucial. Optimal management requires a multidisciplinary approach, and further research is needed to clarify prognostic factors and improve treatment strategies.
