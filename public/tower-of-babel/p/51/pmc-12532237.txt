Evaluating the Impact of Self-designed Dental Storybooks on Anxiety in 5–8-year-old Children: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Satish Maran, Halaswamy V Kambalimath, Kanchan S Adchitre, Rini R Gangwal, Niharika A Vyas, Nivriti Chaddha, Urvi P Gala, Shadma Qureshi, Prerna S Mishra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFear of dentists is a very big factor that discourages children from seeking dental services, and might even cause children to be nervous in general. Most children experience anxiety during their first dentist appointment. Children may be better prepared for their first dental appointments and feel more at ease with the use of interactive activity books or stories that provide good information about the dentist. An important part of a pediatric dentist's job is to help patients regulate their behavior in a healthy way without resorting to medication.AimThe objective of the research was to assess and contrast how a self-designed dental storybook affected anxiety levels, specifically in the age group of 5–8 years.DesignThis research was a controlled, randomized experiment.Materials and methodsThe participants in the research were 400 kids between the ages of 5 and 8. The kids were divided into two groups at random: one that received an intervention via a self-designed dental storybook, and the other that served as a control. Researchers tracked participants' heart rates, facial image scale (FIS) scores, and Venham scale (VS) readings throughout a single dental appointment (screening and examination), both before and after the intervention.ResultsThe results of this study state that the implementation of a self-designed dental storybook in early dental visits will significantly reduce dental anxiety among children.ConclusionUtilization of a self-designed dental storybook as an adjunctive tool is anticipated to be a promising behavior modification technique for children for reducing anxiety and can be used as an oral health education tool.How to cite this articleMaran S, Kambalimath HV, Adchitre KS, et al. Introduction

Although the words are commonly used interchangeably in literature, anxiety and fear are really responses to different types of risk: fear is a reaction to current danger, whereas anxiety is a reaction to impending danger.1

Anxiety is a more complex emotional state that manifests itself in a number of ways, including a lack of control over one's own destiny, an overwhelming sense of powerlessness in the face of uncertain future events, and an increased reaction time to potential hazards.2

If we were to apply the concepts of fear and anxiety to the dental field, we could say that fear is a response to something frightening (such as a dental drill) and anxiety is the feeling of unease (like worrying that something terrible will happen) that builds up before a dental appointment or procedure.3

A child's dental anxiety and worry may originate from a number of places, including their parents' views, their own anxieties, their own medical and dental experiences, and their age. The way a youngster acts while seeing the dentist is greatly affected by these variables.

Nothing can be more crucial than the first dental experience of a child regarding his/her attitude towards dentistry and the outcomes of his/her dental treatment.4

Anxieties related to dental visits and treatments rank sixth most often reported anxiety disorders, with a much greater prevalence in youngsters.5

A number of communication-assisted methods have been suggested to assist individuals in changing their behavior, including visual pedagogies, audiovisual aids, pictorial or iconic imagery, and applied behavioral analysis.6

A number of behavioral methods have been the subject of recent research. These include comic strips, visual aids for instruction, and social stories.7 These techniques are often used to motivate desirable behaviors or to ease youngsters into new situations. Similar to other behavior management techniques, they have proven to be effective, cost-efficient, and easy to implement.8

The American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD) has called for a shift in emphasis away from pharmaceutical interventions and toward behavioral modification strategies that do not include opioids.9

Some of the earlier suggestions made by Gray and Garand included writing stories from the perspective of the child and using a font size that would be relevant to his age. This approach helps children better understand the dentist's point of view and connect with it more effectively.10

There is limited research available on the use of storybooks for preoperative behavior modification in dental settings. The efficacy of dental storybooks in alleviating anxiety during successive appointments has also been the subject of little research. At the pretreatment appointment, it is critical for pediatric dentists to provide behavior modification approaches to help alleviate children's anxiety.

Therefore, this research sets out to determine whether or not a self-designed dental storybook published in both English and the local language (Hindi) had any effect on the anxiety levels of children aged 5–8 years. Materials and Methods

Study Design

In this randomized controlled experiment, children who were having their first dental check up were participants at the Modern Dental College and Research Centre's Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry. The 400 children sample was split evenly into two groups using a coin toss procedure. The children are aged between 5 and 8 years. Group I included children with intervention using a self-designed dental storybook, and Group II was the control group without any intervention.

Study Procedure

A total of 400 children, ranging in age from 5 to 8 years old, participated in the current randomized clinical study in the in vivo setting at the Modern Dental College and Research Centre in Indore, Madhya Pradesh. The trial lasted for 2 months. After a detailed description of the procedure using the local language, consent to participate in the study was provided by the Ethical Committee (Ref. No. MDC/EC/27/2024) of the Institutional Review Board. Parenthood and/or other legally authorized representatives of patients who were willing to participate in the study signed an informed consent. The study was carried out by two examiners in a single visit; one examiner recorded the data before and after the procedure, the other examiner was blinded and carried out the examination. The allocation of samples was done by a simple randomization method using coin-flip method. The allocation ratio was set at 1:1.

The sample size estimation was conducted using G*Power Software version 3.1.9.7. The significance level (α) was set at 0.05, and the statistical power (1−β) at 80% (0.8). Based on the anticipated means and standard deviations, the effect size (Cohen's d) was calculated to be 0.45, indicating a moderate effect.

For a two-tailed independent samples t-test comparing two groups (intervention and control), the required sample size was calculated to be 200 participants per group, totaling 400 participants.

Pulse rate (using Dr Trust [USA] finger oximeter), facial image scale (FIS),12 and Chota Bheem–Chutki scale (CBCS)13 scores were noted for all the participants at two levels, i.e., preintervention and postintervention.

On one end of the visual analog scale is a very joyful face, and on the other end is a very sad face; this is what the Facial Image Scale (Fig. 4) uses. As indicated in Figure 5, we have Chota Bheem–Chutki Scale, which comprises two sets of cards; one set of cards is divided into boys (Chota Bheem) and the other set of girls (Chutki). Group I participants were directed to counseling room to receive intervention using a self-designed storybook before treatment.

During the same time period, members of group II were able to relax in the waiting room. After that, every single one of the kids had their teeth checked.

Statistical Analysis

The statistical analysis program SPSS 23.0 (Version 23, IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was used to examine the data. In this investigation, descriptive statistics were used before the paired and unpaired t-tests, and findings were deemed statistically significant if p < 0.05.

Also, adjusted analysis was performed using an ANCOVA test with preintervention scores as the covariate, two groups as the independent variable, and postintervention FIS and CBS scores as the dependent variable.

Discussion

It should be noted that certain children who suffer from Dental Fear and Anxiety may also exhibit behavioral management issues, such as the “fight” reaction, which may hinder the administration of dental therapy and have a negative effect on the dental care the child receives.3,14

According to Filewich and others, patients with high levels of fear needed about 20% more time in the dental chair compared to those who were less fearful. This finding emphasizes the importance for dentists to adopt effective strategies to ease patient fear and anxiety. Not only does this benefit the patient's experience, but it also has implications for the efficiency and financial performance of dental practices.15

In behavior guidance, all members of the dental team—dentists, hygienists, and support staff—as well as the patient and their parents, engage in a continuous cycle of pre- and post-treatment education and communication.16

Effective behavior management should be adaptable and tailored to the unique needs of each child.

For a child aged 5–8 years, grasping an unfamiliar concept can be challenging, especially without a clear frame of reference to understand the dentist's perspective. To address this, children were introduced to the dental environment and dental concepts featuring their favorite cartoon characters, utilizing a self-designed dental storybook.

This research focused on investigating a psychological method to manage behavior, alleviate fear, and anxiety of 5–8-year-old children, utilizing a self-designed dental storybook.

This self-designed dental storybook can also be used as an oral health education tool.

Various studies examining the effectiveness of providing pre-visit information have yielded compelling results.

Reading dental storybooks to children before appointments seems to help them feel more comfortable and behave better while getting dental work done.17

Similar studies done by Deshpande et al.18, Krishnadasan et al.19 and Rethan et al.20 state that self-designed storybooks proved to be a simple, effective tool for reducing dental anxiety, enhancing awareness, motivating care, and receiving positive feedback from both children and parents.

According to Aminabadi et al., a social learning theory-based picture tale may help youngsters feel more comfortable going to the dentist, perceive less pain, and exhibit better behavior by integrating modeling and procedural knowledge.21

A battery of physiological and behavioral assessment tools was used to gauge dental anxiety in this clinical trial.

An increase in pulse rate, indicative of anxiety or fear, is a common response to sympathetic stimulation in children.22 Consequently, a pulse oximeter was used to quantify dental anxiety by tracking the pulse rate.

The Facial Image Scale (FIS) and the Chota Bheem Chutki Scale (CBCS) were used as behavioral characteristics.

Buchanan and Niven state that a good test should be legitimate, accessible to people with varying levels of education and experience, and simple to administer and score. A physician needs a way to gauge a child's responsiveness to dental therapy, and any assessment of dental anxiety may help with that.12

The FIS serves all the purposes.

According to Sadana et al., CBCS is visually appealing, employs characters from popular animated shows, offers gender-specific cards, and is quick and easy to implement.13

The findings of this study highlight that a self-designed dental storybook is a powerful and efficient tool for easing dental anxiety in children, while also supporting effective behavior management. It can also be used as an oral health education tool.

Clinical Significance

The use of a self-designed dental storybook significantly reduces dental anxiety in children aged 5–8 years, making it an effective and simple nonpharmacological behavior management tool. This intervention not only improves the child's dental experience but also serves as an educational aid to promote oral health awareness among young patients and their families.
