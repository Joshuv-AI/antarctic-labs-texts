IL-18 Promotes the Development of Lupus Nephritis by Activating the Wnt/β-Catenin Signaling Pathway in Renal Tubular Epithelial Cells
Authors: Jiashun Zeng, Yan Ting, Hong Yin, Si Yang, Lifen Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates the specific role of interleukin-18 (IL-18) in lupus nephritis (LN) progression and its molecular mechanisms to provide a theoretical basis for the pathological intervention of LN. IL-18 levels were measured from systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) patients with or without LN. MRL/lpr mice received intraperitoneal injection of IL-18 or its inhibitor to assess renal injury, inflammation, and Wnt/β-catenin pathway activity. In-vivo intervention experiments using Caspase-1 inhibitor (Ac-YVAD-CMK), Wnt pathway agonists (BML-284), and inhibitors (IWR-1-endo) were performed. In-vitro studies were conducted in human renal tubular epithelial cell line HK-2 to investigate the molecular mechanisms. IL-18 was markedly elevated in SLE patients, and further increased in urine and renal tissue of LN. In MRL/lpr mice, exogenous IL-18 significantly exacerbated renal injury, evidenced by increased urinary protein, serum creatinine, anti-dsDNA antibodies, tubulointerstitial inflammation, and increased activity index. This was accompanied by up-regulation of IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6, down-regulation of IL-10, and activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway (increased β-catenin, p-GSK-3β, and CK1α). Conversely, IL-18 blockade or Wnt inhibition attenuated these pathological changes. Notably, the protective effects of IL-18 inhibition were reversed by simultaneous Wnt activation. In-vitro, IL-18 promoted HK-2 cell proliferation and inflammatory secretion by activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling, effects which were blocked by a Caspase-1 inhibitor or a Wnt pathway inhibitor. IL-18 activated the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in renal tubular epithelial cells and disrupted the balance of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokine networks, thereby exacerbating tubulointerstitial inflammation and promoting renal injury in LN mice. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting the IL-18 signaling pathway in LN. Introduction

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disease arising from the loss of immune tolerance to endogenous nuclear antigens, resulting in multi-system inflammatory responses and multi-organ damage [1]. Lupus nephritis (LN) is one of the most common and severe complications of SLE, characterized pathologically by renal inflammation. Approximately 20–40% of patients with LN progress to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) or even death [2]. Although glucocorticoids and immunosuppressive agents have significantly improved outcomes over the past decades, LN remains a leading cause of death among SLE patients. Despite optimal therapy, about 20% of LN patients still progress to ESRD [3].

LN is initiated by the breakdown of immunological tolerance, leading to the emergence of autoreactive lymphocytes that secrete anti-double-stranded DNA and related autoantibodies [4]. These antibodies form immune complexes with antigens that deposit in the kidney [5], and further induce a cascade of immunological and inflammatory responses. These include both innate immunity (e.g., Toll-like receptor 7/9-mediated type I interferon release and the formation of neutrophil extracellular traps [6–8]), adaptive immunity (B cell activation mediated by Tfh and Th17 cells [9, 10], complement activation, and stress responses in resident renal cells (e.g., MAPK/ERK-mediated release of pro-inflammatory and pro-fibrotic factors) [11, 12]. Collectively, these processes contribute to renal inflammation, tissue damage, and ultimately renal failure. Traditionally, the pathological features of LN have been thought to centre on the glomerulus, emphasizing immune complex deposition, complement activation, and podocyte injury [13]. However, single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial transcriptomics have now revealed intense immune cell infiltration in the tubulointerstitial compartment and distinct interferon signaling [14] and metabolic profiles [15]. Overall, although the current International Society of Nephrology/Renal Pathology Society (ISN/RPS) classification remains glomerulocentric [16], tubular injury is an important driver of LN initiation and progression [17].

Interleukin-18 (IL-18), a pivotal member of the IL-1 cytokine superfamily, was originally identified as interferon-γ-inducing factor. In synergy with IL-12, it drives the release of interferon-γ (IFN-γ) from Th1, natural killer (NK) and natural killer T (NKT) cells, thereby amplifying type-1 immunity; independently, it activates macrophages, dendritic cells and B cells to sustain inflammation [18, 19]. IL-18 is stored intracellularly as inactive pro-IL-18 and converted to its mature bioactive form by caspase-1 cleavage; its activity is subsequently regulated by IL-18 binding protein [20, 21]. IL-18 exhibits a dual role in immune regulation: while it synergizes with IL-12 to promote IFN-γ production, thereby enhancing anti-pathogen immunity and maintaining homeostasis, its sustained or excessive expression can drive detrimental inflammation and contribute to autoimmune diseases [22]. The execution of these functions is highly dependent on the tissue microenvironment, enabling IL-18 to exhibit complex and diverse roles across different pathological states [23]. Elevated circulating IL-18 has been documented in multiple inflammatory and autoimmune disorders, including psoriatic arthritis [24], SLE [25, 26], juvenile idiopathic arthritis [27], asthma [28], inflammatory bowel disease [29], and multiple sclerosis [30, 31]. In SLE, serum IL-18 levels correlate strongly with disease activity [32–35]. Nevertheless, the mechanisms through which IL-18 contributes to SLE and LN remain unclear.

Notably, the kidney plays a crucial role in IL-18 biology. In addition to monocytes and macrophages, tubular epithelial cells can also constitutively produce pro-IL-18, which is then cleaved by caspase-1 into active IL-18 under ischemia, toxicity, or immune stress [36–38]. Previous research indicated that IL-18 is universally up-regulated in various chronic kidney diseases and correlates with proteinuria, loss of renal function, and adverse outcomes [39], whereas IL-18 deficiency attenuates renal injury and fibrosis [40]. Serum IL-18 levels are significantly correlated with proteinuria, creatinine, and estimated glomerular filtration rate in immunoglobulin A nephropathy [41]. Moreover, in patients with LN, IL-18 levels decrease in parallel with disease remission following immunosuppressive therapy [42]. These findings suggest that the kidney, particularly the tubulointerstitial compartment, may be a key target where IL-18 exerts its pathogenic effects. The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway plays an important role in renal tubular injury repair and renal fibrosis [43, 44], and previous studies have demonstrated that IL-18 can influence the pathological progression of various diseases by regulating this pathway [45, 46]; however, the causal relationship between the two in LN remains to be elucidated.

In this study, we found that serum IL-18 levels were significantly elevated in SLE patients and rose even further in urine and renal tissue in those with concurrent LN. Specifically, in-vivo experiments showed that exogenous IL-18 administration markedly aggravated renal injury in MRL/lpr mice, leading to inflammatory cell infiltration in the renal tubulointerstitium, a pro-inflammatory cytokine shift (IFN-γ, IL-1β, IL-6 up; IL-10 down), and activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. Conversely, inhibiting IL-18 significantly attenuated renal injury and downregulated the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, and blocking the Wnt pathway produced similar effects. Subsequently, activating the Wnt pathway after IL-18 inhibition resulted in worsened renal injury in MRL/lpr mice, with significant increases in proteinuria, serum creatinine, anti-dsDNA antibody, and inflammatory cytokines. In vitro, IL-18 activated the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in human proximal tubular epithelial (HK-2) cells, enhanced the secretion of IL-1β and IL-6, and suppressed the expression of IL-10 and transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1). Similarly, these effects could be significantly blocked by inhibiting IL-18r or the Wnt pathway. Collectively, our study indicated that IL-18 aggravated tubulointerstitial inflammation and accelerated renal damage in LN mice by activating Wnt/β-catenin signaling in tubular epithelial cells and disrupting the balance of pro- and anti-inflammatory cytokines.

Methods

Clinical Specimen Collection

Renal tissue specimens were collected from LN patients confirmed by renal biopsy. For controls, normal renal tissues were obtained from patients undergoing nephrectomy for renal tumors, specifically from areas > 2 cm away from the tumor margin. Peripheral blood and urine samples were collected during routine clinical visits. The study included four groups: healthy controls (NC), patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA), SLE without nephritis (SLE), and SLE with lupus nephritis (SLE-LN). All enrolled SLE and SLE-LN patients were newly diagnosed and treatment-naive. Peripheral blood was collected via venipuncture, and serum was subsequently isolated. All samples were stored at − 80 °C for future use.

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Guizhou Medical University (approval No. 2023 − 345), and written informed consent was obtained from every participant before specimen collection.

Animal Model

Female MRL/lpr mice and C57BL/6 mice aged 5–6 weeks were purchased from Beijing Speifu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (license number: SCXK[Beijing]2019-0010) and housed in the SPF-grade animal facility at Guizhou Medical University under controlled environmental conditions (temperature: 22 ± 1 °C; relative humidity: 55%; 12 h light/12 h dark cycle). MRL/lpr mice were randomly divided into five groups (n = 6 per group): model control (MRL NC), IL-18 treatment (MRL 18+), Caspase-1 inhibitor (MRL 18−), Wnt signaling inhibition (MRL W−), and combined IL-18 inhibition with Wnt activation (MRL 18 − W+). C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6 per group): normal control (C57 NC), IL-18 treatment (C57 18+), and IL-18 combined with Wnt signaling inhibition (C57 18 + W−). All drugs were administered by intraperitoneal injection at a total volume of 0.1 mL per injection. The treatment period lasted 8 weeks, with every-other-day dosing for the first 2 weeks and daily dosing for the subsequent 6 weeks. The MRL NC group received an equal volume of sterile water, while the MRL 18 + group received recombinant IL-18 (500 ng/mouse). The MRL 18 − group received the Caspase-1-specific inhibitor Ac-YVAD-CMK (12.5 µg/mouse), which blocks pro-IL-18 maturation by inhibiting Caspase-1 activity, thereby suppressing IL-18 activation. The MRL W− group received the Wnt/β-catenin pathway inhibitor IWR-1 endo (409 ng/mouse). The MRL 18 − W+ group received Ac-YVAD-CMK (12.5 µg/mouse) continuously for 3 days, followed by alternating injections of Ac-YVAD-CMK (12.5 µg/mouse) and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway agonist BML-284 (2.1 µg/mouse) until the end of the experiment. The C57 NC group received an equal volume of sterile water, the C57 18 + group received recombinant IL-18 (500 ng/mouse), and the C57 18 + W− group received alternating injections of IL-18 (500 ng/mouse) and IWR-1 endo (409 ng/mouse). Starting from week 10, urine samples were collected every 2 weeks for urinary protein detection. At the end of the experiment, mice were euthanized, and renal tissues and blood samples were collected for subsequent analysis. The protocol was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guizhou Medical University (approval No. 2304903) and complied with international guidelines for laboratory animal welfare.

Cell Culture, Small Interfering RNAs (siRNAs) Transfection, and Experimental Groups

HK-2 cells were purchased from Wuhan Zishan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., and cultured in complete medium at 37°C with 5% CO₂ and 95% humidity. Cells were divided into five groups: control, IL-18, IL-18 + si-IFN-γ, IL-18 + Wnt inhibition, and Wnt activation. The control group received only an equivalent replacement of fresh complete medium without any drug addition. The IL-18 group was treated with IL-18 at a final concentration of 50 ng/mL after medium change. The IL-18 + si-IFN-γ group was first transfected with si-IFN-γ, and 4 h later the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium followed by IL-18 treatment (50 ng/mL). The IL-18 + Wnt inhibition group was pre-incubated with IWR-1 endo at a final concentration of 0.5 µM for 2–3 h before IL-18 (50 ng/mL) was added. The Wnt activation group received BML-284 alone at a final concentration of 10 µM.

Drugs and Reagents

Recombinant mouse IL-18 (Sino Biological, Beijing, China); Ac-YVAD-CMK (MedChemExpress, NJ, USA); BML-284 and IWR-1-endo (Absin Bioscience Inc., Shanghai, China); ELISA kits (IL-6, IL-1β, IL-10, IFN-γ, IL-18, and TGF-β1, Quanzhou Ruixin Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Quanzhou, Fujian, China); urinary protein and creatinine assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China).

Histopathology and Immunohistochemistry

Renal tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned into 3 μm serial sections, followed by hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining for histopathological examination. LN pathological damage was evaluated using the 2000 Hill scoring criteria and the 2018 revised ISN/RPS pathological classification and scoring system [47]. Two renal tissue sections spaced ≥ 100 μm apart were randomly selected from each mouse for scoring. All sections were independently reviewed under double-blind conditions by senior pathologists with experience in renal pathology diagnosis, who comprehensively assessed glomerular, tubular, and interstitial lesions in the renal cortex and calculated the activity index (AI) and chronicity index (CI). For immunohistochemistry, 4-µm sections were baked at 65 °C for 2 h, deparaffinised in xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanol, and subjected sequentially to antigen retrieval, endogenous peroxidase quenching, and blocking. Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: IL-18 (1:100, Abmart), glycogen synthase kinase-3beta (GSK-3β, 1:50, Proteintech), β-catenin (1:50, Proteintech), phospho-GSK-3β (Ser9, 1:200, Absin), and casein kinase 1alpha (CK1α, 1:100, Absin). After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), sections were incubated with an appropriate secondary antibody at room temperature for 50 min, followed by color development with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine. The expression of target proteins was observed and analyzed under a light microscope at 200× magnification.

ELISA

ELISA were performed according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Optical density (OD) was read at 450 nm, and sample concentrations were calculated using four-parameter logistic (4-PL) standard curve fitting. Urinary IL-18 levels were normalized to urine creatinine, and the urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) was determined to assess renal injury. The specific analytes and corresponding sample sources were as follows: serum and urinary IL-18 were measured in LN/SLE patients and healthy controls; serum IFN-γ was measured in MRL/lpr and C57 mice; IL-1β, IL-6, and IL-10 were measured in spleen tissue homogenates from MRL/lpr mice; and IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, and TGF-β1 were measured in HK-2 cell culture supernatants.

Western Blot Analysis

Total protein was extracted from the kidneys of MRL/lpr mice and from HK-2 cells and quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Solarbio). Equal amounts of protein were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes, which were then blocked at room temperature for 5 min with a protein-free blocking buffer (Servicebio). All membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: GSK-3β (1:6000, Proteintech), β-catenin (1:1 000, Proteintech), phospho-GSK-3β (Ser9, 1:1 000, Absin), CK1α (1:750, Absin), and GAPDH (1:3 000, Abclonal). After three washes with Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST), the membranes were incubated with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 2 h. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection kit (NCM Biotech), and band density was quantified using ImageJ software.

Cell Viability Assay

HK-2 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 4 × 10³ cells per well (100 µl medium) and incubated overnight. The next day, different concentrations of IL-18 (0, 1, 10, 50, and 100 ng/mL) were added to the wells, with three replicate wells per concentration. After incubation for 24, 48, and 72 h, 10 µl of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation for 1.5 h. Cell viability was then assessed by measuring OD at 450 nm.

Immunofluorescence Staining

HK-2 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and cultured overnight. After treatment according to the experimental groups, cells were incubated for 48 h. Cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min, and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 on ice for 4 min. After blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) at room temperature for 50 min, cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against β-catenin (1:100, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE), followed by a 1-h exposure to the appropriate fluorescent secondary antibody at room temperature in the dark. After final PBS washes, samples were mounted with anti-fade mounting medium containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole and examined under a fluorescence microscope.

Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from HK-2 cells and reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA). The qPCR was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Apexbio), with glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh) as the internal control. The primer sequences (5′–3′) were shown in Table 1.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were performed with SPSS 26.0 or GraphPad Prism 10.0. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± SD) and were compared using an independent samples t-test (two groups) or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA, multiple groups). Non-normally distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) and were analyzed with the Mann–Whitney U test (two groups) or the Kruskal–Wallis H test (multiple groups). A two-tailed P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The UACR was significantly elevated in the SLE-LN group (Fig. 1D). In renal tissue, immunohistochemistry revealed significantly increased IL-18 expression in the tubulointerstitial compartment of LN patients compared with weak staining in healthy controls (Fig. 1E, J). Collectively, these findings suggest that IL-18 plays an important role in renal injury in LN, with the tubulointerstitial compartment likely being its main site of action.

IL-18 Exacerbated Renal Injury in MRL/lpr Mice

We performed functional assays in both C57BL/6 and MRL/lpr mouse to clarify the pathogenic contribution of IL-18 in LN. The results showed that, compared to the C57 NC group, C57 mice treated with exogenous IL-18 (C57 18 + group) displayed mildly increased levels of proteinuria, serum creatinine, and anti-dsDNA antibodies (Fig. 2A–C). However, their renal histology remained normal with no significant tissue damage observed (Fig. 2F–G). In contrast, MRL/lpr mice that received intraperitoneal injections of IL-18 (MRL 18 + group) exhibited a sustained increase in proteinuria (Fig. 2A), significantly elevated serum creatinine (Fig. 2B), and serum anti-dsDNA antibody levels (Fig. 2C). Renal histopathological analysis (Fig. 2F–I) further revealed that, compared to the MRL NC group, the MRL 18 + group showed mild to moderate mesangial cell proliferation and basement membrane thickening in the glomeruli. However, the main pathological changes were concentrated in the tubulointerstitial compartment, as evidenced by disorganized tubular epithelial cells, luminal dilatation, pronounced interstitial lymphocyte infiltration, and increased focal inflammatory responses, together with patchy tubular atrophy. Moreover, the AI score was significantly higher in the MRL-18 + group (Fig. 2D). Collectively, these findings indicate that IL-18 exacerbates tubulointerstitial injury in MRL/lpr mice by promoting inflammatory cell recruitment and driving tissue remodeling during interstitial damage, thereby accelerating renal dysfunction.

Given that IL-18 aggravated renal injury in MRL/lpr mice mainly in the tubulointerstitial compartment, and that immunohistochemical analysis of renal tissue from LN patients showed IL-18 to be primarily localized in the tubulointerstitium, we hypothesized that renal tubular epithelial cells (the major cellular component of the renal interstitium) could be a key target cell for IL-18. Therefore, we stimulated HK-2 cells with IL-18 in vitro. The results showed that IL-18 promoted cell proliferation, which peaked at 50 ng/mL after 48 h (Fig. 2E). Moreover, IL-18 stimulation increased pro-inflammatory and decreased anti-inflammatory cytokine secretion by HK-2 cells (Fig. 6B), suggesting that IL-18 directly contributes to tissue injury and LN progression by acting on renal tubular epithelial cells, in addition to its systemic effects.

IL-18 Activated Wnt/β-Catenin Signaling

To investigate if IL-18 drove LN renal injury through the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, we systematically analyzed human LN biopsies, in vivo and in vitro models. Immunohistochemistry demonstrated that the key components of the Wnt signaling pathway, including β-catenin, pGSK-3β (Ser9), and CK1α, were markedly increased in the tubulointerstitial region of LN patients, while total GSK-3β remained unchanged (Fig. 1F–J), suggesting sustained Wnt/β-catenin activation in LN.

The animal experiment results were consistent with clinical findings. Immunostaining showed heightened expression of β-catenin, pGSK-3β (Ser9), and CK1α in MRL/lpr kidneys, localizing mainly to tubular epithelia and interstitium (Fig. 3B–E). Western blot confirmed significant increases in these proteins compared to C57 NC, while total GSK-3β remained unchanged. Exogenous IL-18 (MRL/lpr 18 + group) further amplified their expression, whereas blockade of IL-18 with Ac-YVAD-cmk or Wnt inhibition with IWR-1-endo markedly reduced it. Additionally, concurrent IL-18 suppression plus Wnt activation (18–W+ group) partially restored protein levels (Fig. 3A). Thus, IL-18 sustained Wnt/β-catenin signaling in vivo.

To evaluate the direct cellular mechanism by which IL-18 engages the Wnt pathway, we performed in vitro experiments using HK-2 cells. IL-18 treatment significantly promoted β-catenin nuclear translocation, an effect that was markedly inhibited either by siRNA-mediated knockdown of IFN-γ or by using a Wnt signaling inhibitor (IWR-1-endo). Conversely, treatment with the Wnt pathway agonist BML-284 further enhanced nuclear accumulation of β-catenin (Fig. 4A, B). Consistent with our animal studies, Western blot analysis revealed that IL-18 up-regulated the expression of GSK-3β and β-catenin. This effect was attenuated following IFN-γ knockdown or Wnt pathway inhibition, as reflected by reduced levels of pGSK-3β (Ser9) and β-catenin, while total GSK-3β expression remained unchanged; activation of the Wnt pathway produced the opposite effect (Fig. 4D). Additionally, qPCR results demonstrated that IL-18 increased mRNA expression of β-catenin and CK1α (Fig. 4C). Collectively, these findings indicate that IL-18 activates the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway in HK-2 cells.

In the pathway intervention experiments using HK-2 cells, activation of the Wnt pathway with BML-284 reduced β-catenin mRNA levels, yet markedly increased its protein abundance and nuclear translocation (Fig. 4B–D), suggesting a complex regulation of β-catenin transcription, translation, and nuclear translocation by BML-284.

Inhibition of the IL-18/Wnt Pathway Attenuated Renal Injury in MRL/lpr Mice

To assess the pathogenic role of the IL-18/Wnt axis in LN and its therapeutic potential, we examined the renal impact of blocking IL-18 or Wnt signaling in MRL/lpr mice. Compared with the MRL NC group, mice in the MRL 18- group (treated with Ac-YVAD-CMK, 10 mg/kg, every other day from week 12 to 16) exhibited a significant reduction in proteinuria (Fig. 5A), serum creatinine levels (Fig. 5B), and serum anti-dsDNA antibody titers (Fig. 5C). Histologically, MRL-NC kidneys displayed classic lupus lesions dominated by interstitial lymphocytic infiltration (Fig. 5F). These changes were largely reversed by IL-18 inhibition, which yielded lower AI and CI scores (Fig. 5D, E). In contrast, exogenous activation of IL-18 (MRL 18 + group) induced more severe renal inflammation, demonstrated by extensive lymphocytic infiltration in the tubulointerstitium, glomerular mesangial cell proliferation, pronounced glomerular basement membrane thickening, and tubular atrophy (Fig. 5G), along with a significant increase in AI score (Fig. 5D). Thus, inhibitor of IL-18 protects MRL/lpr mice from renal injury, whereas IL-18 activation worsens it.

Treatment with the Wnt pathway inhibitor IWR-1-endo (5 mg/kg) significantly reduced proteinuria and serum creatinine levels in mice (Fig. 5A–C) and attenuated tubulointerstitial pathological changes, an effect comparable to Caspase-1 inhibitor (Fig. 5D, E, H). Notably, administration of the Wnt pathway agonist BML-284 partially reversed the protective effects of IL-18 blockade, as evidenced by increased proteinuria, serum creatinine, and anti-dsDNA antibody levels (Fig. 5A–C), as well as exacerbated tubulointerstitial inflammation (Fig. 5D, E, J). Moreover, Kidneys from this group displayed diffuse interstitial inflammatory-cell infiltration, crescents, tubular brush-border loss, leukocyte casts, tubular atrophy, and interstitial fibrosis (Fig. 5J). Thus, IL-18 fuels LN-related renal injury via tubular Wnt/β-catenin activation, and targeting either IL-18 or Wnt signaling affords substantial protection, identifying the IL-18/Wnt axis as a potential therapeutic target in LN.

IL-18 Modulated Pro- and Anti-Inflammatory Cytokines

To further elucidate the role of IL-18 in LN-associated inflammation, we investigated its effects on cytokine levels (Fig. 6A). The results showed that exogenous IL-18 stimulation in MRL/lpr mice markedly increased serum IFN-γ and splenic IL-1β and IL-6 while decreasing splenic IL-10, indicating a Th1-skewed, pro-inflammatory signature. Conversely, both IL-18 inhibition (MRL 18- group) and Wnt pathway blockade (MRL W- group) significantly reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels; however, reactivation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway after IL-18 inhibition (MRL 18-W+ group) reversed this effect.

In vitro experiments further confirmed the pro-inflammatory effects of IL-18 on human renal tubular epithelial cell line HK-2 (Fig. 6B). Exposure of HK-2 cells to IL-18 or the Wnt agonist BML-284 significantly increased the release of IL-1β and IL-6, while reducing IL-10 and TGF-β1, demonstrating that IL-18 can directly regulate epithelial cell production of inflammatory cytokines. In contrast, Inhibition of the Wnt pathway with IWR-1-endo after IL-18 stimulation reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels and restored anti-inflammatory cytokine expression, underscoring the role of the IL-18–Wnt axis in inflammatory regulation. Consistently, after IL-18 stimulation, treatment with si-IFN-γ not only reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, restored anti-inflammatory cytokine levels, but also significantly inhibited β-catenin stabilization and nuclear translocation (Fig. 4A), suggesting that IFN-γ is critical in IL-18–mediated regulation of the Wnt pathway.

Discussion

LN, one of the most severe complications of SLE, is driven by complex immune dysregulation and aberrant activation of multiple signaling pathways [48]. IL-18, an important pro-inflammatory cytokine, has been documented to modulate the inflammatory microenvironment in various inflammatory diseases [49, 50], yet its mechanistic contribution to LN remains undefined. Here, integrating analyses of human renal biopsies, animal models, and in vitro experiments, we demonstrated that IL-18 is involved in the LN pathogenesis by activating the tubular Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway. These findings suggest that the IL-18–Wnt/β-catenin axis may be a therapeutic target in LN.

In this study, we compared the expression levels of IL-18 in serum, urine, and renal tissue across different clinical populations. The results showed that serum IL-18 concentrations were significantly elevated in both SLE and SLE-LN patients (Fig.1A), suggesting a close association with the degree of systemic inflammation. However, serum IL-18 levels did not differ between the two groups. In contrast, urine IL-18, normalized to urinary creatinine, was significantly higher in SLE-LN patients (Fig. 1C), correlated well with conventional injury markers such as UACR, and rose even when proteinuria was still minimal (Fig. 1D), implying that urinary IL-18 may better reflect early renal inflammation and tubule damage. Similar findings have been reported in other kidney diseases. For example, Parikh et al. identified urinary IL-18 as a specific biomarker of acute tubular injury [51, 52]. Morning IL-18 levels can reflect histological activity in LN [53]. Hall et al. [54] showed that early post-transplant IL-18, together with neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin, predicted the need for dialysis and short-term graft outcome. Collectively, these data support the potential of urinary IL-18 as an early, sensitive indicator of LN. IL-18 is released by many cell types, including T and NK cells, dendritic cells, macrophages, enterocytes, microglia, and synoviocytes [23]. Recent work demonstrated that tubular epithelial cells can produce IL-18 [55]. By engaging with IL-18R1, IL-18 can stimulate both tubular epithelial cells and infiltrating T cells to secrete IFN-γ, thereby amplifying the tubulointerstitial inflammatory response. Consistent with this, we found IL-18 predominantly localized to the tubulointerstitial compartment rather than to glomeruli in LN renal tissue. We therefore propose that the tubulointerstitium is the principal site of IL-18 activity and that tubular epithelial cells are key target cells within this niche.

Based on our clinical observations, we further investigated the functional role of IL-18 using the MRL/lpr lupus mouse model. exogenous administration of IL-18 significantly exacerbated renal injury in MRL/lpr mice: proteinuria, serum creatinine and anti-dsDNA levels all rose significantly (Fig. 2A–C), while histology showed interstitial inflammatory cell infiltration, mesangial expansion and glomerular basement-membrane thickening (Fig. 2F–I) together with a higher AI score (Fig. 2D). In vitro, IL-18 drove excessive proliferation of HK-2 cells and triggered a pro-inflammatory signature (Fig. 2E), indicating that its pathogenic effect is not confined to systemic immune activation but includes direct damage to the local tubular micro-environment. Collectively, our data pointed to a “tubular-signalling axis” as a key pathogenic step in LN. Importantly, we found that inhibiting IL-18 via Caspase-1 inhibitor significantly reduced proteinuria, serum creatinine, and anti-dsDNA antibody levels in MRL/lpr mice (Fig. 5A-C), and partially ameliorated renal histological injury (Fig. 5I), indicating that blockade of IL-18 signaling could slow LN progression and may have therapeutic potential. Consistent with this, previous studies have shown that inhibition of IL-18 signaling can attenuate renal inflammation induced by ischemia-reperfusion injury in mice [56], underscoring its role in both immune- and non-immune-mediated kidney damage. Moreover, Calvani et al. [57, 58] demonstrated that upregulation of IL-18 disrupted the Th1/Th2 balance, promoted the response toward Th1 immunity, and thereby triggered LN.

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway is a critical network regulating cellular proliferation, differentiation, survival, and tissue homeostasis [59]. In the resting state, β-catenin interacts with the destruction complex composed of Axin, adenomatous polyposis coli (APC), and GSK-3β, resulting in its continuous phosphorylation. Phosphorylation of the N-terminus of β-catenin by GSK-3β facilitates its ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, thereby maintaining low β-catenin levels in the cytoplasm [60, 61]. The binding of Wnt ligands to Frizzled receptors and activates Dishevelled proteins, disrupts the complex, and silences GSK-3β, allowing β-catenin to accumulate, enter the nucleus, and drive transcription of T-cell factor/lymphoid enhancer-binding factor (TCF/LEF) target genes [62, 63]. Previous studies have demonstrated that persistent aberrant activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway plays a pivotal role in chronic kidney disease, acute kidney injury, and polycystic kidney disease, promoting renal fibrosis, podocyte injury, and proteinuria [64, 65]. In SLE, evidence on Wnt/β-cateninis pathway is limited: β-catenin signalling is reportedly up-regulated in human LN [66], and the fractalkine (CX3CL1) has been implicated in epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and tubulointerstitial lesions in MRL/lpr mice via a Wnt-dependent mechanism [67]. As a pro-inflammatory cytokine, IL-18 has been shown to modulate the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of various diseases. In a rat chondrocyte model, for example, IL-18 enhanced inflammatory mediator release in synergy with Wnt signaling, accelerating cartilage degeneration [68]. In addition, IL-18 can suppress GSK-3β activity via a PI3K/Akt-dependent pathway, thereby reducing β-catenin degradation and facilitating its nuclear translocation [69]. In oncology, the IL-18–Wnt/β-catenin axis displays dual roles: it can suppress tumour growth by GSK-3β-mediated apoptosis or, conversely, promote EMT and metastasis via β-catenin stabilization [70]. Furthermore, nuclear activation of β-catenin can induce the expression of various pro-inflammatory cytokines and promote EMT, exacerbating tubular-interstitial injury [71]. Brahma-related gene 1 (BRG1) exacerbates fibrosis by restraining autophagy through Wnt/β-catenin signaling [72]. Overall, these findings suggest that the regulatory effects of IL-18 on the Wnt signaling pathway are highly tissue-specific and disease context-dependent. Of note, the causal relationship between IL-18 and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway in SLE and LN remains unclear.

In both LN patients and the mouse model, we documented sustained abnormal activation of Wnt/β-catenin signalling and identified IL-18 as a likely upstream driver. Specifically, we observed significant upregulation of β-catenin, pGSK-3β (Ser9), and CK1α in renal tissues from LN patients (Fig. 1F–J) and MRL/lpr mice (Fig. 3), indicating that Wnt/β-catenin signaling is highly active during LN and may contribute to renal inflammation and tissue injury. These findings were consistent with previous reports of aberrant Wnt activation in chronic kidney disease [73], further supporting a central role for Wnt/β-catenin signaling in renal pathology. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that IL-18 may mediate tubular-interstitial injury in LN by modulating the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. For validation, we conducted a series of experiments to establish a causal link between IL-18 and Wnt signaling (Figs. 3 and 4, and 5). On the one hand, animal studies showed that IL-18 administration in MRL/lpr mice significantly increased renal β-catenin expression and GSK-3β (Ser9) phosphorylation, along with increases in proteinuria, serum creatinine, anti-dsDNA antibody levels, tubulointerstitial inflammation, and AI scores. These findings suggest that IL-18 may indirectly promote Wnt-pathway activation by inhibiting GSK-3β activity and thereby exacerbates renal injury. Moreover, similar effects were observed in C57 control mice, further underscoring this mechanism. Conversely, inhibiting IL-18 significantly reduced β-catenin levels and improved tubulointerstitial pathology, validating the upstream role of IL-18 in Wnt/β-catenin signaling activation. The blockade of the Wnt pathway achieved comparable therapeutic effects to Caspase-1 inhibitor. Reactivating Wnt signaling in IL-18-inhibited animals restored β-catenin levels and was accompanied by increases in proteinuria, serum creatinine, and anti-dsDNA antibodies, together with aggravated tubulointerstitial pathology, further confirming that the Wnt pathway serves as the critical downstream executor of IL-18-driven renal injury. On the other hand, in-vitro studies showed that IL-18 promoted proliferation of HK-2 cells by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, through regulation of β-catenin, including transcription, post-translational modification, and nuclear translocation. This effect was amplified by a Wnt agonist and attenuated by pathway inhibitors. Importantly, immunofluorescence further showed that IL-18induced nuclear enrichment of β-catenin reflects both elevated total protein and its efficient nuclear translocation, a process critical for activating downstream target genes, promoting pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, and aggravating tubular epithelial cell injury. Collectively, our findings revealed that the role of IL-18 in LN extends beyond its conventional function as a pro-inflammatory amplifier; rather, IL-18 directly modulates tubular epithelial cell behavior and the local inflammatory microenvironment via Wnt/β-catenin signaling, serving as an upstream regulatory factor of LN pathogenesis.

GSK-3β is a key negative regulator of the Wnt pathway. Phosphorylation at its Ser9 residue inhibits its kinase activity, thereby indirectly stabilizing β-catenin and promoting its cytoplasmic accumulation [74, 75]. In renal tissues from LN patients and mouse models, pGSK-3β (Ser9) levels were significantly increased, while total GSK-3β remained unchanged (Figs. 1G-H and 3A/C/D), indicating functional inactivation of GSK-3β and resulting in persistent activation of the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. In HK-2 cells, inhibition of IL-18 or Wnt signaling reduced GSK-3β (Ser9) phosphorylation and β-catenin accumulation and nuclear translocation (Fig. 4D), suggesting that IL-18 promotes aberrant activation of the Wnt pathway via GSK-3β (Ser9) phosphorylation. Moreover, IL-18 is a canonical Th1-promoting cytokine that positively regulates IFN-γ in a feedback loop [76], we further explored the relationship between the two: knockdown of IFN-γ significantly attenuated IL-18–mediated GSK-3β inactivation, led to β-catenin up-regulation and its nuclear translocation, demonstrating that interrupting the IL-18/IFN-γ axis negatively regulates Wnt/β-catenin signalling. Reddy et al. [70] showed that IL-18 can activate Akt-dependent phosphorylation of GSK-3β at Ser9 via the WISP1/PI3K-Akt pathway, resulting in its functional inactivation. In neurons, IL-18 increases total GSK-3β protein and alters its kinase activity, thereby driving pathological tau phosphorylation and neurodegeneration [77]. Likewise, in tongue squamous-cell carcinoma, IL-18 concurrently elevates GSK-3β expression and Ser9 phosphorylation; while application of GSK-3β inhibitors partially reversed the pro-proliferative effects [78]. Together, these data indicate that IL-18 regulates GSK-3β activity via distinct signalling modules in a context- and tissue-specific manner. Although GSK-3β inactivation classically stabilises β-catenin and drives its nuclear translocation to activate downstream target genes [79], GSK-3β regulates more than 40 known substrates and participates in diverse signaling pathways, including immunometabolism, inflammatory responses, and cytoskeletal remodeling [80–82]. Therefore, whether IL-18-induced GSK-3β inactivation acts solely through the canonical Wnt/β-catenin pathway or also involves β-catenin-independent mechanisms remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

CK1α, a key kinase in the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, promotes β-catenin degradation under basal conditions by phosphorylating its N-terminus and facilitating formation of the degradation complex with Axin and APC. Upon Wnt activation, this process is blocked, leading to β-catenin accumulation and downstream gene activation [60, 83, 84]. Here we showed that CK1α protein was markedly elevated inrenal tissues from LN patients and mouse models, and in HK-2 cells (Figs. 1I and 3A/E, 4D). Exogenous IL-18 induced CK1α expression; this induction was amplified by a Wnt agonist and reversed by either Wnt inhibition or knockdown of IFN-γ, indicating that IL-18 controls CK1α through a Wnt- and IFN-γ-dependent signalling network. Traditionally, CK1α is considered a negative regulator within the Wnt/β-catenin pathway, primarily facilitating β-catenin degradation. Inconsistently, we found that despite pronounced CK1α upregulation following IL-18 stimulation, β-catenin continued to accumulate and remained functionally active. Several explanations may account for this phenomenon. First, because β-catenin stability depends chiefly on GSK-3β, the observed GSK-3β inactivation may obscure the functional impact of CK1α up-regulation. Second, although CK1α protein levels were elevated, its kinase activity was not assessed. The increased CK1α may be inactive or subject to atypical regulatory mechanisms. Third, as a signaling hub, CK1α’s function may be context-dependent, and its impact across pathways remains unclear. Jia et al. [85] positioned CK1α as a central node between Wnt and Hedgehog signaling, capable of shifting roles under different pathological conditions [86]. CK1ε provides a striking example: altered substrate specificity and subcellular localization convert it from a negative to a positive regulator [87]. Additionally, CK1α participates in the cross-regulation of multiple signaling networks, including Hedgehog [88], circadian rhythm [89], DNA repair, and cell cycle control, highlighting its functional versatility and suggesting a variable role in the inflammatory microenvironment of LN. Taken together, our data indicated that CK1α up-regulation in LN may reflect IL-18-driven compensatory feedback and a pathological functional switch; future studies should incorporate CK1α activity assays and functional intervention experiments to further elucidate its precise role in the IL-18–Wnt/β-catenin signaling axis.

IL-18 functions as a central hub within the inflammatory network, bridging innate and adaptive immunity. It can skew naive T cells toward the Th1 subset [90] and elicit abundant [91], dose-dependent IFN-γ release [92], earning it the designation “IFN-γ-inducing factor”; the two molecules then reinforce each other through a positive-feedback loop [76]. The pathogenic role of IFN-γ in LN is well documented [93–97], which promotes downstream pro-inflammatory cascades, autoimmunity, and renal tubular and interstitial injury. IL-10, primarily secreted by Th2 cells, is an anti-inflammatory mediator that suppresses Th1 cytokines [98, 99]. In this study, exogenous IL-18 administration to MRL/lpr mice markedly elevated serum IFN-γ and splenic IL-1β and IL-6 levels while reducing splenic IL-10 (Fig. 6A); blocking IL-18 produced the opposite profile. These findings indicate tight signal coupling between IL-18 and Th1-promoting cytokines, and align with the renal cytokine signature reported by Liu et al. [10, 100] in LN patients. In vitro experiments further confirmed the pro-inflammatory role of IL-18 in human renal tubular epithelial cell line HK-2. After IL-18 stimulation, HK-2 cells showed marked increases in IL-1β and IL-6, whereas IL-10 and TGF-β1 expression was reduced (Fig. 6B). Activation of the Wnt pathway also promoted this inflammatory response, whereas silencing IFN-γ or pharmacological inhibition of Wnt/β-catenin markedly attenuated this effect. These findings identify the IL-18–Wnt/β-catenin signaling axis as a central regulator of inflammation within the LN milieu. Collectively, IL-18 functions not only as an amplifier of inflammation but also as a pivotal driver of local signaling networks in LN; by enhancing Wnt/β-catenin activity in tubular epithelial cells, it establishes a self-sustaining pro-inflammatory network that exacerbates renal damage.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that IL-18 critically drives LN progression by regulating Wnt/β-catenin signaling in renal tubular epithelial cells and identified GSK-3β as a key signaling node within this axis. IL-18 markedly elevated the levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines IFN-γ, IL-1β, and IL-6 while concurrently suppressing IL-10 and TGF-β1 expression, thereby disrupting immune homeostasis at the cytokine level. Moreover, by activating the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, IL-18 integrated and amplified the inflammatory response that changed the local renal microenvironment, sustained persistent inflammation, and accelerated the accumulation of tissue damage, ultimately driving the progression of LN. However, several limitations remain to be addressed. Firstly, the specific cellular origin of IL-18 in the renal tubulointerstitium (e.g., monocyte-derived or tissue-resident macrophages, T cells, NK cells, or intrinsic renal tubular epithelial cells) remains to be identified. Furthermore, other types of primary glomerulonephritis were not included as disease controls; therefore, future studies must validate the specificity of this pathway in LN. Additionally, the Caspase-1 inhibitor simultaneously affects both IL-18 and IL-1β activation; their relative contributions in LN require further investigation. Secondly, in vitro mechanistic validation was primarily based on the HK-2 cell line, which requires confirmation in primary renal tubular epithelial cells. Additionally, the pleiotropic nature of IL-18 and its effects on non-tubular epithelial target cells warrant further investigation. Thirdly, the specific mechanism by which IFN-γ participates in IL-18-induced activation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway has not been fully elucidated. Further investigation is needed to clarify its hierarchical position in signal transduction, patterns of interaction with key Wnt components, and potential feedback regulatory relationships. Fourthly, the regulation of non-canonical Wnt signaling pathways by IL-18 remains unclear. Finally, this study observed a significant increase in CK1α levels; however, its kinase activity has not yet been verified. In addition, whether CK1α exerts context-dependent multifunctional roles across different signaling pathways remains to be further explored.
