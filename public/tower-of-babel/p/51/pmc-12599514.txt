Interocular Posterior Retinal Curvature Asymmetry and Its Relationship With Myopic Differences in Anisometropic Children
Authors: Gongpeng Sun, Chen Hou, Jing Gong, Muga Emu, Rong Lin, Bi Yang, Guangjing Dong, Ling Zhao, Chunyan Lei, Sheng Gao, Meixia Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate interocular differences in posterior retinal curvature in anisometropic children and their association with interocular differences in myopia.MethodsForty-two non–highly myopic children underwent ultra-widefield swept-source optical coherence tomography for 24-mm × 20-mm macular-centered retinal imaging. Gaussian curvature measurements were derived from corrected three-dimensional retinal reconstructions, based on Bruch's membrane segmentation. The retina was divided into six regions (I–VI) with diameters of 1, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 mm according to the Early Treatment of Diabetic Retinopathy Study grid standard. Regions I to III were classified as the macular zone, while regions IV to VI constituted the peripheral zone.ResultsThe mean difference in spherical equivalent between the more myopic and less myopic eyes was 1.42 ± 0.51 diopters, while the difference in axial length (AL) was 0.58 ± 0.32 mm. The average retinal curvature in the macular zone was significantly steeper in the more myopic eyes (0.55 ± 0.15 × 10⁻² mm⁻²) compared to the less myopic eyes (0.46 ± 0.11 × 10⁻² mm⁻²; P < 0.001). Retinal curvature was significantly higher in regions I to IV of the more myopic eyes but lower in region VI (all P < 0.001). Furthermore, positive correlation was observed between macular retinal curvature disparities and AL differences (R = 0.340, P = 0.028), while peripheral retinal curvature disparities were negatively correlated with AL differences (R = –0.374, P = 0.015).ConclusionsAxial elongation drives region-specific posterior retinal remodeling in anisometropic children, steepening the macula and flattening the periphery.Translational RelevanceBy investigating retinal curvature in anisometropic children, we provide morphologic insights that establish a basis for strategies to prevent and manage pediatric myopia.

Introduction

The prevalence of myopia is increasing at an alarming rate, posing a significant global public health challenge.1 Notably, the increasing incidence of myopia among pediatric populations has garnered considerable attention.2,3 Epidemiologic data indicate a consistent global surge in pediatric myopia cases, with projections estimating a prevalence of 39.8% by 2050.3 Myopia, particularly high myopia, is frequently associated with axial elongation, which triggers structural changes in the eye. These changes involve pathological alterations in the sclera, retina, choroid, and optic nerve, dramatically increasing the risk of complications that can lead to visual impairment or even blindness.4,5 Consequently, early intervention for pediatric myopia is imperative,6,7 as it not only mitigates the progression of myopia but also minimizes the long-term risk of vision loss caused by ocular pathologies in later life.8

Investigating ocular morphology during early childhood can effectively identify initial changes associated with myopia, thereby establishing a basis for developing evidence-based prevention strategies. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) enables direct visualization of ocular structures and three-dimensional measurement of morphologic parameters, including ocular height, width, and volume.9–13 Despite these advantages, the clinical application of MRI is hindered by factors such as operational complexity, prolonged scanning times, limited resolution, and challenges in parameter extraction, rendering it unsuitable for real-time dynamic assessment of morphologic changes. Recently, ultra-widefield swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT) has emerged as a promising alternative. This technology enables rapid acquisition of high-resolution three-dimensional images of the posterior pole of the eye and supports precise reconstruction of retinal morphology along with quantitative analysis of retinal curvature (RC) in localized subregions.14–17 Wu et al.15 compared children with high myopia and low myopia, demonstrating that RC in the macular region increases with myopia progression, while peripheral RC decreases. These findings suggest that myopia progression involves steepening of the macular retina and flattening of the peripheral retina, although individual variability may affect study outcomes.

Patients with anisometropia possess identical genetic backgrounds, environmental exposures, and behavioral patterns in both eyes, yet they demonstrate a distinct gradient in myopia severity. This unique feature provides a valuable opportunity to identify morphologic traits specifically associated with variations in myopia severity, while mitigating potential confounding factors. Building on this premise, the present study examines a cohort of anisometropic children, utilizing SS-OCT technology to reconstruct RC. Additionally, it explores interocular differences in ocular morphology and their association with asymmetric changes in myopia severity between the two eyes. The primary objective is to elucidate the patterns of ocular morphologic remodeling during myopia progression in children, thereby offering morphologic insights to inform strategies for the prevention and management of pediatric myopia.

Methods

Study Participants

This cross-sectional study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of West China Hospital, Sichuan University (Ethics Approval No. 2024-2546), and was conducted in strict compliance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to participation, written informed consent was obtained from all children and their parents. The study was carried out in the ophthalmology outpatient department of West China Hospital, Sichuan University, between September 2024 and February 2025 and involved the recruitment of anisometropic children aged 9 to 14 years. Eligibility criteria included bilateral myopia with an interocular difference in spherical equivalent (SE) refraction of ≥1 diopter (D) and a best-corrected visual acuity of 0.00 logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution or better. Detailed medical histories were collected through parental interviews, and all participants underwent comprehensive ophthalmic evaluations. These examinations included intraocular pressure measurement, cycloplegic subjective refraction, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, dilated fundus examination, ocular biometric measurements, and ultra-widefield swept-source optical coherence tomography angiography (SS-OCTA) imaging. Axial length (AL), corneal thickness, anterior chamber depth, and corneal curvature were assessed using the IOL Master 700 (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany).

All enrolled children had no history of ocular conditions other than myopia, including a verified absence of retinopathy of prematurity. Additionally, none had undergone ocular surgeries or retinal laser treatments, nor had they received any interventions for myopia control, such as atropine eye drops, orthokeratology lenses, or defocus spectacle lenses. SS-OCTA images included in the analysis were required to exhibit complete visualization of the examined region and achieve a quality score of at least 6 (on a scale of 1–10). Data from participants were included in the analysis only if both eyes met all specified criteria.

Image Acquisition

Following pupil dilation with tropicamide eye drops, ultra-widefield SS-OCT imaging was performed on both eyes using the BM-400K BMizar device (TowardPi Medical Technology, Beijing, China) using the OCT angiography protocol (field of view: 81° × 68°). However, only the structural information was used from the angiography protocol for this analysis. The device employs a swept-source vertical cavity surface-emitting laser with a central wavelength of 1060 nm, an A-scan frequency of 400 kHz, an axial resolution of 3.8 µm, a lateral resolution of 10 µm, and an imaging depth of 6 mm for the retina and choroid. Each 24-mm × 20-mm image consists of 1280 B-scan lines, with 1536 A-scans per line and a scanning interval of 15.625 µm. Two repeated scans were performed at each fixed location. To minimize eye motion artifacts, particularly anterior-posterior movements during image acquisition, a single B-scan through the foveal center of the macula in the slow scan direction was obtained prior to OCTA imaging. This prescan was used to correct axial eye movements in the slow scan direction, thereby improving data accuracy.

Analysis of Retinal Curvature

RC is directly derived from the local Gaussian curvature (K) measured on Bruch's membrane at each point. At each surface point, curvatures are first computed along different normal sections, and the maximum and minimum of these values are defined as the principal curvatures, κ₁ and κ₂, respectively. The local Gaussian curvature (K), which represents RC in this study, is calculated as the product of the principal curvatures (K = κ₁·κ₂), expressed in units of mm⁻². The radii of curvature corresponding to κ₁ and κ₂ are denoted as R₁ and R₂, respectively, and a higher Gaussian curvature corresponds to smaller radii of curvature, reflecting a steeper surface. The mathematical relationship among K, κ₁, κ₂, R₁, and R₂ is defined as k1=1R1,k2=1R2,K=k1·k2=1R1·R2.

The device software utilized a graph-based segmentation method to automatically layer OCT scan data and extract Bruch's membrane as the reference surface. A three-dimensional reconstruction of retinal morphology was then performed using plugins based on MATLAB algorithms (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA), accompanied by the generation of distortion-corrected Gaussian curvature at each point (Figs. 1A, 1B; see the Supplementary Method S1 for additional methodologic details regarding the computation of the fundus Gaussian curvature). Prior to retinal morphology analysis, the accuracy of Bruch's membrane segmentation was verified. Manual adjustments were applied in areas with minor inaccuracies to ensure precise delineation of the membrane's surface. Following this, an initial scale correction was applied to the original B-scan frames based on the eye's AL to account for individual ocular magnification effects.18 Subsequently, to further refine the reconstructed retinal geometry and correct for optical distortions inherent in wide-field imaging, Zernike polynomial fitting was applied to the volumetric data. This approach mitigated residual shape inaccuracies potentially caused by minor eye movements or segmentation errors (see Supplementary Fig. S1 for the three-dimensional retinal reconstruction before and after correction). By selecting an optimal Zernike polynomial order, the mean fitting error was maintained below 15 µm, improving correction accuracy. Subsequently, Gaussian curvature of the fundus was calculated using its defined formula, with results visualized via color distribution maps for intuitive interpretation. The detailed algorithm for RC computation is described in relevant studies.14 Repeated measurements have shown high consistency and reproducible reliability.15

Data Acquisition

The mean RC data within the Early Treatment of Diabetic Retinopathy Study grid, centered on the macula, were computed using the device's built-in plugin (Fig. 1B). To ensure reliability, the analysis area was confined to a circular region with a 15-mm diameter centered on the macula, excluding peripheral zones prone to incomplete image acquisition. Within this defined region, the central area, characterized by a 1-mm diameter, was analyzed separately as RC I, while the remainder was segmented into concentric circular zones with outer diameters of 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 mm. Mean curvature values for these zones were calculated and designated RC II, RC III, RC IV, RC V, and RC VI, respectively (Fig. 1C). This segmentation approach aimed to characterize retinal morphologic changes extending from the macula toward peripheral regions. For further analysis, curvature values from regions with diameters of 1 mm, 3 mm, and 6 mm were weighted by their respective areas to define macular curvature, whereas values from regions with diameters of 9 mm, 12 mm, and 15 mm were weighted similarly to define peripheral curvature (Fig. 1D). Additionally, the macular and peripheral regions were segmented into quadrants—nasal, inferior, temporal, and superior—and curvature values for these quadrants were calculated individually. The quadrants in the macular region were labeled as N1, I1, T1, and S1, while those in the peripheral region were designated N2, I2, T2, and S2 (Fig. 1D). This quadrant-based analysis provided finer details of RC in specific orientations for enhanced characterization.

Sample Size Calculation

The sample size calculation was conducted using G*Power 3.1 software (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany). A paired design was adopted, with the two eyes of anisometropic patients serving as paired controls. Using a two-tailed paired t-test, the significance level (α) was set at 0.05, statistical power (1 – β) at 0.8, and the anticipated effect size at 0.5. This calculation determined that a minimum of 34 participants was necessary for the study. Ultimately, 42 participants were enrolled to ensure sufficient statistical power.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test and by inspecting histograms. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and RC measurements were uniformly converted to units of 10⁻² mm⁻² for consistent reporting. Differences in RC and other clinical characteristics between fellow eyes were calculated by subtracting the values of the less myopic eyes from those of the more myopic eyes. Depending on the normality of the data, paired t-tests or Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used to compare biometric parameters and RC differences between fellow eyes in anisometropic patients. Variability in RC across the four quadrants was analyzed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Friedman tests. Correlation analyses, employing either Pearson's or Spearman's correlation methods as appropriate, were performed to evaluate associations between interocular difference variables. To account for multiple comparisons, we applied the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction with q = 0.05. A two-tailed P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Basic Characteristics

The study included 42 children with anisometropia, comprising 22 males (52.4%) and 20 females (47.6%), aged 9 to 14 years, with a mean age of 11.4 ± 1.5 years. The SE and AL of the more myopic eyes were –3.06 ± 1.25 D and 24.83 ± 0.82 mm, respectively, compared to –1.64 ± 1.24 D and 24.25 ± 0.71 mm in the less myopic eyes. The interocular difference in SE was 1.42 ± 0.51 D, while the AL difference was 0.58 ± 0.32 mm. A strong negative correlation was observed between the interocular differences in SE and AL (R = –0.706, P < 0.001). The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study population are detailed in Table 1. Among all included eyes, RC in the macular zone was significantly lower than in the peripheral retina (z = –7.167, P < 0.001). Across regions I to VI, RC was lowest in region I, highest in region V, and subsequently decreased in region VI (Table 2).

Overall and Regional Retinal Curvature Differences between Anisometropic Fellow Eyes

The average RC in the macular region was 0.55 ± 0.15 × 10⁻² mm⁻² in the more myopic eyes and 0.46 ± 0.11 × 10⁻² mm⁻² in the less myopic eyes, with an interocular difference of 0.09 ± 0.09 × 10⁻² mm⁻². The RC in the macular region was significantly higher in the more myopic eyes than in the less myopic eyes (P < 0.001, Table 2). Representative retinal curvature maps from both eyes of an anisometropic patient are presented in Figure 2. Additionally, in all four quadrants of the macular region, the RC of the more myopic eyes was significantly higher (all P < 0.001, Fig. 3A). No significant difference in RC was observed in the peripheral region between anisometropic fellow eyes (P = 0.191) or in any quadrant of this region (all P > 0.05, Fig. 3B).

For the less myopic eyes, no significant differences in RC were found among the quadrants in the macular region (ANOVA, P = 0.353). In contrast, the more myopic eyes showed significant differences in RC among the quadrants (Friedman test, P < 0.001). Post hoc Bonferroni correction revealed that RC I1 and RC T1 were significantly higher than RC S1 (P < 0.001 and P = 0.014, respectively). In the peripheral region, significant differences in RC were observed among the quadrants in both the more myopic eyes (ANOVA, P = 0.003) and the less myopic eyes (ANOVA, P = 0.014).

Correlation of Interocular Retinal Curvature Differences With Myopia Parameters and Other Clinical Characteristics

The interocular AL difference between the more and less myopic eyes was positively correlated with the macular RC difference (R = 0.340, P = 0.028) and negatively correlated with the peripheral RC difference (R = –0.374, P = 0.015, Fig. 5). Within the macular region, curvature differences for RC I, RC II, and RC III were significantly positively correlated with AL differences (all P < 0.05). In the peripheral region, only RC VI showed a significant negative correlation with AL differences (P < 0.001, Fig. 5). No correlations were observed between RC differences and AL differences across individual quadrants in either the macular or peripheral regions. Regarding SE, a significant correlation was found only between RC VI and the SE difference (P = 0.019, Fig. 5; Table 3). After Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction for multiple comparisons, only the correlation between axial length difference and retinal curvature in region VI remained significant (R = –0.536, FDR-adjusted P < 0.05).

Discussion

This study explores alterations in posterior retinal morphology during myopia progression in children by comparing the anisometropic fellow eyes. The more myopic eyes demonstrated greater RC in the macular region compared to the less myopic eyes. Specifically, RC in regions I to IV was higher in the more myopic eyes, whereas it was lower in region VI. Moreover, the interocular difference in macular RC showed a positive correlation with AL difference, while the peripheral RC difference was negatively correlated. These results indicate that during the progression of childhood myopia, the macular region steepens posteriorly, whereas the peripheral retina flattens, highlighting distinct morphologic adaptations associated with axial elongation.

Myopia not only causes visual impairment due to optical defocus but is also strongly associated with irreversible blinding conditions, such as myopic maculopathy, retinal detachment, diffuse chorioretinal atrophy, cataract, and glaucoma.19–22 These complications not only jeopardize ocular health but can also result in permanent visual dysfunction, significantly diminishing quality of life and emphasizing the urgent need for myopia control. Importantly, childhood myopia markedly increases the likelihood of progressing to high myopia and severe vision-threatening complications in adulthood.21 Therefore, effective interventions to slow myopic progression during childhood are essential to reduce the risk of serious visual morbidity.

MRI technology facilitates the capture of comprehensive whole-eye morphologic features that are unattainable through conventional ocular examinations, making it a widely utilized tool in studies of ocular morphology.9–11,13,22 In adult myopic eyes, axial elongation predominantly affects the sagittal diameter, with minimal changes observed in the horizontal and vertical diameters, leading to a gradual transformation of the eyeball shape from spherical to prolate ellipsoid.23 In cases of high myopia in adults, the perifoveal region becomes sharper, and the curvature of the posterior pole is markedly greater compared to the peripheral regions.22 Conversely, the eyeball shape in children, including those with myopia, tends to be predominantly oblate.24 During childhood, axial elongation in nonmyopic eyes occurs in a balanced manner across length, width, and height, whereas myopic eyes exhibit disproportionate axial elongation along with an increase in horizontal width, resulting in a progressively oblong shape.12 This relatively pronounced axial elongation in myopic children may contribute to a more curved, spindle-shaped posterior pole.24

Using ultra-widefield SS-OCT technology, RC measurement methods based on Bruch's membrane morphology enable quantitative analysis of curvature variations across different regions of the posterior pole. Compared to MRI, this approach offers a more precise and detailed evaluation of retinal morphologic changes.14–17 Previous studies have demonstrated that children with high myopia, compared to those with low myopia, typically exhibit increased posterior convexity in the macular retina and flattening of the peripheral retina.15 Consistent findings were observed in this study, albeit with regional differences. Wu et al.15 reported that, in highly myopic children compared to their counterparts with lower myopia, RC I significantly increased, while RC III to RC VI decreased. In contrast, our study found that more myopic eyes exhibited increased RC I to IV and decreased RC VI compared to less myopic eyes. These discrepancies may stem from differences in the inclusion criteria (non–highly myopic children), age distribution, and individual variability. Despite regional variations, the overall trend aligns with previous findings, characterized by greater posterior convexity in the macular regions and flattened peripheral regions. This morphologic pattern may exacerbate peripheral hyperopic defocus, thereby facilitating further axial elongation.24 Additionally, opposing RC in regions IV and VI between fellow eyes may account for the absence of significant differences in peripheral RC between the two groups.

In adults, highly myopic eyes exhibit considerable variability in posterior RC.16 This study revealed that RC differences across macular quadrants were insignificant in less myopic eyes but became significant in more myopic eyes. These findings suggest that even without the pronounced axial elongation observed in high myopia, increased variability in macular retinal morphology may still occur. Additionally, the greater RC in the temporal and inferior regions indicates heightened posterior convexity in these areas, corresponding to the trend of reduced choroidal thickness observed in the same regions.25

This study found significant associations between interocular RC differences in multiple regions and AL differences, whereas the relationship between RC differences and SE differences was significant only in region VI. These results suggest that RC is more responsive to axial elongation during myopia progression, aligning with findings from previous studies.20 After FDR correction, only the AL–RC VI association remained robust. Although some correlations reached nominal significance before correction, none of the macular region associations survived this stringent control for type I error. This suggests that the macular findings should be interpreted with caution, as they may represent chance results. Additionally, the limited sample size may have reduced statistical power to detect true associations after correction. Future studies with larger cohorts or more targeted hypotheses are needed to further clarify morphologic changes in the macular region of anisometropic myopia. Subsequent analysis revealed negative correlations between variations in macular RC and changes in inner retinal thickness, total retinal thickness, and choroidal thickness. The increased RC may contribute to mechanical stretching and stress, resulting in progressive thinning of the associated posterior ocular structures.

The primary strength of this study is its paired anisometropic design, which effectively controlled systemic confounding factors and minimized the impact of individual variability. By comparing fellow eyes, this method offered a clear insight into the retinal morphologic changes linked to more myopic eyes. However, the cross-sectional nature of the study poses limitations, underscoring the need for future prospective longitudinal studies to investigate the dynamic evolution of retinal morphology during myopia progression. Additionally, the study did not include retinal morphologic data from anisometropic children with high myopia, highlighting the need for further research to bridge this gap. Furthermore, current imaging technologies have a restricted field of view, limiting curvature data acquisition to the posterior pole while excluding peripheral regions such as the mid-periphery and equator. Consequently, the analysis focused exclusively on posterior retinal characteristics. Another potential limitation is that all images were acquired using a single imaging device and correction algorithm, which may introduce device- or algorithm-dependent effects. The geometric correction for the fan-shaped scan distortion represents one such limitation, as it only accounted for individual axial length, while other significant anterior segment parameters, such as anterior chamber depth, were not included. Further validation across different imaging platforms and correction methods is warranted. Moreover, most correlations in the macular region did not remain statistically significant after multiple comparison correction, possibly due to limited statistical power. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require confirmation in larger cohorts.

In summary, this study utilized SS-OCT to evaluate posterior RC differences in the anisometropic fellow eyes of children with non–high myopia. The results revealed a significant steepening of macular curvature and a flattening of peripheral curvature as myopia progressed in children. These morphologic changes enhance our understanding of the mechanisms driving myopia progression and offer valuable insights for developing effective strategies for myopia prevention and control.
