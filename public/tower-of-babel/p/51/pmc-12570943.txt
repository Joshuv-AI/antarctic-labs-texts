Assessment of penicillin allergy evaluation practices among clinicians in China: a nationwide cross-sectional survey
Authors: Dandan Ji, Bijie Hu, Zhangzhang Chen, Xiaodong Gao, Yuxia Zhang, Zheng Zheng, Haojun Zhang, Zheng Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThis study investigated the perceived clinicians’ roles in penicillin allergy assessment among medical staff in tertiary hospitals in China.DesignThis was a multicentre cross-sectional survey.SettingThe study was conducted at 89 tertiary hospitals in eastern and western China from March to May 2024.ParticipantsA survey was conducted involving 8493 medical staff from tertiary hospitals in eastern and western China using multistage sampling.Outcome measuresA self-designed questionnaire was used to assess evaluation status of penicillin allergy assessment, including allergy history assessment and recording, skin test result evaluation during hospitalisation, allergic reaction recording at discharge and awareness of allergy assessment teams.ResultsAmong 8493 healthcare professionals (doctors 40.0%, nurses 56.3%, pharmacists 3.7%), significant gaps existed in penicillin allergy documentation: while 92.0% acknowledged the need to document specific drug names, only 66.2% practised this (nurses 62.2% vs pharmacists 82.0%, p<0.01). Nurses dominated skin test evaluation (95.3% participation), with 77.5% of all staff endorsing their primary role. For ambiguous test results, 54.6% preferred multi-person consensus (nurses 63.9%, p<0.01). Post-discharge documentation prioritised drug names (94.4%) over clinical indications (70.3%). Pharmacists demonstrated superior documentation awareness (p<0.05). Only 45.5% confirmed having an antimicrobial allergy assessment team, though clinical pharmacists (89.2%) and specialist nurses (88.2%) were deemed essential members.ConclusionsCurrently, the assessment and recording methods for antimicrobial allergies by the medical staff of tertiary hospitals in China are not sufficiently accurate. Nurses play a key role in the assessment of antimicrobial allergies. Thus, medical staff, especially nurses, should receive more vigorous training, and structured assessment tools for antimicrobial allergies based on clinical decision support systems should be devised for them. Our findings also reiterate the need to establish penicillin allergy assessment teams at the hospital level.Keywords: Hospitals, Allergy, Risk Assessment, China, Antibiotics

Introduction

Over recent decades, it has become standard to include antimicrobial allergy labels due to safety concerns. Globally, approximately 8% to 25% of individuals are reported to have antimicrobial allergy labels, but only 5% to 20% have true allergies.1,3 Over-labelling of antimicrobial allergies has emerged as a significant medical issue, resulting in the unwarranted use of alternative broad-spectrum antimicrobials, escalation of antimicrobial treatment regimens, increased medical costs for patients, prolonged hospital stays and a heightened risk of infection by resistant bacteria.4,6

Internationally, professional and public health organisations have acknowledged the implications of inaccurate penicillin allergy labels. Consequently, they have begun advocating for proactive penicillin allergy evaluations to facilitate the removal of the penicillin allergy label when the allergy is disproven, a process referred to as penicillin allergy ‘delabeling’. A penicillin allergy evaluation typically involves a thorough allergy history assessment, often followed by a drug challenge, and sometimes preceded by skin testing.7 In China, the concept of penicillin delabelling remains in its nascent stages. The objective of allergy assessment is to determine whether patients can safely use penicillin, which includes evaluating the patient’s history of penicillin allergy and conducting penicillin skin tests. In accordance with China’s national circumstances, the evaluation of penicillin allergy in this study does not incorporate drug provocation tests. We found that inaccurate penicillin allergy assessment caused patients to be mistakenly labelled with an allergy label and lose the right to use penicillin. For instance, patients labelled with a ‘penicillin allergy’, or those with a positive penicillin skin test are frequently excluded from receiving penicillin-based treatments, and this restriction may occasionally extend to cephalosporins.8 Novel strategies to increase access to properly standardised penicillin allergy evaluations are urgently needed to reduce excessive penicillin allergy labels.

Accurate penicillin allergy evaluation and documentation are essential for effective drug management and preventing overdiagnosis of allergies. However, uncertainty about who is responsible for assessing penicillin allergies leads to a lack of ownership. This article examines the roles of physicians, nurses and pharmacists in assessing penicillin allergies, including recording allergic history at admission, evaluating skin test results during hospitalisation and documenting allergic reactions at discharge.

Methods

Study participants and data collection

This multicentre cross-sectional study was designed and conducted by our team and coordinated by Shanghai Municipal Hospital Clinical Capacity Promotion and Improvement of Infection Prevention and Control Specialist Alliance and Gansu Provincial Infection Management Provincial Infection Management Quality Control Centre. A total of 89 tertiary hospitals participated in this study, including 64 general hospitals and 25 specialised hospitals, with 27 located in eastern China and 62 in western China.

Hospitals were selected using stratified sampling to ensure geographic and institutional diversity. Departments were prioritised based on antimicrobial use frequency. Institutions equipped with both emergency departments (EDs) and intensive care units (ICUs) were required to include one ED and one ICU, supplemented by four medical and four surgical wards. For hospitals possessing either an ED or ICU but not both, one critical care unit was mandatorily selected along with five medical and four surgical wards. Hospitals lacking both ED and ICU services included five medical and five surgical wards. Among specialised hospitals, five clinical units were selected with priority given to EDs, ICUs (where available) and wards with high antimicrobial utilisation rates.

Between March and May 2024, convenience sampling was employed to recruit medical personnel from designated departments. The sample comprised clinicians and nurses, with six doctors and nurses selected from each department, as well as pharmacists, with six individuals chosen per hospital, prioritising clinical pharmacists.

The researcher contacted the person in charge of the infection prevention and control departments of each centre, introduced the research purpose and general scenario, and elaborated on the criteria and number of included departments and research participants. The person in charge of the infection prevention and control departments of each centre selected the departments and research participants who met the eligibility criteria (according to the research design) and collected the data using an online questionnaire (Online link: https://www.wjx.cn), after obtaining informed consent from the research respondents. To discourage participants from looking up information or providing arbitrary responses, each survey segment had enforced time constraints. Additionally, respondents were restricted from revisiting completed sections to modify their answers. All questionnaire items were completed by the respondents themselves. The web-based survey platform recorded respondents’' IP addresses in conjunction with manually provided hospital names, forming composite identifiers. In cases where multiple responses originated from the same IP address and bore the same hospital name, only the first submission was considered valid. The researcher reviewed the background data and contacted the head of the centre to remind the relevant departments to fill in.

This study followed Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines for cross-sectional studies.

Patient and public involvement

There was no patient or public involvement in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination of this study.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study were professionally qualified doctors, nurses and pharmacists who were engaged in clinical work and volunteered to participate in the survey. The exclusion criteria were training personnel and those who worked for less than 6 months in the year before the survey period.

Measures and instruments

General information questionnaire

The questionnaire covered the respondents’ sex, age, educational background, department, professional title, hospital type, work experience in years and whether they received training on antimicrobial management in the past 2 years.

Questionnaire on the status quo of penicillin allergy assessment management

The questionnaire was systematically developed through a rigorous process. Initially, we conducted a comprehensive literature review and held multidisciplinary team discussions to align the instrument with study objectives. Content validity was established through expert consultation with five specialists in infectious diseases, pharmacology, clinical medicine and nursing, yielding item-level content validity indices ranging from 0.875 to 1.000 and a scale-level content validity index of 0.988. The draft questionnaire underwent three iterative revisions following two rounds of pilot testing (n=30 per round) to optimise question clarity and relevance.

The final validated instrument comprised four domains assessing: (1) allergy history documentation (items 1–3), (2) in-hospital skin test evaluation (items 4–6), (3) discharge allergy recording (item 7) and (4) awareness of allergy assessment teams (items 8–10). Reliability testing in a pre-survey of 233 healthcare workers demonstrated good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α=0.801). Additional details are provided in the online supplemental questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

The data were double-checked, and statistical analysis was performed using SPSS26.0 software. Frequencies, percentages, means and SD were used to describe demographic data and population characteristics. The measurement data were expressed as the mean±SD. The independent sample t-test was used for comparison between two groups, and the one-way analysis of variance was used for comparison between three groups. Count data were expressed as the percentage of cases, and the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test was used for comparison between groups. The Bonferroni method was used for pairwise comparison between groups. Pvalues<0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic characteristics

A total of 9945 individuals responded to the study advertisement with 8514 consenting, and the overall survey response rate was 85.6%. After data examination, 21 questionnaires were excluded and 8493 respondents were included in the study. The study sample included 3392 doctors (40.0%), 4787 nurses (56.3%) and 317 pharmacists (3.7%). Among these, 289 (3.40%) were involved in infection management, and 7378 participants (86.9%) had received antimicrobial management training in the past 2 years (table 1). Assessment and record of allergy history at admission

The survey on the assessment and recording of the history of penicillin allergy among medical staff revealed that 7814 (92.0%) respondents were aware of the specific name of the drug evaluated during assessment of the history of anaphylaxis among hospitalised patients, and there was no statistically significant difference among doctors (92.4%), nurses (91.5%) and pharmacists (92.0%) (p=0.073). However, only 5623 (66.21%) respondents recorded the specific names of allergic drugs in the medical history; nurses (62.2%) recorded the specific names of allergic drugs less than doctors (70.34%) and pharmacists (82.0%), and the differences among the three were statistically significant (p<0.01). A total of 5390 (63.5%) medical staff had recorded the past allergic manifestations of drugs, including 2567 nurses (53.7%), 2546 doctors (75.1%) and 277 pharmacists (87.4%), and the differences among them were statistically significant (p<0.01).

The survey on the need to collect information on the antimicrobial allergy history showed that the skin test results of antibacterial drugs received extensive attention from medical personnel, while the dose received the least attention. A total of 8113 (95.5%) respondents thought that it was necessary to know the results of previous skin tests to evaluate the history of antimicrobial allergy, 7529 (88.7%) respondents thought it was necessary to know the interval between past drug administration and the allergic reaction and 7292 (85.9%) respondents thought it was necessary to know the duration of the past allergic reaction. A total of 6803 (80.1%) respondents thought that it was necessary to know the treatment provided by medical staff during the onset of allergic reactions, and 6427 (75.7%) respondents thought it was necessary to know the previous dose of medication (online supplemental table 2).

Evaluation of skin test results during hospitalisation

The survey on the current status of evaluation of antimicrobial skin tests by medical personnel revealed that 83.6% of medical personnel, including 71.5% of doctors, 95.3% of nurses and 36.6% of pharmacists, participated in the judgement of penicillin skin test results in their daily work.

Nurses are the primary evaluators of the results of penicillin skin tests (95.3%), followed by doctors (71.5%) and pharmacists (36.6%). Most medical workers (77.5%) believe nurses should judge these results, while 13.6% prefer doctors and 3.6% prefer pharmacists. A minority suggest involvement of infectiologists (0.9%) or allergy specialists (4.4%). Among the different professional groups, nurses (95.26%) and doctors (71.5%) were more inclined to believe that the results of clinical antimicrobial skin testing should mainly be judged by nurses. The majority of pharmacists (59.9%) are of the opinion that nurses should evaluate these results, whereas 19.9% of pharmacists favour physicians for this role, and 12.0% prefer allergists.

In the event of ambiguity about the results of antimicrobial skin testing in clinical practice, 4639 (54.6%) respondents would invite two other medical personnel to participate in the judgement and take the majority judgement as the criterion, while 1884 (22.2%) respondents would consider the result positive for safety. Meanwhile, 1717 (20.2%) respondents would make the judgement based on the decision of medical staff with considerable experience or senior professional title, and 131 (1.54%) participants would decide independently.

Nurses are more likely to seek majority judgement (63.9%) compared with doctors (42.2%) and pharmacists (47.6%) (p<0.01; online supplemental table 2).

Records of anaphylaxis on discharge

The pharmaceutical and trade names of the drug (94.4%) and the date and timing of the allergic reaction (92.7%) were the top two contents needing documentation in the medical records after the occurrence of penicillin allergy according to the survey. At the bottom of the list were the indications based on disease diagnosis or selection of the drug for treatment (70.3%) and the drugs that patients were advised and cautioned against (78.4%). The others were the symptoms of the patient during the onset of the allergic reaction (88.5%), route of administration (87.6%), dose and frequency of drug administration or the number of days before the onset of the allergic reaction (86.2%). In addition, 3.4% of the medical staff thought that other information should be recorded such as vital signs during the allergic reaction, drug manufacturer name, batch number, etc. Pharmacists had the strongest knowledge of medical documentation from among doctors, nurses and pharmacists. Except for recording the drugs to be avoided, pharmacists had greater awareness of other aspects besides the medical records (p<0.05; online supplemental table 2).

Medical staff’s awareness of the antimicrobial allergy assessment team

The survey on medical staff’s awareness of antimicrobial allergy assessment teams revealed that 45.5% of participants reported their hospital had such a team, 20.3% said their hospital did not and 34.2% were unsure.

In terms of the prospective composition of the antimicrobial allergy assessment panel, clinical pharmacists (89.2%), specialist or senior nurses (88.2%) and allergy specialists (71.5%) emerged as the top three candidates. This preference was consistent across various professional groups, with doctors, nurses and pharmacists all selecting clinical pharmacists, specialist or senior nurses and allergy specialists as their top choices for panel membership. Notably, there was significant support among nurses for the inclusion of specialist or senior nurses in the antimicrobial allergy assessment team, with 4373 nurses (91.4%) expressing a desire for these roles to be filled by specialist or senior nurses (online supplemental table 2).

Discussion

Penicillin represents a vital and extensively utilised class of pharmaceuticals in clinical practice.9 10 Appropriate administration of penicillin facilitates effective management of a range of infectious diseases, whereas improper use may result in adverse outcomes.11,13 A key requirement for the appropriate use of antibiotics is the accurate assessment of a patient’s allergy status by healthcare professionals.14,16 This study highlights a major issue: there were inaccuracies and incompleteness of the antibiotic allergy record among all clinicians with differential knowledge and confidence in reporting according to clinical role. These results are consistent with previous research showing that allergy labels in electronic medical records frequently lack detailed reaction histories, complicating clinical decision-making.17 This could be due to the relatively traditional and outdated approaches to managing antimicrobial allergies among medical personnel, as well as unclear definitions of their roles and responsibilities.18 19

It is notable that a significantly higher number of Chinese medical personnel conduct penicillin skin tests compared with other countries, likely due to China’s national guidelines recommending routine testing for all patients to preempt allergic reactions.20 China uses the intradermal test as the standard method, following the ‘Chinese Pharmacopoeia - Clinical Medication Guidelines’. This involves using penicillin G or a specific reagent at 500 U/mL concentration. Nurses must undergo 1–2 years of standardised training before independently conducting these tests. National guidelines, especially the β-Lactam Antimicrobial Skin Test Guidelines, offer standardised protocols.20 In contrast, international guidelines suggest testing only for those with suspected allergies.21 22 The nurse-led approach in China increases test availability but may lead to judgement errors due to some nurses’ limited expertise.

Given the issues mentioned above, it is imperative to enhance professional support. This study reveals that although 80% of clinicians interpret antibiotic reactions in skin tests, most healthcare professionals in China express the need for specialised support in both decision-making and practical management of antimicrobial allergies. This underscores the widespread challenges clinicians face during moments of uncertainty. Previous research has demonstrated the benefits of collaborative multidisciplinary teams comprising pharmacists and allergy specialists in assessing, identifying and educating patients with penicillin allergy histories and adjusting antibiotic prescriptions accordingly.23,25 For example, a UK study found that pharmacist-led assessments increased the use of first-line antibiotics and reduced reliance on broad-spectrum antibiotics.26 This study’s findings are in line with those results. Pharmacists have strong skills in structured assessments and record-keeping. However, it is concerning that their actual involvement in clinical penicillin allergy evaluations is the lowest among medical staff. This study also showed that most medical personnel need professional support in making decisions and managing antimicrobial allergies effectively. Therefore, there is an urgent need to enhance pharmacists’ participation in clinical allergy assessments. Correcting inaccurate allergy information is an essential part of antimicrobial management, and multidisciplinary collaboration within antimicrobial allergy assessment teams plays a vital role.27,29 We recommend that hospitals consider creating an antimicrobial allergy assessment team focused on planning antimicrobial management.

Although allergy evaluations are optimally conducted by specialists in allergy and immunology, prevailing limitations in healthcare resources require the deliberate engagement of adequately trained non-specialist personnel to perform initial assessments, especially in environments with restricted resources. Earlier research has demonstrated that, with appropriate training and support, non-allergists are capable of independently assessing low-risk penicillin allergies.30 Assessment of low-risk penicillin allergy by non-allergists can be just as effective, safe and beneficial to quality of life as evaluations conducted by allergists.30 This study demonstrates that 88.17% of medical personnel prefer specialist or senior nurses to administer skin tests. These preliminary findings indicate that nurses hold a pivotal role in performing clinical antimicrobial skin tests in China. In fact, nurses have been incorporated into antimicrobial management guidelines in many countries as part of multidisciplinary teams responsible for implementing antimicrobial treatments, and their contributions have been widely recognised.31,33 In 2019, the CDC included care-based practices such as antibiotic allergy assessments, as core components of hospital antimicrobial stewardship programmes.34 Accurate collection of patients’ antimicrobial allergy information by nurses can help reduce the false-positive rate, thereby minimising the unnecessary use of broad-spectrum or high-grade antibiotics.4 31 However, previous research indicates that nurses’ roles, management attitudes and practical skills in antibiotic stewardship need improvement. Nurses with less experience, lower educational qualifications and junior professional titles tend to have less knowledge and experience in antibiotic management.35

This study revealed an intriguing phenomenon: while most respondents knew the specific name of the drug involved in allergy assessments, only 62–82% documented this critical information in medical records. This discrepancy likely stems from inconsistent documentation practices due to the absence of standardised recording requirements. Implementing structured assessment tools within healthcare systems could effectively address this gap. Implementing structured assessment methods, as recommended by the 2016 guidelines by the Infectious Diseases Society of America and the Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America, can improve the use of first-line antibiotics and overall antimicrobial management. 32Hospitals should prioritise the role of nurses in antibiotic stewardship, enhance their skills in assessing antimicrobial allergies and provide comprehensive training and tools for all medical staff.36,38

In conclusion, we propose the following recommendations: (1) the establishment and comprehensive utilisation of a dedicated hospital allergy assessment team; (2) a significant enhancement of pharmacist involvement, alongside the exploration of collaborative work methodologies and (3) the provision of training programmes for non-allergists, supplemented by the implementation of standardised assessment tools.

Strengths and limitations

However, the present study has several limitations. First, a potential methodological limitation of our study was the selection bias that may arise from the use of convenience sampling. To mitigate this issue, we select medical staff from different departments of each hospital depending on the nature and scale of the hospital, departmental set-up, use of antibiotics and the proportion of medical staff to enhance the representativeness of the sample. Second, there might have been some information bias due to reliance on self-reported data. Therefore, the actual situation may be less optimistic than the results of this study. Third, it is crucial to note that this study was cross-sectional, meaning only associations and not causation could be assessed. Lastly, the questionnaire on the status quo of penicillin allergy assessment management used in our research was based on the context of Chinese medical staff. Despite its scientifically rigorous design, making it suitable for evaluating the penicillin allergy assessment status of most medical staff, it is recommended that other regions consider the unique aspects of their healthcare systems when adapting the scale items for local use. Future longitudinal studies are necessary to better understand the cause-effect relationships between penicillin allergy assessment and over-labelling of antimicrobial allergy.
