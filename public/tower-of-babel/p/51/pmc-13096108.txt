Effects of unilateral step training along with rhythmic auditory stimulation on balance and postural stability in stroke patients
Authors: Sana Khalid, Arshad Nawaz Malik, Furqan Ahmed Siddiqi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The objective of this study was to determine and compare the effects of unilateral step training, unilateral step training with rhythmic auditory stimulation, and conventional treadmill training on balance and postural stability in post-stroke patients. This three-arm, parallel randomized controlled trial (RCT) initially enrolled 63 participants (21 each group) with ischemic stroke aged 40–60 years. Following the dropout of 2 participants from the Conventional Treadmill Training (CTT) group, the final analysis included 61 participants: 21 in Unilateral Step Training (UST), 21 in Rhythmic Auditory Stimulation combined with Unilateral Step Training (RAS + UST), and 19 in the CTT group. Each group completed a 12-week intervention with three 45-min sessions per week. Outcome measures included the Berg Balance Scale (BBS), Fall Risk Score (FRS), Limits of Stability (LOS), Postural Stability Index (PSI), and Timed Up and Go test (TUG), with assessments at baseline, after 4th, 8th and 12th week. Between-group analysis showed that, after 12 weeks of intervention, significant improvement was observed in RAS + UST group for PSI (p = 0.003), FRS (p = 0.03), LOS (p = 0.03), BBS (p = 0.019), and TUG (p = 0.007). Within-group analysis indicated statistically significant improvements for all outcome measures across the three groups (p < 0.05). Introduction

Stroke is a significant public health issue, defined by the World Health Organization in 1970 as “rapidly developing clinical signs of focal (or global) disturbance of cerebral function, with symptoms lasting 24 h or longer, or leading to death, with no apparent cause other than vascular origin”1. Stroke places a substantial burden on health systems worldwide, particularly in developing countries, owing to demographic transitions in aging populations. As such, stroke rehabilitation is a critical area for public health research, especially considering its increasing prevalence in the twenty-first century2.

A major consequence of stroke is persistent impairments in sensorimotor function, postural sway, weight distribution, and stance capabilities, which significantly impact balance and increase fall risk. Studies have shown that individuals with stroke have higher frequency of falls, ranging from 36 to 75% in the first year after stroke, as compared to the general older adult population3. As a result, the heightened risk of falling leads to functional decline and reduced performance in activities of daily living (ADLs), which are key indicators of quality of life and fall efficacy. As maintaining effective and safe mobility has become a primary goal in stroke rehabilitation, improving balance is often the main focus of intervention4.

Individuals with stroke-related hemiparesis experience difficulty in weight bearing on the paretic lower extremity and shifting weight between the legs, which further exacerbates the challenge of restoring balance. These deficits contribute to difficulties in standing, sitting, and walking and are associated with asymmetries in gait, greater energy expenditure, and lateral and vertical instability5. The impaired ability to load the paretic limb is one of the core functional limitations in stroke rehabilitation and can significantly hinder community ambulation6. Therefore, addressing these deficits and restoring the weight-bearing capacity of the paretic limb are critical goals for poststroke rehabilitation7.

Physical activity, various exercises, and advanced interventions have been shown to improve gait quality and performance in individuals with chronic stroke. Treadmill training is a widely studied intervention that has been effective in improving gait speed, endurance, and specific gait patterns in patients with stroke8. However, its effects on postural control and the overall stability index (OSI) are inconsistent due to variability in evidence. As reported in a recent meta-analysis, treadmill training improved dynamic balance but did not significantly affect static or reactive balance outcomes. This indicates an inconsistency in how different aspects of postural control respond to treadmill interventions9. Treadmill training primarily targets walking efficiency but may not directly transfer to improvements in step asymmetry4.

Alternative interventions are available, such as split-belt treadmill training, which uses two belts that target individual legs simultaneously by adjusting the speed of each belt10. However, split-belt treadmills are quite expensive and not readily available in all clinical settings11,12. To address this gap, unilateral step training (UST) and rhythmic auditory stimulation (RAS) have been proposed. UST replicates the effect of split-belt treadmill training by training each leg independently, allowing for targeted rehabilitation and facilitating the neuroplastic adaptations necessary for balance recovery12. This method focuses on enhancing the capacity to load the paretic limb and improve postural control. However, the literature suggests that UST has focused primarily on gait and locomotion, with limited investigation of their effects on balance and postural stability. It has been suggested that future studies should examine its impact on balance, as this aspect remains understudied13.

RAS is another innovative intervention that uses external rhythmic cues (such as music) to facilitate walking and stepping movements14. Rhythmic auditory cues help synchronize internal neural timing, promoting more coordinated movement patterns15. A study investigated the effects of RAS in individuals with stroke and reported significant improvements in balance and spatiotemporal gait parameters16. Another study reported that RAS combined with treadmill training (RAS-TT) significantly improved spatiotemporal gait parameters in individuals with stroke, but did not significantly improve postural stability or balance. The authors recommended further investigations using more focused inclusion criteria, including balance measures or the Functional Ambulation Category (FAC). They emphasized the need for larger trials comparing treadmill training alone with RAS-TT and exploring whether patients with predominantly paretic symptoms benefit more from RAS17. Literature shows that participants receiving RAS training demonstrated improved balance18. A systematic review reported that the RAS use can improve balance and postural stability in patients with hemiparesis. However, they also reported that studies examining balance-related outcome measures in stroke populations remain limited, highlighting the need for further investigation19. A scoping review highlighted that incorporating complementary interventions, such as RAS, alongside conventional rehabilitation exercises shows promise for enhancing treatment outcomes for individuals recovering from stroke20. Despite this evidence, RAS has primarily been used to train bilateral limb movements; its integration with unilateral step training has not yet been explored. This represents a significant research gap, as the potential benefits of combining RAS with UST for improving balance and postural stability in stroke patients remain unknown. Discussion

This study was conducted to determine and compare the effects of Unilateral Step Training (UST), Rhythmic Auditory Stimulation combined with the Unilateral Step Training (RAS + UST), and Conventional Treadmill Training (CTT) on balance and postural stability in patients with stroke. The study found that incorporating RAS with UST improved balance and postural stability, as reflected by significant improvements in the outcome measures.

Static and dynamic balance were assessed using the BBS, and the RAS + UST group showed significant improvement. The findings highlight the effectiveness of task-specific and multisensory interventions for improving balance in patients with stroke. The UST group appeared to benefit more due to the intervention’s focus on weight-shifting and controlled unilateral movements which are key components for improving balance. The addition of RAS with UST, further improved balance by incorporating auditory cues that might have promoted motor synchronization, improve gait rhythm, and enhance postural control19. The combination of targeted physical activity and sensory integration makes RAS + UST a better approach for improving balance. Likely due to its generalized approach, CTT group showed less pronounced improvement, as it does not include activities specifically aimed at improving balance21. Similar results were reported in another study, which found that stroke patients receiving RAS showed greater improvement in BBS scores as compared to conventional training18. Another systematic review reported that several studies demonstrated improvements in BBS scores among participants receiving RAS as an intervention19.

Dynamic balance, which is essential for postural control, often becomes impaired in stroke patients due to asymmetric weight-bearing, with most weight supported by the nonparetic limb. This imbalance limits weight transfer to the paretic side, reducing the balance capacity19. The current study showed significant improvements in dynamic balance and functional mobility, as evidenced by lower TUG scores in both the UST and RAS + UST groups. The TUG test requires a combination of strength, coordination and postural control during transitions such as sitting to standing, walking, and turning22. The greater improvement in TUG performance in RAS + UST group was most likely due to the incorporation of rhythmic auditory cues (RAC) as an external stimulus, which may have resulted in improved motor timing, step synchronization and ultimately gait rhythm. External feedback provided by auditory stimulation supports the correction of asymmetries and accelerates faster adaptation to functional tasks23. Significant improvements were also observed in the UST group, indicating that focused training can enhance lower extremity strength and coordination, enabling more controlled and efficient movements, thus contributing to better functional performance. Overall, these results indicate the importance and benefits of both UST and RAS + UST in improving mobility-related outcomes in the rehabilitation of stroke patients.

The results of the current study are supported by a systematic review that reported a significant improvement in dynamic balance by the addition of RAS, as evidenced by a reduction in the time required to complete the TUG test15. Similarly, another study supported the results of current study’s findings, concluding that incorporating RAS with treadmill training is effective for improving balance in patients with stroke24. Through movement recognition and posture adjustment, auditory feedback (AF) contributes to improving balance. The addition of AF to treadmill training integrates the auditory and somatosensory systems, thereby improving the paretic limb and resulting in improved spatiotemporal symmetry and balance in stroke patients25.

Maintaining postural stability depends on symmetrical control of the lower extremities, which is disturbed in stroke patients. UST works by training one limb at a time, focusing on both paretic and nonparetic limbs13,26. RAS enhances UST by improving motor synchronization, coordination, and timing. This facilitates anticipatory and compensatory postural adjustments and improves trunk control, as well as more effective control of the center of mass within the base of support (BOS)27,28. The current study showed a significant improvement in the limits of stability (LOS) in the RAS + UST group.

In contrast, limited improvement was observed in the CTT group. LOS measures the maximum distance an individual can intentionally move their center of gravity in multiple directions without losing balance or taking a step29. In the CTT group, the lack of significant improvement in the LOS can be attributed to the nature of the intervention. Treadmill training typically focuses on forward gait patterns and may not adequately challenge the dynamic postural adjustments required for lateral, backward, or multidirectional stability30.

Comparatively greater improvements were observed in the UST group, which may be due to a more targeted focus on unilateral weight-shifting and stepping activities. These exercises specifically train the neuromuscular system, which plays a key role in maintaining balance during challenging tasks that require postural changes, thereby directly affecting LOS12. Among the 3 groups, the greatest improvement in LOS was observed in the RAS + UST group. RAS improves motor synchronization via multisensory integration, enabling safer center of gravity control in multiple directions and increasing LOS31. This highlights the importance of incorporating targeted, task-specific and multisensory approaches in rehabilitation to optimize balance and postural stability in stroke patients.

With improvements observed in the BBS, LOS, and TUG tests, the overall stability index, as measured by the postural stability index (PSI), provides a comprehensive picture of enhanced balance and mobility. In the current study, improvements in PSI within RAS + UST suggest that combining RAS with UST significantly enhances the body’s ability to maintain equilibrium during dynamic and static challenges. The results are consistent with a study reporting that incorporating of RAS with action observation therapy was effective in improving postural stability and balance in patients with stroke32. Similarly, a systematic review also reported that the addition of RAS significantly improved OSI in patients with stroke19. This may be because balance control depends on complex interactions between responses to movement changes and adjustments of the center of mass within the BOS33. The integration of somatic sensory and auditory systems may play a significant role in this process. The motor control system depends on feedback from sensory organs to correct movement errors, with proprioception, the auditory system and the visual system being the primary contributors25.

RAS + UST consistently outperformed the other groups across all the parameters, emphasizing the effectiveness of combining task-specific training with auditory stimulation. These interventions improve both static and dynamic balance (as shown by improvements in BBS and OSI), enhance functional mobility (as reflected by better TUG performance), and improve multidirectional postural control (as indicated by improved LOS). This study provides preliminary evidence that integrating RAS with UST may improve balance and postural stability, suggesting a novel approach for stroke rehabilitation, and offering additional insights for future research practices.

Clinical implications

The addition of RAS to the UST protocol improves balance and postural stability; therefore, it should be incorporated into stroke rehabilitation programs. This approach offers a low-cost, efficient, and easy to implement strategy that does not require advanced equipment, making it well-suited for clinical use. Physical therapists may consider incorporating sensory-based, task-specific unilateral training to improve balance outcomes, especially in patients with residual asymmetry.

Limitations

A key limitation of the study was that it was conducted in a controlled clinical environment under the supervision of a trained physical therapist, which, although ensuring standardized training, may have limited the generalizability of the results to community settings. Moreover, the study employed a non-probability convenience sampling technique due to practical and logistical considerations.

Future research

It is recommended that future studies evaluate community-based functional outcomes and also include neurophysiological outcomes to better understand how RAS-based training can improve balance and postural control.

Methodology

This study was a randomized controlled trial (RCT) involving three groups: the Unilateral Step Training (UST) group, the Rhythmic Auditory Stimulation with Unilateral Step Training (RAS + UST) group, and the Conventional Treadmill Training (CTT) group. The sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1.9.4 for a repeated-measures ANOVA (within–between interaction). The calculation specified test family F tests and ANOVA: repeated measures, within–between interaction, with the following parameters: number of groups = 3; number of measurements = 4 (baseline, 4 weeks, 8 weeks, and 12 weeks); effect size f = 0.32; α = 0.05; power (1 − β) = 0.80; assumed correlation among repeated measures = 0.50; and non-sphericity correction ε = 1.0. Under these assumptions, the required total sample size was 63 participants (21 per group). The selected effect size represents a moderate, clinically meaningful effect commonly reported for balance outcomes in stroke rehabilitation trials. These parameters ensured adequate power to detect clinically relevant group × time interaction effects for the primary balance outcomes. 2 participants from CTT group dropped out because commuting was becoming difficult for them, leaving a final sample of 61 participants (Fig. 1). The study was conducted over 1.5 years (January 2023-June 2024) duration at the Foundation University College of Physical Therapy and the Fauji Foundation Hospital (FFH). The sampling technique employed to initially recruit participants was nonprobability convenience sampling, based on a predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Participants were then randomly allocated to one of the three groups using a sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelope method. The envelopes for random group assignments were prepared in advance by an independent researcher not involved in participant recruitment or assessment. These envelopes were opened after the participants were formally enrolled and the baseline assessment was completed. It was a single-blinded study. Only the outcome assessor was blinded. The main objective of the study was to determine the effects of UST, RAS + UST, and CTT and their comparative effects on balance and postural stability in participants with stroke.Fig. 1CONSORT flow diagram.

Ethical considerations

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Foundation University Islamabad (Ethics Code: FF/FUMC/215-277 Phy/23) on December 12, 2022. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment in the study, including consent to publish the data and results. This research was conducted ethically in accordance with the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki (WMA). To ensure participants’ safety, all interventions and assessments were conducted under the supervision of trained physical therapists. A safety harness was used for patients when required.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants with ischemic stroke (subacute or chronic phase) meeting the inclusion criteria of Mini-Mental State Exam (MMSE) score greater than 24, aged between 40 and 60 years and having an ischemic stroke involving the middle cerebral artery, with a functional ambulatory capacity score between 3 and 5, were included to ensure a relatively homogeneous sample. Participants were excluded if they had unstable medical conditions such as uncontrolled hypertension, diabetes, musculoskeletal injuries, or a history of conditions such as heart failure or epilepsy. Individuals with cognitive impairment (MMSE < 24) or spasticity with a Modified Ashworth Scale of ≥ 3 for both affected upper or lower extremity were also excluded.

Assessment tools

Assessments were performed at baseline and every 4 weeks for 12 weeks.

Biodex balance system

The Biodex Balance System SD (Biodex Inc., USA) was used to assess postural stability, limits of stability, and fall risk. The postural stability index was used to assess static balance, whereas the fall risk score and limits of stability testing were employed to evaluate dynamic balance34.

Postural stability index (PSI)

The PSI measures participants’ overall stability index (OSI) by assessing their ability to maintain their center of balance. During the test, participants were instructed to move their center of gravity to align with the center displayed on the monitor and to maintain this position without altering their foot placement. The center of gravity movement is recorded over 20 s. Lower scores indicate more stable postures35.

Limits of stability (LOS)

The LOS (ICC = 0.83) assesses a person’s ability to intentionally move and control their center of gravity toward predetermined targets displayed on screen, staying within their base of support36. Higher test scores indicate better dynamic balance and greater control over stability37.

Fall risk score (FRS)

The FRS represents the overall deviation of the center of gravity on the dynamically moving foot plate. The subject’s ability to maintain their center of balance was assessed via a circular foot plate operating at a dynamic level of 8. The participants were instructed to align their center of gravity with the center displayed on the monitor screen and maintain this position without altering the placement of their feet. The center of gravity movement was recorded over 20 s. A lower FRS indicates better control of the center of gravity on an unstable surface, whereas a higher score reflects poorer balance control under the same conditions37.

Static and dynamic functional balance

Static and dynamic functional balance was assessed using the timed-up-and-go test and the Berg Balance Scale.

Berg balance scale (BBS)

The BBS is a valid (r = 0.92–0.95) and reliable (ICC = 0.92) tool used to assess overall static and dynamic balance38,39. There are 14 items, including sitting, standing, stepping, turning, etc., scored on a 0–4 scale, with a total score of 56. A higher score indicates better balance control40.

Timed up and go test (TUG)

The TUG test is a valid (r = 0.92) and reliable (ICC = 0.96) tool used to assess dynamic balance and functional mobility41. Participants were instructed to get up from a chair, walk 3 m at their comfortable speed, turn around, and sit back down in the chair. The total time (seconds) to complete the task is calculated. A lower time indicated better mobility42.

Intervention

The intervention was delivered over 12 weeks on alternate days, with 45-min sessions for all groups. All sessions were conducted under the supervision of a trained physical therapist in accordance with standardized protocol. In the initial phase (Week 01), all participants were given a familiarization session that included an introduction to treadmill walking, a review of safety procedures, and practice of correct posture and weight-shifting strategies.

Following the familiarization period, participants in all three groups underwent treadmill-based gait training using a standardized motorized clinical treadmill with a minimum speed limit of 0.1 km/h. Walking speed was individualized for each participant and determined using the 10MWT13.

During treadmill training, participants were instructed to hold the side rails to maintain balance and avoid falls. A safety harness was used when required for fall prevention; however, it did not provide body-weight support. Throughout the training sessions, verbal cues for postural correction were provided when necessary12.

In addition, participants in all three groups also received conventional treatment through task-oriented training tailored to their functional needs. Upper extremity tasks included lifting a glass of water, wiping a table, transferring objects between surfaces, combing hair, and grasping and releasing objects. Lower extremity training focused on heel raises, stepping onto and off a footstool, reciprocal hip and knee flexion–extension, step-ups, balance beam walking, and ball-kicking tasks. Balance training comprised of both external and internal perturbations, single-leg and tandem stance, constrained standing, reaching for objects at various heights, sit-to-stand practice, and standing with eyes closed. These exercises were integrated as required to complement treadmill training43,44.

Unilateral step training (UST)

In the UST group, participants were required to stand on a motorized treadmill with one limb stationary on the side platform, while performing stepping movements with the contralateral limb on the moving treadmill belt. Training was conducted alternately for both the paretic and non-paretic lower extremities.

Following an initial familiarization phase, participants trained at a walking speed determined through 10MWT during the first 2–4 weeks of the intervention. During the subsequent 4 weeks, treadmill speed was progressively increased by approximately 25%. In the final 4 weeks, speed was further progressed within each session, with a 25% increase in speed applied every session to provide an increasing challenge12. Treadmill speed (via the 10MWT) was reassessed every four weeks to adjust the training stimulus.

Rhythmic auditory stimulation + unilateral step training (RAS + UST)

Participants performed UST on a treadmill while synchronizing their steps with auditory cues from a metronome. To determine the initial cadence, participants walked on the ground with a physical therapist for 2 min. Step were counted over a 30-s interval using a step counter and stopwatch, then doubled to calculate steps per minute, which was set as the starting cadence. Participants rested for five minutes while listening to auditory cues set at their individualized cadence before treadmill training45.

Cadence was increased by 5% during the first 4 weeks and by 10% in the subsequent weeks, while treadmill speed was progressed according to the protocol outlined in UST46. Cadence (via step counts) and treadmill speed (via the 10MWT) were reassessed every four weeks to adjust the training stimulus and ensure accurate rhythmic entrainment. The auditory cues were delivered via external speakers during treadmill training sessions.

Conventional treadmill training (CTT)

In the CTT group, participants followed the same progressive treadmill training protocol as the UST group, with walking speed individualized based on the 10MWT. Unlike UST, training in this group involved bipedal treadmill walking, with both lower extremities moving simultaneously. Detailed interventions are given in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

The data was analyzed with SPSS v.22.0. Demographics were reported as frequencies and percentages as well as mean ± standard deviation. Normality was assessed using Shapiro–Wilk test. Based on normality, parametric analyses were performed. Interaction effects were assessed via mixed-way ANOVA. A One-way ANOVA was used to analyze differences among the groups at each time point. For multilevel comparisons, Bonferroni post hoc analysis was performed to control for Type I errors. For within-group analysis across time points, repeated-measures ANOVA was applied. Statistical significance was set at a p-value < 0.05.

Conclusions

These findings suggest that RAS combined with UST, through integration of auditory feedback and targeted training, offers a more effective approach for reducing fall risk and improving overall stability in stroke rehabilitation. Unilateral step training remains effective but could be further enhanced with sensory integration, whereas Conventional treadmill training may require modification to include multidirectional challenges and feedback mechanisms to optimize outcomes.
