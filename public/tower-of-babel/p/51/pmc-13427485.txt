Plant polysaccharide nanoparticles: Preparation, applications, and Caenorhabditis elegans pre-screening for bioactive delivery
Authors: Wentong Chen, Bingbing Song, Wen Xia, Rui Hai Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Plant polysaccharide nanoparticles (PPNPs) are promising delivery systems for functional bioactive substances due to their abundance, biocompatibility, biodegradability, and non-toxicity. This review methodically encompasses the preparation methods for starch, pectin and cellulose nanoparticles and their recent applications in delivering hydrophobic and hydrophilic active ingredients, probiotics and volatile antimicrobial agents. Notably, we highlight Caenorhabditis elegans as a strategic in vivo pre-screening platform that bridges the gap between cell-based assays and mammalian models. Leveraging its well-defined genetic background, transparent body, and highly conserved signalling pathways, C. elegans as a highly promising initial screening platform that provides preliminary theoretical support for the further standardization and validation of PPNPs in mammalian models. The paper concludes with an analysis of the challenges in this field, such as large-scale production, long-term stability and safety evaluation. The aim is to provide theoretical guidance for the development and application of plant polysaccharide nanodelivery systems in functional foods.

Introduction

Polysaccharides, as polymers consisting of more than ten monosaccharide molecules linked by glycosidic bonds, are one of the most abundant biomacromolecules in nature and are widely found in the cell walls, extracellular matrices, or secretions of plant and animal bodies and microorganisms (Khotimchenko, 2020). They are important structural support substances, energy storage forms, and biologically active media for living organisms. With their unique molecular structure, diverse sources, excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, renewability, and modifiability, polysaccharides have become an attractive green resource in the fields of food, medicine, chemicals, and materials (Tarahi et al., 2026). Based on their sources, polysaccharides can be divided into three main groups: animal polysaccharides (e.g., chitosan, chondroitin sulfate, glycogen, etc.), plant polysaccharides (starch, cellulose, pectin, etc.), and microbial polysaccharides (xanthan gum, dextran, etc.). Among them, plant polysaccharides are extremely widely available, renewable, and relatively inexpensive, and are the most abundant polysaccharide category in nature. They generally have good physicochemical properties, such as pasty-gelatinizing properties of starch (Yan et al., 2024), rigidity of cellulose (Gopinath et al., 2018), and gelling properties of pectin (Cao et al., 2020). Plant polysaccharides are deeply rooted in human dietary history and are widely accepted by consumers. They align well with the core demands of the modern food industry for natural, clean labels, sustainability, and functionality. Through physical, chemical, or enzymatic modification, their functional properties can be further expanded and optimised. Compared with other plant polysaccharides (such as arabinoxylans, β-glucans, gums, hemicelluloses, and mucilages), starch, pectin, and cellulose are the most widely used, most extensively studied, and have been successfully commercialized in the food industry. Therefore, this paper will focus on these three polysaccharides. In recent years, the rise of nanotechnology has opened up entirely new dimensions for the application of plant polysaccharides. As consumers increasingly prioritize food safety, health, and sustainability, the development of natural, safe, and functional food additives and packaging materials has become a research hotspot. NPs refer to solid or colloidal particles manufactured through physical, chemical, or enzymatic methods, with sizes at the nanoscale level (Kumari et al., 2020). NPs have a large surface-to-volume ratio and excellent adsorption properties. Drugs, proteins, and various active compounds can be combined with NPs through covalent bonds or adsorption, making them ideal carriers (Biliuta & Coseri, 2019).

Natural functional compounds, such as polyphenols, carotenoids, and probiotics, offer numerous health benefits including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-ageing properties (Gong et al., 2024). However, they commonly face challenges such as poor water solubility, instability during processing and in the gastrointestinal tract, and low bioavailability (Li et al., 2025). Traditional oral delivery methods result in significant deactivation of active ingredients due to degradation by gastric acid and enzymes, preventing effective delivery to the target site (Tarahi et al., 2026). The advent of nanotechnology has provided novel approaches to addressing this challenge. Depending on the properties of different materials, the type of payload, and the food matrix, plant polysaccharides can be engineered into nanoparticles; different nanoparticles can enhance the encapsulation efficiency, stability, and targeted release capabilities of bioactive compounds (Xia et al., 2020). Plant polysaccharide nanoparticles (PPNPs) are owing to their excellent biocompatibility, degradability, and responsiveness to pH or enzymatic environments, have emerged as a focal point in research on functional delivery systems for bioactive substances. Constructing plant polysaccharides into NPs can significantly enhance their inherent physicochemical and functional properties. For example, polysaccharides treated by physical, chemical, or enzymatic methods can greatly improve their surface activity, adsorption capacity for active substances, and encapsulation ability. By improving solubility and dispersibility, the limitations of poor solubility in some polysaccharides can be overcome. By hydrolysing or mechanically shearing polysaccharides into smaller sizes with an average diameter of 350–500 nm, they can penetrate cell membranes and enter cells (Wu et al., 2019). The small size of NPs facilitates the formation of stable nanocrystal aggregates (Kumari et al., 2020), which better protect the encapsulated material from damage caused by light, oxygen, heat, pH, and enzymes. By leveraging the environmental responsiveness of polysaccharides, active ingredients can be precisely released at specific sites, thereby enhancing the bioavailability of active compounds. PPNPs can serve as Pickering stabilisers to stabilize emulsions and foams. Additionally, PPNPs can function as nanofillers to significantly improve the mechanical strength and barrier properties of composite materials. Due to their high bioavailability in specific in vivo models and biodegradability (Tarahi et al., 2026), polysaccharide nanoparticles are considered one of the highly promising delivery systems (Khotimchenko, 2020).

Although significant progress has been made in the preparation and application of PPNPs, conventional in vitro models struggle to replicate the multi-organ coordination, metabolic processes and barrier functions observed in living organisms. In order to evaluate the health benefits and safety of PPNP-active ingredient complexes more accurately and comprehensively, validation using in vivo models is essential. Due to its well-characterised genetic background, short life cycle and conserved metabolic and ageing pathways shared with mammals, C. elegans has become a vital bridge connecting in vitro research with mammalian studies (Lim, 2026). Most existing reviews focus on the nanoscale preparation of individual polysaccharides or simply summarize the use of nematodes in the screening of bioactive food compounds, without systematically explaining how the physicochemical properties of PPNPs are directly linked to biological endpoints in C. elegans. This paper aims to provide a detailed review of the preparation strategies, formation mechanisms, and applications in functional bioactive delivery for nanoparticles derived from starch, pectin, and cellulose, which are the three most commonly used and extensively studied plant polysaccharides in the food industry (Fig. 1). It focuses on recent advances in utilizing the C. elegans model to evaluate their delivery efficiency-including encapsulation efficiency and gastrointestinal release rate-as well as bioactivity and safety. This review integrates the preparation techniques of PPNPs, their applications in food delivery, and biological evaluations using C. elegans model, with the aim of offer new perspectives and theoretical foundations for the safe delivery and efficient application of PPNPs in functional foods.Fig. 1Plant polysaccharides.Fig. 1

Preparation of nanoparticles

The shape and size of particles can influence various aspects of food sensory properties or functionality, as well as the strength of many materials. Additionally, the size of NPs can affect their ability to encapsulate active substances or deliver drugs (Gopinath et al., 2018). Typically, particles exhibit different physical and chemical properties and sizes depending on the processing methods and sources used. Unlike materials such as nanoemulsion and nanofibers, PPNPs are primarily formed through methods such as chemical hydrolysis, enzymatic hydrolysis, and physical shearing, resulting in three-dimensionally uniform, nanoscale amorphous or partially crystalline particles. The following are several commonly used methods.

Acid hydrolysis

Acid hydrolysis is a classic and efficient method for preparing NPs. Its core advantages lie in its simplicity, low cost, and lack of requirement for complex equipment. It can selectively decompose the amorphous regions of raw materials while preserving highly ordered crystalline structures, thereby yielding NPs with excellent mechanical strength, high crystallinity, and controllable surface chemistry. For example, a key factor in the acid hydrolysis preparation of starch nanoparticles is the removal of the amorphous regions of starch particles while protecting the crystalline structure of starch. As shown in Fig. 2 (a), Acid hydrolysis of starch occurs in two stages: During the rapid hydrolysis stage, acids preferentially attack the amorphous regions of granules, producing small-sized particles. In the subsequent slow hydrolysis stage, prolonged hydrolysis of the starch disrupts the crystalline regions, leading to a decrease in crystallinity and a broadening of the particle size distribution (Harun et al., 2022). The products obtained after acid hydrolysis have relatively high crystallinity, which gives them high thermal stability (Gopinath et al., 2018), so NPs can be used to improve the stability of active substances. Factors affecting the yield and properties of NPs in acid hydrolysis include the type and concentration of acid, as well as the hydrolysis time and temperature conditions. However, the limitations of acid hydrolysis cannot be overlooked: First, its yield typically falls below 20% (Kim et al., 2013), with substantial raw material losses occurring during amorphous zone hydrolysis, resulting in poor economic viability. Second, the environmental burden posed by waste acid treatment remains unresolved, making green process improvements a research priority. Third, precise control over the acid hydrolysis process is challenging; excessive hydrolysis can disrupt crystal structures, leading to a broad particle size distribution. Despite challenges such as waste acid recovery and environmental pressures, acid hydrolysis remains the dominant technology for the large-scale production of nanocrystals. Ionic gelation

Ionic gelation is a relatively mild method for preparing NPs and is widely used in the preparation of pectin, sodium alginate, and cellulose nanoparticles. It is the mainstream method for preparing pectin nanoparticles. Fig. 2 (b) shows the negatively charged free carboxylic (COO−) on pectin cross-linked with multivalent cations (such as Ca2+, Zn2+, and Fe3+) through electrostatic interactions to form ‘egg carton’ structure gel nanoparticles (Cao et al., 2020). The treated polysaccharide precipitate forms spherical particles, which are filtered, washed with distilled water, and obtained as NPs. The core advantage of the ionic gelation method is that it does not require high temperatures or organic solvents, nor does it require violent physical shearing. As an active substance delivery carrier, it can maximally protect heat-sensitive substances. Research has demonstrated the use of this method to encapsulate phenolic compounds with pectin, achieving high yields and particles with a diameter of 267 nm, suitable for colon-targeted delivery (Siles-Sánchez et al., 2024). In addition, experiments have been conducted to prepare a composite biofilm of sodium alginate and pectin using ionic gelation to encapsulate probiotic cells, thereby achieving protective transport of the strains (Gopinath et al., 2018). Although the ionic gelation method involves mild conditions, it faces multiple challenges in practical applications: First, this method is primarily suitable for low-methoxyl pectin and has limited applicability to high-methoxyl pectin, which restricts the range of available raw materials. Second, the cross-linking process is highly sensitive to ion concentration, pH, and addition rate, and issues regarding inter-batch reproducibility have not yet been systematically studied. Third, the gel networks formed via ionic crosslinking often have large pore sizes, resulting in low encapsulation efficiency for small-molecule bioactive compounds and the occurrence of burst release (Yasmin et al., 2019). Furthermore, the introduction of calcium ions may affect the mineral balance of the final product, a factor that must be considered in food applications. Therefore, future research should focus on the precise control mechanisms of the crosslinking process and the development of improved ionic gel strategies suitable for high-methoxyl pectin.

High-pressure homogenisation (HPH)

HPH is a highly efficient, scalable nanoscale particle preparation technology that relies on mechanical action. This method is environmentally friendly and safe, requiring no organic solvents, and enables continuous production. As shown in Fig. 2 (c), During processing, the continuously flowing liquid is subjected to mechanical shear forces, which reduce particle size due to hydrogen bond breakage, thereby significantly enhancing particle dispersion stability and functional properties. During extrusion, high shear forces break molecular bonds. Therefore, similar to ultrasonic processing, partial or complete degradation of crystal structure may occur during high-pressure homogenisation (Ding & Kan, 2017). In the field of drug delivery, HPH is often used in combination with other methods to achieve smaller particle sizes and better dispersibility. Celecoxib nanoparticles were obtained using high-pressure homogenisation freeze-drying (HPH-FD) after anti-solvent crystallization. The particles prepared using this combined method have better solubility (Homayouni et al., 2014). Although HPH is widely regarded as a green technology, its limitations in practical applications are often overlooked. First, the localized high-temperature effects generated by high pressure may cause the degradation of heat-sensitive bioactive compounds, yet most studies have not monitored this potential risk. Second, high shear forces may lead to excessive fragmentation of polysaccharide chains, altering their molecular weight distribution and functional properties; however, the characterization of structural changes in polysaccharides following HPH treatment is currently often insufficient. Third, the method involves high equipment investment and energy consumption, and multiple treatment cycles are required to achieve the nanoscale, which further increases costs.

Spray drying

Spray drying is an efficient, continuous physical preparation technology. NPs prepared by spray drying have low moisture content and a longer shelf life. As shown in Fig. 2 (d), after atomization, the droplets are dried by hot air, and the resulting particles are collected using an electrostatic precipitator to improve the recovery yield of fine nanoparticles (Arpagaus et al., 2018). Gum arabic prepared using the Büchi Nano Spray Dryer B-90 has particle sizes as small as 350 nm, and the modified fraction obtained by spray drying has a size peak below 500 nm (Li et al., 2010). This method is also used to prepare nano-capsules of active substances, using dispersing enhancers L-leucine and d-mannitol as adjuvants, and spray freeze-drying to prepare nano-particle composites loaded with curcumin, thereby improving the solubility of curcumin and maintaining its antibacterial activity (Yu et al., 2016). Regarding particle collection, while electrostatic collection can improve yield, it may introduce electrostatic effects that influence particle properties. More importantly, although excipients added during the spray-drying process improve particle morphology, their residual levels in the final product and safety assessments are often overlooked. Therefore, future research should strike a balance between process parameter optimization and product safety assessment, and explore novel spray-drying strategies using either no excipients or food-grade excipients.

Enzymatic hydrolysis

As shown in Fig. 2 (e), various enzymes such as α-amylase, β-amylase, pullulanase, etc. are used for starch pretreatment to reduce acid hydrolysis time. The cellulases used for enzymatic hydrolysis of cellulose primarily include: endoglucanase, exoglucanase, and β-glucosidase (Ren et al., 2022). Fig. 2 (f) demonstrates that endo-glucanase acts not only on the amorphous regions of cellulose chains but also on crystalline regions. Exoglucanase shortens cellulose chain length by hydrolyzing glycosidic bonds. At a cellulase concentration of 50 μ/mL, the resulting CNC exhibits a ribbon-like morphology. However, when cellulase concentration reaches 300 μ/mL, the generated CNC adopts a granular form (Chen et al., 2019). Compared with chemical methods, it is more environmentally friendly and efficient. Since no chemical reagents are used in the preparation process, there is no need for additional washing or evaporation, making it more efficient and convenient. Compared with physical methods, enzymatic hydrolysis is more gentle and cost-effective. Enzyme proteins remaining after enzymatic hydrolysis may cause allergic reactions; therefore, their safety must be rigorously evaluated for food applications, yet relevant research in this area is virtually nonexistent. Consequently, future research must achieve breakthroughs in enzyme immobilization technology, the mechanisms of synergistic action in composite enzyme systems, and the safety assessment of enzyme residues in order to advance the industrial application of enzymatic hydrolysis.

Building on the critical analysis above, the subsequent sections of this review are structured to provide a comprehensive understanding of PPNPs from fabrication to functional evaluation. Section 3 delves into the structural and physicochemical properties of three representative PPNPs elucidating how their distinct characteristics arise from different preparation methods and influence their performance as delivery carriers. Section 4 systematically examines the application of these PPNPs in encapsulating four categories of bioactive payloads-hydrophobic compounds, hydrophilic compounds, probiotics, and volatile antimicrobials-with emphasis on the molecular interaction mechanisms that govern payload-carrier compatibility and release behavior. Finally, Section 5 introduces C. elegans as an in vivo pre-screening platform, highlighting its role in evaluating the bioactivity enhancement and biosafety of PPNP-based delivery systems prior to more complex mammalian studies. Together, this integrated framework aims to bridge the gap between PPNP engineering and functional evaluation by linking preparation methods, material properties, application scenarios, and screening strategies in a coherent manner.

Preparation and characteristics of major PPNPs

Starch nanoparticles

Starch is a natural biological macromolecule composed of two types of high-molecular-weight polymers: amylose and amylopectin. Amylose is formed by α-d-glucose units linked by α-1,4 glycosidic bonds to create a linear chain structure, while amylopectin forms a branched structure on the main chain through α-1,6 glycosidic bonds (Yan et al., 2024). The preparation of starch nanoparticles (SNPs) uses crops rich in natural starch, such as corn, potatoes, cassava, wheat, and peas, as raw materials. Through physical crushing, chemical modification, or enzymatic hydrolysis, the crystalline regions of starch granules are disrupted, enabling them to reassemble at the nanoscale (50–500 nm) into discrete particles. Compared with natural starch, SNPs exhibit significantly optimised physicochemical properties and biological functions, including higher solubility, larger reaction surface area, stronger absorption capacity, and biological penetration efficiency (Campelo et al., 2020). The performance of SNPs is significantly influenced by the ratio of amylose to amylopectin. SNPs with a high amylose content contain more helical cavities and can therefore encapsulate hydrophobic active substances more efficiently through hydrophobic interactions (Rostamabadi et al., 2019). For example, SNPs prepared by acid hydrolysis may retain the B-type or A-type crystalline structure of the raw material. The Type A crystal structure is more resistant to enzymatic hydrolysis than the Type B crystal structure, thereby slowing the rate of payload release. While SNPs prepared by nano-precipitation or reverse micelle technology tend to form new V-type single-helix complex crystals. V-type single-helix complex crystals form classic “helix-ligand” inclusion complexes, which can significantly improve the loading capacity for hydrophobic cargo and thermal stability. This unique crystalline structure not only alters the macroscopic morphology of the particles but also endows them with excellent hydrophobic material encapsulation capabilities and sustained-release properties, making them valuable for applications in drug delivery, functional food development, and other fields.

Common methods for preparing SNPs can be divided into ‘bottom-up’ and ‘top-down’ approaches, including high-pressure homogenisation, microjet fragmentation of starch granules, acid hydrolysis, nanoprecipitation, and enzymatic hydrolysis. These methods can be used to obtain SNPs with different particle sizes, shapes, physical and chemical properties, and application characteristics. It is worth noting that acid hydrolysis is a commonly used method for preparing SNPs, but it has low yield, with a recovery rate typically below 20% (Campelo et al., 2020). And SNPs obtained using single high-pressure homogenisation have the problem of uneven size. To overcome the limitations of a single method and optimise particle performance, such as reducing particle size, improving dispersion, and increasing encapsulation efficiency, new combined preparation technologies are increasingly being favoured. For example, vacuum cold plasma (CP) technology combined with ultrasonic treatment is used to obtain uniform spherical and square NPs. Fig. 3 (a) illustrates the preparation principle of this method. Bound water in starch granules is primarily distributed in crystalline regions, while free water is mainly distributed in amorphous regions. During the initial stage of CP treatment, free radicals generated by atmospheric plasma primarily disrupt the amorphous regions. As treatment time increases further, the crystalline regions of starch are also disrupted. Subsequently, ultrasonic treatment further breaks down starch granules into smaller sizes. This method does not use chemical reagents, making it more environmentally friendly, while also significantly reducing ultrasonic treatment time, preparation time, and costs. After ultrasonication treatment, the crystal structure of nano-starch is completely destroyed by the high-speed jet, and at the same time, the linear starch is degraded to form spherical particles at the nanoscale (Xia et al., 2017). Hao et al. used glucose amylase pretreatment followed by acid hydrolysis to obtain square starch nanocrystals with a particle size of 50–100 nm. Enzymatic pretreatment greatly shortened the acid hydrolysis time (Hao et al., 2018). In addition, some studies have used an ultrasound-Fenton system to prepare SNPs. Fig. 3 (b) illustrates the preparation principle of this method. First, ultrasonic treatment generates strong shear forces that cause cracks on the surface of starch. Subsequently, free radicals produced by the Fenton reaction can penetrate the interior of starch granules more rapidly, forming NPs by cleaving glycosidic bonds. Compared with the Fenton reaction alone, this combined method improves the preparation efficiency and dispersion of SNPs (Xie et al., 2023).Fig. 3Novel combined preparation technique for SNPs. In the food industry, SNPs have demonstrated a wide range of applications. Table 1 shows the applications of SNPs in the food industry. Firstly, as an efficient active substance delivery system, SNPs have excellent biocompatibility and biodegradability. Its hydrophilic surface and hydrophobic core can effectively encapsulate hydrophobic bioactive components. It can significantly improve its water solubility, photothermal stability, and targeted sustained release performance in the gastrointestinal environment, thereby enhancing bioavailability. Secondly, starch can be processed to break down into smaller particles. When the particle diameter is less than 3 μm, its texture is similar to that of fat globules, giving it an emulsion or cream-like consistency. Therefore, modified starch treated with acid can be used as a fat substitute to prepare low-fat ice cream. Alternatively, it can form a gel network to give low-fat foods a smooth texture and mouthfeel similar to that of full-fat products (Sreejit et al., 2025). In addition, SNPs themselves or those loaded with antimicrobial agents (such as plant essential oils or nisin) can be integrated into edible coatings or bio-based packaging films. Leveraging their high specific surface area, they enhance the sustained-release efficacy of active substances, effectively inhibiting microbial growth on food surfaces and extending the shelf life of perishable foods such as fruits, vegetables, and meat. Therefore, SNPs, as green nanomaterials derived from renewable resources, have broad application prospects in enhancing food functional properties, improving texture and taste, and ensuring food safety. Pectin nanoparticles

Pectin is a class of complex polysaccharides widely found in plant cell walls, with natural sources primarily including citrus peel, apple pomace, and beet pulp, among other agricultural by-products. As a key component of primary cell walls, pectin can account for over 35% of the dry weight of cell walls in certain plants, and is widely recognized as the most structurally complex polysaccharide system in nature. Pectin polysaccharides are primarily classified into three types: homogalacturonic acid polysaccharides (HG), rhamnogalacturonic acid polysaccharides I (RG-I), and rhamnogalacturonic acid polysaccharides II (RG-II). Its molecular structure is centred on a galacturonic acid backbone connected by α-1,4-glycosidic bonds, with side chains that may contain at least 17 monosaccharide residues, including rhamnose, arabinose, and galactose. Some of the carboxyl groups undergo methyl esterification modification, and the degree of esterification (DE) becomes a key structural parameter regulating the functional characteristics of pectin.

In terms of extraction technology, traditional methods mostly use acid-assisted hot water extraction or enzymatic hydrolysis to separate pectin from plant waste. However, although the new combined physical-enzymatic method (CPE) can maintain pectin activity, it has the problems of low yield and insufficient galacturonic acid (GalA) content, and the resulting product is mostly low-methoxy pectin (Naqash et al., 2017). To optimise extraction efficiency and product quality, researchers have developed innovative technologies such as microwave-assisted extraction, ultrasound-enhanced extraction, and subcritical water extraction (Naqash et al., 2017). These methods significantly improve the purity of pectin extraction and the retention rate of biological activity by regulating thermodynamic parameters and mass transfer efficiency.

The mainstream method for preparing Pectin Nanoparticles (PNPs) is ionic cross-linking, which is particularly suitable for low-methoxy pectin (LM-pectin, DE < 50%). Through the formation of an ‘egg box’ structure cross-linking network between Ca2+ and other polyvalent cations and pectin carboxyl groups, uniformly sized gel nanospheres are formed under controlled calcium ion concentration, drop acceleration rate, and pH conditions (Cao et al., 2020). This structure is resistant to degradation in the upper gastrointestinal tract, thereby significantly influencing its colon-targeted release. While high-methoxy pectin (HM-Pectin) can form a denser cross-linked network through hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds in an acidic environment, thereby enhancing the mechanical stability of the particles (Giacomazza et al., 2016). In addition, PNPs can be prepared using the nano-precipitation method (Ding et al., 2023), whereby organic solvents such as ethanol or propanol are used to induce a decrease in pectin solubility through diffusion, leading to self-assembly and the formation of NPs. PNPs formed using this method have strong bioadhesiveness and good biocompatibility, and their negative charge is conducive to stabilizing colloids and adsorbing active substances. To improve encapsulation efficiency, enhance stability, or expand functionality, innovative joint technologies are becoming increasingly mature. For example, the development of ultrasound-assisted self-assembly methods promotes the self-assembly behavior of pea protein and high-methoxy pectin(PP-HMP) through electrostatic interactions, enabling them to form spherical particles with a core-shell structure and improving the emulsion stability of PP-HMP (Ma et al., 2023). In addition, research on the functionalization of pectin nanoparticles has expanded from simple encapsulation of active compounds to the development of targeted delivery systems and multi-stimulus-responsive systems. Lin et al. developed a galectin-3-targeted nanoplatform responsive to pH, proteases, and oxidative stress, achieving the co-delivery of NO and resveratrol and a 20-fold enrichment in the target region (Lin et al., 2026).

In the field of food technology, PNPs demonstrate multi-dimensional application value due to their unique physical and chemical properties. Table 3 lists some of the applications of PNPs in the food industry. As a natural, non-toxic polysaccharide, pectin cannot be degraded by gastric or intestinal enzymes but can be specifically hydrolysed by colonic microbial enzymes, making it an ideal intestinal-targeted delivery carrier. This nonabsorbable characteristic ensures the precise release of drugs or active ingredients in the colon. Related studies have confirmed that embedding drugs into a pectin matrix can significantly prolong the duration of action and optimise pharmacokinetic parameters (Khotimchenko, 2020). The three-dimensional network structure of PNPs gives them the ability to form water-insoluble gels, exhibiting superior mechanical stability and chemical resistance to alginates in probiotic encapsulation (Voo et al., 2011). In terms of functional delivery, PNPs as composite coatings can significantly improve the physical and chemical stability of sodium caseinate/zein (CZ) complex PNPs and the sustained release efficiency of curcumin in the gastrointestinal tract (Chang et al., 2017). In the field of food structure improvement, PNPs can be used to prepare functional preservation films by loading antimicrobial agents, and can also be combined with proteins to enhance the mechanical strength of the films. As a Pickering emulsion stabiliser, the emulsion formed by its rigid interface exhibits excellent stability in acidic food systems. A more innovative application lies in fat replacement technology, which regulates casein-pectin interactions to construct gel networks with ideal microstructures. The resulting low-fat cheese analogues retain their texture while reducing calories (Liu et al., 2008). Similarly, filled hydrogel particles further demonstrated their ability to stabilize water-in-oil emulsions by encapsulating fat droplets in a pectin matrix (Wusigale et al., 2020). Cellulose nanoparticles

Among plant-derived polysaccharides, cellulose is the most abundant and naturally renewable natural polymer (Gopinath et al., 2018). With its advantages of renewability, biocompatibility, and complete biodegradability, it has become an ideal base material for processing and modification through physical or chemical methods (Biliuta & Coseri, 2019). Based on structural characteristics, cellulose nanoparticles (CNPs) are mainly divided into two categories: cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) and cellulose nanofibrils (CNFs). CNCs are rigid rod-shaped nanomaterials prepared from sources such as plants, agricultural waste, bacteria, and marine organisms (algae, cnidarians). Their crystal structure retains the natural type I cellulose lattice. After selective removal of amorphous regions via strong acid hydrolysis, rod-shaped nanomaterials with widths of 1–100 nm and lengths of 10 to several hundred nanometres are formed (Lam et al., 2012). CNCs prepared using different methods also exhibit distinct shapes. Spherical CNCs are suitable for emulsion stabilisation due to their ability to form a dense coating layer at the oil-water interface, while high aspect ratio CNCs can significantly enhance the barrier properties and crystallinity of composite films (Cao et al., 2021). These special structures give it excellent strength and physical and chemical properties.

The performance of CNCs depends significantly on the source of raw materials and extraction processes. Based on fibre type, plant-based cellulose can be divided into six major categories: bast fibres (jute, hemp), leaf fibres (Manila hemp, sisal), seed fibres (coconut shells, cotton), core fibres (ramie), herbaceous fibres (wheat, corn stalks) and wood fibres (wood, roots and stems) (Dai et al., 2020). Traditional preparation methods include acid hydrolysis (hydrochloric acid, sulfuric acid), enzymatic hydrolysis, ultrasonic disruption, TEMPO oxidation, and mechanical processing (low-temperature grinding, microfluidisation, high-pressure homogenisation). Among them, acid hydrolysis can be used to prepare CNCs with diameters ranging from 5 to 70 nm by controlling the hydrolysis time, temperature, and stirring conditions(Habibi et al., 2010). Enzymatic hydrolysis regulates the shape and size of CNCs by adjusting the concentration of cellulase. At low concentrations, rod-shaped CNCs with a length of 250–900 nm and a width of 30–45 nm are produced, while at high concentrations, granular CNCs with reduced crystallinity are formed (Chen et al., 2019). To improve preparation efficiency and environmental friendliness, innovative combined technologies continue to emerge. For example, ball milling is used to assist in the solid acid hydrolysis extraction of CNCs, and mechanical pretreatment is used to destroy the fibre structure, making it easier for acid hydrolysis. This method is more environmentally friendly and cleaner, and the CNCs obtained exhibit higher thermal stability (Song et al., 2018). Or use sono-assisted TEMPO oxidation to extract and separate CNCs. Acoustic cavitation generated by acoustic treatment makes cellulose more susceptible to TEMPO oxidation, thereby improving yield (Rohaizu & Wanrosli, 2017). These technological breakthroughs provide key support for the high-value utilisation of CNCs in fields such as biomaterials, food packaging, and nanocomposites.

As shown in Fig. 4, in the field of food technology, CNCs demonstrate significant application value due to their unique nanostructure and multifunctional properties. As a bio-based reinforcing material, cellulose nanocrystals improve the moisture resistance and oil resistance of rice protein/sodium alginate-based films. When the addition of CNCs reaches 0.3%, the tensile strength of the film increases by 51%, while also providing good antioxidant protection against flaxseed oil (Li et al., 2022). In terms of biomaterial modification, CNCs and poly(3-hydroxybutyrate-co-3-hydroxyvalerate) (PHBV) form a composite material through strong hydrogen bonding, which not only improves the hydrophilicity of PHBV, but also achieves a high drug loading efficiency of 98.8% and excellent cell compatibility (Cheng et al., 2017). As a Pickering emulsion stabiliser, CNCs are an ideal choice due to their nanoscale size, high aspect ratio, amphiphilicity, and biodegradability. Their emulsifying properties are regulated by factors such as morphology, surface charge, wettability, and crystalline heterogeneity (Dai et al., 2020). CNCs prepared from acid-hydrolysed corn cob cellulose, after ultrasonic homogenisation, form a d-limonene Pickering emulsion that exhibits significantly improved stability (Wen et al., 2014). Current research focuses on the application of stable CNCs emulsions in food packaging and active substance delivery. Its rod-shaped structure can form a dense physical barrier at the oil droplet interface, effectively encapsulating hydrophobic active compounds such as β-carotene in O/W emulsions, preventing separation and delaying oxidative degradation (Fitri et al., 2022). Future research should focus on four key areas: developing efficient and environmentally friendly extraction processes, elucidating the stabilisation mechanisms of multi-component systems, expanding functional application scenarios, and improving safety evaluation systems. In addition, the high specific surface area and abundant surface hydroxyl groups of CNCs endow them with excellent active substance loading capacity. Through hydrogen bonding with brewer's yeast, CNCs can significantly improve the survival rate of probiotics and achieve targeted release in the intestine (Yang et al., 2024). Their intracellular-like size characteristics also make CNCs potential drug delivery nanocarriers (Lam et al., 2012). PPNPs as delivery systems for natural bioactive compounds

Foods contain numerous functional components such as polyphenols, flavonoids, probiotics, and polysaccharides, which have garnered significant attention for their wide-ranging health benefits including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-ageing properties, as well as their role in reducing the risk of cardiovascular and neurodegenerative diseases (Gong et al., 2024). However, these active ingredients face significant challenges in practical application: most exhibit poor water solubility, instability to light or heat, and low bioavailability (Li et al., 2025). During storage and oral administration, it readily undergoes decomposition and denaturation, thereby losing its activity. Furthermore, although the small intestine is the primary site of absorption, the highly acidic environment and digestive enzymes present in the stomach significantly limit the quantity of active substances that reach the small intestine intact (Tarahi et al., 2026). To overcome these obstacles, employing delivery systems to encapsulate active substances has become a key strategy for enhancing solubility, stability, and bioavailability. Existing nanodelivery systems encompass inorganic nanoparticles, polysaccharide-based nanoparticles, and protein-based nanoparticles. Among these, PPNPs demonstrate significant potential for encapsulating and delivering natural bioactive compounds due to their abundant sources, excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and capacity for controlled release and targeted delivery (Tarahi et al., 2026).

Delivery of hydrophobic bioactive compounds

Hydrophobic bioactive compounds, such as curcumin, carotenoids, resveratrol and vitamin D, offer exceptional health benefits including potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties. However, their extremely poor water solubility, instability under light and heat conditions, and low bioavailability severely limit their application in food and pharmaceutical sectors. Encapsulation using PPNPs effectively enhances dispersion in aqueous media, aids resistance to gastric acid degradation, and improves absorption efficiency within the gastrointestinal tract through controlled release (Li et al., 2025). Therefore, plant polysaccharide-based nanodelivery systems hold significant potential in addressing the aforementioned challenges. For instance, a composite delivery system based on pea protein isolate and pectin can form a ternary complex with curcumin. This system not only provides robust protection against UV and thermal degradation but also exhibits favourable sustained-release properties in an in vitro digestion model (Guo et al., 2020). Another study employed the antisolvent precipitation method to form complexes between ovalbumin (OVA) and high-methoxy pectin (PEC) for encapsulating vitamin D3. Results demonstrated that the storage stability of vitamin D3 within the complexes reached 96.37%, and in vitro simulated gastrointestinal experiments confirmed its sustained-release characteristics (Xiang et al., 2020). Furthermore, nano-starch particles prepared via the high-speed jet can form stable complexes with astaxanthin through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions. Based on indicators such as the lifespan of C. elegans and heat stress, it has been confirmed that nano-starch particles can enhance the bioactivity of astaxanthin (Chen et al., 2025). Spherical quinoa starch nanoparticles with an average particle size of approximately 166.25 nm were synthesised via the nanoprecipitation method, demonstrating excellent loading capacity. Upon loading with quercetin, their crystal structure underwent transformation, and their DPPH radical scavenging capacity was enhanced (Jiang et al., 2022). Similarly, Upon adsorption of myricetin onto tapioca starch nanoparticles, not only did sustained-release effects manifest in simulated gastric and intestinal fluids, but the radical scavenging rate of myricetin was also enhanced (Xia et al., 2020). Among various PPNPs, the internal helical structure of SNPs exhibits hydrophobicity, facilitating interactions with hydrophobic molecules. This makes them the preferred carrier for delivering hydrophobic compounds such as curcumin and carotenoids, significantly enhancing their water solubility and photothermal stability. Pectin forms stable complexes with proteins through electrostatic interactions, thereby improving the delivery efficiency of hydrophobic compounds.

Delivery of hydrophilic bioactive compounds

Hydrophilic functional compounds, such as tea polyphenols and anthocyanins, exhibit excellent water solubility and are renowned for their potent antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and cardiovascular protective effects. Despite their tendency to disperse, these high-molecular-weight hydrophilic molecules readily form irreversible bonds with endogenous proteins upon entering the gastrointestinal tract. Under the complex influence of pH levels, enzymatic environments, and ionic strength, they undergo degradation before reaching active sites, thereby diminishing their bioavailability (Tarahi et al., 2026). At the same time, they are highly susceptible to rapid inactivation during processing, storage, and within the digestive tract due to pH fluctuations, enzymatic degradation, and oxidative effects. Encapsulation using PPNPs can establish a physical barrier that effectively isolates adverse environments, thereby significantly enhancing chemical stability and prolonging retention time within the gut. For instance, blackberry anthocyanins can be encapsulated by nanoparticles formed from pectin and lysozyme through self-assembly, yielding spherical nanoparticles with a particle size of approximately 198.5 nm. This composite maintains excellent physical stability across varying pH levels (Rosales et al., 2021). Lysozyme-pectin nanoparticles were prepared via self-assembly to enhance the bioactivity of epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG); in C. elegans experiments, the encapsulated EGCG demonstrated stronger antioxidant and anti-stress capabilities (Zhang et al., 2021). Another study reported the successful encapsulation of catechin, epicatechin and proanthocyanidins within tapioca starch nanoparticles. The encapsulated polyphenolic complexes exhibited enhanced DPPH radical scavenging capacity and demonstrated sustained-release properties in simulated digestive fluids (Lian et al., 2022). For hydrophilic active compounds, the three-dimensional gel network and strong negative charge of PNPs enable efficient encapsulation and protection of positively charged or amphoteric molecules such as anthocyanins and polyphenols through electrostatic interactions and steric hindrance, maintaining their stability across a wide pH range (Wang et al., 2021). SNPs, with their abundant hydroxyl groups and modifiability, can effectively adsorb substances like tea polyphenols through hydrogen bonding and van der Waals forces. Controlling their gelatinization or crystallization behavior enables controlled release (Xia et al., 2020).

Delivery of probiotics and live cell

Probiotics primarily comprise the genera Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Gram-positive cocci (Fayed et al., 2019; Xu et al., 2023). They exert multiple physiological functions by regulating gut microbiota balance, enhancing intestinal barrier function, and interacting with the immune system, thereby promoting digestion and absorption, aiding immune regulation, and maintaining metabolic health. However, following oral administration, a significant proportion of probiotics struggle to withstand the harsh gastrointestinal environment-including gastric acid and bile-and become inactivated. This prevents them from colonising the gut in sufficient quantities to exert effective action, thereby limiting their bioavailability and practical efficacy. Extensive research has demonstrated that encapsulating probiotics within nanomaterials significantly enhances their survival rate and biological activity. For instance, polyvinyl alcohol-pectin nanofibres produced via electrospinning technology can significantly enhance the survival rate of encapsulated Lactobacillus rhamnosus 1.0320 during storage (Xu et al., 2023). A study employed a single-step electrospray process to prepare multilayer microcapsules comprising a core layer (sodium alginate/probiotics/fish oil), a shell layer (sodium alginate/pectin), and an outer coating layer (soy protein isolate). This design encapsulates probiotics within the system, with fish oil incorporation enhancing encapsulation efficiency. Under simulated intestinal conditions, probiotic survival rates increased to 92.53 ± 0.33%, demonstrating enhanced viability across multiple stress environments (Huang et al., 2021). Separate research encapsulated bifidobacteria within multiparticulate microcapsules, successfully extending their shelf life in yoghurt (Fayed et al., 2019). Lactobacillus rhamnosus ATCC 7469 exhibited improved survival capacity under simulated gastric conditions following encapsulation within a whey protein/CNC/inulin composite (Maleki et al., 2020). Nanoparticles of water chestnut starch prepared via alkaline hydrolysis encapsulated Lactobacillus rhamnosus, forming complexes with particle sizes of 322.67 ± 3.06 nm and 427.0 ± 6.25 nm. Results indicate that encapsulated probiotics exhibit higher survival rates in simulated gastric and intestinal fluids compared to free probiotics, alongside improved tolerance under thermal stress conditions (Noman et al., 2023). Moreover, starch-based encapsulation materials demonstrate significant application potential as they do not affect the sensory properties of functional foods such as milk and yoghurt (Noman et al., 2023). Regarding probiotic delivery, upon initial contact with gut microbiota, pectin undergoes metabolic degradation within PNPs, allowing encapsulated compounds to be slowly released with minimal chemical modification. These compounds are subsequently passively absorbed in the colon, ultimately achieving sustained release and targeted delivery (Gopinath et al., 2018). The internal porous structure of SNPs provides a physical refuge for probiotics, effectively buffering external environmental stressors. Additionally, their excellent biocompatibility does not compromise food sensory properties.

Delivery of volatile and antimicrobial agents

Plant essential oils possess multiple bioactive properties including antibacterial and antioxidant effects, with applications spanning pharmaceuticals, agricultural biopesticides, and preservatives and additives in food packaging (Baldassarre et al., 2023). However, in practical applications, essential oils are constrained by their high volatility, poor water solubility, and potential phytotoxicity. Nano-encapsulation technology has been demonstrated to effectively enhance the stability and bioactivity of essential oils (Baldassarre et al., 2023). For instance, whey protein concentrate-pectin nanocomposites encapsulating rose essential oil, prepared via ultrasonic processing, achieved an encapsulation efficiency of up to 96.97% (Kord Heydari et al., 2021). Similarly, at pH = 3, the pectin-whey protein nanocomposite loaded with orange peel oil also achieved the highest loading efficiency (Ghasemi et al., 2017). Microcapsules formed from porous starch prepared via enzymatic hydrolysis and olive oil exhibit superior oxidative stability compared to free olive oil (Lei et al., 2018). Furthermore, the study indicates that pathogenic bacteria such as Enterobacter, Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Staphylococcus aureus exhibit a greater propensity to adhere to fibres of comparable size to themselves. This characteristic enhances the efficacy of antimicrobial agents loaded onto nanofibres (Rodríguez-Tobías et al., 2016). A study employed ion cross-linking, utilizing CNC as a stabiliser and calcium ions as a cross-linking agent, to encapsulate thyme essential oil within an oil-in-water emulsion via high-speed homogenisation. Experimental results demonstrated that, compared to free essential oil, this nanoemulsion system exhibited stronger antibacterial activity against the plant pathogen Pseudomonas savastanoi pv. savastanoi, whilst exhibiting no phytotoxic effects (Baldassarre et al., 2023). Edible packaging films, serving as protective layers on food surfaces, have garnered significant attention in recent years for their ability to reduce moisture evaporation, inhibit respiration, and delay spoilage and discolouration. While studies have explored the use of pectin and essential oils to prepare such films, the synthetic surfactants employed in these formulations exhibit chemical toxicity and are prone to degradation. To this end, Kusuma et al. incorporated CNC as a stabiliser into citrus pectin and limonene essential oil coatings. By enhancing stability through hydrogen bonding interactions at the microscopic level, the films exhibited improved transparency and tensile strength (Kusuma et al., 2024). These studies demonstrate that the CNC-loaded system, as a safe and stable carrier, can both enhance the bioactivity of essential oils and improve the functional properties of edible films. It holds significant research potential in fields such as crop protection and antimicrobial packaging for foodstuffs. For the delivery of volatile and antibacterial active ingredients, CNC nanocrystals have emerged as preferred materials for stabilizing Pickering emulsions of essential oils and preparing long-lasting antimicrobial coatings (Baldassarre et al., 2023). Their exceptional amphiphilicity and ability to form dense barriers at oil-water interfaces enable PNPs to create compact networks through ionic crosslinking or composite aggregation. This effectively slows essential oil volatilization rates while leveraging their mucosal adhesion properties to prolong activity on food surfaces.

Evaluating bioefficacy and safety using C. elegans model

C. elegans is a free-living nematode that occurs naturally in soil and is widely used in studies of active substance efficacy and safety assessments. This nematode model demonstrates unparalleled comprehensive advantages in the initial screening of functional foods. Their diminutive, translucent form, short lifespan and high reproductive rate enable low-cost, large-scale cultivation in laboratory settings, making them ideally suited for high-throughput screening requirements (Lim, 2026). Compared to mammalian models, the nematode life cycle spans only 21 days, enabling observation of the entire developmental journey from birth to senescence within a brief timeframe. With a constant somatic cell count and a fully transparent genetic background, it stands as one of the few complete animal models capable of conducting genome-wide functional screening at the individual level (Kaletta & Hengartner, 2006). In stark contrast, rodent models require experimental cycles spanning months, entail high maintenance costs, and face constraints from ethical review and sample size limitations. These factors make them ill-suited to support the high-throughput screening demanded during the early development stages of functional delivery systems (Xiong et al., 2025). Compared to cellular models, as a complete model organism, C. elegans possess fully developed digestive, nervous, reproductive, and muscular systems. Their pathways for neurotransmitter synthesis, storage, and metabolism are highly conserved with those of mammals (Kaletta & Hengartner, 2006). This feature enables researchers to directly observe the regulatory effects of the PPNPs-active compound complex on locomotor behavior, intestinal barrier function, reproductive output, and stress resistance at the whole-animal level (Chen et al., 2025). This effectively compensates for the shortcomings of cellular models, which cannot replicate the complex physiological environment within the body (Leung et al., 2008). Moreover, C. elegans and humans share significant similarities in their genetic background, with approximately 60–80% of human genes having corresponding homologues in nematodes. Furthermore, numerous signalling pathways are conserved between nematodes and humans (Kaletta & Hengartner, 2006). For instance, pathways such as insulin/IGF-1 signalling (core transcription factor DAF-16/FOXO), the Sirtuin longevity pathway (SIR-2.1), the mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt), and the SKN-1/Nrf2 pathway mediating oxidative stress responses (Navarro-Hortal et al., 2022) also play crucial roles in humans. For the engineered PPNPs with specific preparation strategies and structural characteristics, C. elegans-based pre-screening serves as a fast validation tool to evaluate whether the designed formulations meet food application requirements. For instance, PPNPs engineered for thermal processing tolerance can be screened via C. elegans to assess bioactivity retention after simulated heat stress; those designed for colon-targeted probiotic delivery can be evaluated through nematode intestinal barrier penetration and colonization-related indicators, ensuring the engineering goals are achieved before costly mammalian experiments. Methods for tracking PPNP uptake and absorption in C. elegans

To assess whether PPNPs are effectively taken up by C. elegans and to determine their distribution and accumulation within the organism, researchers have developed and reported a variety of reliable quantitative and imaging methods. Fluorescent labeling and microscopy are the most widely used tracking methods. Studies have shown that by encapsulating or physically adsorbing fluorescent dyes such as FITC, Lumogen® Red, or Rhodamine B onto PPNPs, and then introducing the fluorescently labeled nanoparticles into nematode culture media, live nematodes can be observed in real time using fluorescence microscopy or confocal laser scanning microscopy (Charão et al., 2015; Lucio et al., 2017; Shi et al., 2024). The study found that fluorescently labeled nanoparticles enter the intestine after oral ingestion via pharyngeal pumping, and their distribution and accumulation can be semi-quantitatively assessed by analyzing fluorescence intensity. However, some studies have suggested that fluorescent dyes may stain the nematodes themselves, making it impossible to determine the uptake distribution of PPNPs. It is worth noting that curcumin has been used for cell tracking due to its fluorescent properties (Mogharbel et al., 2018). Although no comprehensive studies have been conducted, certain naturally fluorescent plant polysaccharides or the bioactive compounds they encapsulate hold significant research potential. They can be used directly for optical tracking without the need for exogenous labeling, thereby avoiding any potential effects that labeling might have on the properties of the particles. In addition to fluorescent labeling, Raman spectroscopy is also used for in vivo imaging of nematodes. This method employs endogenous molecular vibrational signals for label-free imaging, enabling the localization and quantification of nutrient distribution within living nematodes without the need for additional probes (Cherkas et al., 2019). For quantitative analysis, in addition to imaging techniques, exposed nematodes can be thoroughly washed to remove surface-adsorbed particles, followed by lysis and homogenisation. The concentration of PPNPs or their loaded active ingredients can then be determined using methods such as fluorescence spectrophotometry and high-performance liquid chromatography, thereby calculating the nanoparticle intake per nematode. Combining these methods to visualize the uptake pathways and absorption sites of fluorescently labeled nanoparticles provides intuitive experimental evidence for understanding the biodistribution and transport pathways of PPNPs within nematodes.

C. elegans: An excellent model for functional evaluation

C. elegans have been extensively employed in research concerning anti-ageing, antioxidant effects, metabolic disorders, and neurodegenerative diseases (Navarro-Hortal et al., 2022). A comprehensive evaluation system has been established: the stress resistance of active compounds is assessed by measuring worm lifespan under normal growth temperatures, elevated temperatures, or under stress conditions. Reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels and antioxidant enzyme activity within the worms are determined using the fluorescent probe H2DCF-DA or dedicated assay kits (Jing et al., 2026). By implanting specific pathogens into the germ-free environment of nematode intestines, or by chemically inducing intestinal damage, the protective effects of target active substances on the gut are observed. Owing to the nematode's transparent body and manipulable genetic material, specific gene-deficient mutant strains can be constructed using RNA interference (RNAi) technology. This enables the observation of specific neurohormone receptor expression and neuronal morphology under a microscope, thereby identifying key targets and mechanisms of action for active compounds (Navarro-Hortal et al., 2022). Based on this model, multiple studies have confirmed the role of PPNPs in enhancing the biological activity of active compounds, such as anti-ageing, antioxidant, and neuroprotective effects. For instance, our preliminary research has demonstrated that the complex formed by adsorbing astaxanthin onto tapioca starch nanoparticles significantly prolongs the motility of nematodes, enhances their stress resistance, and extends their lifespan. This confirms that the carrier enhances astaxanthin's anti-ageing activity (Chen et al., 2025). The antioxidant and anticancer activities of EGCG are often constrained by its solubility. Researchers have utilised self-assembly to prepare lysozyme-pectin nanoparticles for EGCG encapsulation. Spectroscopic analysis confirms the formation of a stable complex through mutual interactions between the two components. Experiments using a nematode model demonstrate that EGCG encapsulated within these nanoparticles enhances nematode stress resistance and extends their average lifespan more effectively than free EGCG (Zhang et al., 2021). Another study employed electrostatic self-assembly to fabricate chitosan-pectin nanocarriers for anthocyanin delivery. In vitro experiments confirmed the system's controlled-release properties and its capacity to shield cells from damage. Further studies in C. elegans demonstrated that nematodes treated with this nanocomposite exhibited enhanced reproductive capacity and extended average lifespan (Zhao et al., 2020).

C. elegans: Biosafety assessment model

In terms of safety assessment, evaluating the potential toxicity of nanoparticles has become paramount as their preparation techniques become increasingly sophisticated and their applications expand across the medicine, cosmetics and food sectors. While the minute size of nanoparticles facilitates cellular uptake, it may also pose a risk of biochemical damage (Wu et al., 2019). Therefore, a systematic assessment of the toxicity of nanodelivery systems is essential. Several model organisms are currently used in toxicity studies, with C. elegans standing out due to its short life cycle and ease of cultivation. Unlike cellular models, worms are complete organisms with nervous, digestive, reproductive and immune systems, making them well-suited to the comprehensive investigation of an organism's absorption, metabolism and overall response to toxic substances (Pluskota et al., 2009). Meanwhile, worms offer advantages such as whole-body transparency, rapid reproduction and low cultivation costs. This makes them more suitable for the high-throughput screening of toxic substances and the direct observation of action sites. Toxicity evaluation metrics encompass multiple aspects: measuring sinusoidal movement frequency, head-shaking frequency, swallowing frequency, and defecation rhythm, among other indicators, to assess the muscle toxicity of target substances (Wei et al., 2025). By measuring the body length and width of worms alongside their egg-laying capacity, the impact of target substances on worm developmental rates and reproductive toxicity is analysed (Chen et al., 2025). Employing gene-edited mutant strains specifically expressing green fluorescent protein (GFP) to decipher the specific molecular pathways involved in toxin action enables visual assessment of neurotoxicity (Navarro-Hortal et al., 2022). Research indicates that when nanoparticles are mixed with food, they can be efficiently ingested by worms. Using Raman spectroscopy and confocal microscopy techniques, it is possible to observe fluorescently labeled nanoparticles being absorbed by worms during feeding and subsequently transported to primary organs such as intestinal epithelial cells, as well as secondary organs including the reproductive tract (Pluskota et al., 2009).

Limitations of C. elegans in PPNP evaluations

Despite the numerous advantages offered by C. elegans as a high-throughput pre-screening platform, it is essential to recognise the limitations of this model when using it for the evaluation of PPNP activity and safety. Firstly, the existing C. elegans experimental protocols are not characterised by a high level of standardization, and the predictive value of C. elegans-based readings for mammalian outcomes has not yet been systematically validated (Desaka et al., 2025). Consequently, any promising PPNP candidates identified through C. elegans screening must undergo further validation in mammalian models before any translational decisions are made. Secondly, there are fundamental pharmacokinetic differences between C. elegans and mammals, including the absence of hepatic metabolism, differences in coelomic distribution, and variations in absorption pathways (Roussos et al., 2023). Thirdly, C. elegans feeds on live bacteria and has a relatively simple microbial background, lacking a complex gut microbiota, which may alter the degradation and interaction patterns of nanoparticles (Ayala et al., 2017). Fourthly, exposure routes generally entail the immersion or feeding of bacteria coated with PPNPs, a method that does not fully replicate oral gavage or voluntary feeding in higher animals. Fifthly, given the substantial disparities in body size, metabolic rate, and lifespan, it is not feasible to extrapolate nematode dosages to humans (Gottschling & Döring, 2019). Collectively, these limitations underscore that nematodes should serve as a preliminary screening tool for PPNPs but cannot replace mammalian models for safety or efficacy assessments.

Challenges and future perspectives

In the context of large-scale manufacturing, extensive research has demonstrated the capacity of PPNPs to enhance the bioavailability of active substances. Furthermore, preparation techniques are becoming increasingly sophisticated. Nevertheless, achieving green, continuous industrial-scale production remains a critical bottleneck for practical application. Consequently, the development of efficient, environmentally friendly large-scale continuous production technologies is a key future research direction. PPNPs have achieved significant progress in targeted delivery, yet their delivery efficiency, targeting precision, and release kinetics within complex physiological environments remain to be systematically optimised. In recent years, the rise of artificial intelligence has offered novel approaches to addressing this challenge. By employing machine learning algorithms to analyse existing physicochemical properties of nanoparticles, encapsulation efficiency, and release profiles under varying conditions, predictive models can be constructed (Yadav et al., 2025). Computational modeling and molecular docking techniques may elucidate the in vivo mechanisms of action of PPNPs, providing molecular-level guidance for targeted delivery (Wang et al., 2025). Although PPNPs are generally considered safe and non-toxic, it should be noted that certain sulphated nanoparticles and specific bioactive polysaccharide derivatives have been reported to potentially exhibit cytotoxicity and immunogenicity under high-dose conditions or specific preparation processes (Bamigbade et al., 2025). For example, the acid hydrolysis and enzymatic hydrolysis methods commonly used for SNPs may leave residues of inorganic acids and amylase (Dukare et al., 2021; Hassan et al., 2022), which can affect the final pH and flavour of the product; residual enzymes may also cause allergic reactions in humans. The ion-gelation method commonly used for PNPs employs multivalent cations such as Ca2+ and Zn2+ as cross-linking agents; whilst this avoids the use of organic solvents, the residual metal ions may pose a risk. Research has shown that zinc-containing pectin nanoparticles exhibit cytotoxicity due to the release of zinc ions (Dukare et al., 2021). Concurrently, owing to their minute size, nanoparticles readily undergo absorption by biological organisms and penetrate tissue cells, subsequently circulating through the circulatory system. This also carries the potential for unknown toxicological risks (Wu et al., 2019). Therefore, prior to market entry, PPNPs must undergo thorough and rigorous in vivo simulation studies alongside systematic toxicological evaluations. It is recommended that future research increasingly incorporate in vivo models such as C. elegans. Within the framework of this evaluation system, the C. elegans model is to be regarded as a highly efficient pre-screening instrument. This model can be used to conduct high-throughput initial screening of a large number of PPNP candidate compounds, rapidly assessing their bioactivity-enhancing effects and potential toxicity. This approach effectively reduces the number of candidates to be considered and optimises the allocation of research resources prior to the commencement of costly and time-consuming mammalian experiments. It is recommended that future research efforts concentrate on the establishment of a validation pathway that encompasses the entire process, from the initial screening in nematode models to the subsequent evaluation in mammals. This approach will capitalise on the strengths of nematode models for preliminary screening, while simultaneously incorporating the confirmatory capabilities of mammalian models. This approach will facilitate a more comprehensive and physiologically relevant evaluation of the functional benefits and long-term biosafety of PPNP delivery systems, and will provide deeper insights into their molecular mechanisms of action. While the studies above demonstrate the utility of C. elegans for evaluating PPNPs, the field lacks standardized protocols that would enable cross-study comparison and meta-analysis. Key parameters requiring harmonization include exposure duration, dosing regimens, and endpoint selection. Establishing such standards would facilitate the use of nematode data to build predictive models correlating in vitro release, in vivo nematode responses, and mammalian pharmacokinetics. This represents a critical next step toward realizing the full potential of C. elegans as a high-throughput screening platform.

Conclusion

PPNPs have found extensive application across multiple food sectors, including the delivery of functional active ingredients, due to their natural origin, excellent biocompatibility and degradability. This paper systematically reviews the primary preparation methods for starch, pectin, and cellulose nanoparticles, elucidating how their encapsulation and protective functions effectively enhance the solubility, stability, and targeted release properties of hydrophobic and hydrophilic active ingredients, probiotics, and volatile antimicrobial agents, thereby significantly improving their bioavailability. It is crucial to note the nematode C. elegans has emerged as a strategic in vivo platform that bridges the gap between conventional in vitro assays and complex mammalian models. C. elegans enables rapid, multi-parametric evaluation of both the bioactivity enhancement and biosafety of PPNP-based delivery systems at the whole-organism level. This model not only effectively addresses the inherent limitations of cellular systems in reproducing multi-organ coordination, metabolic processes, and barrier functions, but also provides an ethically sound and cost-effective solution for high-throughput screening prior to costly mammalian experiments. In summary, PPNPs demonstrate considerable application potential as a green, efficient nanodelivery platform for developing next-generation functional foods and health supplements. Future research should focus on scalable green production, intelligent release systems, and comprehensive in vivo safety assessments using multi-model organisms.
