Application of indocyanine green fluorescence-guided laparoscopic hepatectomy in patients with liver metastases: a retrospective single‑center study
Authors: Lei Liu, Minjing Shen, Qi Wang, Pingchuan Ma, Yin Yin, Fubao Liu, Chunxia Ren, Qingxiang Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present study aimed to investigate the efficacy of indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence-guided laparoscopic hepatectomy for the treatment of liver metastases. Therefore, data from patients with liver metastases who underwent laparoscopic hepatectomy were retrospectively collected. A total of 69 patients were divided into the ICG group (fluorescence-guided) and conventional group, with 24 well-matched pairs analyzed after propensity score matching. Laparoscopic hepatectomy was successfully performed in all patients.The ICG group had significantly wider surgical margins than the conventional group (1.73 ± 0.57 cm vs. 1.04 ± 0.44 cm; P < 0.001). Pre-matching R0 resection rates were 91.9% (ICG) and 90.6% (conventional), with 100% R0 resection achieved in both groups post-matching. Operative time, intraoperative blood loss and postoperative ALT levels were numerically lower in the ICG group without statistical significance. The rates of overall/severe (Clavien–Dindo ≥IIIa) complications and 6-month recurrence were comparable between the two groups (all P > 0.05). In conclusion, ICG fluorescence-guided laparoscopic hepatectomy is safe and feasible for liver metastases, yielding significantly wider surgical margins. Although perioperative outcomes and 6-month recurrence rates were comparable between groups after matching, the ICG-guided approach achieved significantly wider surgical margins—a finding with potential implications for long-term oncological outcomes that warrant further investigation.

Introduction

Liver metastatic tumors are the most common malignant liver lesions and impose a significant healthcare burden on both patients and the community. It has been reported that liver metastatic tumors are commonly associated with colorectal, ovarian, and neuroendocrine cancers, and successful surgical resection serves a key role in determining patient prognosis [1–3]. Approximately 50% of patients with colorectal cancer (CRC) can develop hepatic metastases, and resection of the aforementioned lesions is pivotal for improving prognosis [4]. Furthermore, ovarian cancer liver metastasis (OCLM) exhibits distinct oncological features and different metastatic patterns. Therefore, successful surgical intervention in this setting, particularly when performed by specialists, has been associated with improved patient prognosis [5]. Similarly, surgical resection of neuroendocrine cancer liver metastasis (NCLM) has yielded favorable results, with a 5-year overall survival ranging from 60 to 80% [6].

Laparoscopic liver resection has attracted increasing attention in recent decades. With rapid advancements in laparoscopic technology, there are currently no surgical exclusion zones for experienced laparoscopic surgeons [7]. Laparoscopic liver resection for liver metastases offers several advantages, such as minimal trauma and rapid recovery [8]. However, the lack of tactile perception from laparoscopic forceps and the complexity of intraoperative ultrasound pose challenges for accurate tumor localization and demarcation line confirmation, thus limiting its application. Implementing laparoscopic anatomical hepatectomy procedures is particularly challenging, since the exact boundaries of the liver segments should be directly visualized on the monitor. However, this limitation can be effectively addressed through laparoscopic indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence staining of the liver segments [9, 10].

ICG fluorescence navigation has been validated as a safe and feasible technique in liver cancer surgery, enabling liver tumor detection and transection plane demarcation without impairing liver function. This approach has also been shown to facilitate a wider surgical margin, which can in turn contribute to improved tumor clearance [11, 12]. However, the safety and effectiveness of ICG imaging technology for laparoscopic hepatectomy remain controversial [13, 14]. The present study aimed to explore the application of fluorescence laparoscopy for liver resection of liver metastases, to evaluate its safety and feasibility, and uncovering its possible advantages.

This retrospective cohort study has been reported in line with the STROCSS 2025 guideline.

Materials and methods

Patient cohort and data collection

In the present study the clinical data of 69 patients diagnosed with liver metastases, who underwent laparoscopic resection at the Hepatobiliary Pancreatic Surgery Department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University between November 2017 and August 2024, and also collected relevant data for 6 months after the operation (until February 2025). were retrospectively analyzed. Before 2020, due to the lack of fluorescent laparoscopic equipment, the majority of patients underwent conventional laparoscopic surgery, while after 2020 fluorescent laparoscopic liver resection was commonly performed. Among the 69 patients, 37 were subjected to laparoscopic liver resection under ICG fluorescence guidance (the fluorescence group), while 32 patients underwent conventional laparoscopic liver resection (the control group). The clinical data were collected, compared, and retrospectively analyzed. The mean age of patients, including 41 males and 28 females, was 62.92 ± 8.94 years. The primary tumor origins were colorectal cancer (n = 50), gynecological tumors (n = 9), renal cancer (n = 4), stromal tumors (n = 4), and breast cancer (n = 2). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) Patients clearly diagnosed with liver metastases, and a number of liver lesions of ≤ 5; (ii) with preoperative abdominal enhanced computed tomography (CT) and PRamex magnetic resonance imaging consistent with typical liver metastases; and the diagnosis was subsequently confirmed by postoperative histopathological examination in all enrolled patients; (iii) with liver metastases assessed as resectable; (iv) age > 18 and < 80 years, with no gender restrictions; (v) patients with tolerance to general anesthesia, and no serious heart, lung and brain comorbidities; and (vi) those who provided informed consent. The exclusion criteria were the following: (i) Patients with liver metastases of the liver assessed as unresectable; (ii) those who were unwilling to continue the study or lost to follow up; (iii) patients or their legally authorized representatives unwilling to provide informed consent. (iv) patients with a history of previous liver resection. Only patients undergoing primary hepatectomy were eligible for inclusion. The present study strictly adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University (approval no. PJ2025-09-37). Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of deidentified patient data, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board.

ICG configuration and administration

ICG was dissolved in sterile injection water at a standard dose of 25 mg/vial. It is typically administered through the peripheral vein prior surgery. During the injection process, the patient’s vital signs, including respiratory rate, blood pressure, heart rate, and blood oxygen saturation, were vigilantly monitored to promptly identify potential allergic reactions and to facilitate immediate intervention when needed. Patients were administrated with a standard dose of 2.5 mg positive fluorescence staining 16–18 h prior to surgery. In patients with abnormal liver function test results (three days prior surgery), ICG injections may not be indicated. When preparing the solution, the reconstituted ICG should be thoroughly mixed with sterile water for injection until completely dissolved to ensure the efficacy of the injection. Therefore, the careful and detailed planning of the injection process is of great importance. The ICG fluorescence imaging system used in this study was the Digital Precision Medicine Fluorescence System (Model: DPM-ENDOCAM-02), a sophisticated endoscopic fluorescence imaging system.This system is primarily composed of a video processor/light source (VPI), camera, and display unit. During the surgical procedure, the device emits excitation light at a wavelength of ~ 780 nm to irradiate the liver and detects near-infrared light at ~ 840 nm. Following processing by the host system, the fluorescence signal is rendered into a specialized image for clear visualization.

Surgical methods

Routine surgical procedures: For patients with metastatic liver cancer, laparoscopic liver segment dissection was performed under fluorescence navigation. The key steps of conventional laparoscopic hepatectomy adhered to the following principles and procedures: (i) After induction of general anesthesia, pneumoperitoneum was established with carbon dioxide at a pressure of 13–16 mmHg. A total of five cannulas were strategically placed, based on tumor location; (ii) The perihepatic ligaments were surgically dissected, the first portal obstruction band was applied when required. Intraoperative ultrasound (IOUS) was performed in all cases by senior hepatobiliary surgeons to: (a) examine the entire liver for known and occult metastases; (b) assess tumor proximity to major vessels; and (c) Determine the transection plane for anatomical hepatectomy. Of note, for deeply located tumors, IOUS provided limited real-time guidance during parenchymal transection due to the absence of clear surface landmarks. This IOUS protocol was standardized and identical for both groups; iii. The resection of the liver segments or lobes was performed during surgery using an ultrasonic knife in combination with Baike forceps. Intraoperative portal obstruction was employed to minimize blood loss; iv. During surgery, the liver resection margin was determined based on the position of the ischemic line and ultrasound localization; v. Small blood vessels (< 2 mm) were sealed using electrocoagulation during liver disconnection, while noticeable bleeding or bile leakage was managed with tissue clips or sutures. Portal pedicles and hepatic veins of > 10 mm were divided by laparoscopic linear staples. vi. Accurate hemostasis was achieved along the liver resection plane, followed by placement of hemostatic materials and drainage tubes.

Selection of resection type

Non-anatomical resection was the preferred approach for secondary liver tumors, as a margin > 1 mm is considered oncologically adequate. Anatomical resection was reserved for a small subset of patients. Specifically, when a tumor extensively involved a hepatic segment, lobe, or major portal pedicle, non-anatomical resection risked compromising the blood supply to the remnant liver, leaving residual tumor, or resulting in an insufficient margin. In such cases, anatomical resection was necessary to achieve complete tumor removal while securing a clearer margin. In this cohort, the majority of patients in both groups underwent non-anatomical resection, and the distribution of resection types was similar between the two groups.

Operative methods of the fluorescence group

The surgical approach was chosen based on the location of the lesion and its association with the adjacent blood vessels. IOUS was performed as described for the conventional group. The main differences between the ICG-guided and traditional laparoscopic hepatectomy include the intraoperative exploratory and resection phases. In the ICG group, IOUS and ICG fluorescence were used for distinct but complementary purposes. IOUS served mainly to detect occult lesions, establish spatial relationships with adjacent structures, and determine the transection plane for anatomical hepatectomy. ICG fluorescence, by contrast, provided real-time visualization of the tumor margin during parenchymal transection. For lesions situated near the liver surface, the fluorescence boundary was readily apparent. For tumors located deeper within the parenchyma, IOUS offered little direct guidance once transection was underway, as no surface landmarks corresponded to the advancing plane. This limitation was effectively addressed by ICG fluorescence imaging: the peritumoral fluorescent rim—measuring approximately 1.0 cm and resulting from impaired ICG excretion in compressed hepatocytes—allowed the surgeon to gauge the resection margin continuously, thereby compensating for the lack of real-time IOUS feedback during this critical phase. The line of liver resection was ultimately determined by integrating the ischemic demarcation line, IOUS findings, and ICG fluorescence labeling. A part of the liver tissue was resected using an ultrasonic knife in combination with Baker’s forceps. Blood vessels of < 2 mm in diameter encountered during hepatic disconnection were blocked by electric coagulation. All the remaining surgical procedures were the same as those performed in the control group.

Intraoperative use of fluorescence

During the exploratory stage of ICG-guided laparoscopic hepatectomy, a combination of fluorescent staining and intraoperative ultrasound was effectively applied to identify occult lesions and improve the resection rate. In the resection stage, in addition to the conventional ischemic and intraoperative ultrasound-labeled pre-cut lines, ICG-guided laparoscopic hepatectomy could allow the positive fluorescence staining of the tumor boundaries through preoperative injection of 2.5 mg ICG, thus resulting in precise resection. For deep tumors, intraoperative ultrasound was used for localization, after which 0.025 mg ICG was injected through the portal vein near the tumor margin to provide positive fluorescent labeling of the lesion. When anatomical segmentectomy was indicated, a negative staining approach was adopted. This involved temporarily occluding blood flow to the target liver segment, followed by peripheral intravenous injection of 0.25 mg ICG. The perfused remnant liver then displayed green fluorescence, while the target segment—deprived of inflow—remained dark, outlining the intersegmental plane for resection. In the present study, 33 patients underwent preoperative positive fluorescence staining, while the remaining 4 received intraoperative portal vein puncture for positive staining. In those 4 patients, negative staining was applied to delineate the intersegmental plane during anatomical segmentectomy (Fig. 1).

Definitions

Tumor clearance was defined as microscopically negative resection margins (R0 resection) confirmed by postoperative histopathological examination. A margin distance greater than 1 mm was routinely attempted when anatomically feasible without compromising residual liver volume or surgical safety.

Liver dysfunction was defined as serum alanine aminotransferase or aspartate aminotransferase levels exceeding three times the upper limit of normal, or total bilirubin exceeding two times the upper limit of normal, within three days prior to surgery. Patients meeting these criteria did not receive indocyanine green injection.

Follow-up

After discharge, the patients were followed up via telephone or outpatient visits. Their progress, including ultrasound assessments, CT scans, routine blood tests, liver function evaluations, subsequent chemotherapy, and management of postoperative complications, were meticulously monitored until March, 2025. At six months after surgery, enhanced CT scans were performed to assess tumor recurrence or metastasis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 22.0 (IBM Corp.) and R software. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using Student’s t-test or paired t-test as appropriate, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and analyzed using the chi-square test, Fisher’s exact test, or McNemar’s test. Analysis of covariance was employed to compare postoperative outcomes after adjusting for potential confounders, including baseline liver function and tumor number. To minimize selection bias and address potential temporal confounders, propensity score matching was performed. Propensity scores were estimated by multivariate logistic regression including age, sex, BMI, tumor number, maximum tumor diameter, ASA classification, total bilirubin, albumin, preoperative ALT and year of surgery. The 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper width of 0.2 was used to match patients in the ICG group with those in the conventional group. Finally, 13 patients in the ICG group and 8 patients in the conventional group were excluded due to unmatched propensity scores, and 24 well-matched pairs (48 patients) were included in the post-matching analysis, and covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences, with values < 0.1 considered indicative of adequate balance. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 69 patients were included in this study, with 37 in the ICG group and 32 in the conventional group. Before matching, the two groups were generally comparable in terms of demographic and clinical characteristics (Table 1). After 1:1 propensity score matching, 24 well-matched pairs (48 patients) were identified. Following matching, most covariates achieved standardized mean differences below 0.2, and several key variables—including age, sex, ASA classification, total bilirubin, and year of surgery—had SMDs below 0.1. Of note, the SMD for year of surgery decreased from 0.461 before matching to 0.076 after matching, confirming effective control of temporal bias (Table 1). Intraoperative data and postoperative outcomes

The surgical outcomes of each laparoscopic liver resection procedure are summarized in Table 2. All patients successfully underwent laparoscopic surgery. In the propensity score-matched cohort, operative outcomes were comparable between the ICG and conventional groups. Although the ICG group showed numerically lower values for operation time (223.8 ± 79.6 min vs. 265.0 ± 105.2 min; P = 0.142), bleeding volume (130.0 ± 72.4 ml vs. 166.7 ± 148.7 ml; P = 0.262), and postoperative ALT levels (205.3 ± 143.0 U/l vs. 330.5 ± 327.2 U/l; P = 0.079), none of these differences reached statistical significance. However, the minimum tumor margin was significantly wider in the ICG group (1.73 ± 0.57 cm vs. 1.04 ± 0.44 cm; mean difference 0.69 cm, 95% CI 0.37–1.01, P < 0.001).

Tumor clearance and postoperative recurrence rates

In the fluorescence laparoscopy group, ICG fluorescence successfully visualized tumor lesions in all 37 patients. Of these, 33 received preoperative positive staining, and 4 underwent intraoperative negative staining via portal vein puncture. In tumors located near the liver surface, green fluorescence was observed around the tumor tissue. When the lesion was located within the liver parenchyma, light-green fluorescence became visible around the liver tumor after parenchyma transection. In the present study, three cases of liver metastases, not diagnosed by preoperative imaging, as well as several benign lesions such as hepatic hemangiomas, were identified through ICG imaging. However, ICG may also yield false-positive findings. In such cases, suspicious fluorescent areas were assessed in conjunction with preoperative imaging and intraoperative ultrasound. When located within the planned resection zone and when resection would not compromise surgical safety or remnant liver volume, these areas were removed. All such lesions were confirmed benign on postoperative pathology. Fluorescence imaging also effectively marked the resection margins and boundaries (Fig. 2). In the matched cohort, R0 resection was achieved in all 48 patients (100%). Overall complication rates did not differ significantly between the ICG and conventional groups (7/24, 29.2% vs. 8/24, 33.3%; P = 0.763), nor did the rates of severe complications requiring intervention (2/24, 8.3% vs. 1/24, 4.2%; P = 0.750). The 6-month recurrence rate was also comparable between the two groups (2/24, 8.3% vs. 3/24, 12.5%; P = 0.625).Before matching, the ICG group had a lower incidence of severe complications requiring intervention than the conventional group (2/37, 5.4% vs. 5/32, 15.6%), including bile leakage requiring drainage (0 vs. 2), bleeding requiring transfusion or reoperation (1 vs. 2), and infected ascites requiring drainage (1 vs. 1). In addition, three intraoperative bile leaks detected by ICG fluorescence were repaired immediately, which may have contributed to the comparable postoperative complication rates observed after matching.

Discussion

The incidence of liver metastases is increasing annually, with colorectal cancer, ovarian cancer and neuroendocrine cancer with liver metastases being the most common. It has been reported that ~ 50% of patients with colorectal cancer will develop colorectal liver metastases (CRLM). Until recently, the surgical resection of liver lesions was the primary treatment approach for patients with CRLM. Surgical resection is now considered as a safe and effective intervention, with a reported 5-year overall survival rate of 40–60% [15]. The decision to perform hepatectomy in patients with liver metastases from colorectal cancer depends on several key factors, including general health status, anatomical variations, history of abdominal surgery resulting in adhesions, remaining liver volume, and presence of multiple non-resectable extrahepatic lesions [16].

Laparoscopic hepatectomy is a minimally invasive procedure that has rapidly improved with advances in surgical technology and instrumentation. Emerging evidence has suggested that laparoscopic hepatectomy exhibits comparable efficacy and fewer complications compared with open surgery. Its application in hepatobiliary surgery has significantly improved the outcome of liver metastases [17, 18]. Unlike hepatocellular carcinoma, for secondary liver malignancies, the resection margin of hepatectomy only needs to be greater than 1 mm. Therefore, the use of ICG fluorescence can provide better real-time feedback on the tumor margin and a higher complete tumor resection rate [19] Although intraoperative ICG fluorescence-guided laparoscopic hepatectomy for liver metastases has been widely reported, recent reviews indicate that high-level evidence supporting its routine use remains limited [20]. Therefore, in the current study the clinical data from several patients who underwent ICG-guided laparoscopic hepatectomy at the Hepatobiliary Pancreatic Surgery Department of the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University were analyzed. The results suggested that ICG imaging could assist in identifying the space between the tumor lesion and normal parenchyma, thus promoting surgical precision. Fluorescent laparoscopic hepatectomy offers clear advantages, particularly in identifying the nature of the tumor, evaluating differentiation, detecting hidden lesions, and delineating tumor boundaries. These advantages are of significance importance for improving the prognoses of patients with secondary malignant hepatic tumors, particularly via enhancing tumor localization during tumor resection, thus providing improved perioperative outcomes [21]. Previous studies have focused on the use of fluorescence laparoscopic hepatectomy for primary liver tumors. This technique can clearly define the tumor boundary, ensure the surgical margin, and not increase surgical complications. However, there are few previous studies on liver metastases, and the surgical margin requirements for the two types of tumors are different, which shows a certain degree of novelty.

Particularly, fluorescence laparoscopy for liver metastases can be performed with both positive and negative staining techniques, each with its own merits [22]. Positive staining involves portal injection of an ICG solution into the segmental portal branch under laparoscopic ultrasound guidance, while negative staining is achieved through intravenous ICG injection following clamping of the segmental portal pedicle [23]. Compared with conventional laparoscopic hepatectomy, ICG fluorescence-guided laparoscopic hepatectomy is associated with precise tumor localization, thus resulting in a high tumor resection rate, shorter operation time, reduced resection of normal liver tissue, better preservation of postoperative liver function, and decreased tumor recurrence rate. Additionally, compared with intraoperative ultrasound, ICG fluorescence allows the chief surgeon to more timely identify tumor location and define the extent of the resection [24]. Combining fluorescence laparoscopy with intraoperative ultrasonography can enhance tumor clearance, identify tumor-adjacent blood vessels, prevent damage to blood vessels and bile ducts, and reduce both intraoperative bleeding and postoperative bile leakage [25]. Preoperative injection is mainly used for liver tumors that are superficially located and are scheduled for local resection. Intraoperative injection is mainly used for tumors that are deeply located and are scheduled for liver segment resection or liver lobe resection. These two methods have little impact on the intensity of fluorescence and false positive/negative results. Herein, small bile leakage was identified in three patients using fluorescence laparoscopy, and was immediately repaired, thus alleviating the incidence of postoperative bile leakage. Another advantage of fluorescence laparoscopy is its capacity to detect occult lesions that could elude preoperative imaging [26]. Fluorescence laparoscopy has also been applied to detect imaging-negative hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), thus highlighting its potential as a promising laparoscopic technique. For small superficial tumors, fluorescence laparoscopy enables rapid identification of lesions, thus significantly shortening operative time.

To fully account for the potential differential effects of several staining techniques, a combination of positive and negative fluorescence staining methods for fluorescent laparoscopy were applied in the present study, thus ensuring methodological comprehensiveness and scientific rigor. In the ICG cohort, three distinct fluorescence approaches were employed: preoperative peripheral intravenous positive staining, intraoperative portal venous positive staining, and negative staining for anatomical hepatectomy. Nevertheless, all three methods can achieve the purpose of effectively delineating the tumor boundary and have little effect on the assessment of the margin, thus proving the rationality of combining them into a separate ICG guidance group.The heterogeneity of this technology requires the operator to have high operational proficiency and comprehensive experience. Only by constantly improving the technical ability of the surgeon can we better ensure the promotion of research. The results demonstrated that fluorescence laparoscopy was a safe technique, characterized by reduced intraoperative blood loss. Its capacity in detecting imaging-negative tumors, supported its potential as a valuable laparoscopic technique. Importantly, intraoperative fluorescence border was wider than the actual tumor margin. However, this promoted R0 resection and ensured a sufficient surgical margin. A previous study indicated that wider resection margins were associated with improved prognosis in patients with hepatic malignancies [27]. The fluorescence staining around the tumor is caused by ICG metabolic retention in surrounding tissues compressed by the tumor. It has been reported that there is still a particular distance between the fluorescence boundary of ICG and the tumor margin [28]. In the present series, non-anatomical resection was the standard procedure for secondary liver tumors and was performed in the vast majority of patients in both groups. Anatomical resection was used infrequently and only in selected cases where tumor extent or remnant liver volume necessitated a more extended resection. The two groups were comparable with regard to resection type, and this factor did not influence the comparison of margin width. The observed difference is therefore attributable to ICG fluorescence.For HCC, a wide resection margin is emphasized, while for secondary malignant tumors of the liver, only a resection margin of > 1 mm is generally needed. Prioritizing preservation of the solid matter is essential for complete resection along the length of the tumor. Therefore, resection guided by fluorescence can ensure the integrity of the specimen after surgery, which can be further confirmed by postoperative pathology. Combining intraoperative color Doppler ultrasound and fluorescence can further enhance surgical efficiency for liver metastases [29]. Liver metastasis of colorectal cancer is the most common liver metastases in clinical practice. There are many factors influencing and predicting the prognosis of patients with liver metastasis from colorectal cancer [30–33], and even some factors can predict the complications of rectal cancer surgery [34]. However, the resection margin of liver tumors is also an important factor in predicting and influencing clinical prognosis [35].

Analysis of recurrence patterns showed that the two recurrences in the ICG-guided hepatectomy group were new lesions, whereas those in the control group occurred at the resection margin. Given the small number of events and the short follow-up duration, any inference regarding the relationship between margin width and recurrence patterns is precluded. Larger cohorts with long-term follow-up are required to evaluate this outcome.

Although overall complication rates were similar between the two groups, a difference emerged when complications were stratified by severity. Certain complications—such as bile leakage, bleeding, pancreatic leakage, or infected ascites—generally require intervention once they become clinically significant, while milder complications can be managed conservatively. In this cohort, complications requiring intervention were less common in the fluorescence group. Three intraoperative bile leaks detected by ICG imaging were repaired immediately, which may have contributed to this difference by preventing these leaks from evolving into postoperative complications.This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the 6-month follow-up period is insufficient to assess long-term oncological outcomes such as overall survival or disease-free survival. Second, as a retrospective study from a single institution, it is subject to inherent selection biases despite the use of propensity score matching. Third, we recognize that the introduction of ICG fluorescence technology coincided with a later surgical period, creating a structural temporal bias that propensity score matching cannot entirely eliminate. We included year of surgery as a key covariate in the propensity score model. After matching, the two groups were well balanced with respect to both baseline characteristics and surgical era; the standardized mean difference for year of surgery decreased from 0.461 before matching to 0.076 after matching (Table I). This statistical adjustment does not fully account for unmeasured factors such as cumulative surgeon experience and general workflow improvements. Nevertheless, the laparoscopic hepatectomy program at our institution had been established for more than a decade before the study commenced; by the time ICG fluorescence was introduced, operative techniques had already reached a mature plateau. After matching, key surgeon-dependent metrics—operative time and intraoperative blood loss—were not significantly different between groups. Nonetheless, residual confounding inherent to the study design cannot be definitively excluded. Future studies incorporating sensitivity analyses stratified by operative period are warranted to validate this finding.

It is undeniable that ICG fluorescence also has particular limitations. First, in the current imaging systems, ICG fluorescence has limited tissue penetration under near-infrared light, thus making it challenging to perform parenchymal transection based solely on the intersegmental plane between fluorescing and non-fluorescing parenchyma [36]. In such cases, identifying and exposing the landmark veins are of great importance when the inter-segmental plane is lost. Second, assessment of hepatic perfusion by intravenous ICG injection cannot be rapidly repeated, since the non-ischemic parenchyma can still emit fluorescence signals for several hours once ICG is administered [37]. Failure to detect fluorescence signals after dissection can occur if the intravenously administered ICG has not yet entered the biliary system. Additionally, in some cases, the liver-generated fluorescence noise can hinder the identification of biliary structures.

ICG fluorescence-guided surgery is operator-dependent and requires institutional expertise, representing a potential limitation. However, the characteristic 1.0 cm peritumoral fluorescent rim provides consistent, standardized guidance for resection margin assessment, helping to standardize intraoperative decision-making.

Fluorescence signals can provide guidance during surgery, thus helping surgeons avoid anatomical misidentification, while saving time and resources compared with conventional radiographic cholangiography [38]. Baseline characteristics showed that primary tumors included colorectal cancer (n = 50), gynecological tumors (n = 9), renal cancer (n = 4), stromal tumors (n = 4), and breast cancer (n = 2). Clinically, we observed that epithelial-derived tumors tended to exhibit clearer fluorescence than stromal tumors. However, statistical analysis of this limited cohort revealed no significant differences between colorectal and non-colorectal liver metastases in key surgical outcomes, including operative time, intraoperative blood loss, margin width, and postoperative complication rates (all P > 0.05). These results suggest that the benefits of ICG fluorescence guidance were consistent across different tumor origins and did not influence the overall study findings.Future studies should focus on optimizing the concentration and timing of ICG injection prior surgery, accurately puncturing the portal vein for positive staining, and enhancing the localization of poorly fluorescing tumors through iterative reconstruction images, particularly given the unique characteristics of liver metastases.

After propensity score matching, only surgical margin width remained significantly greater in the ICG group. Existing evidence confirms that wider tumor-free margins correlate with improved prognosis in patients with liver metastases [39, 40]. Our observation of wider margins may suggest a potential prognostic benefit but should be regarded as a hypothesis-generating finding rather than proven clinical benefit. Further multi-center studies with long-term follow-up are warranted to verify this assumption. The differences in operative time, blood loss, and postoperative ALT levels observed before matching were no longer statistically significant, suggesting that these apparent advantages were largely attributable to confounding factors rather than the ICG technique itself. Accordingly, wider surgical margins in the ICG group should be interpreted as a technical observation rather than a proven clinical benefit.

The matched cohort included only 24 patients in each group. The small sample size limited statistical power for secondary endpoints. All analyses of secondary outcomes should therefore be regarded as exploratory, and non-significant differences should not be overinterpreted as clinical equivalence.

The cohort included a broad spectrum of tumor types with highly variable systemic chemotherapy regimens, which precluded an analysis of the prognostic impact of perioperative treatment. The absence of adjuvant therapy data is acknowledged as a potential source of confounding in this study.

In conclusion, the current study summarized the advantages of fluorescence laparoscopy and incorporated data from our center to evaluate its safety and efficacy in the surgical treatment of liver metastases. In addition, its advantages and shortcomings were explored via comparing fluorescence laparoscopy with conventional laparoscopic surgery, thus providing new evidence for its clinical application. Although the follow-up period was relatively short, ongoing follow-up continues to strength the current data.This study suggests that ICG-guided laparoscopic hepatectomy is a valuable technique for achieving wider surgical margins in patients with liver metastases. The observed improvement in margin width is a meaningful surgical outcome. Whether it translates into better local control or affects long-term survival remains to be determined. Additionally, real-time fluorescence imaging aids in the detection of occult lesions and biliary structures, which may help reduce the risk of positive margins and postoperative bile leakage.Although the sample size in this study was limited, the results indicated that this surgical approach could assist surgeons in completing the resection of liver metastases, particularly vvia enhancing tumor boundary visualization, thus enabling complete tumor resection. Furthermore, advanced visualization of the biliary tract through laparoscopic magnification and fluorescence imaging may help reduce the risk of bile leakage and facilitate complete tumor resection.
