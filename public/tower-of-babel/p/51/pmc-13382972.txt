Enzalutamide in biochemically recurrent prostate cancer: clinical implications from EMBARK for real-world practice
Authors: Stephen J Freedland, Neal D Shore
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe fourth episode in this podcast series features a discussion between the two principal investigators from the phase III EMBARK trial (NCT02319837). EMBARK showed that in patients with prostate cancer with high-risk biochemical recurrence, enzalutamide plus leuprolide (enzalutamide combination) and enzalutamide monotherapy significantly improved metastasis-free survival compared with leuprolide alone. Furthermore, overall survival was significantly longer with enzalutamide combination compared with leuprolide alone. No new safety signals were observed, and health-related quality of life was maintained with enzalutamide combination and monotherapy (as determined by patient-reported outcomes). In this episode, the speakers discuss some of the results from EMBARK in more detail and provide their expert opinion on what the findings mean for clinical practice in the real world. They also answer some of the frequently asked questions that they receive about the study from people around the world, including discussion of whether the results apply regardless of primary therapy, if intermittent therapy is a possibility, and how the findings from EMBARK should be applied with respect to the availability of different imaging technologies.KEYWORDS: Androgen deprivation therapy, androgen receptor pathway inhibitors, biochemical recurrence, EMBARK, enzalutamide, nonmetastatic castration-sensitive prostate cancer, prostate-specific antigen, prostate-specific antigen doubling time

Considerations for the appropriate times to start and stop treatment

So, with EMBARK, patients got 37 weeks of treatment with the assigned arm that they were on and if the PSA got down to <0.2 [ng/mL]—which we now know is a very strong prognostic factor for good outcomes—they went off treatment. And so, if you had a patient that got their 37 weeks, did well, got off treatment; and now, going back on treatment, their PSA goes down really low again, could you stop it a second time? And I think the defining paper on that was actually led by Juanita Crook, published in the New England Journal [of Medicine] that really showed in patients with BCR that [were] put on either intermittent or continuous hormonal therapy, overall survival was the same, which has really established intermittent hormonal therapy for BCR as within a standard of care [17]. Real-world perspectives and shared decision-making

Enzalutamide monotherapy or enzalutamide combination therapy with ADT, which is right for which patients?

Dr. Freedland: : So, you know, one of the questions that I get, I think PSMA-PET scan is probably the number one question. The number two question I get is monotherapy or combination, enza[lutamide] plus ADT, which is right for which patients? So, I think, you know, if you’re really looking at efficacy—overall survival particularly—it’s clear, combination is the winner [10,11]. So, if a patient says “I want to live as long as possible, that’s the only thing that’s important to me.” Unequivocally, that’s a combination, that’s a simple answer. But it’s pretty rare that someone says “I don’t care about side effects, I don’t care about my quality of life, I just want to live as long as possible.” And so, it does become a much more nuanced discussion in the real world.

Dr. Freedland: : But you know, one of the other side effects we see with enza[lutamide], whether it’s monotherapy or combination, is fatigue. So, one of the questions we’ve gotten is well, since you can get fatigue with enza[lutamide], why not just start at a lower dose, for example, 80 mg? So, Neal, how would you address that?

Dr. Shore: : Yeah, I get that. You know, there’s always that and some patients sort of like force your hand, and they won’t want to start unless you start at a lower dose. But look, we’ve got so much great data of, you know, phase III trials, such as AFFIRM, PREVAIL [39,40]; wonderful phase II, big phase II studies, TERRAIN [41], STRIVE [42]; other phase IIIs, PROSPER [34], ARCHES [28], and now EMBARK [10]. And we always began with the approved 160 mg dose. And that data goes back to some really great early‑phase data with the folks, with, at Medivation when enzalutamide—we called it MDV 3100—the typical 40 mg tablet or capsule. So, fatigue is definitely a real problem in all tumor types, virtually all oncolytic therapies, certainly in prostate cancer. And so, if I have a patient, frankly, who says “I don’t want to try another therapy,” no matter how hard I’ve explained to them or a of good a job, “look you need this, this is going to help you.” I have had some advantage with a few patients and saying “OK, well look, here’s the starting, appropriate label dose. Why don’t we, we can occasionally start you a little bit less. Let’s see how you do.” And then after I see them back in a couple of weeks or a period of time, and then I try to get them back to the label indication of 160 mg [20].

Dr Freedland: : Yeah, you said it nicely, I mean we see fatigue—just being a 70‑year‑old man in today’s world is fatiguing, having advanced prostate cancer is fatiguing, or therapies can cause fatigue. But actually, my experience is that the majority of these patients don’t get fatigue. And I think it’s very reasonable and, to me, I’m always hesitant to start something at a lower dose where we think it may work, but we actually don’t know, just because we’re worried about some side effect that they haven’t gotten yet. So, I’d rather start at the full dose as much as possible. Again, every now and then you have a patient that just says no and obviously patients can decide for themselves. But I do like to start at that full dose knowing that if we go down—if we go back to phase I data [43], I mean, it’s older data, but I think it probably is efficacious at lower doses, but we don’t ultimately know that. And so, to me, when possible, it’s always nice to, you know, start at the regular dose. And I think there are ways to mitigate fatigue, exercise, diet, other approaches such as that [44].

Dr. Shore: : A really important topic, you know, nutrition and exercise. You’ve done a great amount of work on that [44], that’s a whole other podcast.

If monotherapy is so well tolerated and is already a form of treatment deintensification, why do we need to stop at 9 months? Why not keep it going?

Dr. Shore: : So, one of the questions that always comes up too, and I like this question, if monotherapy is so well tolerated and is already a form of treatment deintensification, why do we need to stop at 9 months as we did in our interruption protocol? Why not keep it going? How do you think about that, Steve?

Dr. Freedland: : I think it’s very reasonable and I think if someone’s tolerating the medicine well, you know, I think it’s a discussion to be had. I mean, obviously stopping a medicine, seeing your PSA go up is going to cause anxiety, stress, worrying about things. So, if the patient’s really tolerating things well, I’m perfectly OK if they want to stay on it. But Neal, you have one of the largest experiences with monotherapy probably in the world. What’s been your experience?

Dr. Shore: : Yeah, I kind of have gone in the arc of my career where we just had to be so intensive and think about how we could do better with combination strategies and intensification. But I like to think that this deintensification—or the sort of the euphemism of a treatment holiday—is oftentimes a real nice break for patients. And so, even the cutting down on their daily pill counts, maybe there’s some psychological quality‑of‑life benefit. This is totally empiric on my part, and there’s probably some really interesting health‑economic outcome‑reporting measures that one needs to further look at, and how the budget‑impact models might affect the healthcare system. I think at the end of the day, we’ll kind of rehash this very important point. We have ARPIs, enzalutamide being really the first true ARPI to get approved [20] after the discovery of the androgen biosynthesis inhibitor, abiraterone acetate. But I think for all of us, it gets back to the importance of individualizing to the patient. What is it that they want? Not necessarily what we think is always their best recommendation but being able to explain the data and what the options are through shared decision‑making.

Dr. Freedland: : No, absolutely agreed. I mean ultimately the patients are making the decisions, so, I think that’s the key take‑home here.

Do all patients need to see a cardiologist before starting treatment?

Dr. Freedland: : So, another question we get is, you know, enzalutamide as well as all the ARPIs have cardiovascular risks [20], ADT has cardiovascular risks in that sense [45,46]. So, adding an ARPI, you know, does add a little bit more. So, do we need our patients to see a cardiologist before they start these medicines? Neal, what do you think?

Dr. Shore: : Yeah, well, it’s just clearly not, but it would be great if we could, right? I mean, I’ve always tried in the community to create models of multidisciplinary access. It’s just a real-world conundrum. We’d love to be able to have your cardio-oncologist, you know, down the hallway, and as well as your neuro-oncologist, your pharmacist, your med-onc [medical oncologist], your uro-onc [urological oncologist], your rad-onc [radiation oncologist], and your pathologist. I mean the list just keeps getting longer and longer, that flywheel of the MDT [multidisciplinary team], not to mention all the important nursing services. So, I think it’s important, I think, for all patients to have a primary care physician, but as with uro-oncology, medical oncology, cardiologists, we’ve got person‑power shortages. That said, I think it’s really incumbent upon all of us who take care of prostate cancer patients to just be more educated and more knowledgeable. The fact that the drugs that we discuss with patients—going back to the vaunted days of Huggins and Hodges T‑suppressors [47]—almost everything we discuss has a potential cardio-oncologic and neuro-oncologic impact. And so, it’s not just for us in the US, it’s really for everybody all over the world. How can we think about, you know, having on speed dial or if we were able to have virtual tumor boards, how do we think about the patients more holistically rather than OK, the PSA goes down and I declare victory. We have to look at the entirety of the patient, and cardio‑oncologic, neuro-oncologic, nutrition, exercise, all these things are—bone health—remarkably important. What about you, Steve?

Dr. Freedland: : Yeah, no, I agree. I don’t want to overstate things. I mean, I think I said ADT has a severe cardiovascular risk, I mean, it seems to increase the risk of cardiovascular disease. So, it’s just we use a lot of ADT, so, it is, you know, affecting a lot of people, but it’s not like everyone’s having bad heart disease from these medications. And if there were, we wouldn’t see the overall survival benefits that we do. So, clearly there are, you know, great benefits to these meds [medications], but there are some risks. And I think—as you said—I mean, essentially seeing a cardiologist is a nice to have, but I don’t think it’s a have to have and there’s just not enough cardiologists to go around, I think is the bottom line.

What is the best approach for managing breast-related adverse events with enzalutamide monotherapy?

Dr. Freedland: : So, in terms of managing breast adverse events with monotherapy, you talked about the tamoxifen but can you give us a little bit more details on that, as well as other things, breast irradiation, etc.

Dr. Shore: : I did mention it earlier. It’s really good for our colleagues to learn what we, what trials and things that we’ve done. We’ve done monotherapy enzalutamide—clearly—the largest cohort, you know, 355 patients in EMBARK [10]. We did a large phase II study called ENACT [48], we published it in JAMA Oncology. And, again, we saw a lot of breast‑related symptoms because of the unopposed increase in estrogen that affects the breast tissue. As I mentioned earlier, a lot of our colleagues around the world, they looked into these different opportunities [49–52]. A lot of it was at high‑dose bicalutamide and we’ve actually now presented this information at APCCC [Advanced Prostate Cancer Consensus Conference] [53], myself and Lisa Horvath, who’s a wonderful medical oncologist in Australia. And we realized that, again, you have the opportunity to work with your radiation oncologist and mitigate a lot of these breast-related symptoms from monotherapy ARPI [38], specifically what we’re discussing now, enzalutamide. But when you’re gonna have other opportunities, such as using a SERM at a very, very low dose. I mean, the 10 mg is, it’s an incredibly low dose and you don’t even have to administer it on a daily basis. So, I think it just adds to our armamentarium for patients. I encourage our colleagues to become more familiar with this, especially if you’re thinking about using the 160 mg enzalutamide monotherapy for high-risk BCR. Steve?

Dr. Freedland: : Yeah, no, I think you said it well. You know, I mean, something that we need to be aware of. We need to do preventative care for these things, not wait for the breast symptoms to happen. But well said.
