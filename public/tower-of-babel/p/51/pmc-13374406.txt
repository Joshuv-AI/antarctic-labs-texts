Real-world efficacy and safety of avacopan in ANCA-associated vasculitis: a retrospective comparative study
Authors: Franz Thiele, Johanna Schneider, Eric Prager, Ilona Jandova, Björn C Frye, Reinhard E Voll, Markus A Schramm, Nils Venhoff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesAvacopan, an oral C5a receptor antagonist, is approved for treatment of severe antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitis (AAV) in combination with rituximab or cyclophosphamide. This study evaluated its real-world efficacy, safety and glucocorticoid-sparing effects.MethodsThis retrospective single-centre study included 35 patients with new-onset or relapsing AAV treated with avacopan plus standard induction therapy (rituximab, cyclophosphamide or both). Outcomes were compared with 70 matched controls receiving standard therapy. Primary endpoints were remission (Birmingham Vasculitis Activity Score (BVAS)=0, prednisolone ≤5 mg/day) at 6 and 12 months. Secondary endpoints included relapse, renal function, glucocorticoid exposure and adverse events.ResultsBaseline characteristics were generally comparable, although median estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was lower in the avacopan group (24 vs 49 mL/min). Induction therapy included rituximab (28%), cyclophosphamide (11%) or combination therapy (61%), reflecting severe disease. Remission rates were similar at 6 months (69% vs 68%) but tended to be higher with avacopan at 12 months (86% vs 68%; p=0.11). Relapses occurred less frequently with avacopan (16% vs 51%, p=0.003), including after treatment discontinuation. Avacopan was associated with accelerated glucocorticoid tapering (≤5 mg/day; 102 vs 164 days, p<0.001), lower cumulative doses (3266 vs 4288 mg, p=0.008) and greater renal function improvement (ΔeGFR+18 vs +7 mL/min after 1 year). Nine patients (26%) discontinued avacopan due to adverse events.ConclusionIn a real-world practice, avacopan was effective with an acceptable safety profile, enabling steroid sparing, improved renal recovery and reduced relapse rates, including in patients receiving combined induction therapy. Benefits persisted beyond 12 months, but prospective confirmation of these presumed long-term benefits is needed.Keywords: ANCA-associated vasculitis, avacopan, real-world, glucocorticoid-sparing, renal outcome, cyclophosphamide, rituximab

Introduction

Antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA)-associated vasculitides (AAV) are systemic autoimmune diseases that primarily affect small vessels, with granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) and microscopic polyangiitis (MPA) as the most frequent subtypes.1 Both conditions can cause severe organ damage and are associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. Treatment requires intensive immunosuppressive therapies,2 often including high-dose glucocorticoids, which are major contributors to long-term treatment-related toxicity.3 Consequently, the development of effective steroid-sparing therapies is of considerable clinical importance.

Avacopan is an oral complement 5a receptor (C5aR) antagonist that inhibits the alternative complement pathway and neutrophil activation, both central mechanisms in AAV pathogenesis. After positive results from phase 2 studies,4 5 the phase 3 randomized trial ADVOCATE (Avacopan for the Development of Vasculitis Other Corticosteroid Alternative Trial Evaluation) demonstrated that avacopan was non-inferior to standard prednisolone taper for achieving remission at 26 weeks, and superior with respect to sustained remission at 52 weeks, with additional benefits in renal function recovery.6,8 However, the ADVOCATE trial excluded patients with severe renal involvement (an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) <15 mL/min) and was limited to 1 year of follow-up. In addition, remission induction was restricted to either rituximab (RTX) or cyclophosphamide (CYC) alone, whereas combination therapy, often applied in real-world practice for severe renal or pulmonary disease, was not evaluated. Furthermore, remission-maintenance therapy with azathioprine was administered only to patients treated with CYC; patients receiving RTX for remission induction did not receive maintenance immunosuppressive treatment until week 52.6

This study provides real-world comparative data on the use of avacopan in AAV, focusing on efficacy, relapse rate, renal outcomes, glucocorticoid exposure and safety. In contrast to the ADVOCATE trial, our study included patients with severely impaired renal function <15 mL/min at baseline, remission induction with combined RTX and CYC, incorporated maintenance immunosuppressive agents following RTX induction, and included follow-up of more than 52 weeks.

Methods

Study design and patients

We conducted a retrospective single-centre study including patients with new-onset or relapsing GPA or MPA, diagnosed according to 2022 ACR/EULAR criteria,9 with organ-threatening or life-threatening disease. The decision to initiate treatment with avacopan was at the discretion of the treating physician; following its approval, the drug was used at our centre primarily for patients with severe AAV (especially those with severe kidney involvement) and comorbidities that required particularly restrictive use of glucocorticoids. Patients treated with avacopan plus standard remission induction therapy (RTX, CYC or both) were compared with matched controls treated similarly but without avacopan. A manual nearest neighbour approach was used for matching (1:2), considering age, sex, disease status (new-onset vs relapse), disease activity (Birmingham Vasculitis Activity Score; BVAS), subtype (MPA or GPA), organ involvement, induction therapy and comorbidities.

Treatment regimens

Avacopan was administered orally at 30 mg two times per day in addition to standard remission induction therapy followed by maintenance therapy. RTX was given as 375 mg/m² once weekly for 4 weeks or 1000 mg on days 0 and 14. CYC was administered intravenously or orally using standard dosing.10 Combined induction therapy (RTX+CYC) was particularly used in patients with severe renal and/or pulmonary manifestations. Duration of induction therapy was at the discretion of the treating physician. Usually six cycles of intravenous CYC, resulting in a cumulative dose of approximately 6g, were administered. Maintenance therapy predominantly consisted of RTX (500 mg every 6 months), while azathioprine, mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) and methotrexate were less frequently used. Prophylaxis against Pneumocystis jirovecii and osteoporosis followed current guidelines.

Outcome measures and definitions

The primary outcome measure was the proportion of patients in remission at 6 and 12 months. Secondary outcome parameters were relapse-free survival, time to remission, cumulative prednisolone doses, renal function, adverse events and avacopan discontinuation. We used the BVAS V.3 to determine AAV activity.11 Disease remission was defined as the absence of signs of active disease (BVAS=0) and a daily prednisolone dose ≤5 mg. An increase in BVAS >0 after a period of remission was defined as relapse. Glucocorticoid doses were converted to prednisolone equivalents. Renal function was assessed using creatinine and the CKD-EPI eGFR formula.12 Adverse events included infections requiring hospitalisation, cytopenia and liver enzyme elevation. Severe cytopenias and liver injury were defined by standard laboratory thresholds. Patients were monitored at approximately 1, 3, 6, 9, 12, 18 and 24 months after induction therapy. Data collected included demographics, disease characteristics, comorbidities, treatment regimens, laboratory results, adverse events and outcomes. All data were extracted from electronic medical records.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median (IQR) or mean±SD; categorical variables as n (%). Comparisons employed Mann-Whitney U, χ2-test, Fisher’s exact or Welch’s t-test as appropriate. Kaplan-Meier analysis with log-rank test assessed relapse-free survival. For multivariate analyses (logistic regression, Cox regression), we used the backward elimination method. Significance threshold: p<0.05. For the adverse event count, we took the total number of reported adverse events in all visits into account. SPSS V.29 (IBM, Armonk, New York) was used for data analysis. GraphPad Prism V.10.3.1 was used for graph preparation.

Results

Study population and treatment outcomes

Thirty-five patients were treated with avacopan and compared with 70 matched controls who did not receive avacopan. Median age was 62 years, 56% of patients had GPA and 79% had new-onset disease. Overall, the cohort included 83 new-onset and 22 relapsing AAV cases, with a median BVAS of 15 (IQR 12–20). Both treatment groups were predominantly male and had comparable disease characteristics, including organ involvement. However, renal impairment was more severe in the avacopan group with a lower median eGFR (24 vs 49 mL/min), and a higher proportion of patients with eGFR <15 mL/min (31% vs 19%). Comorbidities potentially exacerbated by glucocorticoids were present in 66% of avacopan patients and 57% of controls (table 1).

Induction therapy consisted of high-dose glucocorticoids in combination with RTX (28%), cyclophosphamide (11%) or both (61%). RTX was used in 89% of patients overall. The frequent use of combination therapy reflects the high disease severity with renal and pulmonary involvement. Approximately 15% of all patients required intensive care unit treatment at the time of treatment initiation. Maintenance therapy differed between groups, with RTX used predominantly in the avacopan group, whereas controls more often received non-RTX immunosuppressants including azathioprine, MMF or methotrexate (table 1). For this reason, subgroup analyses were conducted to assess remission duration and relapse rates in patients who received RTX for remission maintenance; in the avacopan group, this included 24 patients, compared with 37 patients in our control group.

Avacopan was initiated a median of 4 days (IQR 1–10) after the start of induction therapy. Median follow-up duration was 497 days in the avacopan group versus 703 days in controls. Remission at 6 months was achieved in 18/26 (69%) of avacopan-treated patients compared with 45/66 (68%) in controls. At 12 months, remission rates tended to be higher in the avacopan group, 86% (18/21) compared with 68% (46/68) (p=0.11). We performed a logistic regression for remission status at 12 months, showing maintenance therapy with RTX (OR: 6.5; 95% CIs 2 to 20.9) and a higher cumulative dose of prednisolone (OR: 2.5; 95% CIs 1.4 to 4.4 for one SD) being significantly correlated with status of remission, whereas treatment with avacopan was not among the significant covariates (see online supplemental material). Median time to remission was similar between groups (169 days with avacopan vs 195 days in controls (p=0.3)).

During follow-up, 40% of patients (34/84) experienced at least one relapse. Relapses were significantly less frequent in the avacopan group compared with controls (16% vs 51%, p=0.003), including among patients receiving RTX maintenance therapy (18% vs 44%, p=0.05). Following the definition by Hellmich et al,13 major relapses occurred in 1/4 patients (25%) treated with avacopan as well as in 9/30 patients (30%) in our control group. Although the follow-up time differed slightly between groups when considering patients in whom the achievement of remission has been documented (median 21 months for avacopan vs 25 months for controls), Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated a significantly lower cumulative relapse risk in avacopan-treated patients over the entire follow-up period (log-rank p=0.045, figure 1). In a Cox-regression-analysis including several covariates (avacopan treatment, age, sex, diagnosis, initial diagnosis or relapse at baseline, BVAS, C reactive protein and GFR at baseline, organ involvement, type of induction and maintenance treatment, cumulative prednisolone doses), the significant positive effect of avacopan treatment on relapse-free survival was confirmed with a HR of 4.195 (95% CIs: 1.5 to 12, p=0.007, see figure 1 and online supplemental material).

Renal recovery was stronger in the avacopan group. Despite lower baseline eGFR, median renal function exceeded that of controls after 24 months (figure 2a,b). When performing a subgroup analysis restricted to patients with a baseline eGFR <70/mL/min, improvement of renal function was still more pronounced in patients receiving avacopan (figure 2c). When considering only patients receiving RTX maintenance therapy (n=21 in avacopan and n=35 in control group, dialysis patients excluded), a greater recovery of GFR is still evident in patients treated with avacopan (online supplemental figure 2). Notably, 31% of patients in the avacopan group and 19% of controls had severe renal impairment (eGFR <15 mL/min) at baseline, with a mean eGFR of 9.5 mL/min, patients who would have been excluded from the ADVOCATE trial. Six patients in the avacopan group required haemodialysis at least once, five of whom were dialysis dependent at baseline. None of these six patients achieved long-term renal recovery during follow-up and avacopan was discontinued for all six cases for various reasons.

Avacopan discontinuation, glucocorticoid use and safety

Avacopan was discontinued after a median treatment duration of 210 days, most frequently in the context of stable remission (17/35, 49%). During the first 12 months, five patients in our avacopan group were lost to follow-up, three of them in the first 90 days after enrolment. Two more patients died shortly after enrolment due to severe comorbidities unrelated to AAV and its treatment (figure 3). Avacopan was discontinued in 9/35 patients (26%) due to adverse events. The most common adverse events leading to discontinuation were lymphopenia and hepatopathies. Three patients (one female, two males, age 65–70 years) discontinued avacopan treatment following the diagnosis of a newly diagnosed malignancy (multiple myeloma, prostate carcinoma, urinary bladder carcinoma). Urinary bladder carcinoma was diagnosed 7 months after first intake of avacopan, although it had been suspected prior to avacopan initiation. The two remaining malignancies were diagnosed within the first 2 months of treatment. Two of the three patients had received CYC induction therapy (cumulative doses 600 mg and 2250 mg). Discontinuation of avacopan after 1 year of treatment was common. Six patients received avacopan for more than 400 days, and all discontinued treatment during the second year while in stable remission.

Glucocorticoid exposure was significantly lower in the avacopan group. Time to tapering prednisolone to ≤5 mg/day was shorter compared with controls (median 102 vs 164 days, p<0.001) with lower cumulative doses both until remission (3266 vs 4288 mg, p=0.008) and over follow-up (4317 vs 6795 mg, p<0.001). After once reaching a daily prednisolone dose of 20 mg, the median cumulative prednisolone dose over the following 52 weeks was in median 2046 mg (quartiles: 1433/2385 mg) in patients receiving avacopan.

In patients with at least one follow-up, complete glucocorticoid withdrawal was achieved in 17/31 (55%) avacopan-treated patients compared with 17/68 (25%) controls (p=0.006). Median time to complete withdrawal was significantly shorter in the avacopan group (241 vs 431 days, p=0.015). Discussion

This study evaluated the real-world use of avacopan in patients with AAV, by comparing patients treated with avacopan to a matched control group receiving standard therapy without avacopan. Given the recent approval of avacopan, comparative real-world data remain limited, particularly in patients with severe disease and complex treatment regimens.

Baseline characteristics were largely comparable between groups, including disease subtype, disease activity and organ involvement. However, renal function was significantly more impaired in patients receiving avacopan. This likely reflects treatment selection in clinical practice as the ADVOCATE trial reported a greater benefit in patients with major renal involvement,6 potentially influencing treatment decisions in our centre. In both treatment groups, the high proportion of severe renal involvement among patients with generally highly active AAV may further have contributed to the relatively high number of patients who received combined induction therapy with CYC and RTX. Our cohort reflects epidemiological patterns in Germany, with a predominance of GPA over MPA.14 Renal involvement was the most frequent manifestation in both GPA and MPA, with combined renal and pulmonary involvement observed in 48% of GPA and 35% of MPA patients, underscoring the high disease severity of the patients in this study. Avacopan’s effectiveness in these complex cases with capillaritis-driven severe manifestations in the kidneys (active glomerulonephritis) or lungs (diffuse alveolar haemorrhage) is supported by the literature.15 Comparable studies from Spain16 and the USA17 included more MPA than GPA patients, reflecting regional incidence differences.18 19

At 12 months, remission was achieved in 86% of avacopan-treated patients, consistent with other real-world data,17 and higher than the rates reported in the ADVOCATE trial (72% at 6 months and 66% at 12 months). This discrepancy is likely due to stricter remission criteria requiring complete steroid discontinuation for ≥4 weeks. While ADVOCATE required complete glucocorticoid discontinuation for sustained remission, our study, similar to other real-world cohorts, defined remission as BVAS=0 with a prednisolone dose of ≤5 mg/day. Additionally, patients in our cohort also received higher glucocorticoid doses and frequently underwent combination induction therapy with RTX and CYC, as supported by the Rituximab versus Cyclophosphamide in ANCA-associated Vasculitis (RITUXVAS) trial,20 whereas ADVOCATE permitted only one agent. Combination therapy tends to be associated with higher remission rates21 22 and may partly explain the favourable remission rates observed in our avacopan group. However, it must be stated that the potential positive effects of combined induction therapy have not been investigated in randomised controlled trials, and therefore combined induction therapy is not explicitly recommended in the guidelines of EULAR and ACR. Meanwhile, the latest Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines for the management of AAV recommend considering the combination of RTX and CYC in severe cases.23 Furthermore, in contrast to ADVOCATE, most of our patients received RTX for maintenance therapy, probably contributing to sustained remission. The higher proportion of patients in our avacopan group who received RTX maintenance therapy compared with the control group is particularly relevant for interpreting our results regarding the presence of remission at 12 months and the occurrence of relapses. A subgroup analysis restricted to patients receiving RTX maintenance (n=24 avacopan group vs n=37 control group) still showed significantly lower relapse rates in avacopan-treated patients (18% vs 44%, p=0.05), suggesting an independent contribution of avacopan. These findings are in line with emerging real-world evidence, indicating sustained relapse reduction beyond 1 year,24 25 extending ADVOCATE trial reports. Meanwhile, our study indicates a trend but no significant benefit of avacopan therapy regarding remission at 12 months. The respective logistic regression shows maintenance therapy with RTX and higher cumulative prednisolone doses as significant covariates for remission at 12 months. Both aspects, RTX for maintenance of remission and also prolonged treatment with low-dose prednisolone are known factors associated with lower relapse rates in GPA/MPA. Interestingly, in ADVOCATE, the ratio of patients in sustained remission at 12 months was significantly higher in the avacopan arm despite the absence of RTX maintenance therapy and use of a stricter glucocorticoid tapering protocol than in our real-world setting, suggesting an independent contribution of avacopan. However, given the potential confounding effects of RTX and glucocorticoid treatments, our findings cannot conclusively confirm this assumption.

When time to remission was analysed, patients receiving avacopan achieved remission earlier though this was not statistically significant. Comparing this finding to other studies is challenging, as most report remission only at specific follow-up points (26 and 52 weeks) rather than exact timing. The steroid-sparing effect of avacopan, shown in the ADVOCATE trial,6 was confirmed in our cohort as has been also reported in other real-world cohorts. Patients receiving avacopan achieved prednisolone doses ≤5 mg/day significantly faster and had lower cumulative glucocorticoid exposure both until remission and over follow-up.

Despite this, cumulative prednisolone doses in our study were relatively high, with ~3000 mg in our avacopan group until reaching remission (in median after 170 days), compared with ~1600 mg in the avacopan group of the ADVOCATE study after 52 weeks. Similar observations have been made in other real-world studies also reporting higher cumulative doses.17 26 27 This difference is largely attributable to study design—ADVOCATE excluded patients receiving >20 mg/day prednisolone at baseline,6 whereas our cumulative doses were calculated based on all steroids administered, including the frequently used steroid pulse therapies for remission induction, which are commonly used in clinical practice. When cumulative glucocorticoid exposure in our study was calculated from the point at which patients reached ≤20 mg/day, steroid doses in our avacopan group closely approximated those reported in ADVOCATE. Therefore, it can be concluded that the reported very low cumulative prednisolone doses in the ADVOCATE study are at least partly attributable to the design restrictions of the study, rather than treatment effect alone.

Despite avacopan use, the median time to taper prednisolone to ≤5 mg/day was 102 days, suggesting that the steroid-sparing potential of avacopan may not be fully utilised in clinical practice, especially when compared with the ADVOCATE trial, where patients had to fully taper prednisolone within 4 weeks from baseline. In our study, the treating physicians may have adopted a cautious tapering approach due to the very severe organ involvement at baseline and perceived high relapse risk. While recommendations from EULAR or ACR regarding the tapering of glucocorticoids under avacopan are still lacking, emerging recommendations including from the Canadian Vasculitis Research Network support more rapid tapering strategies.28 Although we believe that completely discontinuing glucocorticoids within 4 weeks is not a realistic goal for all patients—especially those with very severe disease—a more aggressive tapering regimen would certainly have been possible in some patients of our cohort and should be attempted regularly in clinical practice, especially when using avacopan.

Beyond its glucocorticoid-sparing effect, the observed reduction in relapse rates with avacopan, including after treatment discontinuation, raises the possibility of more durable disease control. Sustained inhibition of C5a-mediated neutrophil priming and complement activation may result in deeper immunological remission during the induction phase, potentially lowering relapse risk beyond active treatment. Furthermore, current literature suggests that the combined use of RTX and avacopan for maintenance therapy may have synergistic effects and could, therefore, lead to prolonged remission.29 30 While optimised low-dose glucocorticoid regimens may achieve acceptable disease control in selected patients, avacopan may offer particular benefit in those with severe renal and/or pulmonary involvement, or in patients at high risk for steroid-related toxicity. Given its high cost and limited availability, careful patient selection remains essential. Moreover, as our cohort predominantly represents patients with severe disease, these findings may not be generalisable to less severe AAV phenotypes.

Renal outcomes favoured avacopan despite more severe baseline impairment. While lower baseline eGFR provides greater potential for improvement, subgroup analysis restricted to patients with baseline eGFR <70/mL/min and patients receiving maintenance therapy with RTX confirmed greater eGFR recovery with avacopan. Early initiation of avacopan (avacopan was started at a median of 4 days after induction) may have contributed to this effect, consistent with other real-world studies.17 In line with previous reports,24 renal recovery in our cohort was more pronounced in the first months, but distinct from previous reports, continued into the second year, suggesting potential long-term renal benefits, though data remain limited given the recent introduction of avacopan. A recently published real-world study from the USA indicates that avacopan can be used in patients with severe renal impairment and/or need of dialysis.25 Our findings regarding the use of avacopan in patients on dialysis are less encouraging, as none of the six avacopan-treated patients with need of dialysis reached long-term renal recovery.

Safety of avacopan was generally acceptable. Hospitalisation-requiring infections were slightly more frequent in the avacopan group, but not statistically significant. Lymphopenia, typically considered as an avacopan side effect, was more common in controls, likely due to higher cumulative CYC doses. Severe liver damage was rare, and no life-threatening hepatic events, as described before,31 occurred in our study. Adverse events were concentrated in the first 4–6 weeks, supporting closer monitoring during the first 3 months of treatment. Hypogammaglobulinaemia was slightly more common in avacopan patients, likely influenced by the frequent concomitant use of RTX.32 Avacopan discontinuation due to adverse events occurred in 26% of patients, comparable to other real-world data.17 26 Some discontinuations were for events unrelated to avacopan, such as newly diagnosed malignancies.

Limitations of our study include the retrospective design, differences in treatment groups, in parts non-standardised therapeutic pathways, missing data, variable follow-up time and potential selection bias. Changes in standard-of-care modalities over time may have influenced comparability. Steroid-associated damage was not routinely assessed, as tools like the glucocorticoid toxicity index6 are not regularly used in clinical practice at our centre.

In summary, our findings confirm and extend key conclusions from the ADVOCATE trial. Avacopan demonstrated effective glucocorticoid-sparing, high remission rates, reduced relapse risk and an acceptable safety profile in routine care. Importantly, our data support its use in patients with severe disease, including those receiving combination induction therapy with RTX and CYC, and those with advanced renal impairment. Prospective studies with longer follow-up are needed to define optimal treatment duration and long-term benefits of avacopan across diverse AAV populations.
