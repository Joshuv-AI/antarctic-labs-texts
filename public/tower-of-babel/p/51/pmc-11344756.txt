Impact of NaCl stress on phytoconstituents and bioactivity of Matricaria chamomilla: a multi-analytical approach
Authors: Nabeela Shakir, Sadaf Anwaar, Nyla Jabeen, Tauseef Anwar, Huma Qureshi, Mehmooda Munazir, Wajid Zaman, Walid Soufan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Matricaria chamomilla (Asteraceae), commonly known as chamomile can tolerate freezing temperatures and grows in many soil types. This plant is found on all continents and has significant medicinal value. There are more than 120 chemicals detected in chamomile flowers, with the majority found in the essential oil. In this study, M. chamomilla was given the NaCl stress of 0 mM, 1 mM, 100 mM, and 150 mM concentrations This study was the first to assess the efficacy of German chamomile upon exposure to salt stress hence plant particles that had been dried and powdered were analyzed using, phytochemical tests, Fourier Transform Infrared and UV–Vis spectroscopy, thin layer chromatography, fluorescence recovery after photobleaching assay, antibacterial and antioxidant activity. The characterization and results of these activities show amazing results which enhance their antibacterial property with an increased zone of inhibition when the samples of salt stress of the above-given concentrations were compared to the control samples. More graph analysis indicates an effective impact of salt stress on the phytoconstituents of M. chamomilla. Other than that, there was a clear flower induction upon salt stress, as a variety of compounds are regarded as essential to the biological functions of chamomile flowers according to the phytoconstituent screening which can be further used in the cosmetic industry, pharmaceutical industry, and all other fields as well for various application as a nano-drug or bio-drug. Due to this, this plant became essential for plant biotechnology research.

Introduction

The global agricultural landscape faces numerous challenges, one of which is soil salinity, a critical factor impacting plant growth and productivity. Salinity stress is a significant abiotic stressor that can lead to reduced crop yields and compromised plant health1. This is particularly relevant in regions where soil salinity is prevalent, affecting the livelihood of farmers and the sustainability of agriculture. In response to this challenge, various strategies have been employed to understand and mitigate the effects of salinity on plants, one of which includes the use of medicinal plants known for their resilience and adaptive capabilities.

An annual plant of the family Asteraceae, M. chamomilla is sometimes referred to as chamomile, German chamomile, Hungarian chamomile, blue chamomile, or scented mayweed. True chamomile is an annual plant with spindly-looking roots that are thin and flat-penetrating. The tall, much-ramified stem can grow up to 80 cm in height. The tall, thin bi to tripinnate leaves. The heterogamous, pedunculate flower heads range in diameter from 10 to 30 mm2. It is used to treat skin irritations, bruises, rheumatic pain, rashes, chickenpox, ear and eye infections, and nasal inflammation3. Treatment of respiratory problems, liver diseases, neuropsychiatric problems, common colds, and gastrointestinal disorders are among the traditional applications of M. chamomilla4. Along with treating pain and infections, this plant is commonly used to treat conditions of the skin, eyes, and mouth5. Additionally, it is used in herbal teas, cough medicines, and cosmetics6. The chemical components of chamomile include flavonoids, camphor, camphene, pinene, 1,8-cineole, and bisabolol oxide7. The volatile oil of German chamomile is frequently used in cosmetic treatments to treat skin irritation, reduce redness, and eradicate inflammation. The antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant properties of this oil have been used to heal skin lesions8.

Salinity stress induces a cascade of physiological and biochemical responses in plants, which can significantly alter the synthesis and accumulation of primary and secondary metabolites9. Primary metabolites, such as carbohydrates, amino acids, and organic acids, are directly involved in plant growth and development, while secondary metabolites, such as flavonoids, terpenoids, and alkaloids, play crucial roles in plant defense mechanisms and have significant pharmacological activities. The biosynthesis and accumulation of these metabolites are tightly regulated by environmental factors, including salinity stress. Therefore, understanding the impact of salinity stress on the phytochemical composition of medicinal plants like chamomile is essential for optimizing their cultivation and enhancing their medicinal properties under stressful environmental conditions.

Research into the effects of salinity on M. chamomilla not only provides insights into the plant's adaptive mechanisms but also reveals potential changes in its medicinal properties. Increased salinity may alter the production and concentration of key bioactive compounds such as flavonoids and terpenoids, which are essential for the herb's therapeutic effects10. By examining these changes, we can assess whether the plant's medicinal value is enhanced or diminished under saline conditions. This combined approach—analyzing both the physiological responses and the medicinal properties of M. chamomilla—offers a comprehensive understanding of how salinity impacts this valuable herb, potentially guiding its cultivation in saline-prone areas and maximizing its therapeutic benefits.

The primary objective of this research was to understand how different concentrations of sodium chloride (NaCl) affect the growth and development of Matricaria chamomilla. By examining morphological parameters such as plant height, number of branches, number of flowers, and dry weight of plants and flowers, this study aims to provide insights into the adaptive mechanisms of chamomile under salinity stress. Additionally, the study sought to analyze the phytochemical changes induced by salinity stress and evaluate the antimicrobial activity of chamomile extracts against common bacterial strains. Our research is particularly novel as it integrates a comprehensive analysis of salinity-induced stress responses in M. chamomilla, incorporating advanced techniques such as Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, thin layer chromatography (TLC), and UV–Vis spectrophotometry. Additionally, we examine the antibacterial properties and antioxidant activities of methanolic extracts from plants exposed to different salinity levels. The detailed morphological and phytochemical assessments, combined with robust statistical analysis, provide new insights into the adaptive mechanisms of M. chamomilla under saline conditions. By addressing the gap in understanding the salinity tolerance of this valuable medicinal plant, our findings could inform strategies for its cultivation in saline-prone areas, thus enhancing its medicinal value and agricultural viability.

Material and methods

All the research work and activities were being conducted at the Department of Biological Sciences, International Islamic University Islamabad. This research involves the following experimental setup that was conducted accordingly in a sequential manner greenhouse maintenance, pot preparation, sowing, plant growth, salinity stress, extract preparation, phytochemical analysis, biological assays, and characterization of plant extract. A pot experiment based on a randomized complete block design was carried out in greenhouse conditions with 4 replicates of all concentrations including the control group. Baby plants of M. chamomilla were brought from its native area, Murree in Pakistan which was 3 weeks old and were sown 1 mm deep in pots containing sand. The collected plant samples underwent meticulous processing, which included shade-drying, pressing, cataloging, and identification by Dr. Nyla Jabeen from the Department of Biological Sciences at the International Islamic University, Islamabad. Each herbarium entry was carefully labeled and archived for future reference. Comprehensive records were diligently maintained, detailing everything from the botanical name to the voucher specimen number IIU/ISB/568345, along with growth characteristics and medicinal properties. These records are kept in the Botanical Garden for public access. Our experiment adheres to the relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines, with all necessary legislation, permissions, and licenses obtained.

After settling and adapting to this new habitat in Islamabad within two weeks, the plant had started to grow and increase its length, number of leaves, and flowering showing a sign of its survival that was introduced to new habitat. pH of sand was measured with an interval of 2 weeks throughout the research. Two weeks after transplanting, intruders were irrigated with saline water with different salt concentrations including control (0 mM), 1 mM, 100 mM, and 150 mM. To prevent shock to the plants in a new habitat, irrigation started with 1 mM salt concentration and was increased up to 150 mM. These different salt concentrations were made in the laboratory at IIUI. Plant height, plants and flowers dry weight, number of branches, and number of flowers were measured. Flowers were air-dried in shadow at room temperature. Methanol was used for making plant extraction.

Greenhouse experiment

All overgrown weeds and useless herbs were cleared off with the help of a gardener. Usable materials, such as pots and water cans, were set aside. Twelve pots were cleaned, washed, and kept in sunlight for an hour. After that, the greenhouse was reserved for the project, and the pots were aligned there. The pots were filled with fertile soil, and clumps were removed. Water was poured into each pot to homogenize the soil. Twelve pots were selected for sowing, with one plant per pot. The plants were of a Pakistani breed and were brought from the village of Murree in October. After being planted in pots, the soil was provided with an adequate amount of water to ensure effective growth. This procedure consisted of three parameters: loamy and fertile sand, tap water, and different concentrations of NaCl solution in triplicate.

Morphological analysis

The pH of the soil was measured with the help of a pH meter, recording a pH of 5 on the first day of planting and 3 on the last day of planting. Chamomile is capable of thriving in any summer weather under 100°F, preferring temperatures in the 60 to 68°F range, which were present during our investigation. Since chamomile grows best in cooler climates, it was planted in partial shade, although it can also grow in direct sunlight. The growth parameters were observed at regular intervals and recorded weekly (week #1, week #2, and week #3) from the first day when the plants were settled in the pots. Morphological properties, such as plant height, number of flowers, number of new buds, number of branches, and plant abundance, were also observed. These features were observable to the naked eye. Plant height was measured with a ruler scale in centimeters. Plant abundance was recorded by counting the number of erect stems. The number of buds, branches, and flowers were counted manually from week #1 to week #3. The height of the plants was measured before and after exposure to stress.

A significant environmental stressor that has an impact on plant growth and development is salt stress. Plants must have strong defenses against high-salt situations since they are sessile. Salt stress can lead to a hazardous buildup of sodium and an increase in intracellular osmotic pressure. So different NaCl concentrations were made i.e. 1 mM, 100 mM, and 150 mM in the laboratory. Plant powder material and extract preparation

After the time interval of four weeks, the whole plant of M. chamomilla was collected from all the pots that were grown in green house. The plants were washed properly with distilled water. After washing, these plants were shade-dried which took about 11 days. They were labeled and placed separately according to the concentration i.e. 0 mM, 1 mM, 100 mM, and 150 mM. On the 11th day, the dried plants were ground and crushed well using a mortar and pestle. Then the powder form of these stressed plants that were grown in the green greenhouse was further used for making plant extract using methanol. Plant extract of fresh whole plant of M. chamomilla was then used for phytochemical analysis, characterization and to perform some biological assays. The given M. chamomilla powder of different salt concentrations was weighed and transferred to a sterile beaker. 50 ml of methanol was added to the beaker and mixed well with the shaker for 24 h. Then it was filtered through What Mann No. 1 filter paper. The capacity of methanol to effectively extract a broad spectrum of phytochemical components from plant material is the basis for its application in the field of medication development and agricultural research that’s why methanol was used for plant extraction.

Phytochemical tests

Test for flavonoids

Ten milliliters of distilled water and 0.5 ml of the chosen plant extract were added to a test tube. A portion of the aqueous filtrate was mixed with 5 ml of diluted ammonia solution, which was then combined with concentrated H2SO4. The presence of a yellow color indicated a positive test result11.

Test for saponins

A small quantity of the methanolic extract of M. chamomilla was shaken with 2 ml of water. The formation of foam after 10 min indicated a positive result12.

Test for phlobatannins

Distilled water was mixed with a sample of plant powder in a test tube to obtain the plant extract. Then, 1% aqueous HCl was added to the plant extract, which was heated using a hot plate stirrer. The appearance of red-colored precipitates confirmed a positive result13.

Test for steroids

One milliliter of the methanolic plant extract of M. chamomilla was placed in a test tube, and 5 drops of H2SO4 were added. The mixture was shaken well, and the formation of a reddish-brown color indicated the presence of steroids14.

Test for glycosides

One milliliter of M. chamomilla plant extract was added to a test tube along with 1 ml of sodium nitroprusside solution and NaOH drops. The mixture was shaken gently, and the appearance of pink to blood-red color or precipitates indicated the presence of glycosides15.

Antibacterial activity

Antimicrobial activity was evaluated by the disc diffusion method. Three strains Bacillus cereus, Staphylococcus aureus, and E. coli were used. For bacteria culturing, nutrient agar media was used. 2.8 g nutrient agar was developed in 100 ml distilled water and autoclaved it for 15 min at 121 °C and 15lbs. The media was poured into the autoclaved plates and rested to solidify it at room temperature under a sterile laminar flow cabinet. Then, the plates were placed in an incubator at 37 °C for 24 h.

Susceptibility testing

The disc diffusion method was used to assess microbial sensitivity by the method to evaluate the existence of antibacterial activity in plant extracts. The bacterial strains were swabbed on agar media with sterile cotton buds from cultured plates. 5 mm discs of sterile Whatman filter paper were impregnated with 100 µl methanolic extracts for 2 h. The impregnated discs were placed on cultured agar medium at labeled points and allowed for 1 h for proper diffusion of plant extracts before placing in an incubator at 37 °C for 24 h. The diffused agents in the media inhibited the test microbes and a zone of inhibition appeared around the discs. The zone of inhibition was measured in mm.

Fluorescence recovery after photobleaching (FRAP assay)

After adjusting the water bath's temperature to 50 °C, different dilutions of 100 mg/ml of each plant extract were combined with 2.5 ml of potassium ferrocyanide and 2.5 ml of phosphate buffer. They were then covered and incubated in the water bath for 20 min at that temperature. After adding 2.5 ml of trichloroacetic acid, centrifuging the mixture for 10 min at 3000 rpm while vortexing thoroughly. The upper layer was transferred to a different falcon tube and combined with 0.5 ml of ferric chloride and 2.5 ml of distilled water. The solution turned blue, and ascorbic acid and a blank solution were used to measure the absorbance at 700 nm. The following chemicals were used during this assay. These were sodium phosphate 0.028 M, ammonium molybdate 0.004 M, and sulphuric acid 0.6 M. These chemicals were mixed in equal proportion to make a phosphomolybdate reagent.

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR)

To determine the effect of NaCl salt of different concentrations, FTIR analysis was conducted to observe changes after an 8-week abiotic stress period in a green greenhouse (Fig. 1). The FTIR instrument parameters were as follows: the infrared light transmitted ranged from 10,000 to 100 cm−1. The spectrum collected covered a typical range of 4000 cm−1 to 400 cm−1, with a resolution set at 4 cm−1. To enhance the signal-to-noise ratio, an average of 32 scans was utilized. Both mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) and deuterated triglycine sulfate (DTGS) detectors were employed for the analysis. The specific spectral fingerprints obtained allowed for the classification and characterization of various chemicals, aiding in the identification and description of changes in the samples due to NaCl stress.Fig. 1Morphological features of the plant after stress i.e. some flowers become dried, new branches emerged.

Thin layer chromatography (TLC)

By dissolving 1 mg of each fraction produced from column chromatography in 1 mL of methanol, a stock solution with a concentration of 1000 g/mL was created. 60 F254 TLC plates with silica coating (20 cm) were obtained and sliced (6.5 cm 3.8 cm)17. Using a graphite pencil, the cut TLC plate was marked 1 cm above the bottom. On the specified line at one end of the plate, all test fractions were loaded evenly spaced apart and left to dry. With a micropipette, an identical volume of fraction (0.5 L) was loaded. A mobile phase with the necessary solvent composition was produced, placed into the TLC tank, and covered to achieve uniform vapor saturation. The spotted plate was put in the TLC tank after 20 min. After that, the tank was covered with a lid while we waited for the TLC plate to grow. The plate was taken off when the mobile phase was barely 1–2 mm below the higher end. The solvent front was marked and then dried by air. Without more effort, a color spot was quickly visualized and marked with a graphite pencil. Two visualization techniques were utilized for colorless spots. First, a TLC plate was examined under UV light (254 nm), and the UV active spots were outlined with a graphite pencil. The TLC plate was then stained with phosphomolybdic acid in absolute ethanol (10% w/v) and heated until colorless spots were visible. The Rf value for that compound is the proportion of mobile phase to spot travel distance. UV–Vis analysis

For this analysis, a Shimadzu 2700 spectrophotometer was used, covering the wavelength range of 190 to 900 nm. The instrument parameters included a slit width of 2 nm for UV and 1 nm for the visible range, with a scan speed of 400 nm/min. The photometric accuracy was maintained at ± 0.002 Abs, ensuring precise measurements.

Discussion

The results obtained from various assays and analyses of Matricaria chamomilla under varying NaCl concentrations provide a comprehensive overview of how salinity stress influences both morphological and biochemical aspects of the plant. Morphological observations revealed significant changes in the plant's growth over the 8 weeks. Notably, exposure to higher salt concentrations resulted in stunted growth, reduced flower production, and the emergence of dried flowers and new branches. These findings indicate that salinity stress adversely affects plant development and productivity, aligning with existing literature on the impact of salt stress.

on plant morphology. It modifies its internal structure to deal with the salt stress, possibly synthesizing alternative pigments or secondary metabolites, or going through structural changes18. These alterations in morphological and physiological characteristics provide fascinating insights into the plant's coping mechanisms and its exceptional capacity to flourish in difficult situations19.

Phytochemical screening identified several bioactive compounds in the methanolic extract of M. chamomilla. The presence of flavonoids, saponins, and steroids was consistent across different NaCl concentrations, though variations were noted in the presence of phlobatanins and glycosides. This suggests that while some phytochemicals remain stable under varying salinity conditions, others may be affected by salt stress, influencing their potential therapeutic properties. The FTIR analysis of M. chamomilla under varying NaCl stress concentrations revealed fascinating insights into the plant’s adaptive responses. Conclusion

The study of Matricaria chamomilla under varying NaCl concentrations reveals profound insights into the plant's adaptive mechanisms to salinity stress. The significant morphological changes, such as stunted growth and reduced flower production, alongside biochemical alterations, underscore the plant's ability to modify its internal structure and composition in response to adverse conditions. The consistent presence of certain bioactive compounds and the variability in others highlight the complex interplay between salinity and phytochemical stability. Furthermore, the spectroscopic analyses and antibacterial efficacy data suggest that moderate salt stress may enhance certain therapeutic properties, presenting new possibilities for the medicinal use of M. chamomilla. The observed reduction in detectable compounds at higher salinity levels indicates the potential loss of valuable phytochemicals under extreme stress, emphasizing the importance of understanding environmental impacts on plant biochemistry. This comprehensive analysis not only enhances our understanding of M. chamomilla's resilience but also paves the way for future research into optimizing the cultivation and utilization of this valuable medicinal plant under varying environmental conditions.
