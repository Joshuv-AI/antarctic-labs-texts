Perioperative Setting in Emergency Caesarean Section for a Pregnant Woman With Coronavirus Disease 2019: A First Case Report
Authors: Kiichiro Furuya, Rikuto Hirose, Michael Shannon Hara, Saya Yamashita, Yangsil Chang, Kayoko Shikado, Hiroaki Tsubouchi, Kazuhide Ogita
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEmergency caesarean section (ECS), also referred to as a “category 1 caesarean section,” is less commonly performed but may become unexpectedly necessary in critical obstetric emergencies. During a trial of labour in high‐risk pregnancies, the potential need for ECS should always be anticipated. Even during the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) pandemic, trials of labour in pregnant women with COVID‐19 have remained common. While preoperative management protocols for ECS are essential as contingency plans, well‐defined strategies for the safe and timely preparation of ECS in pregnant women with COVID‐19 remain lacking. We present the case of a 35‐year‐old primiparous woman at term gestation who was diagnosed with mild COVID‐19 during induction of labour. During cervical ripening, severe foetal distress was detected, prompting an ECS due to suspected abruptio placentae and/or uterine rupture. Institutional preoperative management strategies for emergency surgery in patients with COVID‐19, including infection control measures, prepackaged surgical supplies, simulation training, and established ECS protocols, enabled the medical team to promptly arrange the procedure. The infant was delivered in good health within 30 min of the decision to proceed with ECS and tested negative for infection. By integrating obstetric ECS protocols with preoperative management strategies for emergency surgery in patients with COVID‐19, such cases can be successfully managed. This case report describes the preoperative preparation for ECS in a pregnant woman with COVID‐19, despite limited clinical guidance and stringent infection control requirements. Our experience may contribute to the development of rapid and safe ECS preparation protocols for pregnant patients requiring isolation during future infectious disease emergencies.

Introduction

Emergency cesarean section (ECS), also referred to as a “category 1 caesarean section,” is rare but may be required unexpectedly in critical obstetric emergencies [1]. Obstetric emergencies necessitating ECS, such as abruptio placentae, uterine rupture, umbilical cord prolapse, and life‐threatening maternal or foetal deterioration, should always be anticipated during labour trials in high‐risk pregnancies [1, 2, 3, 4].

Even during the COVID‐19 pandemic, labour trials in isolation became a common practice for pregnant women diagnosed with COVID‐19 [5, 6]. Thus, preoperative management protocols for ECS must be adapted to accommodate labour trials in COVID‐19‐positive pregnant women. However, no current guidelines provide detailed recommendations regarding the preoperative setting and preparation for ECS in this specific patient population. Herein, we report the preoperative preparation and procedural details of ECS during labour induction in a COVID‐19‐positive pregnant woman. In this case, with regular ECS training and previously established preoperative management protocols for emergency surgery, clinicians and medical staff could swiftly coordinate the procedure, and unanticipated ECS was successfully performed in a COVID‐19‐positive patient.

Case History/Examination

A 35‐year‐old primiparous woman with a medical history of vulvar herpes was admitted for induction of labour at 39 weeks of gestation due to termination of pregnancy before a herpes flare‐up. There was no finding of abnormal foetal growth and/or development. Cervical dilation using a metreurynter was performed after confirming that the patient was asymptomatic and tested negative for an antigen test 1 week before admission. However, during cervical dilation, she developed a sore throat and was subsequently diagnosed with COVID‐19. She had not yet been vaccinated against SARS‐CoV‐2. Although the obstetric team initially intended to suspend labour induction until the isolation period ended, the procedure had to continue due to the onset of hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and gestational hypertension. Since cervical ripening with the metreurynter did not achieve sufficient cervical ripening (Bishop score: 2) to initiate oxytocin administration, a dinoprostone vaginal insert (PROPESS) was placed in the isolated labour and delivery room (LDR) after the confirmation of reassuring foetal status (i.e., baseline, 140 bpm; acceleration, presence; and deceleration, absence) by cardiotocogram (CTG) monitoring for 30 min.

Forty‐five minutes later, a prolonged foetal heart rate deceleration was detected, with the nadir reaching 60 bpm and persisting for approximately 15 min. Despite prompt removal of the PROPESS insert and intrauterine fetal resuscitation, foetal status did not improve.

Obstetricians carefully evaluated the progress of delivery and the possibility of abruptio placentae and/or uterine rupture. Since the uterine cervical dilation was not fully opened (Bishop score: 3) and the foetal station did not reach the vacuum delivery‐available level, obstetricians ultimately proceeded with an ECS. No abnormal laboratory findings were observed, including those associated with severe anaemia or HELLP (haemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelet count) syndrome. The results were as follows: levels of haemoglobin (Hb) = 11.1 g/dL, aspartate aminotransferase = 23 IU/L, and alanine transaminase = 15 IU/L; and platelet count = 16.0 × 104/μL. Preparations for the procedure were initiated across multiple departments, including the obstetrics ward, operating theatre, and anaesthesiology team. The obstetric team immediately obtained informed consent and coordinated with hospital administrators to secure an isolated transfer route to the operating theatre, ensuring separation from general patients. Simultaneously, operating theatre staff prepared a COVID‐19‐designated surgical environment, including sealing storage shelves, arranging personal protective equipment (PPE), implementing zoning protocols, and setting up pre‐sterilised surgical instruments and disposable materials. These measures were executed according to our standardised protocol.

Once the operating theatre was fully prepared, the patient was immediately transferred from the isolated LDR to the operating theatre via the designated secure transfer route. CTG monitoring had to be discontinued during the patient's preoperative preparation and transfer. The midwife continuously assessed the foetal heart rate using a Doppler device placed on the patient's abdominal wall. Although the foetal heart rate temporarily recovered to 120–140 bpm during preparation, it intermittently decreased to 80–90 bpm in association with uterine contractions during transfer. All staff, including the obstetricians, considered that the abnormal foetal status was likely to persist and agreed that an ECS should be performed without delay. During maternal transfer, all staff and clinicians donned PPE and entered the red zone of the operating theatre, where they assisted in preparing surgical instruments until the patient's arrival. This multidisciplinary preoperative coordination facilitated a rapid decision‐to‐departure time of only 7 min. The medical staff involved in the red zone included three obstetricians, two anaesthesiologists, one neonatologist, three operating theatre nurses, and one midwife. An obstetrician, midwife, and operating nurse stationed outside the red zone assisted with infant transfer, provided additional surgical supplies, and facilitated communication outside the operating theatre.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Upon admission to the operating theatre, cefazolin was administered as preoperative prophylaxis. ECS was promptly performed under general anaesthesia using rapid sequence induction (RSI). The newborn weighed 3356 g, with Apgar scores of 8 and 9 at 1 and 5 min, respectively. Umbilical artery blood gas analysis indicated pH 7.071, base excess = −11.3, pO2 = 11.9 mmHg, and pCO2 = 71.4 mmHg. Despite the presence of moderately thick meconium in the amniotic fluid at delivery and abnormal umbilical artery blood gas results, the infant cried vigorously at birth and required no resuscitation. There was no evidence of vertical transmission. Since no macroscopic findings of abruptio placentae or uterine rupture were observed, tachysystole induced by PROPESS was considered the likely cause of severe foetal deterioration.

The total operative time was 72 min. The decision‐to‐delivery and incision‐to‐delivery times were 30 and 2 min, respectively. Our multidisciplinary preoperative arrangements enabled a rapid transfer to the operating theatre, facilitating the successful completion of ECS within 30 min of decision‐making, despite COVID‐19 isolation protocols. Although the total blood loss reached 1000 mL due to transient atonic bleeding following the third stage of labour, no blood transfusion was required owing to the prompt administration of uterotonic agents, including oxytocin, methylergometrine, and prostaglandin. After confirming COVID‐19‐negative status, the infant was transferred to the obstetric ward for routine postnatal monitoring and immediately evacuated from the red zone. This scheme also contributed to a more comfortable operative environment for all staff working in the red zone (operating theatre); the temperature in the theatre was allowed to be lowered after the infant had been transferred. Following extubation, the patient was transferred to an isolated ward, and early ambulation was initiated on postoperative day (POD) 1. Maternal isolation was lifted on POD 5. There were no severe postoperative complications, including anaemia (Hb levels of 10.1 and 10.3 g/dL at POD 1 and 4, respectively), and both the patient and neonate were discharged in good health on POD 8. Postoperative follow‐up was completed at the 1‐month maternal checkup, with no reported complications.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

This case report illustrates the successful implementation of a meticulously planned and well‐coordinated preoperative protocol for ECS in a pregnant woman with COVID‐19. By adapting our established workflow for emergency obstetric interventions and integrating measures specific to SARS‐CoV‐2 infection control, we achieved a decision‐to‐delivery interval within the recommended 30 min. Our key strategies included systematic zoning of operating theatre areas, prepackaged disposable surgical instrument sets, rigorous infection control protocols, and prompt multidisciplinary coordination. Our experience highlights the feasibility of maintaining high standards of patient safety and operative timeliness despite the logistical complexities posed by emerging infectious diseases. The insights gained from this case study can inform the refinement of obstetric emergency preparedness and serve as a framework for future pandemics or outbreaks, ultimately fostering better maternal and neonatal outcomes.

Discussion

This is the first report detailing a standardised perioperative approach for ECS in COVID‐19 pregnancies that occurred during the peak of the pandemic. Although many obstetric and gynaecologic patients were unable to access medical facilities due to strict movement restrictions [7, 8, 9], our institute, a tertiary care facility, continued to accept all patients requiring high‐quality procedures or treatment, without limitation, even during the pandemic. This included induction of labour for high‐risk pregnancies, in keeping with our clinical responsibilities. Our ECS preoperative protocol for COVID‐19 pregnancies was developed by modifying protocols for emergency surgery in patients with COVID‐19 and integrating refinements from our standard ECS workflow. Consequently, even during the pandemic, our protocol achieved the recommended decision‐to‐delivery interval (DDI) within 30 min for ECS, highlighting its effectiveness in ensuring rapid surgical intervention.

Details of Preoperative Preparations for Emergency Operation in Nonpregnant COVID‐19 Patients

Setting up a COVID‐19‐designated operating theatre requires considerable effort, posing challenges for surgical staff [10, 11]. However, no official guidelines and/or academic statements have detailed preoperative protocols for emergency surgeries in patients with COVID‐19, focusing primarily on preventing infection control and viral containment [10, 11, 12, 13].

Despite the lack of formal guidelines, several studies have highlighted key considerations for COVID‐19‐designated operating theatres: (1) minimising personnel and equipment in the red zone; (2) infection control measures including zoning, securing a dedicated transfer route from LDR to the operating theatre, maintaining a controlled surgical environment, sealing unused shelves and equipment, and equipping anaesthetic machines with HEPA filters; (3) comprehensive PPE preparation for all staff; (4) prepackaged disposable surgical instruments to streamline the preparation; (5) airborne precaution strategies for anaesthesia management; and (6) multidisciplinary preoperative briefings to determine the optimal anaesthesia approach, balancing infection control with surgical efficiency [14, 15].

As a designated infectious disease centre with experience in emergency surgeries during past SARS/MERS outbreaks, our hospital independently developed a preoperative protocol for emergency surgery in patients with COVID‐19 based on simulation training and expert guidance, as well as emerging recommendations [10, 11, 12, 13]. The COVID‐19‐designated operating theatre setup for emergency procedures is detailed in Table 1 and Figure 1, while the details of surgical item preparation are outlined in Table 2 and Figure 2. This preoperative protocol facilitated rapid preparation for emergency surgeries requiring strict isolation and served as the foundation for ECS preparation in COVID‐19 pregnancies. In cases of ECS for foetal rescue, the decision‐to‐delivery interval is generally expected to be within 30 min. In this case, successful ECS within this timeframe was achieved, even under COVID‐19 conditions, through multidisciplinary support and adherence to preestablished protocols. As a result, the infant was delivered in good condition without the need for resuscitation, despite transient acidosis on umbilical artery blood gas analysis at birth.

Preoperative Considerations for ECS in COVID‐19 Pregnant Women

In this case, prolonged severe foetal heart rate deceleration during labour induction prompted obstetricians to perform a category 1 ECS. Given the suspected risk of abruptio placentae or uterine rupture, both life‐threatening obstetric emergencies, achieving a DDI within 30 min was imperative.

Successful ECS within 30 min depends on several factors: (1) institutional capability to handle obstetric emergencies; (2) operating theatre readiness and immediate availability; (3) regular simulation training to enhance response efficiency; (4) multidisciplinary coordination and real‐time communication; and (5) minimising incision‐to‐delivery time to improve neonatal outcomes and prevent anaesthesia‐induced neonatal depression (“sleeping baby”) [2, 3, 4].

Since our hospital routinely conducts simulation‐based ECS training, a system was established before the pandemic to ensure optimised decision‐making and streamlined execution. Key protocols, including securing transfer routes, preprepared surgical items, and multidisciplinary communication, were already well integrated.

During the COVID‐19 pandemic, our hospital remained responsible for managing high‐risk pregnancies, necessitating the immediate development of a COVID‐19‐specific ECS protocol as part of emergency obstetric preparedness. However, implementing ECS under COVID‐19 protocols posed a unique challenge due to limited existing evidence and the dual demand for rapid DDI and strict infection control measures.

Due to limited evidence and guidelines for ECS setup in patients with COVID‐19, we developed an ECS‐specific protocol by refining and adapting perioperative management strategies for emergency surgery in nonpregnant patients with COVID‐19, alongside our existing ECS workflow. Notably, even in the complex context of the COVID‐19 pandemic, our protocol consistently achieved the recommended DDI within 30 min, highlighting its effectiveness in ensuring rapid surgical intervention. Additionally, a comprehensive infection control framework, including zoning, PPE donning, secure patient transfer routes, and red‐zone operating room protocols, was streamlined and enhanced through prior experience with nonpregnant COVID‐19 surgeries.

Anaesthesia selection for ECS in COVID‐19 pregnant women is particularly crucial for both patient and staff safety. Although obstetric and anaesthesiologic societies recommend rapid spinal induction as the preferred approach for emergency operation in COVID‐19 [15, 16, 17], this approach is not always feasible in cases where a trained anaesthesiologist is unavailable, the patient has obesity‐related anatomical challenges, or the situation is extremely urgent. Thus, these recommendations also highlight considerations for the use of general anaesthesia in ECS in patients with COVID‐19. Several case series involving caesarean sections in pregnant women with COVID‐19 have reported that general anaesthesia can be safely administered as a clinical option in situations such as maternal and/or foetal emergencies, maternal dyspnoea, contraindications to epidural procedures (e.g., abnormal coagulation or thrombocytopenia), or failed epidural anaesthesia [18, 19].

Based on these recommendations, reports, and multidisciplinary discussions, we opted for general anaesthesia with RSI in ECS for COVID‐19 pregnancies, ensuring simplified and rapid decision‐making, even when spinal induction is difficult or unfeasible. The aim of this case report was to present our scheme for preoperative preparation of ECS in a pregnant woman with COVID‐19. This scheme was developed on the basis of limited evidence available during the pandemic and is intended to provide practical insights for managing future pandemics involving emerging infectious diseases. There are some limitations of this case report: (1) the scheme may not be applicable to all healthcare facilities, as it was designed not only based on the limited evidence available during the COVID‐19 pandemic but also in consideration of the clinical resources and capabilities specific to our hospital; and (2) the findings are subject to bias inherent in single‐institution, case report‐based data. Nevertheless, even in 2025, few confirmed guidelines or evidence has been published regarding preoperative preparation for ECS in pregnant women with COVID‐19. Therefore, our scheme may contribute to perinatal management in high‐risk pregnancies during future pandemics.
