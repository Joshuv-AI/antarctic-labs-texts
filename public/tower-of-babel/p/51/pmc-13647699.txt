Efficacy of opioid-free versus opioid-inclusive anesthesia on postoperative nausea, vomiting, and quality of recovery in patients undergoing video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Shosaburo Jotaki, Norio Yamamoto, Eriya Imai, Yudai Goto, Takahiro Tamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) is common after video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). Opioid-free anesthesia (OFA) may reduce PONV; however, procedure-specific evidence remains limited. This review evaluated the effects of OFA versus opioid-inclusive anesthesia (OIA) on PONV, pain, and recovery after VATS lung resection.MethodsRandomized controlled trials (RCTs) involving adults who underwent VATS lung resection and compared OFA, defined as no intraoperative opioid use, with OIA, were included. MEDLINE, Embase, and Cochrane Central were searched until January 18, 2026, and ClinicalTrials.gov and the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform were searched on January 19, 2026. The risk of bias was assessed using the RoB 2 tool. Random-effects meta-analyses were performed, and the certainty of evidence was evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation framework. The primary outcomes were PONV and pain at 24 h.ResultsEleven RCTs involving 1183 participants were included, all of which used regional anesthesia. Most studies were conducted in China (n = 8), with one study each conducted in Poland, Turkey, and South Korea. Across the included studies, the mean age of participants ranged from 44.6 to 67.8 years. OFA was associated with reduced PONV within 24 h compared with OIA (eight studies; risk ratio, 0.58; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.41–0.81; moderate certainty). Pain scores at 24 h were lower with OFA (eleven studies; mean difference, − 0.30; 95% CI, − 0.54 to − 0.07), although the difference did not reach the minimally important difference. OFA improved the quality of recovery (QoR) at 24 h (three studies; standardized mean difference, 0.40; 95% CI, 0.07 to 0.72; moderate certainty). No meaningful difference was observed in postoperative opioid consumption, and adverse event findings were inconsistent.ConclusionsIn selected patients undergoing VATS lung resection within multimodal pathways including regional analgesia, OFA protocols probably reduce PONV and improve early QoR compared with heterogeneous OIA regimens, while having little or no clinically important effect on acute postoperative pain. Background

Video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS) is increasingly replacing open thoracotomy because it is associated with less postoperative pain, fewer respiratory complications, and faster recovery [1, 2]. Despite these advantages, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV) remains common after VATS [3], with a reported incidence of approximately 30–40% [4, 5], and may be higher than that observed after other common surgical procedures [6]. Notably, PONV is a major determinant of patient satisfaction and early quality of recovery (QoR) [7], making its prevention an important target for perioperative management. Opioid-free anesthesia (OFA), defined as the avoidance of intraoperative opioids using a multimodal non-opioid approach, has been proposed to reduce opioid-related adverse effects, including PONV [8–10]. Previous systematic reviews of various surgical populations have suggested that OFA reduces PONV and improves early recovery outcomes [11–15].

However, it remains unclear whether these benefits extend to VATS. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in patients undergoing VATS have reported inconsistent effects of OFA on PONV, with some showing a reduction [3, 4, 16, 17] and others showing no notable difference [18, 19]. Notably, its effects on postoperative analgesia are similarly mixed [4, 16, 18–20]. Existing meta-analyses that include VATS within broader surgical populations have several limitations that hinder clinical interpretation. First, they often did not clearly distinguish OFA—defined as intraoperative opioid avoidance—from opioid-free analgesia, which also prohibits postoperative opioids and may further reduce PONV, leading to the inclusion of such studies [11, 13]. Second, they included heterogeneous procedures, such as bariatric surgery, in which OFA may have a larger effect [21]. Third, outcome definitions and assessment time points were not standardized across studies [11, 13, 15]. Additionally, prior reviews did not isolate VATS; one combined VATS with open thoracotomy and relied mainly on observational data [22], whereas another included thoracoscopic procedures only as a subgroup within mixed surgical populations [11]. Consequently, the antiemetic effect attributable to intraoperative opioid avoidance alone in patients undergoing VATS lung resection remains poorly understood.

To address this uncertainty, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs comparing OFA with opioid-inclusive anesthesia (OIA) in adult patients who underwent VATS lung resection. We focused on VATS lung resection and strictly defined OFA as intraoperative opioid avoidance to provide a more clinically specific estimate, with outcomes standardized at 24 h. We hypothesized that OFA reduces PONV without compromising analgesia.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [23]. The PRISMA checklist is presented in Table S1. The study protocol was registered prospectively with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD420261292925) and Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/6w5sd/overview).

Participants and eligibility criteria

RCTs involving adult patients undergoing VATS, including wedge resection, segmentectomy, or lobectomy, were included. Patients with pre-existing chronic pain or those who used opioids preoperatively were excluded. No language restrictions were imposed. Conference abstracts and letters were screened; however, only full-text articles with sufficient data were included in the final analysis.

Interventions and exclusion criteria

The intervention group received OFA, defined as the complete avoidance of opioids from induction to emergence, while allowing postoperative opioid use, such as intravenous patient-controlled analgesia, patient-controlled epidural analgesia, or rescue opioids [24]. The comparator group received OIA, defined as intraoperative opioid administration during anesthesia induction and/or maintenance, in accordance with recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses [12, 25], regardless of opioid dose or whether the original authors described the regimen as opioid-sparing anesthesia (OSA). This definition was intentionally inclusive to capture the full spectrum of clinical practice in the comparator group and was consistent with the pre-registered protocol (PROSPERO: CRD420261292925). The exclusion of OSA in the registered protocol applied exclusively to OFA to ensure that studies evaluating partial opioid reduction, rather than complete opioid avoidance, were not misclassified as OFA. OFA was distinguished from opioid-free analgesia, which prohibits opioid use throughout the perioperative period [24], and studies evaluating opioid-free analgesia were excluded from the review. We considered the inclusion of comparator arms involving low-dose intraoperative opioid administration, including those described as opioid-sparing anesthesia, to represent a protocol interpretation rather than a protocol deviation. In the registered protocol, OFA was defined as the complete avoidance of opioids from induction of anesthesia until full emergence, and opioid-sparing anesthesia was distinguished from OFA to avoid misclassifying partial opioid-reduction interventions as opioid-free anesthesia. In contrast, the comparator was defined as intraoperative opioid administration, without restriction by opioid dose, and concomitant non-opioid agents and regional anesthesia were permitted. Therefore, trials were eligible when the intervention arm met the definition of OFA and the comparator arm involved any intraoperative opioid administration. Nevertheless, because such comparator arms may not represent conventional opioid-based anesthesia, we conducted post hoc sensitivity analyses excluding studies in which the comparator was described as opioid-sparing.

Outcome types

The primary outcomes were PONV within 24 h postoperatively and postoperative pain at 24 h [26]. The definition of PONV included the presence of one or more of the following: nausea, retching, vomiting, or the use of rescue antiemetics. Pain was assessed using the numerical rating scale (NRS) or visual analog scale (VAS) [26]. To avoid double counting, only one pain measurement per study was included. When rest and movement pain were reported at the same time point, movement pain was prioritized [22, 27].

The secondary outcomes included PONV within 48 h, postoperative pain at 48 h, chronic postoperative pain, QoR at 24 h, cumulative opioid consumption within 24 h, and all reported adverse events except for PONV. Chronic postoperative pain was defined as pain persisting for ≥ 3 months with an NRS score ≥ 4 or VAS score > 40 [28]. QoR was assessed using the QoR-40 or QoR-15 [29]. Cumulative opioid consumption was converted to oral morphine equivalents (OME) [30].

Search strategy

A systematic search was performed on January 18, 2026, using MEDLINE via PubMed, Embase via Dialog, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials. To identify ongoing or unpublished studies, ClinicalTrials.gov and the World Health Organization International Clinical Trials Registry Platform were searched on January 19, 2026, and corresponding authors were contacted when necessary. We further manually checked the citations of relevant papers [27, 31]. The search string was constructed using a combination of keywords and MeSH terms associated with thoracoscopic lung resection, OFA, and RCTs. The complete search strategy is presented in Table S2.

Study selection

After duplicate records were removed, two of the three reviewers (SJ, YG, and TT) independently screened the titles and abstracts in duplicate, followed by a full-text eligibility assessment. Missing data were requested from the original authors. Any conflicts were mediated by a third reviewer (NY or EI) if discussion failed.

Data extraction

Two of the three reviewers (SJ, YG, and TT) independently extracted data in duplicate using a standardized and piloted data collection form. The extracted variables included study characteristics, patient demographics, surgical procedures, anesthesia details, and postoperative analgesia protocols, including regional techniques such as epidural anesthesia, paravertebral block, and serratus anterior plane block. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or by consultation with a third reviewer (NY or EI).

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias was evaluated independently by two of the three reviewers (SJ, YG, and TT) using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool [32]. This tool evaluates five domains: randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Each outcome was assigned an overall risk-of-bias judgment of low risk, some concerns, or high risk. Differences in assessment were resolved by consensus or, when necessary, by a third reviewer (NY or EI). Risk-of-bias plots were generated using the robvis web application [33].

Measurement of treatment effects

Treatment effects were measured according to the outcome type. Risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for dichotomous outcomes. For continuous outcomes, mean differences (MDs) with 95% CIs were used when the same scale was applied, whereas standardized mean differences (SMDs) were used when different scales were reported across studies.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the meta package. For studies reporting medians and interquartile ranges, data were transformed into means and standard deviations using validated formulas [34]. Data presented only in graphical form were extracted using WebPlotDigitizer (https://automeris.io/WebPlotDigitizer).

For each planned synthesis, study characteristics, including OFA protocol composition, comparator type, regional anesthesia use, outcome definitions, and assessment time points, were tabulated (Table 1 and Table S3) and compared with the prespecified population, intervention, comparator, and outcome criteria. Studies were included in each synthesis only when the reported outcome definition and assessment time point aligned with the protocol definition, such as PONV within 24 h or pain at 24 h. For PONV outcomes, when multiple time points were reported within a predefined period (e.g., 24 h), the highest reported incidence was used to provide a conservative estimate.

Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and visual inspection of forest plots [40]. A random-effects model was used for all analyses because clinical and methodological heterogeneity was expected [41]. Between-study variance was estimated using the restricted maximum likelihood method. To account for uncertainty in heterogeneity estimation and avoid overly narrow CIs when few studies were available, CIs for pooled effect estimates were derived using the Hartung–Knapp–Sidik–Jonkman adjustment [42]. Missing data were requested from the study authors. Intention-to-treat principles were applied when possible, and no imputation was performed for continuous outcomes [41].

Subgroup analyses

Predefined subgroup analyses were conducted when sufficient data were available. These analyses were based on (1) the extent of surgery, categorized as predominantly lobectomy (> 50% of the study population) versus predominantly wedge resection or segmentectomy (> 50% of the study population) [43], and (2) the maintenance anesthesia technique, categorized as total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) versus volatile anesthesia [7].

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses for the primary outcomes included the exclusion of studies at high risk of bias and restriction to studies reporting pain during movement or when coughing.

Post hocexploratory meta-analysis

As post hoc exploratory measures of functional recovery, we evaluated length of hospital stay (LOS) and post-anesthesia care unit (PACU) stay duration. These analyses were not prespecified in the original protocol but were conducted in response to reviewer requests to provide additional context regarding functional recovery. Because LOS and PACU stay duration were added post hoc as exploratory functional recovery outcomes, they were not included in the prespecified Summary of Findings table. Their certainty of evidence was assessed separately and reported narratively in the Results section.

Publication bias assessment

To assess potential publication bias, funnel plots and Egger’s test were employed when at least 10 studies were included in the meta-analysis [44]. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Certainty of evidence

Two reviewers (SJ and EI) independently assessed the certainty of evidence using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach [45]. Disagreements were resolved by discussion or adjudication. The evaluated outcomes were PONV and pain at 24 and 48 h, QoR at 24 h, cumulative opioid consumption at 24 h, and adverse events. Imprecision was assessed based on absolute risk differences and prespecified thresholds for clinical importance.

For dichotomous outcomes, thresholds were defined a priori using an absolute risk reduction approach, informed by the GRADE-THRESHOLD framework and clinical judgment [46]. Because no established minimally important difference (MID) exists for PONV, absolute risk reduction thresholds were prespecified based on an estimated 26% relative risk reduction per antiemetic intervention reported by Apfel et al. [47] and a baseline risk of approximately 25% in the control groups. Absolute risk reductions of < 5%, 5–10%, 10–15%, and > 15% were considered trivial, small, moderate, and large, respectively. These thresholds should be interpreted as decision thresholds for clinical importance rather than as formally validated MIDs.

For continuous outcomes, imprecision was evaluated using outcome-specific MIDs. A difference of 1.3 points on the 0–10 NRS was used for pain [48]. For QoR, SMDs of 0.2–0.5, 0.5–0.8, and > 0.8 were considered small, moderate, and large, respectively [49]. The MID for OME was set to 10 mg [50]. Given the absence of formally validated MIDs for LOS and PACU stay duration in the VATS setting, thresholds of 1 day and 60 min, respectively, were adopted on the basis of clinical judgment. CIs were interpreted in relation to these thresholds to determine whether they included trivial, small, or clinically important effects.

Protocol deviation

Several prespecified subgroup analyses were not performed. Sex-based analysis was not possible because none of the included studies reported sex-stratified outcomes. Analysis by regional anesthesia use was also not feasible because all 11 studies included some form of regional anesthesia, preventing meaningful comparison. In addition, subgroup analysis of PONV at 24 h by surgical extent was not performed because six of the seven contributing studies predominantly involved non-lobectomy populations, defined as lobectomy in < 50% of participants, limiting balanced comparison.

Some prespecified sensitivity analyses were also not conducted. Exclusion of studies using prophylactic antiemetics was not possible because prophylactic antiemetics were administered in 7 of the 8 studies evaluating PONV within 24 h. Therefore, this prespecified sensitivity analysis was not conducted for either primary outcome. Excluding studies that allowed postoperative opioid administration had been prespecified; however, this would have retained only opioid-free analgesia studies, which had already been excluded during screening.

Post hoc sensitivity analyses that were not prespecified in the protocol were conducted to assess robustness. These included excluding studies in which the control group was defined as OSA and studies in which regional anesthesia was applied only in the OFA group.

Results

Search results

The systematic search of the databases and registries described above initially identified 3,224 records. After the removal of 922 duplicates, 2,302 unique records were screened by title and abstract, of which 2,269 were excluded. Of the remaining 33 reports, 12 could not be assessed in full text: six were ongoing trials without reported results, five were deemed ineligible based on registry information, and one trial had not yet begun recruitment. For one trial registered as completed but unpublished, the principal investigator was contacted but did not respond. The remaining 21 reports underwent full-text eligibility assessment, and 10 were excluded with reasons (Table S4). Ultimately, 11 studies were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1) [3, 4, 17–20, 35–39].

PRISMA flow diagram of study screening and selection. CENTRAL, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials; ICTRP, International Clinical Trials Registry Platform; RCTs, randomized controlled trials

Characteristics of included studies

All 11 included studies were RCTs, comprising a total of 1,183 participants. The sample sizes ranged from 66 to 176 patients. Most studies were conducted in China (n = 8), with one study each from Poland, Turkey, and South Korea. All trials enrolled patients who underwent elective VATS. In all studies, the intervention was OFA, defined as no intraoperative opioid use, with postoperative opioid use permitted. Across studies, the mean age of the participants ranged from 44.6 to 67.8 years. Detailed summaries of the study and baseline patient characteristics are provided in Table 1 and Table S3. Details of regional anesthesia used in each study are provided in Table S5.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias was assessed for each outcome using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool [32]. For the primary outcomes, PONV within 24 h was judged to be at low risk of bias in five studies, to have some concerns in two studies, and to be at high risk in one study. For pain at 24 h, five studies were judged to be at low risk of bias, four to have some concerns, and two to be at high risk. Detailed assessments of all outcomes are provided in Fig. 2 and Fig. S1.

Risk of bias in the included studies for pain scores at 24 h (numerical rating scale)

Primary outcomes

PONV within 24 h

Eight trials reported PONV within 24 h. OFA reduced the risk of PONV compared with OIA (RR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.41 to 0.81), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0.1%) (Fig. 3).

Forest plot of the incidence of postoperative nausea and vomiting within 24 h in the OFA and OIA groups. OFA, opioid-free anesthesia; OIA, opioid-inclusive anesthesia; RR, risk ratio; CI, confidence interval

In the subgroup analysis by maintenance anesthesia technique, the effects were consistent in trials using TIVA (three studies; RR, 0.56; 95% CI, 0.19 to 1.65; I2 = 0%) and volatile anesthesia (five studies; RR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.34 to 1.00; I2 = 21%), with no evidence of a subgroup difference (p for interaction = 0.90) (Fig. S2A).

Sensitivity analysis excluding studies at high risk of bias showed similar results (seven studies; RR, 0.55; 95% CI, 0.44 to 0.70; I2 = 0%), with a narrower CI. Of the trials reporting PONV within 24 h, the excluded study [38] was also the only one in which regional blockade was applied exclusively in the OFA group, and exclusion based on this criterion yielded the same result. Excluding two studies in which the control group was defined as OSA [20, 37] produced similar findings (six studies; RR, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.38 to 0.92; I2 = 26%).

Pain score at 24 h

Eleven trials reported pain scores at 24 h. OFA was associated with lower postoperative pain than OIA (MD, − 0.30; 95% CI, − 0.54 to − 0.07), with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 58.8%) (Fig. 4).

Forest plot of pain scores at 24 h (numerical rating scale) in the OFA and OIA groups. OFA, opioid-free anesthesia; OIA, opioid-inclusive anesthesia; SD, standard deviation; MD, mean difference; CI, confidence interval

Subgroup analysis by surgical extent included 10 trials, excluding one trial [20] because the procedural proportions were unavailable. The effect was consistent across studies in which > 50% of patients underwent lobectomy (three studies; MD, − 0.29; 95% CI, − 1.14 to 0.55; I2 = 52.2%) and those in which > 50% underwent non-lobectomy procedures (seven studies; MD, − 0.24; 95% CI, − 0.42 to − 0.06; I2 = 24.5%). No subgroup difference was observed (p = 0.81) (Fig. S2B).

Subgroup analysis by maintenance anesthesia technique showed a significant reduction in pain with OFA in the volatile anesthesia subgroup (six studies; MD, − 0.26; 95% CI, − 0.52 to − 0.01; I2 = 51.7%), but not in the TIVA subgroup (five studies; MD, − 0.45; 95% CI, − 1.19 to 0.29; I2 = 71.1%). No subgroup difference was detected (p = 0.51) (Fig. S2C).

In the sensitivity analysis restricted to movement or coughing pain, the pooled effect was MD − 0.21 (five studies; 95% CI, − 0.47 to 0.05; I2 = 45.2%). Excluding two studies with OSA controls [20, 37] yielded an MD of − 0.24 (nine studies; 95% CI, − 0.41 to − 0.07; I2 = 33.5%). Excluding studies with a high risk of bias or studies in which regional anesthesia was applied only in the OFA group produced similar results (nine studies; MD, − 0.26; 95% CI, − 0.54 to 0.02; I2 = 62%), as both approaches removed the same trials [36, 38].

Secondary outcomes

PONV within 48 h

Six studies reported PONV within 48 h after surgery. OFA reduced PONV within 48 h compared with OIA (RR, 0.50; 95% CI, 0.30 to 0.83), with low heterogeneity (I2 = 17.7%) (Fig. S3A).

Pain score at 48 h

Seven studies contributed data on pain scores at 48 h after surgery. No significant difference was observed between groups (MD, − 0.25; 95% CI, − 0.69 to 0.19), and heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 72.3%) (Fig. S3B). Excluding the high-risk study [36] and the study that used continuous epidural anesthesia [37] reduced the heterogeneity to a moderate level (I2 = 44%).

Chronic postoperative pain

Only one trial reported chronic postoperative pain; therefore, quantitative synthesis was not possible.

QoR at 24 h

Three trials assessed QoR at 24 h using the QoR-15 in one study and the QoR-40 in the other two studies. The results were pooled using the SMD. OFA was associated with improved QoR scores (SMD, 0.40; 95% CI, 0.07 to 0.72), with no heterogeneity (I2 = 0.0%) (Fig. S3C). Subdomain analyses showed improved physical comfort in two studies [19, 20]. One study [20] also reported improvement in the pain subdomain of the QoR-40; however, NRS scores at 24 h were comparable, and the authors attributed this finding to reduced pain at 0–4 h postoperatively, suggesting that early pain relief contributed to the QoR improvement. Another study [4] found no significant differences across domains.

OME at 24 h

Six studies reported cumulative opioid consumption within 24 h after surgery, converted to OME. No significant difference was observed between the groups (MD, − 10.51; 95% CI, − 29.61 to 8.58), although heterogeneity was considerable (I2 = 96.8%) (Fig. S3D). In a post hoc sensitivity analysis excluding studies in which regional anesthesia was applied only in the OFA group and those at high risk of bias [36, 38], heterogeneity was reduced to a moderate level (I2 = 42%). The effect estimate remained non-significant (MD, − 2.52; 95% CI, − 6.34 to 1.29) (Fig. S3E).

Adverse events

Adverse events were reported inconsistently across studies in terms of event definitions, assessment timing, and outcome categorization, which precluded quantitative pooling of data. Therefore, events were summarized descriptively by category (Tables S6 and S7).

Intraoperative hypotension was reported in three trials. Its occurrence was higher in the OIA group in two trials (OFA, 29–30% vs. OIA, 48–67%) [20, 35], whereas the rates were numerically similar in the third trial (38% vs. 32%) [3]. Hemodynamic disturbance requiring pharmacological intervention was reported in one additional trial and was less frequent with OFA (29% vs. 48%) [20]. Intraoperative complication rates, encompassing hypotension, bradycardia, arrhythmias, delayed awakening, and desaturation, appeared higher with OFA in one trial (7% vs. 4%) [4]. Bradycardia was reported in three trials, with a consistent direction favoring OFA in two trials (2–3% vs. 5–23%) [3, 35], whereas one trial reported bradycardia requiring chest compressions in the OFA group (1% vs. 0%) [18]. Vasoconstrictor use during induction was higher in the OFA group in one trial (12% vs. 5%) [18]. Intraoperative arrhythmias were less frequent with OFA in one trial (0% vs. 4%) [17]. Intraoperative awareness was not observed in any of the patients in the two trials that assessed this outcome [17, 20].

Opioid-related postoperative adverse events were consistently reduced or absent in the OFA group. Pruritus was reported in two trials, with no events in the OFA group in either trial compared with 9–35% in the OIA group [36, 37]. Urinary retention was reported in two trials; in one trial, it occurred exclusively in the OIA group (0% vs. 46%) [36], whereas no events were observed in either group in the other trial (0% vs. 0%) [19]. Respiratory depression was absent in both groups in two trials [19, 38] and was reported at low rates in one trial, with numerically fewer events in the OFA group (5% vs. 10%) [39]. Overall postoperative complications were reported in two trials and were less frequent in the OFA group in both trials (20% vs. 34–43%) [4, 39]. Postoperative cognitive dysfunction, assessed as part of these composite endpoints, appeared less frequently with OFA in one trial (20% vs. 43%) [39]. Among other postoperative events, shivering was less frequent in the OFA group in one trial (9% vs. 28%), whereas psychosis was absent in both groups (0% vs. 0%) [20]. Excessive sedation occurred more frequently in the OFA group at post-anesthesia care unit discharge in one trial (8% vs. 2%), and nightmares or hallucinations were reported in similarly low proportions in both groups (5% vs. 3%) [3]. Oxygen therapy requirement was numerically higher in the OFA group in one trial (64% vs. 44%), and agitation requiring treatment was reported in one patient in the OFA group only (4% vs. 0%) [38].

Post hoc exploratory meta-analysis

The following analyses of LOS and PACU stay duration were conducted post hoc and should be interpreted as exploratory outcomes.

LOS (days)

LOS was assessed in seven studies. Six studies reported LOS as a continuous outcome and were included in the exploratory meta-analysis. One study reported length of stay only as a Kaplan–Meier curve without exact mean and standard deviation values. Therefore, this study was not included in the pooled mean difference analysis and was summarized narratively. OFA probably results in little to no difference in LOS compared with OIA (MD, − 0.02; 95% CI, − 0.38 to 0.35; moderate certainty), with low heterogeneity (I2 = 37.0%) (Fig. S3F). The remaining study also reported no apparent between-group difference [35].

PACU stay duration (min)

PACU stay duration was reported in six studies. Five studies reported PACU stays of less than 1 h and were included in the meta-analysis. One study reported a PACU stay of approximately 1 day, which was not clinically comparable with those reported in the other studies and was therefore summarized narratively. OFA may result in little to no difference in PACU stay duration compared with OIA, although the certainty of evidence was low because of serious inconsistency (MD, 3.33 min; 95% CI, − 6.47 to 13.13; I2 = 87.1%) (Fig. S3G). The remaining study also reported a PACU stay of 1.0 day in both groups, with no apparent between-group difference [18].

Publication bias

The funnel plot for pain at 24 h appeared largely symmetric, with one outlying study [20] in the lower left region (Fig. S4A). Egger’s test did not detect any statistically significant asymmetry (p = 0.090).

For outcomes other than pain at 24 h, Egger’s test was not performed because fewer than 10 studies were available for analysis. Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of funnel plots (Fig. S4B–H). Asymmetry was suspected only for PONV within 48 h.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of the evidence, assessed using the GRADE approach, varied across outcomes [45]. Certainty was rated as moderate for PONV within 24 h, pain at 24 h, pain at 48 h, and QoR at 24 h. Certainty was rated as low for PONV within 48 h and very low for postoperative opioid consumption and adverse events. Detailed GRADE assessments are presented in Table 2. For the post hoc exploratory outcomes of LOS and PACU stay duration, the certainty of evidence was rated as moderate and low, respectively.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 RCTs found that, in patients undergoing VATS lung resection in whom regional analgesia was commonly used, OFA reduced PONV within 24 h and improved QoR at 24 h compared with OIA. Although pain scores at 24 h were statistically lower with OFA, the difference did not reach the prespecified MID. OFA was also associated with reduced PONV within 48 h, whereas no differences were observed in cumulative opioid consumption, and adverse events showed no consistent patterns.

Regarding PONV, our meta-analysis showed a reduction within 24 h (RR, 0.58; 95% CI, 0.41–0.81) with negligible heterogeneity and consistent sensitivity analyses. Prior meta-analyses in diverse surgical populations reported larger effect sizes (RR, 0.34–0.55) [11–15], likely because those analyses included heterogeneous procedures associated with pronounced effects (e.g., bariatric surgery [21]) and applied less strict OFA definitions [11, 13]. By using a strict OFA definition, a 24-h outcome window, and a homogeneous VATS population, our study provides a more precise procedure-specific estimate. Although sensitivity analyses restricted to studies in which the same type of regional analgesic technique was used in both groups yielded results broadly consistent with the primary analyses, the findings should still be interpreted cautiously. This restriction did not standardize all details of the regional analgesic protocols, including block timing, local anesthetic regimen, dose, concentration, or adjuvant use. The specific regional techniques also differed across trials and included paravertebral block, epidural analgesia, serratus anterior plane block, erector spinae plane block, intercostal nerve block, and local infiltration. These techniques may differ in analgesic efficacy and opioid-sparing effects. Therefore, the observed effects should be interpreted as those of OFA-based multimodal protocols within heterogeneous regional analgesic pathways, rather than as the independent effect of intraoperative opioid avoidance alone. The comparator arms should be interpreted as heterogeneous opioid-inclusive regimens rather than uniform conventional opioid-based anesthesia. Some comparator protocols employed relatively low intraoperative opioid doses or were described as opioid-sparing, and therefore may resemble contemporary opioid-minimizing multimodal anesthesia. Although sensitivity analyses excluding studies with explicitly opioid-sparing comparators yielded results broadly consistent with the primary analyses, residual heterogeneity in the opioid dose, timing, and background non-opioid adjuncts remains. Therefore, the present findings do not establish whether OFA provides additional benefit over well-designed opioid-minimizing multimodal anesthesia.

Regarding acute pain, no clinically meaningful differences were observed, consistent with prior systematic reviews across surgical types [11–15]. All included studies incorporated regional analgesia—most commonly thoracic paravertebral block—which likely established effective baseline pain control in both groups [27]. The minimally invasive nature of VATS may have further limited the detectable additional benefit of OFA. Notably, regional analgesia may independently influence PONV through its effects on pain control, as pain may contribute to PONV directly [51] or indirectly through increased postoperative opioid requirements. Because regional anesthesia was administered in all cases, subgroup analyses for this factor were not feasible.

QoR at 24 h improved with OFA. Recent meta-analyses suggested similar findings but reported high heterogeneity (I2 = 82–98%) [13, 52], attributed to differences in sex, surgical technique, and OFA protocols. By restricting inclusion to VATS, the present study reduced these sources of variation and demonstrated low heterogeneity (I2 = 0%) despite including only three studies. Reduced PONV—a key component of physical comfort—may partly explain the improvement in QoR subdomains.

For postoperative opioid consumption, no significant difference was observed, and heterogeneity was very high (I2 = 96.8%), partly explained by differences in regional anesthesia allocation and opioid conversion standardization. Population-level differences in opioid requirements may also contribute, as lower opioid use has been reported in Asian compared with Western patients [53, 54].

Regarding adverse events, although OFA is generally associated with higher bradycardia rates, this was not clearly observed in VATS, possibly reflecting thoracic sympathetic stimulation and frequent regional anesthesia use reducing the need for high-dose dexmedetomidine [27, 55]. However, the induction phase warrants attention: double-lumen intubation may necessitate higher doses of adjuncts, and one study reported a case requiring chest compressions during induction in the OFA group. A well-defined OFA protocol with careful attention to induction is therefore warranted.

The post hoc exploratory analyses of functional recovery outcomes did not provide clear evidence that OFA accelerates recovery after VATS. Because LOS and PACU stay duration were not prespecified outcomes and are strongly influenced by institutional discharge criteria, chest tube management, PACU protocols, and healthcare system factors, these findings should be interpreted with caution and should not be considered confirmatory.

OFA combined with regional anesthesia may be relevant for selected patients at high risk of PONV or opioid-related adverse effects, given that PONV is among the complications patients most wish to avoid and antiemetics carry their own limitations. However, our findings do not support routine OFA for all patients, given the limited number of studies and clinical heterogeneity. Importantly, the present findings should not be interpreted as a definitive comparison between OFA and well-designed opioid-minimizing multimodal anesthesia. The comparator arms varied in intraoperative opioid exposure, and some regimens may have been closer to opioid-sparing or opioid-minimizing anesthesia than to conventional opioid-based anesthesia. In addition, regional analgesic techniques, prophylactic antiemetic strategies, and background anesthetic management differed across studies.

Methodological strengths include a strict OFA definition, restriction to VATS lung resection, standardized 24-h outcome assessment, inclusion of RCTs only, and GRADE evidence evaluation. To our knowledge, this is the first systematic review focusing exclusively on OFA in VATS lung resection.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, most studies were conducted in China; therefore, the applicability of the findings to Western populations remains unclear, which limits generalizability. Second, all included studies used regional anesthesia, and the specific techniques, timing, local anesthetic regimens, and use of adjuvants varied considerably across studies. OFA and comparator protocols were also heterogeneous, with differences in non-opioid adjuncts, hypnotic agents, volatile anesthetic exposure, and comparator opioid dosing. As discussed above, the findings should therefore be interpreted as reflecting the effects of OFA-based multimodal protocols within heterogeneous regional analgesic pathways, rather than the isolated effect of intraoperative opioid avoidance alone. Third, evidence on QoR was limited to three studies, and the 95% CI for the effect estimate was wide; therefore, further high-quality RCTs are required. However, by presenting the findings using the GRADE system and considering the width of the CI, we appropriately reflected the current certainty of the evidence. Fourth, only one trial evaluated chronic postoperative pain. Therefore, the long-term effects of OFA in patients undergoing VATS remain unclear, and future trials with extended follow-up periods are required. Fifth, minimum alveolar concentration exposure, volatile anesthetic concentrations, and bispectral index data were not reported, precluding determination of whether the reduction in PONV was attributable to opioid avoidance or reduced volatile anesthetic exposure. Sixth, prophylactic antiemetic strategies varied across studies. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses according to the presence or absence of prophylactic antiemetic use were not feasible because prophylactic antiemetics were administered in 7 of the 8 studies evaluating PONV within 24 h, leaving few or no comparable studies without prophylactic antiemetic use. Furthermore, because rescue antiemetic use was included in the definition of PONV, differences in prophylactic and rescue antiemetic protocols may have influenced the measured incidence of PONV and the interpretation of the effect estimates. Finally, the limited number of studies precluded formal publication bias assessment, and 12 registered trials lacked published data, suggesting potential reporting bias.

Conclusions

In selected patients undergoing VATS lung resection within multimodal perioperative pathways including regional analgesia, OFA protocols probably reduce PONV at 24 h and improve early QoR compared with heterogeneous OIA regimens, while having little or no clinically important effect on acute postoperative pain. However, the independent contribution of intraoperative opioid avoidance remains uncertain owing to heterogeneity in regional analgesic techniques, comparator opioid exposure, prophylactic antiemetic strategies, and background anesthetic management. Current evidence does not support routine OFA over well-designed opioid-minimizing multimodal anesthesia. Further RCTs using standardized regional analgesia and clearly defined opioid-free, opioid-minimizing, and opioid-inclusive protocols are warranted.
