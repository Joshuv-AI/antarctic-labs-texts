Dementia in the Western Pacific region: address inequalities and invest on research
Authors: The Lancet Regional Health – Western Pacific
Published: 2024

In the Dementia in the Asia Pacific Region report released in 2014, Alzheimer's Disease International warned that the number of people living with dementia in the Asia–Pacific region will triple from 23 million in 2015 to almost 71 million by 2050. The region has faced several challenges, including increasing public awareness of dementia, investing in health systems to meet the care needs of people with dementia, and providing training for care providers. Unfortunately, the situation has not improved since then.

In this issue of The Lancet Regional Health—Western Pacific, clinicians and researchers across the Western Pacific region and beyond published Dementia in the Western Pacific Region: from risk reduction to care. This Series is based on the best available scientific evidence and expert consultation across the region and covers topics on preventable risk factors for dementia, diagnosis for dementia and cognitive decline, new amyloid-targeting therapies for Alzheimer's disease, post-diagnosis care, and unique challenges and regional health priorities for dementia.

Knowledge of risk factors for dementia is transforming into preventive strategies following the work of the Finnish Geriatric Intervention Study to Prevent Cognitive Impairment and Disability (FINGER). This study suggested that a multidomain lifestyle intervention, which includes components such as diet, exercise, and cognitive training, could help prevent cognitive decline among at-risk older people aged 60–77 years. The recent Japan-Multimodal Intervention Trial for the Prevention of Dementia did not show similar effectiveness in preventing cognitive decline by implementing multidomain lifestyle intervention, except the high-risk subgroup who had APOE ε4 allele, a genetic risk factor for dementia. The difference in the primary outcomes on cognitive decline between the two trials and others suggests that the effectiveness of multidomain lifestyle interventions may depend on targeting the at-risk groups who are most likely to benefit. A few ongoing multidomain lifestyle intervention trials were adopted in Australia, China, Japan, Malaysia, Singapore, South Korea, and Taiwan, and we expect to see the implementation of the intervention extend to a broader population. Countries in the Western Pacific region have populations with diverse genetic backgrounds, socioeconomic levels, and cultures. Substantial data gaps on risk factors for dementia exist among women (who generally have higher risk), minoritised ethnic groups, and people from low-income and middle-income countries. Future work aimed at a better understanding of risk factors for dementia is essential for implementing effective and tailored risk reduction interventions in the region.

Dementia services are heterogeneous in high-income countries, whereas they are extremely fragmented and limited in low-income and middle-income countries. Providing timely and proper diagnosis and post-diagnosis care is a crucial step to improve the quality of life for people with mild cognitive impairment or dementia. The handful of available evidence, which sometimes relies on personal communication only, reflects the limited number of research studies on dementia diagnosis, treatment, and care in the Western Pacific region. To address this, it is important to evaluate dementia services in areas where memory clinics and other post-diagnostic services are well established. Additionally, low-resourced countries should be encouraged to lead local studies aimed at raising awareness of dementia and developing new initiatives to increase dementia care capacities. Such initiatives could include integrating dementia care into existing health-care systems, applying digital health technologies, and engaging patients, families, and communities to develop contextually and culturally competent care models.

Countries in the Western Pacific region are largely unprepared on every level to cope with the surge of dementia cases in the future. The approval of new amyloid-targeting monoclonal antibody therapies, aducanumab and lecanemab, by the US Food and Drug Administration provides some hope for therapeutic options for reducing cognition decline for patients with early Alzheimer's disease, one of the most common causes of dementia. However, these new treatments require massive resources, including infrastructure for diagnosis and disease monitoring, specialists, and opportunity costs that are not affordable for most countries with already constrained medical systems. In the Revised criteria for diagnosis and staging Alzheimer's disease released on June 27, 2024, by the Alzheimer's Association Workgroup, Alzheimer's disease is biologically defined based on biomarkers like amyloid β and certain tau fragments rather than syndromic presentation. Although with controversy, these updates may help accelerate the development of disease-modifying therapies, improving the ability to diagnose and monitor disease progression more effectively.

Dementia is not a normal part of ageing. The lack of resources and public awareness of dementia prevention and services in the most affected communities needs to be changed. We hope the publication of this Series will spark broader discussions and encourage future research on dementia prevention, treatment, and care. Ultimately, this should lead to actions that reduce the burden of dementia and improve the quality of life for people with dementia and their families.
