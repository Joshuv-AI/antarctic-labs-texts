Cost of managing brain metastases in patients with ALK+ advanced NSCLC with first-line tyrosine kinase inhibitors in the UK
Authors: Hannah Le, Daniel Ladino, Chrissy Lowry, Nada Rifi, Shobhit Baijal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to estimate the NHS costs of managing patients with anaplastic lymphoma kinase positive (ALK+) advanced non-small-cell lung cancer (aNSCLC), with and without brain metastases. Patients were treated with first-line (1 L) ALK tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs).MethodsWe built an economic model to estimate the annual costs of managing ALK+ aNSCLC patients with and without brain metastases using estimated NHS health care resource use (HCRU) consumption. The ALK TKIs included in our model were lorlatinib, crizotinib, alectinib and brigatinib. We based our estimates on the cumulative incidence of brain metastases in four clinical trials – CROWN, ALEX, ALESIA and ALTA-1L.ResultsFor the first year of treatment, our model estimated the annual cost of managing patients with brain metastases to be £14,870. Patients without brain metastases cost £4,133. The NHS HCRU cost savings generated by treating patients with lorlatinib increased significantly over time and were greater in patients without brain metastases at the start of their treatment (at baseline) with lorlatinib. This reflects the prevention of brain metastases with 1 L lorlatinib.ConclusionThe longitudinal cost data demonstrate that lorlatinib leads to potentially lower NHS management costs of brain metastases in patients with ALK+ aNSCLC.Keywords: ALK+, aNSCLC, lorlatinib, alectinib, brain metastases, CNS, management costs, HCRU

Introduction

ALK and NSCLC

Anaplastic lymphoma kinase positive (ALK+) lung cancer is a type of non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) where the ALK gene abnormally fuses with and takes the place of another gene. In the UK, 80–85% of lung cancers are classified as NSCLC [1], and ALK gene translocations occur in 3–7% of NSCLC tumors [2]; mostly in those of adenocarcinoma histology [3]. People who are more likely to have cancer cells with the ALK fusion gene include those aged 55 years and under, non- or light- smokers, women, and people of East Asian ethnicity [4,5].

In patients with ALK+ advanced non-small-cell lung cancer (aNSCLC) common metastatic sites include the central nervous system (CNS), liver, pleura and bone. Within the CNS, brain metastases are common in patients with ALK+ aNSCLC – some 25–40% of such patients have brain metastases at their initial diagnosis, and approximately 70% develop brain metastases as their cancer progresses [6]. Brain metastases are linked to substantial morbidity, neurocognitive, psychological and physical complications and reduced life expectancy [7]. Moreover, health-related quality of life in patients with aNSCLC is likely to be affected by progression to brain metastasis, which is a significant humanistic burden [8,9].

Current management of ALK+ NSCLC

To guide clinical decisions lung cancer treatment guidelines from the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) and the European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO), recommend that at diagnosis all patients with non-squamous NSCLC should be tested for ALK gene status [10].

Treatment guidelines for ALK TKIs has evolved considerably over the last 10 years. For patients diagnosed with ALK+ aNSCLC, NICE initially in August 2016 recommended first-line (1 L) treatment with crizotinib, a first-generation ALK-inhibitor. NICE then recommended treatment with second-generation ceritinib in November 2017, alectinib in June 2018, and brigatinib (Alunbrig) in December 2020. Since the availability of alectinib and brigatinib, crizotinib and ceritinib are rarely used, and have also replaced traditional chemotherapy [11,12].

In the UK, lorlatinib, a third-generation ALK-inhibitor, is only recommended as a treatment option in adults whose disease has progressed after being treated with alectinib, brigatinib or crizotinib. In contrast, in most EU countries, lorlatinib has been a 1 L treatment since 2022. After the CROWN 5Y data [13], ESMO guidelines recommended that patients should be treated in the 1 L setting with alectinib, brigatinib or lorlatinib [14]. While there are few head-to-head clinical trials comparing alectinib, brigatinib, and lorlatinib, the clinical trial data that is available suggest these treatments are not like-for-like but have different systemic/intracranial efficacy and safety profiles.

Due to the high incidence of brain metastases in patients with ALK+ aNSCLC, the aim of treatment with third-generation ALK inhibitors is to prevent the development or progression of brain metastases. Notably, there are no standard treatments following a diagnosis of brain metastases so therapeutic options vary considerably. These include changes in systemic therapy, such as transitioning to an alternative CNS-active next-generation ALK TKI, holocranial brain radiotherapy, stereotactic radiotherapy, and if possible, surgical resection, although targeted ALK-inhibitor therapies are preferred to surgical resection. Local therapies are not always feasible due to the location or number of brain lesions, risk of adverse events that impair cognitive and physical function and impact on quality of life [7,15]. Despite local therapies and systemic therapies, patients may still develop or progress to brain metastases and consequently to a low quality of life [8], a high economic burden for the health service, followed by death [16,17].

In terms of second-generation ALK TKIs, while they can be effective against brain metastases and reducing patient management costs [18], patients can ultimately develop drug resistance which can lead to CNS progression [19]. Data from a Medicare real-world study showed the cumulative incidence of brain metastases in patients without baseline brain metastases receiving IL alectinib or brigatinib was 20% after 5 years, which is associated with a 2.6 times increase in mortality risk compared with patients without brain metastases [20]. On the other hand, third-generation ALK TKIs show increased efficacy in drug resistance and CNS progression.

The Phase III CROWN trial compared the efficacy of third-generation lorlatinib with first-generation crizotinib as 1 L treatment for patients with ALK+ aNSCLC. After 5-years of follow-up, lorlatinib demonstrated an improvement in time to intracranial progression by blinded independent central review (BICR) compared with crizotinib (HR, 0.06; 95% CI, 0.03–0.12) [13].

Our objectives

CNS metastases in patients with ALK+aNSCLC are a significant economic burden to the NHS. Our study aimed to investigate how the addition of different 1 L ALK TKIs impacts the cost of managing patients with ALK+ aNSCLC in the UK NHS. Our study presents the estimated NHS annual and cumulative cost of managing patients considering the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, and patients with and without metastases in the CNS treated with either lorlatinib, alectinib, crizotinib or brigatinib.

While previous studies using a similar methodology have been limited to evaluating costs within the first year following the start of treatment, our analysis utilizes unpublished 5-year CROWN data to examine the cumulative incidence of CNS progression at 36 and 48 months for lorlatinib and crizotinib in the ITT population, and in patients with and without metastases in the CNS. This approach allows us to estimate the cost savings over a 4-year period by differentiating resource utilization in the initial year from that in subsequent years. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to quantify the costs associated with lorlatinib as a 1 L treatment for ALK+ NSCLC, and to apply this framework within a UK context, informed by health resource utilization data and from interviews with three UK medical oncologists.

Material & methods

Our analysis followed methods reported by Isla et al. 2020 in Spain [16], and Burudpakdee et al. 2018 in the US [21], that estimated the costs of managing patients with ALK+ aNSCLC with and without brain metastases.

We estimated the annual cost of managing patients with ALK+ aNSCLC with or without the development of brain metastases by multiplying resource consumption by the corresponding unit cost. We considered the costs of the following healthcare resources: medical visits, hospitalizations, laboratory tests, imaging techniques, radiotherapy and surgical procedures, depending on the presence or absence of CNS/brain metastases. We did not consider drug costs as our main objective was to estimate the direct costs associated with healthcare consumption associated with CNS progression.

There is little data available on HCRU associated with patients with ALK+ aNSCLC with and without brain metastases in the NHS. To fill this data gap, in interviews with three medical oncologists specializing in lung cancer we used cost categories and resource use estimates in Spain as a starting point to estimate resource consumption in the NHS. These validation interviews differentiated the resource utilization for patients with and without brain metastases during the first and subsequent years of treatment. This allowed us to capture variations in the consumption after the first year as some specific procedures for the treatment of brain metastases are only performed once and mostly during the first year. Additionally, we considered that hospitalizations will probably increase after the first year, given the worsening of the patient’s condition.

The validation interviews with UK medical oncologists provided evidence that patients with CNS metastases used more resources in terms of specific procedures to treat metastases, and visits and hospitalizations over the first and subsequent years when compared with patients without CNS metastases. Consumption of the remaining resources, i.e., laboratory tests and imaging techniques had similar usage between patients with and without CNS metastases over the first and subsequent years. The results of the interviews with the UK medical oncologists showed the following.

During the first year of treatment, the highest variation was observed in the specific procedures for brain metastases: 4% of patients (with a range of 2% to 5%) underwent holocranial brain radiotherapy, and the percentage of patients that required surgical resection was low at 6% (ranging from 2%–10%). All the UK experts we consulted agreed that only one surgical resection was needed. The most common procedure was radiosurgery, which was used in 43% of cases (ranging from 30%–60%) in the UK. After the first year, the holocranial brain radiotherapy percentage was 0% as it was only performed once; the surgical resection percentage was reduced to 1% as the probability of it being required twice was low; and the percentage for radiosurgery or stereotactic radiotherapy was reduced by 30%. The most notable difference compared with Isla et al. 2020 was in the number of surgical resection procedures per year. Conducted in Spain, Isla et al. 2020 estimated three procedures per year [16], while our study conducted for a UK setting considered one procedure was appropriate per year.

The estimated percentage of patients who experience a general admission related to brain metastases in the UK is 8% (ranging from 5%–10%) of patients without CNS metastases versus 17% (ranging from 10%–20%) of UK patients with CNS metastases. Both groups of patients would experience an average of one (1,1) hospitalization. Radiation oncology hospitalizations are not required in patients without CNS metastases, and a low percentage of patients with CNS metastases (1% [0–2%]) experience an average of two (0–3) radiation oncology hospitalizations per year. Additionally, the UK medical oncologists we interviewed noted that an average of 5% of patients with CNS metastases would require one (1–1) inpatient elective surgery hospitalization per year. No significant differences were observed in UK hospitalizations compared with Spain.

Regarding hospital visits, medical oncologists told us that 70% of all patients, with and without CNS metastases, would require 13 medical oncology visits (one every 28 days). For patients without CNS metastases, 30% would require 13 radiation oncology visits. For patients with CNS metastases, the 30% who are monitored by radiation oncology would increase the number of visits to 16. For the 70% monitored by medical oncology (with 13 visits per year), two types of situations are observed in the NHS. Patients can receive either an additional follow-up from radiation oncology (+3 visits per year) or be also completely monitored by radiation oncology (+16 visits per year). In our research, we used the second option as the base case. Additionally, 70% of patients without CNS metastases would require one visit to the emergency service, and patients with CNS metastases would require two visits. Also, 10% of patients with CNS metastases would have two surgery visits per year. In Spain, all patients require 12 visits to medical oncology regardless of their CNS metastases status, and all patients with CNS metastases will require three additional visits to medical oncology, and 30% of those patients will require four additional visits to radiation oncology.

The relevant laboratory tests are blood count, biochemistry and thoracentesis. Regardless of their CNS metastases status, blood count and biochemistry are required for all patients 12 times per year. One thoracentesis is also required for 10% of patients regardless of their CNS metastases status. The use of laboratory tests is similar in the UK and Spain.

We considered four imaging techniques – bone scan, cerebral magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), thorax/abdomen computed tomography (CT) and brain CT. The UK medical oncologists we consulted estimated that 3% (3–3%) of patients would receive one bone scan per year regardless of their CNS metastases status. They agreed that all patients (100%) would receive four thorax CTs per year regardless of their CNS metastases status. The most notable differences were observed in cerebral MRI and brain CT. According to our medical oncologist feedback, cerebral MRI is preferred in the UK, and 50% (ranging from 40%–60%) of patients without CNS metastases would receive 1.7 (1–2) cerebral MRIs per year, and only 30% (30–30%) of patients would receive 1.3 (1–2) brain CTs. Although the MRI is preferred in UK clinical practice, a small percentage of patients would still receive brain CT because MRI facilities are not available where they live. Therefore, the percentage of patients with CNS metastases that receive an MRI increases up to 94% in the UK with a frequency of four uses per year. The most notable differences occur in cerebral MRI for patients without CNS metastases (0% in Spain vs 50% in the UK) and with CNS metastases (50% in Spain vs 94.3% in the UK), and brain CT for patients without CNS metastases (70% in Spain vs 30% in the UK) and with CNS metastases (100% in Spain vs 4% in the UK).

In the lorlatinib group, the cumulative incidence of progression of brain metastases as the first event, with adjustment for the competing risks of progression other than brain metastases and death, was lower than in the crizotinib group (Table 2). At 12 months, the ITT population cumulative incidence of progression of brain metastases as the first event was 2.8% (95% CI 1–8.1) with lorlatinib and 33.2% (24.6–44.7) with crizotinib [23]. At 24 months, the cumulative incidence was 5% (3–11) with lorlatinib and 45% (35–58) with crizotinib [29]. The CROWN 5-year (cut-off: 31 October 2023) 36 and 48-month cumulative incidence of progression of brain metastases in the ITT population showed an increasing difference between lorlatinib (7.8% [4.0 –15.3]) and crizotinib (72.7% [62.5– 84.5]). In patients without CNS metastases at baseline, the cumulative incidence with lorlatinib was 7.4% (1.7–32.0) at 12 months and 18% (8–40) at 24 months [27,29]. For crizotinib it was 72.3% (56.4–92.7) at 12 months and 91% (76–110) at 24 months. Data are not available for 36 and 48 months. In patients with CNS metastases at baseline, the cumulative incidence with lorlatinib was 1.0% (0.1–6.9) at 12 months [27], 1.1% (0.1–7.5) at 24 months [29], and 4.7% (2–11.3) at 36 and 48 months. For crizotinib it was 17.8% (10.0–31.9) at 12 months [27], 28.8% (17.8–46.4) at 24 months [29], and 63.6% (51.2–79.1) at 36 and 48 months.

No head-to-head data were available comparing lorlatinib with alectinib or brigatinib. Therefore we used unadjusted estimates of the cumulative incidence of CNS progression for alectinib and brigatinib from the ALEX, ALESIA and ALTA-1L clinical trials. In the ALEX trial the cumulative incidence of brain metastases in patients without brain metastases at baseline at 12 and 24 months was 4.6% (1.5–10.6) [28], and 7.2% (CI not available) [21], respectively, in the alectinib group. In the ITT population, the cumulative incidence was 9.4% (5.4–14.7) at 12 months [24]. In patients with brain metastases at baseline, the cumulative incidence was 16.0% (8.2–26.2) at 12 months [28]. In the ALESIA trial, the ITT population cumulative incidence of progression of brain metastases in the alectinib arm was 4.7% (CI not available) at 12 months, 8.9% (CI not available) at 24 months, 11.6% (6.7–18.1) at 36 months and 13.4% (CI not available) at 48 months. The cumulative incidence of brain metastases in patients without brain metastases at baseline at 12, 24, 36 and 48 months was 3.9% (CI not available), 6.5% (CI not available), 9.1% (4.0–16.8) and 9.1% (CI not available) respectively. In patients with CNS metastases at baseline, the cumulative incidence with alectinib was 6.5% (CI not available) at 12 months, 13.6% (CI not available) at 24 months, 16.2% (7.0–28.7) at 36 and 20.7% (CI not available) at 48 months [26]. In the ALTA-1L trial, at 12 months the cumulative incidence of CNS progression in the ITT population was 12.0% (6.4–19.5) with brigatinib [25]. No data were available for the cumulative incidence of CNS progression for brigatinib at 24, 36 and 48 months.

As the resource consumption for patients with CNS progression is different during the first year and subsequent years, we estimated the annual costs for each of the years for which the cumulative incidence is available. It is assumed that regardless of whether CNS progression occurs in Year 1 or Year 4, the first year after progression will always have the same costs. Therefore, the annual cost of a patient with CNS progression during the first year after the progression occurs is independent of the year in which the progression occurred. To estimate the year-specific annual costs, we considered the treatment-specific cumulative incidence of CNS progression in the previous year multiplied by the annual costs of patients with CNS progression after the first year; and the increase in the cumulative incidence in the current year multiplied by the annual costs of patients with CNS progression during the first year after progression occurred. The annual costs of patients without CNS progression are weighted by the remaining proportion of patients. All the equations used in the calculations are described in the Supplementary materials.

Finally, we present the annual and cumulative costs for each of the alternatives to estimate the difference in costs of managing patients with ALK+ NSCLC treated with lorlatinib versus patients treated with alectinib, brigatinib and crizotinib.

To understand the impact of adverse events in the total management costs, we performed an additional analysis considering the costs associated with managing grade 3–5 treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAE) reported in at least 5% of patients in either treatment arm (Table 3). For each treatment, adverse events with a frequency lower than 5% are displayed in the table as the frequency is above 5% in another treatment. The adverse events for all treatments are only applied to the ITT population, as no adverse event data specific to patients with or without brain metastases at baseline are available. Adverse events are included in the model as a one-off cost during the first year based on the National Schedule of NHS Costs 2022/2023 (Table 3) [22]. Even though previous NICE appraisals have included the unit costs for certain adverse events [32–34], the medical oncologists we interviewed reported that managing those adverse events will not require additional resources, as the standard management of the disease already includes regular visits and tests.

Discussion

This study contributes to the literature on the economic burden of brain metastases, focusing on the UK NHS management costs of patients with ALK+ NSCLC.

The findings highlight a clear and clinically relevant link between a reduced incidence of CNS metastases and a decrease in HCRU among patients with ALK+ NSCLC. CNS metastases increase the complexity of managing patients since metastases often require costly interventions such as hospital admissions, neurosurgical procedures, radiotherapy, and long-term supportive care. In this context, lorlatinib’s demonstrated ability to prevent or delay the onset of CNS metastases in patients with advanced ALK+ NSCLC represents a potential dual benefit: improving clinical outcomes while also reducing the economic burden on healthcare systems. Compared with crizotinib, lorlatinib’s superior CNS penetration and targeted activity may reduce the frequency of brain metastases, which could translate into meaningful cost savings over the course of disease management, while simultaneously enhancing patients’ quality of life.

The economic impact of brain metastases on healthcare costs has been examined extensively in the United States. For example, Guérin et al. (2014), using pooled data from three large administrative databases between 2011 and 2013, reported that the mean per-patient-per-month (PPPM) cost increased from $5,983 before brain metastases diagnosis to $22,645 after diagnosis – an incremental rise of $16,662 per month [7]. In a later analysis, [17], drawing on claims data from over 60 self-insured Fortune 500 companies across all US Census regions (January 1999–March 2013), calculated pre-diagnosis costs of $35,341 per patient per 6 months (PPP6M), equivalent to $5,890 per month, and post-diagnosis costs of $58,646 PPP6M ($9,774 per month), resulting in an incremental increase of $3,884 PPPM [17].

Similarly, Burudpakdee et al. (2018), using combined data from the PharMetrics Plus and MarketScan claims databases (covering January 2008 to March 2016 and up to December 2015, respectively), found adjusted mean PPPM costs of $18,678 for patients without brain metastases versus $24,707 for those with brain metastases – an incremental difference of $6,029 per month [21]. Fernandes et al. (2017), analyzing medical claims from the HealthCore Integrated Research Environment (HIRE), reported an even greater cost burden, with an increase of $20,301 PPPM in all-cause total healthcare costs following brain metastases diagnosis [35].

Across these US studies, the estimated incremental monthly cost associated with brain metastases ranges from $3,884 to $20,301. In comparison, our analysis estimates an annual incremental cost of £10,746 in the first year following brain metastases diagnosis –equivalent to £896 per month. This lower figure is consistent with differences in healthcare cost structures between the US and UK. For example, the average hospitalization cost for brain cancer in the US is $38,736 (Clinical Classification Software Refined NEO048, 2022 [36]), whereas the UK NHS equivalent is £4,816 (Brain Tumors or Cerebral Cysts: AA24C–AA24H) [22], making US hospitalization costs approximately 7.8 times higher.

When compared with Isla et al. – the primary reference study for our analysis – the estimated annual cost difference due to brain metastases in our study is slightly lower (£10,746 vs €15,464) [16]. This discrepancy is largely explained by the higher radiosurgery unit costs reported in Spain relative to those in the UK.

Our study had some limitations: the main limitation being that no head-to-head data were available comparing lorlatinib with alectinib. Therefore, we used data from the ALEX and ALESIA clinical trials to estimate the cumulative incidence of CNS progression for alectinib. These data were not adjusted for heterogeneity using indirect treatment comparison methods, which is a limitation of this analysis. Additionally, a network meta-analysis (NMA) was conducted for the intracranial time to progression in which the hazard ratio for lorlatinib vs alectinib was found to be 0.39 (0.17–0.89) in the base case NMA [37]. Comparing this with a simple, unadjusted Bucher indirect treatment comparison (HR = 0.38, 95% CI: 0.16–0.90) suggests that using a naïve unadjusted comparison of the cumulative incidence of CNS progression, while limited, is unlikely to have a significant impact on the results of the analysis.

Another limitation concerns the availability of robust data on the cumulative incidence of brain metastases across different ALK+ NSCLC treatments. Only the CROWN trial (comparing lorlatinib with crizotinib) and the ALESIA trial (comparing alectinib with crizotinib) reported follow-up periods extending to 48 months. However, the ALESIA trial exclusively enrolled Asian patients, which may limit the generalizability of its findings. In the case of the ALEX trial, data for alectinib for the 24-month follow-up was available only for patients without brain metastases at baseline. For brigatinib, cumulative incidence data were available solely for the ITT population and covered a shorter 12-month follow-up period.

Finally, in an ideal scenario, data on HCRU for UK patients with ALK+ NSCLC – both with and without CNS progression – would have been obtained directly from robust, previously published real-word evidence studies. However, in the absence of such publications, we adopted an alternative evidence-gathering approach. Specifically, in January 2024, we conducted structured interviews with a panel of three key opinion leaders, all of whom have extensive clinical experience in managing this patient population within the UK healthcare system.

While this methodology is consistent with that used in other economic and clinical studies, our analysis incorporates two important enhancements. First, we applied a broader time horizon when estimating the cumulative incidence of brain metastases, allowing for a more comprehensive assessment of disease progression over time. Second, we modeled healthcare resource consumption as a time-varying parameter, capturing differences in utilization patterns between patients with and without CNS metastases as their clinical status evolves. This approach provides a more dynamic and realistic representation of real-world costs than static, single-timepoint estimates.

Although these limitations are acknowledged, our findings offer evidence to support decision making within the UK healthcare setting. Specifically, the results highlight the importance of strategies aimed at preventing the development of brain metastases and implementing screening programmes to detect ALK rearrangements in patients with NSCLC. The selection of therapies with proven efficacy in reducing the incidence of CNS can improve the quality of life for patients with ALK+ NSCLC, but may also reduce the economic burden associated with managing brain metastases with meaningful cost savings for the NHS.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates the direct relationship between a lower cumulative incidence of progression of brain metastases and lower cost burdens in patients with ALK+ aNSCLC receiving 1 L ALK TKIs in the UK.

The longitudinal cost data demonstrate that 1 L lorlatinib leads to lower management costs of brain metastases in patients with ALK+ aNSCLC compared with crizotinib vs the cost savings seen with other 1 L ALK TKIs vs crizotinib. These results are consistent with data from the Phase 3 CROWN study, where the cumulative incidence of progression of brain metastases with 1 L lorlatinib was low.

Cost savings with lorlatinib 1 L vs other TKIs increase significantly from Year 1 to Year 4 given the significant brain metastases efficacy and low cumulative incidence of brain metastases even in the long follow-up period of 5 years. Specifically, from Year 1 to Year 4, cost savings with lorlatinib vs crizotinib increased by 5 times in the ITT population and by 8 times in patients without baseline brain metastases, reflecting the prevention of brain metastases with 1 L lorlatinib.
