Enzootic bovine leukosis caused by bovine leukemia virus classified as Group C based on viral whole genome sequencing in a 23-month-old Holstein-Friesian heifer
Authors: Yusuke DAIJI, Masaki MAEZAWA, Kento ISHIKAWA, James K CHAMBERS, Kazuyuki UCHIDA, Hisashi INOKUMA
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 23-month-old Holstein-Friesian heifer presented with inactivity and diarrhea. On physical examination, no enlargement of superficial lymph nodes was observed. Hematological examination revealed lymphocytosis. The bovine leukemia virus (BLV) proviral load was 2,122 copies/10 ng DNA, and BLV was classified as Group C based on whole genome phylogenetic analysis. Monoclonal proliferation of B-cells and monoclonal integration of the BLV provirus in the bovine genome were detected by a clonality test of B-cells and inverse PCR, respectively. Although lymph nodes were not swollen at necropsy, histopathological examination revealed neoplastic lymphocyte proliferation in lymph nodes, which were immune positive for CD5 and CD20, and negative for CD3. The heifer was diagnosed with EBL caused by BLV classified as Group C.Keywords: bovine leukemia virus Group C strain, bovine leukemia virus, early onset, enzootic bovine leukosis, heifer

Enzootic bovine leukosis (EBL) is a B-cell lymphoma caused by bovine leukemia virus (BLV) in cattle older than 3 years of age [1], although early-onset EBL in cattle under 3 years of age has also been reported [7, 12]. Enlargement of superficial lymph nodes and masses in the pelvic cavity are important clinical findings that can lead to a suspicion of EBL [1, 2]. However, several EBL cases without those findings have been reported [15].

BLV is divided into 5 groups (A, B-1, B-2, C, and other) according to its complete genome sequence. BLV in EBL cattle under 3 years of age is mainly classified as Group A or B-1 [5, 11], and previous studies have reported no Group C strain in EBL cattle. In fact, Group C BLV has only been isolated from cattle without EBL [5, 7, 11]. Here we report a clinical case of EBL caused by BLV classified as Group C in a 23-month-old Holstein-Friesian heifer with no enlargement of lymph nodes or masses in the pelvic cavity.

The heifer presented with inactivity and diarrhea. On day 1, the initial examination revealed emaciation and bloody diarrhea. Enlargement of superficial lymph nodes was not observed, and no mass was palpated on rectal examination. The heifer was tentatively diagnosed with coccidiosis and treated with 20 mg/kg sulfamonomethoxine sodium (Fujita Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). However, the general condition of the heifer did not improve. On day 3, blood examination revealed anemia (red blood cell (RBC), 4.23 × 106/μL; reference interval (RI): 5.10–7.60 × 106/μL; hemoglobin concentration, 6.6 g/dL; RI: 8.5–12.2 g/dL; hematocrit, 16.7%; RI: 22–33%), lymphocytosis (150,300/μL; RI: 1,600–5,600/µL), and thrombocytosis (15 × 104/µL; RI: 19.3–63.7 × 104/µL) [2]. On day 5, dyspnea, bloat, anterior edema, and enlargement of superficial lymph nodes were not noted (Fig. 1), and hematological examination revealed anemia (RBC, 3.86 × 106/μL; hemoglobin concentration, 6.4 g/dL; hematocrit, 15.4%), lymphocytosis (121,589/μL), and thrombocytosis (8.9 × 104/µL) [2]. Peripheral blood smear examination revealed medium to large atypical lymphoid cells (Supplementary Fig. 1), which were negative for myeloperoxidase (MPO) staining using a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining kit (Muto Pure Chemicals, Tokyo, Japan). Serum biochemical analysis revealed increased activities of total lactate dehydrogenase (LDH: 2,244 U/L; RI: 697–1,450 U/L) and thymidine kinase (26 U/L; RI: <5.4 U/L) [4, 14]. LDH isozyme analysis showed elevated activities of LDH-2 (866 U/L; RI: 137–503 U/L) and LDH-3 (289 U/L; RI: 82–262 U/L) [4].

Emaciation, diarrhea, and no swelling of superficial lymph nodes were noted on Day 5.

The BLV proviral load of DNA extracted from a peripheral blood sample was quantified using the TAKARA BLV detection kit (TaKaRa Bio, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. BLV copy number in the peripheral blood sample was 2,122 copies/10 ng DNA. Phylogenetic analysis of BLV causing the infection was also performed. BLV genomes were sequenced as described previously [11]. The whole BLV genome was amplified using PrimeSTAR GXL polymerase (Takara Bio) and primers previously acquired. PCR products were electrophoresed in 2% agarose gels and purified from the gels using the NucleoSpin Gel and PCR Clean-up (Takara Bio). The purified PCR products were used as templates for sequencing. The sequencing of the products was performed at Eurofins Genomics Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). A phylogenetic tree was constructed by neighbor-joining methods (1,000 bootstrap replications) using the whole genome sequence of BLV in the present case (Accession No. LC775095) and complete genome sequences of BLV obtained from the GenBank database under accession numbers LC733243, LC733245, LC733248, LC733314 to LC733317, LC733320, LC733321, LC733323, LC733327, LC733360, AP0180012, AP0180016, and AP0180018. BLV in the present case was classified Group C (Fig. 2).

Phylogenetic analysis of the whole bovine leukemia virus (BLV) genome sequence. A maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree was constructed from the whole BLV genome sequence of the present case and 15 reference sequences. BLV strains were divided into 5 groups (A, B-1, B-2, C, and other) based on a previous study. Numbers on the figure denote branch lengths.

BoLA-DRB3 genotyping of the present heifer was performed. The DNA from peripheral blood was used as a template for PCR, which was performed using GoTaq® Master Mixes (Promega Corp., Madison, WI, USA) and primer pairs (DRB3FRW and DRB3REV) [10]. PCR products were purified using the QIAquick PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN, GmbH, Hilden, Germany). Purified PCR products were used as templates for sequencing, which was performed at Eurofins Genomics Inc. (Tokyo, Japan). BoLA-DRB3 sequences downloaded from the IPD-MHC database (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/ipd/mhc/group/BoLA/) were used as references. The optimal allele combination was identified using SeqScape (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Foster City, CA, USA). The BoLA-DRB3 genotype of the present case was BoLA-DRB3*007:01/015:01.

B-cell clonality of peripheral blood was examined by PCR amplification of immunoglobulin heavy chain gene V-D-J junctional variability [9]. The DNA from peripheral blood was used as a template for PCR using HotStarTaq DNA Polymerase (QIAGEN) and a primer pair (BoVHF1 and BoVHR1). Genomic DNA from a typical EBL cow (5 years old, BLV positive, B-cell lymphoma) was used as a positive control, and genomic DNA from cattle with inflammatory disease (mastitis), cattle with persistent lymphocytosis, clinically healthy cattle with BLV infection, and clinically healthy cattle without BLV infection were used as negative controls. PCR products were electrophoresed in a 2% agarose gel, in which a single band was detected (Supplementary Fig. 2).

BLV provirus integration clonality in peripheral blood was analyzed by inverse PCR (iPCR) as previously reported [8]. DNA samples used for the B-cell clonality analysis were digested with PstI and self-ligated using Mighty Mix (Takara Bio). Products were used as templates for PCR using inverse primers (Pst-F and Pst-R). PCR products were electrophoresed in a 2% agarose gel, in which a single band was detected (Fig. 3). In contrast, no visible bands were observed for negative controls.

On day 14, the heifer was transferred to meat hygiene inspection center for meat inspection. At the gross level, the spleen was swollen (60 × 20 × 5 cm), and several masses (2–10 cm) were disseminated throughout the heart and kidney (Supplementary Fig. 3). Although lymph nodes were not swollen (Supplementary Fig. 4), the cut surface of the masses and lymph nodes were homogenous and white to grey in color. The sternal bone marrow was pale red. In bone marrow cytology, blast cells constituted up to 82.4% of all nucleated cells, and the myeloid to erythroid (M/E) ratio was 7.3 (Supplementary Fig. 5). These blast cells were further examined by MPO staining using a DAB staining kit (Muto Pure Chemicals); 0.4% of non-erythroid cells were found to be positive. Histopathologically, neoplastic cells diffusely proliferated in the lymph nodes, spleen, abomasum, intestines, and the masses disseminated in the heart and kidney (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 6A). Immunohistochemically, tumor cells were positive for CD5 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and CD20 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and were negative for CD3 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) (Figs. 4B–4D and Supplementary Fig. 6B–D).

Diagnosing EBL in young cattle is difficult. The onset of sporadic bovine leukosis in BLV-infected cattle has been reported [3], and in the present case, EBL was not strongly suspected on day 1 because superficial lymph nodes were not swollen and rectal examination revealed no masses. However, blood examination performed on day 3 for differential diagnosis revealed lymphocytosis, suggesting lymphoma. Moreover, monoclonal B-cell proliferation and monoclonal integration of the BLV provirus were detected by PCR of the immunoglobulin heavy chain gene and iPCR, respectively. Based on these findings, the present case was clinically diagnosed with EBL, despite the young age of the animal. The pathological diagnosis of B-cell lymphoma supported the clinical diagnosis.

Lymph node enlargement due to proliferation of tumor cells is observed in most EBL cases, although EBL without swollen lymph nodes with no neoplastic cells has been reported [13]. In the present case, histopathological examination revealed the proliferation of tumor cells in lymph nodes, but lymph nodes were not grossly swollen. These findings underscore the importance of lymph node examination regardless of the presence or absence of swelling.

Several factors including polymorphisms of BoLA-DRB3 and BLV strain are related to lymphoma development in young cattle [5, 6]. The BoLA-DRB3*015:01 allele was previously reported to be an early EBL onset susceptibility allele [6]. BLV is classified into 5 groups based on whole genome phylogenetic analysis, and the pathogenicity of Group C BLV is considered low [5]. In the present case, the genotype was identified as DRB3*007:01/015:01, and findings from the phylogenetic analysis of the whole BLV genome were consistent with those of Group C BLV. Therefore, host factors might have contributed more to the early onset of EBL than viral factors in the present case. However, the pathogenicity of Group C BLV has not been fully investigated, and further studies are warranted. It will also be important to clarify whether viral or host factors are more influential in the onset of EBL.

In conclusion, although neoplastic cells proliferated in lymph nodes, EBL was not accompanied by enlarged lymph nodes in the present case, suggesting that neoplastic infiltration of lymph nodes does not always cause lymph node enlargement. Moreover, BLV in the present case was classified as Group C, warranting further studies to clarify the pathogenicity of Group C BLV.
