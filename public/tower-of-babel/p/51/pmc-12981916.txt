Situating the salience and parietal memory networks in the context of multiple parallel distributed networks using precision functional mapping
Authors: Young Hye Kwon, Joseph J Salvo, Nathan L Anderson, Donnisa Edmonds, Ania M Holubecki, Maya Lakshman, Kwangsun Yoo, BT Thomas Yeo, Kendrick Kay, Caterina Gratton, Rodrigo M Braga

The authors found a coding error in the spin test analyses reported. This error randomized the vertices vertex-wise rather than preserving spatial structure and rotating. This meant that the null distributions reported in Figure 7B were too lenient. When this error was corrected, in some subjects (S1, S4, S7) the spin test results became non-significant for the anteromedial parts of the network for the two contrasts of interest (P3 > P1, P2 > P1). In one subject (S6), one of the comparisons (P2 > P1) became non-significant while the other (P3 > P1) remained significant. This error did not affect the remaining subjects or analyses.

The unified SAL/PMN shows a repetition enhancement effect throughout the distributed network (corrected)

The error does not affect the main claims of the paper. The corrected spin test results suggest that the task-related activation during the recognition task is not specific to SAL/PMN, because the spin test assesses the spatial specificity of overlap between two maps. This was not a claim made in the original manuscript, which instead argued that both the SAL/PMN and an adjacent network, FPN-A, showed engagement during the task (shown in Figure 7C).

Alternative permutation test results

Given that the spin permutation tests assess the spatial specificity of task responses rather than the significance of task engagement in the contrast of interest, an alternative permutation test was performed. In this test, the task condition labels (i.e., trials corresponding to P1, P2, and P3) were randomly shuffled.

For each of the three contrasts reported (P2 > P1, P3 > P1, and P3 > P2), two-tailed t tests were performed at each vertex and then averaged across the three predefined regions of interest: anteromedial, posteromedial, and the unified SAL/PMN. To assess statistical significance, condition labels were permuted 10,000 times for each contrast to generate a null distribution of t values, which was then compared to the observed t value. The multiple comparisons were corrected using Bonferroni correction. Asterisks indicate statistical significance under different correction levels (* indicates significance when correcting for 3 contrasts, ** for 3 contrasts × 3 regions, and *** for 3 contrasts × 3 regions × 6 subjects; α = 0.05).

As shown in the corrected figure, the results confirmed a repetition enhancement effect in the unified, anteromedial, and posteromedial network regions, which held across most subjects for both of the key contrasts (P2 > P1 and P3 > P1). The only exceptions were S7, who did not show the effect in the anteromedial regions for either P2 > P 1 or P3 > P1 contrasts, and S6, who did not show the effect in the anteromedial regions for P2 > P1 (but did for P3 > P1). The P3 > P2 contrast was not significant in most subjects, as expected based on the other (unaffected) analyses.

The new permutation results thus agreed with conclusions of the manuscript, in demonstrating evidence that all three SAL/PMN regions—anteromedial, posteromedial, and the unified network—show evidence of a repetition enhancement effect, as predicted by their network connections.
