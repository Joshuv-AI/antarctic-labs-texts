Exosome-mediated regulation of the immune microenvironment via JAK/STAT signaling promotes pancreatic cancer progression
Authors: Shunxin Hao, Zhi Yao, Can Li, Tao Xiong, Jingxi Song, Yifeng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPancreatic cancer is characterized by a profoundly immunosuppressive microenvironment. This study aimed to investigate whether pancreatic cancer-derived exosomes contribute to tumor progression and CD8+ T-cell dysfunction in relation to Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) signaling.MethodsExosomes derived from PANC-1 cells were characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), and Western blotting. Their effects on cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and JAK/STAT signaling in PANC-1 cells were evaluated. CD8+ T cells isolated from commercially obtained peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were co-cultured with exosome-treated PANC-1 cells to assess T-cell exhaustion, cytokine secretion, and proliferative function.ResultsExosome treatment enhanced PANC-1 cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, accompanied by increased phosphorylation of JAK1/STAT3. CD8+ T cells co-cultured with exosome-treated PANC-1 cells exhibited increased expression of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3), reduced tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) secretion, and impaired proliferative capacity. These effects were partially attenuated by JAK/STAT inhibition.ConclusionsPancreatic cancer-derived exosomes are associated with tumor progression and CD8+ T-cell dysfunction in a co-culture model, potentially involving JAK/STAT signaling. These findings suggest that the exosome-JAK/STAT axis may represent a potential target for therapeutic intervention, pending further validation.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Pancreatic cancer-derived exosomes promoted PANC-1 cell proliferation, migration, and invasion and were associated with CD8+ T-cell exhaustion-related changes in a tumor cell-T cell co-culture system. These effects were accompanied by activation of Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) signaling and were partially reversed by the JAK/STAT inhibitor AG490.

What is known and what is new?

• Pancreatic cancer is characterized by a highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Tumor-derived exosomes have been recognized as important mediators of intercellular communication and immune regulation, while aberrant JAK/STAT signaling contributes to tumor progression and T-cell dysfunction.

• This study provides evidence linking pancreatic cancer-derived exosomes with JAK/STAT pathway activation and CD8+ T-cell exhaustion-related changes. The findings suggest a potential exosome-JAK/STAT-immune dysfunction axis that may contribute to pancreatic cancer progression.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings support further investigation of exosome-mediated signaling pathways as potential therapeutic targets in pancreatic cancer. Future studies should validate the proposed mechanism in additional cell lines, animal models, and clinical samples, and identify the specific exosomal cargos responsible for JAK/STAT activation and immune modulation.

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer is one of the most lethal malignancies worldwide and is characterized by insidious onset, rapid progression, and resistance to conventional therapeutic strategies. Early diagnosis of pancreatic cancer remains extremely challenging due to the lack of specific symptoms and reliable biomarkers, leading to most patients being diagnosed at advanced stages (1). Although advances in surgery, chemotherapy, and targeted therapy have been achieved in recent years, the overall 5-year survival rate remains below 10%, reflecting the formidable clinical challenge posed by this disease (2). A major factor underlying the poor prognosis is the profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), which enables tumor cells to escape immune surveillance and facilitates disease progression (3). Among the mechanisms of immune evasion, the dysfunction and exhaustion of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells play a particularly critical role in weakening antitumor immunity (4).

The role of tumor-derived exosomes as key mediators of intercellular crosstalk in the TME has recently drawn significant scientific interest (5). These nanosized extracellular vesicles, termed exosomes, are secreted by tumor cells, carrying proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids that can be transferred to recipient cells. Recent studies have demonstrated that pancreatic cancer-derived exosomes actively remodel the TME, particularly by regulating immune responses through the transfer of immunosuppressive cargos that impair immune cell function and promote immune evasion (6,7). Specifically, these cargos have been shown to impair antigen presentation, inhibit natural killer cell cytotoxicity, and promote the expansion of regulatory T cells (7). Such findings emphasize the pivotal role of exosomes in orchestrating tumor-immune interactions and underscore their potential as therapeutic targets. However, the molecular signaling pathways through which pancreatic cancer exosomes compromise CD8+ T cell function have not yet been fully clarified.

As a pivotal signaling mechanism, the Janus kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT) pathway is a fundamental signaling cascade in immune regulation, controlling cytokine signaling and T cell differentiation (8). Dysregulation of this pathway has been implicated in tumor development, chronic inflammation, and the induction of immune checkpoints (9). In pancreatic cancer, STAT3 is frequently hyperactivated and has been linked to tumor cell proliferation, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis (10). Persistent activation of the JAK/STAT pathway in T cells is closely associated with the establishment of an exhausted phenotype, characterized by elevated expression of inhibitory receptors such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3) and reduced production of effector cytokines (8). These observations suggest that JAK/STAT signaling represents a critical link between tumor-derived signals and T cell dysfunction.

Despite these insights, few studies have systematically investigated whether pancreatic cancer-derived exosomes exploit the JAK/STAT pathway to induce CD8+ T cell exhaustion and thereby facilitate immune escape. Existing research has largely focused either on the general immunosuppressive effects of exosomes or on the oncogenic functions of JAK/STAT signaling in tumor cells. The integration of these two aspects into a coherent mechanistic framework remains incomplete and represents an important gap in current knowledge.

This study investigated the potential of pancreatic cancer-derived exosomes to activate the JAK/STAT pathway and contribute to functional exhaustion of CD8+ T cells. Through molecular characterization, functional assays, and immune analyses, this work aimed to delineate a potential regulatory axis within the pancreatic cancer microenvironment. Clarifying this mechanism may deepen our understanding of tumor immunology and provide a rationale for the development of therapeutic strategies targeting exosome-mediated immune suppression. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-1-2903/rc).

Methods

Cell culture and experimental grouping

The human PANC-1 cells (ATCC, USA; Cat. No. CRL-1469) were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; Cat. No. 11965-092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco; Cat. No. 10099-141), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco; Cat. No. 15140-122) at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Exosomes were collected from culture medium through differential ultracentrifugation using an Optima™ XPN-100 Ultracentrifuge (Beckman Coulter, USA) with a SW41Ti rotor. The exosome concentration was determined by BCA protein assay and adjusted to 50 µg/mL for all functional assays. To minimize batch-to-batch variability, all functional assays were performed using exosomes pooled from three independent preparations. The JAK/STAT pathway inhibitor AG490 (Selleck Chemicals, USA; Cat. No. S1143) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA; Cat. No. D8418) and applied at a final concentration of 10 µM. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) used in this study were commercially obtained from pancreatic cancer donors through Mile-stone Biological Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). CD8+ T lymphocytes were subsequently isolated using CD8 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany; Cat. No. 130-045-201). No direct patient recruitment, sample collection, or clinical data acquisition was involved in this study. For functional studies in tumor cells, PANC-1 cells were assigned to three conditions: untreated control, exosome treatment, and AG490 pretreatment followed by exosome exposure. For CD8+ T-cell functional assays, PANC-1 cells were first treated with PBS, exosomes, or AG490 plus exosomes, and were then co-cultured with isolated CD8+ T cells at a ratio of 1:5 for 24 h. This co-culture system was used to evaluate whether exosome-pretreated tumor cells influenced CD8+ T-cell exhaustion, cytokine secretion, and proliferative function.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

Exosome pellets obtained by ultracentrifugation were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). A small volume of the suspension was placed on carbon-coated copper grids (Ted Pella, Cat. No. 01814-F), fixed with 2% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. 158127), and negatively stained with 2% uranyl acetate (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Cat. No. 22400). After air-drying, samples were visualized under a transmission electron microscope (Tecnai G2 Spirit, FEI T12, 80 kV), revealing vesicles of 30–150 nm with a cup-shaped morphology characteristic of exosomes.

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA)

Using a NanoSight NS300 instrument (Malvern Panalytical), NTA was performed to measure exosome size and concentration. Exosome suspensions were diluted in sterile PBS to achieve a particle concentration suitable for measurement. The particle concentration was calculated as particles/mL and used for normalization across independent exosome preparations. Each sample was measured in triplicate, and the coefficient of variation (CV) for particle concentration between batches was less than 15%. Samples were injected into the analysis chamber, and videos were recorded under controlled flow conditions. Videos were captured and analyzed by NTA software (NanoSight NTA 3.4, Malvern) to calculate the mean particle size and concentration.

Western blotting

Western blotting was performed to assess the expression of exosomal markers and JAK/STAT signaling proteins. Exosome pellets and cellular samples were lysed with RIPA buffer (Beyotime, Cat. No. P0013B) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Bimake, Cat. No. B14001, B15001). Protein concentrations were determined using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 23225). Equal amounts of protein (20–30 µg) were resolved by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Cat. No. IPVH00010). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST), membranes were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with primary antibodies specific for CD63 (Abcam, ab134045), tumor susceptibility gene 101 (TSG101, Abcam, ab125011), Calnexin (Abcam, ab92573), phosphorylated Janus kinase 1 (p-JAK1, Abcam, ab138005), Janus kinase 1 (JAK1, Abcam, ab133666), phosphorylated signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (p-STAT3, Abcam, ab267373), and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3, Abcam, ab68153). After washing, membranes were treated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG; Cell Signaling Technology 7074, 7076) for 1 h at room temperature. Detection was performed with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) (Thermo Fisher Scientific 32106) and visualization on a ChemiDocTM MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). For signaling analysis, the levels of p-JAK1 and p-STAT3 were normalized to their respective total proteins (JAK1 and STAT3). For exosome marker analysis, equal amounts of protein were loaded to assess the presence or absence of specific markers. Uncropped full-length blot images for both marker and signaling proteins are provided in the supplementary material (https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/10.21037tcr-2025-1-2903-1.pdf).

Exosome uptake assay

Exosomes were labeled with PKH26 dye (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. PKH26GL) for 5 min, and the reaction was quenched with 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. A7906). After washing with PBS (Gibco, Cat. No. 10010-023) and ultracentrifugation to remove free dye, labeled exosomes (50 µg/mL) were incubated with PANC-1 cells or CD8+ T cells for 12 h. After fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. 158127) and counterstaining with 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Beyotime, Cat. No. C1005), the cells were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMi8, Cat. No. 11525295). Intracellular red puncta indicated exosome uptake.

Cell viability assay [Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8)]

Each well of 96-well flat-bottom plates (Corning, Cat. No. 3599) was seeded with 3×103 PANC-1 cells in 100 µL complete medium and maintained overnight for adhesion. Cells were then subjected to the indicated treatments (untreated control, exosome (50 µg/mL) exposure, or AG490 pre-exposure followed by exosomes). At 24, 48, and 72 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent (Dojindo, Cat. No. CK04) was added to each well and incubated for 1–2 h at 37 °C protected from light. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a multimode microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices, Cat. No. 0200-1017). Medium-only wells were used for background subtraction. Each condition was assayed in technical triplicate.

To determine a suitable working concentration of AG490, PANC-1 cells and isolated CD8+ T cells were treated with AG490 at 5, 10, or 20 µM for up to 72 h, and cell viability was assessed using the CCK-8 assay. Based on these results, 10 µM was selected for subsequent experiments.

Wound healing assay

PANC-1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates (Corning, Cat. No. 3516) and cultured to near confluence. A linear wound was introduced using a sterile 200-µL pipette tip, and debris was removed by PBS wash (Gibco, Cat. No. 10010-023). Cells were maintained in DMEM with 1% FBS, with or without exosomes (50 µg/mL) or AG490 (10 µM), and images at 0 and 24 h were captured using a Leica DMi8 microscope (Leica, Cat. No. 11525295). Wound closure was quantified with ImageJ software [National Institutes of Health (NIH)] and expressed as the percentage of the initial wound area.

Transwell invasion assay

PANC-1 invasion assays were performed in 24-well Transwell inserts (Corning, Cat. No. 3422; 8.0-µm pore size) pre-coated with Matrigel (Corning, Cat. No. 356234). Cells (2×104–5×104) in serum-free DMEM were seeded into the upper chamber, and DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS was added to the lower chamber. Following 24–48 h incubation, with or without exosomes (50 µg/mL) or AG490 (10 µM), non-invading cells were wiped off, while invaded cells were fixed (4% paraformaldehyde, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. 158127), stained (0.1% crystal violet, Sigma-Aldrich, Cat. No. C0775), and imaged using a Leica DMi8 microscope (Leica, Cat. No. 11525295).

Flow cytometry analysis of CD8+ T cells

Flow cytometry was applied to assess both CD8+ T cell purity and exhaustion markers. Cells were stained with anti-CD45-FITC (BioLegend, Cat. No. 304006), anti-CD3-APC (BioLegend, Cat. No. 300412), anti-CD8α-PE (BioLegend, Cat. No. 301008), and a viability dye (Invitrogen, Cat. No. 65-0865-14). For exhaustion analysis, anti-PD-1-APC (BioLegend, Cat. No. 329908) and anti-TIM-3-PE (BioLegend, Cat. No. 345006) were used. Samples were acquired on a BD LSRFortessa™ cytometer (BD Biosciences, Cat. No. 658251) and analyzed with FlowJo v10 (BD). Results were expressed as CD45⁺ CD3⁺ CD8⁺ purity and the percentages of PD-1⁺, TIM-3⁺, and PD-1⁺TIM-3⁺ subsets.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Culture supernatants from CD8+ T cell co-cultures were collected, cleared by centrifugation (10,000 ×g, 10 min) and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Human tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) concentrations were quantified using DuoSet ELISA kits (R&D Systems; TNF-α, Cat. No. DY210; IFN-γ, Cat. No. DY285) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were assayed alongside serial standards (duplicate wells), with appropriate dilutions to fall within the linear range. Absorbance was read at 450 nm with 570 nm reference on a SpectraMax iD3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, Cat. No. 0200-1017). Cytokine levels (pg/mL) were calculated from a 4-parameter logistic standard curve after background subtraction.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates. For each biological replicate, three technical replicates were included. Results are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Batch-to-batch variability was assessed by comparing exosome preparations from three independent isolations, and no significant differences were observed in particle concentration or functional activity. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 23 (IBM Corp., USA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. Comparisons between two or more groups were conducted using Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test, respectively. A two-tailed P<0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Characterization of exosomes derived from PANC-1 cells

TEM revealed that the isolated vesicles exhibited a typical cup-shaped morphology, consistent with exosomes (Figure 1A). NTA showed that these vesicles displayed a size distribution with a major peak at approximately 110 nm (Figure 1B). Western blotting confirmed enrichment of the exosomal markers CD63 and TSG101 in the exosome fraction, whereas the endoplasmic reticulum marker Calnexin was detected in the parental PANC-1 lysate but not in the exosome fraction, supporting the identity and relative purity of the isolated vesicles (Figure 1C). In the uptake assay, prominent red fluorescence was observed in the cytoplasm of PANC-1 cells incubated with PKH26-labeled exosomes, whereas only minimal background fluorescence was detected in the PKH26 dye-only control group, indicating effective internalization of exosomes by PANC-1 cells (Figure 1D).

(D) Uptake of PKH26-labeled exosomes by PANC-1 cells. Red fluorescence was observed in the exosome-treated group, whereas minimal signal was detected in the PKH26 dye-only control. Effects of pancreatic cancer-derived exosomes on PANC-1 cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and JAK/STAT pathway

Before functional experiments, the effect of AG490 alone on cell viability was evaluated in PANC-1 cells and isolated CD8+ T cells. AG490 at 5 and 10 µM had minimal effects on the viability of both cell types over 72 h, whereas 20 µM significantly reduced viability, particularly in CD8+ T cells. Therefore, 10 µM was selected for subsequent experiments to minimize nonspecific cytotoxicity (Figure S1). The CCK-8 assay showed that exosome treatment significantly increased PANC-1 cell viability at 72 h compared with the control group, whereas AG490 pretreatment effectively reversed this effect (P<0.001; Figure 2A). In wound healing assays, exosome-treated cells exhibited a markedly higher migration rate than control cells, and this increase was significantly attenuated by AG490 pretreatment (P<0.001; Figure 2B). Similarly, Transwell assays showed that exosome treatment significantly increased the number of invaded cells, whereas AG490 pretreatment reduced this effect (P<0.001; Figure 2C). Western blot analysis further showed that exosome treatment increased the ratios of p-JAK1/JAK1 and p-STAT3/STAT3, while total JAK1 and STAT3 levels remained relatively unchanged. These increases were significantly suppressed by AG490 pretreatment (P<0.001; Figure 2D).

Uptake of pancreatic cancer-derived exosomes by CD8+ T cells

Flow cytometry confirmed the successful isolation of CD8+ T cells. After sequential gating of singlets and lymphocytes, more than 90% of cells were identified as CD45+ CD3+ T cells, and the final CD8+ T-cell purity reached 99.6% (Figure 3A). Fluorescence microscopy further revealed prominent red puncta within the cytoplasm of CD8+ T cells following incubation with PKH26-labeled exosomes, whereas minimal signal was observed in the dye-only control group. These findings indicate that CD8+ T cells are capable of internalizing PANC-1-derived exosomes, suggesting that direct exosome-T-cell interaction may occur within the co-culture system (Figure 3B).

(B) Representative fluorescence images of CD8+ T cells incubated with PKH26-labeled exosomes (red) or PKH26 dye-only control. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (blue). Scale bars: 50 µm. Each experiment included three biological replicates (n=3), with three technical replicates per biological replicate. DAPI, 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole.

Effects of exosome pretreatment of PANC-1 cells on CD8+ T-cell exhaustion and function in the co-culture system

In the co-culture system, CD8+ T cells cultured with exosome-pretreated PANC-1 cells showed significantly increased proportions of PD-1+, TIM-3+, and PD-1+TIM-3+ subsets compared with the control co-culture group (all P<0.001; Figure 4A,4B). In contrast, co-culture with AG490-pretreated PANC-1 cells significantly reduced these proportions compared with the exosome-pretreated group (P<0.01 for PD-1+ cells; P<0.001 for TIM-3+ and PD-1+ TIM-3+ cells; Figure 4A,4B). ELISA analysis further showed that, in the co-culture system, CD8+ T cells cultured with exosome-pretreated PANC-1 cells exhibited significantly decreased TNF-α and IFN-γ secretion compared with the control co-culture group, whereas co-culture with AG490-pretreated PANC-1 cells partially restored cytokine production (both P<0.001; Figure 4C). Furthermore, carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE)-based proliferation assays demonstrated that CD8+ T cells co-cultured with exosome-pretreated PANC-1 cells showed a significantly lower proliferation rate than those in the control co-culture group, and this effect was partially reversed in the AG490-pretreated co-culture group (P<0.01; Figure 4D).

Discussion

In this study, we found that pancreatic cancer-derived exosomes enhanced the malignant behavior of PANC-1 cells and were associated with exhaustion-related changes in CD8+ T cells in a tumor cell-T cell co-culture system. These effects were accompanied by activation of JAK/STAT signaling in PANC-1 cells, and AG490 treatment partially alleviated both the malignant phenotype of tumor cells and the immunosuppressive changes observed in co-cultured CD8+ T cells. Together, these findings suggest that exosome-mediated JAK/STAT signaling may contribute to the establishment of an immunosuppressive microenvironment in pancreatic cancer. Unlike previous studies focusing separately on exosomal cargoes or JAK/STAT signaling, our study supports a potential link among tumor-derived exosomes, JAK/STAT activation, and CD8⁺ T-cell dysfunction within the context of tumor cell-immune cell interaction.

Tumor-derived exosomes have increasingly been recognized as essential vehicles for intercellular communication in the TME (11). Previous work revealed that exosomes from breast, gastric, and lung cancers can transfer oncogenic proteins, microRNAs, and other cargos to recipient cells, thereby promoting proliferation, migration, invasion, and angiogenesis (12). In pancreatic cancer, exosomes have been reported to facilitate desmoplastic stroma formation and drive resistance to chemotherapy (13). Our results are consistent with these observations, showing that exosome treatment of PANC-1 cells markedly increased cell proliferation, migration, and invasion. These phenotypic changes were reduced when JAK/STAT signaling was pharmacologically inhibited, suggesting that exosome-mediated oncogenic effects are closely associated with intracellular signaling cascades. Compared with other solid tumors, pancreatic cancer exosomes appear particularly effective in reshaping the TME, which may contribute to the aggressive clinical course of the disease (14).

The JAK/STAT pathway is a canonical cytokine signaling cascade that plays pivotal roles in tumorigenesis and immune regulation (15). Aberrant activation of STAT3 has been detected in more than half of pancreatic cancers and has been associated with enhanced tumor growth, epithelial-mesenchymal transition, and chemoresistance (16). Our findings provide further evidence that exosomes derived from pancreatic cancer cells induce strong phosphorylation of JAK1 and STAT3 in recipient cells, while total protein levels remain unchanged, suggesting that exosomes primarily influence post-translational activation rather than protein abundance. Previous work has indicated that tumor exosomes can deliver interleukin-6 (IL-6) family cytokines or microRNAs that regulate suppressors of cytokine signaling (SOCS), thereby augmenting STAT3 activity (17). The activation of JAK/STAT signaling observed here may therefore reflect the ability of pancreatic cancer exosomes to transfer bioactive cargos that modulate intracellular kinase activity. Potential mediators include specific microRNAs (e.g., miR-155) that target SOCS to activate the JAK/STAT pathway (18), cytokines such as IL-6, which is known to mediate malignant progression in pancreatic cancer (19), or exosomal programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) that suppresses T cell activity via JAK/STAT signaling (20). Similar observations have been made in hepatocellular carcinoma and glioblastoma, where exosome-induced STAT3 activation promoted cell survival and invasiveness (21).

A hallmark of pancreatic cancer is the profound dysfunction of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells within the TME (22). T-cell exhaustion is characterized by persistent expression of inhibitory receptors such as PD-1 and TIM-3, together with reduced effector cytokine secretion and impaired proliferative or cytotoxic capacity (23,24). In our co-culture experiments, CD8+ T cells cultured with exosome-pretreated PANC-1 cells showed increased proportions of PD-1⁺, TIM-3⁺, and PD-1⁺ TIM-3⁺ subsets, along with reduced TNF-α and IFN-γ secretion and impaired proliferative activity. These observations are consistent with previous reports showing that tumor-derived exosomes can impair T-cell activation and contribute to an immunosuppressive microenvironment (25). Importantly, because the functional assays were performed in a co-culture system with pretreated PANC-1 cells, the observed immune changes may reflect both direct exosome uptake by CD8+ T cells and indirect tumor cell-mediated effects. Our uptake assay demonstrated that CD8+ T cells were capable of internalizing PANC-1-derived exosomes, suggesting that direct exosome-T-cell interaction may occur within this system; however, the relative contribution of direct versus indirect mechanisms could not be fully distinguished in the present study.

The present findings suggest a potential exosome-JAK/STAT-CD8+ T-cell dysfunction axis with translational relevance. Targeting exosomes, including inhibition of their release, uptake, or bioactive cargos, has emerged as a promising strategy in multiple tumor models (26,27). In addition, JAK/STAT signaling represents a tractable therapeutic target in pancreatic cancer, and combining JAK/STAT inhibitors with immune checkpoint blockade may enhance T-cell function within the TME (28). Exosomes may also serve as accessible biomarkers in liquid biopsies for disease monitoring (29). Recent translational studies further support these strategies. For example, a first-in-human trial demonstrated that engineered exosomes carrying KRASG12D siRNA increased intratumoral CD8+ T cell infiltration in PDAC patients (30). Moreover, a triple combination therapy targeting KRAS, EGFR, and STAT3 induced complete tumor regression in preclinical PDAC models (31), while an exosome-based platform successfully reprogrammed the immunosuppressive TME (32). These findings highlight the emerging potential of targeting STAT3 signaling and exosome-mediated immune modulation in pancreatic cancer.

This study has several limitations. First, it was limited to an in vitro design and relied on a single pancreatic cancer cell line (PANC-1), which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, CD8+ T cells were isolated from commercially obtained human PBMCs rather than freshly collected patient-derived samples, which may reduce the direct clinical relevance of the immune-related findings. Therefore, further validation using additional cell lines, in vivo animal models (e.g., tail vein injection models), and well-characterized patient samples is required to substantiate the proposed exosome-JAK/STAT-T cell pathway. Moreover, the specific exosomal cargos responsible for activating JAK/STAT signaling were not identified. Future studies incorporating proteomic or transcriptomic analyses are needed to determine the key functional molecules (e.g., proteins, microRNAs, or long noncoding RNAs) involved. In addition, the relationships among exosomal content, STAT3 activation, T cell exhaustion, and clinical parameters (such as prognosis, tumor stage, and treatment response) were not evaluated and warrant further investigation in clinical samples. Furthermore, exosome uptake was assessed primarily by qualitative fluorescence microscopy without quantitative analysis. T cell functional assessment was limited to cytokine secretion and proliferation, while direct tumor-killing assays were not performed. Although exosome uptake by CD8+ T cells was confirmed, the co-culture system may involve indirect tumor cell-mediated effects. Future studies should incorporate cytotoxicity assays and purified exosome systems to better define CD8+ T cell function and distinguish direct from indirect mechanisms. In addition, direct measurement of p-STAT3 in CD8+ T cells was not performed and should be addressed in future work. Overall, further studies integrating in vivo validation, molecular cargo characterization, and clinical investigation are required to advance the translational potential of these findings.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that pancreatic cancer-derived exosomes promote proliferation, migration, and invasion of PANC-1 cells and are associated with CD8+ T-cell exhaustion-related changes in a tumor cell-T cell co-culture system. Exosome treatment enhanced JAK1/STAT3 signaling in PANC-1 cells, while CD8+ T cells co-cultured with exosome-pretreated PANC-1 cells exhibited increased PD-1 and TIM-3 expression, reduced TNF-α and IFN-γ secretion, and impaired proliferative capacity. These effects were partially reversed by JAK/STAT inhibition with AG490. However, this study is limited by its in vitro design, the use of a single cell line, and the lack of identification of specific exosomal cargos. In addition, the co-culture model does not fully distinguish direct exosome effects on CD8+ T cells from indirect tumor cell-mediated mechanisms. Further studies incorporating in vivo validation and molecular characterization of exosomal components are required. Overall, these findings suggest that targeting the exosome-JAK/STAT-CD8+ T-cell interaction axis may represent a potential therapeutic strategy, pending validation in animal models and clinical settings.
