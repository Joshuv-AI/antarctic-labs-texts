Seasonal changes in black carbon footprint on the Antarctic Peninsula due to rising shipborne tourism and forest fires
Authors: Newton Magalhães, Heitor Evangelista, Sérgio J. Gonçalves, Alexandre S. Alencar, Elaine Alves do Santos, Márcio Cataldo, Joseph R. McConnell, Renata Simões Silveira, Paul A. Mayewski, Mariusz Potocki, Jefferson C. Simões, Ricardo Jaña
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Refractory black carbon (rBC) has great potential to increase melting when deposited on snow and ice surfaces. Previous studies attributed sources and impacts of rBC in the northern Antarctic Peninsula region by investigating long-range atmospheric transport from South Hemisphere biomass burning and industrial regions or by assessing impacts from local tourism and research activities. We used high-resolution measurements of refractory rBC in a firn core collected near the northern tip of the Antarctic Peninsula, as well as atmospheric rBC from Modern-Era Retrospective Analysis for Research and Applications, Version 2, satellite measurements, modeling, burned area data, and tourism statistics, to assess combined impacts of both long-range transported rBC and locally emitted rBC. Our findings suggest that tourism activities have a regional rather than local impact and the increase in rBC concentrations during late spring-summer, influenced by tourism activities and fires in the Southern Hemisphere, can enhance ice melt. This highlights the need for strategies to reduce local and distant rBC emissions.

INTRODUCTION

Refractory black carbon (rBC), or soot, is a light-absorbing aerosol globally produced by the incomplete combustion of fossil fuels and biomass burning (1, 2). rBC is short-lived (3) with a residence time on the order of 5.5 days. However, despite its short residence time in the atmosphere, rBC microparticles can travel great distances and deposit far from their emission sources in both polar regions (4–6). Once deposited on snow and ice, rBC may cause surface melting due to its potential to reduce albedo, locally increasing the absorbance of solar radiation (7–10). Particularly in the maritime northern Antarctic Peninsula (nAP), we have been witnessing a change in rBC sources that contribute to the observed local concentrations: from the record attributed to the first human settlements in New Zealand in the early 14th century (11) to the detection of the vast fires coming from the Amazon Basin since the 1980s (6). The first detections of rBC in nAP were associated with human activities far away from the Antarctic continent. Presently, the impacts of in situ modern shipborne tourism and scientific logistics play a pivotal role (12). However, although it has been asserted that the predominant local rBC source in the nAP is fossil fuel combustion (12), the detection of emissions from the massive 2019–2020 wildfires in southeast Australia at the Marambio Station in Antarctic (13) demonstrates that several geographically disparate sources may contribute simultaneously to the total amount of rBC found in the nAP. Therefore, understanding how local and distant emissions determine the levels of rBC found in the nAP snow and ice, their interannual and seasonal variability, and their impacts on melting are vital for elaborating policies that can minimize the rBC-related degradation of the cryosphere.

In general, levels of rBC in Antarctica are relatively low compared to other continents because of its remote and isolated location (14, 15). However, relatively high rBC contents can be found close to sites of local human activities such as research stations (16) and tourism areas (12). Historically, the nAP has been one of the most intense zones for tourism and research activities during the summer season. Snow samples collected near research facilities and popular shore landing sites for tourists in the nAP (samples collected from the summers of 2016/2017 to 2019/2020) show rBC concentrations ranging between 1 and 8 ng/g (12), which are well above the background levels determined from the James Ross Island (JRI) ice core over the past 700 years, approximately 0.1 to 0.2 ng/g (11). In addition, the contribution of biomass burning from the surrounding continents to rBC concentrations in the nAP and the Weddell Sea Sector was recognized during the 1990s based on their common seasonal variability with the biomass-burning indicator non–sea salt potassium. Atmospheric rBC mostly peaks during the Austral spring/summer, aligning with the Southern Hemisphere biomass burning seasonality (6, 17). Spring/summer peaks of atmospheric rBC were observed at the South Pole (4, 18), Neumayer Station (70°40′28″S, 8°16′27″W) (19), and Comandante Ferraz Station (62°05′06″S, 58°24′12″W) (6). In general, models and ice core measurements for the Antarctic continent indicate a latitudinal gradient of rBC in snow and ice layers and in the atmosphere, with higher rBC concentrations in lower-latitude regions and lower concentrations in higher-latitude regions (19).

Currently, scientific research addressing the rBC issue in the nAP has primarily focused on human impacts by assuming local and distant source contributions separately. However, it is conceivable that rBC in the nAP has mixed sources, particularly with increasing tourism activity and extreme fire events on the surrounding continents. This study represents the first attempt to comprehensively address the current rBC source paradigm in the nAP. Using high-resolution rBC measurements from a 20-m shallow firn core retrieved from the Detroit Plateau (DP) near the northern tip of the Peninsula in December 2007, as well as atmospheric rBC data from Modern-Era Retrospective Analysis for Research and Applications, Version 2 (MERRA-2), our goal is to elucidate rBC sources through an integrated approach. This involves considering both local fossil fuel sources and distant biomass burning sources in the same context. While the DP coring site is characterized by high snow accumulation, thereby preventing evaluation of longer-term trends, it provided a very detailed record of the seasonal variability of rBC from the spring of 2003 to the summer of 2007/2008. The timing of tourism and research activities in summer, as well as biomass burning activities in Southern Africa in winter, North Australia in early spring, and South Australia and South America in mid-to-late spring (20), enables seasonal deconvolution of the sources and impacts of rBC on the region. The 2003 to 2008 span of the DP firn core record is of special interest since it encompasses periods of high rates of deforestation and fires in South America, including especially high fire activity in 2007, as well as the first tourism boom in Antarctica during the 2007/2008 summer.

Study area and firn core drilling location

The DP is located on the nAP (64°10′S, 60°0′W) (Fig. 1A), the region with the highest flux of rBC in the modern era (Fig. 1B) (21). It is relatively close to the southern tip of South America and near the regions where most tourism activities and extensive ship routes to Antarctica are located (fig. S1). Ship traffic in Antarctica is related closely to Antarctic tourism and station infrastructure, with passengers being transported to Antarctica on ships departing from southern South America. The DP straddles the Antarctic Peninsula with an average altitude of 1700 m above sea level and is the largest plateau in the Graham Land region. Because of its difficult accessibility, the region is almost completely isolated from human presence, making it an excellent site for studying regional pollution from rBC emissions. The firn core drill site was located at 64°05′07″S and 59°38′42″W at an elevation of 1937 m above sea level and ~100 km from the JRI ice core location. The coring was part of the Brazil-Chile-US joint drilling project CASA (Climate of the Antarctic and South America), which collected the 20-m-core object of this study and a 133-m-deep ice core (22).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

rBC concentration in the DP firn core

Hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) and rBC (23) were measured simultaneously in the 20-m DP firn core using the continuous ice core analytical system (24) at the Desert Research Institute (DRI). The core chronology was determined using the high-resolution profile of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) concentrations (see Materials and Methods) (22). The high rate of snow deposition at DP (4.5 to 5.0 m/year) enabled interannual dating of the firn core, where seasons were well established through maximum and minimum concentrations of H2O2 that occur around summer and winter solstices, respectively. This allowed us to precisely infer rBC concentrations in seasonal resolution by associating rBC variability with seasonal changes of H2O2 (Fig. 2A). rBC concentrations showed high intraannual variability with values ranging from 0.01 to 3.73 ng/g (Fig. 2A). Some rBC concentration peaks (mainly in the summer and spring) exceeded the modern regional background value (approximately 0.8 ng/g from the JRI ice core), with especially high rBC concentrations (>1.0 ng/g) observed in summer 2004/2005 (maximum rBC concentration of 1.4 ng/g), spring 2006 (maximum rBC concentration of 2.3 ng/g), spring 2007 (maximum rBC concentration of 3.7 ng/g), and summer 2007/2008 (maximum rBC concentration of 1.6 ng/g). The highest rBC concentrations were observed during the spring and summer, followed by the winter and the autumn (Fig. 2B). This seasonal pattern in the DP firn core is comparable to the seasonality of atmospheric rBC concentrations monitored at King George Island (~120 km north of the Antarctic Peninsula) (6). rBC concentrations showed higher values for the past 2 years of analysis (2006 and 2007), indicating increased rBC deposition (Fig. 2C), predominately reflecting the spring-summer deposition rather than autumn-winter (Fig. 2D).

Seasonal sources of rBC to DP

Autumn and winter

To better clarify roles of different rBC sources during different seasons, we compared seasonal variabilities for burned area in the Southern Hemisphere [data from Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) Global Burned Area Product, Collection 6) and tourism activities from 2003 to 2008 (data from 26). Autumn is the season with the lowest burned area in the Southern Hemisphere (Fig. 3, A to F), with almost no tourism activities (Fig. 3G) (25), coincident with the lowest seasonal rBC concentration levels in the ice core record (Fig. 2). During winter, no tourism activities occurred in Antarctica (Fig. 3G). However, biomass burning was high in southern Africa, with some fires also occurring in southern South America (Fig. 3, B and E).

Seasonal variations in burned area across the Southern Hemisphere and length of Antarctic tourism season from 2003 to 2008.

(A) Amazon Basin. (B) Southern South America. (C) Australia and New Zealand. (D) Central Africa. (E) Southern Africa. (F) Entire Southern Hemisphere. (G) Length of Antarctic tourism season from 2003 to 2008 as documented by Lynch et al. (25).

Analysis of the global Ultraviolet Aerosol Index (UVAI) data (https://www.temis.nl/index.php) for the period from 2003 to 2008 confirmed that no important sources of rBC emissions in the Southern Hemisphere exist during autumn (fig. S2). For winter, the analysis of the global UVAI showed high UVAI values in the west coast of southern Africa, suggesting this region as an important emission source of rBC (fig. S2). Hierarchical cluster analysis (using the Euclidian distance and Ward’s method), correlation analysis, and principal components analysis (PCA) confirmed this hypothesis showing relatively higher correlations and a closer association between autumn and winter rBC concentrations and southern Africa burned area (Fig. 4 and fig. S3).

Hierarchical cluster analysis and correlation matrix between combined autumn and winter rBC concentrations in the DP firn core and burned area (B.A.) by Southern Hemisphere continent, as well as Antarctic tourism activities.

Spring and summer

For spring, burned area in the Southern Hemisphere was high in the Amazon Basin (September to October), southern South America (September), and southern Africa (August to September) (Fig. 3, A, B, and E, respectively). At the same time, tourism was not active during the spring seasons of 2003, 2004, and 2005 but started in late October 2007 and 2008 (Fig. 3G) (25). Tourism activities in Antarctica not only increased from 2003 to 2008 but also started earlier each year (25). Tourism activity levels during the spring seasons of 2006 and 2007 were higher than in previous years but still low (fig. S4), which means that biomass burning from surrounding continents was probably the main rBC source, although a combined source is possible for 2006 and 2007.

For summer, burned area was high in Australia and New Zealand (November to December) and central Africa (January to February) (Fig. 3, C and E). At the same time, shipborne tourism activities were at their maximum in the nAP (fig. S4) (25). We found that from 2003/2004 to 2007/2008 summers, rBC deposited at the DP site increased concomitantly with the increase in the number of shipborne tourists (Fig. 5). Furthermore, rBC concentrations showed a statistically significant correlation (r = 0.95, 95% confidence interval, n = 5) with the number of large-ship tourists (Fig. 5).

Summer rBC concentration in the DP firn core compared with the number of shipborne tourists in the Antarctic Peninsula.

Our multivariate approach using hierarchical cluster analysis, correlation matrix analysis, and PCA showed a high correlation and close association between spring-summer rBC concentration and Amazon Basin area burned (r = 0.75), number of passengers (r = 0.87), numbers of ship voyages (r = 0.84), and number of passengers/cruise only (r = 0.99) (Fig. 6 and fig. S5) pointing to Amazon Basin biomass burning and summer shipborne tourism as the main sources of rBC to the nAP during the spring-summer seasons.

Hierarchical cluster and correlation matrix analysis between combined spring and summer rBC concentrations in the DP firn core and corresponding Southern Hemisphere burned area by continent, as well as Antarctic tourism activities.

Air mass back trajectory analyses using the HYSPLIT/NOAA (National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration) model indicate linkages between South American biomass burning emissions and nAP atmospheric and precipitation chemistry consistent with other atmospheric transport and deposition modeling (11, 21), with most of the air mass trajectories passing through the west side of the Antarctic Peninsula where most of the shipborne tourism activities occurred (fig. S6). Although local shipborne tourism can explain summer rBC concentration variability, spring rBC concentrations are more difficult to apportion since they depend not only on rBC emission but also on favorable conditions for atmospheric transport from South America to the nAP. The 2007 fire season was one of the most intense since the start of satellite monitoring in terms of burned area and active fire number in the Amazon Basin (fig. S7A). For the same year, the DP firn core record exhibited the highest rBC concentration peak (3.7 ng/g) during the spring. Concentrations in the DP firn core showed a statistically significant correlation (r > 0.75, 95% confidence interval, n = 5) with UVAI in the Amazon Basin (region of the deforestation arch) and Patagonia (fig. S7B), indicating these two regions as potential sources. We investigated climatological data and identified at least one period in September that coincides with the peak of the Amazon Basin fire season, exhibiting favorable smoke transport from South America to the Antarctic Peninsula. This occurred specifically from 17 to 21 September 2007. During this period, a low-pressure center had formed in the Bellingshausen-Amundsen Sea, leading to a cyclonic atmospheric circulation that strengthened the meridional wind component. As a result, there was a southward migration of air masses from South America to the Antarctic Peninsula (fig. S8). During the same period, 22,470 active fires were detected in South America by the satellite survey (fig. S9). Furthermore, elevated smoke was identified over both South America and the Antarctic Peninsula through the classification of aerosol subtypes using CALIPSO (Cloud-Aerosol Lidar Infrared Pathfinder Satellite Observations) Lidar profiles (figs. S10 to S14).

Seasonal relative contribution (%) to annual rBC concentrations

On the basis of the methods presented below, we inferred the relative amounts of rBC deposited each season at the DP site and their most probable origin. For 2004, the winter and spring seasons dominated the annual rBC concentration, contributing 35.1 and 34.3%, respectively, with 26.0 and 4.6% in summer and autumn, respectively (Fig. 7A). Spring concentrations dominated the annual rBC concentrations in 2005, 2006, and 2007, contributing 45.2, 70, and 74.5%, respectively (Fig. 7, B to D). During the same period from 2005 to 2007, the summer season was the second largest contributor, representing 33.1, 21.0, and 15.7% (Fig. 7, B to D). For 2005, 2006, and 2007, winter concentrations represented 15, 1.7, and 6.7%, while autumn represented 6.7, 7.3, and 3.1%, respectively. From the assessment of relative seasonal contributions, it is possible to confirm that the annual rBC concentration in the DP firn core from 2003 to 2007 was determined largely by the combined spring and summer signals, implicating biomass burning in South America and local human activities as the main sources of rBC at the DP site.

Comparison between annual rBC concentration in the DP and JRI cores

rBC concentrations in the DP firn core were twice as high as those in the JRI ice core for 2003 and 2004. In addition, the rBC concentrations were three times higher for 2005, five times higher for 2006, and nine times higher for 2007 (Fig. 8, A and B). The DP firn core was collected approximately 100 km from the JRI ice core and is closer than JRI to regions where a notable amount of ship-based tourism takes place (Fig. 8C). The differences in rBC concentrations between the two cores for 2004 and 2005 may be attributed to the proximity to tourism areas, as rBC concentrations during the summer substantially contribute to the annual average concentration for these 2 years. Air mass trajectories simulated by the HYSPLIT/NOAA model indicate that during summer, most of the back trajectories arriving at the DP site originated from the region with the highest density of ship-based visitors (Fig. 8D). We analyzed the spatial distribution of the number of visitors on cruise ships and engaging in activities on small motorized boats and compared the results with the rBC data from the DP firn core. Statistically significant correlations were observed primarily in regions surrounding DP (see fig. S15). These regions have shown increased tourism activities in recent years.

(C) Spatial pattern of tourism activities in the Antarctic Peninsula from 2003/2004 to 2007/2008 including the total number of visitors, cruise ship visitors, and small boat visitors. As tourism activities in the region intensified in the 2007/2008 summer, including the influx of visitors, ship cruises, and small boats, it is reasonable to assume that the emissions of rBC and other pollutants also increased. During the coring activity, clean atmospheric samples collected in the summer of 2007/2008 at the DP site confirmed the presence of volatile organic compounds of petrogenic origin (data S1). These volatile organic compounds included butane, (S)-2-hydroxypropanoic acid, cyclopropane, acetone, dichloromethane, cyclopropane 1,1-dimethyl, cyclopropane ethyl, 1-butanol 3-methyl, propane 1-(ethenyloxy)-2-methyl, pentane 3-methyl, cyclopentane methyl, benzene, cyclohexane methyl, 1-octanol, hexane 2,4-dimethyl, benzene 1,2-dimethyl-(o-xylene), nonane, and benzene 1,2,4-trimethyl. These findings further support the notion that tourism-related activities in the vicinity of the DP site contributed to the presence of anthropogenic pollutants in the local/regional atmosphere and snow deposits.

rBC impact on snow and ice melt

Although rBC concentrations were sometimes higher during the spring in the nAP, this season is characterized by low air temperatures at that site. Therefore, any rBC contribution to melting of nAP snow and ice would likely occur during the summer (December to February) when daily air temperatures often approach 0°C (fig. S16). To investigate any effect from this process, we assessed the maximum potential impact of rBC on snow melt during the summer season using the highest rBC concentration of 1.6 parts per billion (ppb) observed in the DP record during this season (see Materials and Methods). Simulations using the Snow, Ice, and Aerosol Radiation (SNICAR) model (26) indicate that average albedo reduction of 0.4% may occur from the concentration of 1.6 ppb (fig. S17). Although snow albedo was higher in the east side of the Antarctic Peninsula than in the west side (Fig. 9A), solar radiation was higher on the east side (Fig. 9B). Consequently, the regional impact of rBC was higher on the east side (Fig. 9, C and D). The maximum increment in absorbed surface radiation due to rBC was ~0.6 W/m2, which resulted in a maximum potential enhanced snow melt of 150 g/m2 per day. This estimate was derived using a simplified model that assumes persistent melting conditions throughout the summer. While there is inherent uncertainty in the estimate, it suggests that rBC could contribute to increased melt in the region.

Potential impact of local rBC emissions from shipborne tourism activities on summer snow melt during 2007/2008 based on MERRA-2 reanalyzes and SNICAR model simulations.

(A) Broadband shortwave (SW) albedo averaged for December, January, and February (DJF). (B) Downwelling shortwave all-sky irradiance. (C) Increment in radiative forcing caused by rBC. (D) Potential maximum snow melt caused by rBC.

To evaluate the performance of our model results, we assessed daily snow/ice melting from November 2007 to March 2008 using satellite passive-microwave observations (Fig. 10) and found that between 1 November 2007 and 15 March 2008, a maximum of 45 days of surface melting occurred. The spatial pattern of melting was similar to the radiative forcing and maximum potential snow melt caused by rBC in our model. Specifically, the east side of the Antarctic Peninsula experienced more melt days than the West side (Fig. 10A). One possible reason is the influence of cloud cover on the shortwave radiation that reaches the surface. Cloud fraction data from MODIS showed a higher cloud fraction on the west side of the Peninsula than on the east (fig. S18A). The average cloud cover fraction in areas that experienced melting from 1 November 2007 to 26 February 2008 was 72%, and the minimum cloud cover fraction for the same area was 27% (fig. S18B). Daily minimum cloud cover fraction and melt extent exhibited statistically significant negative correlation (r = −0.42, 99% confidence interval, n = 87). The relationship between cloud cover and melt extent highlights the role of solar radiation in the melting process because less cloud cover means more direct solar radiation reaching the snow and ice surface. Consequently, rBC deposited in snow/ice under low cloud/clear sky conditions may amplify melting. Substantial melting episodes were detected from late November to March when temperatures were close to 0°C (Fig. 10B). Air temperature and melt extent exhibited a statistically significant positive correlation (r = 0.36, 99% confidence interval, n = 87), although this accounted for only 13% of melt extent variance (r2 = 0.13). To clarify the relationship among melt extent, temperature, and cloud cover, we analyzed three episodes of peak melt extent that occurred on 20 December 2007, 3 January 2008, and 25 January 2008 (Fig. 10C).

(A) Total melt days for the Antarctic Ice Sheet from 1 November 2007 to 15 March 2008. Yellow rectangles depict regions where summer melt ponds and shelf breakup occurred in 2007/2008. (B) Air temperature every 6 hours at Vernadsky Station [represented in (A) as a yellow star]. (C) Daily melt extent (in square kilometers), daily mean air temperature at Vernadsky Station, and MODIS cloud fraction from November 2007 to March 2008.

Average daily air temperatures at the Vernadsky Station on 20 December 2007 and 3 January 2008 were −0.16° and −0.12°C (with maximum positive temperatures of 0.8° and 0.7°C), respectively. On both days, melt was detected on the Larsen C Ice Shelf (figs. S19 and S20A), and meteorological data from the National Centers for Environmental Prediction (NCEP) and the National Center for Atmospheric Research (NCAR) reanalyses showed daily mean surface air and sea surface temperatures below 0°C (figs. S19 and S20, B and C), with minimum cloud covers of 5 and 19%, respectively. The observations suggest that solar radiation played an important role in melting during the events, providing sufficient energy to melt snow/ice when the surface air temperature was just below or above 0°C.According to our model (Fig. 9), the amount of energy absorbed could have been amplified by relatively high concentrations of rBC in the summer of 2007/2008 resulting from increased shipborne tourism activities. On the other hand, the melting peak event on 25 January 2008 was more geographically distributed (see fig. S21A) and likely caused by the abrupt increase in air temperature associated with displacement of an atmospheric river from the Pacific Ocean toward the Antarctic Peninsula (fig. S21, A to D). The average daily air temperature at Vernadsky Station on 25 January 2008 was 4.0°C, with the maximum temperature reaching 6.0°C.

Increasing trend in late spring-summer atmospheric rBC and tourism activities

The MERRA-2 data from 1991 to 2022 show an increased trend in atmospheric rBC concentration at the Antarctic Peninsula for the late spring-summer (period from November to February) (fig. S22A). The spatial pattern of atmospheric rBC concentration depicts a more prominent increase in the Antarctic Peninsula’s west side, which coincides with the location of tourism activities (fig. S21A). In summary, the late spring-summer atmospheric rBC concentration at the Antarctic Peninsula from 1991 to 2022 can be divided into four periods (fig. S22B): (i) 1991 to 2003 when low atmospheric rBC concentrations coincided with low tourism activities in Antarctica; (ii) 2003 to 2011 when increased atmospheric rBC concentrations coincided with increased tourism activities in the Antarctic Peninsula, whereas late spring-summer burned areas in South America, Africa, and Australia showed no significant trend; (iii) 2011 to 2016 when a slight increase in atmospheric rBC concentrations coincided with increased tourism activities, and no trend was observed for burned areas in South America, Africa, and Australia; and (iv) 2016 to 2023 when burned area still showed no positive trends, but tourism in Antarctica notably increased, and atmospheric rBC concentrations also increased. It is interesting to observe that during the period from 2016 to 2023, there was a prominent increase in atmospheric rBC concentration during the 2019–2020 late spring-summer, which coincided with a second peak in tourism activities and also with the 2019–2020 megafire in Australia. The 2019–2020 megafire in Australia was the one of the most intense in terms of biomass burning aerosols emission (27). It was observed that the smoke arrived in Antarctica in January (28) and the aerosol load in the atmosphere persisted until March (28). On the other hand, during the 2020–2021 late spring-summer, there were no tourism activities in Antarctica due to the COVID-19 pandemic. The atmospheric rBC concentration for December-January-February (DJF) dropped, and values were the lowest since 2001 (fig. S23).

In addition, we conducted correlation and clustering analyses for late spring-summer atmospheric rBC (from MERRA-2), tourism, and burned area datasets spanning the period from 2002 to 2023 (n = 21) (fig. S24). We found statistically significant correlation between atmospheric rBC and the number of tourists (all tourists) (r = 0.48, 95% confidence interval, n = 21), the number of all ship voyages (r = 0.36, 90% confidence interval, n = 21), and burned area in Australia (r = 0.36, 90% confidence interval, n = 21). Clustering analysis also points to a close association between atmospheric rBC, tourism activities, and burned area in Australia (see fig. S24). The stronger correlation between atmospheric rBC and the total number of tourists probably occur because when the number of tourists increases, others activities that consume diesel and emits rBC, such as motorized boat excursions, also increase.

Late spring-summer atmospheric rBC has been increasing since the rise of tourism activities (see fig. S25). However, not only are atmospheric rBC concentrations increasing, but also the seasonality is changing. After approximately 2015–2016, late spring-summer rBC concentration levels have been close to spring levels (see fig. S24). We assessed the maximum atmospheric rBC concentration for each year from 1992 to 2023 (see table S1) and observed that from 1992 to 2003, rBC concentration peaks occurred in September and October, which corresponds to the peak of biomass burning in South America. From 2004 to 2015, rBC concentration peaks occurred in October and November. In addition, from 2016 to 2023, some peaks occurred during the summer (in January for 2016, in December for 2018, in February for 2020, and in January for 2022). For 2021, when no tourism activities occurred because of the COVID-19 pandemic, the atmospheric rBC peak occurred in September. The most likely explanation is that the increase in rBC emissions from tourism activities (typically occurring from November to March) had been mixing with rBC emissions from fires in Australia, thereby boosting BC concentrations during the late spring-summer and causing the observed changes in atmospheric rBC seasonality.

It is important to highlight that another tourism boom occurred in Antarctica during the summer of 2022–2023. The number of visitors to Antarctica increased from 34,354 in the 2007/2008 season to 74,381 in 2019/2020 and 103,988 in 2022/2023. That is a growth of 216% compared to the values of 2007/2008 and 140% compared to 2019/2020. Despite that new record in the number of visitors to Antarctica, atmospheric rBC concentration in the 2022–2023 summer (DJF) was lower than in the 2019–2020 summer (DJF) (fig. S24), pointing to the large influence that an extreme fire event can have on rBC concentrations in the nAP.

Integrated discussion

Early 21st-century model simulations provided the first insights about carbonaceous microparticles reaching nAP. These simulations indicated that the nAP was primarily influenced by South American biomass burning emissions (6, 29), with some contribution from South African fires (30). This assertion was supported by the observed seasonal peaks of rBC in the atmosphere from diverse sites in Antarctica, such as the Antarctic Peninsula (6), East Antarctica (17, 19) and the South Pole (18). Improvements in air mass back trajectories modeling further contributed to this postulation (30). Our study based on the high-resolution record from the 20-m DP firn core and atmospheric BC concentration from MERRA-2 shows a changing seasonal pattern and diverse rBC sources affecting the Antarctic Peninsula. These patterns align closely with the seasonal fluctuations in biomass-burning activity around the Southern Hemisphere and tourism activities.

During the 2003 to 2008 summers, we observed an increase in rBC concentration concomitant with the increase in maritime tourism activities in the Antarctic Peninsula. Both statistical and multivariate analyses, as well as analysis of air mass backward trajectories, substantiate attribution the increased summer rBC to tourism. Comparison between rBC concentrations in the DP and nearby JRI cores further underscores the impact of tourism-related activities. Notably, the rBC concentration measured in the DP firn core was higher than that of the JRI ice core during the same time period, and this discrepancy can at least partly be explained by the proximity of the DP firn core to maritime tourism activities that increased substantially between the 2003/2004 and 2007/2008 summers. Tourism statistics provided by the International Association of Antarctica Tour Operators (IAATO) from 25 seasons (1989/1990–2013/2014) show that passenger landings and marine traffic are highly concentrated at a few specific locations, particularly along the Peninsula’s southwestern coast (31). The duration of the nAP tourism season (defined as the number of days between the first Peninsula landing and the last) has increased as have passenger landings. The two longest seasons (173 and 175 days from first landing to last) were in 2007/2008 and 2008/2009 and were ~68 days longer than the shortest season (107 days) in 1995/1996 (31). The DP firn core records the consequences to rBC concentration in the nAP. Chemistry-climate models also point to those ships’ emissions (both aerosol BC and sulfurous) dominating anthropogenic pollution in Antarctica (32). A previous study claimed that, on average, each tourist expedition to Antarctica contributes to approximately 5.44 metric tons of CO2 emissions per passenger, equating to 0.49 metric tons per passenger per day, and around 70% of these emissions are associated with cruise activities (33). For rBC concentration in snow/ice, the impact of shipborne tourism was also evident. Given that carbon emissions from tourism primarily occur during the late spring to the end of the summer, a period characterized by rising temperatures and solar radiation peak, it becomes imperative to assess its repercussions on ice melt dynamics comprehensively. This evaluation holds intrinsic value in gaining insights into the potential implications of tourism-related emissions in the broader context of ice-melting processes. Our model outcomes reveal an average reduction of approximately 0.4% in albedo, corresponding to an increase in radiative forcing by approximately 0.6 W/m2, leading to a maximum potential snowmelt rate of 150 g/m2 per day during the summer. Our results align with local estimations of the impact of BC on the melting process, as detailed by Cordero et al. (12). In their study, Cordero and coauthors (12) estimated that rBC concentrations ranging from 1 to 4 ng/g (the last representing the highest rBC concentration within snow) could trigger a local radiative forcing of 0.25 to 1.00 W/m2, contributing to snowmelt ranging from 5 to 23 kg/m2, for the entire summer.

We showed that the rBC impact can be more substantial on the east side of the Peninsula on a regional scale due to a combination of rBC concentrations and the local meteorology, including less cloud cover. An analysis of melting days based on remote sensing data underscores a more extensive melting extent of days on the east side of the Peninsula. It is already known that a local increase in melting days is associated with ice shelf breakup in the Antarctic Peninsula (34). Furthermore, it was observed for the Larsen C ice shelf that clouds strongly influence the amount of radiation received at the surface and can determine whether melting occurs (35). Here, we showed two events where melting happened in the Larsen C ice shelf when air and ocean temperatures were close to 0°C, indicating the importance of incident shortwave radiation and the possible increment of absorbed energy on the surface due to rBC. Although there is not an established cause-effect relation between rBC concentration and ice shelf melting/breakup, it is interesting to notice that in 2008 summer, both ice shelf melting ponds and breakup were detected in the Antarctic Peninsula, at Larsen C ice shelf (36) and Wilkins ice shelf (37), respectively.

Not less important is the possibility of atmospheric rBC creating conditions for more liquid water–containing clouds. Liquid water–containing clouds can favor liquid precipitation and, thus, increase shortwave radiation absorption due to the melt-albedo feedback (38).

Atmospheric rBC is not only increasing but also changing its seasonality. Before 2004 atmospheric rBC peaks (annual maximum concentration) usually occurred in September/October coincident with the fire season in South America, however, this pattern is changing. After 2015–2016, atmospheric rBC peaks has occurring in the late spring-summer (November to February), which coincides with the periods of tourism activities and fire season in Australia.

Climate warming may increase the potential risks for more intense fire seasons in mid- and low-latitude regions surrounding Antarctica in the future (39), resulting in higher rBC concentrations in the snow and ice. At the same time, rapid increases in ship-based Antarctic tourism have emitted rBC and other pollutants directly into the region during the late spring-summer. This trend undoubtedly will continue. Our findings highlight the importance of comprehensively assessing the impacts of the rapidly increasing tourism-related emissions in polar regions and of forest fires, particularly in light of the well-established relationship between BC emissions and ice-melting processes. Understanding these impacts is paramount to policy decisions aimed at mitigating the negative environmental impacts of human activities on the Antarctic Peninsula. Using low-sulfur marine fuels, which typically produce less BC, alongside other actions such as exploring alternative fuels, such as biofuels (which have lower BC emissions compared to traditional marine fuels), and installing emission control technologies on ships, such as diesel particulate filters, can be effective in reducing BC concentrations in the region.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Firn core measurements and dating

H2O2, rBC, and a broad range of elements and chemical species were measured concurrently in the 20-m DP core using the well-established continuous ice core analytical system (24) at the DRI, Reno, USA, resulting in concentration records that are exactly coregistered in depth. rBC was measured using the single-particle soot photometer (SP2)–based method for continuous ice core analyses originally developed at DRI (23). The SP2 measures the individual mass of rBC particles through laser-induced incandescence (40). H2O2 was measured using a fluorimetry-based method following standard procedures (41).

The DP core chronology was established by counting annual deposition layers, discerned here specifically through variations in H2O2 concentrations. The seasonal cycle of H2O2 is associated with the production and degradation processes of H2O2 in the atmosphere through photochemical reactions. Atmospheric H2O2 is generated through the interaction of hydroperoxyl and hydrated hydroperoxyl radicals, which are produced by the photochemical reactions of atmospheric gases such as ozone and volatile organic compounds. As light and water vapor play fundamental roles in forming H2O2, the seasonal H2O2 cycle in polar regions is well defined since the variations in solar luminosity and absolute humidity between winter and summer are considerably greater than at lower latitudes. We assumed that the midpoint of the lower H2O2 concentration is the period of the lowest winter luminosity (21 June). At the same time, the point of maximum H2O2 concentration was considered to correspond to the period of peak summer luminosity (21 December). Solar radiation data collected at the Comandante Ferraz Antarctic Station (62°05′06″S, 58°24′12″W) were overlain onto the H2O2 curve as auxiliary data to refine the dating. The high snow deposition rate in the region (4.5 to 5 m/year) and continuous analyses resulted in a very-high-resolution record that enabled the identification and interpretation of seasonal layers.

Tourism data

The IAATO has carefully monitored, analyzed, and reported Antarctic tourism trends since 1991. Sea-born tourism data (number of tourists, number of ship voyages, number of large ship passengers, and spatial data of activities) were obtained from the IAATO web page: https://iaato.org/information-resources/data-statistics/visitor-statistics/visitor-statistics-downloads/.

Biomass burning data

Burned area data are from MODIS Global Burned Area Product (Collection 6). The MODIS burned area mapping algorithm detects the approximate burning date at a spatial resolution of 500 m by locating the occurrence of rapid changes in daily surface reflectance. The algorithm maps the spatial extent of recent fires and not of fires that occurred in previous seasons or years (42). Data can be found at https://modis-fire.umd.edu/ba.html.

Active fire data are from The Brazil National Institute for Space Research (http://terrabrasilis.dpi.inpe.br/queimadas/portal/), which uses the Collection 6 MODIS Active Fire Detection algorithm (42).

UVAI data

UVAI is based on wavelength-dependent changes in Rayleigh scattering in the ultraviolet spectral range where ozone absorption is minimal (43). UVAI also can be calculated in the presence of clouds so that daily global coverage is possible. This is ideal for tracking the evolution of episodic aerosol plumes from biomass burning, dust outbreaks, and volcanic ash. We used the UVAI monthly mean to identify the main emission sources of rBC for each season. The UVAI data can be obtained at www.temis.nl/airpollution/absaai/.

Cloud fraction data

Cloud fraction data are from the MODIS Cloud Product produced both day and night at five-by-five 1-km-pixel resolution by the infrared retrieval methods (44). The data can be obtained at https://neo.gsfc.nasa.gov/view.php?datasetId=MYDAL2_M_CLD_FR.

NCEP/NCAR meteorological data

Daily meteorological data are from NCEP/NCAR (45). The image was provided by the NOAA Physical Sciences Laboratory, Boulder, Emergency Use Authorization (https://psl.noaa.gov/).

MERRA-2 rBC reanalysis data

The release of MERRA-2 in 2017 marked a great advancement in reanalysis data brought forth by NASA’s Global Modeling and Assimilation Office (46). This innovative dataset builds upon the foundation of the Goddard Earth Observing System Model, Version 5, and the Data Assimilation System, Version 5.12.4. MERRA-2 effectively integrates and fuses a multitude of Aerosol Optical Depth (AOD) datasets. This encompasses corrected AOD data from sources such as the MODIS, the Advanced Very-High-Resolution Radiometer instruments, AOD retrievals obtained by the Multiangle Imaging Spectroradiometer, and even AOD measurements directly captured by ground-based instruments in the Aerosol Robotic Network.

Moreover, MERRA-2 encompasses a variety of remote sensing inversion datasets and traditional observation datasets derived from radiosondes and aircraft. The temporal dimensions of MERRA-2 simulations extend back to 1980, enabling a comprehensive analysis of atmospheric BC concentration trends. The monthly MERRA-2 rBC dataset (M2TMNXAER) has a spatial resolution of 0.5° by 0.625°. MERRA-2 rBC data are hosted on the Goddard Earth Sciences Data and Information Services Center website, accessible at https://disc.gsfc.nasa.gov.

Ice sheet melt extent data

Ice sheet melt extent data were derived from passive microwave remote sensing. The dataset is generated using a dynamically changing threshold obtained by simulating the passive microwave brightness temperatures for every grid cell each year using a microwave emission model (47, 48). The National Snow and Ice Data Center (NSIDC) provided the dataset.

Air temperature data

The Ukrainian Hydrometeorological Institute and National Antarctic Scientific Center of Ukraine provided air temperature data from Vernadsky Station (Ukraine Antarctic Station).

JRI ice core data

rBC concentrations from the JRI ice core were provided by the US Antarctic Program Data Center (49) (available at www.usap-dc.org/view/dataset/601464). rBC concentrations for the Gomez ice core and the DRI Antarctic ice core array (Fig. 1B) are from Liu et al. (21).

Observations of smoke plumes over the Antarctic peninsula

To verify whether smoke plumes from wildfires within the South America/Amazon Basin can reach the Antarctic Peninsula, we analyzed paths of the CALIOP (Cloud-Aerosol Lidar with Orthogonal Polarization) sensor onboard the CALIPSO satellite. The instrument records information regarding the vertical distribution of aerosols within the atmosphere, supporting the identification of air masses and the characterization of different aerosols (50, 51). CALIPSO’s algorithm for aerosol-type classification uses the aerosol layer’s optical, geophysical, and temporal characteristics as decision points to navigate a flow chart that ultimately selects the most likely aerosol model for each aerosol layer (52). The aerosol types, including “polluted continental,” “biomass burning,” “desert dust,” and “polluted dust,” are derived from a comprehensive cluster analysis applied to Aerosol Robotic Network data gathered from numerous sites around the globe. “Clean continental” and “marine” sources are synthesized using measurements of long-range continental transport and sea salt acquired using a backscatter nephelometer. Products from the CALIOP sensor were obtained from the NASA website: www-calipso.larc.nasa.gov/.

Multivariate analysis

The correlation matrix, hierarchical cluster analysis, and PCA were conducted using the FactoMineR Package in R. The FactoMineR package is used for multivariate data analysis and data visualization in R (53).

HYSPLIT analysis

Backward air mass trajectories were analyzed using the HYSPLIT model (internet-based version—NOAA Air Resources Laboratory) (54). The HYSPLIT is a complete system for computing simple air parcel trajectories and complex transport, dispersion, chemical transformation, and deposition simulations. It is one of the atmospheric sciences community’s most extensively used atmospheric transport and dispersion models. We used back trajectory analysis to determine the origin of air masses arriving at the DP firn core and to analyze possible source-receptor relationships. The HYSPLIT model can be run at www.ready.noaa.gov/hypub-bin/trajtype.pl.

Snowmelt estimation for rBC impact on the Antarctic Peninsula

To estimate snow melting, we assume a simplified model in which the surface temperature is at 0°C and, thus, all available energy is used for the snow melting process (latent heat of fusion of snow). This can be true during the summer when the air temperature is above 0°C. This hypothetical scenario considers that all the energy has already been used to raise the surface temperature to 0°C. That way, the snow melt (W) due to solar radiation can be estimated using the following equation:W=(1−A) × I × dEwhere A is the snow albedo, I is the shortwave irradiance at the surface, d is a period of interest (here equal to the 90 DJF days), and E is the energy needed to melt 1 kg of snow [latent heat of fusion, 334 kJ/kg; Cohen (55)]. To estimate the snowmelt due to rBC, we calculated the difference between the amount of snow that melts per unit of the surface with and without albedo changes due to rBC (Abc), as followsBC impact on snow melt=((1−Abc) × I × dE)−((1−A) × I × dE)

Snow albedo and shortwave irradiance data are from MERRA-2 for the 2007–2008 summer (DJF). We incorporated albedo change due to rBC calculated using the SNICAR model v3 (56) (the average broad albedo considering different snow grain sizes).

The SNICAR model v3 provides spectral albedo, calculated in each of the 480 spectral bands (range of 0.2 to 5 μm at 10-nm resolution), and solar broadband albedo, which is weighted by surface spectral irradiance. Surface irradiance band fractions are applied and provided in the model output. These fractions are normalized to sum to 1.0 over the SNICAR spectral range such that broadband albedo is simplified to the following equationα¯=∑i=1480αi ƒiwhere αi is the albedo of band i and ƒi is the fraction of surface irradiance within band i.

The grain size of the superficial snow layer is a key determinant of the surface albedo in Antarctica. In Antarctic coastal areas, dominant grain size at superficial snow layer can range from 100 to 800 μm (57). In our approach, the average broad albedo change (relative to pure snow) was calculated as the mean albedo change for different snow grain sizes (100, 300, 500, and 800 μm) taking into account rBC concentration in snow. The detailed method for broad albedo calculation is described in Flanner et al. (56). The rBC concentration used in SNICAR simulations was 1.6 ppb. We used this method to compare the results with the work of Cordero et al. (12), which used the same model simplifications. The results represent the upper bound of the possible impact of rBC on snow melt during the summer.
