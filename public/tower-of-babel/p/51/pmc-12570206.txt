Protective Effect of Pediococcus pentosaceus Li05 on Constipation via TGR5/TPH1/5‐HT Activation
Authors: Hui Chen, Fengjiao Wang, Shengjie Li, Longxian Lv, Hao Xu, Jiafeng Xia, Lvwan Xu, Ding Shi, Lanjuan Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Pediococcus pentosaceus Li05, a strain of lactic acid bacteria isolated from the faeces of healthy volunteers, exhibited potential protective effects against various diseases. This study performed third‐generation sequencing and detailed characterisation of its genome. The Li05 chromosome harboured conserved genes associated with acid resistance (atp), bile salt resistance (bsh), oxidative stress resistance (hsl, dltA, and et al.), and adhesion (nrd, gap, and et al.), whereas the plasmid did not contain antibiotic resistance or virulence genes. Following intervention with Li05 in loperamide‐induced constipated mice, constipation symptoms improved. Meanwhile, alterations in gut microbiota, increased BSH activity in faeces, and modifications to the faecal bile acid profile were observed. Additionally, expression levels of TGR5 and TPH1 in the colon of the mice increased, leading to elevated 5‐HT levels. When the TGR5 gene was knocked out or the TPH1 inhibitor LX1606 was administered to suppress 5‐HT synthesis in constipated mice, the beneficial effects of Li05 on gastrointestinal motility and mucus secretion were reversed. Culturing intestinal organoids demonstrated that increased bile acids such as DCA, Iso‐LCA, and EALCA could enhance 5‐HT levels through the TGR5/TPH1 axis. Therefore, we concluded that Li05 regulated bile acid metabolism, subsequently increasing 5‐HT levels through the TGR5/TPH1 axis, thus alleviating constipation.Keywords: 5‐HT, bile acid metabolism, constipation, Pediococcus pentosaceus Li05, TGR5

Introduction

Constipation is a common gastrointestinal symptom that can significantly impact patients' quality of life. The normal process of defecation relies on the coordinated functions of intestinal motility, secretion, visceral sensation, pelvic floor musculature, and the enteric nervous system, with gut microbiota playing a critical role in this process (Aktar et al. 2020; Fu et al. 2021). Consequently, microecological preparations may be utilised to alleviate constipation. Lactic acid bacteria (LAB) have long been used as food additives and health‐promoting agents, playing an essential role in enhancing human health (De Filippis et al. 2020; Garbacz 2022). Various strains of LAB have been reported to relieve symptoms of constipation (Kubota et al. 2020; Shang et al. 2022; Vitellio et al. 2019; Zhang, Liu, et al. 2023), including increasing defecation frequency (Ma et al. 2023), shortening colonic transit time (Kang et al. 2021), and improving faecal consistency (Madempudi et al. 2020). Pediococcus pentosaceus is a species belonging to the family Lactobacillaceae and the order Lactobacillales, known for its lactic acid production (Jiang et al. 2021). It has been reported that P. pentosaceus B49 can alleviate loperamide‐induced constipation in mice, which was derived from human colostrum (Huang et al. 2020). In this study, P. pentosaceus Li05 was isolated from healthy volunteers' faecal samples in our laboratory. This strain is a Gram‐negative, non‐motile, acid‐resistant, non‐spore‐forming, spherical, facultatively anaerobic bacterium (Lv, Li, et al. 2014) that has demonstrated protective effects in various diseases (Xie et al. 2021; Han et al. 2023; Bian et al. 2020; Lv, Hu, et al. 2014). Additionally, our research group previously analysed the genome of Li05 using second‐generation sequencing, with the GenBank assembly number GCA_000708635.1. With the advancement of high‐throughput technologies, third‐generation sequencing has become widely used for bacterial genome sequencing, offering complete and contiguous genomic information compared to second‐generation sequencing, making it suitable for the assembly and analysis of complex genomes. Consequently, this study conducted third‐generation sequencing of the Li05 strain and provided a detailed characterisation of its genomic features. Although Li05 shows great potential as a probiotic, its role in the context of constipation remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to explore the therapeutic effects and mechanisms of action of Li05 in loperamide‐induced constipation in mice.

Materials and Methods

Strain and Culture Conditions

P. pentosaceus Li05 (CGMCC 7049) was isolated from the faeces of a healthy individual in our laboratory (Lv, Hu, et al. 2014). The strain was inoculated into Man Rogosa Sharpe (MRS) broth (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA) and cultured anaerobically at 37°C for 12 h. Following this, the culture was centrifuged at 4000 g for 10 min, washed twice with PBS, and resuspended to a concentration of 1 × 109 CFU/mL for oral gavage in mice.

Mice and Experimental Design

Aged 6–8 weeks male C57BL/6J mice (GemPharmatech, Nanjing, China) were maintained in a specific pathogen‐free (SPF) environment with ad libitum access to food and water at a temperature of 23°C ± 2°C, under a 12 h light and 12 h dark cycle. Mice were randomly divided into three groups: control, model, and Li05, with 12 mice in each group. Body weight, food intake, and water consumption were measured weekly. The experimental design is illustrated in Figure 2A. In the first phase, lasting 3 weeks, the Li05 group received daily oral gavage of 0.2 mL of the bacterial suspension (1 × 109 CFU/mL), whereas the control and model groups were administered 0.2 mL of PBS as a control. In the second phase, for 2 weeks, the Li05 and model groups received 10 mg/kg loperamide hydrochloride (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, USA) daily, 2 h after the aforementioned gavage, to induce constipation, whereas the control group continued to receive PBS. Every 3 days during the second phase, the mice were placed in clean cages, and the number of faecal pellets produced by each mouse over a 2 h period was recorded. Faeces were immediately collected to measure wet weight, and after drying at 70°C for 24 h, the dry weight was recorded. The faecal water content (%) was calculated using the formula: Faecal water content (%) = [((Wet weight 1−Dry weight 1)/Wet weight 1) + ((Wet weight 2−Dry weight 2)/Wet weight 2)/2] × 100%. On the third day post‐induction, the mice were fasted overnight. On the fourth day, each mouse was gavaged with 0.2 mL of activated carbon solution (prepared and stored as previously reported) (Wang et al. 2019), and the time to the first passage of black faeces was recorded, referred to as the black stool defecation time (in minutes). On the 7th day post‐induction, a bead expulsion experiment was conducted. Mice were lightly anaesthetised, and a 2.5 mm glass bead was inserted 2 cm into the mouse's anus, with the bead expulsion time recorded (in seconds). On the 14th day, the mice were fasted overnight, and each mouse received a gavage of 0.2 mL of activated carbon solution on the next day. Twenty minutes later, the mice were euthanized, and the total length of the small intestine was measured, as well as the activated carbon migration distance. The gastric intestinal (GI) transit rate (%) was calculated using the formula: GI transit rate (%) = (Activated carbon migration distance/Total length of the small intestine) × 100%. Blood, colonic tissue, caecal contents, and colonic contents were collected for subsequent analyses.

TGR5 −/− mice (strain No. T012754) were purchased from GemPharmatech (Nanjing, China). Genotyping was performed using DNA extracted from tail tips, with the following primer sequences: forward, GCTTGTAGGGTGATGTGACAGGAC; reverse, CTTTGACTTTGATTCGCTGGTGT. Male mice aged 6–10 weeks were randomly assigned to three groups: control, model, and Li05, with 10 mice per group.

Cell and Intestinal Organoid Experiment

The Caco‐2 cell line (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA) was cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco, MD, USA) supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Opcel Biotechnology, Inner Mongolia, China) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco) in a 37°C incubator with 5% CO2. Caco‐2 cells were plated in 6‐well plates, and 2 μM bile acids (Aladdin, Zhenzhun, China) and 30 μM INT‐777 (MCE) were added and incubated for 24 h on the following day (the intervention concentration was determined through preliminary exploration using the CCK‐8 cell viability assay). Cells were then harvested, and RNA was extracted for subsequent experiments (Detailed information on all materials and devices used in this study is provided in Tables S1 and S2).

Intestinal organoids were generated using male C57BL/6J mice aged 6–10 weeks. After isolating the small intestine, it was washed with cold PBS and digested using Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (GDCR, Stemcell, Canada) to isolate the crypts. The isolated crypts were suspended in a 1:1 mixture of matrigel (Corning, USA) and IntestiCult organoid culture medium (Stemcell) and allowed to polymerise. Following this, 750 μL of culture medium was added, and the organoids were incubated at 37°C with 5% CO2, with medium changes performed every 3–4 days and passaging conducted weekly. To induce the enrichment and differentiation of enterochromaffin (EC) cells (Chen et al. 2021; Basak et al. 2017), organoids were cultured under standard conditions for 2 days before being placed in a mixture containing IWP2 (1.5 μM; Sigma‐Aldrich, USA), DAPT (10 μM; Sigma Aldrich), and PD0325901 (1 μM; Sigma‐Aldrich) for 72 h. Subsequently, 2 μM bile acids and 30 μM INT‐777 were added for a 24 h intervention, after which the supernatant was collected for quantification of 5‐HT, and RNA was extracted from the organoids for further analysis.

Measurement of Gastrointestinal Hormones and Neurotransmitters

Blood samples from the mice were collected and then centrifuged at 3000 g for 15 min at 4°C, with the supernatant retained for analysis. Approximately 50 mg colonic tissue was weighed and homogenised in 450 μL of PBS. Following ultrasonic disruption for 5 min, the mixture was centrifuged at 15,000 g for 10 min at 4°C, and then the supernatant was collected for protein quantification and further experimentation.

Serotonin (5‐HT), substance P (SP), gastrin (GAS), acetylcholine (ACh), somatostatin (SST), and vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) levels were measured using ELISA kits (Shenggong). Additionally, the supernatant from the intestinal organoid culture medium was centrifuged at 8000 g for 10 min at 4°C, and the resulting supernatant was used to determine 5‐HT content via ELISA.

Quantitative PCR Analysis and Western Blot Analysis

From colonic tissues and cells, RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (Qiagen, CA, USA), followed by reverse transcription to cDNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Kusatsu, Japan). The target gene quantification was conducted using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II reagent (Takara), and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method, with GAPDH serving as an internal reference. The primer sequences for the target genes are detailed in Table S3.

Protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) were added to RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime), followed by homogenization of the colonic tissue for protein extraction. Following electrophoretic separation, the proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes, which were rapidly blocked for 15 min before incubating with primary antibodies overnight. On the following day, secondary antibodies were incubated for 2 h before detection with an ECL kit (Beyotime). ImageJ (Rawak Software, Stuttgart, Germany) was utilised to quantify the protein intensity. Antibodies were procured from Cell Signalling Technology (Boston, USA) and Proteintech (Wuhan, China). β‐Actin or GAPDH served as the loading controls for total proteins.

Histological and Immunohistochemical Analysis of Colonic Tissue and Immunofluorescence Staining of Intestinal Organoids

Proximal and distal colonic tissues were collected from mice and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h before being paraffin‐embedded. Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed for histological analysis. The distal colonic tissue was analysed for muscle thickness, whereas the proximal colonic tissue underwent periodic acid Schiff and alcian blue (AB‐PAS) staining to evaluate the number of goblet cells and mucus thickness, along with immunohistochemical staining for mucin 2 (MUC2) to assess protein expression levels. Intestinal organoids were rinsed with cold PBS to remove Matrigel and then fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h prior to paraffin embedding. Sections of 4 μm thickness were cut for immunofluorescence analysis. Co‐localization was performed using antibodies against 5‐HT (Abcam, UK) and chromogranin A (Chga) (Abcam), with 4′6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (DAPI) (Abcam) utilized for nuclear staining. A laser scanning confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany) was used to get immunofluorescence images, and screenshots were captured using CaseViewer software.

Statistical Analysis

The data are presented as the mean ± SEM. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism v8 and SPSS v25. For data with equal variance, one‐way ANOVA was performed, followed by pairwise comparisons using the LSD test. For data with unequal variance, Welch's test was applied, with pairwise comparisons conducted using the Games–Howell test. Repeated measures data were analysed using repeated measures ANOVA. A p‐value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Genomic Characterisation of P. pentosaceus Li05

The GC content of the Li05 genome is 37.5%, consisting of a chromosome with a size of 1,812,373 bp and carrying two plasmids, sized 43,800 and 18,214 bp, respectively (genomic information can be found in Table S4). The genome of Li05 was functionally annotated using the RAST tool, and Figure 1A shows the distribution of genes from the Li05 strain according to COG functional categories. Using the genome sequences of 156 P. pentosaceus strains available on NCBI as of October 2023 (details in Table S5), a phylogenetic tree was constructed. The complete phylogenetic tree was shown in Figure S1, and Figure 1B demonstrates that the genome most closely related to Li05 is the strain with the GenBank assembly accession number GCA 902386405.1. According to NCBI sample information, this isolate was derived from the human gut in China in 2019. To confirm the genetic identity between the two strains, we used the EZBioCloud ANI Calculator to compare their genomes and obtained an ANI value of 99.99%. In addition, both genomes share the same BioSample (SAMN02639794), which was originally uploaded in 2014 for Li05, although further details remain unclear. A comparative genomic analysis was conducted between the chromosome sequence of the reference genome strain P. pentosaceus ATCC 25745 and the genome of strain SMFM2016‐YK1, which shared the closest evolutionary relationship and had a complete chromosome sequence available in the NCBI database under accession number GCA 030291835.1. The SMFM2016‐YK1 strain was isolated from kimchi by Sookmyung Women's University in Seoul, Korea, in 2016. As depicted in Figure 1C, genes marked in green encode F0F1 synthase, conferring acid resistance to the strain; genes marked in purple encode bile salt hydrolase, associated with bile salt resistance; genes marked in blue are related to oxidative stress resistance, whereas genes marked in brown are related to adhesion. These genes exhibited a high degree of conservation among the chromosomes of Li05, ATCC 25745, and SMFM2016‐YK1 strains. Additionally, the two plasmids of Li05 were visualised (Figure 1D,E), showing that they do not carry antibiotic resistance or virulence genes identified by Resfinder and VFDB tools. Studies have shown that Bifidobacterium strains carrying the abfA gene cluster, which enhances arabinoxylans utilisation, can improve functional constipation (Zhang, Yu, et al. 2023). Although the abfA cluster was not detected in the Li05 genome, the presence of contiguous genes encoding arabinose‐proton symporter, ribulokinase, L‐ribulose‐5‐phosphate 4‐epimerase, and L‐arabinose isomerase (black‐labelled genes in Figure 1C) indicated the ability to utilise L‐arabinose. Previous research has also demonstrated that Li05 can use L‐arabinose as its sole carbon source (Lv, Li, et al. 2014), prompting the hypothesis that Li05 may also have the potential to alleviate constipation. Therefore, we subsequently employed a loperamide‐induced mouse model of constipation to investigate this possibility.

Li05 Intervention Alleviated Constipation in Mice by Enhancing Gastrointestinal Motility and Mucus Secretion

As shown in Figure 2B,C, there were no statistically significant differences in body weight among the intervention group, model group, and control group at baseline, week 3, and week 5. Similarly, no significant differences were observed in the average water intake and food consumption measured across multiple time points, including weeks 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5, with no significant interaction effects between time and treatment factors. This indicated that neither loperamide administration nor Li05 intervention had a statistically significant impact on body weight, water intake, or food consumption in the mice. As illustrated in Figure 2D, the overall mean number of defecations within 2 h and faecal water content showed statistically significant differences among the three groups. On days 1, 10, and 13 following loperamide administration, the Li05 group exhibited a higher frequency of defecation within 2 h compared to the model group. Additionally, on days 1, 4, and 13, the Li05 group showed an increase in faecal water content relative to the model group. On day 0, the day before loperamide administration, no significant differences in defecation frequency or faecal water content were observed among the three groups. These findings suggested that the Li05 intervention in advance does not influence the number of faecal pellets or water content but can alleviate defecation symptoms following the induction of constipation in mice. Figure 2E demonstrates that the Li05 intervention significantly reduced the first black stool defecation time in constipated mice, increased GI transit rate (Figure 2F), and markedly decreased bead expulsion time. In summary, the use of the probiotic Li05 significantly improves constipation symptoms in mice.

As shown in Figure 3A,B, compared to the model group, Li05 increased the expression levels of gastrointestinal motility‐promoting hormones and neurotransmitters in the serum, including 5‐HT, ACh, and SP, whereas the expression levels of SST and VIP, which inhibit gastrointestinal motility, were reduced. HE staining indicated that the colonic tissue structure in all groups remained relatively intact, with no significant abnormalities in cell morphology or notable infiltration of inflammatory cells. However, the muscle layer thickness in the model group was reduced (Figure 3D). AB‐PAS staining revealed a decrease in the number of goblet cells and mucus thickness in the model group (red arrows indicate goblet cells, green arrows indicate the mucus layer). The mRNA expression levels of aquaporins AQP1, AQP3, AQP4, and AQP8 among the three groups did not display statistically significant differences (Figure S2B). In contrast, in the Li05 group, the expression level of MUC2 was significantly elevated compared to the model group (Figure 3C,D). In summary, Li05 intervention may improve constipation in mice by enhancing gastrointestinal motility and mucus secretion.

Li05 Enhanced Gastrointestinal Motility and Mucus Secretion via the 5‐HT Pathway

5‐HT is primarily synthesized by EC cells in the gut, with tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH1) serving as the rate‐limiting enzyme. A decrease in 5‐HT release has been associated with reduced gastrointestinal motility in mouse models of depression (Israelyan et al. 2019). In this study, Li05 intervention in constipated mice resulted in increased serum 5‐HT level and enhanced gastrointestinal motility. However, to confirm whether Li05 exerts its effects specifically through the 5‐HT pathway, further validation was required. Consequently, the TPH1 inhibitor telotristat ethyl (LX1606) was used to block 5‐HT synthesis and secretion. The results showed that LX1606 did not exacerbate the constipation phenotype in the model group, but did reverse the effects of Li05, including the increase in serum 5‐HT level, mRNA expression of TPH1 and MUC2 in the colon, faecal water content, and improvement in gastrointestinal motility (Figure 3G,H). Therefore, we suggested that Li05 may enhance gastrointestinal motility and mucus secretion through the 5‐HT pathway, thereby alleviating constipation.

Effects of Li05 Intervention on Gut Microbiota, Untargeted Metabolomics, and Bile Acid Metabolism

To elucidate the impact of Li05 on the gut microbiota of constipated mice, we analysed colonic contents using 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Unweighted Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) revealed that the composition of the microbiota in the model group was significantly different from that in the control group, and Li05 intervention ameliorated these differences (Figure 4A). Figure S2A,D illustrated species with differential abundance at the phylum, family, genus, and species levels (top 50 species) among the three groups. The LEfSe method was employed to identify biomarkers among the groups, with Figure 4B,C showing that Li05 intervention primarily reduced the abundance of pathogenic bacteria such as Escherichia/Shigella, Enterobacteriaceae, and Enterobacterales in constipated mice, while increasing beneficial bacteria associated with short‐chain fatty acid and bile acid metabolism, including ASF356, Allobaculum, Anaerostipes, Roseburia, Dubosiella, and Lachnospiraceae UCG‐001. Figure 4D,E shows differential abundance species at the genus and species levels between Li05 and the model group. In addition to the aforementioned increases, Li05 also elevated the abundance of the Eubacterium siraeum group, Monoglobus (Yang et al. 2022), Enterorhabdus (Kolli et al. 2023), Streptococcus (Huang et al. 2019), and Clostridium sp. (Ridlon et al. 2006), all of which have been reported to be associated with bile acid metabolism.

Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (PLS‐DA) indicated significant differences in the overall metabolite profiles among the three groups (R 2 X (cum) = 0.578; R 2 Y (cum) = 0.996; Q 2 (cum) = 0.906) (Figure 5A). Orthogonal Partial Least Squares Discriminant Analysis (OPLS‐DA) further revealed metabolic differences between the model and control groups, as well as between the model and Li05 group (Figure 5B,C), with model validation results presented in Figure S3A. Compared to the model group, the Li05 intervention upregulated 266 metabolites and downregulated 219 metabolites. In contrast, the model group exhibited 290 metabolites upregulated and 280 downregulated compared to the control group, with a volcano plot visualising the differential metabolites (Figure 5D). The above findings indicated that Li05 intervention markedly upregulated bile acid–associated microbiota, accompanied by an increased excretion of bile acids. Research indicates that increased bile acids in the colon can stimulate colonic motility and secretion (Kirwan et al. 1975), suggesting that Li05 may improve constipation through this mechanism. Consequently, we conducted a targeted analysis of bile acid metabolism in the colonic contents, quantitatively assessing 40 bile acids. Among these, seven bile acids [lithocholic acid glycine conjugate (GLCA), glycoursodeoxycholic acid (GUDCA), glycohyodeoxycholic acid (GHDCA), taurohyocholic acid (THCA), glycoursocholanic acid (GUCA), glycohyocholic acid (GCHCA), and 3β‐ursodeoxycholic (3β‐UDCA)] were not detected; chenodeoxycholic acid glycine conjugate (GCDCA) was only found in the Li05 group; lithocholyltaurine (TLCA) and dehydrocholic acid (DHCA) were exclusively detected in the model group; and ursocholic acid (UCA) was not detected in the control group. As shown in Figure 5F, the overall bile acid profile in faeces was altered: the total bile acid and free bile acid proportions were increased in the Li05 group compared to the other two groups. Compared to the model group, the proportion of secondary bile acids was elevated in the control group, with the Li05 intervention group showing increased mean values compared to the model group, although without statistical significance. Comparative analysis of various bile acids among the three groups is illustrated in Figure 6 and Figure S3. Notably, the Li05 group exhibited increased excretion of conjugated bile acids such as deoxycholic acid glycine conjugate (GDCA) and free bile acids including hyocholic acid (HCA), ursodeoxycholic acid (UDCA), α‐muricholic acid (α‐MCA), 3β‐cholic acid (3β‐CA), deoxycholic acid (DCA), epiallolithocholic acid (EALCA), isolithocholic acid (Iso‐LCA), and murideoxycholic acid (M‐DCA), whereas the model group did not demonstrate statistically significant increases when compared with the Li05 group. We also assessed bile salt hydrolase (BSH) activity in mouse faeces, revealing increased BSH activity in the Li05 group compared to the model group (Figure 5G). This finding aligned with the elevated proportion of free bile acids observed in the Li05 group. Additionally, genetic sequencing of the probiotic Li05 indicated the presence of the bsh gene on its chromosome (Figure 1C). Therefore, it can be inferred that Li05 may directly or indirectly regulate bile acid metabolism by altering the gut microbiota composition.

Elevated Free Bile Acids Promoted 5‐HT Production and Secretion by Activating the TGR5 Receptor

Research indicates that the Takeda G protein‐coupled receptor 5 (TGR5) mediates the pro‐motility effects of bile acids, with TGR5 gene knockout resulting in constipation in mice (Alemi et al. 2013). Although the above findings suggested that the administration of the probiotic Li05 in constipated mice led to increased total bile acid excretion in faeces and improved gastrointestinal motility via the 5‐HT pathway. Furthermore, TGR5 receptors are widely expressed in the gastrointestinal tract, including in intestinal epithelial and EC cells (Sorrentino et al. 2020). We hypothesized that Li05 may modulate bile acid metabolism to activate TGR5 receptors on EC cells, thus promoting 5‐HT secretion and alleviating constipation symptoms. To validate this hypothesis, we measured the expression levels of TGR5 and TPH1, as well as 5‐HT content in the colon of mice. As illustrated in Figure 6C–E and Figure S4, the Li05 group exhibited increased expression of TGR5 and TPH1, along with elevated tissue 5‐HT levels compared to the model group. When the TGR5 gene was knocked out in mice, although Li05 intervention continued to enhance faecal BSH activity, it failed to ameliorate constipation symptoms (Figure 6F,G). Moreover, the expression levels of MUC2 and TPH1 mRNA in colonic tissues, as well as 5‐HT concentrations (Figure 6H,I), did not increase. These findings suggested that Li05 may exert its laxative effects by stimulating the TGR5 receptor, thereby modulating 5‐HT synthesis and secretion.

To clarify the role of bile acids in regulating 5‐HT secretion and to identify which specific bile acids exert this effect, we selected conjugated bile acid GDCA and free bile acids 3β‐CA, DCA, EALCA, and Iso‐LCA—substances that were increased in the Li05 group—as well as TLCA, a conjugated bile acid only detected in the model group, and taurochenodesoxycholic acid (TCDCA) and lithocholic acid (LCA), which showed no difference between groups, for cellular intervention experiments, using the TGR5 agonist INT‐777 as a positive control. As shown in Figure 7A, after 24 h of treatment with INT‐777, DCA, Iso‐LCA, and EALCA in Caco‐2 intestinal epithelial cells, the mRNA expression levels of TGR5 and TPH1 were elevated, whereas conjugated bile acids and other free bile acids (LCA and 3β‐CA) did not show statistically significant differences compared to the control group (Figure S3G). Additionally, INT‐777 and Iso‐LCA treatment also increased MUC2 mRNA expression (Figure 7B).

Subsequently, we isolated intestinal crypt cells from 6 to 10‐week‐old mice for organoid culture and induced EC cell differentiation. As depicted in Figure 7C, the co‐localization of ChgA with 5‐HT indicated the induction of EC cells within the intestinal organoids. Following treatment with INT‐777, DCA, Iso‐LCA, and EALCA for 24 h, we collected the supernatants to measure 5‐HT levels, whereas the cellular components were extracted for RNA analysis of TGR5 and TPH1 mRNA expression. The results (Figure 7D) demonstrated that TGR5 and TPH1 mRNA expression levels increased following treatment with INT‐777, DCA, Iso‐LCA, and EALCA, accompanied by elevated 5‐HT concentrations in the supernatants. Thus, we inferred that free bile acids such as DCA, Iso‐LCA, and EALCA may promote 5‐HT production and secretion by activating the TGR5 receptor on the surface of EC cells.

Discussion

Our study is the first to investigate the effects and underlying mechanisms of Pediococcus pentosaceus Li05 in a murine model of constipation. We demonstrated that Li05 increased the abundance of gut microbes involved in bile acid metabolism, enhanced BSH activity, and promoted the excretion of total bile acids, including several free bile acids such as DCA, Iso‐LCA, and EALCA. These free bile acids activated TGR5 on EC cells, upregulating the TPH1 expression and promoting the biosynthesis and secretion of 5‐HT, which enhanced colonic motility and mucosal secretion and thereby alleviated constipation symptoms.

The biosynthesis of bile acids involves the classical pathway initiated by cholesterol 7α‐hydroxylase (CYP7A1) in hepatocytes and an alternative pathway initiated by sterol 27‐hydroxylase in extrahepatic tissues (Yao et al. 2023). The primary bile acids synthesised are conjugated with glycine or taurine and stored in the gallbladder, where they are secreted into the duodenum after food intake and metabolised by the gut microbiota along the gastrointestinal tract. Approximately 95% of bile acids are reabsorbed before reaching the terminal ileum, with the remainder entering the colon, where they are excreted with faeces after passive reuptake (Rizzolo et al. 2021; Collins et al. 2023). Therefore, increased faecal bile acids may result from enhanced bile acid synthesis, reduced intestinal reabsorption, or a combination of both. In this study, following the intervention with the probiotic Li05 in constipated mice, we observed the upregulation of total bile acid excretion in faeces. Previous studies have also indicated that administration of Li05 can enhance bile acid excretion in a model of cholestasis induced by 3,5‐diethoxycarbonyl‐1,4‐dihydrocollidine (DDC) (Han et al. 2023). To investigate the underlying mechanisms for the increased bile acid excretion observed in this study, we assessed the expression levels of the apical sodium‐dependent bile acid transporter (ASBT), bile acid transporter (OST‐α/β), farnesoid X receptor (FXR), and fibroblast growth factor 15 (FGF‐15) in ileal tissues. Intestinal FXR serves as a bile acid receptor; upon activation, it stimulates the production of FGF15 in the intestine, which then circulates to the liver and binds to FGF receptor 4 (FGF‐R4) and the membrane protein klotho β, thereby activating the c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase/extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (JNK/ERK) signalling pathway. This, in turn, suppresses hepatic CYP7A1 expression and inhibits bile acid synthesis (Kim et al. 2007; Ding et al. 2015). Bile acids in the ileum are actively absorbed via ASBT and transported by OST‐α and OST‐β, ultimately returning to the liver through the enterohepatic circulation (Guan et al. 2022). Our results indicated that the mRNA expression of FXR and FGF15 was elevated in both the Li05 and model groups, but the levels of OST‐α and OST‐β were reduced in the Li05 group compared to the model group. Although the relative expression of ASBT was lower in the Li05 group compared to both the model and control groups, this difference did not reach statistical significance (Figure S3F). Therefore, we hypothesized that in the Li05 group, the increased bile acid excretion observed was more likely due to reduced intestinal reabsorption. Nonetheless, further experimental evidence is needed to substantiate our hypothesis.

Bile salt hydrolase (BSH), a member of the bile salt‐activated hydrolase family (EC 3.5.1.24), hydrolyzes conjugated bile acids into free bile acids and glycine or taurine, thereby endowing bacteria with the ability to resist bile salts and enhancing their colonisation capacity in the gastrointestinal tract (Jia et al. 2020). Additionally, BSH is the sole enzyme involved in the first step of the intestinal bile acid metabolism decoupling reaction (Ridlon et al. 2006), influencing the composition of the bile acid pool and thereby regulating the structure of gut microbiota (Wahlström et al. 2016). The bsh gene has been identified in various bacterial genomes (Song et al. 2019), and our study also demonstrated that Li05 carries the bsh gene. BSH activity increased in faecal samples following Li05 intervention. As Li05 contained the bsh gene, it is plausible that Li05 directly regulated faecal BSH activity. Moreover, Li05 may have indirectly modulated BSH activity by altering the gut microbiota composition, particularly through the upregulation of several bacterial taxa associated with bile acid metabolism. To confirm this, both in vitro and in vivo analyses of BSH activity should be conducted using wild‐type and bsh gene knockout Li05 strains.

Excess bile acids reaching the colon can enhance colonic motility and secretion, leading to a condition known as bile acid diarrhoea (BAD), which commonly occurs after ileal or gallbladder resection (Camilleri and Nurko 2022). Nonetheless, the mechanism by which bile acids promote intestinal motility remains unclear. Xu et al. established a humanised mouse model of post‐cholecystectomy diarrhoea (PCD) through faecal microbiota transplantation, demonstrating that the application of the tryptophan hydroxylase 1 inhibitor (LX1606) eliminated the diarrhoea phenotype in PCD mice. Furthermore, the increased levels of secondary bile acids such as DCA, HDCA, and LCA in the faeces were shown to elevate 5‐HT levels in RIN14B cells and in vivo, indicating that alterations in the gut microbiota and increased colonic bile acids following gallbladder resection can promote colonic 5‐HT levels and motility, ultimately leading to diarrhoea (Xu et al. 2023). Consistent with the findings of Xu et al., our results suggested that, from a constipation perspective, free bile acids (such as DCA, Iso‐LCA, and EALCA) increase 5‐HT levels by activating TGR5 receptors on EC cells, thereby promoting colonic motility. Notably, both the knockout of the TGR5 gene and the administration of LX1606 did reverse the anti‐constipation effects of Li05. Thus, our study focuses on the ameliorative effects of probiotic Li05 intervention on constipation, highlighting its influence on gut microbiota and bile acid metabolism as a means to enhance the 5‐HT pathway.

In summary, this study provided a detailed characterisation of the genomic features of potential probiotic Li05, which carries the bsh gene. Following Li05 intervention in loperamide‐induced constipated mice, we observed improvements in constipation symptoms, enhanced gastrointestinal motility, increased mucus secretion, and alterations in the gut microbiota and faecal bile acid composition, along with heightened BSH activity in faeces. Additionally, the expression of TGR5 and TPH1 in the colons of the mice increased, resulting in elevated 5‐HT levels. Culturing intestinal organoids further demonstrated that the increased free bile acids, such as DCA, Iso‐LCA, and EALCA, can elevate 5‐HT levels through the TGR5/TPH1 axis.

Our study also provides preliminary evidence supporting its feasibility as a candidate probiotic for clinical application. From a safety perspective, Li05 is derived from the human gut, and whole‐genome sequencing confirmed the absence of antibiotic resistance or virulence genes, suggesting a favourable safety profile for therapeutic use. Furthermore, its efficacy has been mechanistically and functionally validated in both a murine model of constipation and intestinal organoid systems. Future research should focus on formulation standardisation, dose optimization, and early‐phase clinical trials to facilitate the translational development of Li05 as a therapeutic strategy for functional constipation.

This study has several limitations. First, although our findings indicated that Li05 alleviated constipation through the increased 5‐HT pathway, the specific neural pathways and molecular mechanisms involved in subsequent 5‐HT signalling remain unclear. Previous studies have shown that 5‐HT activated the 5‐HT4 receptor, enhancing ACh release from enteric neurons and promoting gastrointestinal motility (Grider et al. 1998; Galligan et al. 2003; Mawe and Hoffman 2013). Whether the 5‐HT/5‐HT4 receptor/ACh pathway contributes to the enhanced motility observed in our study still requires further investigation. However, our results indicated that the Li05 intervention indeed elevated ACh levels, suggesting that this pathway may be involved. Second, through the cultivation of intestinal organoids, we identified that the upregulated bile acids DCA, Iso‐LCA, and EALCA enhanced 5‐HT production. Further verification using an in vivo mouse model is warranted. Additionally, bile acids upregulated by Li05, such as Iso‐LCA, have been reported to be enriched in the faeces of centenarians (Sato et al. 2021) and inversely associated with diabetes and obesity (Choucair et al. 2024), suggesting potential beneficial effects of Li05 on other diseases. This will be the focus of our future investigations.

Conclusion

We concluded that Li05 intervention regulated gut microbiota and bile acid metabolism, increasing 5‐HT levels through the TGR5/TPH1 activation to ameliorate constipation (Figure 7E). This mechanism underscores the potential application of Li05 as a probiotic in the treatment of constipation and provides a theoretical basis for developing microecological formulations as a therapeutic strategy for constipation management.
