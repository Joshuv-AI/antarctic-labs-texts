The real-world safety profile of amitriptyline: A comprehensive pharmacovigilance analysis based on the large-scale FDA adverse event reporting system database
Authors: Ying Yang, Yanhua Xie, Liangjuan Hou, Xiang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite its established efficacy, the long-term safety profile of amitriptyline among broad patient populations remains insufficiently characterized. We thoroughly characterized the real-world safety profile of amitriptyline through a systematic analysis of the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS). Adverse events were collected from Q1 2004 to Q1 2026, and four disproportionality algorithms—reporting odds ratio (ROR), proportional reporting ratio (PRR), Bayesian confidence propagation neural network (BCPNN), and multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS)—were employed to identify safety signals. Weibull distribution modeling was used to assess temporal trends, with subgroup analyses stratified by age and sex. Among 5301 amitriptyline-related cases involving 20,451 adverse events, risk signals were identified for psychiatric, nervous system, and cardiac disorders. Analysis confirmed recognized risks, including somnolence, confusional state, drug interaction, and xerostomia. Additional risk signals were identified for potential drug abuse or misuse and for serious adverse events not currently listed on package insert, like aspiration pneumonia, cardiorespiratory arrest, and specific congenital anomalies (e.g., syndactyly, polydactyly, and adactyly). Temporal analysis revealed 52.84% of events occurred within 30 days, whereas 26.14% occurred beyond six months. Subgroup analyses revealed distinct susceptibility patterns: cardiovascular events were more common in females, neuropsychiatric events (e.g., migraine, nightmares) in males; fetal exposure was concerning in pediatric populations, suicide and overdose predominant among adults, and confusion and drug interactions prominent in elderly patients. This pharmacovigilance study confirmed amitriptyline’s established adverse reactions and highlighted previously under-characterized risks. These findings recommended implementing integrated risk management in clinical practice to improve patient safety.Keywords: Amitriptyline, Depression, Pharmacovigilance analysis, FAERS, Adverse events

Introduction

Depression is one of the most widespread mental disorders in the world, affecting an estimated 300 million individuals across diverse educational and socioeconomic backgrounds [[1], [2], [3]]. This condition manifests through a constellation of symptoms encompassing social withdrawal, feelings of hopelessness, diminished self-worth, and anhedonia, which substantially lowers overall quality of life [4,5]. The disorder’s clinical complexity is further compounded by the fact that it frequently manifests in combination with concomitant emotional problems, especially anxiety disorders, or chronic medical diseases [6,7]. Relapse prevention, functional restoration, and clinical remission are the core therapeutic goals in depression treatment [8]. Currently, the mainstay of therapeutic approaches in clinical practice remains pharmacological intervention.

The classic tricyclic antidepressant amitriptyline was first introduced in the 1960s and remains one of the most widely prescribed medications in its class [9]. The primary mechanism of its antidepressant action is serotonin and noradrenaline reuptake inhibition, with additional activity at serotonergic, noradrenergic, muscarinic, opioid, and histaminergic receptors [10]. As an essential medicine listed by the World Health Organization, amitriptyline is recommended as first-line or alternative therapy for major depression, particularly in patients with melancholic or chronic features who are refractory to newer antidepressants [11]. A meta-analysis of 34 clinical trials has demonstrated its efficacy in preventing relapse and reducing depressive symptoms [12]. However, these therapeutic benefits are offset by substantial adverse effects, including xerostomia, impaired vision, constipation, urinary retention, sedation, tachycardia, dizziness, fatigue, and increased body weight [[13], [14], [15]]. These adverse effects may impair patient compliance and compromise treatment efficacy. As the prevalence of depression continues to rise, the increasing use of amitriptyline may uncover previously unrecognized safety concerns. Thus, a comprehensive evaluation of amitriptyline’s long-term adverse effects in widespread clinical practice is necessary.

Most existing studies on amitriptyline-related adverse events have been based on randomized controlled trials and meta-analyses. Although these studies have provided fundamental insights, they are subject to inherent limitations, such as rigorous eligibility requirements, small sample sizes, homogeneous research groups, and short follow-up periods. As a result, these approaches are not well-suited to detect the complex, uncommon, or delayed safety profiles that arise in more extensive clinical settings. An important complementary resource can be obtained from large-scale pharmacovigilance databases, such as the FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS). The FAERS, one of the biggest databases of spontaneous adverse event reports globally, compiles post-marketing safety data from millions of patients across diverse clinical settings, enabling the detection of potential safety signals often go unrecognized in controlled clinical studies. Therefore, the present research employed multiple disproportionality analysis algorithms to systematically analyze the large-scale FAERS database, aiming to shed light on the real-world safety characteristics of amitriptyline.

Materials and methods

Study design and data sources

Data for this study were extracted from FAERS, a publicly accessible spontaneous reporting system maintained by the FDA that collects submissions from patients, healthcare professionals, and manufacturers. Updated datasets are published quarterly. All reports listing amitriptyline as the primary suspect (PS) drug were retrieved, covering the interval between the first quarter of 2004 and the first quarter of 2026. Only PS-coded reports were retained to maximize signal specificity. Duplicate removal followed FDA-established guidelines [16]. Specifically, the record with the most recent FDA_DT was retained when several entries had the same CASE_ID. The record with the highest PRIMARY_ID was chosen for retention when CASE_ID and FDA_DT matched across entries. Adverse event reports were standardized using Preferred Terms (PT) in the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA, version 28.1) and further classified by System Organ Class (SOC) to increase the dependability of the ensuing statistical analyses. Statistical analysis

Disproportionality analysis serves as the cornerstone of pharmacovigilance signal detection, which measures the potential connections between medication and adverse events by contrasting the frequency of a particular medication and adverse event with the frequency of those occurrences reported with all other medications [17]. Frequentist and Bayesian techniques are the two types of commonly used algorithms [18,19]. The reporting odds ratio (ROR) and proportional reporting ratio (PRR), two frequentist techniques, produce straightforward risk estimates that are easy to understand. The Bayesian techniques include the multi-item gamma Poisson shrinker (MGPS) and the Bayesian Confidence Propagation Neural Network (BCPNN). BCPNN provides improved sensitivity for signal detection by modeling intricate nonlinear connections between medications and adverse events using a neural network architecture. MGPS employs a Bayesian shrinkage estimator based on gamma–Poisson distributions to account for data variability and uncertainty, yielding more stable and reliable results. To minimize false-positive signals, only adverse events that reached statistical significance across all four methods (ROR, PRR, BCPNN, and MGPS) were retained as positive signals for the next analysis. Supplementary Tables S1 and S2 contained specific calculations and threshold requirements for each technique. It is important to note that disproportionality analysis identifies statistical associations (signals) rather than causal relationships between a drug and adverse events. Stronger signal strength was indicated by higher values, which showed a greater association between the target medication and the adverse event. All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2).

Time-to-onset and Weibull distribution analysis

The temporal interval between amitriptyline initiation and adverse event manifestation, designated as time-to-onset (TTO), was calculated by subtracting the drug start date (START_DT) from the event onset date (EVENT_DT). Records with incomplete dates, illogical timing (event before treatment), or intervals beyond the study window were removed. To characterize how adverse event risk evolved over the treatment course, we fitted a Weibull distribution model governed by two fundamental parameters: scale (α) and shape (β). The scale parameter (α) determined the temporal spread of the distribution function, with smaller values indicating a more concentrated occurrence pattern and larger values signifying broader temporal dispersion. The shape parameter (β) captured the dynamic nature of risk variation throughout the exposure period. Three distinct risk profiles emerged based on the 95% confidence interval (CI) bounds for β. A declining hazard over time, consistent with early failure dynamics, was inferred when the upper bound of the 95% CI fell below unity. A stable hazard profile corresponding to random failure patterns was identified when β approximated one, and its 95% CI bracketed this value, reducing the distribution to an exponential form. Conversely, an escalating hazard indicative of wear-out failure was recognized when the lower bound of the 95% CI surpassed one.

Results

Clinical characteristics description

As Fig. 1 illustrated, after deduplication, 4,063,184 records were eliminated as duplicates from the initial 24,389,996 entries in the DEMO dataset, yielding 20,326,782 unique reports. Among this refined dataset, amitriptyline was identified as the primary suspected drug in 5301 cases, comprising 20,451 recorded adverse events. Table 1 summarized the baseline characteristics of these cases. A substantially larger proportion of events was reported by females (58.6%, n = 3106) compared to males (29.7%, n = 1574). Adults between the ages of 18 and 64.9 (51.7%, n = 2743) constituted the largest age group, followed by those between the ages of 65 and 85 (17.7%, n = 937). More than half of the reports (68.7%, n = 3641) were classified as serious. Geographically, the United States accounted for the largest percentage of the reports (46.9%, n = 2446), followed by the United Kingdom (21.2%, n = 1103) and Germany (7.3%, n = 383). Healthcare professionals submitted nearly half of the reports (46.1%, n = 2445), followed by consumers (31.0%, n = 1643). The number of reports peaked in 2019 and remained relatively elevated over the next five years.

Signal mining results at SOC levels

Table 2 showed the SOC-level signals of amitriptyline-related adverse events. A total of 27 SOCs were detected (Fig. 2). The most frequently reported SOCs were Nervous System Disorders [n = 4875, ROR (95% CI) = 2.18 (2.11–2.25)], Psychiatric Disorders [n = 4276, ROR (95% CI) = 2.97 (2.87–3.06)], and Injury, Poisoning And Procedural Complications [n = 3650, ROR (95% CI) = 1.2 (1.16–1.24)], whereas Psychiatric Disorders [n = 4276, ROR (95% CI) = 2.97 (2.87–3.06)], Congenital, Familial And Genetic Disorders [n = 206, ROR (95% CI) = 2.39 (2.08–2.74)], and Cardiac Disorders [n = 1702, ROR (95% CI) = 2.38 (2.27–2.5)] showed strongest signals. Other SOCs, such as Vascular Disorders [n = 697, ROR (95% CI) = 1.14 (1.06–1.23)] and Eye Disorders [n = 645, ROR (95% CI) = 1.11 (1.02–1.2)], also exhibited robust signals. These findings indicated that amitriptyline-related psychiatric, neurological, and cardiac adverse reactions should be treated with increased vigilance. Additionally, the great signals exhibited by Congenital, Familial and Genetic Disorders in our research warranted further attention.

Signal mining results at PT levels

We systematically assessed and ranked all amitriptyline-related adverse events, focusing particularly on the top 50 PTs by report frequency (Fig. 3) and the top 50 by ROR signal strength (Fig. 4). Table 3 displayed the 50 most commonly reported PTs associated with amitriptyline in the FAERS database. Among these, completed suicide, overdose, drug interaction, intentional overdose, somnolence, confusional state, suicide attempt, hypotension, electrocardiogram QT prolonged, and loss of consciousness were the 10 most commonly reported. These results were in line with the adverse effects listed in the package insert, with the exception of electrocardiogram QT prolonged. Whereas the PTs with the greatest signals were oculocephalogyric reflex absent, electrocardiogram QRS complex, pseudophaeochromocytoma, electrocardiogram QRS complex prolonged, anion gap abnormal, electrocardiogram QT interval, persistent genital arousal disorder, antidepressant drug level increased, anticholinergic syndrome, and urine osmolarity decreased. Additionally, potential adverse reactions that were not listed in the package insert included drug abuse, cardio-respiratory arrest, cardiac arrest, metabolic acidosis, respiratory depression, mydriasis, bradycardia, sedation, cognitive disorder, myoclonus, circulatory collapse, and pneumonia aspiration.

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analysis of amitriptyline-related adverse events indicated that, among the 50 most frequent adverse events, migraine, depressed mood, abnormal dreams, and coordination abnormal were unique to males, whereas hyperhidrosis, cardiac arrest, arrhythmia, and bradycardia were exclusive to females (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Additionally, the age subgroup analysis showed that the most commonly reported adverse event in pediatric patients was fetal exposure during pregnancy, suggesting cases in which maternal use of amitriptyline during gestation led to drug exposure in gestation. This result highlighted the need for increased awareness of amitriptyline application in pregnant women and its potential impact on neonatal and early childhood outcomes. The most common adverse outcomes among those aged 18 to 65 were completed suicide and overdose. Confusional state and drug interaction were the most frequently reported adverse events in patients aged over 65 years. Details were presented in Supplementary Tables 5–7.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the impact of reporter type on signal detection, after excluding consumer-submitted reports (n = 1,643, 31.0%) and reports with missing reporter information (n = 1,213, 22.9%), we conducted a reanalysis of the top 50 positive adverse events at the PT level restricted to reports submitted by healthcare professionals only (n = 2,445, 46.1%). As presented in Supplementary Table 8 and Supplementary Fig. 1, the potential adverse events remained essentially consistent with the previous findings, such as drug abuse, electrocardiogram QT prolonged, cardio-respiratory arrest, cardiac arrest, metabolic acidosis, respiratory depression, mydriasis, bradycardia, sedation, myoclonus, circulatory collapse, pneumonia aspiration, and specific congenital anomalies (e.g., syndactyly, polydactyly, and adactyly).

Time to onset and Weibull distribution analysis of adverse events

As demonstrated by Fig. 5, after reports of missing or incorrect start/onset dates were excluded, 688 adverse events were gathered, with the majority occurring from 0 to 30 days (n = 372, 52.8%), followed by over 360 days (n = 117, 16.2%). Additionally, adverse events were also noted at the following time periods: in 31–60 days (n = 75, 10.6%), 61–90 days (n = 24, 3.4%), 91–120 days (n = 22, 3.1%), 121–150 days (n = 15, 2.1%), 151–180 days (n = 12, 1.7%), and 181–360 days (n = 67, 9.5%). Fig. 6 presented the cumulative incidence curve of adverse events. The median onset time for amitriptyline-related adverse events was 25 days (Q1–Q3: 5–168). Weibull distribution analysis revealed an early failure mode that reflected a decline in the occurrence of adverse events with time. The specific analytical parameters were summarized in Table 4. Of note, inadequate reporting details may compromise representativeness and introduce selection bias, necessitating cautious interpretation of these time-to-onset findings.

Discussion

The increasing global use of amitriptyline across diverse populations has intensified demands for comprehensive safety surveillance. The FDA Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) represents one of the most extensive international resources for post-marketing pharmacovigilance research. By leveraging this large-scale database, the current study systematically analyzed and characterized amitriptyline’s risk signals, providing a deeper understanding of its real-world safety profile and offering evidence-based guidance for clinical practice.

Given the baseline information, a substantially higher proportion of adverse events was reported by female patients compared to male patients, which was consistent with the sex-specific epidemiology of depression. According to epidemiological research, depression appears more widespread in women than in men. The start of puberty has been associated with an increased prevalence of depression in women, which continues throughout the period of reproduction while oestradiol levels are constantly fluctuating [20,21]. It could also be impacted by women’s greater reporting rates and preferences [22]. In terms of age distribution, patients between the ages of 18 and 64 constituted the largest group, followed by those between the ages of 65 and 85. This probably reflects the wider use of amitriptyline in these age groups. Concerning reporter type, the majority of reports came from healthcare providers and consumers, highlighting the cooperative roles that many stakeholders played in monitoring adverse events. Regionally, the United States contributed the preponderance of reports, a distribution congruent with amitriptyline’s status as an FDA-sanctioned agent and its prior commercial launch in that jurisdiction [23]. About clinical outcomes, serious cases were more commonly observed, implying that certain adverse manifestations possess elevated clinical gravity and merit focused attention. Future studies leveraging machine learning algorithms combined with structured electronic health records or prospective cohort studies that provide complete covariate information could enable the development of predictive models for severe amitriptyline-related adverse events, thereby advancing from signal detection to individualized risk stratification and informing clinical decision-making. Temporally, amitriptyline-associated adverse event reports peaked in 2019 and subsequently remained at comparatively elevated levels throughout the ensuing five-year interval. This tendency may correspond to augmenting psychosocial stressors, given the progressively rising depression incidence accompanying intensified societal competition [24]. Notably, depression occurrence among pediatric, adolescent, and young adult populations increased markedly during and after the COVID-19 pandemic [25,26].

As an antidepressant, amitriptyline caused adverse reactions that were closely related to its pharmacological processes and predominantly impacted the psychiatric and nervous systems. Notably, our study identified a number of risk signals associated with suicide, such as completed suicide, suicide attempt, and suicidal ideation. In 2004, the FDA required boxed warnings for increased suicidal thoughts and behaviors in children and adolescents on all antidepressants; this requirement was extended to include patients up to age 25 in 2007 [27]. While epidemiological studies suggested population-level suicide incidence lowered with greater antidepressant prescribing [28,29], meta-analyses and observational studies revealed enhanced suicide risk during the early period of antidepressant treatment, especially in adolescents [[30], [31], [32]]. This contradiction may reflect the temporal dynamics of treatment response. The increased risk observed during early treatment potentially involves antidepressant-related activation syndrome, whereas the long-term protective effect may emerge only after the therapeutic latency period. Activation, characterized by increased activity, impulsivity, and agitation, constitutes a significant risk factor for suicidality [33]. Similar to other antidepressants, amitriptyline’s therapeutic onset typically begins at approximately two to four weeks, and this characteristic delay in clinical benefit may be linked to activation [34]. Specifically, during the initial treatment phase, amitriptyline elevates synaptic monoamine concentrations, including serotonin and norepinephrine. This neurochemical perturbation might destabilize affect and behavior. During this early window, patients may experience restoration of motivation and energy prior to mood improvement, thereby creating a critical period during which suicidal ideation may be translated into action. Additionally, in certain vulnerable subpopulations, such as depressed patients with unrecognized bipolar disorder, amitriptyline may trigger manic or mixed states, further amplifying suicide risk [35]. Therefore, close monitoring is required, especially for younger patients, with intensified follow-up during the initial weeks and after dosage adjustments, including assessment of mood, agitation, akathisia, and suicidal ideation. A low starting dose with slow, planned titration is recommended to mitigate treatment-related activation.

Drug misuse is a prevalent and potentially hazardous form of intoxication [36]. Our study detected several signals indicative of a potential for abuse, including overdose, intentional overdose, and drug abuse. Although amitriptyline is not a traditional addictive drug like opioids or benzodiazepines, its pharmacology may contribute to misuse. Its potent anticholinergic effects may, at certain dosages, produce “dissociative” or hallucinogenic-like experiences that can be sought by some individuals [37,38]. Furthermore, the initial “activating” or mood-elevating effects from elevated monoamine levels, along with the sedation and confusion that can occur with overdose, may be exploited for self-medication or to achieve an altered mental state [9,39]. Preclinical research indicated that at high doses, its metabolite nortriptyline exerts non-competitive antagonism at N-methyl-d-aspartate (NMDA) receptors, producing psychotropic effects partially resembling those of ketamine, which may constitute a potential pharmacological trigger for abuse [[40], [41], [42]]. The signals observed in this study suggested that the abuse and overdose risks of amitriptyline in real-world settings may be more pronounced than previously recognized. Consequently, clinicians are advised to assess this risk before prescribing, especially for patients with a history of drug addiction or impulsive behavior, and to further enhance medication safety education and monitoring.

Another system severely affected by amitriptyline was the cardiac system. Our analysis identified significant associations between amitriptyline and conditions including QT prolongation, QRS complex widening, cardiorespiratory arrest, cardiac arrest, arrhythmia, ventricular tachycardia, and sinus tachycardia, findings that were generally consistent with prior literature. Animal research by Nagano et al. demonstrated the extensive electrocardiographic effects of amitriptyline [43], while retrospective studies by Javadipour et al. reported electrocardiographic abnormalities such as QRS widening and QT prolongation in patients receiving tricyclic antidepressants [44]. Furthermore, an observational study encompassing 30 case reports and 14 clinical trials showed that amitriptyline and its metabolite nortriptyline were associated with an increased cardiac toxicity risk, including heart block, tachycardia, arrhythmia, and cardiac arrest [45]. The precise pathophysiological mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated. However, amitriptyline appears to activate ryanodine receptors in ventricular myocytes, increasing calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum while inhibiting calcium reuptake, effects that collectively contribute to cardiac toxicity [46]. Additionally, amitriptyline blocks potassium channels encoded by the human ether-a-go-go-related gene (hERG), leading to electrocardiographic abnormalities [47]. Notably, this cardiotoxicity exhibits clear concentration dependence [48]. At conventional therapeutic doses, risk is generally controllable; however, in overdose scenarios, as suggested by the overdose and intentional overdose signals detected in this study, sharply elevated plasma concentrations cause nonlinear amplification of ion channel blockade, substantially increasing the risk of severe conduction disturbances, malignant arrhythmias, and cardiac arrest [45]. These results suggest that clinicians should remain vigilant for amitriptyline cardiac risks, especially in patients with cardiovascular disease or those taking QT-prolonging drugs. Individualized ECG monitoring, careful dose titration, and avoiding supratherapeutic doses may be considered.

Additionally, our study identified adverse events associated with the Congenital, Familial and Genetic Disorders system, including adactyly, syndactyly, and polydactyly, which had been rarely reported in previous studies. A few of these events demonstrated high signal strength, warranting focused attention. Antidepressant exposure and poor newborn outcomes have received greater attention due to the rising prevalence of peripartum depression [49]. A systematic review reported associations between antidepressant use and multiple adverse pregnancy outcomes, including preterm birth and neonatal morbidity [50,51]. Cohort study findings further indicated that prenatal amitriptyline exposure significantly increased the risk of ocular, auricular, facial, and cervical defects in neonates [52]. The potent anticholinergic properties of amitriptyline and its effects on multiple neurotransmitter systems, including serotonin and norepinephrine, may theoretically disrupt critical developmental signaling pathways during embryogenesis, particularly those involving neural crest cell migration and limb bud formation, potentially contributing to the observed limb anomalies [53,54]. Our findings suggested the presence of potential signals for specific congenital anomalies, particularly limb abnormalities, that might have been previously underrecognized. It is noteworthy that the signals identified in pharmacovigilance represent statistical associations rather than causal relationships. Given the inherent limitations of spontaneous reporting systems, including the absence of information on potentially important confounders such as maternal underlying disease, smoking, and alcohol consumption, as well as reporting biases, these signals required interpretation with considerable caution. To further evaluate the robustness of these signals, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding reports with concomitant use of known teratogens commonly co-prescribed with amitriptyline, including valproic acid, carbamazepine, phenytoin, topiramate, and lithium. As shown in Supplementary Table 9 and Supplementary Fig. 2, after excluding these reports, persistent congenital anomaly signals included adactyly, syndactyly, and polydactyly. Additionally, some studies indicated that antidepressants affecting serotonin reuptake during embryonic development may raise the risk of overall and organ-specific malformations [52,55]. Therefore, considering the lack of studies on this subject and the possibility of gestational exposure, we remained cautious about amitriptyline exposure during pregnancy, even though definitive mechanisms have not been established. Further research is necessary to clarify the underlying processes. Nevertheless, given these signals, for women of childbearing potential, we emphasized the importance of preconception counseling, confirmation of pregnancy status before prescribing, and folic acid supplementation to mitigate potential teratogenic risks, in alignment with standard prenatal care recommendations.

We also identified drug interaction and somnolence among the most frequently reported adverse events associated with amitriptyline. As a drug primarily metabolized by CYP2D6 and CYP2C19, amitriptyline has been documented to interact with multiple medications, including carbamazepine, cimetidine, and omeprazole [56,57]. These interactions are closely related to the inhibition or induction of metabolic enzymes. Concurrently, the broad effects of amitriptyline on multiple neurotransmitter systems render it susceptible to synergistic or additive effects on sedation, respiratory depression, and cardiotoxicity when co-administered with other central nervous system depressants such as alcohol, benzodiazepines, and opioids [58]. Therefore, clinicians should conduct comprehensive medication reconciliation and avoid known high-risk combinations during prescribing. Furthermore, as a multimodal antidepressant, amitriptyline influences various neurotransmitter systems, including serotonin, norepinephrine, and histamine, which may precipitate somnolence in susceptible individuals. Specifically, amitriptyline potently antagonizes central histamine H1 receptors, producing marked sedative effects; simultaneously, its serotonin reuptake inhibition and anticholinergic activity further synergistically exacerbate somnolence [59,60]. Clinically, amitriptyline-related somnolence may reduce attention, impair cognitive function, and negatively impact daily activities, necessitating close patient monitoring and individualized treatment strategies to mitigate its effects on functional outcomes.

Interestingly, we detected pneumonia aspiration within the Infections and Infestations category. The underlying mechanism likely involves two synergistic pharmacological pathways. First, amitriptyline’s potent anticholinergic activity induces xerostomia, which reduces salivary flow and compromises the mechanical cleansing and antimicrobial (e.g., lysozyme, secretory IgA) protective functions of saliva, thereby promoting oropharyngeal bacterial overcolonization and increasing the risk that aspirated microorganisms reach the lower airways [[61], [62], [63]]. Second, amitriptyline’s central sedative effects may lower consciousness and interfere with cough and swallow reflexes [15]. These effects collectively compromise the protective airway defense mechanisms, thereby increasing the risk of oropharyngeal or gastric content aspiration, particularly in vulnerable patients such as the elderly, those with neurological disorders, or individuals with swallowing dysfunction. The association between high anticholinergic burden and increased pneumonia risk has been well documented in large cohort studies [[64], [65], [66]]. Given these findings, we recommend that clinicians assess patients’ swallowing function before prescribing, particularly in older adults, patients with neurodegenerative diseases, or those receiving concomitant central nervous system depressants (e.g., benzodiazepines, opioids). Periodic reassessment during treatment is also advised.

Subgroup analyses revealed distinct sex-specific adverse event patterns: female patients warranted enhanced vigilance for cardiac arrest, arrhythmia, and bradycardia, whereas male patients required additional monitoring for migraine, abnormal dreams, and coordination abnormalities. These disparities likely reflect multifactorial interactions, including established sex differences in cardiac electrophysiology, hormone levels, drug metabolism, and medication responsiveness [[67], [68], [69]]. Age-stratified analyses revealed that fetal exposure reports predominated among pediatric patients, suggesting transplacental drug transfer following use during pregnancy [70,71]. Among adults aged 18–65 years, overdose and completed suicide emerged as the most frequent outcomes, highlighting misuse potential and suicide risk. In patients older than 65 years, confusion and drug interactions predominated, consistent with heightened vulnerability to anticholinergic burden and polypharmacy complications [72]. Nevertheless, given the limitations of spontaneous reporting systems, including potential biases and the inability to establish true incidence rates, these observations required cautious interpretation and future prospective validation studies.

According to the analysis of onset time, 52.84% of amitriptyline-related adverse events occurred during the first 30 days, emphasizing the significance of timely monitoring. Following that, the frequency of adverse events decreased, which was consistent with our Weibull model’s early failure pattern and the continued risk reduction [73]. This temporal pattern aligns with prior clinical evidence demonstrating that amitriptyline related adverse events tend to concentrate in the early treatment phase. Leucht et al. documented that anticholinergic symptoms, including dry mouth, visual disturbances, and constipation, reached their highest frequency within the first one to five weeks of therapy [74]. Likewise, Brueckle et al. reported that the greatest burden of adverse events among amitriptyline users emerged during the initial weeks of administration [15]. Notably, 26.14% happened after six months, which may reflect interindividual metabolic variation, cumulative exposure, or delayed toxicity [75,76]. This highlights that for long-term users, adverse reactions can still occur after six months, necessitating continued regular follow-up to capture rare but clinically significant occurrences.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the FAERS database is a spontaneous reporting system, which inherently suffers from underreporting (e.g., clinicians and patient providers may not recognize some symptoms as adverse events or fail to report them), reporting biases (such as awareness bias regarding adverse events), and incomplete information (e.g., missing data on medication dosage, patient demographics, and treatment duration). Additionally, pharmacovigilance signal identification mainly offers associative information; it estimates the strength of a statistical link rather than confirming a causal relationship between amitriptyline and the observed adverse events. Third, the geographic distribution of reports was uneven, with the majority originating from the United States and Europe, while data from Asia, Africa, and other regions were limited. This imbalance may restrict the generalizability of our findings across different ethnic populations and healthcare settings. Finally, due to the lack of detailed clinical information in the database, we were unable to adequately control for potential confounders, including differences in baseline patient characteristics, comorbidities, severity of underlying illness, and genetic predispositions that could affect adverse event occurrence. External validation through prospective studies is crucial. Future research adopting prospective cohort designs, incorporating multicenter and multiethnic populations, is warranted to validate and extend our findings, thereby providing a more comprehensive safety profile to inform clinical practice.

In conclusion, by utilizing the large-scale FAERS database, this study provided a comprehensive characterization of the real-world adverse event spectrum of amitriptyline. These results offered novel insight that further contributed to amitriptyline’s more thorough safety profile and provided significant guidance regarding its use in clinical settings. Further comprehensive prospective, multicenter clinical trials and epidemiological research are warranted to confirm these findings and more accurately assess amitriptyline’s safety.
