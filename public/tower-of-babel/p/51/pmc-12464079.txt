Design, Optimization, and Analytical Performance Evaluation of LAMP-Based Rapid Detection Assay for Severe Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome Virus
Authors: Han-Heom Na, Hee-Jeong Youk, In Bum Suh, Sang Hoon Lee, Yoonsung Kang, Keun-Cheol Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesSevere Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome Virus (SFTSV), a tick-borne pathogen, presents a growing public health threat in East Asia. Although conventional RT-PCR methods are effective for detection, they are limited by the need for specialized equipment and time-consuming procedures. This study aimed to develop and evaluate a rapid, sensitive, and field-deployable diagnostic method for SFTSV using Loop-Mediated Isothermal Amplification (LAMP).MethodsTwelve sets of LAMP primers were designed to target the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (L segment) of the SFTSV genome. This diagnostic tool could enhance early detection and improve outbreak response for SFTSV infections.Keywords: LAMP, SFTS virus, molecular diagnostics, primer screening, efficiency, POCT

INTRODUCTION

Severe Fever with Thrombocytopenia Syndrome (SFTS) is an emerging tick-borne zoonotic disease caused by the SFTS virus (SFTSV), a novel phlebovirus of the Phenuiviridae family [1]. Since its initial identification in rural China in 2009, SFTS has become endemic in South Korea and Japan, posing a significant public health concern due to its increasing incidence and high mortality rate [2, 3]. Clinically, SFTS is characterized by acute fever, gastrointestinal symptoms, thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, and, in severe cases, multi-organ failure.

Early and accurate detection of SFTSV is critical for timely treatment and effective public health interventions. Currently, real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) is the standard diagnostic method, offering high sensitivity and specificity by targeting viral RNA [1, 4, 5]. However, RT-PCR requires expensive thermal cyclers, RNA extraction systems, and trained personnel, which limits its use in resource-limited or field settings.

To address these limitations, Loop-Mediated Isothermal Amplification (LAMP) has emerged as a promising alternative [6]. LAMP is a nucleic acid amplification technique that operates under isothermal conditions (typically 60℃-65℃), allowing for rapid detection with minimal equipment, such as a heat block or water bath [7, 8]. Initially described by Notomi et al. in 2000, LAMP has been widely adopted for the detection of various infectious agents due to its speed, robustness, and high amplification efficiency [7-9].

More recently, reverse transcription LAMP (RT-LAMP) has been adapted for RNA virus detection, including Zika, Dengue, Influenza, and SARS-CoV-2, demonstrating its potential in low-resource diagnostic applications [8, 10]. Several studies have reported LAMP-based assays for SFTSV, highlighting promising sensitivity and specificity [11-15]. These assays offer potential as portable, field-deployable diagnostic tools that enable rapid screening and real-time epidemiological monitoring [16, 17]. Nevertheless, many existing LAMP assays require further optimization, in terms of reaction time, specificity, and primer efficiency.

The SFTSV genome consists of three segments of negative-sense single-stranded RNA: Large (L), Medium (M), and Small (S) [18]. The L segment encodes RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), essential for viral RNA synthesis and replication. The M segment encodes envelope glycoproteins Gn and Gc, which mediate host cell attachment and entry. The S segment encodes the nucleoprotein (N), which encapsidates the viral RNA, and a non-structural protein that modulates host immune responses and contributes to viral pathogenesis [19, 20]. Among these segments, the L segment is highly conserved across SFTSV strains, making it an ideal target for molecular diagnostic assays [11].

In this study, we aimed to develop a rapid, sensitive, and specific RT-LAMP assay targeting the L segment of the SFTSV genome. We hypothesized that the optimized LAMP assay would achieve diagnostic performance comparable to that of RT-PCR, while providing additional advantages in terms of speed, simplicity, and suitability for field deployment. To this end, we designed and screened a series of novel primer sets to identify the optimal target region. Our results demonstrate that the newly developed LAMP assay reduces detection time by 15 to 30 minutes compared with conventional LAMP protocols, enabling the reliable detection of SFTSV within 30 to 60 minutes. These findings advance the development of accessible diagnostic tools that facilitate early intervention and improved outbreak management in SFTSV-endemic regions [4, 10, 12, 21].

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Sample collection

SFTSV RNA (NCCP No. 43260) was obtained from the National Culture Collection for Pathogens (NCCP). Negative control samples, including various viral and bacterial strains, were also acquired from the NCCP. All samples were stored at –80℃ until further processing.

2. Construction of plasmid containing partial SFTSV L segment

A recombinant plasmid, pMG-Amp (2,710 bp), was constructed by inserting a 432 bp fragment of the SFTSV L segment into the multiple cloning site. Two amplification targets within this insert—designated L1-2 (green) and L3-2 (red)—were selected for evaluation with LAMP primer sets. The precise locations of these amplification regions are indicated within the insert sequence.

3. Primer design for LAMP

The SFTSV L segment (6,368 bp) was divided into four fragments (L1 to L4). LAMP primers targeting each fragment were designed using PrimerExplorer V5 software. Fragment 1: Three primer sets (L1-1, L1-2, and L1-3) were designed to amplify the 5’ region of the L segment, corresponding to the RdRp coding sequence. Fragment 2: Two sets (L2-1 and L2-2) were developed to increase coverage of an alternative RdRp region. Fragment 3: Four sets (L3-1 through L3-4) were designed to amplify multiple sites within the central region of the L segment, thereby improving robustness and minimizing false negatives. Fragment 4: Three sets (L4-1 to L4-3) were designed to cover the remaining region of the L segment. These primers were optimized to achieve faster amplification compared with conventional LAMP primers, reducing detection time by 15-30 minutes. For comparison, an RT-PCR primer set targeting the same genomic region was also employed [8].

4. Colorimetric LAMP assay

Colorimetric LAMP assays were performed to evaluate the performance of each primer set, using the 2X Colorimetric LAMP Master Mix (M1800L, New England Biolabs, MA, USA). Each 25 µL reaction contained 12.5 µL of master mix, 1.6 µM each of forward inner primer (FIP) and backward inner primer (BIP) primers, 0.2 µM each of forward outer primer (F3) and backward outer primer (B3) primers, 2 µL of template RNA, and nuclease-free water. Reactions were incubated at 65℃ for 60 minutes using a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System. Amplification was monitored by visual color change: positive reactions changed from pink to yellow, whereas negative reactions remained pink [22]. Additionally, time-course measurements were recorded at 0, 15, 20, 25, 30, and 50 minutes to assess amplification speed. Primer sets that produced a detectable color change within 20 minutes were considered optimal and selected for further analysis.

5. Fluorescent LAMP assay using SYBR green

The RT-LAMP assay was performed using a fluorescent-based detection method on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System. Each 20 µL reaction mixture consisted of 2 µL of 10× Isothermal Amplification Buffer (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), 0.8 µL of 100 mM MgSO₄, 2 µL of 10 mM dNTPs (Enzynomics, Daejeon, Korea), 1.6 µM each of FIP and BIP, 0.2 µM each of F3 and B3, 0.4 µM each of loop forward primer (LoopF) and loop backward primer (LoopB) primers, 2 µL of 20X SFC Green dye (Biofact, Daejeon, Korea), 8 U of Bst DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), and SFTSV RNA template ranging from 1 nanogram (ng) to 10 attograms (ag). Reactions were incubated at 65℃ for 40 minutes, and real-time fluorescence was monitored using SYBR-based detection. Data were analyzed using Bio-Rad CFX Manager software. The optimized primer design enabled detectable signal generation within 15-30 minutes, offering faster results than previously reported LAMP protocols [8].

RESULTS

1. Design of LAMP primers targeting the L segment of the SFTS virus

The genome of SFTSV comprises three negative-sense, single-stranded RNA segments: L, M, and S, each encoding essential viral proteins [23]. The L segment encodes the RdRp, a key enzyme required for viral replication and transcription. Owing to its high degree of conservation and central role in the viral life cycle, the L segment represents a valuable target for diagnostic development and antiviral research. A total of 12 LAMP primer sets were designed to target distinct regions of the L segment using specialized primer design software. Based on genomic location, the primer sets were grouped into four fragments (Fig. 1). Fragment 1 (L1-1, L1-2, L1-3) targeted the RdRp coding sequence. Fragment 2 (L2-1, L2-2) covered an adjacent RdRp region to broaden detection coverage. Fragment 3 (L3-1 through L3-4) targeted multiple loci within the central region of the L segment to enhance amplification robustness and reduce false negatives. Fragment 4 (L4-1, L4-2, L4-3) covered the remaining downstream portion of the segment. All primers were designed to generate amplicons ranging from 150 to 220 bp, and regions with minimal homology to RNA polymerases from other viruses were preferentially selected to ensure assay specificity.

(A) This panel illustrates the genomic and structural organization of SFTSV. The diagram shows the virion surrounded by a host-derived lipid envelope embedded with Gn and Gc glycoproteins. Inside the virion, the L, M, and S genomic segments are encapsulated by nucleoproteins (N) and associated with RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp), forming viral ribonucleoprotein complexes (vRNPs). (B) A total of 12 primers were designed to target the L segment, each selected for low sequence homology with L segments from other viruses to enhance specificity.

2. Screening of LAMP reactions using SFTS RNA samples

To assess the efficiency of the designed LAMP primer sets, an initial screening was performed using a colorimetric LAMP assay (Fig. 2A). Each LF and BF primer pair was individually evaluated in a 2× Colorimetric LAMP Master Mix under standard reaction conditions. Primer performance was determined by the presence of a visible color change at 30 and 50 minutes post-incubation. Among the 12 primer sets tested, four (L1-2, L3-1, L3-2, and L4-3) exhibited distinct color changes, indicating effective amplification (Fig. 2B). These four sets were subsequently assessed in complete LAMP reactions, incorporating all six primers (F3, B3, LF, BF, FIP, and BIP). All sets produced detectable color changes within 30 minutes, with L1-2 and L3-2 yielding signals as early as 15 minutes (Fig. 2C). To further evaluate the performance of L1-2 and L3-2, synthetic plasmid DNA (pDNA) corresponding to each target region was prepared. Sensitivity testing demonstrated that the LAMP assay could reliably detect as little as 100 ag of target DNA. However, the L1-2 primers exhibited nonspecific amplification, including false-positive signals in negative controls. In contrast, the L3-2 primers demonstrated both high sensitivity and specificity, establishing them as the most suitable candidate for assay development. Subsequent LAMP assays using serial dilutions of template DNA ranging from 10 ag to 10 pg confirmed that the L3-2 primer set consistently produced specific amplification down to 100 ag, with no signal observed in the negative control. Collectively, these results identified L3-2 as the optimal primer set for a diagnostic LAMP assay for SFTSV.

Stepwise Optimization of LAMP Primers for Rapid and Sensitive SFTSV Detection Using a Colorimetric Assay. (A) Workflow of primer screening to identify optimal primer sets. The first round was conducted without loop primers, the second round included loop primers and utilized colorimetric evaluation, and the third round assessed concentration-dependent amplification efficiency. (B) Reactions were performed at 65℃ to identify the most effective primer sets for SFTSV RNA detection. Red arrows indicate primer sets that successfully produced amplification. (C) Secondary screening incorporating loop primer optimization revealed that all four primer sets yielded visible colorimetric changes. The L1-2 and L3-2 primer sets produced the fastest amplification. (D) Colorimetric LAMP assays targeting the L3-2 region were performed using serial dilutions of pDNA ranging from 10 ag to 10 pg. Tubes were observed at 0, 20, and 25 minutes post-reaction initiation. By 20 minutes, color changes from pink to yellow were evident for reactions containing 10 pg to 100 ag of template. By 25 minutes, amplification was detectable in all dilutions down to 10 ag. The negative control (N) remained pink, demonstrating high assay specificity. These results indicate that the L3-2 primer set enables rapid and ultrasensitive detection of SFTSV, with a detection limit in the attogram range.

3. Sensitivity test using serial dilutions of SFTS virus RNA

To enhance detection accuracy and minimize ambiguity in result interpretation, fluorescent dyes such as SYBR Green were employed in place of colorimetric indicators [24]. Using the L3-2 primer set, LAMP assays were performed with serial dilutions of SFTS RNA ranging from 10 ag to 1 ng in the presence of SYBR Green or equivalent fluorescent reagents. At a template concentration of 1 ng, amplification was detected within 2 minutes (Fig. 3). A clear concentration-dependent amplification profile was observed, with even the lowest RNA concentration (10 ag) yielding a detectable signal within approximately 30 minutes. These results confirm that the L3-2 primer set provides highly sensitive and rapid detection of SFTSV RNA, supporting its utility for diagnostic applications.

Sensitivity of the LAMP assay using serial dilutions of SFTSV RNA. The sensitivity of the LAMP assay was evaluated using SFTSV RNA samples ranging from 10 ag to 1 ng. Sequential amplification across this concentration range using the 3-2 primer set demonstrates high analytical sensitivity.

4. Specificity test via cross-reactivity evaluation

To evaluate the specificity of the LAMP assay, cross-reactivity tests were performed using nucleic acids from a panel of non-target pathogens commonly present in clinical and environmental samples. This panel included Adenovirus, Chikungunya virus, Dengue virus-1, Encephalitis virus, Campylobacter jejuni, Enterococcus faecium, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Escherichia coli. SFTSV RNA was included as a positive control. As shown in Fig. 4, strong amplification signals were observed exclusively in the SFTSV-positive samples (samples 1 and 2), with fluorescence curves crossing the threshold between cycles 20 and 30. No amplification was detected in any of the non-SFTSV samples or in the negative control throughout 50 cycles, with fluorescence levels remaining below threshold. These results demonstrate that the LAMP assay is highly specific for SFTSV and does not exhibit cross-reactivity with other viral or bacterial genomes tested. The absence of false-positives signals further underscores the assay’s suitability for clinical and field-based diagnostic applications.

Specificity of the LAMP assay against viral and bacterial samples. Samples 1 and 2, containing SFTSV RNA at 100 pg and 10 pg, respectively, exhibited strong and early amplification signals. No amplification was observed in non-target samples, including Adenovirus, Chikungunya virus, Dengue virus-1, Japanese Encephalitis virus, and bacterial strains such as Campylobacter jejuni, Enterococcus faecium, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Escherichia coli. These results confirm the high specificity of the LAMP assay.

DISCUSSION

We developed a LAMP-based molecular diagnostic assay targeting the L segment of the SFTSV and evaluated its performance in terms of sensitivity, specificity, and practical applicability. The results demonstrate that the newly designed 3-2 primer set enables rapid and accurate detection of SFTSV, offering advantages over conventional diagnostic methods, particularly in resource-limited settings.

The selection of the L segment as a diagnostic target was strategic, as this region encodes the RdRp, which is essential for viral replication and highly conserved across SFTSV strains [25]. Compared with the M and S segments, which exhibit greater sequence variability, targeting the L segment enhances assay robustness and reliability. Although prior studies have often focused on the S segment, owing to its shorter length and accessibility, our findings highlight the value of the L segment in achieving both high sensitivity and cross-strain compatibility [26].

Among the 12 primer sets designed to target different regions of the L segment, the 3-2 set demonstrated superior performance in screening assays. It produced a detectable colorimetric signal within 15 minutes and maintained high sensitivity down to 100 ag of RNA. While other primer sets, such as L1-2, also showed early signal generation, they were prone to nonspecific amplification and false positives in negative controls, underscoring the importance of rigorous primer validation in LAMP-based diagnostics. In contrast, the 3-2 primer set exhibited high reproducibility, clear signal discrimination, and a minimal risk of false amplification.

To improve interpretation and reduce ambiguity associated with colorimetric readouts, SYBR-based fluorescence detection was incorporated into the assay protocol. This approach enhanced clarity and allowed precise monitoring of amplification dynamics [27]. Fluorescence signals were observable within 2 minutes at high RNA concentrations, and even at the lower detection limit of 10 ag, reliable amplification was achieved within 30 minutes. These results indicate that the 3-2 primer set provides a wide dynamic range and high analytical sensitivity, making it suitable for both clinical diagnosis and epidemiological screening.

Specificity testing using nucleic acids from a diverse panel of common bacterial and viral pathogens confirmed that the assay does not cross-react with non-target organisms, including other arboviruses such as Dengue and Chikungunya viruses. Only SFTSV RNA samples produced positive amplification signals, while all other samples remained negative throughout 50 cycles. This high specificity is critical for application in real-world settings, where the differential diagnosis of febrile illnesses is crucial, and false positives could lead to inappropriate clinical decisions and public health interventions.

Compared with the current gold standard of RT-PCR, the LAMP assay offers several practical advantages [28]. RT-PCR is highly sensitive and quantitative but requires thermal cycling equipment, RNA purification steps, and trained personnel, which restricts its use in rural or resource-limited settings [29]. In contrast, the LAMP assay developed in this study operates under isothermal conditions using simple equipment such as a heat block or water bath. When combined with visual or fluorescence-based readouts, this approach enables rapid, on-site diagnostics with minimal infrastructure. Furthermore, the total detection time of 30 to 60 minutes is substantially shorter than that of standard RT-PCR protocols.

Previous reports of SFTSV RT-LAMP assays have demonstrated potential, but often lacked rigorous primer optimization or did not achieve comparable detection limits [12]. Our assay, capable of detecting RNA at attogram levels, represents a substantial improvement over conventional LAMP methods and provides an important contribution to the point-of-care diagnostics for emerging viral infections.

Despite the encouraging results, some limitations warrant consideration. Sensitivity and specificity were assessed using purified RNA and synthetic DNA templates rather than clinical specimens; therefore, further validation using patient-derived samples from endemic regions is required to confirm the assay’s performance in real-world diagnostic scenarios. Additionally, while the use of fluorescence dyes enhances sensitivity and interpretability, it necessitates access to fluorescence devices, which may not be available in the most remote environments. Future studies could address this limitation by integrating lateral flow strips or smartphone-based fluorescence detection systems to enhance field applicability. Although the present assay focuses on the SFTSV RNA detection, the development of multiplex platforms capable of simultaneously detecting other febrile pathogens common in endemic areas—such as Dengue virus, Orientia tsutsugamushi, or Leptospira—would further enhance diagnostic utility and clinical decision-making. This approach is particularly relevant in regions where co-infections or clinically similar diseases complicate diagnosis based solely on symptoms.

The increasing geographic spread of SFTSV, likely driven by ecological changes and climate-mediated expansion of tick populations, underscores the urgent need for rapid and accessible diagnostic tools. The assay presented in this study addresses this need by offering a sensitive, specific, and practical alternative to laboratory-based diagnostics. Its simplicity and speed make it particularly suitable for deployment in field investigations, rural clinics, and border health checkpoints where access to advanced diagnostics is limited.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, we report the development of a LAMP assay targeting the L segment of SFTSV, featuring an optimized primer set that enables rapid, highly sensitive, and specific viral detection of the virus. The assay provides a practical alternative to RT-PCR, particularly in resource-limited and field settings, and makes a meaningful contribution to efforts aimed at early diagnosis and effective control of SFTS outbreaks. With further validation and potential integration into portable diagnostic platforms, this assay could serve as a valuable tool in the global response to emerging tick-borne diseases.
