Olanzapine effectiveness on weight gain in anorexia nervosa patients: systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Bushra F Alqurashi, Anas R Alserihi, Ahmed Albariqi, Abdulmajeed Ayesh Alenazi, Yosra Alhindi, Rawan Lafi Alghamdi, Bander Abdullah Alotaibi, Sobhia Nasser Abanmi, Fares Abdulrahman Alhejaili, Fatimah Hassan Nahhas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnorexia nervosa (AN) is a complex psychiatric disorder marked by extreme caloric restriction, intense fear of weight gain, and distorted body image perception. Not only influence physical health, but it also creates serious psychological burdens. Treatment of AN is especially daunting due to its multi-dimensional nature. Recent research indicates that a pharmacological approach, such as the prescription of atypical antipsychotics like Olanzapine, may help treat both the nutritional deficiency and psychological distress related to the disorder. By allowing an individual gain weight and potentially reducing comorbid depression and anxiety, Olanzapine can be a valuable add-on to standard treatments.AimThis study aims to investigate the effectiveness of Olanzapine in causing weight gain among anorexia nervosa patients. Based on comparisons of results in a number of clinical trials, the study aims at establishing the effect of Olanzapine on weight restoration and investigating any accompanying shift in the psychological state of the patients.MethodA comprehensive literature search was performed on Medline, ClinicalTrials.gov, Scopus, and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews for all records from the earliest date available through to August 22, 2024. Randomized controlled trials of olanzapine treatment in anorexia nervosa were included. Studies were screened, and data were extracted. Risk of bias was assessed with the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool. Records for meta-analysis were combined in a random-effects model to calculate effect sizes, and heterogeneity was quantified with the I2 statistic.Result207 records were found, 159 of which were excluded after title and abstract screening. After full-text review, seven RCTs were found. These trials involving 152 patients assessed the impact of olanzapine on weight gain in anorexia nervosa. Meta-analysis of 4 studies (91 patients) showed a mean difference in BMI of 1.68 kg/m² (95% CI: 0.82, 4.18) in favor of olanzapine but not statistically significant (p = 0.19), with high heterogeneity (I² = 94%). Sensitivity analysis reduced the heterogeneity to 28% but not the effect size.ConclusionAlthough olanzapine showed a numerically greater weight gain compared to control conditions, the pooled effect did not reach statistical significance and was associated with substantial heterogeneity. Therefore, current evidence remains inconclusive. Olanzapine may be considered in selected cases, particularly where psychological comorbidities are prominent, but larger and methodologically robust trials are needed before routine clinical use can be recommended.Clinical trial numberNot Applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08017-w.Keywords: Olanzapine, Anorexia nervosa, Weight gain, Systematic review, Meta-analysis, Psychological symptoms, Treatment efficacy, Atypical antipsychotics, Clinical trials, Mental health

Introduction

Anorexia nervosa (AN) is a broad and multidimensional eating disorder that includes rigorous food restriction, inappropriate fear of gaining weight, and distorted body image [1]. The debilitating condition mars the physical as well as mental health of patients, many times leading to serious medical complications and high mortality. The Complications involve all organ systems, including the cardiovascular (bradycardia, arrhythmias, hypotension), gastrointestinal (constipation, delayed gastric emptying), endocrine (amenorrhea, osteoporosis), hematologic (pancytopenia), neurologic (cerebral atrophy), pulmonary (respiratory failure, aspiration pneumonia), and metabolic (refeeding syndrome). The complications do not significantly alter the pulse and blood pressure vital signs, and in the most severe cases, can cause death [2]. AN is also extremely challenging for physicians to treat, with the necessity to balance stabilization and psychotherapy [3]. While the cornerstone of treatment continues to be psychotherapy, integration of pharmacological therapy has increasingly received attention as an adjuvant strategy to promote recovery [4, 5].

Olanzapine, a medication for schizophrenia and bipolar disorder that was developed as an atypical antipsychotic, has been suggested as one of the drugs to treat AN. Evidence suggests that olanzapine might cause weight gain and can reverse co-morbid affective symptoms such as anxiety, rigidity, and depressive symptoms, which in general are observed in this disorder [5, 6]. Early reports are that olanzapine increases appetite and improves mood, alleviating most of the complicated issues with AN [4, 7]. Its effectiveness remains unpredictable, and treatment response also fluctuates with age and length of illness [6].

Although previous systematic reviews have examined the role of olanzapine in anorexia nervosa, methodological differences warrant an updated evaluation. For instance, Han et al. (2022) included both randomized and non-randomized or open-label studies and pooled heterogeneous outcome measures such as body weight and BMI. Similarly, Çöpür et al. (2020) incorporated various study designs and provided primarily qualitative synthesis. In contrast, the present review restricts inclusion exclusively to randomized controlled trials to enhance internal validity and minimize methodological bias. Additionally, we prespecified BMI change as the primary outcome to promote greater outcome consistency and conducted sensitivity analyses to explore heterogeneity. By updating the search through August 2024, this study offers a contemporary reassessment of the available randomized evidence regarding olanzapine’s efficacy in anorexia nervosa [5, 6].

The following systematic review and meta-analysis suggest combining currently available literature regarding the efficacy of olanzapine in causing weight gain and reducing psychological symptoms in AN patients. By analyzing randomized controlled trials, the study aims to establish a robust and up-to-date evaluation of olanzapine use as an adjunctive pharmacologic therapy. In addition to assessing clinical effectiveness, this review also reflects gaps in the evidence and highlights the importance of individualized treatment plans for the patient based on the severity of the illness and comorbidities [7, 8]. The findings of this study are anticipated to guide clinicians in making informed choices in anorexia nervosa’s multidisciplinary treatment.

Methodology

The result of this systematic review was presented and synthesized by using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [9]. The study protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD42024579572.

Eligibility criteria

The study utilized the PICOS framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, and Study design). The selected studies examined patients with anorexia nervosa diagnosed according to the DSM 5/4/3 criteria who were treated with olanzapine. The comparison group consisted of patients receiving a placebo or other treatments. The outcome of primary interest was the change in weight. Only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were included. Single-arm non-RCTs, observational studies, case reports, and RCT protocols were excluded.

Search strategy & information sources

We carried out a thorough search of Medline, ClinicalTrials.gov, Scopus, and the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, including all records available since each database was established up to August 22, 2024. Database-specific search strategies were used, utilizing Boolean operators (AND, OR) to restrict the search results. The search terms’ keywords were those related to ‘Anorexia Nervosa,’ ‘Olanzapine,’ and ‘Randomized Controlled Trials.’ The non-English literature studies were excluded to eliminate any variation in the interpretation of the findings. A full description of the database-specific search queries is presented in the Supplementary Material.

Selection process

The retrieved studies were imported into the Rayyan software for title and abstract screening. Each study was independently reviewed by two coauthors, with any disagreements resolved through consultation with a third expert author. Subsequently, full-text screening was conducted independently by the authors. In cases of conflict, the study was re-evaluated, and a final decision was reached through consensus or contacting the third author.

Data extraction process

The data extraction process was done using Google Sheets. We extracted the following variables: author name, year of publication, country, study design, number of participants (intervention and control), intervention, control, comparison, and conclusion or outcome. Risk of bias

Risk of bias was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) [10]. This tool evaluates five domains: the process of randomization, departures from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selective report bias. From these domains, the overall risk of bias was graded as low, high, or unclear. Two independent reviewers, in pairs (S and F, B and A), undertook the assessments. Differences were resolved by consensus, restudy of the studies, or consensus with a third reviewer.

Quality assessment and statistical analysis

The efficacy of olanzapine in causing weight gain in patients with anorexia nervosa was measured by meta-analysis, in which the main outcome measure was body mass index (BMI). BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared. The meta-analysis was done using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4 with mean difference as the statistical method of data synthesis and presentation under a random-effects model. Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran’s guidelines, and the occurrence of an I² > 50% indicated important heterogeneity. Only studies reporting change in BMI as mean difference with corresponding standard deviation (SD) or sufficient data to calculate these values were included in the meta-analysis. Studies reporting outcomes solely as body weight (kg) or those lacking dispersion measures (e.g., SD, standard error, or confidence intervals) were included in the qualitative synthesis but excluded from pooling due to non-comparable outcome metrics. A sensitivity analysis was performed by recalculating the overall effect and sequentially excluding studies that affected consistency. The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) tool was used to determine the reliability of the evidence [11]. The small number of studies resulted in the publication bias could not be assessed using funnel plots or other methods like Egger’s test.

Result

Study selection

A total of 207 records were identified by searching four large databases: Medline (n = 55), ClinicalTrials.gov (n = 15), Cochrane (n = 24), and Scopus (n = 113). Following the removal of 48 duplicate records, 159 distinct studies were screened at the title and abstract level. Of these, 139 records were excluded because they did not meet the predetermined inclusion criteria. Full-text retrieval was facilitated for 20 studies, though three reports were not accessible. The remaining 17 studies underwent full-text eligibility screening, and following the exclusion of 4 studies for an inappropriate study design and 6 for an inappropriate study population, seven RCTs were determined to be eligible and included in the systematic review and meta-analysis. The list of studies is depicted in Fig. 1. Of the seven included RCTs, four provided sufficient data on BMI change (mean ± SD) and were included in the meta-analysis. Three studies were excluded from quantitative synthesis: two reported weight change in kilograms without corresponding BMI data, and one reported BMI outcomes without providing standard deviation values, precluding calculation of pooled effect estimates.

Study characteristics

RCTs that have been included in the review estimated the impact of olanzapine on weight gain among anorexia nervosa patients. The studies enrolled 152 patients and had trial sizes ranging from 15 to 50 [12–18]. The included studies involved adolescents, and age ranges varied from 18 to 65 years. The studies involved varied dosing schedules and durations of treatment, and the control either received placebo, therapy, or other drug therapies, including aripiprazole and chlorpromazine [12–18]. The principal outcome measure in all studies was weight change, as assessed by change in BMI and absolute weight. Table 1 provides a full description of study characteristics, including intervention details, control conditions, and key results.

Qualitative synthesis

Attia et al. (2019) compared olanzapine to placebo in AN patients. The study used weight gain and Yale-Brown-Cornell Eating Disorder Scale (YBC-EDS). The results indicated that the mean weight gain in the olanzapine group was higher than in the placebo group by 4.7 lbs. However, the YBC-EDS scores were nearly identical between the two groups [13]. Brambilla et al. (2014) investigated the effect of olanzapine in addition to cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) for AN. The study measured plasma homovanillic acid (HVA), plasma 3-methoxy-4-hydroxy-phenylglycol (MHPG), and platelet [3 H]-paroxetine-binding Bmax and Kd to assess dopamine and noradrenaline secretion as well as serotonin transporter function. Additionally, the study evaluated physical changes in the participants. The findings demonstrated a significant effect of olanzapine on HVA levels and psychopathology [14]. Brambilla et al. (2007) investigated the effect of olanzapine vs. placebo, both of whom received CBT and nutritional rehabilitation. Weight, BMI, leptin, and ghrelin levels were assessed. Similar to our analysis, BMI showed a comparable effect in both the placebo and olanzapine groups. Additionally, leptin and ghrelin levels remained unchanged [15]. Mondraty et al. (2005) compared olanzapine with chlorpromazine and found that olanzapine may help reduce anorexic rumination. The study used the Eating Disorder Inventory (EDI-2) and the Padua Inventory (PI) as outcome measures [16]. Bissada et al. (2005) examined olanzapine plus a day hospital program compared to placebo plus a day hospital program. The study used BMI and Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) scores, which were both found to have better outcomes in the olanzapine group [17]. Attia et al. (2017) conducted a study with a larger sample size to investigate the effects of olanzapine on weight change rate and psychological symptoms using the Y-BOCS. The rate of weight change was higher in the olanzapine group; however, Y-BOCS scores were similar between groups [18].

Meta-analysis: BMI

We conducted a meta-analysis of four trials involving 91 patients to contrast the results of BMI [14–17]. The overall results showed a mean difference in BMI of 1.68 kg/m² (95% CI: 0.82, 4.18), favoring olanzapine over placebo, but it was not statistically significant (p = 0.19). The analysis demonstrated significant heterogeneity (I² = 94%). The complete meta-analysis outcome can be seen in Fig. 2.

Sensitivity of analysis

Heterogeneity was reduced to 28% when Brambilla et al. (2014) was removed, which suggests that specific study designs had unduly influenced the global effect estimate. Despite the reduction in heterogeneity, the aggregated effect size did not change significantly, i.e., uncertainty regarding the effectiveness of olanzapine in producing weight gain remained [14]. This sensitivity analysis was conducted post hoc and should be interpreted cautiously. The sensitivity analysis results are shown in Fig. 3.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence

The quality of evidence was established based on the GRADE framework. Despite the limitation to consideration of only RCTs, heterogeneity of study protocol and methodological heterogeneity resulted in an overall rating of “moderate.” Limitations on the level of certainty of evidence were due to heterogeneity of intervention protocol, potential selection bias, and heterogeneity of treatment adherence across the studies. For risk of bias, three papers were scored as low risk, and the remaining four were examined, two of which were discovered to have some problems, and the remaining two were scored as high risk. The overall certainty of evidence is limited by methodological concerns in several included trials, particularly related to randomization and deviations from intended interventions. The global risk of bias assessment is explained in Figs. 4 and 5.

Publication bias

Publication bias was controlled for using funnel plot analysis. Graphical inspection provided no obvious asymmetry, consistent with an expectation of trivial publication bias, but the limited number of included studies (n = 7) means that formal statistical testing, i.e., Egger’s test, to establish this result as unbiased was not feasible. The risk of unpublished results being negative can be a lingering concern in interpreting this evidence.

Discussion

This meta-analysis and systematic review observed increased weight gain associated with the use of olanzapine in AN patients, a psychiatric disorder that is still one of the most difficult to treat. Although the meta-analysis revealed a statistically nonsignificant positive mean weight change with olanzapine, there was high heterogeneity (I² = 94%), likely as a result of differences in study design, treatment duration, pretreatment body mass index, and dosing regimens. Sensitivity analysis validated that omission of olanzapine from the most heterogeneous study reduced I² to 28%, but the overall effect was not significantly changed. Results are illustrated in the forest plot given in Fig. 2. This suggests that olanzapine is beneficial in some instances to resume weight, but that its influence is modest and imprecise when it is administered to a mixed population of patients and varied clinical contexts. Psychological results were similarly non-uniform. Weight gain by itself is no longer assumed to reflect clinical improvement in AN. While some studies, such as Bissada et al. (2008), correlated weight gain and symptom improvement in obsession, others, such as Attia et al. (2019), found statistically significant changes in weight change (rising BMI of 0.259 vs. 0.095 kg/m²/month; p = 0.026) without matching psychological symptom improvement (YBOCS change: -0.325 vs. -0.017 units/month; p = 0.60). This difference suggests that whatever somatic effect of olanzapine occurs, it is not necessarily analogous with psychopathology change. Biological outcomes between studies presented are uninformative. Brambilla et al. (2007), for example, found no significant change in leptin or ghrelin after treatment with olanzapine, and Brambilla et al. (2014) found increased HVA with no correlation with symptomatic improvement observed. These findings refute the hypothesis of olanzapine normalizing neuroendocrine or dopaminergic function to a clinically important extent. Overall certainty of evidence was moderate by GRADE methodology on methodological issues and heterogeneity of study protocol. Three trials in isolation were graded as low risk of bias, while all the rest had high risk or some concerns. Although funnel plot analysis was not excessive for over-estimation of the results, it is the limited number of studies that negates this finding. These findings have important clinical implications. Olanzapine does not appear to represent a robust or generalizable benefit for the entire patient population but may be conceived of as an adjunctive approach in particular clinical settings—i.e., in refractory subjects who are insensitive to behavioral treatment or in subjects with high comorbidities like obsessive-compulsive features. Clinicians must exercise vigilance, however, since olanzapine is not to be used as a replacement for established first-line therapies such as psychotherapy and nutritional rehabilitation. Future research will require conducting properly powered, multicenter randomized controlled trials with uniformly dosed regimens and sufficient follow-up to ascertain long-term outcomes. Stratification of patients according to AN subtype, illness severity, or psychiatric comorbidity can reveal subgroups at an increased probability of response. Inclusion of biomarker analysis would also educate regarding treatment response and neurobiological processes. These deficits need to be treated to ascertain the role of olanzapine in the multidisciplinary treatment of AN. One drawback of this review is the comparatively small number of studies included in the analysis. This was also partially due to eligibility criteria, which focused the search on RCTs and limited database access, screening out the likes of PsycINFO and grey literature. Moreover, the high heterogeneity likely reflects substantial clinical diversity across trials, including differences in age group, treatment duration, olanzapine dosing (2.5–10 mg), inpatient versus outpatient settings, and type of comparator. Due to the small number of studies, formal subgroup or meta-regression analyses were not feasible. Also, anorexia nervosa itself, being a rare condition, could be a reason for the few trials available. Due to the small number of included studies (< 10), formal assessment of publication bias using funnel plots is underpowered and unreliable. Therefore, no definitive conclusions regarding publication bias can be drawn.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis reveal that although some evidence of possible weight gain advantages of olanzapine in selected subgroups of AN patients is provided in the included studies, there is no evidence to support its use as monotherapy regularly. Thus, the clinical significance of this magnitude remains uncertain. While there is an adjunctive modest effect, findings are inconclusive since the sample sizes are tiny and there is immense heterogeneity between the study designs. Therefore, Olanzapine should not be considered a first-line treatment for anorexia nervosa. However, it may be considered as an adjunctive option in treatment-resistant cases or in patients with significant obsessive-compulsive symptoms. Properly conducted randomized controlled trials in the future are necessary to confirm these findings, optimize dosing regimens, and determine long-term safety and psychological effects of olanzapine in this population.
