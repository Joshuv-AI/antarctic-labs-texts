Atorvastatin pre-exposure in hypercholesterolemia remedies 27HC mediated dendritic cell dysfunctions in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Pritha Roy Choudhury, Mohona Chakravarti, Saurav Bera, Pooja Arora, Jasmine Sultana, Muqtada Ali Khan, Rushikesh Biradar, Aishwarya Guha, Sukanya Dhar, Indranil Ghosh, Nilanjan Ganguly, Aarti Lasure, Priyanka Kashyap, Juhina Das, Biswajit Mandal, Prodipto Das, Anirban Sarkar, Dipak Datta, Sukhendu Nandi, Neyaz Alam, Rathindranath Baral, Saptak Banerjee, Anamika Bose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early inception of hypercholesterolemia has led to prevalent statin pre-exposure among cancer patients. Furthermore, emerging associations between increased circulating cholesterol, dyslipidemia, and cancer recurrence have spurred the repurposing of statins to combat cancer. However, the mechanistic impact of statin pre-exposure on cancer progression remains unclear. Here, our retrospective study with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) patients showed that atorvastatin pre-exposure promotes recurrence-free survival. Atorvastatin pre-exposure in TNBC patients and a preclinical high-fat diet-induced hypercholesterolemic murine tumor model lowered intratumoral 27-hydroxycholesterol (27HC) concentration, as estimated by LC-MS/MS. Within the tumor microenvironment, elevated 27HC interacted with estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) on dendritic cells (DCs), hindering DC maturation, migration, and antigen presentation. Atorvastatin reduced ERα expression on DCs and remediated 27HC-ERα-induced DC dysfunctions, improved DC-mediated T cell priming, thereby facilitating tumor restriction when adoptively transferred to 4T1-bearing NOD/SCID/IL2Rγ−/− mice. Targeted intratumoral knockdown of cyp27a1 (rate-determining enzyme for 27HC production) alongside atorvastatin pre-exposure uplifted cDC1 functionality. Atorvastatin pre-treatment exerts its action by reducing the availability of intratumoral 27HC and expression of ERα on DCs, which eventually enhances DC functionality and restores their efficiency to prime anti-tumor CD8+ T cell responses. Therefore, atorvastatin pre-exposure adjunct to 27HC-ERα inhibition may serve as an effective immunotherapeutic intervention in TNBC.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) constitutes 15%–20% of the newly diagnosed breast cancer (BC) cases.1 The aggressive pathophysiology and substantially high recurrence rate render TNBC to be an impending cause of death related to malignancies among women.2 It lacks effective therapeutic targets due to the absence of hormone receptors like human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), estrogen receptor (ER), and progesterone receptor (PR) on tumor cells3 rendering cytolytic chemotherapy as the therapeutic mainstay.4 Recent cohort studies report delay of BC recurrence among patients with statin therapy, emphasizing its emerging role as a promising onco-therapeutic alternative.5 In light of the clinical association of total cholesterol to mammary malignancies,6 the underlying influence of chronic statin pre-exposure on BC incidence and progression needs further investigation.

Cholesterol-relegating medications, like statins, are globally prescribed for management of lipid disorders and cardiovascular diseases (CVDs).7 Statins are inhibitors of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase. They inhibit the rate-limiting reaction of de novo cholesterol biosynthesis and simultaneously curtail levels of low-density lipids (LDL) in blood.8 Given the looming concerns about hypercholesterolemia and dyslipidemia in relation to multiple malignancies,9,10 lipophilic statins like atorvastatin are currently being re-purposed for combatting cancer.11 However, epidemiological trials with diverse classes of statins alongside chemotherapy posted conflicting responses owing to their therapeutic use in different cancers including BC, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), prostate malignancies, renal cell carcinoma (RCC), etc.12,13,14,15,16

Cholesterol is also the chief constituent of the plasma membrane; it contributes to both antigen presentation17,18 and T cell-mediated anti-tumor immune surveillance.19 However, cholesterol-associated modulation of effector T cell functions has been associated with metastasis in breast malignancies.20 Furthermore, oxidized cholesterol components have been observed to hamper cross presentation of tumor antigens by dendritic cells (DCs), which is essential for T cell priming and anti-tumor activity.21 Among oxidized metabolites of cholesterol, 27-hydroxycholesterol (27HC) has been lately related to tumor-promoting mechanisms including enhanced metastatic potential, induction of extracellular vesicle release, and modulation of immune components such as γδ T cells and polymorphonuclear neutrophils.20,22,23

Due to the early onset of CVDs and the widespread use of statins even in young adults, there is an extensive population of cancer patients having a history of chronic statin use.24,25,26 Atorvastatin is a widely prescribed pharmacological formulation of statin. Given the dual interplay of cholesterol metabolism and statins in malignancies, a conclusive delineation of the effects of statin pre-exposure in cancer remains to be a pressing priority.

Hence, we sought to investigate whether prior atorvastatin exposure alters the intratumoral immunosuppressive effects of hypercholesterolemia. In the course of the present study, 27HC was recognized to promote tumor progression in hypercholesterolemia by disrupting immune surveillance, principally through the dysfunction of the professional antigen-presenting cell (APC), DC. Consequently, the present research focusses on exploring the research lacuna involving the mechanistic impact of 27HC on intratumoral DC-mediated immune surveillance.

This study provides the first empirical evidence that atorvastatin pre-exposure retards TNBC progression by enhancing optimal DC maturation and intratumoral antigen presentation, converging mechanisms of decreased 27HC synthesis and ERα suppression. Moreover, our mechanistic study also discloses that 27HC-ERα interaction limits the Notch1-IRF8-Flt3 cascade on conventional type 1 DCs (cDC1s), interfering with anti-tumor immune responses.

Results

Atorvastatin pre-exposure retards 27HC-mediated promotion of breast tumor in hypercholesterolemia

Therapeutic use of statins in combination with anti-cancer agents has been associated with different clinical outcomes depending on the cancer type.11,15,26 To investigate whether and how atorvastatin pre-exposure influences progression of breast malignancy, a retrospective analysis was conducted with TNBC patient data obtained from hospital records of Chittaranjan National Cancer Institute and Apollo Medical Hospitals, Kolkata, India (Table S1). A total of 171 patients met the inclusion criteria, with a median follow-up of 73.2 months and a median age of 61.5 years (Tables S2, S3, and S4). Phi-Cramer’s V statistical analysis demonstrated an inverse relation between atorvastatin use and recurrence in TNBC (Figure S1A). After adjusting for potential confounders including age, stage, and atorvastatin dosage (40 mg), TNBC patients with and without atorvastatin pre-exposure were evaluated using Kaplan-Meier recurrence-free survival analysis. The retrospective patient study also revealed a duration-dependent pattern between atorvastatin exposure and relapse among TNBC patients. The TNBC patients with more than 3 years of atorvastatin pre-exposure had reduced relapse rates in contrast to patients with less than or equal to 3 years of pre-exposure (Table S5). Although the study was conducted in a relatively small cohort, yet it was significant that among the 171 patients, extended atorvastatin pre-exposure was associated with lower relapse rates and prolonged recurrence-free survival (p value = 0.0001, χ2 = 42.391), hinting at a duration-dependent protective effect of atorvastatin (Table S5). Cumulative clinical findings suggested that atorvastatin pre-exposure endorses prolonged relapse-free survival in TNBC, underscoring the need for downstream investigations in both in vitro and in vivo models to elucidate the biological basis of this association.

To determine whether the observed effects of atorvastatin pre-exposure in TNBC patients are cholesterol-dependent or independent, a hypercholesterolemic mouse model was established. Initially, the impact of atorvastatin pre-exposure was evaluated in an aggressive 4T1 TNBC model and a less aggressive, hormone receptor-positive Ehrlich carcinoma (EC) BC model. Mice were randomized before the commencement of required dietary regimens and treatment. The experimental plan is depicted in a schematic Figure 1B). A subset of the mice was fed with a high-fat diet (HFD) comprising 37.1% fat, 1.25% cholesterol, and 0.5% sodium cholate for a period of 12 weeks. In contrast, the control group of standard diet (STD) was fed with regular chow that provided 22% fat with no added cholesterol or sodium cholate. The body weight was monitored every 3 days and plasma cholesterol was measured every 14 days. A steady increase was observed in the body weight and plasma cholesterol of the HFD cohort due to development of diet-induced hypercholesterolemia (Figures 1C and 1D). Once the plasma cholesterol increased beyond normal (200 mg/dL)27 and the LDL levels elevated, then the HFD cohort was divided into an atorvastatin-treated (HFD + ST) and untreated (HFD) group (n = 15). Following the onset of hypercholesterolemia, atorvastatin exposure was maintained until experiment completion (approximately 9 weeks). After the plasma cholesterol was restored to normal in the HFD + ST mice, tumor (EC and 4T1) cells (1 × 106) were inoculated in the mammary fat pad of (Swiss and BALB/c) mice of all groups. The HFD mice exhibited enhanced tumor progression compared to the other groups. The hypercholesterolemia-mediated tumor progression was found to be restricted on atorvastatin pre-treatment (Figures 1E–1G).

In the TNBC model, additional observations were made in control experiments comprising an STD group with/without atorvastatin pre-treatment. Atorvastatin pre-exposure in the normo-cholesterolemic group modestly reduced plasma cholesterol levels and tumor volume. The variation in tumor burden between the STD and STD + ST groups was less pronounced than in the HFD and HFD + ST groups (Figures S1B, S1C, and S1D). Moreover, in TNBC, the survivability was compromised in the hypercholesterolemic cohort compared to the STD cohort; this was restored on atorvastatin pre-exposure (Figure S1E).

As discussed earlier, elevated levels of LDL and its derivatives are drivers of BC progression. Hence, to determine the cholesterol moiety responsible for hypercholesterolemia-mediated tumor progression, the cholesterol-oxidizing enzymes were checked at the transcriptional levels. Among the enzymes, transcript and protein level expressions of CYP27A1 enzyme were notably upregulated in malignant cells of the hypercholesterolemic cohort, which was alleviated on atorvastatin pre-exposure (Figures 1H and 1I). The CYP27A1 expression was also augmented in the tumor tissues of hypercholesterolemic mice compared to normo-cholesterolemic and atorvastatin pre-exposed groups (Figure S2A). CYP27A1 is responsible for the synthesis of the oxysterol, 27HC. Thus, LC-MS/MS estimation was performed to detect the status of intratumoral 27HC concentration in tumors of murine origin (Figures S2B and S2C). Intratumoral availability of 27HC was amplified in the murine hypercholesterolemic cohort compared to the control and atorvastatin-treated cohort (Figure 1J). So, the experimental findings indicated that rapidly progressing tumors in hypercholesterolemic mice had higher intratumoral 27HC concentrations.

Moreover, CYP27A1 expression was also downregulated in adjacent and tumor tissues obtained from TNBC patients with atorvastatin pre-exposure compared to those having no atorvastatin pre-exposure (Figure 1K). LC-MS/MS study of the intratumoral availability of 27HC in patients affirmed the results of CYP27A1 obtained in western blot analysis. Elevated intratumoral 27HC production was observed in tumor tissue of control patients relative to atorvastatin-exposed cases (Figure 1L).

Evaluated TNBC patient data collected alongside preclinical mouse models recommend that pre-exposure to atorvastatin results in improved TNBC prognosis including extended recurrence-free survival. Pre-exposure to atorvastatin reduced LDL-derived 27HC levels in hypercholesterolemic 4T1 tumors as well as in patient tumors. Importantly, the marginal differences observed in tumor progression between STD and STD + ST groups further suggest that, beyond cholesterol lowering, atorvastatin exerts additional pleiotropic effects contributing to tumor modulation.

Atorvastatin pre-exposure rescues hypercholesterolemia associated dwindling of intratumoral cDC1 and restores morphology

Given the improved prognosis and relapse-free survival associated with atorvastatin pre-exposure, along with reduced intratumoral 27HC levels, we next examined how these alterations influence the tumor immune niche. Since 27HC is a bioactive metabolite derived from LDL with recognized immunomodulatory functions,20,22,23,28 analyzing the effect of its altered availability on the immune milieu is crucial. The subsequent analysis revealed notable differences in DC infiltration and phenotype among tumors of hypercholesterolemic and atorvastatin pre-exposed cohorts, suggesting that 27HC-mediated alterations in the intratumoral DC population may modulate the primary stages of immune response within the TME.

We initially looked into the infiltration of different DC subsets (Figure S4A). The CD11c+CD11b− lymphoid DC showed maximum variations among hypercholesterolemic and atorvastatin-exposed groups, compared to myeloid and plasmacytoid DCs. Among the CD11c+CD11b− lymphoid DC subset, the DCs particularly essential for intratumoral cross-presentation are CD11c+CD8α+cDC1.29,30,31 Hence, immunofluorescence (IF) imaging was performed for detection of CD11c+CD8α+cDC1 infiltration within the murine TNBC tumor microenvironment (TME). CD11c+CD8α+cDC1 infiltration was significantly depleted in the TNBC tumors developed in hypercholesterolemic mice compared to tumors developed in normal BALB/c mice. The consequent experimental plan is elucidated in Figure 2B. Further characterization of intratumoral cDC1s suggested upregulation in expression of Flt3 and CD103 observed in atorvastatin pre-treated groups, which was otherwise downregulated in hypercholesterolemic tumors (Figure 2C). MHC class I expression was also elevated in cDC1s of the atorvastatin pre-exposed group relative to the hypercholesterolemic cohort (Figure 2D). Intratumoral CD8α+CD11c+cDC1s showed a decrease in the pro-inflammatory cytokine IFN-γ. Elevated levels of IL-10 and TGFβ secretion were also detected by ELISA in HFD mice, which was reversed on atorvastatin pre-treatment (Figure S4B). Therefore, intratumoral cDC1s of the HFD group displayed reduced infiltration, lowered MHC class I expression signifying impaired cross-presentation capacity, along with weak expression of key cDC1 markers such as FLT3. These cDC1s also exhibited a more tolerogenic phenotype, characterized by elevated secretion of immunosuppressive cytokines, including TGF-β and IL-10, and reduced secretion of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IFN-γ compared to control and atorvastatin-pretreated groups.

An in vitro culture setup of tumor-conditioned, cross-presenting DCs was established in the presence and absence of 27HC to study its impact on DC morphology, migratory ability, and antigen uptake efficiency (Figure 2E). Cofilin contributes to maintenance of cell morphology and motility in mature DCs. IF staining suggested decreased Cofilin content and reduction in dendritic length of DCs when cultured in 27HC-rich media, suggesting reduced plasticity and migratory capability (Figures 2F and S5). However, addition of atorvastatin in 27HC-rich media rescued both plasticity and migratory ability of DCs. Cellular uptake of DCs was measured by incubating DCs with OVA-FITC for 4 h, followed by IF staining. Uptake of OVA-FITC was hampered in tumor-conditioned DCs of 27HC-rich culture. Hence, exposure to 27HC-enriched culture generated DCs with reduced antigen uptake potential, as demonstrated in the OVA-FITC uptake assay. However, treatment with atorvastatin enhanced uptake efficacy of the same (Figure 2G). Similar results were also observed in vivo (Figure S4C). IF imaging of Filipin was performed to assess cellular cholesterol content. Tumor-conditioned DCs exposed to 27HC-enriched cultures showed depleted intracellular cholesterol levels and reduced numbers of dendritic projections (Figures 2H and S5).

Although cholesterol is indispensable for the development of immunological synapses mediating APC-T cell interaction through clustering of receptors in professional APCs like DCs,32 yet oxylipids hinder antigen cross-presentation.21 Our cumulative experimental analysis established that atorvastatin pre-exposure substantially reversed the detrimental effects of cholesterol-derived 27HC on cDC1s. Atorvastatin pre-exposure improved DC infiltration, morphology, and functionality. Surprisingly, in vitro atorvastatin exposure was also observed to effectively augment DC maturation in 27HC-rich tumor conditions. The unexpected in vitro effects of atorvastatin suggest that its pre-exposure not only reduces hypercholesterolemia or 27HC synthesis but also has pleiotropic effects on the mechanisms of action of 27HC.

Atorvastatin pre-treatment ameliorates 27HC-rich tumor microenvironment-mediated dampening of antigen processing and subsequent DC-T cell interaction

Followed by the characterization, cDC1-mediated antigen presentation, co-stimulation, and subsequent effector T cell activation were checked in hypercholesterolemic and atorvastatin pre-treated tumor-bearing groups (Figure 3A). Hypercholesterolemia-mediated downregulation was observed in several antigen-processing and -presenting genes, namely, ciita, tap1, tap2, calnexin, calreticulin, and β2m, of the intratumoral cDC1s. Nevertheless, the antigen-processing machinery was not compromised in the intratumoral cDC1s of the atorvastatin pre-treated group with reduced intratumoral 27HC levels (Figure 3B). In corroboration, expression of co-stimulatory molecules like CD80, CD86, and CD40 was found to be downregulated in tumor-infiltrating CD11c+CD8α+cDC1s of hyperlipidemic BALB/c mice relative to that of normal mice. Enhanced expression of CCR7 in intratumoral cDC1s of atorvastatin pre-exposed hypercholesterolemic mice suggested improved infiltration of DCs in mice with controlled levels of intratumoral 27HC (Figure 3C). When examined in the STD and STD + ST groups, atorvastatin exposure resulted in a partial increase of DC infiltration and functions, indicating an immunomodulatory effect independent of hypercholesterolemia (Figures S6A and S6B). Co-inhibitory markers like PDL1, IDO, and FAS-L showed increased expression in CD11c+CD8α+cDC1s within tumors of hypercholesterolemic mice; however, it was downregulated in the hypercholesterolemic atorvastatin pre-treated cohort (Figure 3D).

DCs primarily activate effector T cells to drive cytotoxic anti-tumor responses; thus, next we assessed the status of CD8+ T cell infiltration, activation, and function. DCs from 27HC-rich and atorvastatin-treated cultures were co-cultured with autologous splenic CD8+ T cells. Post co-culture, reduced CD3+CD8+ T cell clump formation was evident in 27HC-treated DCs, which suggested impaired T cell activation compared to control. CD3+CD8+ T cell activation was augmented in the atorvastatin-exposed setup (Figure S7A). Consequential alleviation was noted in CD8+ T cell activation and proliferation markers like CD69, CD25, and Ki-67 in 27HC-rich hypercholesterolemic tumors compared to atorvastatin pre-treated groups (Figure 3E). Moreover, a modest increase of T cell infiltration and activation was observed in the STD and STD + ST cohorts (Figure S7B). A functional impairment in intratumoral CD3+CD8+ T cells of hypercholesterolemic BALB/c mice was observed with heightened expression of the exhaustion marker pd1 alongside diminished pro-inflammatory ifnγ levels (Figure 3F). Hence, atorvastatin improved intratumoral T cell infiltration and activation.

Effector T cells were isolated from the differentially conditioned DC-T cell co-culture setup and assessed for the secretion of cytotoxic T cell effector enzymes. Fluorescence microscopy showed reduced expression of Perforin and Granzyme-B in effector T cells that were presented with 27HC-treated DCs compared to normal. In the case of combinatorial atorvastatin and 27HC treatment, post co-culture T cells exhibited elevated Perforin and Granzyme-B levels (Figure 3G).

Hence, atorvastatin ameliorated 27HC-induced DC dysfunction, including antigen-presentation and co-stimulation potential. It also restored DC-mediated T cell activation and T cell cytotoxicity, both in vivo and in vitro.

Exposure to atorvastatin modulates 27HC-associated ERα signaling to improve DC maturation and facilitates CD8+ T cell-oriented tumor restriction

The conventional receptors targeted by 27HC are LXR receptors.28 However, LXR receptors on DCs showed nominal expression and trivial alterations with atorvastatin pre-exposure (Figure S8A). To ascertain the putative signaling mediator of 27HC in tumor-conditioned DCs, we evaluated the expression of ERs because 27HC is a recognized selective ER modulator (SERM).33 Evaluation of these receptors on cDC1s revealed an upregulation of ERα on the surface of tumor-conditioned, 27HC-treated dysfunctional DCs. The ERα transcript levels were reduced on atorvastatin exposure (Figure 4A). Thereafter, to validate the involvement of the 27HC-ERα axis in DC functional impairment and subsequent T cell priming, DCs were cultured in media containing varying combinations of 27HC, atorvastatin, and tamoxifen (a pharmacological blocker of ERα). In an in vitro co-culture setup, estrogen was added exclusively to evaluate whether conventional estrogen-mediated ERα signaling had any impact on DC maturation. The mature DCs of each group were co-cultured with autologous CD8+ T cells in tumor-conditioned media. Hence, 27HC acted through ERα to induce DC dysfunction. This was reversed by tamoxifen, which blocked ER signaling and restored normal DC function. Likewise, atorvastatin also continued to curb the effect of 27HC in vitro by suppressing ERα expression. Estrogen supplementation had no impact, suggesting no involvement of estrogen-mediated ERα signaling in DC dysfunctionality. CD8+ T cell-mediated tumor restriction was lacking in the 27HC-treated group, which led to profound lung metastasis resulting in secondary lesions and decreased alveolar area. The lung histological sections of tamoxifen- and atorvastatin-treated groups showed considerable alveolar space compared to the 27HC-treated cohort (Figures 4G and S8B).

The cumulative results advocate that atorvastatin pre-exposure regulates 27HC-ERα downstream signaling to restore the priming ability of DCs for optimum T cell functions, which was essential in the restriction of murine tumor growth and lung metastasis. The effect of atorvastatin was not only restricted to the reduction of 27HC synthesis but also extended to the downregulation of ERα receptors, thereby interrupting the 27HC-ERα axis.

In order to evaluate the downstream signaling, involvement of several pathways of DC maturation was tested through qRT-PCR and ELISA in intratumoral CD11c+CD8α+cDC1s (Figures S9 and S10). Both in vivo and in vitro experiments suggested a decline in the expression of notch1 and irf8 in the DCs with compromised functions. The expression of both molecules at the mRNA level was restored on atorvastatin pretreatment. ERα blocking with tamoxifen also resulted in upregulated expression of notch1, irf8, and flt3 (Figure 4H). Confocal microscopy indicated that 27HC hindered Notch1 nuclear translocation, rendering DCs with short extensions (Figure S11).

Therefore, an in vitro culture was established to analyze the contribution of Notch1 in atorvastatin-mediated revival of DC functionality. The treatment also promoted expression of key α-DC maturation markers like RBPJ (downstream effector molecule of Notch1), Flt3, and IRF8 (Figure 6G). Discussion

The impact of atorvastatin exposure in BC remains inconclusive owing to heterogeneity in pre-clinical outcomes.34,35 This study concentrates on the effect of atorvastatin pre-exposure in both TNBC patients and a pre-clinical HFD-induced hypercholesterolemic murine model. Our study revealed that hypercholesterolemia impairs intratumoral immune surveillance of cDC1s through the action of 27HC. Although preliminary studies have suggested a role for 27HC in tumor immune evasion,23,36 yet its effects on intratumoral antigen presentation, co-stimulation, and subsequent cytotoxic T cell activation remain undeciphered. Here, we disclose the mechanisms of 27HC-mediated hindrance in the maturation of intratumoral cDC1, a DC subset primarily responsible for cross-presentation of tumor antigens. We also report how atorvastatin pre-exposure restores cDC1 function through dual targeting of 27HC availability and ERα on DCs.

An initial retrospective analysis conducted on a small cohort of 171 TNBC patients validated that atorvastatin pre-exposure conferred notable delay of recurrence in stage I-III patients compared to the control cohort. Moreover, prolonged atorvastatin pre-exposure (>3 years) was associated with extended years of recurrence-free survival. The survival impact of atorvastatin on stage IV TNBC patients could not be assessed due to restricted patient availability. In order to examine whether atorvastatin’s effect on TNBC was primarily through lowering hypercholesterolemia or by other pleiotropic mechanisms, a hypercholesterolemic murine TNBC model was established. Hypercholesterolemia was induced by an established protocol of HFD. The experimental diet offered 37.1% of total caloric fat consumption with 1.25% cholesterol and 0.5% sodium cholate. In contrast, the control cohort was given standard chow that provided 22% fat intake and had no added cholesterol or sodium cholate.20,37 Once the plasma cholesterol increased beyond normal in the HFD group, a subset of mice was exposed to atorvastatin. Lowering of plasma cholesterol in the atorvastatin-exposed cohort was followed by 4T1 tumor inoculation in all groups. This in vivo TNBC model demonstrated a heightened progression of tumor growth in the hypercholesterolemic cohort. The tumor growth was retarded in the atorvastatin-pretreated group, validating that hypercholesterolemia contributed to tumor progression. In a control experiment, atorvastatin pre-exposure modestly retarded tumor growth in STD-fed mice with minor variations in the plasma cholesterol levels. The effect was less pronounced than in HFD conditions.

Elevation of LDL levels in hypercholesterolemic mice directed our investigation toward examining cholesterol-oxidizing enzymes within the tumor niche, as oxidized LDLs have been deemed to be crucial contributors in progression of malignancies.38 Intratumoral CYP27A1 was upregulated in the hypercholesterolemic cohort both at the transcript and protein levels, which was comparatively reduced among atorvastatin-pre-exposed mice. CYP27A1 downregulation was also prominent among tumor and adjacent tissues of atorvastatin-pre-exposed TNBC patients compared to control. Augmented expression of CYP27A1 is allied to advanced tumor grades in multiple malignancies, as it catalyzes the production of an oxysterol, 27HC.39 Similarly, accumulation of intratumoral 27HC is also implicated in poor prognosis of tumors.40,41 In LC-MS/MS analysis, we noted substantially elevated intratumoral 27HC levels in rapidly progressing triple-negative breast tumors of murine and patient origin. Hence, atorvastatin pre-exposure was associated with reduced availability of intratumoral 27HC and subsequent tumor restriction in TNBC.

While cholesterol is considered essential for antigen presentation and DC-T cell interaction,42,43 LDL-derived oxysterols promote cholesterol efflux44 and disrupt antigen presentation.21 The aforementioned findings laid the rationale to explore the influence of atorvastatin exposure on intratumoral DCs in hypercholesterolemic TNBC mice. Among the DC subsets, CD11c+CD8α+cDC1s showed profound depletion in the hypercholesterolemic cohort contrasted atorvastatin-pre-exposed groups. As discerned from earlier reports, the cDC1 subset is indispensable for the intricate coordination of antigen cross-presentation and co-stimulation of cytotoxic effector T cells within the TME.45,46,47 Initially, we found a decrease in the infiltration of cDC1s into the tumor contexture among HFD mice relative to control. Characterization of the intratumoral CD11c+CD8α+cDC1 population by flow cytometry illustrated decline in Flt3, CD103, and MHC class I molecules in the hypercholesterolemic tumor-bearing group compared to normocholesterolemic or atorvastatin-pre-exposed cohorts. ELISA-based cytokine profile of intratumoral cDC1s in 27HC-rich tumors showed decline in pro-inflammatory IFN-γ, while concomitant enhancement was observed in IL-10 and TGFβ secretion. Aligned with prior literature, such shift indicated switching of DCs into a tolerogenic phenotype.48,49 Collectively, our findings provide the first evidence that 27HC-enriched TNBC tumors are associated with reduced DC infiltration, decreased expression of characteristic cDC1 markers, and a shift toward a tolerogenic phenotype, whereas these alterations were mitigated on atorvastatin pre-exposure.

Research thus far has indicated that Cofilin is necessary for the migratory capacities of DCs.50,51 In our in vitro experiments, 27HC exposure downregulated Cofilin expression on DCs and triggered a collapse of the canonical DC cytostructure, drastically reducing dendritic projections, thereby generating a rounded, morphologically abnormal DC phenotype. Surprisingly, in vitro atorvastatin treatment reversed both DC morphology and 27HC-mediated downregulation of Cofilin. Moreover, the OVA-uptake assay revealed that atorvastatin retrieved the antigen-uptake capacity of DCs, which had been impaired by 27HC exposure. The efficacy of atorvastatin in vitro, where 27HC was directly supplemented, indicates that these effects are independent of 27HC synthesis. Hence, atorvastatin exposure not only lowers hypercholesterolemia and 27HC synthesis but also interferes with the mechanisms of action of 27HC.

DCs primarily function as APCs within the tumor microenvironment. 27HC-enriched tumors demonstrated sweeping downregulation of ciita, tap1, tap2, calnexin, calreticulin, and β2m in intratumoral cDC1s of the hypercholesterolemic cohort, destabilizing antigen processing and presentation. The molecular decline cascaded further by downregulation of co-stimulatory molecules such as CD80, CD86, and CD40. The ablated migratory capacities of such DCs were indicated by relatively low CCR7 expression. In line with contemporary evidence, intratumoral cDC1s of 27HC-enriched tumors had a tolerogenic profile validated by upregulated FAS-L, PDL1, and IDO expression.52,53,54,55 Hence, intratumoral cDC1s of the hypercholesterolemic cohort exhibited extensive dysfunctionality. In 27HC-enriched tumors, cDC1s showed an immune-suppressed profile with impaired co-stimulation and antigen presentation, which were reversed in atorvastatin-treated groups. Notably, in additional experiments where STD mice were exposed to atorvastatin, only modest changes were induced in cholesterol levels, accompanied by a slight reduction in tumor progression.

Atorvastatin-exposed intratumoral effector T cells showed heightened expression of T cell activation markers and IFNγ compared to 27HC-rich cohorts. In vitro co-culture setup also displayed revived activation-clump formation and cytotoxicity among effector T cells primed by atorvastatin-treated 27HC-containing cultures of DCs as opposed to those of 27HC-enriched cultures. Although the impact of 27HC was evident, yet the mechanism of action seemed unknown, as the classical 27HC receptors LXRα and LXRβ showed nominal expression and marginal intercohort changes.

In order to assess the in vitro antigen priming efficiency of DCs in a TNBC model, CD8+ T cells post co-culture with 27HC and atorvastatin exposure were isolated and adoptively transferred in 4T1 tumor-bearing immunocompromised NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid mice that are devoid of endogenous T cell functions. Experimental validation of CD8+ T cell-mediated tumor restriction in immunocompromised mice proved that actions of 27HC were arbitrated through ERα because, in the tamoxifen-treated DCs, 27HC was unable to exert its action. It aligned with contemporary reports classifying 27HC as a functional SERM.33 Further downstream studies showed that 27HC-ERα interaction dampened the maturation cues of cDC1, primarily through Notch1. The Notch1-DLL1 followed a juxtacrine communication among maturing DCs. According to published studies, Notch signaling is crucially involved in DC maturation.56,57,58 Engagement of 27HC with ERα reduced Notch1 transcription and confined it to the cytoplasm. This molecular interaction is consistent with prior reports where ERα has been reported to bind with the Notch1 promoter to modulate Notch1 activity.59,60 In 27HC-rich TME, further downstream molecules essential for DC maturation, like IRF8 and Flt361,62,63 were also downregulated on cDC1s. Atorvastatin restricted ERα expression on DCs, thereby limiting the 27HC-ERα axis and promoting cDC1 maturation and functionality. This is consistent with atorvastatin’s reported ERα inhibitory activity.64 Moreover, synergistic impact of cyp27a1 inhibition and prevention of 27HC-ERα interaction by atorvastatin provoked robust cDC1 responses facilitating intratumoral antigen presentation and optimal T cell priming, resulting in tumor restriction.

Analysis of a small cohort of TNBC patients showed similar immunomodulatory effects of atorvastatin. A notable increase was discerned in co-stimulation and antigen presentation among the Lin−CD45+HLADR+Flt3+CD141+cDC1 population of the atorvastatin-exposed cohort compared to the control. The cDC1 maturation signals, like RBPJ, IRF8, and Flt3, were upregulated in cDC1s of tumors belonging to atorvastatin-treated cohorts.

Collectively, our findings suggest that combination of atorvastatin with targeted inhibition of the 27HC-ERα axis in DCs is a potential immunotherapeutic strategy for TNBC. The constraints of this study include the inaccessibility of atorvastatin-naive hypercholesterolemic patients with TNBC, restricting a direct comparative evaluation. Assessment in a confined cohort with limited patients highlights the necessity of evaluation across a wider pool of patients. The in vivo pre-clinical experiments were limited to approximately 9 weeks of atorvastatin exposure. Further, variable durations of atorvastatin exposure in pre-clinical models are needed to confirm the duration-dependent effect of atorvastatin observed in TNBC patients.

Conclusion

Our present study reports that atorvastatin pre-treatment confers relapse-free survival benefits for TNBC patients by attenuating intratumoral 27HC-ERα mediated immune suppression. The work provides early evidence of 27HC-mediated effects on CD11c+CD8α+cDC1, a subset of hypercholesterolemic mice, and CD141+cDC1s of TNBC patients. The work revolves around the immunomodulatory effects of atorvastatin in restoring the functional integrity of DCs impaired by 27HC. It depicts the role of atorvastatin in modulating Notch1-RBPJ-IRF8-Flt3 signaling of cDC1s to promote sustained immune surveillance mechanisms in TNBC.

Taken together, this study highlights that with consistent validation in expansive cohorts of patients, the adjunct therapy of atorvastatin with targeted inhibition of the 27HC-ERα axis on DCs might emerge as a potential immunotherapeutic avenue in TNBC. Such intervention would lead to intensified intratumoral immune surveillance and invigorate T cell response. Our observations also indicate that before endorsing 27HC as a viable therapeutic target, a deeper exploration of its impact on other components of the immune contexture is essential.

Materials and methods

Reagents and antibodies

RPMI 1640 and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were acquired from Hi-Media. Streptavidin Particles Plus-DM, rGM-CSF, rIL4, and Flt3L were obtained from Miltenyi Biotech. Fluoroshield with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), N-[N-(3,5-difluorophenacetyl)-L-alanyl]-S-phenylglycine tert-butyl ester (DAPT), 27HC, estradiol, tamoxifen, atorvastatin, and β-mercaptoethanol were attained from Sigma-Aldrich. The antibodies used have been enlisted in Table S7.

Mice model

Wild-type (Wt) female Swiss albino (RRID: MGI:5652853) and BALB/c (RRID: IMSR_CRL:028) mice (age: 4–6 weeks; average body weight: 18–25 g) were procured from the Institutional Animal Care and Maintenance Department, Chittaranjan National Cancer Institute, Kolkata, India. Female immunocompromised NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJin (RRID: IMSR_JAX:005557) strain was obtained (age: 4–6 weeks, average body weight: 18–22 g) from the facility of CSIR-Central Drug Research Institute, Lucknow, India. The experimental mice were accommodated in a pathogen-free environment; they were provided with water ad libitum and fed with autoclaved dry pellets (Epic Laboratory, West Bengal Government, Kalyani, West Bengal, India). The experimental procedure was approved by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC; approval no. IAEC-1774/SBn-6/2021/11 and IAEC/2024/62 Renew-0/Sr. No.08). The IAEC is registered under the Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Experiments on Animals (CPCSEA), Government of India.

Establishment of hypercholesterolemia, atorvastatin treatment, and tumor inoculation

The mice were co-housed and assigned to either the standard or hypercholesterolemic diet. STD offered 22% of fat intake with no added cholesterol or sodium cholate, and the hypercholesterolemic diet contained 37.1% of total caloric fat, 1.2% cholesterol, and 0.2% cholate.20,37 The total blood cholesterol and LDL were measured using Autospan (Arkray) Cholesterol Assay Kit. On development of hypercholesterolemia, atorvastatin (40 mg/kg) was administered individually by oral gavage,20 till experiment completion (9 weeks). For the establishment of orthotopic triple-negative breast tumors in the mammary fat pad and subcutaneous Ehrlich breast carcinoma tumors, 1 × 105 cells (4T1 and EC) were inoculated subcutaneously in the mammary fat pad of female BALB/c and Swiss albino mice, respectively. The tumor growth was monitored every 3 days with a vernier caliper and analyzed using the formula 0.5 × length × (width)2. The maximum tumor volume did not exceed 2,000 mm3 as permitted by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee.

Liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry analysis of 27HC

Tumor and adjacent regions (50 mg wet weight) were washed with chilled PBS multiple times. Sonication of tissue samples was performed with a 10-fold volume of PBS (w/v) and was centrifuged for 10 min at 3,500 × g at 4°C. Supernatant (10 μL) was dissolved in methanol, followed by alkaline hydrolysis using ethanolic potassium hydroxide (1 N). The sterols were isolated using n-hexane.

Cholesterol and 27HC were estimated with an Agilent 6545 QTOF mass spectrometer that worked in Auto-MS/MS mode, attached to an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC system. Chromatographic separation was done with a Phenomenex Luna C18 column (5 μm, 150 mm × 4.6 mm) under reverse-phase conditions. The mobile phase contained Solvent A: water/methanol (50:50, v/v) comprising 0.1% formic acid and 10 mM ammonium formate, and Solvent B: isopropanol/methanol (80:20, v/v) comprising 0.1% formic acid and 10 mM ammonium formate. The LC flow rate was fixed at 0.5 mL/min, and the complete run time was 30 min. Temperatures of the column compartment and sample compartment were kept at 35°C and 10°C, respectively. Mass spectrometry-mediated detection was done with a dual Agilent Jet Stream (AJS) electrospray ionization (ESI) source working in positive ion mode. MS/MS acquisition was directed by an ideal ion list specific to cholesterol and 27HC, with up to four precursors selected per cycle and a fixed collision energy of 5 eV.

LC-MS/MS data analysis and quantitation

To evaluate the results of the LC-MS flow, the data were handled in Agilent Mass Hunter Qualitative Analysis 10.0 (RRID: SCR_015040), and each of the recognized peaks was manually confirmed depending on retention times of the LC run and fragments attained in the MS/MS spectral analysis. A mass accuracy threshold of 10 ppm was applied to all analytes for every experiment described in this study. Estimation of the absolute concentrations of cholesterol and 27HC was done by analyzing the area under the curve (AUC) of the analytes.

Quantification of cholesterol and 27HC

Cholesterol and 27HC were estimated by a high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) method, using retention times of 17.9 and 15.7 min, respectively. A sequence of standard solutions was needed to produce calibration curves with concentrations ranging from 50 to 10,000 ng/mL for both analytes. Interpolation of cholesterol and 27HC concentrations in the samples was done from the respective calibration curves.

Triple-negative breast tumor samples

Triple-negative breast tumors and histopathologically normal adjacent tissues, along with postoperative blood of female patients, were collected from the hospital division of Chittaranjan National Cancer Institute (Approval No: CNCI-IEC-SB-2022-34) and Apollo Medical Hospitals, Kolkata, India (Approval No: IEC/BRI2024/06/09), with approval from the Institutional Ethical Committee (IEC) of the respective hospitals. Tumor-node-metastasis staging classification was followed.

Cell culture

4T1 cells (RRID: CVCL_0125) were cultured in vitro in RPMI 1640 media containing 10% FBS. Ehrlich carcinoma (EC) (RRID: CVCL_3873) cells were maintained by passaging in the intraperitoneal cavity of Swiss albino mice. 4T1 and EC cell lines were purchased from National Center for Cell Sciences (NCCS), Pune, India. Authentication of cell lines was validated by the cell repository. Cells were proliferated and preserved according to the instructions of the provider. The morphology of the cell lines was checked regularly, and viable cells were preserved at passage two, and all in vitro culture set-ups were performed between 7 and 10 passages after thawing.

Generating cross-presenting bone marrow-derived DCs from mice

The mice were euthanized as per ethical guidelines. The hind limbs were dislocated, and the femur and tibia were dissected out aseptically from BALB/c mice. The bone marrow was flushed out in sterile chilled PBS. The erythrocytes present in the marrow cells were lysed. The cells were cultured in 10% FBS-containing RPMI 1640 medium, supplemented with recombinant mouse GM-CSF (rm GM-CSF) (5 ng/mL), recombinant mouse IL-4 (rm IL-4) (20 ng/mL), and rm Flt3L (25 ng/mL). Cultures were maintained for 6 days at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere comprising 5% CO2 in air.65 On the third day, the supplement-containing media was replenished, and on the sixth day, the non-adherent cells were harvested from the culture as immature DCs.

Preparing tumor supernatant and tumor antigen

Tumor supernatants were harvested from media of 70% confluent 4T1 and MDA-MB-231 (RRID: CVCL_0062) cultures, centrifuged, and filtered before further use. Cellular lysates of 4T1 cell lines were prepared from single-cell suspensions having approximately 2 × 107 cells/mL PBS and subjected to repetitive freeze (liquid nitrogen) and thaw (37°C water bath) cycles 7–10 times. The produced lysate was centrifuged, filtered, and the protein content was estimated by Bradford assay.

Tumor conditioning and antigen loading of bone marrow DCs

The immature mouse BMDCs were cultured overnight in complete RPMI 1640 media supplemented with recombinant mouse GM-CSF (rm GM-CSF) (5 ng/mL), recombinant mouse IL-4 (20 ng/mL), rm Flt3L (25 ng/mL), tumor antigen (5 μg/mL) from 4T1 cultures, and tumor supernatant. Chilled PBS-EDTA buffer was used to collect antigen loaded mature DCs.

For in vitro human DC culture and loading with specific tumor antigen, PBMCs were isolated and immature cells were incubated for 24 h in 10% FBS-containing AIM-V media with 100 ng/mL of GM-CSF, 20 ng/mL of IL-4 and 100 ng/mL of FLT3L, along with 5 ng/mL of tumor antigen derived from MDA-MB-231 cells.65,66

Supplementation of 27HC, atorvastatin, estradiol, tamoxifen, and DAPT in DC culture

In in vitro experiments, 27HC was prepared by diluting a 10 mM ethanol solvent stock directly into the medium according to prior standardized dosage.67 Since the commencement of culture, the cells were treated with a standardized dosage of 10 μM atorvastatin dissolved in DMSO. Control cultures contained the same ethanol and DMSO concentrations. Estradiol was dissolved in water and added at a dose of 0.1 μg/mL.68 The dosage of tamoxifen was standardized to 10 μM.69 DAPT was added at a concentration of 5 μg/mL to DC culture.70

Purification of CD11c+CD8α+DCs

The required cell suspensions were magnetically labeled with CD8/CD11c/lineage-cocktail antibodies as required. Cell purification was performed following the manufacturer’s protocol of the lineage sorting kit. The Lin−CD45+HLADR+ cells were selected through flow cytometry of human DCs.

Co-culture setup

Purified CD11c+CD8α+ DCs were isolated and co-cultured with MACS-purified CD3+CD8+ T cells in a 1:5 ratio in RPMI 1640 medium containing β-mercaptoethanol. After incubation under differential culture conditions for 48 h, the suspension of T cells was removed and collected separately. The semi-adherent DCs were then harvested using chilled PBS-EDTA buffer, centrifuged, and retrieved for downstream experimental assays.

Flow cytometry

The cells to be analyzed counted and stained with fluorescently labeled antibodies against respective antigens (1:100 dilution). The cells were incubated in the dark for 30 min. In case of intracellular staining the cells were incubated with Brefeldin (10 nM) for 4 h for intracellular retention of cytokines. The cells were subsequently treated with 0.2% saponin-containing membrane permeabilization buffer in PBS. Following antibody incubation, the cells were fixed in 1% PFA. The cell analysis data were acquired utilizing LSR Fortessa X-20 Cell Analyzer (Becton Dickinson, USA) (RRID: SCR_018655) and evaluated with respect to isotype controls after examining cell morphology through FSC-A and SSC-A. Analysis of data was done using FlowJo (10.8.1) (RRID: SCR_008520) (Becton Dickinson, USA) software.

RNA isolation, cDNA synthesis, and qRT-PCR

The phase separation strategy was used to isolate total RNA from the target cells using Trizol Reagent (Invitrogen). The procedure for RNA isolation was performed in an RNase-free environment within a laminar air-flow cabinet. The isolated RNA was preserved at −80°C, and cDNA was prepared by reverse transcription using cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as per the manufacturer’s protocol. 2X Go Taq Green Mix (Promega) was used to amplify the target genes, and respective gene bands were electrophoresed on 1.5% agarose gels and visualized after ethidium bromide staining. The intensity of bands was assessed (visualized) and captured on ChemiDoc XRS+ (BioRad Laboratories, CA, USA). The band intensities were quantified with Image Lab 6.1 (RRID: SCR_014210) software.

ELISA

The cytokine-containing supernatants isolated from experimental groups of DC cultures were immobilized overnight in 96-well microplates (50 μL/well), and blocking was done with 5% BSA (200 μL/well) at 4°C for 3 h. Subsequently, the antigens were labeled with specific primary antibodies (1:500 dilution) and incubated overnight at 4°C. The unbound antibodies were washed with PBS-Tween 20, followed by addition of HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:1000 dilution), which was incubated in the dark for 2 h. Finally, TNB substrate was added, which produced a cyan-colored visible result; the color converted to yellow on addition of 1 N H2SO4. The variations in optical densities were measured through Spectramax i3x (Molecular Devices) (RRID: SCR_026346) at 450 nm. The software SoftMax Pro 7.1 (RRID: SCR_014240) was utilized for data procurement.

Extraction of total, cytosolic, and nuclear proteins from cells

For the extraction of total protein, the cells were incubated at 4°C for 30 min in a protein extraction buffer comprising 1% Triton X-100, 10 mmol/L Tris-HCl (pH 7.4), 1 mmol/L EDTA, 0.2 mmol/L sodium orthovanadate, and 0.5% Nonidet P-40 in PBS, with a cocktail of protease inhibitors. The suspension was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 30 min at 4°C for the collection of total cellular protein. The single-cell suspension was treated with ice-cold cytoplasmic protein extraction buffer for an hour at 4°C. The cells were then centrifuged at 8,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C, and subsequently the supernatant was collected as cytosolic protein extract. The remaining pellet was resuspended in nuclear protein extraction buffer and thoroughly mixed. It was further centrifuged at 14,000 × g at 4°C for 30 min. The supernatant was collected as nuclear protein.

Western blotting

Protein concentration was estimated by Bradford assay. 30–50 μg of protein extracts were mixed with Laemmli buffer (50 μg/mL) and boiled at 100°C for 2 min. It was then fractionated or separated by 12% SDS-PAGE and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. Blocking of nonspecific sites was achieved with 6%–8% of skimmed milk at room temperature for 1 h. The membrane was incubated overnight with respective antibodies (1:500 dilution) at 4°C. HRP-tagged secondary antibodies (1:10,000 dilution) were used for probing of bands, which were visualized with ECL HRP substrate (Advansta).

Cryo-sectioning, H&E staining, and IF

2% paraformaldehyde- and 30% sucrose-fixed samples were set in an Optimal Cutting Temperature compound (OCT, Leica Biosystems). Cryostat (Leica CM1950) (RRID: SCR_017543) was used for sectioning (3–5 μm) of samples, which were adhered on poly-L-lysine-coated slides and preserved at −80°C. For hematoxylin and eosin (H & E) staining, the standard laboratory protocol was followed.

The cryosections on slides were washed with PBS for 10 min, and the cells were permeabilized for intracellular staining with 0.15% Triton X-100, followed by blocking in 5% BSA overnight. The next day, fluorescent-tagged antibody was added (1:200 dilution) and kept overnight at 4°C. Slides were mounted using Fluoroshield-DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich). The images were captured with a fluorescence microscope (BX-53, Olympus, Japan) (RRID: SCR_022568) and a confocal microscope (FV4000, Olympus, Japan) (RRID:SCR_022568). Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) was evaluated by means of ImageJ software.

Measurement of dendrite length of DCs

For every fluorescence image, spatial calibration was done to convert pixel-based dimensions to micrometers (μm). The scale bar generated by the microscope was used as a reference.

The straight-line tool was used to draw a line along the scale bar, and the Analyze→ Set Scale function was applied. Next, the known distance (μm) was entered to generate a pixel-to-micrometer conversion factor across datasets. Each dendritic length was traced from the cell body to the distal tip with the segmented line tool, and the measurements were made with the Analyze → Measure function. An average was estimated from multiple dendrites per cell. The assessment was performed under identical conditions and in a blinded manner to minimize bias.

Adoptive transfer of antigen-primed T cells

CD3+CD8+ T cells from DC-T cell co-culture under differential conditions were magnetically sorted. Cells (1 × 106) were adoptively transferred into the tail vein of 4T1 tumor-bearing NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ strain. Tumor volume was monitored every 3 days.

si-RNA-induced in vivo silencing

Murine cyp27a1 siRNAs were synthesized with Silencer® siRNA Construction Kit (Life Technologies) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The constructed primers are enlisted in Table S8. Knockdown of cyp27a1 within the tumor was achieved through the intratumoral administration of siRNA (10 μg), in combination with in vivo-jet PEI (1.2 μL) and a 5% sucrose solution (50 μL). The delivery volume was determined based on the N:P ratio, following the manufacturer’s guidelines. The initial delivery was carried out when the tumor volume reached around 70 to 90 mm3.63

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

The modulation of Flt3 and IRF8 genes by RBPJ was determined by chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assay according to a previously established protocol.71,72 Placing the transcription start site at the center, 2,000 nucleotides for both murine Flt3 and IRF8 genes were chosen from Eukaryotic Promoter Database, and precise single-nucleotide positions of TFBMs for binding to RBPJ were identified using JASPAR CORE (RRID: SCR_003030) 2018 version for vertebrates. Consensus sequences for binding of RBPJ were obtained from TRANScription FACtor database (TRANSFAC 8.3) (RRID: SCR_001624) using PROMO software of ALGGEN (RRID: SCR_016926) (BarcelonaTech) web server, which is a TFBM-predicting algorithm. ChIP was performed according to the protocol provided by the manufacturer (Millipore). Initially, murine BMDCs (1 × 105) from different groups were placed in 2% RPMI 1640 medium, followed by fixation using 1% paraformaldehyde. Subsequently, the cells were suspended in 1 mmol/L PMSF-containing PBS and washed. The cells were then lysed with SDS lysis buffer. Ultrasonication was conducted to shear DNA with 10 repeats of on/off pulses at 30 kHz, succeeded by an overnight incubation with RBPJ antibody (5 μg), which was then collected using anti-rabbit IgG-coated protein agarose beads. Extraction of eluted DNA was performed with phenol/chloroform and precipitated using ethanol. This was followed by RT-PCR with primers specific to murine Flt3 and IRF8 promoters. The primers used for chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) are added in Table S7.

Statistical analysis

The data presented are the mean ± SD from either three to six in vivo independent experiments or approximately five in vitro independent experimental groups to ensure sufficient statistical power. All mice cohorts and patient samples were randomized before being assigned to groups. Blinding was applied at each stage of the studies, including group distribution, data assessment, and result collection. These were performed by investigators blinded to treated groups to minimize bias. It was assumed that the data followed a normal distribution with comparable variances. Statistical significance was determined using an unpaired Student’s t test (for two groups) or one-way/two-way analysis of variance (for more than two groups). Patient data were randomized and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics (RRID: SCR_016479) (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the Phi-Cramer’s V test, while recurrence-free survival was evaluated by Kaplan-Meier analysis. All statistical analyses and heatmap visualizations were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.4.2 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) (RRID: SCR_002798). For experimental data, the number of in vitro or in vivo replicates and the specific statistical tests used are indicated in the respective figure legends. A p value ≤0.05 was considered statistically significant.
