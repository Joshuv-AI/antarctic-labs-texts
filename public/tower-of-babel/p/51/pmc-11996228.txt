Applications of artificial intelligence to myeloproliferative neoplasms: a narrative review
Authors: Andrew Srisuwananukorn, Jordan E. Krull, Qin Ma, Ping Zhang, Alexander T. Pearson, Ronald Hoffman
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Artificial intelligence (AI) is a rapidly growing field of computational research with the potential to extract nuanced biomarkers for the prediction of outcomes of interest. AI implementations for the prediction for clinical outcomes for myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPNs) are currently under investigation.Areas covered:In this narrative review, we discuss AI investigations for the improvement of MPN clinical care utilizing either clinically available data or experimental laboratory findings. Abstracts and manuscripts were identified upon querying PubMed and the American Society of Hematology conference between 2000 and 2023. Overall, multidisciplinary researchers have developed AI methods in MPNs attempting to improve diagnostic accuracy, risk prediction, therapy selection, or pre-clinical investigations to identify candidate molecules as novel therapeutic agents.Expert opinion:It is our expert opinion that AI methods in MPN care and hematology will continue to grow with increasing clinical utility. We believe that AI models will assist healthcare workers as clinical decision support tools if appropriately developed with AI-specific regulatory guidelines. Though the reported findings in this review are early investigations for AI in MPNs, the collective work developed by the research community provides a promising framework for improving decision-making in the future of MPN clinical care.

Introduction

The Philadelphia-negative chromosome (Ph-) myeloproliferative neoplasms (MPN) are a heterogenous group of chronic hematological malignancies with core clinical and histopathological characteristics. The Ph-MPNs include polycythemia vera (PV), essential thrombocythemia (ET), and primary myelofibrosis (PMF). These blood cancers can change their clinical phenotype over time, as demonstrated by ET patients evolving to PV, as well as ET or PV patients progressing to a secondary form of MF that resembles PMF. The molecular underpinnings that drive disease pathogenesis and clinical manifestations involve constitutive activation of the signaling pathways involving Janus Kinase (JAK) and signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) proteins. The ongoing JAK-STAT activation is a consequence of driver mutations in Janus Kinase 2 (JAK2), Calreticulin (CALR), or Myeloproliferative leukemia virus (MPL). JAK2 mutations can be associated with each of the MPNs, while mutations in CALR and MPL occur exclusively in ET and PMF patients. Patients with MPNs typically present with a variable constellation of abnormalities, including elevations of blood counts (PV, ET), cytokine-driven systemic symptoms (PV, ET, PMF), hepatosplenomegaly due to extramedullary hematopoiesis (PV, ET, PMF), megakaryocytic hyperplasia, increased marrow microvessel density, and bone marrow fibrosis (PMF). The clinical course of the Ph- MPNs is associated with significant morbidity and mortality due to a predilection for venous and arterial thrombotic events as well as progression to advanced forms of myeloid neoplasms such as secondary MF, myelodysplastic syndrome, and a form of secondary acute myeloid leukemia termed MPN-blast phase (MPN-BP) [1].

To date, the clinical care of MPN patients relies heavily on the appropriate classification of the type of MPN, which in some patients can be challenging due to inconsistent diagnostic evaluations which ultimately results in the delivery of substandard care. For example, the currently accepted diagnostic criteria for the MPNs as defined by the World Health Organization and the International Consensus Consortium rely on laboratory and pathological considerations that overlap across disease states and are frequently inaccurately employed due to erroneous evaluations by clinicians and hematopathologists [2–4]. Patients are evaluated for the severity of systemic symptoms and risk of thrombosis or progression to a more advanced form of disease, which determines the implementation of a limited number of treatment paradigms. These therapies can palliate the consequences of each of these MPNS, but with the exception of allogeneic stem cell transplantation, are incapable of providing curative therapy. The current risk stratification systems are helpful but imperfect in defining criteria with which to select appropriate treatment options, and valid biomarkers to reliably predict MPN clinical outcomes or the degree of response to initial therapy are lacking. Although the number of therapeutic options for MPN patients have increased during the last decade, significant challenges and unmet needs persist for the accurate assessment and management of these patients.

Clinicians and researchers have a strong interest in standardizing diagnostic and therapeutic considerations with the goal of identifying standard strategies for optimizing patient-driven outcomes. With the ever-increasing volume of clinical data readily available from MPN patients, novel subtle associations with clinical outcomes may be difficult to extract. The advent of novel technologies within the realm of artificial intelligence (AI), machine learning, and deep learning and have rapidly garnered interest by clinician scientists for the automated extraction of novel biomarkers that might be used to accurately predict clinical outcomes.

In this narrative review, we will describe the status of efforts to implement AI frameworks that address the improvement of MPN-specific clinical care. This review will explore how AI can be utilized both to assess not only clinically available data for pragmatic decision tool development but also exclusively research data which could be used for scientific discovery (Figure 1).

Artificial intelligence in clinical medicine and hematology

In the modern computational era, AI is a poorly defined term that encapsulates the broad notion of training an algorithm with large quantities of domain-specific data to understand the complex similarities and differences of input data [5]. As a pragmatic resource, clinicians may deploy an algorithm’s nuanced understanding of relevant data for the accurate identification or representation of particular subgroups of interest. In a number of clinical applications of AI, the training data is often paired with annotations for clinical outcomes, such as patient diagnosis, risk category, or response to therapy.

AI algorithms are the building blocks needed to create systems capable of learning, adapting, and evolving. AI algorithm architectures can be engineered to analyze a flexible array of data modalities, such as neatly organized discrete tabular data that is already used in non-AI research. However, several novel architectures are available to interpret modalities less frequently analyzed by traditional statistics, such as non-structured text reports and radiographic/histopathologic images. A single prototypical AI model is not available, but a number of popular algorithm architectures exist that are chosen due to their known performance in various data domains and their familiarity with the individual investigator. The specific choice of the type of AI architecture is largely rooted in empirical model performance as opposed to preconceived hypotheses. For clinicians not familiar with AI models, popular specific architectures typically employed in clinical research include Random Forest or XGBoost models for discrete data [6], Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) for imaging data [7], Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) for the generation of synthetic representative images [8], and large language models (LLMs) for text data [9].

To briefly describe the general framework of a supervised AI experiment, research data is typically separated into mutually exclusive groups for ‘training’ and ‘validating’ a given AI model. Throughout training, the model assesses the data to create a prediction for an outcome of interest, producing iteratively more accurate predictions due to a pre-specified method for ‘correcting’ the model’s predictions. Once a model is deemed appropriately highly performing, the model is then evaluated on previously unseen validation data to establish performance metrics. Ideally, AI models should also be evaluated on a ‘test’ data cohort from external institutions to assess generalizability. However, frequently models may be evaluated on ‘hold-out’ datasets from the same institution that created the training data. AI models are commonly appraised based on metrics such as the area under the receiver operator characteristic (AUROC), sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy. The AUROC metric is a combined measure comparing the sensitivity and specificity of a model at various cutoff points for classification. For reference, an AUROC of 1.0 is an ‘ideal’ model, contrasting with an AUROC of 0.5 which is equivalent to random predictions.

Separate from outcome prediction, certain AI architectures such as GANs and LLMs have been implemented to generate novel synthetic data that is representative of a desired concept. As an explicit example, researchers have utilized GANs trained on pathology images as an explanation tool to create synthetic histological images that exaggerate subtle morphological features associated with specific disease states. Our group has shown in thyroid cancer that BRAF and BRAS-mutated cancers have previously unidentified appearances that could be interpretable by humans by synthetic image generation from GANs; subsequently, the morphologies were prospectively identified by pathologists-in-training [10]. Similarly, LLMs are able to generate novel text data representative of the desired context, which allows for the development of chatbot tools such as ChatGPT by OpenAI, Copilot by Microsoft, and Bard by Google. For example, a medicine-specific LLM, Med-PaLM, was developed by Google and tested for its medical knowledge with questions in the style of the U.S. Medical Licensing Examination (USMLE). Med-PaLM was able to achieve a physician-level passing score upon USMLE-style questions, and furthermore, Med-PaLM provided long- form answers to explain the algorithm’s reasoning [11].

As such, the use of these novel AI frameworks in clinical medicine have rapidly attracted a great deal of attention. Particularly in medical oncology, AI algorithms have been developed as clinical decision support tools to predict clinically relevant decision points such as diagnosis, prediction of patient outcomes, therapy selection, and survival. The U.S. Food and Drug administration (FDA) has already approved numerous AI-informed medical devices [12], including oncology-specific cases such as prostate cancer detection on tissue biopsies [13]. However, the use of AI for hematological disorders is comparatively at an earlier stage of development [5]. With the field of AI in hematology growing rapidly, the focus of the current narrative review will be the current investigations of AI algorithms impacting MPN clinical care.

In this endeavor, we have performed a literature review by searching the PubMed and American Society of Hematology databases for abstracts and manuscripts detailing ‘artificial intelligence’ or ‘machine learning’ in each MPN disease between 2000 and 2023.

AI for MPN outcome prediction on clinically available data

For the management of MPN patients, clinicians are often asked to render a subjective assessment and recommendation for management based on available clinical and histopathological information. Due to the inherent subjectivity that influences both diagnostic and therapeutic considerations, the lack of uniformity of MPN assessments may lead to systematic discrepancies in the care of MPN patients. Previous researchers have clearly noted a high degree of discordance in making an MPN diagnosis, even when based upon the opinions of expert hemato-pathologists, who frequently evaluate a high volume of individual cases [4].

Diagnosis

AI researchers have attempted to create objective decision tools for the diagnostic assessment of digitized bone marrow biopsy (BMB) images. Prior to the hematopathologist interpretation, BMB specimens are stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or reticulin to assess for relevant morphological features. In clinical practice, the established criteria for MPN diagnoses such as PV, ET, and PMF rely on morphologic analyses of megakaryocyte morphology and distribution, presence of myeloid and erythroid precursor subpopulations, and the degree of bone marrow fibrosis [2,3].

Sirikunwattana et al. (2020) have developed a multi-step AI workflow automatically appraising H&E-stained BMB to predict a specific type of MPN. Within their investigation, they developed two distinct models: first for megakaryocyte identification from an entire BMB image, second for MPN diagnosis prediction from isolated megakaryocyte sub-images. By associating megakaryocyte morphology with MPN subtype, the authors were able to differentiate 45 ET, 18 PV, and 24 MF patients from 43 non-cancer patients using their automated workflow. The final H&E BMB model achieved high predictive power with an AUROC of 0.95 [14]. In a similar initiative, AI has been utilized to differentiate between two MPNs that share many clinical features but have distinct characteristics and clinical outcomes, namely prefibrotic PMF and ET, by examining reticulin-stained biopsies [15]. To accomplish this task, Ryou et al. (2023) have developed a scoring system termed the Continuous Indexing of Fibrosis (CIF) score, which is a semi-quantitative score of fibrosis within extracted images of the whole digitized BMB. The authors utilized a semiautomated extraction workflow to rank the amount of fibrosis among a library of image tiles. Using a CNN AI model to predict fibrosis score from the entire reticulin-stained BMB, the authors characterized the heterogeneity of fibrosis and subsequently associated topological parameters of fibrosis in conjunction with an AI-extracted megakaryocyte morphology for the final patient classification of prePMF or ET. Their model was able to achieve high performance of AUROC 0.94 in internal cohorts and maintained adequate performance with AUROC of 0.77 on 35 ET patients who participated in the Medical Research Council PT-1 clinical trial, which was an international, multi-center, randomized controlled trial comparing outcomes of ET patients at high-risk of vascular events (based on age, platelet count, or cardiovascular risk factors) treated with anagrelide or hydroxyurea.

Our group has similarly developed an AI algorithm to automatically assess H&E BMB for the differentiation of pre-PMF from ET patients. In an international investigation, we chose a biologically informed model that was previously trained on over 32,000 pan-cancer digital biopsies. We further trained our model on 100 prePMF and 100 ET patients treated at the University of Florence, Italy, and validated the model on an external cohort of 20 ET and 6 prePMF patients treated at Moffitt Comprehensive Cancer Center. Our model achieved an AUROC of 0.90 and an accuracy of 92.3% on the external cohort [16].

Although not necessarily evaluated by currently accepted diagnostic guidelines, pathological analysis of readily available peripheral blood smears may help identify specific types of MPNs. Kimura et al. (2021) developed an AI framework to differentiate PV, ET, and PMF using AI-identified cellular morphologies as assessed on May – Grunwald – Giemsa stained peripheral blood smears [17]. In a combinatorial system combining both CNN-predicted individual cell types with hematological parameters, the authors trained an XGBoost model for diagnostic outcomes. Their model was trained on 23 PV, 101 ET, and 36 MF patients achieved >90% sensitivity and specificity for MPN diagnosis in hold-out cohorts. Potentially, future clinical diagnostic work-up for MPN may be sufficient with peripheral blood and AI alone, obviating the need for bone marrow biopsies.

Disease state assessment

Splenomegaly is a hallmark symptom of ongoing MPN disease activity due to extramedullary hematopoiesis. The presence of splenomegaly may cause abdominal discomfort, early satiety, and in rare situations, splenic rupture. The characterization of splenomegaly is traditionally performed by palpation of the abdomen. Splenomegaly can also be quantified for volume calculations with imaging modalities such as ultrasonography, computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or positron emission tomography (PET-CT). The reduction of the degree of splenomegaly and relief of associated symptoms are key components of response criteria developed by the International Working Group-Myeloproliferative Neoplasms Research and Treatment (IWG-MRT) that are frequently used to assess the effectiveness of candidate therapeutic agents by regulatory agencies [18]. The IWG-MRT criteria are often used for outcome assessment within clinical trials for symptomatic myelofibrosis patients with splenomegaly, and as such a splenomegaly is a common reason for patients to initiate therapy.

AI researchers have developed various novel algorithms for the identification and quantification of the degree of splenomegaly in PMF patients using a variety of radiological imaging modalities. Yang et al. (2021) have developed a segmentation model using a CNN framework to automatically delineate splenic tissue within abdominal CT imaging. Within their study, the authors identified 138 secondary myelofibrosis patients enrolled on a phase 1 clinical trial [19]. When compared to manual annotation, the AI segmentation model achieved a Dice similarity coefficient of 0.951 and subsequent volume calculations achieved R2 of 0.998, both implying high concordance with human interpretation [20]. AI in imaging applications for MPN care have the potential to expedite clinical evaluation through automation and to expedite the degree of anticipated spleen size reduction.

Determination of risk for thrombosis or progression to MF or MPN-BP

Patients with PV and ET are at increased risk for the development of thrombotic events. Currently, the risk of thrombosis is determined by age, history of previous thrombosis, and JAK2 mutation status (in ET). Krichevsky et al. (2023) trained a dynamic AI model of 526 PV patients to predict the near acquisition of thrombosis. After assessing over 1 million model architectures and parameter configurations, the investigators’ final model revealed an AUROC of 0.84 with clear separation of thrombotic risk after 1-year of follow-up. Prospective evaluation and validation of this thrombosis-prediction model is ongoing, involving two large international cohorts [21].

Verstovsek et al. (2023) utilized a U.S. database to study 3852 PV patients on hydroxyurea, developing a model using random survival forest analysis for thrombosis risk prediction. Their analysis highlighted novel factors such as lymphocyte and neutrophil percentages, plus red cell distribution width as key indicators of higher thrombosis risk. They later validated their model with a Croatian sample of 100 patients, where it performed well with AUROC of over 0.8 for predicting thrombosis occurrence between 6 and 18 months [22].

Patients with PMF are at increased risk for transformation to MPN-BP. Various risk stratification scoring systems have been developed to classify patients into low or high-risk categories for progression. Standard prognostication systems in clinical practice include the International Prognostic Scoring System (IPSS) and its contemporary derivative systems, as well as the Myelofibrosis Secondary to PV and ET-Prognostic Model (MYSEC-PM) [23,24]. These scoring systems take into consideration clinical parameters such as cytopenias, percentage of blast cells present in the peripheral blood or bone marrow, constitutional symptoms, and cytogenetic or genomic abnormalities. Patients with higher risk of myelofibrosis are more frequently started on JAK2 inhibitors or enrolled in clinical trials and eventually referred for consideration of curative allogeneic stem cell transplantation [25].

Although utilized in clinical practice, contemporary scoring systems are imperfect, and the addition of AI-based biomarkers for progression risk prediction is under active investigation. From a large retrospective registry of 1617 Spanish MF patients, Mosquera-Orguiera et al. (2023) developed a novel tool named the ‘Artificial Intelligence Prognostic Scoring System for Myelofibrosis’ (AIPSS-MF) to predict overall survival (OS) and leukemia-free survival (LFS) among MF patients. Using a Random Survival Forest framework, the authors’ model relied on eight clinical variables regarding demographic, laboratory values, and presence of symptoms. AIthough AIPSS-MF did not incorporate cytogenetic or mutational abnormalities, this model achieved a c-index of 0.744 for OS and 0.703 for LFS in hold-out cohorts. Preliminary data suggest superior performance to the IPSS and MYSEC-PM models, and this AIPSS-MF model is currently publicly available as an interactive web-application [26].

AI for MPN outcome prediction of experimental data generated in research laboratories

Although clinicians are often tasked to develop nuanced recommendations for MPN management from clinically available information, biomarkers for highly accurate outcome predictions may lie within domains not clinically readily available. Active areas of investigation including interpretation of ‘big data’ within various multi-omic domains such as the tumor microbiome, chemical and protein structure analyses, and free-text reports. Although subsequent investigations rely on data not utilized by physicians in current practice, highly performant models developed in this endeavor may influence the direction of future clinical information assessment.

Diagnosis

For the discovery of novel associations with MPN pathogenesis, multiple AI models have predicted the presence of MPN diagnosis from largely investigational data types.

Recently certain solid cancers have been reported to be associated with specific infectious diseases, and that microbial gene signatures can accurately predict the presence of a specific type of cancer [27]. Similarly, Woerner et al. (2022) utilized deep DNA sequencing of blood and bone marrow from MPN patients to search for bacterial, fungal, and viral specific sequences among 1870 patients with myeloid malignancies, including acute myeloid leukemia (AML), myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), MPNs, and MDS/MPN overlap syndromes. Distinct microbial signatures were identified to predict the presence or absence of an MPN (regardless of subtype) as compared to MDS or AML. Researchers noted that various bacterial genera differed among myeloid malignancy disease types, and therefore created a random forest classifier for disease prediction. For the presence of any MPN diagnosis, their model achieved an AUROC of 0.79, and the authors noted an enrichment of Gordonia genus DNA among MPN patients. In a subgroup analysis isolating the MPN cohort, a microbiome signature was further able to predict the presence of a JAK2 mutation with an AUROC of 0.63, suggesting a possible association between microbiome and genomic status [28]. These results suggest further investigation of the contributions of dysbiosis in hematological malignancy pathogenesis and responses to treatment.

In a series of investigations, Guleken et al. (2023) performed protein structure analyses of patient peripheral blood, using investigational spectroscopy methods for the identification of patients with ET and PMF. By interrogating molecular interactions with Raman or Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy, the authors developed multiple AI models to predict MPN disease states with frameworks supporting discrete data including Support Vector Machine (SVM), discriminant analysis, ensemble learning, and k-nearest neighbor frameworks. Their models were trained with spectroscopy analyses of patient-derived blood from 45 ET patients, 85 PMF patients, or healthy controls. The optimal reported performance of each investigation resulted in prediction accuracies of >90% each in hold-out cohorts for ET with FTIR [29], PMF with Raman [30], and PMF with FTIR [31].

Risk assessment

In an effort to complement the laboratory and clinical parameters needed for IPSS and related risk assessment tools, research initiatives are ongoing to explore the added benefit of AI analysis of transcriptomic data for the identification of biomarkers for disease progression. Shen et al. (2021) have developed specific platelet transcriptomic signatures with predictive capabilities for MPN disease progression. The authors studied 97 MPN patients and developed an AI model utilizing LASSO regression. By training on >3000 platelet-derived RNA transcripts data, the authors’ LASSO model identified a subgroup of ET/PV patients that progressed to MF. Upon two validation cohorts separated from the training data by time or geography, the LASSO model of platelet transcriptomics achieved high performance with AUROCs of 0.96 and 0.97, respectively. The final model relied on <5 candidate platelet gene markers, which warrants further mechanistic evaluation for MF progression [32].

Therapy response and novel agent discovery

The predominant therapeutic strategy for treating myelofibrosis patients with splenomegaly or constitutional symptoms is directed at inhibiting constitutive activation of JAK-STAT signaling. Approved JAK inhibitors in clinical practice include ruxolitinib, fedratinib, pacritinib, and momelotinib [33]. However, the benefit of JAK2 inhibition is not universal with clinical trial endpoints for spleen and symptom response varying between 18% and 46% in Phase 3 clinical trials [34]. Novel methods to predict response to therapy or identification of novel therapeutics represent an area of significant clinical unmet need.

The presence JAK2, CALR, or MPL mutations do not currently influence therapeutic decision-making in MPN patients; there are presently no approved ‘targeted’ MPN therapies in clinical practice. Wildschut et al. (2023) established a rigorous ex vivo experimental strategy with the objective of identifying proteomic and genomic signatures to rationally target therapeutic vulnerabilities. Ultimately, through a series of elaborate experiments characterizing drug sensitivities of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC), the authors were able to identify three novel MPN subgroups with susceptibilities to Bromodomain and Extra-Terminal motif (BET) or Histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors, drugs targeting DNA replication, and inhibitors of unfolded protein response. As a crucial module within their overall experimental design, they utilized a CNN AI model of immunofluorescent images of single PBMCs from therapy-treated MPN patients to classify cell viability. The assessment of sensitivity to a panel of 79 clinical-grade drugs required image analysis of over 34 million individual cells, which was expedited with the use of AI classification and allowed for drug sensitivity profiling at scale [35].

The development of AI to identify novel therapeutic candidates is of special interest. Independently, Yang et al. (2019) and Belenahalli Shekarappa et al. (2023) have interrogated publicly available molecular databases to potentially identify novel JAK inhibitors [36,37]. In each investigation, the molecular structures were represented by ‘fingerprint’ signatures at the discretion of each experimental design. The engineered fingerprints represent information involving chemical structure such as atomic distances, presence of substructures, or direction of substructures. AI models trained on predefined fingerprints for the prediction of JAK2 activity by half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) have been validated with external molecular databases. For further justification, both authors subsequently performed in silico studies to computationally model the protein-ligand docking capabilities of AI-identified candidate molecules against the JAK2 protein.

In order to identify more effective JAK inhibitors, Yang et al. (2019) developed an XGboost model to predict the degree of JAK2 inhibition. The authors identified 13 commercially available candidate molecules with pyrrolopyrimidine-like structure. These 13 molecules were compared to ruxolitinib and tofacitinib (which have known high JAK2 and JAK3 potency, respectively) and two molecules with high JAK2 potency and selectivity versus JAK3 were identified. A clinical evaluation of the final two identified molecules in patients has not been reported to date. Separately, Belenahalli Shekarappa et al. (2023) validated a similar framework, but instead with a random forest model, as a virtual screening method to identify novel JAK2 inhibitors. Based on fingerprints representing atomic connectivity, the authors utilized an AI model to identify nine potential candidates with high binding affinity to JAK2 per molecular docking simulations. The authors did not perform in vitro validations. Though these methods have not yet led to the discovery of novel therapeutics, a coincidental finding of Belenahalli’s AI model revealed that the already-approved JAK2 inhibitor pacritinib was picked as one of the candidate JAK2 inhibitors.

Patient-interactive AI implementations

Healthcare systems and investigators query how AI can assist with clinical operations, particularly direct patient-facing deployment such as conversational AI chatbots. Multiple endeavors are under active investigation to determine how AI LLMs can be used to answer medical queries and offering other helpful information directly to patients without physician involvement. Efforts to use LLMs and other generative AI models to improve MPN quality experience are ongoing.

Though not developed for patient outcomes, Fu et al. (2020) implemented a natural language processing (NLP) workflow to extract diagnosis dates from clinical notes by using a cohort of MPN patients. Their non-AI rule-based method was able to extract dates with a recall of 83%, but in their analyses, AI-based methods such as Random Forest did not perform well due inaccurate date extraction [38]. Regardless, these results indicate the future direction and potential of AI-based text analyses, particularly for MPN patients.

Furthermore, the use of generative AI to directly improve patient quality of life has been developed by the pharmaceutical company Incyte. In an artistic program named ‘Unseen Journey,’ the program asked four MPN patients to describe their symptoms associated with their experience and symptoms of their MPN. Graphical artists then utilized undescribed generative AI models to produce images portraying the patient’s responses. Although this tool is not publicly available for all MPN patients, analogous methods can be developed in the future for similar objectives of artistic expression [39].

Limitations of AI

The results of each investigation cited above typically exhibit high performance upon either internal held-out cohorts or modest external datasets. As shown by the stated AUROC or accuracies exceeding 0.9% or 90%, the early AI models used for MPN patient data are encouraging. It is prudent to foster cautious optimism when interpreting these results, as AI models are prone to ‘memorize’ training data with seemingly high performance that does not generalize to other populations. While we welcome all efforts to use AI methodologies to enhance patient outcomes, we recognize that the majority of such efforts to date were completed prior to the advent of consensus guidelines for clinical AI development. For future investigators, familiarity with regulatory considerations is paramount for the ultimate goal of changing the current state of clinical practice. Multiple international working groups have published the STARD-AI [40] and TRIPOD-AI [41] guidelines for diagnostic/prognostic model development, as well as the SPIRIT-AI [42] and CONSORT-AI [43] guidelines for trial development of AI methods. Adherence to appropriate AI-specific study guidelines is paramount for the future of transformative clinical AI tools.

Furthermore, it is of crucial importance that AI algorithms are informed of biological reasoning and not technical artifacts. There exist numerous ‘explainability’ techniques to identify the discrete and/or imaging features that are highly associated with the outcome of interest. Explainability refers to the process of explaining to a human why and how a machine learning model makes a decision. Particularly for pathology image analysis, it is important to be assured that the algorithm bases prediction on histological features as opposed to nonsensical artifacts, such as pen marks [5]. Few AI explorations implement rigorous explainability, which hopefully will be standard practice in future investigations.

Conclusion

The clinical care of patients with myeloproliferative neoplasms requires the subjective interpretation of large volumes of clinical and imaging data. Current risk stratification systems are helpful but imperfect; accurately predicting patients who will eventually develop thrombosis or progress to myelofibrosis/MPN-BP is critical. Furthermore, modern-day therapies are directed toward reducing thrombotic risk, mitigating disease-related symptoms, or improving anemia. Therapeutic strategy outside of allogeneic stem cell transplantation are incapable of definitively preventing MPN disease progression. As such, the investigations of AI for MPN outcome improvement are encouraging and welcomed.

The role of AI in MPN care is in its early infancy. Within the above narrative review, we described 15 wide-ranging AI algorithms developed for use across multiple time points within the care of MPN patients. Using clinically available data modalities such as electronic medical record or histopathological and radiological images, AI models may assist in treating clinicians as a decision support tools. Investigations have identified novel AI algorithms with potential use as diagnostic assessment tools of bone marrow biopsy or peripheral blood smear images. Furthermore, attempts to automate aspects of disease assessment such as spleen volume may be assisted with AI segmentation methods of abdominal CT images. Clinical parameters are weighted heavily in currently used myelofibrosis risk stratification methods. AI might allow for more refined progression risk stratification with the use of modern methodology.

Furthermore, the use of AI to evaluate experimental laboratory data may lead to the discovery of helpful biomarkers for precise patient/drug selection. For MPN patients, we have described AI frameworks examining microbiome, spectroscopy, or platelet transcriptomic analyses of peripheral blood to improve the collective understanding of diagnosis and sequelae of MPNs. Intriguingly, interrogation of molecular databases may lead to the isolation of candidate inhibitors of JAK-STAT signaling or other relevant molecular pathways.

With ever-increasing amounts of clinical data, it can be challenging for practicing physicians to efficiently access and interpret clinical and imaging in order to provide optimal MPN care. Early investigations with AI models have implied possible improved efficiency and accuracy of diagnosis, prognosis, and therapy selection, but have yet to be rigorously validated in clinical trials. Patient-facing AI without the intervention of physicians may be implementable in the future, particularly to assistance with relaying medical information or pictorially describing nuanced symptoms. Thus, the utility of AI technologies in MPN care are vast, innovative, and potentially disruptive. In the future, we hope that physicians and patients may feel empowered by mature and robust AI tools and know that precision medicine for MPN care may soon be at our fingertips.

Expert opinion

The era of artificial intelligence in clinical hematology and specifically MPNs will likely impact clinical practice within the next two decades. There is ample opportunity for AI tools to augment physician decision-making across every phase of clinical care, from diagnosis to selection of therapy. We envision a future state of AI-based automatic assessment of bone marrow biopsies and peripheral blood smears facilitating morphological analysis and fibrosis scoring. It is unlikely that AI tools will supplant human judgment; likely AI will provide a ‘first pass’ analysis that will require further interpretation by expert physicians. The greatest utility of such a diagnostic tool lies in its potential use at centers that do not encounter high volumes of MPN patients. Furthermore, nuanced risk stratification systems are paramount for the future of precision medicine in MPNs, but the information domain from which AI should use for accurate predictions is unclear. In our opinion, clinically available data may already provide maximal predictive power, so developing AI prognostic tools upon ‘big data’ collections of the inflammasome, single-cell sequencing, multiparametric flow cytometry, radiologic imaging, and other high throughput biomarker panels may be needed to increase the power of automatic AI interpretation. We hope that AI will parse such human-incomprehensible multi-omic datasets to produce relevant signatures with a minimal subset of markers, which would provide a path for approval of AI-derived biomarkers.

The general field of AI has made significant, unprecedented improvements that continue to impact our day-to-day lives. Ten years ago, it would be difficult to imagine the modern-day achievements of text-to-video generation, three-dimensional protein structure prediction, and general intelligence chatbots that fill our daily conversations. Similarly, attempting to predict the capabilities of AI models 10 years from now is at best an exercise in futility. Numerous academic institutions and technology-based start-up companies have feverishly developed novel AI tools from proprietary and private data, but with relatively limited size datasets, many AI models cannot provide generalizable solutions. The scale of data required to train the large-scale ‘foundation’ models far exceeds what is currently publicly available within clinical domains of the hematology community. To move forward, the physician and commercial AI community must embrace a collaborative model to share resources and the precious digitized biological specimen involving rare disorders like myeloproliferative neoplasms.

AI tools will enter the medical arena, and it is of utmost importance for physicians to not be fearful of their implementation; rather it is crucial for healthcare providers to understand and embrace the added value they may bring. The fear of AI adoption among physicians and other medical thoughtleaders may be hyperbolized but is based on reasonable skepticism. ‘Emergent behavior’ broadly describes the phenomenon of large generalizable AI models producing unexpected, and often undesired, predictions and outcomes. It is often challenging or impossible to ‘open the black box’ and truly understand the mechanisms behind an algorithm’s prediction. Thus, medical researchers should push future investigations to assure that novel tools are ethical, equitable, and biologically reasonable.

Throughout medical training, physicians are taught the concepts of sensitivity, specificity, and other non-AI metrics to evaluate the validity and informativity of diagnostic tests used in medical practice. In the next era of medical training, it is likely that the principles of STARD-AI and TRIPOD-AI guidelines will be embedded in the training of the next generation of care providers to critically appraise future AI toolsets. Like how ultrasound training is a novel extension of the physical exam, health-care providers may soon be at a point where we are as comfortable with AI tools as we are with the stethoscope.
