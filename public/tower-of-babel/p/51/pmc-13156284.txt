The impact of improved export quality on urban green total factor productivity
Authors: Li Tian, Risidaxshinni Kumarusamy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rapid urbanisation has intensified the challenge for cities to balance economic growth with environmental sustainability. This study examines how higher export quality influences urban green total factor productivity (GTFP), using panel data from 120 Chinese cities (2011–2021). Employing a slack-based measure (SBM) model and two-way fixed effects estimations with robustness checks, the results show that a 1% increase in export quality is associated with an approximate 0.03% improvement in GTFP. The effect is linear and robust across alternative specifications. Further analyses reveal that the positive impact of export quality is contingent on complementary factors: green innovation and industrial upgrading provide steady reinforcement, while digital innovation exerts the strongest amplifying effect, and market openness facilitates international spillovers. Heterogeneity tests indicate that large and coastal cities benefit more strongly than inland or smaller cities. These findings contribute to the trade–environment literature by identifying export quality as a distinct driver of urban green productivity. Policy implications highlight the need to prioritise digital innovation, support green R&D, and deepen trade liberalisation to ensure that export upgrading translates into sustainable urban growth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43733-z.

Introduction

As urbanisation accelerates worldwide, cities face increasing pressure to reconcile economic development with environmental sustainability. Rapid urban growth often leads to higher resource consumption and environmental degradation, posing significant challenges for urban economies. In this context, enhancing urban green total factor productivity (GTFP)—the capacity of cities to efficiently utilise resources while minimising environmental impact—has become a critical priority for urban policymakers and planners. Urban GTFP serves as a key indicator of a city’s ability to achieve sustainable economic growth, integrating both the efficiency of resource use and the environmental performance of urban production systems. As cities strive to meet sustainability goals, one important factor that can potentially drive improvements in GTFP is international trade, particularly the quality of exports.

Improved export quality, characterised by technologically advanced, eco-friendly, and value-added products, is believed to hold substantial promise for improving urban GTFP. Export quality goes beyond the traditional quantity and cost measures, incorporating advanced technologies, environmental considerations, and higher value-added components. These aspects can play a crucial role in influencing urban green productivity by promoting technological innovation, facilitating the transfer of sustainable technologies, and optimising the use of resources. However, despite the growing recognition of the importance of export quality in fostering urban sustainability, relatively few studies have explored the direct impact of improved export quality on urban GTFP.

The existing literature on urban GTFP has primarily focused on factors such as policy innovation, industrial structure, and technology adoption. For example, Wang et al.1 highlighted the role of smart city policies (SCPs) in significantly enhancing GTFP by fostering urban sustainability through innovative governance and technological infrastructure. Liu and Luo2 analysed urban land and energy structures, revealing that inefficient land use and energy consumption structures can negatively impact GTFP by stifling technical progress. Additionally, Zhao et al.3 examined the role of foreign direct investment (FDI), finding that FDI can hinder GTFP growth, especially through its adverse effect on green technological progress. These findings underscore the complexity of GTFP, indicating that a variety of factors influence urban sustainability. Yet, the quality of exports—particularly in terms of their technological sophistication and eco-friendly characteristics—remains underexplored.

International trade, especially through trade openness, digital trade, and FDI, has been widely studied in relation to GTFP. Several studies have examined how trade liberalisation and increased openness impact GTFP by promoting technological advancements and sustainability. Li et al.4 found that trade has a significant positive impact on GTFP in China, primarily by fostering technological progress and enhancing resource efficiency. Liu et al.5 revealed that green trade barriers—restrictions aimed at promoting sustainable production practices—positively affect GTFP by encouraging the adoption of green technologies. Moreover, the increasing significance of digital trade has been explored by Zhang6 and Ding et al.7, who found that digital trade openness enhances GTFP by improving production efficiency and reducing environmental costs. However, these studies have primarily focused on trade liberalisation and technological adoption without specifically addressing the role of export quality in driving urban green productivity.

Compared with trade volume indicators such as trade openness or FDI, export quality provides a distinct measure that reflects the technological sophistication, value-added content, and environmental sustainability of products. Unlike the “quantity-oriented” approach in existing studies, this paper emphasises export quality as a more direct channel for enhancing GTFP. Nevertheless, the direct link between export quality and urban GTFP remains underexplored in the literature. Moreover, while prior studies often focus on national or provincial levels (e.g.Wang et al.1, Zhao et al.3) they fail to capture the heterogeneity across cities. Coastal cities with advanced industrial clusters and stronger global linkages may benefit differently from improved export quality than inland cities with weaker absorptive capacity. Therefore, a city-level perspective is essential for uncovering heterogeneous effects and for formulating targeted policy strategies.

This study aims to address this gap by systematically investigating the urban-level impact of improved export quality on GTFP and examining the moderating roles of green technology innovation, industrial structure upgrading, digital technology innovation, and market openness. Unlike prior studies in which these factors are examined separately, this study conceptualises them as city-level conditions that shape how improvements in export quality are translated into urban green total factor productivity. By incorporating heterogeneity analyses (e.g., coastal vs. inland, large vs. medium-sized cities), the study further demonstrates why city-level dynamics offer unique insights beyond provincial or national studies.

The paper makes three main contributions to the literature. First, it advances the theoretical understanding of export quality by introducing it as a distinct driver of GTFP, moving beyond conventional trade indicators such as trade openness, FDI, and digital trade. Second, it enriches the empirical evidence by using panel data from 120 Chinese cities over the period 2011–2021, uncovering heterogeneous effects across different urban contexts, and providing a nuanced understanding of how export quality impacts GTFP at the city level. Third, it offers policy implications by providing city-specific strategies for leveraging export quality to promote sustainable urban growth, rather than relying on one-size-fits-all national policies.

Literature review and hypotheses

Literature review

This section reviews the theoretical background and studies that affect the total factor productivity of urban greenness. Furthermore, research hypotheses are proposed based on the theoretical background and related literature.

Theoretical review

The Environmental Kuznets Curve (EKC) theory, first proposed by Grossman and Krueger8, aims to explain the dynamic relationship between economic growth and environmental degradation. According to this theory, the initial stages of economic growth are often associated with an increase in environmental pollution, as industrialisation tends to exacerbate resource consumption and emissions. However, after reaching a certain level of economic development, environmental quality begins to improve, driven by factors such as technological advancements, stricter regulations, and higher-quality exports. This relationship forms an inverted U-shape, where pollution increases at lower levels of income and decreases at higher levels of income.

Building on the EKC theory, we hypothesise that economic growth, industrial structure upgrading, and technological progress—especially those driven by the improvement in export quality—contribute to the enhancement of urban GTFP. The key idea is that cities, through industrial and technological upgrading, gradually transition from resource-intensive, polluting industries to high-tech, low-pollution green industries. This shift is often facilitated by the adoption of green technologies, such as energy-saving, emission-reduction technologies, and cleaner production methods, which significantly reduce environmental pollution. These innovations help cities optimise their resource use, thereby improving both environmental quality and productivity.

Rather than assuming that export quality automatically generates these transformations, we emphasise a conditional perspective: the extent to which export upgrading contributes to green productivity critically depends on cities’ pre-existing technological capacity, industrial structure, and institutional environment. Improving export quality may create opportunities for firms to meet stringent environmental and technological standards in international markets, but the realisation of these opportunities requires complementary local conditions, such as green innovation capacity, digital infrastructure, and effective policy support.

In this sense, export quality can be viewed as a potential driver whose association with urban green total factor productivity depends critically on local economic and institutional conditions, rather than operating uniformly across cities. In cities with stronger technological absorptive capacity and more supportive policy environments, improvements in export quality are more likely to be translated into higher green productivity. By contrast, in cities with weaker innovation systems or limited institutional support, similar improvements in export quality may generate only modest gains in urban GTFP.

Hypothesis 1 (H1)

Improved export quality positively affects urban GTFP.

Empirical literature review

Recent studies have looked at many factors that affect urban green total factor productivity (GTFP) in Chinese cities. Wang et al.1 found that smart city policies (SCPs) can improve GTFP. They showed that policy changes can help cities become more sustainable. Liu and Luo2 used the SBM method and Luenberger productivity index to study the differences in input-output between cities. They found that bad urban land and energy structures hurt GTFP because they slow down technical progress and reduce efficiency.

Zhao et al.3, using data from 284 cities between 2004 and 2019, found that foreign direct investment (FDI) can reduce GTFP. This happens because FDI negatively affects the development of green technologies. On the other hand, Liu et al.9 found that green financial reforms and innovation policies in pilot zones helped increase GTFP in those cities. They also found that these effects spread to nearby areas. Xie et al.10 showed that land resource mismatch directly reduces GTFP and affects nearby cities. Cheng et al.11 looked at the impact of externalities and found that both Jacobs’ and Porter’s externalities can increase GTFP. They found that Porter’s externalities are especially effective in improving GTFP by encouraging innovation in industrial clusters.

Some studies focus on the effects of specific policies and economic factors on GTFP. Dai and Zhu12 studied the effects of climate policy uncertainty and found that it hurts GTFP by stopping investments in green technologies. Li et al.13 showed that China’s new urbanisation policy raised urban GTFP by 43.5%. This helped cities grow in a more sustainable way. Zhang et al.14 found that urban pilot policies that encourage innovation in green technologies are effective in boosting GTFP.

Wang et al.15 showed that industrial agglomeration has an inverted ‘U’-shaped relationship with GTFP. This means that moderate levels of industrial concentration can boost GTFP, but too much concentration may hurt it. Guo et al.16 found that combining the digital economy with the real economy helps raise GTFP. Shang and Feng17 also found a positive link between digitalisation and GTFP. Zheng et al.18 showed that the internet helps improve GTFP by improving digital infrastructure.

International trade and green total factor productivity

Many studies have looked at how international trade affects green total factor productivity (GTFP). Li et al.4 used data from 30 Chinese provinces between 2004 and 2017. They found that trade helps technological progress and increases GTFP. This shows that trade can drive technological growth and help sustainable development. Liu et al.5 used data from China and OECD countries between 2005 and 2019. They found that green trade barriers improve GTFP by encouraging green technologies and making productivity more sustainable. Zhang6 studied data from 30 Chinese provinces from 2002 to 2018. He found that increasing digital trade openness improves GTFP by making production more efficient and reducing environmental costs. Ding et al.7 also studied data from 30 Chinese provinces, from 2006 to 2015. They found that trade openness improves GTFP by promoting better use of resources. Dai et al.19 used a fixed-effect model and found that digital trade, trade openness, and FDI all help increase GTFP. They said digital trade is especially important for improving green productivity. Jiang et al.20 used a method based on microdata to study the Shanghai Pilot Free Trade Zone (SPFTZ). They found that the SPFTZ helped increase GTFP, showing that regional trade policies can improve green productivity.

Most studies have focused on how trade openness, digital trade, and FDI affect GTFP. Few studies look at how better export quality affects urban GTFP. Most research focuses on trade liberalisation or digital trade without looking at how export quality directly improves green productivity. High-quality exports, such as those with advanced technologies, higher value, or eco-friendly features, can improve urban productivity by encouraging innovation, helping technology transfer, and making resource use more efficient.

However, existing studies often implicitly assume that these benefits materialise uniformly across regions. In contrast, we argue that the impact of export quality on urban GTFP is inherently conditional, depending on cities’ technological readiness, industrial foundations, and institutional environments. This perspective highlights the importance of examining not only whether export quality matters, but also under what conditions it can be effectively translated into green productivity gains.

This study therefore adopts a city-level perspective to investigate how export quality affects urban GTFP and how this relationship varies across different urban contexts, characterised by differences in innovation capacity, industrial structure, digital development, and openness to international markets.

Hypothesis development

High-quality exports generally imply higher technology content and added value, particularly in green and eco-friendly products. This export upgrading can directly promote technological progress in cities, thereby improving production efficiency, reducing environmental costs, and enhancing resource use efficiency1. At the same time, the effect of export quality may vary across different urban contexts, depending on factors such as technological absorption capacity, infrastructure, and policy environment3.

Crucially, we do not conceptualise green innovation, industrial upgrading, digital development, or market openness as automatic outcomes of export quality upgrading. Instead, these factors are viewed as pre-existing city-level conditions that shape the effectiveness of export quality in generating green productivity gains. In cities with stronger innovation systems, more advanced industrial structures, and better digital and institutional environments, firms are better positioned to leverage export upgrading to adopt cleaner technologies and improve resource efficiency. By contrast, in cities with weaker absorptive capacity and limited policy support, improvements in export quality may not be fully translated into higher green total factor productivity.

Accordingly, our analytical framework adopts a conditional-effect perspective, in which export quality serves as a potential driver of green productivity whose impact is moderated by local structural and institutional characteristics. We thus propose the following moderating hypotheses:

H2a

Green technological innovation positively moderates the impact of export quality on urban GTFP.

H2b

Industrial structure upgrading positively moderates the impact of export quality on urban GTFP.

H2c

Digital technological innovation positively moderates the impact of export quality on urban GTFP.

H2d

Market openness positively moderates the impact of export quality on urban GTFP.

Methodology and data

Dependent variable

Urban Green Total Factor Productivity (U-GTFP): Traditional total factor productivity (TFP) measures how efficiently a city uses labour, capital, and technology to produce output. U-GTFP is a similar concept but also includes environmental factors. It shows how well a city uses its resources while reducing harm to the environment. This captures the idea of green and sustainable development.

We use the slack-based measure (SBM) model to calculate U-GTFP, following Liu and Luo2. This model considers unwanted outputs like industrial wastewater, SO₂ emissions, and solid waste. These outputs are treated under the weak disposability assumption, which means that reducing harmful outputs requires reducing good outputs in the same way. This assumption reflects the trade-off between economic growth and environmental protection.

Our model uses an input-oriented directional distance function. This method helps to increase good outputs like GDP and clean energy while reducing bad outputs like pollution and waste. By using this method, we make sure that environmental limits are taken into account when measuring efficiency. Moderating variables

Green Technological Innovation (Green-inno): This variable measures innovations that contribute to environmental protection and sustainable development through advances in science and technology. It includes improvements in energy efficiency, pollution control, and resource conservation22. We define green innovation as the number of green patents filed by residents in each city, following Gao et al.23 To adjust for city size, we normalize this indicator by population and GDP. The results from these adjustments remain consistent, indicating robustness.

Industrial Upgrading (Industrial-Up): Industrial upgrading refers to the process where industries shift from low value-added and low-skill production to higher value-added and more skill-intensive industries. This process involves technological advancements, improvements in production efficiency, and upgrading industrial chains. Industrial upgrading promotes a more efficient, diversified, and sustainable economic growth24. We measure the degree of industrial upgrading using the ratio of the tertiary (service) industry to the secondary (manufacturing) industry. A higher ratio reflects a greater degree of industrial transformation toward more advanced, service-oriented sectors.

Digital Technological Innovation (D-inno): This variable refers to the development and application of digital technologies, including information technology, artificial intelligence, cloud computing, big data, and the Internet of Things. These technologies drive the transformation of traditional industries, improving resource efficiency, reducing energy consumption, and lowering environmental pollution. To control for city size differences, we normalize this indicator by using measures such as patents per capita or patents per unit of GDP. The results from these normalized indicators are consistent with the main findings.

Market Openness (M-open): Market openness refers to how well-integrated a city is with the global economy through trade liberalization, investment facilitation, and cross-border technology flows. A higher degree of market openness provides cities with access to advanced technologies, capital, and international best practices, particularly in green industries like renewable energy and environmental protection technologies. We measure market openness as the ratio of total imports and exports to GDP. As a moderating factor, a higher degree of openness enhances the effect of export quality on green total factor productivity by facilitating technology transfer, foreign investment, and international collaboration.

Control variables

Economic Development (GDPp): GDP is commonly used as an indicator of a region’s economic development level. Higher GDP typically correlates with better technological innovation, more investment, and increased awareness of environmental sustainability25. Thus, cities with higher GDP tend to have greater capacity for adopting green technologies, improving efficiency, and enhancing green productivity.

Foreign Direct Investment (FDI): FDI plays a key role in technology transfer, capital injection, and industrial upgrading. The inflow of foreign capital can introduce efficient, environmentally-friendly technologies, particularly in manufacturing and high-tech sectors. This leads to advancements in green technology research and application, improving a city’s green total factor productivity (GTFP)26. Therefore, FDI generally has a positive impact on GTFP.

Public Expenditure on Education (Educ): Public investment in education influences technological progress, innovation capacity, and workforce adaptability. Higher education spending leads to better education and skill development27. Specifically, investments in STEM (science, technology, engineering, and mathematics) education promote technological innovation and green technology adoption, contributing to higher GTFP28.

Urban Population Size (Population): The size of the urban population directly affects both production and consumption. A larger population can drive demand for green technologies and innovations but also imposes pressures on resources and the environment29. This dual effect needs to be considered when analyzing the relationship between export quality and GTFP.

Digital Infrastructure (D-Infra): The development of digital infrastructure, such as Internet access and 5G coverage, can enhance resource allocation, improve production efficiency, and support the use of green technologies. In particular, digital infrastructure is essential for smart manufacturing and green economic transformations30.

Urban Fixed Asset Investment (Fixed): Investments in urban infrastructure, especially in green projects, play a crucial role in improving environmental quality and green productivity. Such investments can enhance environmental protection facilities and support clean energy projects, directly contributing to higher GTFP31.

These control variables are essential for providing a more comprehensive analysis of how export quality affects urban GTFP by accounting for other factors that could influence green productivity.

To assess external validity and address potential sample-selection concerns arising from data availability, we further construct an expanded city panel of 221 prefecture-level cities using the same data sources and variable definitions, and re-estimate the baseline model as a robustness check.

Empirical methodology

To analyze the relationship between improved export quality and urban GTFP, we adopted a two-way fixed effects model, following the methodology of Ding et al.7 This model is well-suited for our study as it accounts for both city-specific and time-specific factors. The two-way fixed effects approach effectively controls for unobserved heterogeneity across cities (such as differences in urban policies, industrial structures, and infrastructure quality) and over time (such as changes in technological advancements and global economic conditions). By controlling for these fixed effects, we aim to minimize biases arising from unobserved factors, thereby enhancing the accuracy of the estimated coefficients and improving the reliability of our results.

The two-way fixed effects model is particularly appropriate for panel data, as it accounts for the cross-sectional variation between cities and the temporal variation across years. These variations are essential for understanding how export quality impacts GTFP at the urban level. Additionally, this model helps address omitted variable bias, where potential confounding variables, such as local economic conditions, policy changes, or technological adoption rates, may vary across cities and over time but cannot be directly observed in the data.

We transform all variables into logarithmic form to reduce heterogeneity and ensure comparability across cities with varying levels of development and economic size. To prevent issues caused by small or zero values, we added a constant (e.g., +1) to all variables with zero or near-zero values before applying the logarithmic transformation, ensuring that the transformed variables are valid and comparable.

To address potential heteroskedasticity, which can lead to biased standard errors, we used robust standard errors in all regression analyses. The t-statistics presented in parentheses reflect these adjusted standard errors, ensuring that the hypothesis tests are robust to heteroskedasticity.

To examine the possibility of non-linear relationships between export quality and urban GTFP, we introduced a squared term for export quality in the model (as shown in Equations 6 and 7). We then performed a U-test to assess whether the relationship follows a U-shape or an inverted U-shape. The criteria for rejecting a linear relationship were as follows: Both the linear and squared terms must be statistically significant. The coefficients of the linear and squared terms must have opposite signs. The calculated turning point (where the relationship between export quality and GTFP changes direction) must fall within the sample range.

In our analysis, the squared term of export quality was insignificant, indicating that the relationship does not follow a non-linear pattern. Model test.

To ensure the robustness of our findings, we conducted a series of tests, each aimed at addressing specific concerns. First, to reduce the potential impact of external shocks, we truncated the sample period to 2011–2019, excluding the COVID-19 pandemic years (2020–2021). These years were marked by significant and atypical disruptions in trade and productivity, which could distort the relationship between export quality and urban GTFP.

Second, we excluded four municipalities—Beijing, Shanghai, Tianjin, and Chongqing—from the analysis. These cities have unique political, economic, and administrative characteristics (e.g., direct governance, higher investment, and stronger institutional capacity), which may not reflect the conditions of typical prefecture-level cities. Including these cities could lead to biased results, so they were excluded in the robustness checks.

Third, to rule out the possibility of spurious correlations, we performed a placebo test, as outlined by Cong et al.32 Specifically, we randomly reassigned the export quality data across cities and re-estimated the model. The placebo regressions yielded statistically insignificant coefficients, confirming that the observed relationship between export quality and urban GTFP is not driven by random noise but reflects a genuine, meaningful connection.

In addition to the above checks, we extended the robustness analysis in three key directions: We conducted multicollinearity diagnostics beyond the standard VIF < 5 criteria. All diagnostics indicated no serious multicollinearity issues, ensuring that the model estimates remain stable and reliable.

We performed heterogeneity analyses by splitting the sample based on city size (large vs. small) and location (coastal vs. inland). The positive effect of export quality on urban GTFP remained statistically significant across all subsamples, reinforcing the robustness of the primary findings.

To further address concerns of reverse causality, we implemented Granger causality tests. The results showed that export quality Granger-causes urban GTFP, with a significant F-statistic (F = 4.21, p < 0.01), whereas the reverse—urban GTFP causing export quality—was not statistically significant (F = 1.07, p = 0.32). This confirms that improvements in export quality drive gains in urban green productivity, thus mitigating concerns of reverse causality.

Moderating effects analysis

To explore how green technological innovation and industrial structure upgrading might influence the relationship between export quality and urban GTFP, we specified two sets of equations:

The first set of equations examined the direct effects of green technological innovation, industrial structure upgrading, digital technological innovation, and market openness on urban GTFP. These equations helped isolate how each of these factors directly influenced GTFP, particularly in the context of improved export quality.

The second set of equations tested the moderating effects of these factors by introducing interaction terms between the moderating variables and export quality. Specifically, these equations examined how green innovation, industrial upgrading, digital innovation, and market openness affected the relationship between export quality and urban GTFP. By incorporating these interaction terms, we assessed whether the impact of export quality on urban GTFP was amplified or diminished by these moderating factors.

Although the numerical size of the coefficient may appear small, it is economically meaningful when viewed in context. For example, a 10% improvement in export quality leads to about a 0.3% increase in urban GTFP. In large urban economies, even this moderate percentage change implies substantial gains in green output over time, especially when the effect accumulates year by year. Moreover, the magnitude of this effect is comparable to that of other key policy-related factors, such as environmental regulation, digital development, and industrial upgrading, whose estimated impacts on GTFP typically fall within a similar range. This suggests that export quality should be seen as a relevant policy lever, rather than an economically negligible factor.

In Columns III and IV, the coefficients on the squared term of export quality are not statistically significant, indicating that the relationship between export quality and GTFP is linear rather than non-linear. As export quality improves, its effect on green productivity remains stable and continuous, with no evidence of sharp thresholds or diminishing returns. This implies that export upgrading can contribute to sustained improvements in urban GTFP over time.

Regarding the control variables, the coefficient on GDP is negative and statistically insignificant, which suggests that economic scale alone does not automatically translate into higher green productivity. This result highlights that GTFP reflects efficiency and environmental performance, not just output size. While economic growth may increase energy use and industrial activity, it does not necessarily lead to improvements in green productivity.

By contrast, the coefficient on education is positive and significant (ranging from 0.038 to 0.026), indicating that investment in education plays an important role in raising urban GTFP. Higher education spending improves human capital and innovation capacity, which supports the adoption of green technologies and the diffusion of sustainable production practices. As a result, education contributes to long-term improvements in green productivity.

Finally, the coefficient on fixed asset investment is negative (−0.019 to −0.020) but statistically insignificant. This suggests that although fixed investment may have a restraining effect on GTFP in some cities, the evidence is not conclusive. One possible explanation is that a large share of investment is still directed toward traditional, resource-intensive sectors and infrastructure projects, which may limit gains in green productivity. This finding implies that, for fixed asset investment to consistently support GTFP, it needs to be reoriented toward green infrastructure and clean technologies.

Robustness checks results

To ensure the reliability and consistency of the results, several robustness tests were conducted to verify the impact of improved export quality on urban GTFP.

A robustness test was performed by shortening the sample period by two years. This analysis showed that the results remained significant, even with the shorter time frame, indicating that the positive impact of export quality on urban GTFP is stable and not driven by a longer-term effect. This strengthens the conclusion that the impact of export quality on green productivity is sustained over time.

A further robustness check involved excluding the four municipalities (Beijing, Shanghai, Tianjin, and Chongqing) from the sample. These cities have unique political, economic, and administrative characteristics that could potentially distort the results. After excluding these cities, the positive relationship between export quality and urban GTFP remained significant, confirming that the main findings were not driven by the specific features of these municipalities.

A placebo test was conducted to ensure the robustness of the results and rule out the possibility of the effect being driven by random factors or pseudo-variables. The placebo test showed that the coefficient for export quality was not significant, and the results were significantly different from those of the baseline model. This suggests that the impact of export quality on urban GTFP is genuine and not attributable to random or spurious effects, further validating the findings.

Our baseline estimates rely on a two-way fixed effects specification. While these estimates provide a descriptive benchmark, we further strengthen the identification by exploiting the staggered rollout of Cross-Border E-Commerce Comprehensive Pilot Zones (CBEC) as an exogenous institutional shock to cities’ trade digitalization environment and export-upgrading conditions. Rather than focusing on the average treatment effect of CBEC alone, we use the policy rollout as a quasi-experimental setting to examine whether the productivity effect of export quality becomes stronger after a city is exposed to CBEC. Specifically, we estimate a DID-style specification with city and year fixed effects and include the interaction term between export quality and the CBEC policy exposure indicator (CBEC×Export quality). A positive and significant coefficient on this interaction implies that, conditional on city and year fixed effects, export quality translates into larger gains in urban GTFP in cities after they become CBEC pilot zones, consistent with the view that CBEC improves the institutional and digital infrastructure needed for export upgrading to generate green productivity gains. The results (Column X) show that the coefficient on CBEC×Export quality is 0.163 and statistically significant at the 1% level, suggesting that the CBEC policy environment significantly amplifies the export-quality–GTFP relationship.

To explore the dynamic effects of export quality on urban GTFP, we additionally estimate a system GMM model. The lagged dependent variable is positive and statistically significant, indicating persistence in urban GTFP. Importantly, the estimated effect of export quality remains consistent with the baseline results. We interpret the system GMM evidence as supplementary rather than as a primary identification strategy.

Endogeneity diagnostics (Durbin–Wu–Hausman test, p = 0.000) indicate that export quality may be endogenous. We therefore report 2SLS estimates as a sensitivity check. The 2SLS results continue to show a positive and significant impact of export quality on urban GTFP. Moreover, the Shea partial R2 statistic supports the relevance of the instruments, alleviating concerns about weak identification. Given the well-known challenges of finding fully convincing instruments in this context, we treat the IV results as robustness evidence, while placing greater weight on the policy-based quasi-experimental design.

In addition, to address the concern that unit values may conflate product quality with production costs or inefficiency33, we construct an alternative export quality measure based on a residual-demand approach and include it as an additional robustness check. Following Khandelwal33, export quality is proxied by the residual from a market-share equation in which log export market share is regressed on log prices, product fixed effects, destination fixed effects, and year fixed effects. We then aggregate the residual-based quality measure to the city–year level using export-value weights and re-estimate the baseline specification. Column XII of Table 4 reports the results. The coefficient on the alternative export quality measure is 0.014 and statistically significant at the 1% level, indicating that cities with higher residual-based export quality also exhibit significantly higher levels of urban green total factor productivity.

Lastly, to assess the external validity of our baseline findings and alleviate concerns about sample selection, we construct an expanded city panel of 221 prefecture-level cities using the same data sources and variable definitions. We re-estimate the baseline two-way fixed effects model on this enlarged sample. The coefficient on export quality remains positive and highly significant, and its magnitude is very close to that obtained in the baseline 120-city panel. This consistency suggests that our main conclusions are not driven by the baseline sample composition. Appendix Table 7 reports balance tests comparing the baseline 120-city sample with the remaining cities. The results show that the baseline sample is characterized by significantly higher levels of GDP per capita, FDI inflows, and education expenditure, reflecting a common data-availability bias toward more economically developed cities. By contrast, no significant differences are observed in city size, and differences in fixed asset investment are only marginal. Importantly, re-estimating the baseline model using the full 221-city sample yields highly consistent results, indicating that sample selection does not drive the main findings.

Moderating analysis

Consistent with our conditional-effect framework, the moderating analysis examines how pre-existing city-level characteristics shape the extent to which export quality can be translated into higher urban green total factor productivity. The results show that all four factors—green technological innovation, industrial structure upgrading, digital technological innovation, and market openness—significantly condition the positive association between export quality and urban GTFP, although their magnitudes differ notably.

Among them, digital technological innovation exerts the strongest moderating influence: the interaction coefficient (0.018, significant at the 1% level) indicates that cities with stronger digital innovation capacity are better able to leverage export upgrading into green productivity gains, with marginal effects almost quadrupling in areas with high levels of digital patenting. This finding highlights the role of digital development not as a transmission channel generated by export quality, but as a structural condition that amplifies the effectiveness of export upgrading.

By contrast, green technological innovation and industrial structure upgrading provide steady but moderate reinforcement. Green innovation (coefficients around 0.012, significant at the 5% level) strengthens the impact of export quality on GTFP primarily by enhancing cities’ absorptive capacity for cleaner technologies and energy-efficient production methods, especially when patenting activity exceeds the sample median. Industrial upgrading (interaction coefficient 0.019, significant at the 1% level) increases the marginal effect of export quality by providing a more advanced industrial base in which export upgrading can be more effectively translated into productivity improvements, although its impact remains smaller than that of digital innovation.

Finally, market openness plays a supportive role, with positive but relatively weaker moderation (coefficient 0.104, significant at the 10% level). Rather than being an outcome of export quality upgrading, market openness functions as an enabling environment that facilitates international technology flows, foreign investment, and cross-border spillovers. These channels help cities better exploit the potential benefits of export upgrading, but their effectiveness depends critically on local absorptive capacity and institutional readiness.

When comparing coastal and inland cities, the results show that coastal cities (columns V and VI) exhibit stronger positive effects, with coefficients of 0.019 and 0.012, both significant at the 1% level. These results suggest that policy efforts should be tailored to the specific needs and capacities of different city types. Large and coastal cities should continue leveraging their existing advantages in innovation and infrastructure to foster sustainable growth, while small and inland cities may require more targeted institutional support, infrastructure investment, and innovation incentives to fully capitalize on the benefits of improved export quality.

Conclusion and policy implications

This study examines the impact of export quality upgrading on urban green total factor productivity (GTFP) using panel data from 120 Chinese cities over the period 2011–2021. The empirical results show that a 1% improvement in export quality is associated with about a 0.03% increase in urban GTFP, highlighting the important and economically meaningful role of export upgrading in promoting sustainable urban development. While the magnitude of the effect is moderate, it implies sizable gains when applied to large urban economies and when accumulated over time. The results also reveal clear heterogeneity across cities. Large and coastal cities, with stronger innovation systems and deeper global linkages, benefit more from export upgrading, whereas small and inland cities face structural constraints that limit the translation of export quality improvements into green productivity gains.

The moderating analysis further shows that digital technological innovation plays the strongest amplifying role in this relationship, followed by green technological innovation and industrial upgrading. Market openness provides additional support by facilitating international technology diffusion and cross-border knowledge spillovers.

Given its strong moderating effect, policymakers should place greater emphasis on digital innovation, especially in areas such as artificial intelligence, big data, and cloud computing. Incentives that encourage firms to adopt digital technologies can help improve export quality and, at the same time, magnify its contribution to green productivity. Policy tools may include pilot programs that integrate digital technologies with green innovation and targeted subsidies for digital patents in environmentally sustainable sectors.

Green innovation is essential for ensuring that export upgrading aligns with environmental sustainability. Local governments can promote this process by offering tax incentives for green R&D, supporting firms engaged in clean technology development, and encouraging public–private partnerships. These measures can help ensure that improvements in export quality deliver both economic and environmental benefits.

Industrial policies should reflect city-specific conditions. Large and coastal cities can focus on strengthening high-tech manufacturing and modern service sectors, while small and inland cities may require greater fiscal support and infrastructure investment to move away from resource-intensive industries and toward cleaner production models.

Market openness can serve as a channel for sustainable development when it is aligned with green objectives. The design of special economic zones with environmental standards, together with trade agreements that emphasize low-carbon development, can help ensure that foreign investment and technology transfers support greener export structures.

The heterogeneity of effects highlights the importance of local context. While large and coastal cities are well positioned to build on their existing advantages, small and inland cities face more constraints. These cities would benefit from targeted institutional support, education and training programs, and infrastructure upgrades that enhance their capacity to absorb new technologies and fully benefit from export upgrading.

Limitations

Despite the strong results, several limits of this study should be noted. First, although we use a quasi-experimental method based on the Cross-Border E-Commerce Comprehensive Pilot Zones and apply a Difference-in-Differences model, some unobserved factors may still affect both export quality and urban green total factor productivity. These factors may include changes in local environmental rules, informal local practices, or city-level development plans. We use fixed effects and many robustness checks to reduce this problem, but we cannot fully remove it. Second, the export quality index is built using common methods in the trade literature. However, measurement error may still exist because unit values are used to represent product quality. To deal with this issue, we add a robustness check using a Khandelwal-style residual quality measure. Even so, future studies may improve this measure by using firm-level data or more detailed cost information. Third, our baseline analysis relies on a balanced panel of 120 cities due to data availability for all key variables. To assess external validity, we additionally re-estimate the baseline model using an expanded sample of 221 cities; the results remain highly consistent. Nevertheless, some cities are still excluded because of missing information, so caution is warranted when extrapolating to data-poor regions. Fourth, the results are based on econometric analysis. We use a quasi-natural experiment and many checks to support a causal link. However, this method is still different from a real experiment. So, the findings should be seen as strong evidence under clear assumptions, not as final proof of causality. Finally, we include several key factors, such as green innovation, digital innovation, industrial upgrading, and market openness. But other factors, such as financial development, enforcement of environmental rules, and public views on sustainability, are not included. These factors may also affect how export quality leads to higher green productivity.
