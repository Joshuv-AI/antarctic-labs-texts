Reviewing the 36-hour trigger rule through a global lens
Authors: Elizabeth L Wolfe, LaTasha B Craig
Published: 2025

The timing between administrating the oocyte maturation trigger and performing the oocyte retrieval is a critical factor in assisted reproduction. Individual differences in follicular physiology and trigger pharmacokinetics have been shown to influence oocyte maturity, and at present, the optimal timing from trigger to retrieval has yet to be definitively established (1, 2). Currently, many fertility clinics adhere to a standard of retrieval within 36 hours of trigger administration. This recent large-scale study by Enatsu et al. (3) from Hanabusa Women’s Clinic in Japan, analyzing more than 59,000 in vitro fertilization cycles, offers important global insight into this issue. The investigators compared outcomes based on trigger type (gonadotropin-releasing hormone agonist [GnRHa] nasal spray Buserelin acetate vs. recombinant and urinary human chorionic gonadotropin [hCG]), and retrieval occurred before or after 36.5 hours. The study concludes that longer intervals may improve mature oocyte (MII) and blastocyst yield in GnRHa cycles, while shorter intervals may be more favorable in hCG cycles. The age-dependent effects, most evident among women older than 40, add an interesting dimension to an already clinically relevant topic. This investigation is notable not only for its size but also for its suggestion of clinical applicability. The enormous dataset and the focus on the timing of retrieval addresses an issue that every in vitro fertilization program encounters daily. Even small improvements in MII yield can influence cumulative success when applied across thousands of cycles.

For US practices that increasingly rely on GnRHa triggers in freeze-all cycles, the findings suggest that modestly extending the interval between trigger and retrieval might yield a small but consistent improvement in number of mature oocytes. However, the magnitude of benefit, noted to be approximately 0.5–1 additional MII oocyte per cycle, should be interpreted with caution. While this difference may be meaningful at a population level when applied across thousands of cycles, it is unlikely to significantly impact pregnancy or live birth rates for individual patients.

One of the most important takeaways from this study is the value of examining trigger regimens that differ from US standards. The investigators used intranasal Buserelin, a formulation not currently available in the United States. Global collaboration in reproductive medicine allows clinicians to learn from regions with access to alternative formulations or strategies. As fertility treatment becomes increasingly international, sharing data on diverse pharmacologic approaches can help drive innovation, enhance patient safety, and improve reproductive outcomes worldwide.

Despite its large sample size and thoughtful design, this study should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing as the ratio of MII oocytes to the total number of oocytes retrieved was not significantly different across any age group. Furthermore, the retrospective design and single-center setting limit generalizability of the findings. While the 36-hour standard remains widely used due to its consistent performance across diverse patient populations, this study reminds us that ovarian physiology and drug pharmacodynamics can vary depending on trigger type, and that refining timing might yield incremental gains. However, before modifying protocols, prospective, randomized, multicenter trials are needed to compare multiple interval lengths for hCG, GnRHa, and dual triggers. Such trials would help determine whether the biologic trends identified here translate into meaningful clinical outcomes.
