High salt supplementation of a MASH-inducing diet causes lean MASH phenotype with increased hepatic urea cycle activity and EIF5A hypusination
Authors: Shaopeiwen Luo, Norihiko Morisawa, Anissa Anindya Widjaja, Brijesh Kumar Singh, Derek John Hausenloy, Paul Michael Yen, Jin Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMASLD/MASH can occur in lean individuals; however, the environmental triggers and molecular mechanisms underlying lean MASH are unclear, and suitable animal models are lacking.ResultsMice fed a Western diet with liquid fructose (WDF) develops obesity and MASH (obese MASH). In contrast, high salt supplementation of WDF (HSWDF) produced a lean MASH phenotype with reduced steatosis but induced significant inflammation and fibrosis (lean MASH). In WDF-induced obese MASH, we observed decreased urea cycle activity and flux, along with reduced eukaryotic translation initiation factor 5 A hypusination (EIF5AH) and mitochondrial biosynthesis. High salt supplementation of WDF unexpectedly ameliorated these alterations, enhanced hepatic fatty acid oxidation and reduced hepatosteatosis. However, single-cell sequencing revealed that dietary high salt was associated with pro-inflammatory responses in hepatic immune cell subpopulations.ConclusionsIn summary, we have established a dietary mouse model of lean MASH that differs from obese-MASH in hepatic urea cycle, mitochondrial protein synthesis, and immune cell activation, providing new mechanistic insight into lean MASH.Keywords: Excess dietary salt, Obese MASH, Lean-MASH, Urea cycle, EIF5A hypusination

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly termed non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), is the most prevalent chronic liver disease [1]. It is the leading cause of liver-related morbidity and mortality, accounting for more than 1.6 million deaths each year [2]. It also is commonly associated with obesity and diabetes [[3], [4], [5]]. MASLD starts from simple hepatosteatosis that can develop into progressive hepatic inflammation and fibrosis known as metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), which can further advance to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [[6], [7], [8]].

Although MASLD/MASH is commonly associated with obesity, a subset of patients with MASLD/MASH are non-obese with a body mass index (BMI) below 25 kg/m2 in Western populations or below 23 kg/m2 in Asian populations [9]. These individuals are described as having “lean MASLD/MASH” in contrast to the more common form of MASH associated with obesity (obese MASLD/MASH). Currently, it is estimated the prevalence of lean MASLD/MASH ranges from 1.94% to 13.11% [[10], [11], [12]], depending upon the populations studied. Among lean MASLD patients, 39% have MASH [11]. Compared to obese MASLD/MASH patients, lean MASLD/MASH patients typically have less hepatic steatosis and ballooning degeneration histologically [13], and lower prevalence of diabetes while paradoxically having higher risk of all-cause mortality [14]. Despite the clinical recognition and characterization of the lean MASLD/MASH phenotype, the contributor(s) and molecular mechanism(s) that cause the development and progression of lean MASH are poorly understood.

Epidemiological studies show that Asian countries have a higher prevalence of lean MASLD/MASH compared with other populations [15]. Moreover, Asian countries have higher salt consumption [16]. It is known that excess salt consumption increases the risk of hepatic fibrosis and cirrhosis [17,18], and there is an association between the frequency of salt addition to food and MASLD risk that is stronger in lean vs. obese individuals [17,19]. Therefore, it is intriguing to speculate that increased salt consumption could be an environmental factor contributing to the higher prevalence of lean MASH.

In the current study, we supplemented an obesogenic and MASH-inducing diet, Western diet plus fructose (WDF), with 8% high salt (HS2WDF), and report that mice fed HS2WDF developed hepatic inflammation, fibrosis and mild hepatosteatosis without obesity (lean MASH). We also examined the mechanisms contributing to the mild hepatosteatosis in this lean MASH phenotype. High salt intake increased urea cycle flux and the production of urea cycle intermediates, arginine and ornithine. The latter can be metabolized to spermidine, which serves as the sole substrate for the rare post-translational modification, hypusination, of the protein translation factor EIF5A (EIF5AH). Indeed, we found more hepatic EIF5AH, mitochondrial biosynthesis, and fatty acid β-oxidation in HS2WDF-fed mice than WDF-fed mice. Additionally, single-cell RNA-seq analysis showed that HS2WDF further increased the pro-inflammatory response in hepatic immune cells compared to WDF-fed mice. In summary, high salt supplementation of an obesogenic and MASH-inducing diet generated mice with lean MASH that exhibited distinct metabolic, molecular, and immunological changes from WDF-induced obese MASH. Our findings suggest that high dietary salt could play a contributing and/or pathogenic role in lean MASH patients.

Results

HSWDF causes a lean-MASH-like phenotype

We first examined the effects of long-term high salt intake on the liver under basal conditions by feeding mice with normal chow diet (NCD), NCD containing 4% salt (HSD1), or 8% salt (HSD2) for 16 weeks, a well-established model for excess dietary salt (Fig. S1A). Compared with mice fed with NCD, mice fed the HSD1 and HSD2 showed a trend towards reduced body weight gain (Fig. S1B), with statistically significant changes observed in the HSD2 group. Both HSD1 and HSD2 reduced the increase in lean mass (Fig. S1D). Hepatic triglyceride content (Fig. S1E-F), and portal inflammation (Fig. S1G), were increased in both HSD1 and HSD2, whereas hepatic fibrosis was significantly increased in HSD2 (Fig. S1H-I). These results are consistent with a previous report [20] showing that long-term dietary intake of excess salt induced hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis.

We next investigated the effects of chronic high salt intake in mice fed an obesogenic MASH-inducing diet WDF. We compared a group of mice fed WDF for 16 weeks, a regimen that induces hepatic and metabolic features resembling obese MASH patients [21], with groups fed WDF supplemented with 4% salt (HS1WDF), or 8% salt (HS2WDF) for the same period of time (Figure 1A).

Compared with WDF, both HS1WDF and HS2WDF groups reduced body weight and fat mass, reaching levels comparable to those of control mice fed NCD (Figure 1B–C). The HS2WDF group also had lower lean mass than the WDF or NCD group (Fig. 1D). Glucose tolerance test (GTT) showed that WDF-induced glucose intolerance was ameliorated in the HS1WDF and HS2WDF groups (Fig. S2). Similar findings were reported by Pereira-Silva et al. [22]. This effect may be partially attributed to the normalized body weight observed in the HS1WDF and HS2WDF.

We next evaluated hepatic steatosis, inflammation and fibrosis in mice fed WDF, HS1WDF, and HS2WDF. Liver weight and hepatic triglyceride (TG) content were reduced in both HS1WDF and HS2WDF compared with WDF (Figure 1E–F). However, hepatic TG remained higher in both HS1WDF and HS2WDF groups than in the NCD group (Fig. 1F), with the HS1WDF and HS2WDF groups exhibiting the mild steatosis often observed in lean MASH patients [13]. Notably, histological analysis showed that portal inflammation and fibrosis in the HS1WDF or HS2WDF groups were comparable to those in the WDF group (Figure 1G–J). Taken together, these results showed that addition of high salt to an obesogenic MASH-inducing diet produced a lean-MASH phenotype that exhibited lower hepatic steatosis with comparable levels of inflammation and fibrosis. Thus, mice fed HS1WDF and HS2WDF recapitulated the major physiological and histological features typically observed in lean MASH patients.

No reduction in energy consumption nor absorption in mice fed HS2WDF

To determine whether HS2WDF affected energy consumption that could account for the reduced hepatic steatosis and lean phenotype in mice fed HS2WDF, we monitored their food and water intake, calculated the average daily caloric intake, and found no difference between the WDF and HS2WDF groups (Fig. S3A). To determine whether the presence of salt affected energy absorption, mice were subjected to an overnight fast followed by a 6-hour refeeding regimen [23]. Serum was collected for measurement of very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) levels as a readout of gut absorption of long chain fatty acids.We found that mice fed HS2WDF exhibited higher circulating VLDL levels than mice fed WDF (Fig. S3B), suggesting that the presence of salt did not decrease or rather increased energy absorption.

We next assessed several lipogenic markers to better understand the divergent effects of 8% salt supplementation to NCD or WDF on hepatic steatosis. Compared with NCD, HSD2 increased the protein levels of several lipogenic markers, including fatty acid synthase (FASN), acetyl-CoA carboxylase alpha (ACACA) and stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD1) (Fig. S4A). In contrast, WDF also increased the expression of these proteins (Fig. S4B), whereas HS2WDF had no effect on WDF-induced FASN or ACACA, but reduced SCD1 level (Fig. S4C). Similarly, mRNA expression of Srebf1 (encoding SREBP1c) was increased in HSD2 compared with NCD but decreased in HS2WDF compared with WDF (Fig. S4D). In addition, HS2WDF reduced WDF-induced Cidea expression (Fig. S4D). These results suggest differential effects of salt on hepatic lipogenesis depending on the dietary context, with distinct response observed in the presence or absence of WDF.

Hepatic urea cycle flux is reduced during MASH and improved by high salt

In response to hypertonic conditions, cells upregulate the expression of transporters that imports organic osmolytes [24]. We assessed the expression of several salt-responsive genes [25,26] by qPCR and found that, compared with WDF, HS2WDF increased hepatic mRNA expression of Hspa1b, Slc5a3 and Slc6a6 (Fig. S5). In addition, urea recycling in the kidney and urea production in the liver increase in order to limit natriuresis-driven water loss [27]. Here, in HSD2 vs. NCD, we observed a significant increase in the protein expression of the urea cycle enzyme arginosuccinate lyase (ASL; Fig. S6A). Consistently, hepatic urea content and the urea cycle intermediates ornithine, aspartic acid, and arginosuccinate were increased in the HSD2 group compared with the NCD group (Fig. S6B-C). Hepatic amino acids, which are the main nitrogen sources for urea synthesis, and the common organic osmolytes in the liver [24], were increased in the HSD2 group compared with NCD controls (Fig. S7).

Urea cycle flux and ureagenesis capacity are diminished during MASH due to reduced expression and activity of urea cycle enzymes [28]. In this connection, we found that, compared with mice fed NCD, WDF-fed mice exhibited lower mRNA and protein expression levels of the urea cycle enzymes: Carbamoyl phosphate synthetase 1 (CPS1), arginase 1 (ARG1), and arginosuccinate lyase (ASL) (Figs. S8 and 2A). Consistently, several urea cycle metabolites, including ornithine, citrulline, and arginosuccinate, also were decreased in WDF-fed mice (Fig. 2C).

Given the opposing effects of WDF and high salt on urea cycle activity, we examined whether HS2WDF could improve the impaired urea production and urea cycle activity seen in mice fed WDF. Indeed, HS2WDF increased the protein level of ASL without affecting its mRNA expression (Fig. 2B and S8). Liquid Chromatography–Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS) analysis revealed that HS2WDF increased the urea cycle intermediates that were decreased by WDF, reaching levels equal to or higher than control (Fig. 2C). Hepatic urea concentration (Fig. 2D) and hepatic amino acids (Fig. S7) also were increased in mice fed HS2WDF compared to mice fed WDF. In addition, cellular organic osmolytes, choline and betaine, was increased in HSD2 compared with NCD (Fig. S6D-E) and in HS2WDF compared with WDF (Fig. 2E). Taken together, these findings demonstrated that the decreased hepatic urea cycle flux in mice fed WDF was improved in mice fed HS2WDF.

High salt enhances EIF5A hypusination, mitochondrial protein synthesis, and β-oxidation in MASH

Two urea cycle intermediates, arginine and ornithine, are substrates for the biosynthesis of spermidine [29]. Spermidine also is the sole substrate for a rare post-translational modification of the eukaryotic translation factor EIF5A, known as hypusination (EIF5AH). Catalyzed sequentially by deoxyhypusine synthase (DHPS) and deoxyhypusine hydroxylase (DOHH), EIF5AH is essential for the efficient synthesis of several mitochondrial proteins that regulate mitochondrial activity and β-oxidation capacity [21]. A previous study showed that the DHPS-DOHH-EIF5AH pathway were disrupted in MASH patients and a mouse model of MASH [21]. Here, in mice fed WDF, we found decreased protein expression of the hypusination enzymes DHPS, DOHH and reduced EIF5AH (Figure 3A). Additionally, the protein expression of several mitochondrial proteins that require EIF5AH-dependent translation such as ATP synthase (ATP5A), the subunits of electron transfer chain enzymes (SDHB, NDUFB8), and cytochrome C (CYCS) were reduced (Fig. 3B).

On the other hand, mice fed HS2WDF showed increased EIF5AH level (Fig. 3C) and enhanced biosynthesis of mitochondrial proteins regulated by EIF5AH (Fig. 3D). Additionally, HS2WDF increased the protein expression of the hypusination enzyme, DHPS (Fig. 3C), without affecting its mRNA expression (Fig. S9). Under basal conditions, EIF5AH also was elevated in mice fed HSD2 compared to mice fed NCD (Fig. S10A), with enhanced expression of the mitochondrial protein, cytochrome C (CYCS) (Fig. S10B). Collectively, these data support the notion that high salt increased hypusination of EIF5A and the biosynthesis of a subset of mitochondrial proteins that require EIF5AH-mediated translation.

We also observed increased hepatic peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) expression in HS2WDF compared with WDF (Fig. S4C), along with reduced expression of several lipogenic markers (Fig. S4), suggesting a possible shift toward enhanced lipid oxidation. We next investigated whether these changes were associated with increased mitochondrial β-oxidation of fatty acids, which may contribute to the reduced hepatosteatosis observed in mice fed HS2WDF vs. WDF. Accordingly, we performed acylcarnitine profiling and observed increases in short-chain and medium chain acylcarnitines (SCACs and MCACs), suggesting mice fed HS2WDF had increased and mitochondrial β-oxidation (Fig. 3E). Long-chain and very long-chain acylcarnitines (LCACs and VLCACs) also were increased, suggesting there was increased mitochondrial import of fatty acids. Thus, mice fed HS2WDF had increased β-oxidation of fatty acids. The increase in fatty acids utilization in the liver may also contribute to the lean phenotype observed in HS2WDF mice.

HSWDF enhances pro-inflammatory response during MASH

Lipotoxicity from excess fat accumulation in the liver plays a key role in inflammation and fibrosis during MASH [30]. However, we observed a similar or greater amount of inflammation/fibrosis in mice fed HS2WDF vs. WDF, even though the former had less hepatosteatosis. Consistently, stress marker DNA-damage inducible transcript 3 (DDIT3, also known as CHOP) was increased in WDF vs. NCD (Fig. S11A), and further increased in HS2WDF compared with WDF. In addition, we also observed increased mRNA expression of nuclear respiratory factor 1 (Nrf1) and antioxidant enzymes glutathione reductase (Gsr), glutathione peroxidase 1 (Gpx1), and superoxide dismutase 2 (Sod2) in HS2WDF compared with WDF (Fig. S11B), suggesting a possible adaptive antioxidant response in the HS2WDF group.

Several reports have demonstrated that salt exposure can induce the pro-inflammatory status of monocytes, macrophage, and T cells in a cell-autonomous manner [[31], [32], [33], [34]]. Accordingly, we investigated whether chronic high salt intake modulated the inflammatory responses in the liver in lean MASLD/MASH vs. obese MASLD/MASH. We examined the hepatic immune profiles by performing single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on fresh liver tissues of NCD, HSD2, WDF and HS2WDF mice. Since hepatocytes constitute up to 80% of total cell population in the liver [35], we enriched immune cells by a brief centrifugation before sequencing (see Methods). After applying quality control measures (see Methods), we obtained 82,164 cells in total from four conditions. We then annotated their cell identities by using the pre-trained cell type classifier, CellTypist [36], and further refined their classification manually based upon their expression of cell type-specific markers (Fig. 4A).

We then examined four key immune cell populations previously shown to modulate and exacerbate the disease progression of MASH: monocytes and monocyte-derived cells (Mo/MoMCs) [30,37], Kupffer cells (KCs), B cells, and T cells. We observed an elevated pro-inflammatory response in all four immune cell populations in livers from mice fed HS2WDF vs. WDF (Fig. 4B–C and Fig. S12A-C), and WDF vs. NCD (Fig. S13). These findings indicated that HS2WDF further enhanced the pro-inflammatory response induced by WDF in these cell populations.

Consistent with this notion that high salt intake per se can enhance inflammatory pathways independent of lipotoxicity, we also observed pro-inflammatory changes in immune profiles and gene expression of hepatic immune cells in HSD2 vs. NCD (Fig. S14).

To better understand the salt-induced immune cell activation, we next analyzed scRNA-seq data by performing CellChat analysis [38]. CellChat applies network analysis and pattern recognition approaches to predict major signaling inputs and outputs of cells and how these cells and signals coordinate for functions. Compared with NCD, WDF increased the signaling outputs of neutrophils, NK cells, KCs, and Mo/MoMCs. Among these, HS2WDF further increased the signaling outputs of KCs and Mo/MoMCs (Fig. 4D). These results suggest that KCs and Mo/MoMCs may be the major cell types mediating pro-inflammatory signaling in response to HS2WDF.

We next treated RAW264.7 macrophages with NaCl for 48 h. Treatment with 40 mM NaCl increased the expression of the osmolyte sensor nuclear factor of activated T cells (Nfat5) and its target genes, Hspa1b, Slc6a6 and Slc5a3 (Fig. S15A), and was associated with increased expression of inflammatory (Tnf, Il1b, Ccl3, Ccl5) and fibrotic genes (Tgfb1, Col4a1, Col4a2, Fig. S15B-C). Furthermore, co-treatment with both NaCl and palmitic acid (PA) for 24 h induced a stronger inflammatory and fibrotic response than either NaCl or PA alone (Fig. 4E). These results demonstrate that NaCl exposure induces osmotic stress as well as inflammatory and fibrotic response in macrophages, which can be further amplified under lipotoxic conditions.

Discussion

Approximately 19% of MASLD/MASH patients are lean [10]. The lean patients with MASLD/MASH have a lower risk for type 2 diabetes, dyslipidemia, and cardiovascular disease (CVD) [10,14], but have greater risks of all-cause mortality and liver-related events. These clinical findings highlight the need for tailored treatment strategies in patients with lean vs. obese MASLD/MASH. Currently, the mechanism(s) underlying MASLD/MASH development in the absence of obesity remains largely unknown. The potential environmental or lifestyle factors contributing to development of this subtype remain unidentified, and the molecular pathogenic mechanism(s) differentiating lean MASLD/MASH from obese MASLD/MASH is obscure. These knowledge gaps pose serious challenges for the clinical management of lean MASLD/MASH.

Emerging epidemiological evidence suggests that high salt intake is an independent risk factor for MASLD and advanced liver fibrosis [19,39]. Among people with existing MASLD, frequent salt addition conferred a 3-fold higher risk of advanced fibrosis [19,39]. However, molecular studies of the effects of excess salt intake on hepatic metabolism and MASLD/MASH progression are very limited. In this study, we showed that while mice fed WDF induced obesity and MASH, high salt supplementation of WDF surprisingly mimicked the main physiological and histological features found in lean MASH patients (Fig. 1). Specifically, mice fed HS2WDF had reduced body weight and fat mass that approached those in mice fed NCD (Fig. 1B–D). They also exhibited mild hepatosteatosis, yet still had inflammation and fibrosis comparable to mice fed WDF (Fig. 1F-J). Several metabolic defects that were observed in mice fed WDF, including decreased urea cycle activity, EIF5AH and biosynthesis of mitochondrial proteins, were restored by HS2WDF, associated with increased β-oxidation of fatty acids and reduced hepatosteatosis (Figure 2, Figure 3). Additionally, HS2WDF increased pro-inflammatory responses in the immune cells of the liver (Fig. 4A–C) which could account for the heightened hepatic inflammation and fibrosis observed in mice fed HS2WDF vs. WDF. Of note, the current study used only male mice. As female mice generally exhibit delayed MASH progression [40] and may have estrogen-mediated protection against salt-induced immune activation [41], investigating the response of female mice to high salt supplementation with or without WDF warrants future study.

The mechanism(s) by which high salt prevented obesity in mice fed WDF remain unclear. However, Frieler et al. also observed that supplementation of excess salt to a high fat diet (HFD) for 8 weeks completely reversed HFD-induced obesity [42]. Kitada et al. examined the systemic effects of excess salt intake for 4 weeks on energy metabolism, and showed that excess salt consumption promoted muscle protein breakdown, selective increases in hepatic amino acid species, and energy expenditure that were part of the osmolyte-sensing and energy-demanding water conservation response involving the kidney, muscle, and liver [27]. In our lean MASH mouse model, we observed reduction in lean mass (Fig. 1D) and elevation of hepatic amino acid levels (Fig. S7). These results suggested that protein catabolism was similarly upregulated in our model.

It is possible that the metabolic and physiological responses to conserve water in the face of a high salt load may have beneficial effects on reducing obesity and hepatosteatosis. In the liver, salt increases production of organic osmolytes such as urea, and enhances lipid oxidation to meet the increased energy demand for water/salt regulation [27]. In this connection, we observed enhanced expression of salt-responsive genes (Fig. S5), increased production of hepatic urea and urea cycle metabolites, and the organic osmolyte, choline and betaine [24], in response to high salt (Fig. 2C–F). Choline also is important for regulating hepatic lipid metabolism and preventing steatosis by supporting phosphatidylcholine synthesis and VLDL assembly [43]. Its hepatic levels decreased in MASH but increased in mice fed HS2WDF, suggesting its production due to osmotic stress may be an additional mechanism by which high salt attenuates hepatosteatosis. HS2WDF also improved EIF5AH and mitochondrial protein biogenesis which are decreased in MASH [21]. These changes, in turn, may contribute to increased mitochondrial β-oxidation of fatty acids and decreased hepatosteatosis in mice fed HS2WDF compared to mice fed WDF (Figure 2, Figure 3). The observed increase in EIF5AH could be due to elevated levels of urea cycle metabolites, arginine and ornithine (Fig. 2C), which serve as precursors for spermidine which is required for hypusination of EIF5A. HS2WDF also increased protein expression of the hypusination enzyme, DHPS (Fig. 3C), which could be another reason for increased hepatic EIF5AH in mice fed HSWDF.

Osmotic-sensing-related mechanisms have been proposed as salt-induced pro-inflammatory response in T cells [32,34]. Other studies also demonstrated cultured human monocytes or Treg cells exposed to elevated NaCl concentrations triggered pathogenic changes in these cells [33,44], indicating that high salt exposure can perturb immune cell function in a cell-autonomous manner. We believe this mechanism also applies to the HS2WDF vs. WDF groups as HS2WDF-fed mice exhibited enhanced immune response especially in Mo/MoMCs, KCs, B cells and T cells (Fig 4 and Fig. S12) despite a lower degree of steatosis. Increased pro-inflammatory response was also observed in Mo/MoMCs, KCs, B cells in HSD2-fed mice compared with NCD-fed mice (Fig. S14). Salt treatment of RAW264.7 macrophages increased the expression of hypertonic stress-responsive genes and was associated with elevated expression of inflammatory and fibrotic genes (Fig. S15), with even greater induction observed following co-treatment with NaCl and PA (Fig. 4E). These findings support the notion that, in addition to lipotoxicity [30], chronic high salt intake itself can trigger pro-inflammatory responses in hepatic immune cells, which may be further exacerbated under lipotoxic conditions during MASH.

In summary, our study showed that high salt intake in combination with an obesogenic and MASH-inducing diet produced a novel model of lean MASH and suggested that high salt intake could be an environmental factor contributing to lean MASH. However, the causal role of high salt intake on lean MASH in man still needs to be verified in patients with this condition. The global average salt intake of adults is 10.78 g/day, which is more than double the recommended level (less than 5 g/day) [45]. In countries with high salt intake and increased prevalence of lean MASH, such as China and South Korea, salt consumption reaches 17.7 g/day and 12.3 g/day respectively [45]. Thus, our findings provide new insights on the role of high salt intake on lean MASH phenotype, and if applicable to man, could be a potential prognostic factor for MASH in lean individuals. Furthermore, salt restriction may be a readily modifiable risk factor to reduce the incidence of lean MASH in vulnerable populations.

Methods

Animal models

All animal experiments were performed following the principles and procedures of the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the SingHealth Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC; 2020/SHS/1549; 2024/SHS/1859). All experimental procedures were conducted in compliance with the relevant institutional and national regulatory requirements.

Animals were housed at 21–24 °C with 40–70% humidity under a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle, with food and water available ad libitum.

Six dietary conditions were included in the studies: (1) NCD group, mice were fed a normal chow diet (Specialty Feeds, SF00-100) and supplied with tap water; (2) HSD1 group, mice were fed a NCD containing 4% salt (D05011707, Research Diets Inc.) and supplied with tap water; (3) HSD2 group, mice were fed a normal chow diet containing 8% salt (D05011703, Research Diets Inc.) and supplied with tap water; (4) WDF group, mice were fed RD Western Diet (D12079B, Research Diets Inc.) and supplied with 15% fructose in drinking water; (5) HS1WDF group, mice were fed with RD Western Diet containing 4% salt (D06111702, Research Diets Inc.) and supplied with 15% fructose in drinking water; (6) HS2WDF group, mice were fed with RD Western Diet containing 8% salt (D06111701, Research Diets Inc.) and supplied with 15% fructose in drinking water.

Male C57BL/6 J mice (8–10-week-old) were fed the NCD (n = 5), HSD1 (n = 6), HSD2 (n = 5), WDF (n = 6), HS1WDF (n = 7), or HS2WDF (n = 7) diets for 16 weeks. The animal work was repeated with NCD (n = 8), HSD2 (n = 8), WDF (n = 8), or HS2WDF (n = 8). Food was supplied in special containers to allow the measurements of food weight before and after each top up. Food intake was recorded every 2–4 days. Water intake was measured by recording the volume before and after every 2–4 days. Body weight was monitored every 2–4 days. MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) was performed every 2 weeks to assess lean and fat mass. Mice were fasted for 6 h before dissection. During dissection, blood and liver tissues were collected. To assess glucose tolerance, mice were first fasted for 6 h. A 20% (w/v) glucose solution was injected intraperitoneally at a dose of 2 g glucose per kg of lean mass. Blood glucose levels were measured at baseline, and at 15, 30, 45, 60 and 90 min after glucose injection.

To investigate nutrient absorption, mice (after 15 weeks on diet) were subjected to overnight fasting followed by 6 h of refeeding as previous studies have demonstrated that digestion and nutrient absorption were completed within 6 h [23]. Blood samples were collected through tail vein.

Biochemical assays

Triglyceride concentration in liver homogenate was quantified with the Triglyceride Colorimetric Assay Kits (10,010,303; Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, MI). Hepatic total hydroxyproline was determined using the Quickzyme Total Collagen assay kit (Quickzyme Biosciences, Leiden, The Netherlands). Mouse hepatic levels of urea were measured using Urea Assay Kit (MAK006; Sigma–Aldrich). All assays were performed according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Histology

Freshly harvested liver tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 48 h. The fixed tissues were then dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) or Masson's Trichrome. Portal inflammation was graded using the Brunt scoring system, on a scale of 0–3, based on the quantity of inflammatory cells per 20x fields. The degree of portal inflammation was assigned as follows: 0 (none or minimal), 1 (mild), 2 (moderate), and 3 (marked).

Cell culture

The murine RAW264.7 macrophage cell line was maintained at 37 °C in Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

For the NaCl does-response experiment, RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells/well and allowed to adhere for 24 h before treatment. Cells were then exposed to an additional 0, 10, 20, or 40 mM NaCl for 48 h prior to harvest. For co-treatment with NaCl and palmitic acid (PA), cells were treated with 40 mM NaCl, or 0.2 mM PA conjugated with 0.5% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA), or both for 24 h prior to harvest.

Metabolomics profiling of acylcarnitines and urea cycle metabolites in the liver

Liver extracts were analyzed semi-quantitatively for urea cycle metabolites and quantitatively for acylcarnitine species by the Metabolomics Core Facility of Duke-NUS Medical School using established mass spectrometry (MS)-based methods [[46], [47], [48]]. Liver tissues were homogenized in 50% acetonitrile with 0.3% formic acid.

For urea cycle metabolites measurement, 30 μl of the homogenates were dried and derivatized with phenyl isothiocyanate and then reconstituted in 5 mM ammonium acetate in methanol. After centrifugation to remove protein, samples were diluted with water and analyzed by LC-MS on a Waters Acuity UPLC BEH C18 column (1.7um, 2.1 × 50 mm) with a Waters Xevo TQ-XS mass spectrometer. Separation employed 0.2% formic acid in water as mobile phase A and in acetonitrile as mobile phase B. The LC gradient proceeded from 5% B to 12% B (1.5min), 17.5% B (2.7 min), 50% B (4min), 100% B (4.5–5.0 min), and then returned to 5% B (5.1–5.8 min). Flow rate was 0.8 ml/min except 1.0 mL/min briefly during 4.7–5.1 min. The column temperature was 50 °C and the injection volume was 5 μL. Positive-mode electrospray ionization was employed for all measurements. LC-MS data were processed with Waters TargetLynx software v4.2. Results were reported as peak-area ratios (analyte area/internal standard area) in arbitrary units (AU). Analyses included only peaks with signal-to-noise (S/N) > 10 (peaks are above the limit of quantitation) were carried forward for further analysis.

For acylcarnitine species, 50 μl of tissue homogenates were subjected to methanol extraction. The extract was derivatized with 3 M hydrochloric acid in methanol, dried, and reconstituted in methanol for LC-MS analysis. Acylcarnitine species were quantified by flow injection–tandem mass spectrometry on the Agilent 6430 Triple Quadrupole LC/MS system (Agilent Technologies, CA, United States). The injections used 2 μL of sample with a mobile phase of 80:20 methanol: water at a flow rate of 0.4 ml/min. Data was collected and analyzed with Agilent MassHunter Workstation B.06.00 software. All species were calibrated from 125 to 5000 nM, except C2 carnitine, which is calibrated from 1250 to 10000 nM. According to ICH guidelines, the quantitation limit is typically indicated by a signal to noise ratio (S/N ratio) of 10:1. Only signals with S/N ≥ 10 were used for downstream analysis.

RNA purification and RT-PCR

InviTrap Spin Universal RNA mini kit (Invitek Diagnostics, 1060100300) was used to isolate total cellular RNA. RNA concentration was assessed by Nanodrop ND-1000 spectrophotometer. 1 μg of RNA was reverse transcribed using the iSCRIPT cDNA synthesis kit (Bio-Rad, 1708890). cDNA was quantified by real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) on the Applied Biosystems HT-7900, with SDS software 2.4, version 2.4.1. PCR was performed using QuantiFast SYBR Green PCR Kit (Qiagen). All experiments were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions.

Western blot analysis

Proteins were separated by SDS–PAGE under reducing conditions and then transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. The membranes were blocked for 1 h with 5% nonfat milk in phosphate-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (Sigma–Aldrich, P9416; PBST) at room temperature. Primary antibodies were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Immunoblotting was conducted using an enhanced chemiluminescence procedure (GE Healthcare, RPN2106). Western blot images were captured with the ChemiDoc™MP system (Bio-Rad), Image Lab version 6.1.0. Densitometry was performed using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health).

The following antibodies were used: anti-ACTB (JLA20), 1:3000; anti-CPS1 (MA5-35463, clone ARC0929, Invitrogen), 1:1000; anti-ARG1 (#93668 S, Cell Signaling Technology), 1:1000; anti-ASL (PA5-22300, Invitrogen), 1:1000; anti-DHPS (NBP1-82648, Novus Biologicals), 1:1000; anti-DOHH (HPA041953, Sigma–Aldrich), 1:1000; anti-EIF5A (611976, BD Biosciences), 1:1000; anti-Hypusine (ABS1064, Millipore), 1:1000; anti-Cytochrome c (136F3, #4280, Cell Signaling Technology), 1:1000; total OXPHOS Rodent WB Antibody Cocktail (anti-Atp5a/Sdhb/Ndufb8, ab110413, Abcam), 1:1000; anti-FASN (#3180, Cell Signaling Technology), 1:1000; anti-ACACA (#3662, Cell Signaling Technology), 1:1000; anti-SCD1 (#2438, Cell Signaling Technology), 1:1000; anti-PPARα (sc-9000, Santa Cruz), 1:1000 Primary antibodies were diluted in PBST containing 1% BSA.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

The single-cell isolation, capture, and processing were performed by Singleron Biotechnologies. Briefly, freshly harvested liver tissues from mice fed a normal chow diet (NCD), high-salt (8% NaCl; HSD2), Western diet supplemented with 15% fructose water (WDF), or WDF with 8% high salt (HS2WDF) were cut into small pieces in sCelLiVE™ Tissue Preservation Buffer. Tissue samples were dissociated into single-cell suspensions using sCelLiVE™ Tissue Dissociation Buffer according to the manufacturer's protocol, followed by a 1-min spin at 100×g to enrich immune cells [49]. Cell concentration and viability were measured to evaluate sample quality. Samples from NCD, HSD2, WDF and HS2WDF groups were then subjected to single-cell capture and processed for GEXSCOPE Single Cell RNA Library Construction-SD. Sequencing was performed on Illumina NovaSeq platform (Singleron Biotechnologies).

Single-cell RNA sequencing data processing

Seurat 96 (V5 and R version 4.4.0) was applied to the scRNA data for downstream analysis of matrix files. First, cells within the range of 200 to 12,000 features detected were kept for analysis. Then, cells with more than 20% mitochondrial reads were excluded. After log-transformation, 5000 highly variable genes were identified, followed by dimensionality reduction (PCA) and UMAP embeddings. Cell identities were determined by CellTypist [36] and refined manually by expressions of cell-type specific markers: Kupffer cell (Marco, Spic and Clec4f), monocytes and monocytes derived macrophages (Wfdc17, Adgre1 (F4/80) and C1qc), T cells (Cd3d, Cd3g and Cd3e), B cells (Ebf1, Ms4a1 and Cd79a).

Differential expression analysis and gene set enrichment analysis

Differential expression analysis was carried out using the Wilcoxon rank sum test (Seurat). Genes were ranked by a combination of logFC and p-value. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed on top 500 differentially expressed genes by clusterProfiler (v.4.0.5) using MSigDB as the database, to identify biologically relevant pathways and functional gene modules enriched in specific cell clusters or experimental conditions within our scRNA-seq dataset. All genes in the genome were used as the enrichment background. Terms with a p-adjusted value < 0.00001, and log2FC > 0.5 were collected for all analysis, except for comparisons involving HSD2 vs. NCD B cells and WDF vs. NCD B cells, where a log2FC fold change threshold of >0.25 was applied.

Cell–cell communication analysis using CellChat

Cell–cell communication was inferred using CellChat (v1. x) in R. The processed Seurat object was converted to a CellChat object, and the default CellChat pipeline was applied with the built-in mouse curated ligand–receptor interaction database to compute communication probabilities between all pairwise cell group combinations, followed by pathway-level aggregation and network centrality scoring.

Signaling role scatter plots were generated using the “netAnalysis_signalingRole_scatter” function in CellChat, which projects each cell group into a two-dimensional space defined by outgoing interaction strength (sum of outgoing communication probabilities) on the x-axis and incoming interaction strength (sum of incoming communication probabilities) on the y-axis. Dot size is proportional to the total number of inferred ligand–receptor interactions associated with each cell group, and dot color encodes cell identity. Analyses and visualizations were performed independently for each dietary condition using identical pipeline parameters and database versions to enable direct cross-group comparisons [38,50].

Statistical analysis

Grubb's test provided by the GraphPad Prism website (https://www.graphpad.com/quickcalcs/Grubbs1.cfm) was used to determine outlier in each dataset. If the z-score was above the critical value of z - 0.05, we assumed the data point was an outlier. Shapiro–Wilk normality test was used to evaluate normal distribution after removing outliers in our data. When the Shapiro–Wilk Test yielded a P-value >0.05, the data were considered normally distributed and either parametric two-tailed Student's t-test or, as appropriate, One-Way ANOVA followed by Fisher's least significant difference (LSD) test was applied. For dataset that did not meet the normality assumptions (Shapiro–Wilk Test P < 0.05), non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test or Kruskal–Wallis tests were used. All statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0.1.
