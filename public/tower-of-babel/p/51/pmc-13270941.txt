Simple programming, significant impact: Pacemaker Sleep Function extends device longevity
Authors: Kazuma Chikura, Daisuke Kawano, Hiroshi Taniguchi, Takayuki Miyake, Masami Sakurada, Hitoshi Mori
Published: 2026

Key Teaching Points •Sleep-specific rate reduction features, such as Medtronic's Sleep Function, are designed to temporarily lower the programmed lower rate limit during sleeping hours. This adjustment allows the pacing rate to more closely follow the physiological circadian rhythm, reducing unnecessary pacing activity during rest periods.•Activation of these features can significantly decrease the atrial pacing burden in patients with pacemakers. In this case, the reduced pacing burden was associated with an estimated one-year prolongation of battery longevity, achieved without any adverse effects such as nocturnal bradycardia.•Sleep-specific rate reduction features represent a simple and non-invasive strategy to optimize device management in patients with stable sleep patterns, including many elderly individuals. They may be especially useful when conventional approaches, such as AV interval optimization or voltage adjustments, fail to effectively preserve battery life.•Each manufacturer offers unique algorithms for implementing sleep rate reduction (e.g., Medtronic's Sleep Function, Biotronik's Night Rate, and Abbott's Rest Rate). Increased awareness and education among clinicians, device clinic staff and allied professionals regarding these features may help optimize pacing strategies during routine ambulatory follow-up.

Introduction

Cardiac pacemakers (PMs) are widely used in patients with bradyarrhythmias to maintain an adequate heart rate and improve quality of life.1 With increasing life expectancy of patients, battery longevity has become an important clinical concern across all age groups, with particular relevance in elderly patients at a higher risk of device replacement–related complications.2, 3, 4 Excessive pacing is a well-recognized factor to accelerate battery depletion.5 Strategies, such as optimization of pacing parameters (eg, voltage, pulse width, and pacing rate) and adjustment of pacing modes and atrioventricular (AV) interval, are commonly performed to suppress excessive pacing, thereby prolonging battery longevity.6 Sleep-specific rate reduction features, which temporarily lower the programmed lower rate limit (LRL) during sleep periods, could be another strategy to prolong battery longevity. However, although previous studies have reported physiological benefits, such as improvements in nocturnal blood pressure dipping patterns,7 there have been few reports demonstrating an actual extension of device battery longevity. Here, we present the case of a 90-year-old man in whom activation of the sleep-specific rate reduction features (Sleep Function, Medtronic Inc, Minneapolis, MN) significantly reduced atrial pacing burden, resulting in an estimated 1-year extension of the PM battery longevity.

Case report

A 90-year-old man underwent a dual-chamber PM implantation for advanced AV block in December of 2018 (device generator: Azure XT DR MRI [Medtronic], atrial screw-in lead; SelectSecure 3830 [Medtronic], ventricular screw-in lead; SelectSecure 3830 [Medtronic]). The PM was initially programmed to the DDD mode with a lower/upper rate of 70/130 beats per minute (bpm). The patient was followed in the PM clinic every 6 months. The atrial pacing threshold remained relatively high (2.25–2.75 V) at 0.4 ms (pulse width) from the initial postimplantation check. In addition, full ventricular pacing became necessary, possibly owing to the progression of AV block. Although the ventricular pacing threshold remained low (0.75–1.125 V at 0.4 ms pulse width) and optimization of the AV interval was attempted, the percentage of ventricular pacing did not decrease (Figure 1A). Consequently, battery depletion was noted to be slightly accelerated (Figure 1A). In June 2022, the atrial pacing threshold had risen to 3.5 V at 0.4 ms (battery longevity 3.9 years; atrial pacing percentage 84.5%). To reduce the atrial pacing percentage, the LRL was also changed from 70 to 60 bpm. However, in December 2022, despite a reduction in the atrial pacing percentage, the decline in battery longevity could not be suppressed (battery longevity 2.5 years; atrial pacing 61.3%). Analysis of the device-stored heart rate trend revealed a higher atrial pacing burden during nighttime (sleep periods) than daytime, with the nocturnal heart rate remaining almost constant at the programmed LRL (Figure 1B). Focused on this trend, Sleep Function was introduced to suppress nocturnal atrial pacing, with the LRL lowered from 60 to 50 bpm between 11 PM and 6:30 AM. In June 2023, atrial pacing had decreased to 43.3%, and the estimated battery longevity improved to 2.9 years, representing an effective prolongation of approximately 1 year (Figure 1A). Intrinsic atrial activity was observed in the analysis of the heart rate trend (Figure 1B). No episodes of nocturnal bradycardia (less than 40 bpm) were observed. After the introduction of the Sleep Function, the patient reported no degradation in sleep quality or other health-related symptoms. In addition, no adverse physiological or clinical changes were observed during follow-up. Discussion

Several manufacturers have developed sleep-specific rate reduction features: Sleep Function (Medtronic), Night Rate (Biotronik), and Rest Rate (Abbott) (Table 1). Implantable cardioverter-defibrillators from different manufacturers also incorporate similar sleep-specific rate reduction features. Although minor technical differences exist among them, their common goal is to lower the programmed LRL during sleep periods, allowing the pacing rate to better align with the physiological circadian rhythm and thereby suppress unnecessary pacing. Although the potential for these features to reduce an excessive pacing burden and extend the battery longevity has been repeatedly emphasized in technical documents and theoretical analyses,8 clinical reports directly confirming this effect remain limited. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to demonstrate a measurable prolongation of battery longevity associated with the application of the sleep-specific rate reduction feature (Sleep Function). Sleep-specific rate reduction features are turned off by default; therefore, clinicians should be aware of the technical differences among manufacturers when applying these features to individual patients. Sleep Function (Medtronic) and Night Rate (Biotronik) require manual programming of bedtime and wake time to define the sleep period during which the LRL is lowered, whereas Rest Rate (Abbott) uses a rate-response sensor to detect reductions in respiratory and cardiac activity, thereby automatically identifying sleep periods and adjusting the pacing rate to the programmed LRL. Therefore, for the Sleep Function (Medtronic) and Night Rate (Biotronik), a detailed assessment of the patient’s daily sleep and wake schedule is necessary. These 2 features may be particularly suitable for patients with stable sleep patterns. In addition, when patients travel across time zones, the programmed sleep period should be adjusted accordingly, or the function should be turned off. Moreover, careful patient selection is essential, given that the inappropriate application may cause symptomatic nocturnal bradycardia in rare instances.9,10 In our patient, stable sleep patterns and the absence of nocturnal bradycardia made this approach feasible and effective. This experience supports the consideration of sleep-specific rate reduction features when conventional strategies fail to improve battery longevity. In particular, elderly patients with stable and predictable sleep patterns may represent a subgroup in whom the clinical impact of battery preservation is particularly meaningful, given the higher procedural risks associated with generator replacement. In addition to their potential impact on pacing burden and battery longevity, sleep-specific rate reduction features have important educational implications for daily device management. These features are turned off by default in most PM systems and may therefore be overlooked during routine follow-up, particularly in busy ambulatory device clinics. Conclusion

Sleep-specific rate reduction features such as Sleep Function can be a simple and effective strategy to reduce excessive pacing and extend battery longevity, particularly in carefully selected patients, including elderly individuals with predictable sleep patterns. In our case, this strategy reduced atrial pacing burden and extended estimated PM battery longevity by approximately 1 year. Wider use of such features could improve patient management and possibly reduce the need for device replacement, especially in high-risk populations. Prospective studies would be needed to further evaluate the safety, efficacy, and long-term battery impact of early activation of sleep-specific rate reduction features.
