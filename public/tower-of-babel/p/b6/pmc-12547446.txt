A framework for governing Canada's tobacco settlement funds
Authors: Daniel Eisenkraft Klein
Published: 2025

Canada's $32.5 billion tobacco settlement, the largest legal resolution in the country's history, has entered its implementation phase. The settlement allocates $24 billion to provinces, $2.5 billion to compensate individual smokers, and $1 billion to a research foundation.1 Payments began in August 2025 and will continue for the next 18 years.

While previous settlements over corporate health harms have often yielded substantial financial recoveries, they have consistently failed to advance public health objectives. The 1990s tobacco Master Settlement Agreement (MSA)—a $206 billion payout to 46 states over 25 years—set the first major precedent for monetizing and distributing public health harms, yet offered no framework to ensure fair allocation of funds. American states currently spend less than 3 cents of every dollar on tobacco control.2 More recently, while American cities and states have received $57 billion in opioid settlement funds, spending has seldom been transparent, resources have often been directed toward law enforcement rather than public health, and fewer than one in seven dollars is overseen by boards that include people with lived experience of opioid use.3

Tobacco kills 48,000 Canadians annually and remains the leading preventable cause of premature death in the country.4 The Canadian settlement funds represent a distinctive policy opportunity: a large, time-limited resource influx directed at a persistent crisis. Yet the settlement currently lacks the adequate governance structures, transparency mechanisms, and tobacco control measures to ensure that funding actually advances public health objectives. Building on Whaley and Sharfstein's framework for evaluating opioid settlement spending,5 we propose a framework to guide whether the tobacco settlement will achieves meaningful health impacts.

Who decides?

Provincial governments hold complete decision-making authority over settlement spending. Unlike other settlements that include mechanisms for local distribution or independent oversight, the Canadian agreement provides no such structures. Control is therefore concentrated within provincial cabinets, health ministers, and finance ministers.

This concentration of authority makes broad stakeholder engagement crucial. Policymakers must prioritize current smokers as the primary beneficiaries while also engaging other affected groups: former smokers, healthcare professionals, individuals harmed by tobacco but ineligible for compensation, family members, caregivers, and disproportionately affected communities. Provinces can have flexibility in determining the ultimate authority of settlement oversight bodies, which could range from advisory groups to decision-making boards. While governments need not follow these recommendations, requiring formal responses would strengthen accountability.

How transparent?

The settlement does not presently require any public disclosures about how provinces will spend tobacco settlement dollars. Unlike settlements that create dedicated tracking systems, Canadian provinces have indicated they will direct funds into general revenue without earmarking. As of September 2025, no province has designated a section of government websites for settlement-related materials or created dashboards to track spending.

Provinces should establish formal mechanisms to publicly report 100% of expenditures, including—and especially—when funds are directed to general provincial budgets rather than dedicated tobacco control expenditures. Governments should make these reports publicly available, easily accessible, and stratified by types of spending. There are precedents to build from—in Rhode Island, opioid settlement funding reporting includes public meetings, published minutes, and funding plans.5 These transparency measures would encourage trust at a moment when Canadian public confidence in public health and government has reached extraordinarily low levels.6

What will be funded?

Current provincial spending strategies vary dramatically without coordination. Manitoba has pledged “every single dollar” to cancer care and smoking prevention.7 Alberta has taken a different approach entirely, directing its first payment of $713 million into a savings fund rather than immediate health programs.8 Quebec and Ontario—the provinces receiving the greatest share of the settlement—have made no specific commitments, suggesting funds will support general healthcare or deficit reduction.

Jurisdictions should integrate spending into a broader strategy that considers specific provincial health needs and evidence-based tobacco control measures. For instance, a province with high adult smoking rates but low youth uptake could prioritize cessation programs over prevention campaigns. Equally important, provinces must ensure settlement funds target tobacco-related harm rather than unrelated purposes, as occurred in the US where states diverted tobacco settlement funds to non-health priorities.

How will progress be measured?

Currently no province has announced evaluation frameworks or outcome measurement systems for tobacco settlement spending. This leaves provinces unable to measure impact or even define what would constitute success in the use of settlement funds. Moreover, since the funds will be dispersed over 18 years, establishing outcome measures could assist in redirection of funding over time. Metrics should capture both intervention reach (e.g. cessation program enrollment) and population-level impact (e.g. smoking prevalence and tobacco-related disease rates).

Provinces can evaluate their plans against these four questions now, while implementation approaches remain flexible. If provincial plans are not clear on decision-making power, transparency, funding investments, and oversight, they are unlikely to translate settlement funds into population health improvements.
