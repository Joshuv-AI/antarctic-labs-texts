Development and acceptability of gist-based decision aids for prostate and breast cancer screening: a two-phase qualitative interview study in an online setting
Authors: Sunny Nalavenkata, Oskar Bergengren, Kathleen Lynch, Nicholas Emard, Mia Austria, Sené Martin, Gabriel Ogbennaya, Khadra Dualeh, Kristina Stevanovic, Manish I Patel, Jason Gonsky, Andrew Vickers, A Fagerlin, Jada G Hamilton, Jennifer L Hay, Sigrid Carlsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditional approaches to designing decision aids have focused on providing completeness of information and quantitative detail. An alternative approach based on psychological research emphasises understanding the essence or ‘gist’ of the decision. Few gist-based decision aids exist. The objective of this study was to develop novel gist-based decision aids for prostate and breast cancer screening and evaluate their acceptability.MethodsMen aged 40–60 years and women aged 40–49 years eligible for prostate or breast cancer screening were recruited from ResearchMatch (National Institutes of Health) and a public hospital located in New York City. Three rounds of semistructured interviews were performed to determine self-reported comprehensibility and acceptability, with iterative modifications after each phase, including: Phase 1: initial feedback; Phase 2, Round 1: evaluating our modifications; and Phase 2, Round 2: confirming final content.ResultsA total of 80 participants were involved in the two-phase qualitative interview process; Phase 1 and Phase 2 included 32 and 48 participants, respectively. Racial distribution was 50% white, 30% black and 10% Asian; 9% of participants were Hispanic or Latino. Most participants were highly educated. Interview themes established content validity of the final decision aids. In a quantitative analysis of the final decision aids using a questionnaire with fixed response options, acceptability was high, with two-thirds finding the length and amount of information of the decision aid to be optimal. The majority found the decision aid visually appealing, easy to read and easy to get through, and indicated that it held their interest and did not require much mental effort to read. One-quarter said the tool made them feel somewhat nervous, and three-quarters not at all. Most participants could understand and relate to the images, graphs and patient stories in the decision aid. After Phase 2, 45 of the 48 participants (94%) said they would find the decision aid helpful when making a decision.ConclusionThis study describes the iterative development and pilot testing of a novel gist-based decision aid for cancer screening, demonstrating high acceptability. Our findings provide a foundation for randomised trials comparing gist-based and traditional tools.

Introduction

Patient decision aids are recommended to help patients make preference-sensitive medical decisions and are considered best practice to improve the quality of decision making for patients.14 To facilitate the design of decision aids, the latest version of the International Patient Decision Aid Standards (IPDAS) recommends a minimum standard of 44 items across 10 domains, often requiring a large amount of complex information.58

This prioritisation of quantitative detail and complex information has not suitably incorporated knowledge from numeracy and literacy research.914 For example, a commonly used prostate cancer screening decision aid lists 11 separate quantitative estimates (eg, that 33% of prostate cancers are aggressive and that 0.4% of men who get the screening test still die of prostate cancer).15 However, evidence regarding health numeracy demonstrates that many patients struggle to understand a single risk estimate, let alone multiple countervailing estimates.91116 17

Furthermore, contemporary patient decision aids only weakly integrate behavioural and communication science into their design and evaluation. For example, fuzzy-trace theory emphasises understanding the essential ‘gist’ of information rather than merely verbatim words and numerical detail.1823 Gist refers to characterising the meaningful essence of information to allow individuals to make judgments or discrete choices. Unlike heuristics or simply reducing information load, gist involves integrating dimensions of information to distil their meaning. Research in fuzzy-trace theory demonstrates that decision makers often prefer gist-based representations over verbatim detail, applying them across choices, judgements and numerical responses.22 23 Furthermore, when presented with too much detail, individuals may experience cognitive overload or interference, which has been demonstrated to have negative effects on decision making; this suggests that, in some cases, providing less detail could help patients make better medical decisions.202426

Although gist-based approaches have been applied to cancer screening best practices for systematically developing and refining such tools remain underexplored.222729 Our study extends this literature by applying a multiphase, patient-informed process to develop and test gist-based decision aids for prostate and breast cancer screening. The objective of this study was to develop two decision aids, one for prostate-specific antigen (PSA) screening and the other for breast cancer mammographic screening using a gist-based design, and to test the feasibility of an iterative revision approach with direct patient interviews.

Methods

Our steering committee, comprised of a primary care physician, urologist, ethicist and statistician, laid the groundwork for the scoping and design of the gist-based decision aids. We followed the systematic process for developing patient decision aids described by the Ottawa Decision Support Framework and IPDAS.8 30 Additional recommendations, as described by Coulter et al,31 were also incorporated. In brief, this process included: scoping and design, prototype development and finally, iterative alpha testing with end users.

The target audiences for the decision aids were individuals aged 40–60 years eligible for prostate cancer screening, without a personal history of prostate cancer and individuals aged 40–49 years eligible for breast cancer screening, without a personal history of breast cancer. For informational content, we reviewed and synthesised the best available evidence on screening recommendations as supported by major guideline organisations and randomised controlled trials on the effectiveness of screening on cancer-specific mortality and major harms such as false positives and overdiagnosis.7 31 We incorporated the principles from our prior work on a prototype decision aid for PSA screening. In this prototype, ‘A Simple Schema’, we went beyond providing evidence-based information and minimal numerical estimates. It emphasised a deep, integrative overview that distilled the meaningful essence of cancer screening decisions, consistent with fuzzy-trace theory’s emphasis on schema-based gist.32 This integrative approach distinguishes gist-based design from other methods of simplifying decision aids.7

Gist-based design prototype

The prototype was a two-page decision aid that included subheadings with information on the target cancers (prostate or breast), cancer screening programmes, estimation of risk, pros and cons of cancer screening, and the gist of cancer screening. Although the methodology for developing each decision aid was the same, the final versions were slightly different given the differences in cancer screening for prostate cancer versus breast cancer, which translated into distinct gist messages.

For breast cancer screening, the issue of false alarms needing further investigation for the individual was promoted as the gist. In prostate cancer screening, the implications of opting for active surveillance following a low-grade cancer diagnosis was promoted as the gist. The second page focused on the discrete dichotomised choice and implications between undergoing screening or forgoing screening.

Patient quotes facilitated the gist messaging. These quotes were developed based on clinician expertise and anecdotes of what individuals typically prioritise when undergoing screening. For the breast cancer aid patient quotes were developed such as “Even if it might be scary and uncomfortable, I’m the sort of person who would rather go through medical tests and exams than have even a small risk of missing a bad cancer” and “Hearing that my mammogram wasn’t normal and that I might have breast cancer sounds very scary.” These quotes were categorised under two statements: (1) “Yes, false alarms are ok with me” or (2) “No, false alarms are not ok for me” to reflect the choice of undergoing or forgoing screening, respectively.

For prostate cancer, the developed quotes were categorised under the two statements: (1) “Yes, PSA screening is right for me” or (2) “No, PSA screening is not for me” depending on how individuals felt in the event low-risk prostate cancer was detected, and active surveillance was recommended. Patient quotes such as “I don’t like having to go to the doctor and getting tests but I guess I could deal with it” represented those choosing to undergo PSA screening. Comparatively, the quote “I can’t imagine waking up every morning and going about my day knowing there was cancer in my body, even if it was low risk. I don’t think I could deal with it” was chosen to represent those forgoing PSA screening.

Approach

This study was conducted from March 2021 to March 2023. Participants were recruited from a public hospital located in New York City (approached face-to-face by a collaborating physician) and from ResearchMatch (email), a national health volunteer registry that was created by several academic institutions and supported by the US National Institutes of Health as part of the Clinical and Translational Science Awards programme. ResearchMatch has a large population of volunteers who have consented to be contacted by researchers about health studies for which they may be eligible. Stratified sampling was used to enrich diversity in race, ethnicity and gender. Participants were recruited both from a public hospital serving an underserved urban population and from ResearchMatch, a large national volunteer registry, to ensure a broad range of perspectives. Semistructured cognitive interviews, lasting for about 1 hour, were conducted by one of four trained clinical research coordinators (three females, one male), using a semistructured interview guide developed by the study team and a qualitative methods specialist. This guide included standardised prompts used across all participants, to minimise framing and order effects.

Interviews were organised into topics to assess decision aid self-reported comprehension and acceptability: (1) comprehension and acceptability of information presented, (2) suggestions for improvement, (3) emotional responses to the information presented, (4) perceived utility of information presented and the format overall, (5) perceived influence on decision making, (6) comparison of different decision aids and (7) exploratory questions from the participant wrap up. Clinical research coordinators provided the participants with a study information sheet detailing the research goals and established a rapport before conducting interviews. Each interview had two clinical research coordinators: one who conducted the interview and another who took notes. Clinical research coordinators were trained by a qualitative methods specialist from MSK’s patient-reported outcomes, community-engagement and language core facility, who reviewed the transcripts for thematic quality and provided iterative feedback on the interviews. All interviews were audio-taped and transcribed. Transcripts were not returned to participants for comment or correction due to the anonymous nature of the interviews. Review and approval for this study and all procedures was obtained from the hospital’s institutional review board.

Alpha testing

Next, we directly involved the end users in the development process (figure 1) by conducting three rounds of alpha testing—Phase 1 (initial feedback): n=32 (16 men, 16 women); Phase 2, Round 1 (evaluating our modifications): n=32 (16 men, 16 women); and Phase 2, Round 2 (confirming final content): n=16 (8 men, 8 women)—with different participants in each round. We conducted this assessment to determine self-reported comprehensibility and acceptability of the information presented (modified Ottawa acceptability scale, Net Promoter Score), gather suggestions for improvement, explore emotional responses to the information presented and assess content validity of the tools.33 34 Participants were sent the decision aids in advance of the interview to familiarise themselves with the content. At the end of the interview, participants were asked demographic questions regarding education, ethnic background, health literacy (Single-Item Literacy Screener) and numeracy (3-item Subjective Numeracy Scale).3537 In Phase 2, participants also filled out an evaluation form developed by our study team to assess the acceptability of the decision aids which used a 3-point or 5-point Likert scale, depending on the question. Acceptability of each aid was assessed through questions on amount and length of information, mental effort required and the helpfulness of the aid. The evaluation form explored 15 domains relating to visual appeal, reading ease and comprehension of images, graphs and charts.

Data analysis and revisions

Structural coding and analysis of the interview transcripts was performed by a clinical research coordinator, guided by two qualitative methods specialists, using the framework method to facilitate rapid, iterative analysis to inform intervention development.38 39 The analytical team constructed a data matrix in Microsoft Excel to summarise key participant feedback within each domain. A summary report of themes and recommendations for changes was generated after reviewing participant feedback within each column.38 40 41 Comprehension issues and content eliciting confusion or misinterpretation led to revision or ultimate elimination of that content. Each aspect of the decision aid identified as problematic by at least three participants was marked for revision and retested in the second round of cognitive interviewing. Areas identified as problematic no more than two times were retained in the final tool and were not retested in the subsequent cognitive interviewing round. To address content issues and confirm patient comprehension, two additional rounds of cognitive interviews were performed with new participants with the revised tool, following identical procedures as in the first round.

Final versions

The study team made final decisions about content retention based on the resolution of major barriers to comprehension and team consensus. In the last round, we also included a professional graphic designer to assist with the final design and layout.

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients eligible for cancer screening were involved from the early alpha-testing stage through three iterative rounds of cognitive interviews that directly informed the design and refinement of the decision aids. Their feedback shaped the framing of gist messages, visuals, layout and readability, and they were asked to comment on the burden of participation, including time and information load. Although patients were not involved in recruitment or study design, their insights ensured that the decision aids reflected user priorities and experiences.

Phase 1 (initial feedback)

Participants described the aids as ‘informative without being overwhelming’ and highlighted subheadings, visuals, graphs and patient quotes as helpful in supporting their decisions without being directive. Suggestions included adding pictographs to illustrate risk statistics and improving representation in patient images, which some felt did not reflect their identities. In response, we added pictographs and updated the top-banner visuals to better reflect participant diversity.

Phase 2, Round 1 (evaluating our modifications)

In this round, participant feedback centred around design and structure. For example, full sentences rather than bullet points were preferred to enhance readability and clarity. For visuals, icons were preferred over pie charts. One participant described this as “[…] content that isn’t overwhelming is extremely beneficial. Visual aids are huge. This is helpful when giving out information” (male, prostate cancer group). With respect to prior changes regarding patient visuals, one participant explained “I like that it featured two minority women. As a minority woman, I feel that we need to do more for our health […] so I like that the aid was visually appealing” (female, breast cancer group).

Changes made after this round included creating a PSA screening pathways pictogram to replace a bulleted list describing the screening pathway. An icon array of female icons for breast cancer depicted absolute frequencies of benefits and harms to support intuitive comparison and highlight the gist that screening can save lives but also causes false alarms, consistent with fuzzy-trace theory and evidence-based visual design principles. Bullet point form was also removed in favour of short sentence structure. The formal subheadings ‘Decision tool for prostate cancer screening’ and ‘Decision tool for breast cancer screening’ were revised into questions to maximise impact: ‘Should you get screened for prostate cancer?’ and ‘Should you start breast cancer screening mammograms in your 40s or 50s?’ respectively.

Phase 2, Round 2 (confirming final content)

In this round, participants described the aids as ‘informative and detailed while being concise’, and emphasised that they supported decision-making by helping to confirm their choice to undergo or forgo screening. Several participants noted that the aids would be useful for prompting questions with a general practitioner, for reviewing information after a visit and even as a resource to share with family and friends. One participant reflected, “I actually just turned 40 and got a screening… this decision aid would’ve been great at the time I was making a decision” (female, breast cancer group).

Based on this feedback, we introduced several final refinements. A QR code was added to both aids to link participants directly to institutional resources for further information on cancer screening. In the prostate cancer aid, the PSA value of 3 ng/mL was specified as the cut-off between normal results and when to seek further clinical input. In the breast cancer aid, the section on breast self-awareness was removed as it was not central to the gist message. Finally, gist-based value statements were reformatted from a horizontal to a two-column vertical layout to more clearly emphasise the discrete choice between screening and not screening.

The decision aids were also scored according to the Flesch-Kincaid Grade and Ease Level, a widely used and validated readability formula that assesses the approximate reading grade level of a text based on word and sentence length.42 Flesch-Kincaid reading ease scores for our prostate and breast cancer decision aids were 70.6 and 71.8, respectively, each of which corresponds to a US seventh grade reading level.

Discussion

In this study, we designed two gist-based decision aids for prostate and breast cancer screening through a multiphase iterative process. At least two-thirds of participants found the content and length of information to be appropriate without requiring much mental effort to read. Most participants found our aid visually appealing without making them nervous or losing interest. This design is in contrast to the standard methodology for developing a decision aid, as outlined by IPDAS and the Ottawa Decision Support Framework, neither of which consider information or cognitive overload during development.831 4345 For our study, we adapted technical elements from both to refine our aid while emphasising gist-based decision making and maximising readability. Importantly, gist goes beyond readability by emphasising meaningful integration of information, not just simplified text.

Although there has been research into simplifying overly complex decision aids, there is still significant heterogeneity in the terms used, whether ‘abbreviated’, ‘short’ or ‘gist-based’ decision aids. We define a gist-based aid as one that distils the meaningful essence of information, integrating the most important aspects into a clear bottom line for decision making. A limitation with this approach is that aspect may not be universally salient or of primary importance for all individuals. We acknowledge that our approach involves certain assumptions regarding what constitutes the most critical factor in decision-making for all. For example, in this study, we focused on one salient aspect of each screening decision (false alarms for breast cancer, active surveillance for prostate cancer) to promote clarity. However, gist does not necessarily reduce decisions to a single dimension; rather, it typically integrates multiple aspects into a meaningful bottom line. Prior work demonstrates that experts and patients often converge on a core gist once they share background knowledge22 46 suggesting that future iterations of our decision aids could incorporate a more integrative gist.

Our findings also highlight the need to distinguish gist in fuzzy-trace theory from simple simplification. Unlike heuristics or reducing information load, gist conveys essential meaning in a concise bottom line.18 22 Clarifying this distinction is crucial to avoid conflating gist with shortcuts in future decision aid design. Patient quotes reflect both options and values (eg, ‘better safe than sorry’), yet while our design framed screening dichotomously, many may accept false alarms despite anxiety if it lowers the risk of missed cancer. This underscores the need to capture value-based trade-offs beyond a simple binary choice. Furthermore, presenting benefits and risks with equal weight can misrepresent the gist by implying false neutrality.47 48

Some technical studies attempting to promote gist-based decision making refer to specific amendments to content and design without mentioning gist. For example, a paper by Bilodeau et al outlines the process they used to develop an abbreviated decision aid for patients with dementia and their caregivers to enhance shared decision making.49 Although there was no mention of gist, they essentially shortened the decision aid. They outlined specific refinements vital in simplifying the message, such as presenting clear statements in large fonts and use of pictograms and personal quotes with appropriate context. Another report by Schonberg et al documented their development of a decision aid tailored for women over 75 years of age undergoing mammography screening.50 Although they aimed to enhance clarity, there was only brief alpha testing of the decision aid and the final version was 11 pages. In comparison, our gist-based decision aid was designed to facilitate a discrete choice (in this case, to undergo or forgo screening); at two pages in length, our final versions were brief and concise.

Information overload is a known concern but an underexplored concept in medical decision-making.5155 Behavioural science literature suggests that decision making under information overload can lead to suboptimal outcomes.18 56 This is significant given that a study assessing patient decision aids found that over 90% of trials did not report on health literacy or readability.14 In the research that did measure health literacy, information overload and difficulty reading decision aids were found to be more challenging for individuals with lower educational attainment. For example, a randomised controlled trial comparing decision aids found that low health literacy participants who received a simplified qualitative decision aid had higher knowledge and reduced decisional conflict compared with participants who received a traditional aid.57

The main strength of this study is the incorporation of gist-based decision making and information overload as a domain in our development of the aid. Our findings build on prior gist-based interventions in breast cancer screening by applying a structured, multiphase development process with direct patient feedback.22 27 This extends existing work by demonstrating a practical method for creating and refining gist-based aids across two cancer screening contexts. By comparison, in most literature on decision aids only half of patient decision aids were reported to have patient involvement in their development.58 Lastly, we confirmed our gist-based aids to be at an appropriate reading level for widespread usage, with both aids measuring at a seventh grade reading level (Flesch-Kincaid test). Our recruitment also facilitated representation of individuals from across different races and ethnicities, which allowed for diversity of perspectives and feedback improving the relevance of our decision aids. While most participants reported that the aids required little mental effort to read, literature suggests that some effort may promote deeper learning and this point will be critical in future research.

An important consideration for future iterations is that presenting pros and cons with equal weight may distort the gist, as these options are rarely equivalent even in preference-sensitive decisions.47 Future developments should account for the fact that the relative weight of benefits and harms may shift choices and should be represented accordingly. Other limitations of this study include the recruitment of predominantly highly educated and health literate participants. Although this would not be representative of the wider population, the aim of promoting gist-based decision making is likely to be even more applicable for those with lower educational attainment. In fact, one randomised controlled trial of colorectal cancer screening participants demonstrated that a decision aid that was more sensitive to the needs of people with low literacy (characterised by plain language, basic design and efforts to reduce cognitive effort) improved the quality of the decision-making (improved knowledge, better informed, reduced decisional conflict and no difference in anxiety or cancer worry) compared with a control group receiving a consumer information booklet.57

We also acknowledge that while interviewer training enhanced consistency, qualitative interviews inherently have lower reliability than hypothesis-driven experiments.

Additionally, direct comparison to a traditional aid was not performed. However, the development of these novel gist-based decision aids in our study is part of a larger research project assessing complexity of information and evaluating gist-based decision making in a randomised trial. It should also be noted that during the time our study was conducted many guidelines (including the US Preventive Services Task Force (USPSTF)) recommended individual decision making for mammography for women between ages 40–49; USPSTF now recommends screening for all women starting at age 40.

Our approach to developing gist-based decision aids demonstrates potential in improving decision-making quality without information overload. We developed and tested gist-based decision aids for prostate and breast cancer screening through an iterative, patient-informed process, demonstrating feasibility and high acceptability. Beyond their practical utility, these aids exemplify a shift from traditional information-heavy approaches toward designs grounded in fuzzy-trace theory, emphasising the meaningful essence of decisions. Future research should evaluate their comparative effectiveness against traditional tools and further explore how integrative gist representations—capturing multiple dimensions of benefits, harms and values—shape decision quality. By advancing both conceptual and methodological understanding, our work provides a foundation for cumulative progress in the science of decision aid design.

Conclusion

We developed gist-based decision aids for prostate and breast cancer screening using an iterative, patient-informed process and found them to be acceptable and feasible. These findings advance existing work on gist-based approaches and provide a practical foundation for randomised trials directly comparing gist-based and traditional decision aids.
