Soft-tissue augmentation techniques for enhancing peri-implant volume: A systematic review
Authors: Farjad Zafar, Maryam Riaz, Rahul Kumar, Rafay Ahmed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:This systematic review aimed to critically evaluate the effectiveness of soft-tissue augmentation (STA) techniques for enhancing peri-implant soft-tissue thickness and volume around dental implants with compromised soft tissue.Materials and Methods:A comprehensive electronic and manual search was performed in MEDLINE via PubMed and EMBASE via Ovid, covering studies published in English up to December 2024. Human studies investigating STA techniques for peri-implant soft-tissue volume augmentation were included. The Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tools, selected according to study design, were used to assess the risk of bias in randomized controlled trials (RCTs), prospective clinical studies (PCS), and case series (CS). Due to methodological heterogeneity, data were synthesized qualitatively rather than through meta-analysis.Results:Thirty-one studies (20 RCTs, 5 PCS, and 6 CS) were included. Reported soft-tissue thickness gain ranged from 0.08 to 3.59 mm and volume gain from 0.16 to 186.38 mm3, where gains ≥2 mm were generally considered clinically relevant for peri-implant tissue improvement and mucosal stability. Despite a predominantly high risk of bias, the subepithelial connective tissue graft (SCTG) combined with a split-thickness flap provided the most consistent outcomes. Variations in surgical protocols, follow-up durations, and measurement methods precluded quantitative meta-analysis.Conclusion:Within the limitations of heterogeneity and potential bias, STA techniques, particularly SCTGs, show promising potential for enhancing peri-implant soft-tissue thickness and volume. However, given the moderate-to-high risk of bias and the lack of a pooled analysis, these results should be interpreted with caution. Further well-designed RCTs with standardized methodologies, consistent outcome measures, and long-term follow-up are essential to validate these findings and optimize clinical protocols.

INTRODUCTION

Dental implants have become a cornerstone in modern dentistry, offering a reliable solution for the restoration of lost teeth and significantly improving patients’ quality of life.[1] However, while functional outcomes such as osseointegration and implant stability are well-documented, achieving optimal esthetic results remains a persistent challenge.[2] The definition of implant success has evolved, shifting from a narrow focus on long-term survival to a broader emphasis on functional performance, esthetic harmony, and patient satisfaction.[1] Today, patients increasingly expect dental implants to not only function like natural teeth but also to blend seamlessly with their smile, highlighting the critical role of soft-tissue integration in implant therapy.

Peri-implant soft-tissue thickness and volume play a central role in both esthetic and biological stability. Adequate soft-tissue volume contributes to favorable mucosal contour, supports interdental papillae, and appears to protect the underlying crestal bone from remodeling. Conversely, thin peri-implant mucosa (<2 mm) has been consistently associated with increased marginal bone loss and a higher risk of mucosal recession in clinical studies.[345678] These findings emphasize the strong interdependence between soft-tissue thickness, peri-implant bone stability, and esthetic outcomes, reinforcing the need for effective soft-tissue management strategies in implant therapy.

Following tooth extraction, the alveolar process undergoes dimensional changes characterized by bone resorption and soft-tissue collapse, both of which can complicate esthetic implant placement and restoration.[2] To address these challenges, a spectrum of surgical and regenerative procedures has been developed, including ridge preservation, guided bone regeneration, and soft-tissue augmentation (STA).[910] Among these, STA has gained particular attention for its ability to enhance soft-tissue thickness and volume, thereby improving peri-implant esthetics. Evidence suggests that a thick gingival biotype provides clinical advantages such as better plaque control, reduced inflammation, and minimized marginal bone loss.[11121314] These factors collectively improve the long-term success of dental implants.[11121314] In contrast, a thin gingival biotype is often associated with higher susceptibility to mucosal recession and esthetic compromise.[1516]

The thickness and volume of peri-implant soft tissue are therefore critical determinants of both esthetic and functional outcomes.[17] Key factors influencing implant esthetics include the contour, color, and symmetry of the soft tissue, as well as the presence and position of interdental papillae.[18] Adequate soft-tissue volume not only enhances the natural appearance of implants but also helps reduce alveolar bone remodeling and marginal bone loss, contributing to long-term stability.[14192021] However, peri-implant soft-tissue dehiscence, often occurring even in the absence of peri-implantitis, remains a clinical concern that underscores the importance of STA strategies.[22]

STA can be performed at various stages of implant therapy, including pre-implant, intra-implant, and post-implant phases. While early-stage grafting generally results in more favorable outcomes, late-stage procedures may increase the risk of complications such as graft shrinkage or peri-implant infection.[14] The evaluation of soft-tissue esthetics has also evolved with indices such as the White Esthetic Score, Pink Esthetic Score, and Implant Soft-Tissue Dehiscence Coverage Esthetic Score, allowing for more standardized outcome assessments.[232425] Numerous clinical trials have demonstrated that STA improves esthetic outcomes as perceived by both clinicians and patients.[26]

Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have explored different aspects of peri-implant soft-tissue management. For instance, Bassetti et al. (2017) and Akcalı et al. (2017) analyzed the role of subepithelial connective tissue grafts (SCTGs) and soft-tissue substitutes, while Thoma et al. (2018) and Tavelli et al. (2021) reviewed STA procedures at implant sites and their impact on esthetic and volumetric outcomes. Nevertheless, these reviews vary widely in their scope, i.e., some focus on keratinized mucosa width, others on the timing of soft-tissue phenotype modification, and few specifically assess quantitative volumetric changes measured using standardized 3D or digital techniques.[14152728] The heterogeneity in grafting materials, surgical protocols, and outcome parameters has limited direct comparison between techniques and precluded robust, evidence-based clinical recommendations.

Given these gaps, the present systematic review aims to synthesize the available clinical evidence regarding STA techniques for peri-implant volumetric and thickness improvement. This review emphasizes quantitative outcomes, timing of intervention, and comparative performance of grafting methods to provide clinicians with practical, evidence-based insights for enhancing peri-implant esthetics and stability. Ultimately, by consolidating and critically analyzing the literature, this study seeks to guide clinical decision-making and identify areas where future research can refine protocols and standardize evaluation methodologies.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The primary objective of this systematic review was to evaluate the effectiveness of STA procedures in enhancing peri-implant soft-tissue volume and thickness, particularly in sites with compromised soft-tissue conditions. Following the PRISMA guidelines, a focused research question was formulated using the PICO framework to ensure methodological transparency and reproducibility.[29] This review protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (Registration number: CRD42024539342).

Search strategy

A comprehensive and systematic electronic literature search was conducted across multiple databases, including MEDLINE (via PubMed) and EMBASE (via OVID), supplemented by a manual search to ensure broad coverage of available evidence. The search included studies published between January 1, 2014, and December 31, 2024.

A detailed Boolean search strategy incorporating both MeSH terms and free-text keywords related to dental implants, STA, mucogingival surgery, connective tissue graft, soft-tissue thickness, and volume was applied [Table 1]. Only studies published in peer-reviewed English-language journals were included to ensure scientific rigor and interpretability, acknowledging this as a potential source of language bias.

Study selection

Two independent reviewers (F. Z. and M. R.) conducted title and abstract screening, followed by full-text evaluation of potentially eligible studies. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consensus, and, when necessary, adjudicated by two other reviewers (R. K., R. A.).

Data extraction and risk of bias assessment

Data were extracted using a standardized form that captured relevant details, including study design, sample size, participant characteristics, intervention and comparator types, follow-up duration, measurement techniques, and quantitative outcomes of peri-implant soft-tissue thickness or volume.

The methodological quality and risk of bias of included studies were independently assessed according to study design using established Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Tools.[3031] Each study was categorized as low, moderate, or high risk of bias based on adherence to methodological criteria. Any discrepancies in study selection, data extraction, or quality assessment were resolved through discussion among the reviewers (F. Z., M. R., R. K., and R. A.).

Data synthesis

Given the anticipated methodological and clinical heterogeneity among the included studies, a qualitative synthesis of findings was planned. A quantitative meta-analysis was not considered since at least three studies were not available for a specific outcome measure.

RESULTS

The initial database search yielded a total of 2418 records after the removal of duplicates. Following title, abstract, and full-text screening based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 31 studies were included in the final synthesis. Instead, outcomes were analyzed discretely across study categories. Most RCTs reported a statistically significant gain in peri-implant soft-tissue thickness ranging from 0.8 to 2.5 mm at 6–12 months, with SCTG consistently outperforming substitute materials such as collagen matrices or acellular dermal matrices.[3536373940414243444648495051525456596062] PCSs demonstrated comparable qualitative improvement in peri-implant contour and soft-tissue stability, though without consistent volumetric quantification.[4757586163] CSs primarily reported favorable tissue thickness, but due to limited sample sizes and lack of controls, their findings were considered exploratory rather than confirmatory.[333438455355]

Overall, the qualitative synthesis suggests that STA procedures, particularly autogenous SCTGs, tend to yield favorable improvements in peri-implant tissue thickness and volume outcomes. However, due to the predominance of studies with a high risk of bias and methodological variability, these conclusions should be interpreted with care.

DISCUSSION

This systematic review aimed to assess the effectiveness of various STA techniques in improving peri-implant soft-tissue thickness and volume. Across the 31 included studies, which comprised RCTs, PCSs, and CSs, STA procedures generally demonstrated favorable improvements in tissue dimensions. However, these findings should be interpreted carefully due to a predominant high risk of bias in the studies and the considerable methodological heterogeneity in study design, interventions, and outcome measures.

RCTs consistently reported that SCTGs are the most predictable approach for increasing soft-tissue thickness and achieving long-term stability, with reported gains typically ranging from 0.8 to 2.5 mm in thickness and 20–150 mm3 in volume after 6–12 months of follow-up.[3334353639404142434446474849505253545758596062] Combinations of SCTG with coronally advanced flaps or split-thickness flaps further improved peri-implant tissue contour and stability, reflecting the critical role of adequate flap thickness in ensuring vascularity and graft survival.[61] In contrast, XCM and VCMX produced more variable outcomes, with thickness gains between 0.3 and 1.2 mm and volume changes from 0.9 to 136 mm3.[3536384647515257585962] While these materials offer reduced patient morbidity and shorter surgical times, their outcomes were generally less predictable compared to SCTG, particularly for long-term tissue stability.

PCS further corroborated the clinical benefits of STA procedures, demonstrating modest but perceptible gains in tissue thickness (0.7–1.4 mm) and enhanced peri-implant contour.[4757586163] However, many of these studies lacked standardized volumetric assessments, and follow-up periods were mostly limited to under 12 months. CS provided additional insights into individualized surgical approaches, often in anterior implant sites, and documented qualitative improvements in tissue architecture.[333438455355] Nevertheless, the absence of control groups and the small sample sizes limited the strength of their evidence.

Soft-tissue shrinkage following grafting was evaluated in twelve studies and remains a significant factor influencing long-term outcomes.[354344495051525355565960] SCTGs exhibited volumetric shrinkage ranging from 8 to 34 mm3 or thickness reductions between 0.2 and 1.1 mm within 6–12 months, suggesting partial but clinically acceptable contraction.[3543444950525359] Xenogeneic matrices demonstrated slightly lower overall shrinkage (10–15 mm3 or 0.3–0.6 mm); moreover, one long-term study reported a reduction of <0.3 mm over 2 years when combined with a buccal pedicle flap.[5255] Clinically, even gains of approximately 1 mm in soft-tissue thickness are meaningful, contributing to reduced marginal bone loss and improved peri-implant thickness, highlighting the practical relevance of these outcomes despite quantitative variability.[5156]

This review is subject to several limitations. The search was restricted to PubMed and EMBASE databases and English-language publications, introducing potential language and publication bias. The inclusion of CS, which represents lower-tier evidence, and the predominance of high-risk studies limit the strength of conclusions. Significant heterogeneity in study design, intervention protocols, and outcome reporting precluded meta-analysis. Subgroup analyses based on implant location, timing of grafting (immediate versus delayed), or mucosal phenotype could not be performed due to inconsistent reporting. Moreover, patient-reported outcomes were rarely captured systematically, limiting the ability to fully evaluate clinical relevance from the patient perspective.

Despite these limitations, the evidence suggests that SCTGs, alone or combined with coronally advanced or split-thickness flaps, provide the most reliable improvement in peri-implant soft-tissue volume and thickness. Xenogeneic matrices, while less predictable, remain a valuable option for patients seeking less invasive alternatives. Future research should focus on well-designed, adequately powered RCTs with standardized outcome measures, longer follow-up, and the inclusion of quantitative volumetric assessments. Such studies should also explore subgroup analyses to identify the impact of implant location, graft timing, and soft-tissue phenotype on treatment success. By addressing these methodological considerations, future research can provide more robust, evidence-based guidance for optimizing STA in implant dentistry.

CONCLUSION

Enhancing peri-implant soft-tissue thickness and volume is essential for achieving long-term stability and optimal esthetic outcomes. The available evidence indicates that SCTGs, particularly when combined with split-thickness or coronally advanced flaps, provide the most consistent improvements in tissue dimensions, while xenogeneic or volume-stable collagen matrices can serve as viable alternatives in patients where SCTG is not feasible. Observed gains in soft-tissue thickness and volume, though variable across studies, are generally sufficient to confer meaningful clinical benefits, including improved contour and protection of peri-implant bone. The degree of graft shrinkage varies by technique and material, highlighting the need for careful surgical planning, precise flap management, and careful selection of graft materials. Given the predominance of studies with high risk of bias and methodological heterogeneity, these conclusions should be interpreted vigilantly. Well-designed, long-term RCTs with standardized outcome measures and patient-centered assessments are necessary to provide robust, evidence-based guidance for optimizing STA around dental implants.
