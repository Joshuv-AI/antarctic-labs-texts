The relationship between premonitory urges, interoceptive sensibility, alexithymia, psychological problems and tic severity in adolescents with tic disorders
Authors: Merve Onat, İrem Haliloğlu, Ayşe Eylül Özel Gümüş, Hanife Avcı, Aslı Nur Özkaya Parlakay, Gökçen İlçioğlu Ekici, Gülser Şenses Dinç, Esra Çöp
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent evidence suggests that tic symptoms and premonitory urges may be related to altered interoceptive processing. However, the relationship between interoceptive sensibility, alexithymia, psychological problems, and tic severity remains underexplored, particularly in adolescents. The aim of our study was to determine the relationship between premonitory urges, interoceptive sensibility, alexithymia, psychological problems, and tic severity in adolescents with tic disorders.MethodsThis cross-sectional study included 46 adolescents diagnosed with tic disorders and 42 healthy controls, aged 11–17 years, who applied to a city hospital child and adolescent psychiatry outpatient clinic between July 2024 and December 2024. Participants were evaluated with the Yale Global Tic Severity Scale (YGTSS), Premonitory Urges for Tics Scale (PUTS), Alexithymia Questionnaire for Children (AQC), Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness in Youth (MAIA-Y), and Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ). We used the SPSS Statistics 23.0 program and R (version 4.1.1) for analysis. p < 0.050 was accepted as the significance level.ResultsAdolescents with tic disorders showed elevated levels of alexithymia and psychological problems and demonstrated different patterns of interoceptive sensibility in comparison to controls. Alexithymia and psychological problems were positively correlated with the severity of tics, whereas capacity to listen to and trust the body was negatively correlated. No statistically significant association was found between premonitory urges and tic severity. According to multiple linear regression analysis, the total score of the YGTSS increases by 4.329 units with a 1-unit increase in the SDQ emotional symptoms score. A 1-unit increase in the body listening subscale score of MAIA-Y decreases the total score of YGTSS by 3.494 units.ConclusionsAdolescents with tic disorders may have different characteristics compared to controls in terms of alexithymia, psychological problems, and interoceptive sensibility, and that tic severity may be related to emotional symptoms and body listening capacity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04410-y.Keywords: Adolescent, Alexithymia, Interoceptive sensibility, Premonitory urges, Tic disorders

Introduction

Tics are defined as sudden, rapid, repetitive, and irregular motor movements or sounds. The occurrence of tics for more than one year is defined as chronic tic disorder, and the co-occurrence of motor and vocal tics is defined as Tourette disorder, although they do not necessarily occur at the same time [1]. Some of the patients with tic disorder feel an uncomfortable physical sensation before the tic. This phenomenon, which manifests as itching, a feeling of pressure in a certain body area, and the thought that something is incomplete or incorrect, is defined as ‘premonitory urges’ [2]. Premonitory urges are often followed by a subjective sense of relief following tic expression [3]. Along with internalizing symptoms like anxiety and depression, premonitory urges may also be associated with the severity of tics [4–7]. In a study conducted on children and adolescents aged 5–16 years, it was shown that premonitory urge intensity positively predicted tic severity [8].

It has been hypothesized that premonitory urges in tic disorders may represent increased or abnormal bodily sensations [9]. Recent studies suggest that tics and premonitory urges may be closely linked to altered interoceptive processing [10, 11]. The concept of interoception refers to the conscious perception of sensations coming from within the body that enable the perception of the physiological state of the body, such as heartbeat, respiration, satiety, and autonomic nervous system sensations related to emotions [12, 13]. Interoceptive sensibility constitutes a dimension of interoception, which describes the subjective awareness of one's capacity to perceive internal stimuli [14]. In the study by Eddy et al., it was shown that patients with Tourette's disorder reported to have more awareness of internal bodily sensations [15]. In addition, researchers indicate that interoceptive sensibility is a predictor of the intensity of premonitory urges and tics [16]. A recent study suggested that interoceptive sensibility explains a considerable proportion of the variance in premonitory urges in young people with tic disorders [17]. Developmental variations in interoceptive abilities have been linked to age-related cognitive changes and may be relevant to vulnerability to psychopathology during adolescence [18]. Therefore, investigating interoception during adolescence may be particularly important.

Interoceptive sensibility has been associated with alexithymia, which is related to many psychiatric disorders, such as depression and anxiety [19, 20]. Alexithymia is defined as a state of mind characterized by difficulty in recognizing and expressing one's emotions and a tendency to focus more on the non-emotional aspects of one's experiences [21]. Individuals who struggle to perceive their bodily sensations may also struggle to identify and describe their emotions [22]. Previous research suggests that interoceptive deficits in alexithymia are predominantly linked to difficulties in identifying and describing feelings, rather than to externally oriented thinking [23]. This pattern has been further supported by recent meta-analytic findings demonstrating associations between alexithymia and self-reported interoception [24]. Alexithymia has been shown to partially mediate the association between heightened interoceptive sensibility and anxiety, highlighting its relevance for emotional difficulties [25]. In addition, a clinical study in adolescents reported that difficulties identifying and describing feelings were more prominent in internalizing disorders, whereas externally oriented thinking was elevated in externalizing disorders [26].

There are few studies on the relationship between tic disorder and alexithymia in the literature. In an adult study, researchers found no difference in alexithymia levels between Tourette's disorder patients and controls [27]. However, in a recent study it was found that children with tic disorders focused more on non-emotional aspects of experience in relation to alexithymia compared to controls [28]. In addition, difficulty identifying feelings has been shown to predict the severity of premonitory urges, although it does not predict the severity of tics [28].

This study aims to contribute to the growing body of research on tic disorders by examining how premonitory urges, interoceptive sensibility, alexithymia, and psychological difficulties together relate to tic severity in adolescents. In our study, we aimed to explore the relationship between these factors in adolescents diagnosed with tic disorders and to compare our findings with those of healthy controls. The first hypothesis of our study is that adolescents with tic disorders will have increased levels of alexithymia, interoceptive sensibility, and psychological problems compared to the control group. Furthermore, we hypothesize a correlation between the tic severity and intensity of premonitory urges, interoceptive sensibility, alexithymia, and psychological problems in adolescents with tic disorders. We believe that our study will contribute to the relevant literature by demonstrating the potential relationship between the investigated factors and tic severity.

Methods

Participants

This study was designed as a cross-sectional study including adolescents with tic disorders and healthy controls. 54 of 62 adolescents between the ages of 11 and 17 who applied to the Ankara Bilkent City Hospital Child and Adolescent Psychiatry Outpatient Clinic with tic symptoms between July 2024 and December 2024 volunteered to participate in our study. The inclusion criteria of the study for the case group are being between the ages of 11 and 17, meeting the diagnostic criteria for Tourette's Disorder or Persistent Motor or Vocal Tic Disorder according to the Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School Aged Children Present and Lifetime Version, DSM-5 (K-SADS-PL-DSM-5) semi-structured diagnostic evaluation interview performed by a child and adolescent psychiatrist and the American Psychiatric Association’s Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), and having clinically normal intelligence. We included adolescents with tic disorder if their tics had persisted for at least the previous week and if they had been on a stable dose of medication for tics or comorbid psychopathology for the previous two months. Additionally, both the participant and their parents must volunteer and give their consent to participate in the study. Clinical diagnoses of intellectual disability, autism spectrum disorder, acute psychotic disorder, and bipolar disorder, and being under the influence of a substance were considered exclusion criteria for the study, as they may hinder evaluation. The research excluded five adolescents who did not fully meet the diagnostic criteria for Tourette's Disorder or Persistent Motor or Vocal Tic Disorder and three adolescents because they had co-existing intellectual disability. The study's inclusion criteria for the healthy control group include being between the ages of 11 and 17, not having met any psychiatric diagnosis according to the K-SADS-PL-DSM-5 and DSM-5 criteria in the past or current period, having clinically normal intelligence, and both the participant and their parents volunteering and giving consent to participate in the research. Therefore, the healthy control group consisted of adolescents aged between 11 and 17 years, with sociodemographic characteristics similar to those of the case group, who applied to the pediatrics outpatient clinic of our hospital, who had no history or current psychiatric disorder according to a psychiatric evaluation performed by a child and adolescent psychiatrist, and who and whose parents volunteered and gave consent to participate in the study. The exclusion criteria for both the case and control groups included the presence of chronic physical illnesses such as diabetes, hypertension, epilepsy, heart disease, or kidney disease, which could be associated with alexithymia [29–31]. The adolescents participating in the study were recruited consecutively. As a result, a total of 88 adolescents, 46 of whom constitute the tic disorders group and 42 healthy controls, were included in our study.

Alexithymia, interoceptive sensibility, and psychological problems were evaluated in all participants using the Alexithymia Questionnaire for Children (AQC), Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness in Youth (MAIA-Y), and Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ), respectively. In addition, tic severity and premonitory urges in the tic disorder group were evaluated using the Yale Global Tic Severity Scale (YGTSS) and the Premonitory Urges for Tics Scale (PUTS), respectively. YGTSS ratings were conducted by two child and adolescent psychiatrists, who were randomly assigned to participants, under the supervision of a child and adolescent psychiatry specialist. Although formal inter-rater reliability statistics were not calculated, ratings were performed according to the standardized YGTSS interview structure, and clinical consensus was maintained through regular supervision. The study obtained informed consent from all individual participants and their parents. Ethics approval was obtained from the Ankara Bilkent City Hospital Ethics Committee (Approval No: TABED 2–24–302; Date: 26.06.2024). This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample size calculation

A sample size calculation was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). Based on the comparison between the clinical and control groups reported in Silvestri et al. (2019), Cohen’s d effect size was calculated as 0.74 using the mean and standard deviation values of the Alexithymia Questionnaire for Children–Externally Oriented Thinking (AQC-EOT) scores [28]. Using a two-tailed Student's t-test with an alpha level of 0.05, and a power of 0.90, the required total sample size was determined to be 80 participants (40 in each group). However, during data collection, 42 participants were included in the control group and 46 in the case group, ensuring a robust sample size for the analysis.

Data collection tools

Sociodemographic data form

It was prepared by researchers based on previous studies on tic disorders to investigate the features of the participants, such as age, gender, family characteristics, socioeconomic level, and psychiatric disorders in the family [32–34].

Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia for School-Age Children- Present and Lifetime Version, DSM-5 (K-SADS-PL-DSM-5)

It is a semi-structured interview that evaluates the current and lifelong psychopathologies of children and adolescents according to DSM-5 diagnostic criteria. Current psychopathologies cover the last 2 months, while lifelong psychopathologies cover all diagnoses of the child or young person in the past. It was developed by Kaufman et al., and its Turkish adaptation, validity, and reliability study was carried out by Ünal et al. [35]. In the present study, the K-SADS-PL-DSM-5 was administered as part of the clinical assessment to confirm psychiatric diagnoses, including tic disorders and comorbid conditions.

Alexithymia Questionnaire for Children (AQC)

It was developed by Rieffe et al. in 2006 to measure children's alexithymia levels [36]. The scale comprises 20 items, with scores ranging from 0 to 2, and it is divided into three sub-dimensions: Difficulty Identifying Feelings (DIF), Difficulty Describing Feelings (DDF), and Externally Oriented Thinking (EOT). Higher total scores in the scale indicate higher levels of alexithymia. Koçak et al. adapted AQC into Turkish and conducted a validity and reliability study. The Cronbach's alpha internal consistency coefficient for the entire scale was determined to be 0.78. The Cronbach alpha coefficients for the subscales are 0.82 for DIF, 0.76 for DDF, and 0.77 for EOT [37]. In our study, Cronbach's alpha coefficient of the AQC was found to be 0.66.

Premonitory Urges for Tics Scale (PUTS)

Woods et al. developed the PUTS in 2005 to measure the current frequency of different types of premonitory urges in patients with tic disorders [38]. The original form of the scale comprises 10 items, each rated on a Likert scale ranging from 1 to 4. However, Woods excluded item 10 from the calculation of the total score due to its lack of relevance to the rest of the scale. Therefore, the total score ranges from 9 to 36, with a higher score representing a higher intensity of premonitory urges. Takayısı conducted a validity and reliability study of the PUTS scale in Turkish in 2019. Tayakısı's Turkish adaptation study found the scale's Cronbach's alpha internal consistency coefficient to be 0.91, indicating its sufficient validity and reliability for application in children and adolescents [39]. The Cronbach's alpha coefficient of the PUTS obtained in our study is 0.74.

Multidimensional Assessment of Interoceptive Awareness in Youth (MAIA-Y)

The MAIA is designed to assess multidimensional aspects of interoceptive awareness that may be relevant to clinical conditions. Jones et al. adapted the MAIA questionnaire used in adults to children and adolescents and showed that the youth form (MAIA-Y), which consists of 8 subscales, 32 items, and a 6-point Likert scale, has acceptable psychometric properties. The sub-dimensions of MAIA-Y encompass "noticing" which denotes the recognition of unpleasant, pleasant, and neutral bodily sensations; "not-distracting" indicating a tendency to neither deny nor divert attention from discomfort or pain; "not-worrying" suggesting the ability to experience discomfort or pain without emotional distress; "attention regulation" which refers to the capacity to sustain and manage focus on bodily sensations; "emotional awareness" highlighting the understanding of the connection between bodily sensations and emotional states; "self-regulation" pertaining to the ability to mitigate discomfort through attentiveness to bodily sensations; "body-listening" meaning the active observation of the body for insight; and "trusting" reflecting the perception of one's body as dependable and secure [40]. In the Turkish validity and reliability study in adolescents, the Cronbach's alpha coefficient of MAIA-Y was found to be 0.79, indicating that the scale is reliable [41]. In our study, the Cronbach's alpha coefficient of the MAIA-Y was determined to be 0.82. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients for the MAIA-Y subscales ranged from 0.67 to 0.83 in the present sample; alpha values were 0.71 for noticing, 0.68 for not-distracting, 0.67 for not-worrying, 0.81 for attention regulation, 0.79 for emotional awareness, 0.69 for self-regulation, 0.81 for body listening, and 0.83 for trusting.

Yale Global Tic Severity Scale (YGTSS)

Leckman et al. developed the YGTSS, a schedule based on a semi-structured interview [42]. The clinician records the motor and vocal tics present for the last week based on the child's and parent's reports. The clinician rates the severity of motor and vocal tics on 5 dimensions: number, frequency, intensity, complexity, and interference. The scores in this rating are summed to obtain a motor tic severity score and a vocal tic severity score. Five indicator scores are total motor tic score (0–25), total phonic tic score (0–25), total tic score (0–50), overall impairment rating (0–50), and global severity score. The Turkish validity-reliability study was conducted by Zaimoğlu et al. in 1995 [43]. The Cronbach's alpha coefficient of the YGTSS in our study was determined to be 0.79.

Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ)

The SDQ, which is used to screen psychological and behavioral problems of children and adolescents, was developed by Goodman [44]. The SDQ has five sub-dimensions: emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity-inattention, peer problems, and prosocial behavior scales. Each sub-dimension includes five three-point Likert-type questions graded 0–2. The scores obtained from the first four subscales constitute the total difficulties score (TDS). The validity and reliability study of the SDQ in the Turkish sample was conducted by Güvenir et al. Cronbach's alpha value of the TDS was calculated as 0.73 for the SDQ youth self-report form [45]. The youth self-report form of the SDQ was used in our study. In the current sample, Cronbach's alpha coefficient of the SDQ was found to be 0.56.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using the free and open-source software R (version 4.1.1, https://cran.r-project.org) and the SPSS for Windows Version 23.0 statistical package (Chicago, IL) by an academic biostatistician. To assess the normality of the data, both the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual methods such as Q–Q plots and histograms were used. Levene’s test was applied to test variance homogeneity. Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation when normally distributed or as median (25th–75th percentiles) when they did not follow a normal distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. There were no missing data in the analyses. Continuous variables that met the normality assumption were compared using Student's t-test, while variables that did not follow a normal distribution were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Yates correction for continuity chi-square test and Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate, was used for categorical variables. Variables with p < 0.200 in univariate analyses were considered candidate variables for the multiple linear regression model to reduce the risk of excluding potentially important predictors or confounders, as recommended in methodological literature [46, 47]. Multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF) values. Given the relatively small sample size and the number of potential predictors, the forward selection method was initially used as an exploratory model-building approach to identify the most relevant predictors while maintaining a parsimonious regression model. This approach was selected as collinearity diagnostics indicated no problematic multicollinearity among the candidate variables [46]. The assumptions of linear regression were evaluated by examining the distribution of residuals, which were found to be normally distributed. Model adequacy and validity of model-related assumptions were evaluated with residual graphs. The Q–Q plots used to assess the normality of residuals and the Spearman correlation matrix were generated in R, while all other statistical analyses were performed using SPSS. To perform Spearman rank correlation coefficients, the “Hmisc” package [48], “corrplot” package [49], and “GGally” [50] were employed. The "ggplot" [51] and "qqnorm" [52] functions were used to draw the residual graphs and Q-Q plot, respectively. Internal consistency of the psychometric scales was evaluated by calculating Cronbach’s alpha coefficients in the current sample. The significance value was set at a two-tailed p-value of < 0.050.

Results

We found no statistically significant difference in age, gender, and familial characteristics between the tic disorder (n = 46) and healthy control (n = 42) groups in our study. Mothers and fathers of adolescents with tic disorders were more likely to have a history of psychiatric illness (p = 0.015, p = 0.006, respectively). Table 1 presents the sociodemographic characteristics of both groups. The median duration of disorder in adolescents with tic disorder was 36 months (25th-75th percentile: 18–48 months). In our study, 47.8% (n = 22) of adolescents with tic disorders had attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, 17.4% (n = 8) had anxiety disorders, 8.7% (n = 4) had specific learning disorder, 4.3% (n = 2) had obsessive–compulsive disorder, and 2.2% (n = 1) had enuresis. It was determined that 63.0% (n = 29) of adolescents diagnosed with tic disorder used psychotropic medications, while 37.0% (n = 17) did not. Details regarding the medications they used are provided in Table S1 (See Supplementary Material).

We applied Spearman correlation analysis to determine the relationship between tic severity, premonitory urges, interoceptive sensibility, alexithymia, and psychological problems in adolescents diagnosed with tic disorders. A significant positive correlation was found between the total score of the YGTSS and the total score of the SDQ and AQC scales (r = 0.454, p = 0.002; r = 0.328, p = 0.026, respectively). A significant negative correlation was found between the total score of the YGTSS and the body listening and trusting subscales of the MAIA-Y scale (r = −0.435, p = 0.002; r = −0.376, p = 0.010, respectively).

A significant positive correlation was found between the total score of the PUTS scale and the noticing, attention regulation, and body listening subscales of the MAIA-Y scale (r = 0.362, p = 0.013; r = 0.412, p = 0.004; r = 0.355, p = 0.015, respectively). However, the total score of the PUTS scale was significantly negatively correlated with the not-distracting subscale (r = −0.369, p = 0.012). There was no statistically significant correlation between the total PUTS and YGTSS scores.

A significant negative correlation was found between the noticing and not-distracting subscales of the MAIA-Y scale (r = −0.389, p = 0.008). A significant positive correlation was found between the noticing subscale of the MAIA-Y scale and the attention regulation, emotional awareness, and self-regulation subscales (r = 0.475, p < 0.001; r = 0.588, p < 0.001; r = 0.349, p = 0.017, respectively). The MAIA-Y not-distracting subscale showed a significant negative correlation between the attention regulation, emotional awareness, self-regulation, and body-listening subscales (r = −0.532, p < 0.001; r = −0.330, p = 0.025; r = −0.539, p < 0.001; r = −0.412, p = 0.004, respectively). A significant positive correlation was found between the MAIA-Y attention regulation subscale and the emotional awareness, self-regulation, and body-listening subscales (r = 0.558, p < 0.001; r = 0.512, p < 0.001; r = 0.417, p = 0.004, respectively). A significant positive correlation was found between the MAIA-Y emotional awareness and self-regulation subscales (r = 0.404, p = 0.005). A significant positive correlation was found between the MAIA-Y body-listening subscale and the self-regulation and trusting subscales (r = 0.624, p < 0.001; r = 0.546, p < 0.001, respectively) (Fig. 1).

Studentized residuals plotted against predicted values show a random scatter around zero, supporting homoscedasticity and indicating no systematic model error. According to the results of the Shapiro–Wilk goodness-of-fit test, it was found to be z = 0.959; p = 0.108 for studentized deleted residuals, suggesting the residues were normally distributed (See Supplementary Material). A multiple linear regression model was used to identify factors associated with tic severity. Based on the p < 0.200 criterion in the univariate analyses, emotional symptoms, conduct problems, hyperactivity-inattention, peer problems of the SDQ, attention regulation, body listening, and trusting subscale scores of MAIA-Y, and the AQC total score were determined as candidate variables for the multiple linear regression model. As a result, a 1-unit increase in the SDQ emotional symptoms score was associated with a 4.329-unit increase in the total YGTSS score. A 1-unit increase in the body listening subscale score of MAIA-Y decreases the total score of YGTSS by 3.494 units. The SDQ emotional symptoms variable has the highest contribution to the model. In an additional adjusted analysis, psychotropic medication use (yes/no) was included as a covariate to assess potential confounding. Adjustment for medication status did not materially change the effect estimates or the statistical significance of the primary associations, indicating the robustness of the findings (Table 4).

Discussion

In our study, adolescents with tic disorders showed higher levels of alexithymia and psychological problems compared to controls. They were also less able to recognize bodily emotions and less likely to divert their attention when experiencing uncomfortable feelings. Both alexithymia and psychological problems correlated with the severity of tic symptoms. However, we discovered a negative correlation between the severity of tic symptoms and the ability to listen to and trust the body. We found no relationship between premonitory urges and tic severity.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the sensibility dimension of interoception using the MAIA-Y in adolescents with tic disorders. Our results indicated that adolescents with tic disorders reported significantly lower scores on the noticing subscale of the MAIA-Y, suggesting a reduced tendency to recognize bodily sensations. In addition, adolescents with tic disorders scored significantly higher on the not-distracting subscale, indicating a tendency not to divert attention away from discomfort. We also discovered a link between less severe tics and the ability to listen to and trust the body. Contrary to our study, Narapareddy et al. showed that adults with chronic tic disorders were more likely to recognize bodily sensations compared to controls [53]. We believe that the difference between the findings could be explained by the fact that interoception is a structure that may change with age [54, 55]. Schütteler et al. examined the interoceptive accuracy dimension of interoception, defined as the process of accurately detecting and monitoring internal bodily sensations, in children and adolescents with Tourette disorder. In their research, patients with tics exhibited no significant differences in interoceptive accuracy compared to healthy controls [56]. However, Ganos et al. evaluated interoceptive accuracy and found reduced performance on interoceptive tasks in adults with Tourette disorder compared to controls [57]. We believe that the distinct and dissociable nature of the interoceptive dimensions may be the cause of this discrepancy [14].

Recent research suggests that interoceptive processes may represent modifiable mechanisms in tic disorders, with interoceptive awareness potentially contributing to tic suppressibility [58, 59]. Interoceptive processes can be modulated through neural, behavioral, and psychological interventions, highlighting interoception as a potential target for symptom-focused treatment approaches across various clinical conditions [60]. From a clinical perspective, our findings suggest that interventions targeting interoceptive sensibility could be considered as complementary components in the clinical management of adolescents with tic disorders.

Our study found no relationship between the intensity of premonitory urges and the tic severity, contrary to our expectations. Similar to our study, Gulisano et al. found no correlation between premonitory urges and tic severity in their study on children and adolescents diagnosed with Tourette syndrome [61]. In contrast to our study, Kyriazi et al. found that the presence of premonitory urges was associated with tic severity in children and adolescents with tic disorders, especially in older children [62]. The fact that a significant proportion of the children in this study received a provisional tic disorder diagnosis may explain the contradictory findings. A recent meta-analysis reported a slight to moderate positive correlation between premonitory urge severity and tic symptoms, which may vary depending on the patients' ages [63]. Although premonitory urges and tics are closely linked in real time, previous studies have noted that their severity, as assessed by clinical scales, is not consistently associated [64]. We think that the different results regarding the relationship between premonitory urges and tic severity may be related to sample differences.

Our study demonstrated that the severity of premonitory urges increased in adolescents who were better able to recognize their bodily sensations, tended to focus more on these feelings, and listened to their bodies more. However, adolescents who tend not to distract themselves during disturbing bodily sensations experience a decrease in the severity of premonitory urges. Narapareddy et al.'s study, in which interoceptive sensibility was evaluated using the MAIA-2, found that increased bodily awareness was associated with premonitory urges in adults, similar to our study [53]. Schütteler and Gerlach analyzed interoceptive sensibility using the Body Perception Questionnaire and observed a parallel relationship between interoceptive sensibility and premonitory urges in young people [17]. The findings of our study regarding the relationship between interoceptive sensibility and premonitory urges seem generally consistent with literature regarding both adults and young people.

In our study, we found that adolescents with tic disorders had more difficulty identifying feelings. In addition, alexithymia was correlated with tic severity. In the literature, there are very few reports on alexithymia in tic disorders. Two studies conducted in children and adults showed that total alexithymia scores did not differ between individuals with tic disorders and controls [27, 28]. However, children with tic disorders scored higher on the externally oriented thinking subscale of the AQC scale in the Silvestri et al. study. Furthermore, difficulty in identifying feelings was correlated with premonitory urges and predicted their severity [28]. The fact that the study by Silvestri et al. focused only on males and the age range of 8–13 years may be related to the contradictory results. When interpreting the results of these studies, the presence of comorbidities such as anxiety and depression should be considered. These conditions frequently accompany tic disorders and may affect the results related to alexithymia [65, 66]. Overall, the current findings suggest that alexithymia may be an important factor in tic disorders and that further studies are needed.

Our study revealed that adolescents with tic disorders had more psychological problems than controls, and that these problems were the primary factor increasing the severity of their tics. Considering that tic disorders are frequently accompanied by behavioral and emotional problems, the findings of our study are as expected [67–69]. Similar to our findings, other studies have demonstrated an association between psychological problems and an increase in tic severity [70–72]. The findings of our study are consistent with literature.

The present study had a few limitations. Our study's cross-sectional nature hindered the full revelation of the cause-and-effect relationship. As the study was conducted in a single clinical center, the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Evaluation of alexithymia, interoceptive sensibility, and premonitory urges using self-report scales is another limitation of our study. Future studies should consider evaluating adolescents using multiple methods, such as interviews and scales, to assess these variables. Since there is no valid and reliable tool to measure interoceptive sensibility in younger age groups in our country, our study focused more on the adolescent age group. We suggest that the parameters investigated in future studies should target a wider age group. The relatively high rate of psychiatric comorbidities in the tic disorder group should be considered when interpreting the findings, as these conditions may influence emotional and interoceptive processes. Finally, the modest internal consistency of the AQC and SDQ in the current sample may have introduced measurement noise, potentially attenuating observed associations toward the null; therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

Our study emphasizes the relationship between tic severity and alexithymia, interoceptive sensibility, and psychological problems that adolescents with tic disorders may experience. It is thought that future studies investigating the effect of interventions on adolescents with tic disorders to increase interoceptive sensibility and emotional awareness on tic severity are needed.
