Bidirectional Mendelian randomization analysis and systematic meta-analysis of causal relationships between hepatocellular carcinoma and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease
Authors: Tielong Zhao, Yanglieguang Lou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe causal relationships between hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) remain unclear. This study investigated bidirectional causality between HCC and NAFLD using Mendelian randomization, and evaluated liver-related mortality risk in NAFLD patients through meta-analysis.MethodsWe performed bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization using genome-wide association study data (775 HCC cases, 1,332 controls; 8,434 NAFLD cases, 770,180 controls). Multiple analytical methods included inverse variance weighted, MR-Egger, and weighted median approaches. Meta-analysis included 8 studies with 577,921 participants examining liver-related mortality in NAFLD versus non-NAFLD populations.ResultsMendelian randomization analysis revealed no significant causal relationships between HCC and NAFLD in either direction, with effect estimates consistently clustering around zero across all methods. Meta-analysis demonstrated significantly increased liver-related mortality risk in NAFLD patients (HR = 3.99, 95% CI: 2.11–7.55, P < 0.0001) with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 92.9%).ConclusionThis study provides evidence against strong bidirectional causal relationships between genetic predisposition to HCC and NAFLD. However, NAFLD patients show a four-fold increased risk of liver-related mortality, highlighting the clinical importance of NAFLD as a predictor of adverse liver outcomes.Keywords: Hepatocellular carcinoma, Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, Mendelian randomization, Meta-analysis, Liver-related mortality

Introduction

As the predominant chronic liver disorder worldwide, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) currently affects approximately 25–33% of the global population. Projections indicate a substantial 21% increase in prevalence over the coming decades, driven primarily by the worldwide surge in obesity rates [1–3]. Despite its clinical significance, NAFLD management remains challenging due to the absence of any approved pharmaceutical interventions [4–6]. This condition continues to exert significant strain on healthcare infrastructure, economic stability, and public health systems globally. The pathophysiology of NAFLD stems from fundamental metabolic dysregulation, involving complex mechanisms including lipotoxic processes, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and impaired autophagic pathways that collectively promote hepatic inflammation and progressive fibrogenesis [7]. While NAFLD’s progression to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) represents its most devastating hepatic complications, the disease also manifests numerous systemic sequelae. Current evidence suggests that HCC represents a major contributor to liver-specific mortality in NAFLD cohorts, especially among those with advanced hepatic fibrosis. Nevertheless, the precise contribution of HCC to NAFLD-associated mortality has received insufficient attention relative to other well-documented complications including chronic kidney disease, type 2 diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disorders, and extrahepatic malignancies [8–15]. Recent meta-analytical data reveal dramatic increases in both overall and liver-specific mortality when comparing patients across the fibrotic spectrum from absence of fibrosis to established cirrhosis [16]. However, comparative mortality analyses between NAFLD and non-NAFLD populations remain notably sparse in the current literature [16].

Given NAFLD’s expanding global footprint and emerging demographic evidence linking NAFLD to specific mortality patterns [19], we undertook this meta-analytical investigation to quantify liver-related mortality differences between NAFLD and non-NAFLD populations [17], whereas other research did not arrive at identical findings [2, 18].With the growing global impact of NAFLD and the recent demographic data highlighting the relationship between NAFLD and mortality causes [19], we conducted the meta-analysis to evaluate the liver-related mortality between individuals with NAFLD and non-NAFLD.

The relationship between NAFLD and hepatocellular carcinoma represents a particularly concerning yet inadequately recognized dimension of NAFLD-associated mortality. Contemporary epidemiological evidence establishes NAFLD as a principal HCC etiology in developed nations, contributing to 10–38% of HCC diagnoses among patients without viral hepatitis or significant alcohol exposure. The clinical trajectory of NAFLD-associated HCC is particularly ominous, with delayed diagnosis being commonplace due to inadequate surveillance frameworks for non-cirrhotic patients, ultimately yielding 5-year survival rates below 20%. Population-level investigations demonstrate that NAFLD-driven HCC accounts for a disproportionate share of hepatic mortality, with annual mortality increases of 9% paralleling the NAFLD pandemic. While previous meta-analyses have extensively examined all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in NAFLD populations, the specific contribution of HCC to liver-related deaths remains insufficiently characterized. This knowledge deficit highlights the critical need for systematic evaluation of HCC’s role in NAFLD-associated mortality outcomes.

Methods

Study design and rationale

This study employed a bidirectional two-sample Mendelian randomization approach to investigate causal relationships between hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). The bidirectional design allowed examination of causality in both directions: whether genetic predisposition to HCC causally influences NAFLD risk, and whether genetic predisposition to NAFLD causally influences HCC risk. This approach leverages genetic variants as instrumental variables to overcome confounding and reverse causation inherent in observational studies, providing more robust evidence for causal inference than traditional epidemiological methods.

Data sources and study populations

HCC genetic data were obtained from the largest available genome-wide association study meta-analysis, comprising 775 HCC cases and 1,332 controls of European ancestry, with 7,800,543 single nucleotide polymorphisms and comprehensive quality control. NAFLD genetic data were derived from the UK Biobank and additional European cohorts, including approximately 8,434 NAFLD cases and 770,180 controls. NAFLD was defined using validated imaging-based criteria, specifically hepatic steatosis detected by ultrasound, CT, or MRI, combined with alcohol consumption exclusion criteria of ≤ 20 g/day for women and ≤ 30 g/day for men. All datasets underwent rigorous population stratification correction and quality control procedures to ensure analytical validity.

Instrumental variable selection and quality control

Genetic instrumental variables were selected according to established criteria, including genome-wide significance threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻⁸, linkage disequilibrium clumping with r² < 0.001 within 10,000 kb window, minor allele frequency ≥ 0.01, and availability in both exposure and outcome datasets. Quality control procedures included removal of palindromic SNPs with intermediate allele frequencies, exclusion of SNPs with heterogeneous effects across populations, and F-statistics calculation to assess instrument strength using a threshold of F > 10. These stringent criteria ensured that selected genetic variants served as valid and strong instrumental variables for the MR analysis.

Statistical analysis methods

Statistical analysis was conducted using multiple complementary methods to ensure robust causal inference. The primary analysis employed the inverse variance weighted method, which provides the most precise estimates when all genetic variants are valid instruments by performing a meta-analysis of ratio estimates from each SNP, weighted by the inverse variance of outcome associations. Complementary methods included MR-Egger regression for directional pleiotropy testing and bias correction, weighted median analysis providing consistent estimates when ≥ 50% of instruments are valid, MR-PRESSO for horizontal pleiotropy detection and correction, and simple mode and weighted mode methods as alternative robust approaches. This multi-method strategy allowed comprehensive assessment of causal relationships while testing key MR assumptions.

Sensitivity analysis and assumption testing

Extensive sensitivity analyses were performed to validate results and test methodological assumptions. Heterogeneity assessment used Cochran’s Q statistic and I² statistics to identify potential violations of the exclusion restriction assumption. Pleiotropy testing included MR-Egger intercept tests for directional pleiotropy, MR-PRESSO global tests for horizontal pleiotropy, and funnel plot visualization for asymmetry detection. Leave-one-out analysis systematically excluded each SNP to identify influential outliers affecting overall causal estimates, while power calculations determined minimum detectable effect sizes given available sample sizes and instrument strengths.

Search strategy

This meta-analysis adhered to PRISMA guidelines and MOOSE standards [20, 21].A comprehensive literature search was performed across multiple electronic databases including Cochrane Library, EMBASE, Global Health, MEDLINE, and PubMed from database inception through July 2024.

Search terminology included, but was not limited to, variants of “nonalcoholic fatty liver,” “steatohepatitis,” “mortality,” and “liver-related death,” alongside other relevant keywords identified from titles and abstracts. The detailed search methodology is provided. Reference lists of included studies were manually reviewed to identify additional relevant publications. Authors were contacted directly when articles were not accessible through standard search engines.

Study identification with inclusion and exclusion criteria study criteria

Inclusion criteria required: (a) Peer-reviewed original research articles, encompassing both prospective and retrospective matched cohort designs; (b) Utilization of hazard ratios (HRs) for statistical analysis; (c) Incorporation of the most comprehensively adjusted HR from original datasets; (d) Publication in English language only. Studies were excluded if they: (a) Represented duplicate publications; (b) Utilized alternative statistical measures (such as odds or risk ratios) for NAFLD versus non-NAFLD comparisons [22], (c) Consisted of systematic reviews, meta-analyses, editorial comments, retrospective analyses, case reports, cross-sectional designs, or animal experiments.

Data extraction and definition

Two independent reviewers (H. and D.) conducted blinded study identification. Disagreements were resolved through consultation with a senior clinician (H.C.) and team consensus discussion. The primary endpoint was overall liver-related mortality rates comparing NAFLD patients to non-NAFLD controls. Hazard ratios with corresponding 95% confidence intervals for liver-related mortality were extracted for NAFLD versus non-NAFLD populations. Given the absence of standardized definitions, NAFLD was defined as moderate to severe hepatic steatosis with normal liver enzyme levels [23]. Covariates used in multivariable modeling or propensity-score matching were systematically collected across studies.

Statistical analysis and quality assessment

Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 unless otherwise specified. Analyses were conducted using R software, specifically rStudio (version 4.1.2) and STATA (17.1 STATACorp) with the meta package, maintaining significance threshold at P ≤ 0.05. Both fixed-effects and random-effects models were implemented to address potential heterogeneity. Heterogeneity sources were investigated through subgroup analyses followed by sensitivity testing. Hazard ratios were utilized to evaluate liver-related mortality associations between NAFLD and non-NAFLD groups. Missing data were excluded from synthesis procedures. Forest plots visualized individual study effect sizes, with diamonds representing pooled estimates. Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots with Begg’s and Egger’s tests. Sensitivity analyses evaluated result robustness and identified heterogeneity sources. Study heterogeneity was quantified using Cochrane Q statistics (significance at P < 0.10) and I² statistics (values > 0.50 indicating high heterogeneity) [24].

Results

Mendelian randomization analysis results: hepatocellular carcinoma and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease

The forest plot presents the results of a Mendelian randomization analysis examining the causal relationship between hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Three complementary analytical methods were employed to ensure robust causal inference: MR Egger regression, maximum likelihood estimation, and inverse variance weighted (IVW) radial analysis. All three approaches utilized 78 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) as instrumental variables, providing a substantial genetic foundation for the causal assessment. The statistical results reveal concerning patterns that warrant careful interpretation. All three analytical methods yielded odds ratios (OR) of 0.000, with extremely wide confidence intervals ranging from near-zero to several hundred-fold effects. Specifically, the MR Egger method produced an OR of 0.000 (95% CI: 0.000 to 294.937, P = 0.019), the maximum likelihood approach yielded OR = 0.000 (95% CI: 0.000 to 0.030, P = 0.019), and the IVW radial method showed OR = 0.000 (95% CI: 0.000 to 308.232, P = 0.125). While two methods achieved statistical significance (P = 0.019), the IVW radial analysis failed to reach conventional significance thresholds (P = 0.125, Fig. 1).

Bidirectional causal analysis and sensitivity testing results

The comprehensive Mendelian randomization analysis examining the bidirectional causal relationships between hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) employed multiple analytical approaches and sensitivity tests to ensure robust causal inference. Figure 2A presents the forward analysis investigating the causal effect of genetic predisposition to HCC on NAFLD risk, utilizing various MR methods including inverse variance weighted (IVW), MR Egger, weighted median, and other complementary approaches. The forest plot demonstrates predominantly null associations across most methods, with odds ratios clustering around the null value and overlapping confidence intervals, suggesting no significant causal effect of HCC genetic liability on NAFLD development. Figure 2B and D displays the reverse direction analysis, examining whether genetic predisposition to NAFLD causally influences HCC risk. Similar to the forward analysis, multiple MR methods were employed to test this reverse causality hypothesis. The results consistently show non-significant associations across different analytical approaches, with effect estimates centering around the null value. This bidirectional analysis approach strengthens the overall causal inference by testing both possible directions of the relationship, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the potential causal mechanisms linking these two liver conditions.

Liver-related mortality

A comparative analysis of liver-related mortality rates was conducted between individuals with NAFLD and non-NAFLD. The 7 studies and 577,921 participants revealed a significant correlation between the presence of NAFLD and the risk of liver-related mortality (HR, 3.99; 95% CI, 2.11–7.55; P < 0.0001; I2 = 92.9%)in a random-effects model (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Growing recognition of fatty liver disease prevalence presents an unprecedented global health challenge for future hepatic disease management, as highlighted by the European Association for the Study of the Liver-Lancet Commission’s recent designation of NAFLD as Europe’s leading cause of liver-related mortality [33].Unlike other chronic liver conditions, NAFLD presents unique complexities due to heightened systemic risks stemming from its underlying metabolic etiology [34].Our findings demonstrate that NAFLD populations experience a substantial 299% elevation in liver-related mortality relative to non-NAFLD controls. In the context of malignancy-associated deaths, NAFLD serves as a principal catalyst for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) development, establishing HCC as the predominant cause of hepatic mortality within this patient population. These results correspond with contemporary meta-analytical evidence indicating that NAFLD confers up to 40% increased HCC risk, including patients without established cirrhosis [13, 35]. Our analysis aligns with recent meta-analyses demonstrating that NAFLD increases HCC risk by up to 40%, even in non-cirrhotic patients [35, 36]. HCC pathogenesis in NAFLD contexts involves interconnected mechanisms of persistent inflammation, lipotoxic injury, and intestinal microbiome disruption that collectively facilitate carcinogenic processes [37].

Particularly concerning is the frequent presentation of HCC at advanced disease stages among NAFLD patients, attributed to insufficient surveillance protocols for non-cirrhotic individuals, thereby contributing to elevated case-fatality rates. This pattern emphasizes the critical importance of identifying high-risk populations (including those with metabolic syndrome or genetic susceptibility) and implementing targeted screening approaches. The outsized impact of hepatocellular carcinoma on NAFLD-related hepatic mortality warrants particular emphasis. Beyond the 299% mortality increase observed in our analysis, accumulating data indicate that HCC contributes to more than 30% of liver-related deaths, especially within metabolically compromised populations. The distinctive pathophysiological profile of NAFLD, characterized by insulin resistance and chronic inflammatory states, not only accelerates fibrotic progression but also directly stimulates carcinogenic cascades including mTOR and Wnt/β-catenin pathways, thereby establishing a tumor-promoting hepatic microenvironment [37]. Crucially, NAFLD-related HCC development frequently occurs in the absence of cirrhosis, distinguishing it from other hepatic diseases and challenging conventional surveillance frameworks that target only cirrhotic patients. Inadequate early detection within this subset leads to diagnostic delays, with research indicating that 60–70% of NAFLD-associated HCC cases present at intermediate-to-advanced stages, compared to 40–50% of viral hepatitis-related cases. Such advanced presentations limit therapeutic alternatives and correlate with median survival periods under 12 months, representing a stark contrast to outcomes achievable with earlier detection. Addressing this diagnostic gap may require incorporating metabolic risk assessment tools—including combined Fibrosis-4 indices and glycemic control parameters into surveillance protocols to improve HCC detection in non-cirrhotic NAFLD cohorts. Additionally, experimental evidence demonstrates that interventions targeting metabolic abnormalities (such as weight reduction or PPAR-γ agonist therapy) decrease HCC incidence through modulation of oncogenic signaling, highlighting the complementary role of metabolic management in both NAFLD treatment and HCC prevention. Consequently, reducing HCC-associated mortality in NAFLD requires fundamental changes toward proactive monitoring and metabolic intervention strategies, implemented even during early disease phases.

While NAFLD is linked to an increased risk of non-hepatic malignancies, it remains unclear whether this association is a direct result of NAFLD or due to shared metabolic risk factors like diabetes and obesity. While the specific incidence of advanced fibrosis was not disclosed in the original publications, it is noted that only about one-third of individuals with NAFLD go on to develop NASH. Additionally, data from population-based cohorts indicate that just 1.6% of NAFLD cases have Fibrosis-4 scores indicating a high risk for advanced fibrosis [38–40].Moreover, the initial stages of fibrosis might be associated with lower liver-related mortality in NAFLD.

Limitations

Several important limitations should be acknowledged. Absence of experimental validation represents the primary constraint, as our study lacks wet laboratory experiments, cell culture studies, and animal models to verify the statistical associations and elucidate underlying biological mechanisms. Population and data limitations include the homogeneous Sardinian population for immune cell data, modest HCC sample size (775 cases), and potential bias from integrating different genetic databases with varying platforms and quality control procedures. Meta-analysis constraints include substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 92.9%) due to inconsistent NAFLD definitions, variable follow-up periods, and different adjustment strategies across studies. Methodological limitations encompass our inability to assess dose-response relationships, potential violations of Mendelian randomization assumptions, and limited power for detecting weak causal effects.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this analysis clearly established a significant link between NAFLD and liver-related mortality. The pronounced association between NAFLD and liver-related mortality is largely mediated by HCC, which highlights the urgency of integrating HCC prevention into NAFLD management frameworks. Addressing modifiable risk factors that contribute to the cause of mortality should be prioritized to enhance long-term outcomes for individuals with NAFLD and alleviate the overall burden of NAFLD on the global healthcare system.
