Catalyzing innovation: exploring the potential of eco-friendly synthesized magnetic iron oxide nanoparticles for heavy metals removal from water
Authors: Nayab Raza, Aneela Anwar, Amna Shoaib, Shahzeb Khan, Azeem Intisar, Muhammad Salman Habib, Faisal Irshad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIron oxide nanoparticles (IONs) due to its properties have taken over the applications to remove industrial waste, can treat wastewater, and catalyze reactions. The emerging green nanotechnology has emerged as a sustainable and eco-friendly approach for synthesizing IONs. The most effective iron oxide nanoparticles with excellent magnetic, photocatalytic, and adsorption properties, as compared to conventional physical and chemical methods. This review covers the eco-friendly synthesis of IONs utilizing biomaterials such as bacteria, fungi, plants, and algae. The study broadly investigates magnetic behavior, surface functionalization, and contact areas of IONs with diverse biocompatible coatings like silica, PEG, PVA, organosilane, and chitosan etc. Furthermore, the impact of size, shape, and morphology with intrinsic features on the magnetic properties of the nanoparticles is thoroughly discussed. Additionally, it highlights the practical advantages of these biosynthesized IONs, emphasizing their easy separation and reusability for heavy metal removal, and concludes by summarizing key findings and outlining promising future research directions in this field. The literature studies have shown excellent adsorption capacities of IONs for heavy metals under optimal pH, along with good magnetic separability and reusability, providing an efficient and sustainable approach for both rare earth and heavy metal removal from aqueous systems. This review focuses on the green synthesis, functionalization, and reusability of low-cost coated IONs for efficient toxic element removal. It also stresses the need to enhance their durability, eco-compatibility, and real-world application.Graphical abstract Keywords: Sustainable nanomaterials, Plant-mediated synthesis, Surface functionalization, Heavy metal adsorption, Water purification, Superparamagnetic nanoparticles

Introduction

The rapid growth of industries such as chemical, pharmaceutical, agricultural, and textile has contributed significantly to environmental pollution, adversely affecting soil, aquatic systems, and overall ecosystem health [1]. Water contamination from various pollutants, primarily heavy metals, are extremely toxic and can have major health consequences [2]. The following heavy metals are commonly found in water: cadmium (Cd), zinc (Zn), chromium (Cr), cobalt (Co), arsenic (As), nickel (Ni), mercury (Hg), and lead (Pb). Their discharge is considered a severe threat to the environment owing to its hazardous and non-degradable properties. According to the World Health Organisation, the heavy metal accumulation and their toxic exposure cause several toxic effects, particularly disrupting plant life and overall ecological balance [3]. Therefore, it is essential to clean contaminated water to protect aquatic life and human health [4].

Previous research has reported recent advances in dangerous contaminants removal from aqueous solutions by utilizing a range of methodologies, including ion exchange, enhanced oxidation processes, membrane/ electro technologies, and Fenton-based processes [5]. Although there are several physical and chemical methods like chemical precipitation, electrochemical treatment, electrodialysis, evaporative recovery and ion-exchange that have been employed for heavy metal removal and adsorption has gained widespread attention due to its simplicity, easy accessibility, low cost and less sludge generation [3]. The modeling of iron oxide can be attained by multiple methods in which most important is density functional theory (DFT) for the applications of drug delivery systems [6]. However, certain unavoidable restrictions prevent their commercialization, and the majority of therapies are not cost-effective, with some being worthless at lower doses. These methods are also related to high operational and maintenance expenses, inadequate metal removal, high energy consumption, and the formation of toxic residual metal sludge, raising disposal problems [7]. Adsorption is preferable to alternative technologies for water treatment due to numerous reasons. It is an easy, quick, cost-effective, less toxic, easy to create/recycle, and broadly applicable technology [8]. The development of sustainable and affordable adsorbents has sparked interest in this field of study. Adsorbents recently utilized in water treatment, such as metal–organic frameworks and carbon-based compounds, clay, zeolite, magnetic nanosized particles, and quartz gel, can clean up water contaminants [6].

Nanotechnology is a rapidly expanding scientific subject that functions on particles that range in size from 1 to 100 nm while overcoming current technology limitations. New functional materials and composites have been developed by nanotechnology and these materials are now being employed in biomedicine, manufacturing, energy generation, storage, electronics, and environmental applications [9]. Their crystallinity, geometry, and enormous surface area to volume ratio, give them special characteristics like electrical, magnetic, physicochemical, etc. [10].

Magnetic materials are increasingly employed in the treatment of water to remove contaminants [11] and have numerous advantages, including affordability, regenerability, reusability, and ease of separation, making them suitable for water purification. For removing pollutants, magnetic adsorbents offer a simple and low-maintenance substitute for conventional adsorbents. IONs show significant potential as nano adsorbents for wastewater treatment [12, 13]. The IONs adsorbents offer numerous advantages, including stability, high adsorption capacity, and reusing ability [14–17]. Additionally, they are superparamagnetic, which facilitates their separation from the matrix with the use of an external magnetic field [18].

Hematite (α-Fe2O3), maghemite (γ-Fe2O3), and magnetite (Fe3O4) are the three primary iron oxides that are classified as IOs. Researchers are increasingly using IONs to solve environmental problems, as they are efficient in removing a huge range of pollutants from the water, soil, and air through photo-degradation and adsorption [7]. They are better adsorbing agents than traditional adsorbents because of their high reactivity and enhanced catalytic properties. It also improves their interactions with other particles, particularly metal ions, as they have more active sites. They are attracted to an external magnetic field but lose it when it is removed, making their recovery more convenient and easy. They are the most suitable candidate materials to be employed as adsorbents in an easy, and fast chemical species separation [19]. Further, dispersion, size, shape, and crystallinity also make IONs a good alternative for selective adsorption and metal binding processes. As a result, they have been effectively employed as sorbent materials to remove harmful metals from water sources [20]. Biological, chemical, enzymatic, and physical techniques were utilized to synthesize nanomaterials. Physical techniques include plasma arcing, ball milling, lithographic techniques, thermal evaporation, layer-by-layer growth, pulsed laser desorption, spray pyrolysis, sputter deposition, ultrathin films, molecular beam epistaxis, and diffusion flame synthesis [21]. Chemical techniques include the sol–gel process, electro-deposition, chemical solution deposition, hydrolysis, wet chemical method, chemical vapor deposition, Langmuir–Blodgett method, catalytic route, soft chemical method, and co-precipitation [22, 23]. Biomodified IONs were preferred over chemically modified ones due to several significant advantages. This eco-friendly approach relies on renewable and readily available biological resources such as plant extracts, fungi, diatoms, bacteria, algae, and enzymes for both nanoparticle synthesis and stabilization [24–26]. By minimizing the use of hazardous chemicals and reducing energy consumption, green synthesis substantially lowers the generation of toxic waste and its environmental impact. Furthermore, this method offers more cost-effective, enhanced biocompatibility and reduced toxicity, making them particularly suitable for biomedical and environmental applications [27, 28]. Surface modification plays a crucial role in overcoming one of their main aggregation problem of IONs. By altering surface properties, aggregation is minimized, leading to enhanced stability and adsorption efficiency of the nanoparticles. Additionally, their magnetic properties allow easy separation from aqueous systems using an external magnetic field [26, 29–31]. Surface coating can occur during or post-synthesis, either in situ or by encapsulation, promoting features such as end-grafting, hyper-branching, and hydrophobic interactions, resulting in more hydrophilic nanostructures. Plant extracts are extensively utilized as both coating and reducing agents due to their safe, widely available nature and rich metabolite content, which help in ion reduction and also improve the multidimensional absorption of various organic pollutants and inorganic ions found in polluted water, as illustrated in Fig. 1 [32–34]. In addition to natural compounds, various synthetic chemical agents have also been used to modify the nanoparticle surfaces, increasing their compatibility, dispersibility, and surface reactivity. These modifications support the creation of multifunctional and multimodal IONs, as illustrated in Fig. 2 [35].

Common chemical substances that connect polymers and functional groups on the nanoparticle surface

Various analytical techniques are employed to characterize these nanoparticles, including Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR), Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method, Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS), Scanning and Transmission Electron Microscopy (SEM/TEM), X-ray Diffraction, X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy, Raman Spectroscopy, Vibrating Sample Magnetometer (VSM), and Thermal Gravimetric Analysis (TGA). These techniques help measure particle size, shape, surface charge, and magnetic properties, which are crucial for separating nanoparticles from water. Modified types of IONs have demonstrated promising results and can be reused multiple times following desorption [36]. Regeneration involves permanent magnets but can result in particle loss. Advanced methods like thermo-enhanced magnetic recovery, ultrafiltration, and electromagnets are appropriate for extensive applications. Chemical treatments like EDTA can also be used for regeneration [37].

The details of the study reported here describe an innovative, eco-friendly synthesis and surface functionalization of magnetic IONs using various biomaterials and coating agents to enhance heavy metal removal from water. The use of polyphenol-rich biomaterials improves adsorption efficiency while minimizing secondary pollution, offering a sustainable alternative to conventional methods. The study further investigated the particle size, shape, and surface characteristics influence magnetic behavior, using advanced characterization techniques (FT-IR, TEM, UV-Vis, XRD, SEM, EDX). Previous studies have reported the use of modified nanomaterials for wastewater treatment [3], but this work offers an eco-friendlier and more sustainable alternative with superior performance. Kinetic and thermodynamic analyses were performed to study the adsorption mechanism, emphasizing the key parameters for effective remediation [1]. Compared to previous studies, the developed nanocomposite system provides significantly improved dispersion, magnetic separability, stability, and adsorption capacity. Additionally, these multifunctional nanomaterials show strong potential for applications in drug delivery, catalysis, biosensing, and environmental monitoring [38].

Magnetic nanoparticles

Magnetic nanoparticles (MIONs) have gained significant attention nowadays due to their various desirable properties, which can include superparamagnetism, tiny size, easy production process, chemical stability, biocompatibility, high yields, environmental friendliness, and recyclable nature [39]. They are non-reactive to humans, have an efficient adsorption capacity, a high ratio of surface area to volume, and low toxicity [40–42]. The transition metal oxides, specifically Fe2O3 and Fe3O4, are among the most significant MIONs due to their broad applicability in many industries such as biomedicine, electrical devices, catalysts, water and sewage treatment, and good magnetic characteristics, as well as their chemical composition. Their use in the water treatment sector has grown in importance among the existing technologies for the quick removal of impurities through adsorption or breakdown processes. The capacity to reuse magnetic nanomedicines several times and their simple recovery from reaction mixtures via an external magnetic field are additional advantages [43–45].

Iron oxide and its forms

Iron is the most common transition material, which includes the majority of the land surface with numerous applications [46]. A frequent anion shape is cubic or hexagonal, and iron oxide vacuoles are half-filled with divalent or trivalent iron [12]. Researchers in nanoscience and nanotechnology are increasingly interested in IO [47, 48]. Iron-based materials in the treatment of water have various benefits, such as magnetic properties, affordability, environmentally benign, high adsorption capacity, and strong reactivity [49, 50]. IONs have a higher adsorption capacity, surface reactivity, lower toxicity, catalytic effectiveness, and magnetic characteristics. Biocompatibility, superparamagnetism, and ultrafine size are all factors contributing to this. By altering the reaction conditions, biological approaches can produce three different IONs with intriguing features. The unique properties and nanosize of iron nanoparticles have led to their development as a new technology and their great efficiency in various applications. Zero-valent nanoparticles are often synthesized using physical or chemical processes. They easily oxidize in the presence of humidity and air. They are efficient bioremoval adsorbents of contaminants. Magnetite exhibits minimal toxicity, antibacterial activity, and biocompatibility [51].

The literature employs sixteen distinct IO phase types; the most common iron-based nano adsorbents, are magnetite, haematite, and maghemite [12, 52]. These three iron oxides exhibit distinct biochemical, magnetic, catalytic, and biological properties. Goethite (α-FeOOH), a fourth phase, has a high surface area of over 200 m2/g, making it suitable for water treatment [53]. Various magnetic and physical properties of different forms of IOs

Haematite is environmentally benign, biocompatible, nontoxic, most stable, and corrosion-resistant [56]. However, the stability of haematite reduces as temperature rises and particle size decreases [57]. This means that micro and macro-haematite show more stability than nanoscale haematite. In a hexagonal tightly packed lattice, haematite contains O2 anions, and Fe3+ cations in octahedral locations [58], giving it a crystallized rhombohedral system [59]. The indirect charge transfer band gap energy of this n-type semiconductor is between 1.9 and 2.0 eV [60]. However, hematite’s anti-ferromagnetic properties limit its use in the majority of environmental processes [61]. Under oxidizing conditions, it can withstand high temperatures. It can be transformed into maghemite or magnetite, under reducing conditions with temperature above 350 °C [62].

Maghemite reflects the similarity of its structure by combining the structure and composition of hematite and magnetite [34]. Maghemite comprises 32 O2− oxide ions, and 21 1/3 Fe+3 ions, with empty spaces in a spinel structure [59]. Oxygen ions are organized in cubic close-packed configurations, while Fe+3 tetrahedral sites (eight Fe+3 ions per unit cell) and octahedral sites (the remaining Fe+3 ions and vacancies) [63]. With a 2.0 eV band gap energy, maghemite is an n-type semiconductor that is fully oxidized magnetite [46]. Compared to magnetite, the stability of maghemite is higher in acidic environments, making it more suitable for use in certain severe situations [64]. Magnetite has a cubic inverse spinel structure, containing trivalent and divalent iron with face-centered cubic oxygen anions, and Fe cations in interstitial tetrahedral and octahedral sites [59, 65]. Fe 2 + 3 + species prefer octahedral positions, whereas Fe-cations are evenly dispersed across octahedral and tetrahedral sites [61]. Magnemite and magnetite are both ferrimagnetic, although magnetite has a large magnetic saturation (~ 92–200 emu/g) compared to maghemite (~ 80 emu/g) [61]. Magnetite is the most often used phase for water treatment due to its large magnetization saturation, which permits regenerability after photodegradation/adsorption procedures.

Goethite is the yellow, antiferromagnetic IO phase with an orthorhombic crystal structure, and it is the most common and stable IO phase found in rocks and soil [66, 67]. With the sixfold coordinated Fe atoms occupying the octahedral position, the orthorhombic structure of iron atoms has six octahedral neighbors, including oxygen and hydroxyl, in perfect hexagonal close-packing [68]. Goethite has a 2.1–2.5 eV band gap energy [69]. Su et al. found that α-FeOOH is more stable than other iron oxide phases under aerobic circumstances, despite its weak crystalline structure [70, 71]. Goethite can lose its hydroxyl group and become haematite at temperatures ranging from 140 to 350 °C [72, 73]. At temperatures above 350 °C (Gontijo C) and with a reducing agent, goethite transforms into magnetite [62].

Magnetic behavior of IONs

The magnetic properties of IONs play a critical role in their practical applications, such as water treatment, where magnetic separation, recovery, and targeted delivery are essential. As illustrated in Fig. 4, materials can exhibit different magnetic behaviors such as paramagnetism, ferromagnetism, and superparamagnetism, based on their atomic dipole responses to external magnetic fields. When an external magnetic field strikes a paramagnetic material, its atomic dipoles align with the field, resulting in paramagnetism; however, once the field is removed, the material’s magnetization almost completely disappears, and the atomic dipoles can rotate freely. In contrast, ferromagnetic materials retain their magnetic properties without an external field. When subjected to a magnetic field, ferromagnetic materials’ unpaired electrons align their spins with the field, and this alignment persists even after the removal of the magnetic force [34].

Superparamagnetic materials exhibit a unique combination of both ferromagnetic and paramagnetic properties [76]. In these materials, magnetic dipoles align with an applied magnetic field, but due to the influence of thermal energy, their magnetization can spontaneously reorganize, particularly in nanoparticles, even if they were previously aligned with the magnetic field. Superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticles (SPIONs), which are made of magnetite (Fe3O4) or maghemite (Fe2O3), typically have core diameters ranging from 10 to 100 nm and are classified as single-domain particles with remarkable magnetic properties. These particles display superparamagnetism, a high saturation field, additional anisotropy contributions, and the ability to function effectively in high-field environments. These unique magnetic behaviors of IONs make them highly versatile for a wide range of applications including wastewater treatment (for magnetic separation and pollutant removal), biomedical imaging (such as MRI contrast agents), targeted drug delivery, and catalytic processes. Their adjustable magnetic properties enhance efficiency, reusability, and selective targeting, making IONs highly attractive in both environmental and biomedical fields.

The magnetic and physicochemical properties of IONs are strongly influenced by their size and shape. They exhibit diverse morphologies, including spherical, cubic, rod-like, or octahedral, each imparting unique characteristics. The particle size plays a key role in determining magnetic behavior, while shape anisotropy affects the orientation of the magnetic moment and saturation values. Synthesis of magnetic nanoparticles

MIONs can be synthesized in two ways: Top-down and Bottom-up as shown in Fig. 5.

Nanoparticle synthesis techniques

Top-down approach

In the Top-down approach, the structure size is reduced to the nanoscale. It breaks down bulk materials into tiny nanosized particles. Top-down methods are simple to employ and include physical processes like lithography [81], high-mechanical grinding [82], thermal evaporation, sputtering, ball milling, plasma arcing, etching, and attrition aerosols [83].

Bottom-up approach

This approach is regarded as a constructive methodology. It is opposed to the top-down approach and constructs large nanostructures by using smaller atoms and molecules. The major components required for bottom-up NP synthesis include metal precursors, solvents, reducing and stabilizing agents, and wet chemical methods involving sol–gel, co-precipitation [11], hydrothermal, electrodeposition, and physical vapor deposition [84–87], as shown in Fig. 6 [88].

Eco-friendly synthesis of IONs

Plants, bacteria, fungi, algae, polysaccharides, seaweed, and biodegradable polymers using eco-friendly green techniques are used to synthesize nanoparticles sustainably. Plant extracts were used as the primary biomaterials, because of their capacity to stabilize nanoparticles containing a variety of biomolecules. As a result, extremely stable nanoparticles can be created with no hazardous chemicals, pollutants, or byproducts [90].

Plant biomaterials

Plant materials such as extracts, and biomass, can be employed to synthesize nanoparticles. Black tea, green tea, and oolong tea with their extracts were frequently employed in producing IONs. These show good catalytic efficiency in a variety of natural processes. As a reducing agent, agro waste may also be used in this process. At room temperature, other plant species were also used as stabilizing agents including Colocasia esculenta, Sorghum sp., Terminalia chebula, Eucalyptus globulus, pomegranate, grape seed, Tridax procumbens, Dodonaea viscosa, plantain peel, and others, (Table 3). The hydrothermal technique involves an autoclave containing a biomaterial mixture and precursor salt for a specific time interval with various temperature settings and atmospheric pressure conditions. This approach was successful in synthesizing iron-based nanomaterials from green tea and aloe vera. To prevent agglomeration, plant biomaterial is a renewable, affordable, and high source of carbon that works well as a support material. This can be accomplished by creating iron-based nanostructures with the required shape using a template [91]. Basave gowda et al. employed Artemisia annua leaf extract to create spherical iron nanoparticles with an average size of 6 nm. They used Fe3O4 nanoparticles as a biocatalyst to produce benzoxazinone and benzthioxazinone derivatives [92].

Algae

Seaweeds are plant-like creatures, also known as macroalgae, composed of polysaccharides, physiologically active proteins, and other components. They have medical applications for ailments such as diabetes, allergies, and cancer. Algae have bioactive carboxyl and hydroxyl amino acids that can reduce precursors and stabilize synthesized nanoparticles, making them a promising tool for the sustainable synthesis of metal nanoparticles [116]. El-Kassas et al. synthesized IONs from FeCl3 precursor solution using two brown seaweeds: Thivy Sargassum acinarium (Linnaeus) and Padina pavonica (Linnaeus). At 402 and 415 nm, SPR peaks were observed. Particle diameters varied from 10 to 19.5 nm and 21.6 to 26 nm. According to the FTIR analysis, algae include sulfated polysaccharides, as a capping agent in the eco-friendly IONs production [116]. Shalaby et al. employed Spirulina platensis microalgae and investigated the elimination of crystal violet (CV) and anionic methyl orange (MO) from water. They used Langmuir adsorption across several cycles to produce pseudo-second-order kinetics [117]. Sheekh et al. used brown seaweed aqueous extract of Colpomenia sinuosa, Padina pavonica, and Petalonia fascia for iron nanoparticle production by reducing ferric chloride. Their research emphasized reducing excess nutrients like phosphorus, nitrogen, and toxic algae, which lead to ocean pollution. FT-IR spectra revealed that algal extracts contain proteins and lipids that can reduce FeCl3 and stabilize nanoparticles. They measured the chlorophyll-a concentrations and optical density of seawater samples and found that phosphorus removal and chlorophyll reduction were most effective [118]. Sakineh Mashjoor et al. employed the macroalga Ulva flexuosa (wulfen) J.Agardh for the fabrication of Fe3O4 nanoparticles by using ferric chloride reduction. They obtained good antifungal and antibacterial efficiency for bio-route and chemo tests of prepared nanoparticles in brackish water rotifers [119, 120]. Kasivelu et al. conducted a thorough evaluation of studies on the production of nanoparticles from seaweed species.

Various macroalgae, concentrations, and experimental circumstances produce nanomaterials of varying shapes and sizes. Algae have a lower synthesis rate than plants [121, 122]. Microorganisms

Fungi, bacteria, and mushrooms accumulate metal ions on their surface and create extracellular or internal enzymes that aid in iron nanoparticle formation. Proteins and peptides are produced by microorganisms that prevent agglomeration and act as stabilizers [105]. Without rupturing the cell wall in a microorganism culture medium, iron nanoparticles may be synthesized extracellularly. It is determined by the following factors: temperature, pH, pCO2, pO2, redox potential, etc. Their metabolic activity causes them to release metabolic products into the solution, which form nanoparticles by reacting with the precursor salt solution. Using microorganisms, iron oxide nanoparticles can be synthesized by reducing Fe (III) or sulfate. At high pH, bio-reduction is favored by the transport of electrons via redox reactions involving bacterial NADH and cytochromes.

Bacteria

IONs are often synthesised employing a variety of microorganisms, especially bacteria. The sample is cultured on a medium such as BH1, blood agar, or nutrient agar and incubated at 37 °C for 24 to 48 h. It is then moved to a nutrient-rich media, such as MRS broth, nutritive broth, or Luria broth, where it receives further nourishment after growing. The microbial biomass was then removed through incubation and centrifugation. After centrifugation, the most crucial step in ION production starts with either supernatant [129–131]. The use of bacteria in IONP biosynthesis has several advantages, including eco-friendliness, regulated particle characteristics, and biocompatibility (Table 5). Fe (III) reducing bacteria may produce 5000 times more magnetite per unit of biomass as compared to magnetotactic bacteria [132]. Arpit et al. used Aspergillus japonicus isolate AJP01 to produce iron oxide nanoparticles extracellularly by bio-reducing iron cyanide complexes at room temperature. The produced magnetite nanoparticles were 60–70 nm in size and were cubic. FTIR revealed the protein existence that offers stability [133]. Actinomycetes and prokaryotic bacteria are often used microorganisms for metal oxide synthesis. However, significant disadvantages of this technology include the difficulties of optimization, and impurity issues, and as compared to chemical processes, it is slower. This approach must address product quality variability and regulatory implications. While investigating new avenues in ION biosynthesis using bacteria, it is necessary to optimize microbial strains, precursor proportions, and culture environments to boost production and regulate the size of the particle. Examining different bacterial species and their metabolic processes may produce IONs with unique properties [134].

The study focuses on improving fungal strains, using eco-friendly growing media, and enhancing culture conditions for ION synthesis, with possible applications in targeted drug administration and environmentally friendly nanomaterial production.

Mushrooms

Iron nanoparticles can be synthesized from edible fruiting bodies produced by macroscopic fungi (Basidiomycota and Ascomycetes phyla). The ability of fungi to synthesize enzymes and biomolecules is valuable, allowing for the production of environmentally acceptable and cost-effective nanoparticles. The unique chemical composition of fungi-derived NPs opens up new possibilities for use in medicine, catalysis, and environmental solutions [150]. Macrophageal fruiting bodies contain enzymes, nutrients, and proteins that act as stabilization agents, allowing them to produce nanoparticles by decreasing metal salts. This enzyme activity will enable them to produce large amounts of biomass and accumulate nanoparticles in their environment. Bioactive chemicals found in mushrooms have a high potential for medical applications, including beta-glucans, phenolic compounds, flavonoids, triterpenoids, lectin, steroids, glycopeptides, terpenes, and saponin. Fungal-mediated nanoparticles can be produced in vitro and in vivo, with most potentially hazardous transition metal ions transformed into nontoxic forms.

Mushroom-mediated nanoparticles can be created in vitro from fungal cell-free extracts, fermented fungus supernatant, cell wall rupture, or fungal mycelium extract, with varied sizes and high crystalline content. They can be monodisperse or polydisperse [150]. Mushroom-derived IONs have superior stability, an extended shelf life, and more biological capabilities in medication administration, water treatment, metal ion sensors, dye degradation, and agricultural and biosensing applications that improve harvest output. Several investigations have shown the production of nanoparticles utilizing mushrooms as shown in (Table 7). However, there is still room to improve our understanding of iron nanoparticle formation in mushroom mycelia. Investigating innovative research trajectories in creating IONs with mushrooms provides opportunities for creative advancement. Researchers can enhance the selection of mushroom varieties to reduce and stabilize functions, modify NP properties by optimizing growth conditions, and explore the molecular processes that drive biosynthesis.

IONs and their surface functionalization

Surface functionalization of IONs involves modifying their surface with various agents to enhance their performance across multiple applications. Green-synthesized IONs, such as γ-FeO3 or FeO4, are increasingly favored in biomedical and environmental applications due to their eco-friendly production and excellent biocompatibility. The application of a coating agent significantly influences the nanoparticles’ physicochemical characteristics, improving stability by avoiding agglomeration caused by high surface energy, and modifying surface charge to enhance colloidal stability and biological interactions [159–164]. These coatings also introduce functional groups that enable further functionalization, such as loading or the attachment of targeting ligands, increasing their potential in medicinal or diagnostic applications [77, 165, 166]. Moreover, IONs are protected against oxidation and denaturation by a protective covering, which is typically carbon, noble metals, or polymers. Creating a core–shell structure with tailored functional moieties remains one of the most effective approaches for maximizing ION performance.

The latest research into the surface functionalization of IONs has significantly expanded their applications in water treatment, notably pollution removal. Modified IONs are used in numerous applications, including heavy metals, dyes, and other hazardous compounds from water systems. The distinctive characteristics, including huge surface area, recovery, and stability, serve as a promising alternative for eliminating toxins from aqueous solutions, maintaining public health, and ensuring everyone’s access to clean water [167]. Numerous research in the existing publications has shown different types of ligands and molecules, like albumin, oleic acid, silica (excellent biocompatibility, high thermal stability, non-toxic) [168, 169], PEG, PAA, PVP, PVA, PLGA [77, 78], organosilane, gelatin, lactic acid, starch, DMSA, carbon, alginate, casein, dendrimers, citric acid [170], Methacrylic acid (MAA) (increases coated particle dispersion in biological systems) [168], aluminum oxide Al(OH)3 coating. Chitosan is an excellent candidate for coating IONs due to its high reactivity, cheap availability, outstanding chelation behavior, low cost, and good specificity for chromium metals [20, 33]. Sharifi et al. discovered that Chitosan, Fe3O4 a highly efficient nanoadsorbent for heavy metals removal, notably Co2+ and Ni2+, from aqueous solution [171]. Adsorption experiments show 53 mg/g adsorption capacities for Co2+ and 30 mg/g for Ni2+ under optimum parameters (2 h contact time, 2 g/L dosage of adsorbent, 6 pH). A hybrid IONs constructed of polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) displayed adsorption against the metal ions Cu+2, Hg+2, Co+2, and Pb+2 for surface modification of IONs [79].

Modifying the IONs surface typically includes three methods: Ligand exchange, Ligand addition, and Encapsulation (Fig. 6) [172, 173]. The process of attaching a new ligand to the outer surface of IONs without removing the old one is known as ligand addition [174, 175]. The ligand may be attached directly, interact hydrophobically, adsorbed onto its surface, or covalently attached to the surface. Unlike the Ligand exchange approach, the initial non-polar ligand is replaced with strong interacting polar ligands [176]. Smolensky et al. reported that IONs are hydrophobic and may be produced by thermal breakdown and substituted with polar ligands as MRI contrast agents [177]. To obtain nanoparticles that are compatible with water, encapsulation has been suggested. Furthermore, it is covered with amphiphilic compounds, and the hydrophobic part binds with the preceding ligand on the substrate while the hydrophilic component migrates into the solution’s surface. Additionally, the functional group can aid in bio-modification or bio-conjugation [174]. IONs, with their large surface area, magnetic properties, and surface modification capabilities, are gaining popularity in water treatment due to their versatility in physical and chemical techniques.

Different methods of characterization

The characterization of IONs is pivotal in determining their efficacy for water treatment applications. Key factors such as surface functional groups, crystallinity, shape, size of the particle, charge on the surface, elemental composition, thermal stabilization, oxidation, state, morphology, and magnetic property directly influence their performance in contaminant adsorption and removal. Due to their significance, such attributes can be measured using a variety of methods, the most popular of which are discussed in this section. The accuracy of these approaches is determined by the sample purity and the appropriate preparation processes.

Numerous methods for characterization have been employed for comprehensive analysis, including FT-IR spectroscopy, UV–visible spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), Scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy dispersive X-ray analysis (EDX), atomic force microscopy (AFM), zeta potential analysis, vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM), Sp-GAC, dynamic light scattering (DLS), BET, TGA, Raman spectroscopy and magnetometry (SQUID) [12, 20, 33, 34, 168]. Atomic force microscopy (AFM), dynamic light scattering (DLS), X-ray diffraction (XRD), Field emission scattering electron microscopy (FESEM), Raman spectroscopy (RS), Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), Photon correlation spectroscopy (PCS), high-resolution condensation particle counter (CPC) and transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) provide insights into particle size, shape, and morphology. DLS assesses hydrodynamic size and polydispersity index in colloidal solutions [178], while FTIR and Raman spectroscopy identify functional groups and molecular vibrations [179, 180]. XRD and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) analyze crystalline structure [46, 181, 182], whereas X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Mössbauer spectroscopy assess oxidation states and elemental composition [183–186]. BET evaluates surface area and porosity [187]. Thermal stability and decomposition patterns are analyzed using TGA and TGA–DSC [188, 189]. Magnetic properties such as saturation magnetization and coercivity are examined via VSM [182], SQUID, and electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR), which help confirm superparamagnetic behavior. TEM and SEM are utilized to demonstrate the size, aggregation, heterogeneity, and degree of dispersion of IONs [190]. SEM is the most commonly utilized method due to its ease of operation [178]. SEM–EDX is particularly useful for quick analysis of morphology and surface elemental composition. TEM provides higher spatial resolution than SEM [191].

Recent studies highlight that the synthesis method, whether green or conventional, plays a crucial role in shaping the physicochemical properties of IONs, directly affecting their environmental performance. For instance, IONs synthesized using Psidium guajava leaf extract exhibited a well-defined cubic crystalline structure with an average crystallite size of 21.75 nm, as revealed by XRD and TEM analyses. These green-synthesized nanoparticles demonstrated effective catalytic efficiency in the Fenton-like degradation of methylene blue dye, highlighting their potential for effective use in wastewater treatment [192].

Similarly, a green synthesis approach utilizing dried fruit extract of Ficus carica (common fig) yielded spherical IONs with an average diameter of 9 ± 4 nm. Characterization techniques such as FTIR, XRD, TEM, and DLS confirmed the successful formation of IONs, and demonstrated the effective reduction and stabilization capabilities of the plant extract. These findings highlight the potential of biomaterials in eco-friendly nanoparticle synthesis [193]. In another study, iron oxide nanoparticles synthesized through a sonochemical approach were characterized using FTIR, Raman spectroscopy, XRD, FESEM, EDS, HRTEM, and VSM analyses. The resulting nanoparticles demonstrated high efficiency in heavy metals removal, like lead and chromium from wastewater, with removal efficiencies of 97.96% and 82.8%, respectively [194].

Several studies demonstrate the adsorption efficiency of these models in describing heavy metals adsorption, with examples in the literature. For example, Fe3O4@SiO2@PTA nanoparticles have shown a remarkable adsorption capacity of 920 mg/g for Hg2+. The adsorption of Hg2+ was also investigated with the variation of pH in the range 2–8 and maximum adsorption was noted at the pH 7.0. A detailed kinetic study on the adsorption process was also carried out, and the data fitted both the pseudo-first-order and pseudo-second-order semi-empirical kinetic models. Out of these, the pseudo-second-order model exhibited excellent fitting, with a correlation coefficient R2 = 0.99 for all Hg2 concentrations [195]. Similarly, Fe3O4@C-GO nanocomposite exhibited excellent adsorption ability for Ag(I), Pb(II), Cr(VI) and Al(III) in aqueous solution. The maximum adsorption capacities were respectively 162.9 mg/g, 125.8 mg/g, 158.2 mg/g and 173.9 mg/g for Ag(I), Pb(II), Cr(VI) and Al(III), at pH = 6 for Ag(I), Pb(II), and Al(III) as well as pH = 2 for Cr(VI). Adsorption kinetics and isotherms were further investigated and show that Fe3O4@C-GO nanocomposite is a promising material for the efficient removal of metal ions from an aqueous environment [89].

In another study, Wang et al. synthesized a Fe3O4/RGO/PEI composite that effectively removed Cr6+ through electrostatic interaction with –NH3+ groups and reduction via π-electron- rich RGO layers. The resulting Cr3+ were subsequently complexed by surface –COO⁻ and –NH2 groups, and the material achieved the maximum removal capacity of 266.6 mg/g. These findings underscore the importance of surface functional groups and reduction mechanisms in enhancing adsorption selectivity and capacity [196]. Fe3O4/MnO2 nanocomposites also showed improved adsorption for Cu2+, Cd2+, Zn2+, and Pb2+ compared to unmodified Fe3O4 nanoparticles. The sorption equilibrium data were well fitted to the Langmuir model, and the maximum adsorption capacity toward Cd (II) was 53.2 mg g–1. Fe3O4/MnO2 retained over 80% of its adsorption capacity after multiple reuse cycles. The findings propose that Fe3O4/MnO2 could be used as an effective recyclable adsorbent for heavy metal ions [197]. Li et al. developed a silica-coated magnetite nanoparticle (SiO2@Fe3O4) as well as a magnetically recovered chitosan/carbon nanotube composite (Fig. 7g). The study shows that the maximum adsorption capacity for Gd3+ reached 88 mg g–1 at 303.15 K, reported for most rare-earth ion-imprinted adsorbents in recent years. The selectivity coefficients compared to La3+ and Ce3+ were 3.50 and 2.23, respectively, aligning closely with values reported for other Chitosan-based ion-imprinted materials [198].

IONs reusability

IONs have garnered significant attention for their reusability and regeneration potential in heavy metal adsorption. Their intrinsic magnetic properties facilitate easy separation from aqueous media using an external magnetic field, making them efficient and sustainable candidates for wastewater treatment applications [203, 204]. The adsorption and desorption mechanisms are discussed in detail and illustrated in Fig. 8. The adsorption process begins with the introduction of IONs into the contaminated solution, where heavy metal ions are captured through various interactions, such as electrostatic attraction, ion exchange, surface complexation, and chelation with functional groups present on the nanoparticle surface. Shaking enhances the contact between the adsorbent and contaminants, leading to efficient adsorption.

Schematic representation of the adsorption–desorption process using magnetic IONs for water purification

Following adsorption, magnetic separation allows for the rapid and effective separation of nanoparticles from the solution using the magnetic properties of IONs. The desorption process involves treating the metal-saturated nanoparticles in an ultrasonic bath with a suitable desorbing agent (e.g., EDTA or a dilute acid), which disrupts the bonds between the metal ions and the nanoparticle surface, releasing the adsorbed contaminants. This regeneration process enables the reuse of IONs across multiple cycles, promoting a cost-effective and sustainable approach for water purification. However, repeated cycles may result in a gradual decline in adsorption efficiency due to structural degradation or the loss of surface activity over time. These mechanisms have been thoroughly described to support the practical applicability of the materials studied.

Previous studies have reported excellent regeneration potential of ION-based adsorbents. For example, Doliente et al. reported 86% phosphate adsorption capacity after five cycles, while Emara et al. demonstrated that Fe3O4-coated polymer composites maintained 98% efficiency for Cr6+, Pb2+, and Cd2+ over four cycles. Similarly, Kaleem et al. showed that IONs maintained up to 80% efficiency after five regeneration cycles using EDTA [205–207]. Magnetic recovery methods vary in scale and effectiveness. In small-scale laboratory setups, permanent magnets are sufficient for nanoparticle recovery, although slight material losses may occur over repeated cycles due to incomplete retrieval [208]. To address this, advanced methods like electromagnets, ultrafiltration, and thermo-enhanced magnetic separation are recommended for larger-scale operations. These techniques provide higher recovery efficiency but require greater energy inputs, making them more suitable for industrial applications [209].

Numerous studies emphasize the advantages of green synthesis using biological agents and plant extracts, which improve both environmental compatibility and nanoparticle performance. For instance, Li et al. (2024) reported efficient and rapid Pb2+ removal using biogenic O-Fe3O4 nanoparticles, maintaining performance over multiple reuse cycles [210]. Similarly, Bhat et al. (2024) and Romeh & Saber (2022) demonstrated the successful removal of heavy metals using nanoparticles synthesized from microbial and plant-based sources. The present study introduces a novel approach by integrating green-synthesized IONs with natural polymer coatings. This enhances metal-binding capacity, magnetic separability, and long-term stability, effectively addressing the limitations of previous approaches and offering a sustainable, efficient, and cost-effective solution for environmental remediation [211, 212].

Efficiency and advantages of using green-synthesized IONs

Green-synthesized IONs offer a versatile and sustainable solution for water purification applications. Unlike traditional chemical methods, the sustainable synthesis of IONs using biological materials such as plant extracts, fungi, or algae offers significant advantages. This eco-friendly approach utilizes naturally occurring phytochemicals as reducing and stabilizing agents, eliminating the need for toxic reagents, reducing energy consumption, and minimizing environmental and health hazards. Additionally, the use of abundant and inexpensive biomaterials makes the process cost-effective and less waste-generating.

Functionalization with coatings such as chitosan, silica, or carbon-based materials further enhances the adsorption performance by improving metal ion selectivity, adsorption capacity, and reusability. These modified nanoparticles demonstrated high adsorption capacities in removing toxic metals like Pb2+, Cr6+, Hg2+, and Cd2+, often outperforming conventional adsorbents in performance. Their regeneration capability reduces the frequency of replacement and lowers overall treatment costs, making them a practical and scalable solution for wastewater treatment. Their magnetic nature allows easy separation from aqueous systems, facilitating regeneration, reuse, and reducing operational costs. Overall, eco-friendly IONs combine sustainability, economic efficiency, and high removal efficacy, making them highly attractive for scalable, environmentally responsible water treatment technologies.

Challenges and future perspectives

The increasing global water scarcity crisis is intensified by the contamination of toxic metal ions in water systems, necessitating the urgent need for efficient and sustainable remediation technologies. Green-synthesized IONs have emerged as promising candidates due to their eco-friendly nature, cost-effectiveness, and reusability. Derived from plant extracts or microorganisms, these nanoparticles exhibit high environmental compatibility. Surface functionalization further improves their structural stability, selectivity, and adsorption capacity for both heavy metals and organic pollutants. However, despite their strong potential and reusability, key challenges must still be addressed, including incomplete metal recovery, the risk of nanoparticle leaching, and limited understanding of their long-term environmental impact.

Future research should focus on optimizing synthesis parameters, such as size, shape, and surface chemistry, to maximize adsorption capacity and minimize toxicity. Investigating dynamic flow systems, rather than batch setups, can bridge the gap between lab-scale and real-world applications. Integration of predictive modeling tools, including machine learning, can provide insights into interactions between nanoparticles and pollutants, improving treatment outcomes. Moreover, evaluating the environmental fate, biodegradability, and safe disposal of functionalized IONs is critical to prevent secondary pollution. By addressing these challenges, the present study contributes to advancing IONs as a sustainable solution for efficient water treatment and supports broader environmental sustainability objectives.

Conclusion

Sustainable nanotechnology offers an eco-friendly and cost-effective alternative for synthesizing IONs, surpassing traditional physical and chemical methods in terms of safety, affordability, and scalability. This review highlights the value of biologically derived IONs fabricated using microorganisms such as bacteria, fungi, plants, and algae, which demonstrate enhanced magnetic, photocatalytic, and adsorption capabilities. Functionalization with environmentally compatible agents like silica, polyethylene glycol (PEG), chitosan, and polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) significantly improves their stability, selectivity, and reusability for heavy metal removal. Advanced characterization tools such as SEM, TEM, XRD, DLS, AFM, VSM, and Raman spectroscopy play a crucial role in assessing the morphological, structural, and magnetic properties of these nanoparticles. Findings from recent studies confirm that magnetic nanocomposites like Fe3O4@GO, Fe3O4@SiO2, and chitosan-based systems exhibit high adsorption capacities for various heavy metals under optimal conditions. The inherent magnetic properties of IONs allow for easy recovery and reuse, supporting their practical application in wastewater treatment. Overall, biosynthesized and functionalized IONs emerge as a highly effective and sustainable alternative for mitigating heavy metal pollution in aquatic environments.
