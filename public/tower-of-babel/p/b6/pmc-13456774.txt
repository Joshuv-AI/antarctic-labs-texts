Submucosal colonic hemangioma at the splenic flexure mimicking a neoplastic lesion: a rare case report and diagnostic challenge
Authors: Mahmud Dasuki, Hadi Farhat, Anthony Kanaan, Ayah Dasuki, Antoine Younan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and aim:Submucosal tumors (SMTs) of the colon represent a heterogeneous group of lesions with variable malignant potential. Colonic hemangiomas are rare vascular tumors that are frequently misdiagnosed, often leading to delayed diagnosis and inappropriate management. They are most commonly located in the rectosigmoid region, while involvement of proximal segments, such as the splenic flexure, is exceptionally rare. We present a case of a submucosal colonic hemangioma arising at the splenic flexure, highlighting the diagnostic challenges associated with this unusual location.Presentation of case:A 50-year-old male presented with a 1-month history of painless hematochezia. Colonoscopy revealed a broad-based submucosal lesion at the splenic flexure with a positive cushion sign. Multiple biopsies were inconclusive. MRI demonstrated a 3.15 cm submucosal mass without lymphadenopathy. Due to persistent symptoms, lesion size, and uncertain diagnosis, a laparoscopic left hemicolectomy was performed. Histopathology confirmed a benign submucosal hemangioma. The postoperative course was uneventful.Clinical discussion:Colonic hemangiomas may mimic neoplastic lesions, particularly when located outside the rectosigmoid region. Preoperative diagnosis remains challenging because endoscopic biopsies are frequently nondiagnostic and may carry a bleeding risk. Although endoscopic management may be feasible in selected cases, surgical resection remains an important diagnostic and therapeutic option for symptomatic or indeterminate lesions.Conclusion:Colonic hemangiomas are rare and may mimic neoplastic lesions, particularly when located outside their typical rectosigmoid distribution. Lesions arising at the splenic flexure are exceedingly uncommon and may contribute to diagnostic uncertainty during preoperative evaluation. Surgical resection remains the preferred treatment for large or symptomatic lesions.

Introduction

Submucosal tumors (SMTs) of the gastrointestinal tract are mesenchymal lesions with diverse origins, including muscular, neural, and vascular components [1]. These lesions pose a diagnostic challenge, particularly in distinguishing benign from malignant entities, and often require correlation between endoscopic appearance, cross-sectional imaging, and histopathology for accurate diagnosis[2].

Colonic hemangiomas are rare vascular tumors, first described in 1839, and typically present with lower gastrointestinal bleeding[3]. Due to their rarity and nonspecific presentation, they are frequently misdiagnosed, with reports indicating that many patients initially undergo inappropriate procedures, such as hemorrhoidectomy or repeated nondiagnostic interventions; diagnostic delay may extend over many years[4,5].

Colonic hemangiomas are most frequently reported in the rectum and rectosigmoid region, with proximal colonic involvement being distinctly uncommon[3,5]. Cases arising at the splenic flexure are sparsely described in the literature, which may contribute to diagnostic uncertainty and delayed recognition[5]. This report highlights a rare presentation of a submucosal colonic hemangioma at the splenic flexure, emphasizing the importance of considering vascular lesions even in atypical anatomical locations. This case is reported in accordance with the SCARE criteria[6].

Presentation of case

A 50-year-old previously healthy male presented with a 1-month history of painless hematochezia, characterized by daily episodes of moderate-volume fresh blood mixed with stool. He denied melena, dizziness, syncope, fatigue, palpitations, or other symptoms suggestive of significant anemia. The patient reported regular baseline bowel habits, consisting of one to two well-formed bowel movements daily, without constipation, diarrhea, or obstructive symptoms. There were no associated symptoms such as abdominal pain, fever, weight loss, perianal discomfort, or anorexia. The patient had no known food or drug allergies and has no significant social history. The patient denied the use of anticoagulant, antiplatelet, or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory medications and had no known history of bleeding disorders or coagulopathy.

On examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable, and laboratory investigations demonstrated a hemoglobin level of 14.2 g/dL, a normal white blood cell count, and normal inflammatory markers. His surgical history was significant for a hemorrhoidectomy performed in 2019 for symptomatic grade III hemorrhoids associated with prolapse and intermittent rectal bleeding using the Milligan–Morgan technique.

Three years before the current presentation, the patient experienced a self-limited episode of painless hematochezia. Colonoscopy revealed a 2.5-cm broad-based, smooth, sessile submucosal lesion near the splenic flexure (approximately 50 cm from the anal verge) with intact mucosa, no ulceration or active bleeding, and mild bluish discoloration but no definitive vascular features. The lesion was initially suspected to be a benign SMTs, such as a lipoma. Superficial biopsy was inconclusive, showing only nonspecific mucosal tissue. Given symptom resolution, the absence of concerning features, and presumed benign pathology, surveillance with interval follow-up was recommended.

At the current presentation, colonoscopy demonstrated a broad-based submucosal lesion measuring approximately 3 cm, located at the splenic flexure (approximately 60 cm from the anal verge), an uncommon site for colonic hemangiomas, with a positive cushion sign (Fig. 1). Multiple superficial biopsies were obtained because the lesion was initially suspected to represent a submucosal neoplasm, such as a gastrointestinal stromal tumor. Histopathology showed only normal colonic mucosa with inadequate submucosal sampling and no definitive diagnosis. Concurrent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed no identifiable source of bleeding. Given interval growth, persistent symptoms, and diagnostic uncertainty, an MRI was performed and demonstrated a 3.15-cm well-circumscribed submucosal lesion at the distal transverse colon near the splenic flexure with intermediate T1 and mildly hyperintense T2 signal, without local invasion or regional lymphadenopathy. No definitive radiologic features of hemangioma, including phleboliths or characteristic progressive vascular enhancement, were identified (Fig. 2). The lesion’s localized submucosal nature and absence of large dilated vascular channels may have contributed to its nonspecific imaging appearance. For intraoperative localization, the lesion was tattooed using sterile carbon-based ink. After multidisciplinary discussion, the patient underwent laparoscopic left hemicolectomy with oncologic principles (Fig. 3). Given interval lesion growth, persistent hematochezia, nondiagnostic biopsies, and the inability to exclude malignancy, multidisciplinary consensus favored definitive surgical resection. Endoscopic resection was considered technically challenging because of the lesion’s broad sessile morphology and splenic flexure location. The patient underwent laparoscopic left hemicolectomy with oncologic principles and intracorporeal side-to-side stapled colo-colic anastomosis. Operative time was 180 minutes, with an estimated blood loss of 50 mL. Figure 3.(A) Intraoperative tattoo localization of the splenic flexure lesion during laparoscopic surgery (white arrow). Histopathological examination demonstrated a benign submucosal hemangioma, characterized by haphazardly arranged small and medium-sized vascular channels without cytologic atypia. All lymph nodes were negative for malignancy.

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 4 after tolerating diet advancement and recovering bowel function. The patient followed a progressive dietary advancement protocol, beginning with clear liquids, followed by a gradual transition to a soft and regular diet as bowel function recovered. At the 6-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with complete resolution of hematochezia and no evidence of recurrence or postoperative complications. He reported normal bowel function without fecal urgency, incontinence, or obstructive symptoms, and his quality of life remained unaffected.

Discussion

Submucosal colonic tumors encompass a broad spectrum of benign and malignant lesions. Benign lesions include lipomas, leiomyomas, schwannomas, neurofibromas, and vascular tumors such as hemangiomas, whereas malignant lesions include gastrointestinal stromal tumors (GISTs), leiomyosarcomas, and metastatic disease[1,7].

The differential diagnosis in this case included lipoma, GIST, leiomyoma, and vascular lesions such as hemangioma. The presence of a cushion sign initially suggested a lipoma; however, imaging and clinical progression raised suspicion for other pathologies.

Colonic hemangiomas most commonly present with lower gastrointestinal bleeding, reported in 60–90% of cases[3,5]. Other manifestations include anemia and, less commonly, intestinal obstruction[5,8]. These lesions are typically diagnosed in younger patients, often with symptoms dating back to childhood. Our case is unique due to its presentation in adulthood with an acute onset of symptoms.

Colonic hemangiomas are most frequently reported in the rectum and rectosigmoid region, likely due to their richer vascular supply and the higher clinical detection rate associated with rectal bleeding[3,5]. In contrast, involvement of more proximal segments of the colon, such as the transverse colon or splenic flexure, is uncommon[8,9]. The present case is therefore notable not only for its adult presentation but also for its atypical location at the splenic flexure, which has only rarely been described in the literature, including a recent report of a splenic flexure lobular capillary hemangioma requiring surgery after bleeding following biopsy[10]. This unusual location broadens the spectrum of anatomical presentations of colonic hemangiomas and highlights the need to consider vascular lesions in the differential diagnosis of SMTs throughout the entire colon.

Management of submucosal lesions depends on their size, symptoms, and suspected pathology. Small, asymptomatic lesions may be monitored, whereas larger or symptomatic lesions warrant resection.

Endoscopic biopsy is generally discouraged in suspected vascular lesions due to the risk of significant bleeding[3,13,14]. Imaging modalities such as MRI and CT play a key role in preoperative evaluation, as characteristic radiologic findings may suggest the diagnosis and help avoid hazardous biopsy attempts. Endoscopic ultrasound may also represent a useful adjunctive modality in selected cases, as it can help define the layer of origin and vascular nature of submucosal lesions. However, its use in colonic lesions, particularly at the splenic flexure, is technically limited and not routinely available in many centers. Furthermore, even with advanced imaging modalities, definitive diagnosis often requires histopathologic evaluation following resection[2,10,13,14].

Endoscopic resection techniques, including endoscopic submucosal dissection, may be appropriate for selected lesions, particularly those that are pedunculated or small[15–19]. However, surgical resection remains the preferred approach for large, broad-based, or diagnostically uncertain lesions, as in our case, and laparoscopic colectomy has been successfully used in prior reports[15,20].

This case highlights a rare anatomical presentation of colonic hemangioma at the splenic flexure, contributing to the limited literature on this topic. However, as a single case report, generalizability is limited.

Conclusion

Colonic hemangiomas are vascular lesions that may mimic neoplastic processes. Diagnosis is often challenging due to nonspecific clinical and endoscopic findings. Biopsy may be inconclusive and carries a risk of bleeding. While most cases occur in the rectosigmoid region, involvement of the splenic flexure is exceedingly uncommon and may further obscure the diagnosis. Surgical resection remains the treatment of choice for large, symptomatic, or indeterminate lesions. Early recognition and appropriate management are essential to avoid misdiagnosis and unnecessary procedures.
