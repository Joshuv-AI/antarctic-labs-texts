Photolytic disruption of Alzheimer’s amyloid Aβ42-fibrils by sialic-acid decorated glycodendrimers
Authors: Rituparna Das, Swarnendu Roy, Rahul Das, Balaram Mukhopadhyay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe formation of amyloid Aβ42-fibrils is linked to the pathology of cognitive dysfunction in Alzheimer’s patients. There is a dire need for developing therapeutics to reverse Aβ42 fibrillation and to stop or slow down the disease progression. Here, we report rationally designed sialic acid functionalized porphyrin cored first and second-generation dendrimers, synthesized using “click chemistry.” The glycoporphyrin dendrimer shows a promising ability to degrade Aβ42 fibrils to lower-order soluble oligomers upon irradiation. The gradual fragmentation of the β-sheet-rich Aβ42 fibrils to the unstructured forms by photoactivation of the dendrimers is evident from high-resolution imaging, particle size analysis, and peptide secondary structure distribution. Further, the glycodendrimers are non-toxic even when irradiated and can rescue the neuroblastoma cells from Aβ42 fibril-mediated cytotoxicity. We propose that the photoirradiated dendrimers degrade the Aβ42 fibrils by rupturing the H-bonds by the singlet oxygen species (ROS) generated from the excited porphyrin core. Subject terms: Glycobiology, Drug discovery and development, Glycobiology

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia globally, accounting for almost two-thirds of all neurodegenerative diseases1. It is estimated to affect around 131 million people by 20502,3. Two main lesions associated with AD, (a) the extracellular senile neurotic amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and (b) the intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs), are responsible for the progressive neuroanatomical and cognitive decline. Soluble Aβ42 is the normal product in the cortical and cerebrospinal fluid, derived through the proteolytic processing of amyloid precursor protein (APP). However, the presence of abnormally phosphorylated tau proteins in the perinuclear cytoplasm of neurons4 and excessive accumulation of extracellular 40- and 42-residue Aβ protein in the brain leads to its aggregation into insoluble, amorphous, diffused and filamentous senile plaques5,6. The natively unstructured monomers of Aβ42 peptide undergo nucleation-dependent polymerization to self-assemble into small, spherical cross-β-sheet rich aggregates7. These aggregates are further consolidated by the cooperative contribution of the hydrogen bonds8, making them resistant to regular proteolytic attacks9. Thus, AD therapy primarily requires targeting the cross-β fibrillar structures of Aβ42, either by the precautionary inhibition of fibrillation to reduce the risk of AD before its progression or by disintegrating them once the presence of the β-fibrils has been diagnosed in the AD patients at an early stage. Early onset of AD10,11 in people below the age of sixty-five is a major challenge in the preventive therapeutic model. Moreover, the disproportionate higher risk of the female population12 towards the cognitive disease makes the preventive therapy even less effective. Therefore, to target the post-AD disintegration of the Aβ42 fibrils, here we report the design and synthesis of porphyrin-cored light-harvesting sialic acid conjugated first and second-generation glycodendrimers (1 and 2, Fig. 1) to develop novel AD therapeutics. It also promises to neutralize the adverse effects caused by precautionary chemical therapeutics.

Developing a potential therapeutic molecule for treating AD is a global challenge. Majority of the existing drugs concentrate on symptom relief rather than being curative. They mainly aim at ameliorating cognitive symptoms and limiting the progression of behavioral and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD)13–16. For example, a cholinergic-based molecule (rivastigmine) only improved caregiver burden and treatment adherence by inhibiting acetylcholinesterase, the enzyme that catalyzes acetylcholine degradation17. Recently, the FDA approved the use of “Lecanemab,” a monoclonal antibody targeting insoluble toxic amyloid protein aggregates16,18. However, this monoclonal antibody treatment had limited implementation since it caused side effects like amyloid related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) with edema and infusion-related reactions19,20. These prevalent therapeutic approaches showed a modest effect only in the asymptomatic stages. Meldal et al. has also reported a cascade-type auto-hydrolysis of an extended nucleation sequence of Aβ peptides21. The difficulty in permeating the blood-brain-barrier (BBB) during CNS circulation significantly increases the drug’s dosage, which in turn enhances the side effects. Moreover, the Aβ42-binding agents, like synthetic peptides22, small ligands stabilizing the soluble monomeric Aβ4223, or amphiphilic polyelectrolyte polystyrene sulfonate polymers24 are unable to cross the BBB, causing a significant roadblock for amyloid-centric therapeutics.

An ideal next-generation molecule should have a higher affinity for Aβ42 fibrils (nM) and the ability to degrade the fibrils with a trigger. Some oligomannuronic or konjac-derived mannan oligosaccharides25,26 reduce neuroinflammation by modulating the gut-brain axis of patients with mild to moderate AD. Additionally, 6-O-phosphorylated heparan sulfate oligosaccharides also effectively inhibit Aβ42 fibril formation27. So, we aimed to utilize the inherent bioavailability and biocompatibility of carbohydrate-based drugs28 to design our glycodendrimers 1 and 2. Gangliosides (GM1), the major sialic acid carriers in the microglial cells of the brain, interact with the His13 to Leu17 residues at the N-terminal of the Aβ42 protein, where His13 specifically binds with the carboxylic acid in the sialic acid moiety29 of the gangliosides. Thus, the intrinsic ability of the sialic acids to bind Aβ42 peptide helps it to act as a recognition site, facilitating biological identification and masking the site, allowing them to exert dual and opposite effects. Thus, we introduced sialic acids at the periphery of our designed dendrimers to effectively target the Aβ42 protein30.

Introduction of multiple sialic acids at the periphery in the designed molecule (Fig. 1) mimics the neurological cell membrane and increases the affinity for senile β-sheet Aβ42 plaques31 manifold due to the “glycoside cluster effect.”32–34 Moreover, multiple sialic acid units have the potential to protect the glycodendrimers from premature degradation by sialic acid aldolases, catalyzed by cytoplasmic Sia-specific pyruvate lyases35. Thereby, it will facilitate the dendrimers to effectively cross the BBB even in low doses and target AD with increased avidity36. The multivalent sialic acid also nullifies the low binding affinities exerted by monovalent interactions or those predicted from the sum of constitutive interactions. Previous literature shows that a sialic acid-based PAMAM can attenuate Aβ42 dependent cytotoxicity in neuroblastoma cells32,37,38. The study elaborated on the concept of enhanced Aβ binding affinity of the PAMAM dendrimers by sialic acid conjugation. It, thereby, also contributed towards increased viability and protective attributes of the sialic acid-based glycodendrimers, implementing the biocompatibility of carbohydrate-based therapeutics. Literature studies aiming at the photodegradation of senile plaques39,40 using organic dyes, inorganic photosensitizers and polyoxometalates exhibited inhibitory properties when induced with a noninvasive source of light41–43. However, their shorter lifetimes and low stability limit their use as efficient photo-harvesting agents. To trigger the degradation of Aβ42 fibrils, the porphyrin core may be a better alternative photo-harvesting agent. We anticipate that photoirradiation on the porphyrin core of the synthesized glycodendrimers will generate reactive oxygen species (ROS)41,44 capable of disrupting amyloid plaques45 and may show independent attributes of inhibiting Aβ42 fibril formation by suppressing its aggregation41,46. Previous porphyrin-based systems relied mainly on nonselective hydrophobic or electrostatic interactions45,47. This led to low target specificity, non-selectivity and potential phototoxicity as they did not incorporate any targeting ligands.

Here, we report the design of high-affinity glycodendrimers 1 and 2 (Fig. 1) containing multiple sialic acid moieties, which bind Aβ42 fibrils with nM affinity due to the multivalent effect. The glycodendrimers carry a photo-harvesting porphyrin core that ruptures the Aβ42 fibrils to low molecular weight soluble oligomers by breaking the H-bonds upon photoirradiation. Finally, our in vitro studies showed that the glycodendrimers 1 and 2 rescue the SH-SY5Y neuroblastoma cells from the cytotoxic effect of Aβ42 fibrils. This approach promises the development of next generation therapeutics that could slow down and potentially reverse the disease progression in AD patients.

Results

Synthesis of the sialic acid decorated porphyrin-cored glycodendrimers targeting Aβ42 fibrils

We synthesized the porphyrin-cored first- and second-generation sialic acid dendrimers (1 and 2, Fig. 1) with four and nine peripheral sialic acid units, respectively, by extensive use of copper(I) catalyzed “click” chemistry48–50. The sialic acid conjugation was done at the reducing end of the sugar instead of the carboxylic acid group, which invariably increases its ability to sequester Aβ42 fibril-induced neurotoxicity.

Synthesis of the porphyrin core (Scheme 1)

Synthesis of Zn(II) tetra(propargylbenzyl)porphyrin core (4).

We decided to opt for a synthetic “inside-out” strategy involving a divergent approach wherein the azido-functionalized sialic acid dendrimer wedges are introduced onto the previously synthesized Zn(II)-porphyrin moiety. Thus, we synthesized tetra(propargylbenzyl)-substituted porphyrin core 351 in a modest 42% yield following previously reported Lindsay-type condensation protocols (Scheme 1) with pyrrole conjugated with 4-O-propargyl benzaldehyde. The alternative convergent route involving the condensation of benzaldehyde functionalized sialic acid counterpart with pyrrole to form the glycodendrimer proved to be time-consuming, leading to extremely low yields with poor isolation efficiency. It was primarily due to the formation of a mixture of macrocycles and by-products like dipyrromethene, mono and di-substituted porphyrinogen derivatives. Thus, pyrrole and 4-O-propargyl benzaldehyde were condensed with the help of the Lewis acid, BF3·OEt2 to form the porphyrinogen as the intermediate, which was further oxidized to obtain the required porphyrin (3) by employing chloranil as the oxidizing agent. Chloranil proved to be a better oxidizing agent than DDQ, which gave a low 19% yield. Before proceeding with the conjugation of sialic acid units onto the porphyrin ring by Cu-catalysed click reaction, we premetallized the porphyrin ring to prevent Cu-insertion in the porphyrin cavity. Hence, we inserted zinc in the porphyrin core using zinc acetate to obtain the zinc-porphyrin (4) in a promising 98% yield. We chose zinc for pre-metallation because it is immune to being replaced by other heavy metals following the size affinity order of Zn(II) ~ Cu (II) » Mg(II) towards the porphyrin ring52. Disappearance of the peak at δ −2.75 (compound 4 NMR, Supporting Information) affirmed the successful insertion of Zn(II) in the porphyrin ring.

Synthesis of the conjugation-ready sialic acid derivatives (Scheme 2)

Synthesis of azidoethyl derivative of sialic acid, 8α.

Next, we converted the commercially available N-acetyl neuraminic acid (5) to its corresponding acetylated methyl ester53,54 (Scheme 2), followed by the incorporation of thiotolyl group at the reducing end to obtain the anomeric mixture of 6α/β. We substituted the anomeric thiotolyl group with the azidoethyl group in two steps to successfully obtain crude azidoethyl functionalized sialic acid (8α/β) in 71% yield as an α/β inseparable mixture. So, we deprotected all the acetyl groups by Zémplén transesterification reaction using freshly prepared sodium methoxide in methanol to get the anomeric mixture 9α/β in 97% yield. We purified the α-anomer by flash chromatography to obtain the desired α-azidoethyl sialic acid, 9, in 64% yield, and further per-acetylated with Ac2O and pyridine to obtain pure acetylated 8α in 97% yield. We later implemented 8 as the peripheral sialic acid receptors for first and second-generation glycoporphyrin dendrimers.

Synthesis of the TRIS derivatives for the introduction of consecutive branching, introducing the multivalent platform (Scheme 3)

Synthesis of tribranched sialic acid dendrimeric appendage 15.

To introduce more peripheral sialic acid units for the second-generation dendrimer, we used an interim branching through a tris(hydroxymethyl) aminomethyl group. We chose the commercially available pure TRIS over TRIS-hydrochloride for this purpose. The selective t-butyloxycarbonyl (Boc) protection of the free amine group of the pure TRIS (10) produced 11 in 96% yield (Scheme 3). The distinct singlet at δ 1.38 confirms the insertion of the Boc group in the TRIS substrate. We alkylated the free hydroxyl groups with propargyl bromide in the presence of KOH as the base to get the derivative 11 in 65% yield. Further, we functionalized the propargylated TRIS derivative 11 with the α-azidoethyl sialic acid derivative 8 by click reaction using CuSO4 and Na-ascorbate to get the trisialic acid dendron 13 in 81% yield. The new characteristic peak of the triazole at δ 7.69 and the complete disappearance of the acetylenic triplet at δ 2.42 in the 1H NMR spectra confirmed the formation of the triazole ring. Moreover, the anomeric carbon at δ 98.81 in the 13C spectra confirmed the insertion of the sialic acid sugar residues in the dendron scaffold, 11. Then, we used TFA to selectively cleave the Boc protection to get 14, which was subsequently converted to an azido group in 15 in 79% yield over two steps. Corresponding removal of the characteristic singlet for Boc protection in the NMR spectra at δ 1.4 confirmed the successful cleavage of Boc protection.

Construction of the first- and second-generation dendrimers targeting photolytic Aβ42 fibrils disruption (Schemes 4 and 5)

Synthesis of first-generation glyco-porphyrin dendrimer 1.

Synthesis of 2nd generation glyco-porphyrin dendrimer 2.

Further, for the synthesis of the glycoporphyrin dendrimers, we performed the Cu-catalysed “click-reaction” using the sialic acid derivative, 8 and the trisialylated dendron 15 with the propargylated Zn(II) porphyrin unit (4), respectively, in the presence of CuSO4 and ascorbic acid to get the corresponding peracetylated first-generation (16) and second-generation (17) glycoporphyrin dendrimers in 65% and 53% yield, respectively (Schemes 4 and 5). We implemented Zémplén transesterification to remove all the acetyl groups to get the corresponding deacetylated compounds. Further addition of water hydrolysed the methyl ester to get the globally deprotected water-soluble first and second-generation glycodendrimers 1 and 2 in 87% and 82% yield, respectively. The aqueous solutions of the dendrimers were triturated with minimal amount of CH2Cl2 to remove any possible organic contaminants. The aqueous layer was separated and freeze-dried. The dendrimers were characterized by MALDI-TOF MS, which confirmed the formation of our desired product.

Evaluation of the photochemical potential of the glycodendrimers as a possible tool towards photo-disruption of Aβ42 fibrils

The synthesized sialic acid conjugated dendrimers 1 and 2 exhibited an inherent color due to the electronic transition of the porphyrin ring from π to vacant π* orbitals. We recorded the absorption and emission of the protected dendrimers 16 and 17 in dichloromethane to estimate the effect of introducing multiple sialic acids on the porphyrin core. In accordance with the typical absorbance spectra of metalloporphyrin ring, the UV–vis spectra of 16 and 17 distinctly showed Soret or B absorption bands in 408–432 nm representing strong transition from S0 to S2 state and Q bands in 540–562 and 580–596 nm representing weak transitions from S0 to S1 state (Fig. 2A). Again, upon excitation at 423 nm, the corresponding major emission peak was obtained at 604 nm and a minor peak at 648 nm. Moreover, a comparative study of the excitation and emission spectra of 16 and 17 with the unconjugated porphyrin ring 4, both the dendrimers exhibited almost similar nature with a slight red shift in the emission maxima (Δλ = 7 nm) (Fig. 2A). The intrinsic emission maxima underwent a 1.15 times enhancement on progressing from unconjugated porphyrin to 16 and a ~1.7 times enhancement with 17, indicating higher inherent fluorophore activity of the synthesized dendrimers. First- and second-generation glycodendrimers bind Aβ42 fibrils with nanomolar affinity

Targeting the Aβ42 peptide aggregates, we begin with the conversion of the unstructured Aβ peptide to β-fibrils by systematic curling of the peptide strands (representative Fig. 3A). Fully grown Aβ42 fibrils were formed by incubating the monomers at 37 °C for 24 h in the dark. Real-time monitoring of the morphological transitions of Aβ42 peptide by circular dichroism (CD) (Fig. 3B) showed a distinct minima at 216 nm and a maxima at 197 nm, which successfully corresponds to the typical CD profile for β-sheet, indicating the conversion of Aβ42 monomers and small aggregates into β-sheet-rich secondary fibril structure55 (amyloidogenic pathway).

Next, we investigate the effect of the dendrimers 1 and 2 on Aβ42 fibrils (representative Fig. 2B). We evaluated the binding efficiency of the dendrimers 1 and 2 by plotting the variation of fluorescent emission maxima at 604 nm against increasing Aβ42 concentration. The dissociation constant (Kd) values obtained were 380 ± 105 nM for first-generation glycodendrimer 1 and 171 ± 12 nM for second-generation glycodendrimer 2 (Fig. 2C, D).

First- and second-generation glycodendrimers degrade Aβ42 fibrils upon photo-irradiation

Once the binding efficiency of the dendrimers with Aβ42 fibrils were evaluated, we examined the degrading efficiency of the dendrimers. The effect of the dendrimers 1 and 2 on Aβ42 fibrils was examined using CD spectroscopy (representative Fig. 3A) to measure the secondary structure of the peptides (Fig. 3C). The CD spectra of the Aβ42 peptide treated with the glycodendrimers showed only a slight change in the characteristic secondary structure in the absence of photoirradiation. Photoirradiation subsequently results in the disappearance of the strong negative peak at 216 nm corresponding to the characteristic β-turn angle to an unstructured CD spectrum, indicating the disruption of the Aβ42 fibrils.

We further analyzed the inhibitory effect of the dendrimers 1 and 2 by ThT fluorescence assay (representative Fig. 4A). Thioflavin T (ThT), a benzothiazole dye, effectively binds with Aβ42 aggregates and β-fibril, exhibiting a strong enhancement in its fluorescence intensity56,57. We estimated the inhibitory concentration (IC50) values by measuring the ThT fluorescence intensity against the increasing concentration of the dendrimers. Initially, we incubated the fresh Aβ42 peptides (20 μM) for 24 h at 37 °C. Once the soluble peptides were converted to their insoluble fibrillar form, we treated them with the glycodendrimers 1 and 2, respectively. We observed that the native dendrimers alone showed a nominal decrease in the fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4C). The normalized plots with the native glycodendrimers provided the IC50 values of 154.2 ± 18.75 nM for the first-generation dendrimer, 1 and 97.90 ± 5.15 nM for the second-generation dendrimer 2.

Subsequent photoirradiation of the treated Aβ42 fibrils with the dendrimers showed promising results, indicating the extensive defibrillation of the insoluble Aβ42 peptides. Based on the time-based kinetics data, we concluded that irradiation for 3–5 min showed the optimum defibrillation (Fig. 4B, D). Thus, we irradiated the Aβ42 fibrils treated with the dendrimers 1 and 2 at 423 nm for 5 min and evaluated the change in ThT fluorescence intensity as a function of the concentration of the dendrimers. A significant reduction of IC50 values was observed, 52.81 ± 6 nM for 1 and 41.95 ± 4 nM for 2 (Fig. 4C).

Moreover, dynamic light scattering (DLS) data also show that glycodendrimers 1 and 2 significantly degrade the size of amyloid fibril aggregates from ~100 to ~10 nm (Fig. 5).

Glycodendrimers prevent Aβ42 fibril-mediated cytotoxicity in neuronal cells

We checked the cytotoxicity of dendrimers 1 and 2 and the cell viability of HEK293T cells by 3-(4,5-dimethylthioazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay (Fig. 9). Reports indicate the importance of the HEK293T cell line in various neurodegenerative assays due to the transfection of the immortalized cell line in high throughput assays63. The dendrimers 1 and 2 showed a typical cell viability of 86 ± 4.78% and 74 ± 1.81%, respectively, using concentrations as high as 200 μM (Fig. S1 and Supporting Information, page S3). They did not exhibit much difference in the cell viability even after photo-irradiation with long wavelength UV-radiation that showed cell viability of 88.8 ± 4.5% and 76.3 ± 3.86% for 1 and 2, respectively.

Next, we assessed the ability of the dendrimers to rescue the neuroblastoma cells from Aβ42 fibrillation-induced cytotoxicity. We determined the viability of SH-SY5Y human neuroblastoma cells exposed to Aβ42 aggregates. SH-SY5Y cells are widely chosen and frequently implemented for assessing the Aβ42 aggregation-mediated cytotoxicity owing to their catecholaminergic neuronal properties64. As previously reported, Aβ42 shows significant toxicity and decreased viability of SHSY5Y cells in fibrillar or higher-order oligomer form65–68. Glycodendrimer failed to reverse the Aβ42-induced toxicity without irradiation. However, upon irradiation, the glycodendrimers completely negated the toxic effect of Aβ42 (Fig. 9B).

The cellular uptake and intracellular distribution of dendrimers 1 and 2 were also investigated by incubating HEK293T and SHSY5Y cells at 37 °C with 1 and 2 separately for 24 h. Fluorescent images (Figs. 9C and S1, Supporting Information) of the cells were recorded on a confocal laser scanning microscope using an excitation laser of 488 nm. The green fluorescence observed around the nuclei indicates the successful penetration of the glycodendrimers inside the cells.

Discussion

We have developed sialic acid decorated porphyrin-cored glycodendrimers 1 and 2 that effectively degrade Aβ42 fibrils upon irradiation. The use of copper-catalyzed “click chemistry” in their synthesis marked the implementation of “green” chemistry in developing the large molecule. It involved rigorous protection and deprotection steps and systematic optimization of the linker and the branching unit. The portrayal of multiple units of sialic acids in the periphery accomplishes the role of “Glycocalyx,” allowing effective, specific binding to the Aβ42 fibrils, bringing the porphyrin core into proximity. Moreover, studies reveal that oligoGM1 with peripheral sialic acids can effectively cross the BBB69. Further studies showing the transport efficiency of the sialic acid-based nanoparticles in the brain epithelial cells in vitro have shown promising results70. This was also further consolidated by the in vivo studies showing the penetration of sialyl oligosaccharides in brain epithelium71. In view of the given results, which shows the efficiency of sialic acid-based glycoconjugates to transverse the BBB, our synthesized multivalent sialic acid-based porphyrin glycodendrimers show the potential to be effectively used to penetrate the BBB and be used at neurotherapeutic domain.

Since Aβ42 peptide remains mostly unstructured, we converted it to its corresponding β-fibrillar state by incubation for 24 h. Time-based analysis showed that the peptide attained maximum fibrillation after incubation for 24 h, after which it attained a stable β-fibrillar state, consistent with the increased ThT fluorescence intensity measure as a function of time (Fig. 4B). We further envisaged that an increased number of sialic acid moieties on the periphery of the dendrimers would result in higher Aβ42 binding affinity. The dendrimers 1 and 2 against increasing Aβ42 concentration showed strong enhancement of the fluorescence emission intensity at different Aβ42 ratios. This confirmed the high physiological binding association of the dendrimers with the amyloid peptides. Figure 2C also clearly indicates that glycodendrimer 2 with twelve sialic acid units at the periphery showed a lower dissociation constant (Kd) of 171 ± 12 nM when compared with dendrimer 1 with four sialic acid units, which showed a Kd value of 380 ± 105 nM. The lower dissociation constant value of 2 ascertains a stronger affinity and greater strength of interaction of molecules with Aβ42 fibrils. It is worth noting that the Kd values of the dendrimers are significantly lower than those of popular small molecule Aβ42 markers or inhibitors, viz. ThT (Kd = 700–1000 nM), curcumin (Kd = 5000 nM) or Rose Bengal (Kd = 17,000 μM). Although the second generation glycoporphyrin dendrimer 2 with twelve sialic acids showed an increase in binding affinity on proceeding from first generation dendrimer 1 with four sialic acid units, the increase can be argued to be less significant than expected. The higher peripheral carbohydrate density may sterically hinder the interaction of the incoming Aβ42 peptide. The results conform to the previous studies, which state that a higher number of epitopes does not necessarily follow a linear progressive binding efficiency72,73.

However, the binding affinity of both the dendrimers ascertained their specific recognition towards the Aβ42 fibrils. The glycodendrimers, on irradiation, also showed their capability to degrade stable and insoluble Aβ42 fibrils. The disappearance of the negative peak at 216 nm in the CD spectral analysis of dendrimers 1 and 2 incubated with Aβ42 fibrils upon irradiation showed continuous defibrillation of the fibrillar structure. The DLS data and the corresponding calculation of the hydrodynamic radius using the Hullrad algorithm depict significant disruption of the number of Aβ42 fibrils on treatment with the dendrimers, followed by irradiation (Table S1, Supporting Information). The decrease in the size of amyloid fibril aggregates from ~100 to ~10 nm (Fig. 5) corresponds to a reduction from ~6000 peptide units to ~300 peptide units (Table S1, Supporting Information).

The inhibitory studies of the dendrimers towards Aβ42 fibrils using the ThT fluorescence assay showed promising results. The IC50 values of the native dendrimers 1 and 2 alone with Aβ42 fibrils without photoirradiation, showed a nominal decrease in the fluorescence intensity (Fig. 4C). The normalized IC50 values showed that second-generation glycodendrimer 2 exhibited a lower IC50 value of 97.90 ± 5.15 nM compared to first-generation glycodendrimer 1, which recorded a value of 154.2 ± 18.75 nM. Further photoirradiation of the incubated Aβ42 fibrils with the dendrimers exhibited their property of degrading and defibrillating the insoluble Aβ42 fibrils. The IC50 values of 1 reduced from 154.2 ± 18.75 to 52.81 ± 6 nM, while the IC50 value of 2 reduced from 97.90 ± 5.15 to 41.95 ± 4 nM (Fig. 4C). Thus, the photoirradiation influenced a ~3-fold increase in defibrillation efficiency for dendrimer 1 and a 2.3-fold increase for dendrimer 2. The time-based kinetics data (Fig. 4D) showed the optimum irradiation time required for the disruption of the Amyloid-β fibrils upon treatment with the synthesized dendrimers 1 and 2. Comparative analysis of the dendrimers show that 1 showed maximum defibrillation after 4.52 min of photoirradiation, whereas 2 showed a slightly higher timeframe of 5.38 min to attain maximum defibrillation. From the values obtained, we observed that the second-generation dendrimer 2 showed lower IC50 values than those of the first-generation dendrimer 1. A lower IC50 value ascertains higher binding and higher inhibitory effect of 2, conforming to the principle of multivalency. The recorded IC50 values are significantly lower than those of similar hemin and hematin inhibitors (1 μM), which reported one-tenth of the β-amyloid peptide concentration74. Our glycodendrimer molecules exhibited IC50 values of almost 1/400th of our implemented amyloid concentration of 20 μM. Upon irradiation, the excited state porphyrin transfers its energy to ground state O2 to produce singlet oxygen species (1O2). The singlet oxygen weakens the hydrogen bond and oxidizes the peptide backbone, leading to photo-disruption of the secondary structure of Aβ42 fibrils. Polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Fig. 6) also shows an increase in low-weight oligomers of <10 KDa order upon irradiation. The SEM images (Fig. 7) also show a significant reduction in the Aβ42 fibrillar aggregates and subsequent formation of shorter and more dispersed small-sized aggregates, affirming the disruption of the highly tangled aggregates. The AFM images (Fig. 8) also conformed with the results, where photoirradiation of the treated Aβ42 fibrils showed a significant reduction in the fibril height, forming smaller aggregates or lower-order oligomers. In summary, comparative biophysical characterization by DLS indicates that the Aβ42 fibrils (made of >2000 Aβ42 peptides) degrade to small oligomers with ~72–180 peptides for 1 and ~1–72 peptides for 2. This is obtained by precise calculation of DLS data that conform with our conclusion that the second-generation dendrimer 2 shows higher defibrillation capacity than the first-generation dendrimer 1 (Fig. 5I).

MTT assay (Fig. 9) to check the cytotoxicity of the dendrimers against HEK293T cells showed insignificant cytotoxicity, and we eventually failed to achieve the corresponding IC50 values (Fig. S1 and Supporting Information, page S3). The effect on neuroblastoma cells SH-SY5Y when exposed to Aβ42 fibrils and treated with the glycodendrimers were also observed. Photoirradiation of the glycodendrimers exhibited their capability to rescue the neuroblastoma cells. Figure 9B shows that the toxic effects of the Aβ42 fibrillation in the SH-SY5Y cells were completely negated by dendrimers 1 and 2. The reduction in the cytotoxicity also affirms the disruption of the robust fibrillar and toxic fibrils of Aβ42 peptide. The singlet oxygen generated from the porphyrin scaffold showed a negligible effect on the viability of the cells. These results from the in vitro MTT assay of HEK293T and human SH-SY5Y cells showed the efficiency of the dendrimers in exhibiting an inhibitory effect on the cytotoxic Aβ42 aggregates. We also obtained favorable results while investigating the cellular uptake and intracellular distribution of the glycodendrimers 1 and 2 with both normal hepatic cell HEK293T and neuroblastoma cell SH-SY5Y cells by a confocal laser scanning microscope (Figs. 9C and S1, Supporting Information, page S3). Almost homogenous fluorescence distribution around the nuclei provides insights into possible subcellular penetration of the glycodendrimers. Interestingly, although 2 exhibited limited cellular distribution in normal hepatic cell HEK293T, but it showed higher permeability in neuroblastoma cell SH-SY5Y. While the precise mechanism underlying this cell-type-dependent behavior remains to be fully elucidated, one plausible explanation is the higher expression of sialic acid-containing receptors on SH-SY5Y cells. The increased density of sialic acid residues on 2 may facilitate enhanced interaction with these receptors, thereby promoting cellular uptake.

Herein, it is also emphasized that although the studies are performed in vitro, there are also sufficient provisions for delivering sufficient amounts of light into the brain. Implantable optical fibers and minimally invasive endoscopic systems enable in vivo optogenetic stimulation in rodents and are already in use in photodynamic neurotherapy to precisely deliver light in deep brain tissues75,76.

Conclusion

We obtained encouraging results with the sialic-acid decorated porphyrin-based glycodendrimers 1 and 2, which showed binding with the Aβ42 fibrils in nanomolar affinity. They also showed effective degrading ability of the toxic, insoluble and stable Aβ42 fibrils, which is one of the most vital characteristics of AD-related neurodegenerative diseases. The dendrimers were observed to be highly nontoxic in nature. It also rescued the neuroblastoma cells from Aβ42 fibril-induced toxicity. Considering the effective cellular uptake of the dendrimers and their excellent Aβ42 fibrils-degrading ability, our nontoxic glycodendrimers are potential candidates in developing therapeutics to treat AD.

Experimental section/methods

Experimental procedure for the preparation of the glycodendrimers

General methods

The methods for the preparation of the compounds were performed through standard protocols. All the solvents and the reagents were made anhydrous before performing the reactions. The commercially purchased reagents were used without any further purification unless otherwise mentioned. Dichloromethane was dried and distilled over P2O5. Dimethylformamide was made ammonia-free by the half-distillation method. The reactions were monitored by thin layer chromatography (TLC) on Silica-Gel 60-F254 with detection by ultraviolet (254 nm) or fluorescence, which was followed by charring in a 10% ethanolic solution of H2SO4. Chromatography purification was done with silica gel 230–400 mesh. Nuclear magnetic resonance spectra were recorded on Bruker Advance 500 MHz spectrometer (1H NMR at 500 MHz and 13C NMR at 125 MHz) at 25 °C. HRMS analysis was done with XeVO G2-XS Q-TOF (Waters Corporation) instrument by +ve mode electrospray. MALDI-TOF was done on Autoflex Speed Bruker Daltonics GmbH. IR Spectra were recorded in Perkin Elmer FT-IR Spectrometer. Aβ42 peptide used in the experiments is procured from GenScript (Catalog no. RP20527-1, Lot No. E3480821K, purity ≥ 95.0%). HEK293T cells (CRL-3216) and SHSY5Y cells (CRL-2266) were procured from ATCC.

Cytotoxicity assay

HEK293T and SHSY5Y Cells were grown in 96-well format DMEM media at starting cell density of 105 cells/mL. The cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 12 h to allow them to adhere and grow. The cells were then treated with 1 and 2 and incubated for 48 h. After 48 h of incubation with the compounds, the MTT stock solution prepared in PBS was added. The compound-treated cells were irradiated at 366 nm in an incubation chamber for 15 min. The cells were further incubated for 1 h after irradiation. Formation of Formazan Crystals was observed after 2 h of incubation with MTT. The formazan crystals were dissolved in DMSO, and the absorbance was recorded at 570 nm using Molecular Devices SpectraMax M2e plate reader.

Cytotoxicity assessment after treatment with fibrillar Aβ42

SHSY5Y cells were grown in 96-well format DMEM media at starting cell density of 105 cells/mL and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2. One hundred micromolar of Aβ42 was incubated at 37 °C for 48 h to ensure fibrillation. Cells were incubated with 20 µM of Aβ42 fibrils to assay the cytotoxic effect of Aβ42. The cytotoxicity was measured by monitoring the reduction in cell viability using MTT assay (as discussed in earlier experiments). Aβ42 fibrils were treated with 1 and 2, followed by irradiation for the designated samples. These treated Aβ42 were also added to the cells to assay the reduction in the cytotoxic effect upon glycodendrimer treatment.

Calculating the binding constant of the 1 and 2 with Aβ42 fibrils

Both the glycodendrimers have intrinsic fluorescence property due to their porphyrin rings. This property was used to detect the binding of the compound with Aβ42. The compounds 1/2 were titrated with increasing concentrations of Aβ42 fibrils, and the fluorescence of the compounds was monitored. The fluorescence intensities obtained were normalized between 0 and 1 for each experiment. The normalized intensities were fit to the equation using Graphpad Prism v8.0.2.

The fluorescence readings were captured using a Take3 plate in BioTek Cytation 5 system. The dendrimers and Aβ42 were mixed in the required ratio, and 2 μL of the sample was loaded onto the plate. The three separate intensity values from three independent experiments were recorded and used for calculating the Kd values.

Monitoring cellular uptake

HEK293T or SHSY5Y cells were seeded in a glass bottom 35 mm dish at a cell density of 105 cells/mL. The cells were allowed to adhere to the glass bottom for 24 h. The fibrils were treated with IC50 concentrations of 1 and 2 (determined from Thioflavin T binding assay, Fig. 4C). The cells were treated with 1 and 2 and incubated for 6–8 h. The cells were monitored using Leica Confocal Microscope. The porphyrin rings of the compounds were directly monitored using the 405 nm laser and an emission range of 650–670 nm.

Monitoring Aβ42 fibrils with Thioflavin T

The lyophilized Aβ42 samples were dissolved in DMSO at a final concentration of 5 mM. The samples were diluted to a final working concentration of 20 μM in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer. The samples were incubated for 24 h to ensure fibril formation. The fibril formation was monitored using Thioflavin T, which specifically binds to amyloid fibrils. The thioflavin fluorescence was monitored with an excitation at 450 and 350 nm, with respective emission maxima at 482 and 438 nm. The samples were treated with compounds 1 and 2 for 24 h. Following incubation, the samples were irradiated at 365 nm. The disruption of the fibrils was observed by monitoring the ThT fluorescence.

Monitoring secondary structure of Aβ42 using circular dichroism

The incubated samples of Aβ42 were diluted to 5 μM in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer. The samples were monitored at different timepoints up to 5 days from 190 nm to 300 nm using a Jasco CD1500 spectrophotometer. The fibrils were treated with IC50 concentrations of 1 and 2 (determined from the Thioflavin T binding assay, Fig. 4C). The raw CD spectra were converted to molar ellipticity before plotting the final data. The path length is 1.0 mm, number of scans: 3, baseline subtracted (buffer).

Monitoring the size of the Aβ42 fibrils using DLS

The incubated samples of Aβ42 were diluted to 20 μM in 20 mM sodium phosphate buffer. The fibrils were treated with IC50 concentrations of 1 and 2 (determined from Thioflavin T binding assay, Fig. 4C). The particle size and zeta potential of the amyloid fibril samples were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements, using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument (Malvern, UK) equipped with a 4 mV He–Ne laser operating at λ = 633 nm (scattering angle = 173°). Each Sample was recorded for 3 repeats with 11 scans each. The treated samples were irradiated for 2 h before measuring the decrease in fibril formation with DLS. The theoretical hydrodynamic radius was calculated using the Hullrad algorithm77. The PDB IDs: 6ZSF and 5KK3 were used for the monomer and oligomeric state (18 Aβ42 units), respectively. The higher order oligomeric states (up to 1056 Aβ42 units) were generated on PyMol, exported as PDB files, and uploaded to the Hullrad server for calculation.

Monitoring formation of Aβ42 multimers using urea SDS PAGE

The Aβ42 multimers were stabilized using photo-induced cross-linking of unmodified proteins. The fibrils were treated with IC50 concentrations of 1 and 2 (determined from Thioflavin T binding assay, Fig. 4C). Fifty micromolar of Aβ42 was loaded per reaction sample along with 100 μM of RuBPy and 2 mM ammonium persulfate. The samples were mixed in the dark and irradiated (25 watts) at 425 nm at a 10 cm distance for 1 min. The 30 μL of these samples were then loaded onto 16.5% urea SDS PAGE and run at 120 V with G-250 loading dye. The samples were then monitored by staining using G250 containing gel staining dye or silver staining.

Atomic force microscopy

Diluted samples (final concentration of each sample was 20 µM) were deposited on the cleaned silicon wafers. The fibrils were treated with IC50 concentrations of 1 and 2 (determined from Thioflavin T binding assay, Fig. 4C). The drop-casted samples were dried and imaged by tapping mode probe using Oxford MFD-3D Infinity AFM.

Scanning electron microscopy

For the preparation of samples, 3 μL of the sample was drop-cast on a quartz sheet. The fibrils were treated with IC50 concentrations of 1 and 2 (determined from Thioflavin T binding assay, Fig. 4C). The sample was allowed to settle for 5 min, and excess solution was wicked off. The sample was dried overnight in a desiccator. To remove any additional water, the sample was further dried in a heated vacuum oven at 50 °C. For SEM analysis, quartz sheets with the samples were mounted on an adhesive stand; a 3 nm gold coating was done by a gold sputter coating unit (SCD 040 Balzers Union sputterer). The surface topology was recorded using a Field Carl Zeiss Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (Carl Zeiss, Germany make; model: SIGMA-0261) with a tungsten filament at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV and 100000X magnification.
