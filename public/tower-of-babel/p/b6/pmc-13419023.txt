Serological epidemiology of hepatitis B virus and vaccine-induced immune dynamics in 58,169 children in Wuhan, China (2019–2025): a cross-sectional analysis of waning immunity and adolescent susceptibility
Authors: Jie Yang, Fan Wang, Jingtao huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesDespite the success of the Expanded Program on Immunization (EPI) in reducing hepatitis B virus (HBV) prevalence, the long-term durability of vaccine-induced immunity and the precise trajectory of antibody decay in Chinese pediatric populations remain under-characterized. This study investigated the seroprevalence of hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) and the kinetics of hepatitis B surface antibody (anti-HBs) in a large cohort of children in Wuhan to inform prime-boost vaccination strategies.MethodsWe conducted a single-center, cross-sectional retrospective analysis of 58,169 children aged 0–17 years evaluated at Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University between January 2019 and November 2025. Serum HBsAg and anti-HBs levels were qualitative/quantified using chemiluminescence immunoassays (CLIA). Data were stratified by age, gender, and year. Statistical analyses utilized the Chi-square test for categorical variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test with Bonferroni‑corrected post-hoc pairwise comparisons for continuous variables.ResultsThe overall HBsAg seroprevalence was 1.27% (723/56,956). However, this rate was heavily skewed by a high frequency of transient HBsAg reactivity in neonates aged 1–30 days (11.97%), likely attributable to vaccine-induced antigenemia and assay interference. After excluding infants < 1 year, the adjusted HBsAg prevalence was 0.47%, which declined significantly from 2019 (0.81%) to 2023 (0.27%). A secondary peak in HBsAg prevalence (1.16%) was observed in adolescents aged 13 years. Among 57,004 subjects assessed for immunity, 66.27% were seroprotected (anti-HBs ≥ 10 mIU/mL). Anti-HBs seropositivity peaked at 92.64% in infants aged 7–12 months but declined precipitously to 48.98% by age 3 years. From age 5 to 17 years, protective antibody rates stabilized at suboptimal levels (30–40%). While males exhibited a higher overall response rate than females (67.21% vs. 65.03%; P < 0.001), median titers in responders did not differ by sex.ConclusionsAlthough the HBsAg prevalence in Wuhan’s pediatric population has decreased, the persistence of a 0.47% prevalence in children > 1 year and a resurgence of infection in early adolescence highlight gaps in the current elimination strategy. The rapid decay of anti-HBs titers by age 3 and the “juvenile pause” in immune surveillance suggest critical windows of vulnerability. These findings support the implementation of serological monitoring and targeted booster vaccination at ages 2–3 and 12–13 years to sustain population-level immunity and achieve the WHO 2030 elimination targets.

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection remains a critical global public health challenge and a leading cause of liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). According to World Health Organization data, an estimated 254 million people—approximately 3.3% of the global population—were living with chronic HBV infection in 2022, with 1.23 million people(corresponding to 16 per 100,000 people) newly infected, and about 1.1 million people (14 per 100,000) died of chronic HBV in 2022 [1]. The burden is disproportionately concentrated in the Western Pacific region, where vertical and early horizontal transmission have historically driven high endemicity.

China has achieved remarkable milestones in HBV control through the stepwise strengthening of its National Immunization Program (NIP). The introduction of the hepatitis B vaccine in 1992, followed by its inclusion as a fully state-funded immunization for all newborns in 2002 [2, 3], together with the implementation of a nationwide program to prevent mother-to-child transmission of HIV, syphilis, and HBV—which initially covered 1,156 counties (representing 44% of pregnant women nationwide) before being expanded to universal coverage in 2015 [4]-contributed to a significant decline in HBsAg seroprevalence. Among individuals aged 1–29 years, the rate dropped from 10.13% in 1992 to approximately 2.64% in 2014, with a reduction as high as 97% observed in children under 5 years of age [5, 6].

However, the “last mile” of HBV elimination presents distinct immunological and epidemiological challenges. First, vaccine-induced immunity is not lifelong for all individuals. While immune memory B cells persist, circulating anti-HBs titers decline over time, a phenomenon known as “waning immunity.” Studies indicate that 15–50% of vaccinees may have undetectable antibodies (< 10 mIU/mL) 5–15 years post-immunization, potentially leaving them susceptible to breakthrough infections upon high-load exposure. It is estimated that about 12% of the global burden of chronic HBV infection occurs in children [7]. HBV infection acquired in childhood often progresses to chronic hepatitis B, which requires long-term medical management [8].Second, the emergence of “breakthrough infections” in adolescents—characterized by HBsAg seroconversion in previously vaccinated individuals—has been documented, particularly during the physiological transition of puberty. Third, regional disparities persist; while community-based surveys often report low prevalence, hospital-based data from tertiary centers may reveal a different landscape of occult or complex infections.

Wuhan, a central city in China, represents a unique epidemiological niche with high population mobility and a mix of urban and rural healthcare seekers. Recent data from 2023 reported a pediatric HBsAg prevalence of 0.13% in Wuhan [9], but comprehensive data on the kinetics of antibody decay and age-specific infection risks in the post-pandemic era (2019–2025) are limited.

This study aims to provide an exhaustive serological analysis of 56,956 children aged 0–17 years admitted to a tertiary hospital in Wuhan. We sought to: (1) determine the precise age-stratified prevalence of HBsAg and anti-HBs; (2) characterize the trajectory of vaccine-induced antibody decay from infancy to adolescence; (3) investigate the phenomenon of high HBsAg reactivity in neonates; and (4) identify specific age windows of vulnerability to inform evidence-based booster vaccination policies.

Method

Study design and patient population

This single-center, cross-sectional study analyzed clinical data from pediatric patients (0–17 years) admitted to Renmin Hospital of Wuhan University between January 1, 2019, and November 12, 2025. Inclusion criteria: (1) Children and adolescents aged 0–18 years, irrespective of their primary diagnosis; (2) Subjects who undergone serological testing for HBsAg or HBsAb in our hospital. Exclusion criteria: (1) Duplicated records: multiple test records from the same subject obtained more than one year apart were all retained, whereas those within a one-year window were processed as follows: first, records with identical results were deduplicated (a single record was randomly selected and retained); second, all records with differing results were kept; (2) Subjects’ demographic and clinical characteristics were incomplete. For the analysis of HBsAb dynamics, patients who tested positive for HBsAg were excluded to prevent confounding. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Hubei People’s Hospital (WDRY2019-K080), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective, anonymized nature of the data.

Laboratory assays and definitions

Serum specimens from participants were processed in accordance with standard hospital laboratory protocols. HBsAg and HBsAb testing was performed at the time of initial blood collection. Analyses were conducting using the ARCHITECT i2000SR (Abbott Diagnostics) or the Cobas e602 (Roche Diagnostics) chemiluminescence immunoassay (CLIA) platforms.

Statistical analysis

The database was built with Microsoft Excel software. Categorical variables (seroprevalence rates) were compared using the Chi-square (χ²) test. Continuous variables (antibody titers), which followed non-normal distributions, were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) and compared using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons utilized the Mann-Whitney U test with Bonferroni correction for multiple hypothesis testing. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 19.0, with statistical significance set at two-tailed P < 0.05.

A total of 58,169 patients records were screened. After excluding 1213 cases with missing basic information (40 cases) or HBsAg results (1173 cases), 56,956 cases were included in the HBsAg analysis (Table 1). Among them, 37,778 were HBsAb-reactive, yielding a positivity rate of 66.27% (Table 3.). Within the reactive cases, normal response accounted for the highest proportion at 41.39%, followed by low response at 38.10%, and high response at 20.51%. Of the total samples, 32,307 were male and 24,697 were female. Among males, 21,715 were HBsAb-reactive, compared with 16,063 reactive females. The reactive rates were 67.21% for males and 65.04% for females, with a statistically significant difference in positivity rates (χ² = 31.502, P < 0.001). However, among the HBsAb-reactive subjects, no significant difference in HBsAb titers was observed between males and females (Z = -0.418, P = 0.676). Median titers [IQR] were 178.80 [50.59, 739.62] for males and 182.94 [48.47, 737.25] for females.The HBsAb reactive rate in children showed an increasing trend from within 24 h after birth up to 1 year of age, rising from 69.13% within 24 h to a peak of 98.48% in the 7–12-month age group. After the first year, the rate gradually declined with age, falling to 55.17% by 12 years of age, and thereafter remained stable between 50% and 55% in children over 12 years old.

An HBsAb titer above 100 mIU/mL (corresponding to a normal or high response) is generally considered indicative of adequate protection. Among the pediatric population studied, the overall rate of normal and high responders was 41.02% (Fig. 1).

From within 24 h after birth up to 1 year of age, the proportion of infants with a protective titer (normal or high response) showed a generally increasing trend. However, a temporary decline was observed at 1 month of age compared to the 1–30‑day age group, after which the rate continued to rise. The proportion peaked at 92.64% in infants aged 7–12 months, then gradually decreased thereafter.

Notable declines of more than 10% points occurred at 2 years (dropping to 60.87%) and again at 3 years (48.98%). By 5 years of age, the rate had fallen to 37.50%, and thereafter remained between 30% and 40% in children older than 5 years.

Regarding the antibody titers among all reactive children, the trend in median antibody titer aligned with the rate of normal and high responders from birth to 1 year of age, showing a general upward trend (< 24 h vs. 1-30D, Z=-5.278, P < 0.001). However, the median titer at 1 month of age was lower than that in the 1-30-day group (1-30D vs. 1 M, Z=-4.092, P < 0.001), before resuming its increase. The median titer peaked at 1001 mIU/mL in infants aged 7–12 months, after which it gradually declined.

A steep drop occurred at 2 years of age, falling from 560.50 mIU/mL at 1 year to 183.95 mIU/mL at 2 years (Z=-16.140, P < 0.001). The titer reached its lowest point of 108.70 mIU/mL at 5 years of age. Although differences in median titers were observed among the 3, 4, 5, and 6-year-old groups, they were not statistically significant ( χ²=7.705, P = 0.053).

There was a noticeable, though not statistically significant, increase from age 6y- to 7y- (Z=-1.560, P = 0.119). No significant difference in antibody titers was found among the 6y-, 7y-, 8y-, and 9-year-old groups (χ²=6.821, P = 0.078). A relatively clear increase was also seen from age 9y- (154.15 mIU/mL) to 10y- (175.20 mIU/mL), which was again not statistically significant (Z=-1.269, P = 0.205). Similarly, no significant differences in titers were observed among children aged 10 to 17 years (χ²=9.422, P = 0.224).

Discussion

In China, the hepatitis B infection rate among individuals aged 0–80 years varies between 5.0% and 10.0%, depending on the region and study population [10–13]. Although the overall infection rate has been declining, it remains higher compared to rates observed in European and American countries [14].

This large-scale retrospective study of over 56,000 children in Wuhan identifies distinct phases of HBV immune dynamics, highlighting a critical window of waning immunity in early childhood that precedes an adolescent resurgence in infection risk.

Our overall HBsAg positivity rate of 1.27% appears high compared to recent national surveys (0.47% in children aged 1–10 years and 1.1% in individuals aged 1–20 years [12]). It also exceeds the 0.51% positivity reported in another study focusing on children aged 0–10 years in Jilin Province [15]. Similar to other studies [9, 16], HBsAg-positive cases in this study were primarily observed among infants and adolescents. However, this is largely driven by the 11.97% reactive rate in neonates (1–30 days). We posit that this high neonatal rate largely reflects false positives rather than true infection, for three reasons: (1) Median titers in this group were low ; (2) Neonatal hyperbilirubinemia, common in this age group, is a known interferent in CLIA assays [17]; and (3) Transient antigenemia can occur up to 14 days post-vaccination [18, 19]. When excluding infants <, the adjusted HBsAg reactivity rate was 0.47%, aligning with national goals, though slightly higher than the 0.13% reported in a strictly urban Wuhan cohort [9]. This likely reflects our hospital’s status as a regional referral center receiving complex cases from rural areas where prevalence remains higher [13, 14].

Our findings indicate no significant gender-based difference in HBsAg reactivity among children aged 0–17 years. While some studies suggest that males generally have a higher overall HBsAg positivity rate than females [12, 15], the same retrospective study reports no gender disparity in individuals ≤ 20 years old, with differences becoming apparent only in those older than 20. Similarly, a study of children aged 0–18 years in Wuhan observed no statistically significant gender gap in HBsAg positivity rates [9].

Females typically mount stronger innate, humoral, and cellular immune responses to viral infections and vaccination compared to males. Sex hormones influence antiviral immunity by binding to specific receptors on immune cells: estrogens generally enhance immune activity, whereas androgens tend to be immunosuppressive [20]. Notably, androgens can also directly bind to integrated HBV DNA in the nucleus and promote the transcription of viral oncoproteins.

However, during childhood and early adolescence, the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis remains largely suppressed—a phase often termed the “juvenile pause”—during which sex hormone levels are low and gender differences in hormonal activity are minimal. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) pulsatility is restrained throughout this developmental period [21]. Thus, sex hormone-mediated immune differences between genders likely become more pronounced with increasing age, as suggested in the retrospective analysis [12]. Moreover, in early life, boys and girls generally have comparable exposure to HBV infection and vaccination, with access not significantly influenced by gender.

HBsAb is an antibody produced by the body in response to stimulation by HBsAg, which helps clear HBV and provides protection against infection [22]. Therefore, a positive HBsAb indicates established immunity against HBV. Since patients in gastroenterology or hepatobiliary surgery departments may have reduced levels of hepatitis B surface antibody due to their underlying conditions, but our hospital is not a specialized center in this field, with its strengths primarily in Pediatrics, psychiatry, Otorhinolaryngology, and ophthalmology. Most of our patients are concentrated in these departments. The total number of cases in hepatobiliary-pancreatic surgery and gastroenterology combined is 1,435. Moreover, after subgroup analysis, there were no statistically significant differences among the three groups–the overall ungrouped population and the two subgroups (i.e., the hepatobiliary-pancreatic surgery and gastroenterology group and the non- hepatobiliary-pancreatic surgery and gastroenterology group)--in terms of the HBsAb positive rate, the composition of different positive reaction intensities (χ²=3.700, P = 0.717), or antibody titers (Z=-1.221, P = 0.222). Therefore, no distinction was made among the groups in the analysis. In this study, the overall HBsAb reactivity rate in our population was 66.27%, with normal and high reactivity rates being even lower at 41.02%. HBsAb positivity increased with age starting from the neonatal period, which is closely associated with China’s vaccination protocol: the first dose of HBV vaccine is administered within 24 h of birth, followed by the second and third doses according to schedule [10].

A key finding is the precipitous drop in HBsAb titers between ages 1 and 3. While the 0-1-6 vaccination schedule induces a robust response peaking at 7–12 months. Multiple studies have demonstrated that an anti-HBs titer > 100 mIU/mL is required to confer effective immunoprotection against HBV infection [23–25]. Although HBV breakthrough infections can occur in vaccinated individuals, a single adolescent booster dose has been shown to protect high-risk individuals against chronic HBV infection in adulthood [26], for children with titers below 100 mIU/mL, timely administration of a booster dose is necessary. We observed that by age 3, fewer than 50% of children maintained titers ≥ 100 mIU/mL. This contradicts the assumption that infant vaccination universally confers high-titer protection throughout early childhood.While immune memory (anamestic response) often protects against clinical disease despite low titers [27–30], the concurrent rise in HBsAg positivity observed in our adolescent cohort (rising from 0.26% at age 11 to 1.16% at age 13) suggests that waning humoral immunity may leave some individuals vulnerable to breakthrough infections upon increased exposure.

The spike in HBsAg prevalence at age 12–13 marks a second epidemiological risk point. This age correlates with the onset of puberty, increased social autonomy, and the “juvenile pause” in hormonal suppression ending. While we found no significant gender differences in infection rates—likely due to the low sex hormone levels in prepubescent children—the rising infection rate in teenagers suggests that infant vaccination alone may not suffice for lifelong sterilization of the virus in all individuals.

Current guidelines debate the necessity of boosters. Our data supports a targeted approach:

Surveillance at Age 2–3: Given the sharp titer decay, serological screening at this age could identify low-responders who might benefit from an early booster.

Pre-Adolescent Booster: The clear increase in HBsAg prevalence at age 12–13 supports the administration of a booster dose at age 10–11 (similar to HPV vaccination timing) to “top up” immunity before the high-risk adolescent period.

In any case, HBV is transmitted through contact with infected blood or body fluids-such as during pregnancy, delivery, sexual contact, or injection drug use-with the highest risk of chronic infection occurring following perinatal exposure [31]. Global studies conducted between 1974 and 2007 found that 14%-60% of household contacts of persons with chronic HBV infection showed serological evidence of resolved infection, while 3%-30% had chronic infection [32]. We emphasize the necessity of establishing comprehensive family-based prevention and control measures through continued testing, immunization, prevention, and treatment to reduce HBV infection rates and improve population protection.In summary, the detection of HBV serological markers remains the most reliable indicator for determining the status of HBV infection and viral replication in vivo, as well as for evaluating the body’s protective immunity against HBV [33]. Our serological analysis of HBsAg and HBsAb in children aged 0–17 years provides valuable insights for monitoring HBV epidemiological trends and characteristics, thereby supporting the optimization and improvement of HBV prevention and control strategies.

Limitations.

This study has limitations inherent to its retrospective design. We lacked individual vaccination histories and maternal HBsAg status, preventing differentiation between vaccine failure and perinatal transmission. Additionally, as a hospital-based study, the population may be enriched for comorbidities compared to the general healthy population.

Conclusion

While HBV prevalence in Wuhan children remains generally low, vaccine-induced antibody levels decline significantly by age 3, leaving a substantial proportion of children with low circulating antibodies. Coupled with the observed resurgence of HBsAg positivity in early adolescence, these findings argue for enhanced seromonitoring and consideration of booster doses at ages 2–3 and pre-adolescence to eliminate residual HBV transmission.
