Optimizing Surgery Strategies in Stage IB Lung Squamous Cell Carcinoma: Insights from Interpretable Machine Learning
Authors: Qunzhe Ding, Chutong Lin, Yatsu Lam, Fuxin Guo, Shanwu Ma, Guangliang Qiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundStage IB lung squamous‐cell carcinoma (LSCC) lacks individualized survival prediction tools. There's debate on adjuvant chemotherapy's benefit. This study aimed to develop an interpretable machine‐learning model for stage IB LSCC survival prediction and re‐evaluate postoperative chemotherapy's added value.MethodsA total of 6445 patients with stage IB LSCC diagnosed between 2000 and 2015 were extracted from the SEER database. Patients from 2000 to 2014 (n = 5740) were split 7:3 into training and internal validation cohorts, while those from 2015 (n = 705) served as the external validation cohort. Six machine‐learning algorithms (including logistic regression and gradient‐boosting models) were trained to predict 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year overall survival (OS) with hyperparameter optimization via 10‐fold cross‐validation. SHAP analysis ensured model interpretability, and 1:1 nearest‐neighbor propensity‐score matching evaluated chemotherapy benefit in completely resected patients.ResultsThe LightGBM model achieved the best discriminative performance (AUC = 0.834, 0.828, 0.800 for 1‐, 3‐, 5‐year OS) with excellent generalizability in external validation. SHAP analysis identified treatment modality as the top survival predictor; both surgery alone and surgery plus chemotherapy improved survival, but no significant OS difference was observed between the two strategies across all timepoints, consistent across subgroups (age, tumor size, etc.). Propensity‐score matching of 565 patients confirmed similar outcomes (median OS: 64 vs. 62 months; HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.82–1.20; p = 0.893).ConclusionThis study gives individualized survival estimates for stage IB LSCC, backing a risk‐adapted conservative adjuvant treatment approach. High‐risk subgroups got no extra benefit from postoperative chemotherapy, which may help integrate precision medicine and shared decision‐making in early‐stage LSCC management.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the most common cancer and a leading cause of cancer‐related mortality worldwide [1, 2]. Non–small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for 85% of all lung cancers [3]. Lung squamous cell carcinoma (LSCC) is a primary subtype of NSCLC, accounting for approximately 20% of all cases [4, 5]. Stage IB LSCC represents a critical early phase in cancer progression, characterized by tumors that, while still relatively small, begin showing signs of local invasion. The treatment varies with the stage of cancer. For patients with stage IB lung cancer who have a positive epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) gene test result, current clinical guidelines recommend postoperative adjuvant targeted therapy. However, due to the relatively low positive rate of gene mutations in patients with LSCC, postoperative gene testing is generally not recommended at present [6]. For these IB LSCC patients, however, if the postoperative pathology indicates high‐risk factors for recurrence, such as poorly differentiated tumors, visceral pleural invasion, vascular invasion, and spread through air spaces, adjuvant therapy after surgical resection could be considered in these cases combined with the patient's wishes and the results of the multidisciplinary team (MDT) [7]. Study shows that the 5‐year OS rate for LSCC patients remains at just 18% [8]. In contrast, the 5‐year overall survival rate for stage I non‐small cell lung cancer patients who are typically treated with surgery remains up to 80% [9].

Despite the preference for surgical intervention, the choice for postoperative treatment remains controversial. Clinical guidelines recommend adjuvant targeted therapy for stage IB lung cancer patients with driver gene positivity after surgery [10]. However, the proportion of driver gene positivity in squamous cell carcinoma of the lung is quite low, and clinical guidelines generally do not recommend gene testing for this group [11]. There is still controversy regarding whether adjuvant chemotherapy is needed for these stage IB lung squamous cell carcinoma patients after surgery, and no definitive recommendations have been made by the various clinical guidelines [12, 13, 14]. Additionally, the recurrence rates of patients at the same stage of cancer are heterogeneous, making such homogeneous treatment choices implausible [15]. Therefore, the clinical management of early‐stage LSCC is hindered by a lack of predictive models that can forecast patients' survival by different characteristics.

To overcome these limitations, the current study utilizes data from the SEER database [16], consisting of 10 864 patients diagnosed with early‐stage (IB) LSCC between 2000 and 2019. Using six different machine learning algorithms—Linear Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and CatBoost—we aimed to develop a robust prognostic model for predicting patient survival across 1‐year, 3‐year, and 5‐year intervals with validation.

A significant limitation of machine learning models is their “black‐box” nature, which makes it challenging to interpret and understand the relationship between patients' clinical features and the prediction outcomes. To address this issue, we incorporate the Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) method, a powerful algorithm rooted in game theory, to interpret the model's predictions. SHAP provides a comprehensive framework for explaining individual predictions by assigning an importance value to each feature, thereby offering insights into how each variable influences the model's output [17]. This interpretability is crucial for clinical applications, as it allows healthcare professionals to understand the factors driving the model's predictions, ultimately aiding in more informed decision‐making for clinicians. Furthermore, SHAP is a powerful method for explaining individual predictions and interpreting machine learning models on a global scale. It uses Shapley values to fairly distribute credit among features, providing a quantifiable measure of their impact on model predictions. Specifically, SHAP force plots offer an intuitive way to visualize how individual features influence a specific prediction, aiding in the understanding of the model's decision‐making process. In terms of global interpretation, SHAP not only identifies the importance of each feature but also reveals their interactions and relationships with the model's output, offering a comprehensive overview of feature influence. Moreover, SHAP ensures fair distribution of predictions across various feature values, which enhances the overall interpretability and reliability of the model.

By combining machine learning techniques with the SHAP method, our study not only aims to develop an accurate prognostic model for early‐stage LSCC but also to provide an interpretable framework that can support clinicians in tailoring treatment strategies, thereby improving patient outcomes.

Materials and Methods

Data Extraction/Study Population and Preprocessing

This study was conducted relying specifically on the data sourced from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database. This database holds comprehensive information on cancer incidence, treatment, and survival, and it pertains to approximately 28% of the US population [18, 19]. The data used in this study were generated from “SEER Research Plus Data, 17 Registries, Nov 2023 Sub (2000–2021)” using SEER * Stat software (version 8.4.0.1; National Cancer Institute, USA).

This study used SEER database public data for retrospective analysis. The data had all personally identifiable information removed, meeting public data ethical exemption criteria. Thus, it required no Institutional Review Board approval or subject informed consent, and data use complied with SEER regulations.

Patients with a histological diagnosis of LSCC between January 2000 and December 2015 were selected from the SEER database to form the cohort. Among these patients, those diagnosed between 2000 and 2014 were used for model construction and internal validation, whereas patients diagnosed in 2015 constituted the external validation cohort. The following was the selection process (as shown in Figure 1). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Lung squamous cell carcinoma was confirmed by histopathology and morphologically in accordance with ICDO‐3/WHO 2008 guidelines; (2) Patients with squamous cell carcinoma in stage IB according to the 8th edition of the AJCC (American Joint Committee on Cancer) lung cancer staging system. (3) Patients only received lobectomy, sub‐lobectomy, or did not receive surgical resection.

Flow chart of patients' selection.

Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients whose survival time was unknown; (2) Patients whose surgery approach was either unknown or non‐standard (Rx Summ–Surg Prim Site (1998+) except of 00, 20–24, 30, 33); (3) Missing value for independent variables listed blow; (4) Patients who have undergone preoperative radiotherapy or chemotherapy.

To ensure the dataset's usability and consistency, several preprocessing and feature engineering steps were implemented, focusing on both demographic and pathological characteristics. Demographic features included patient age at diagnosis, marital status, rural–urban continuum, household income, sex, and race. Pathological features encompassed primary site, laterality, radiation record, chemotherapy record, regional nodes examined, surgery type, tumor size, and grade.

The variable “Regional Nodes Examined” was categorized into five ordinal groups: 0, 1–5, 6–10, 11–90, and “Unspecified.” Surgical records were reclassified into “Surgery Performed” and “Surgery Not Performed,” and subsequently combined with chemotherapy records to create a composite variable, “Surgery Type,” comprising four categories: “Surgery Performed Only,” “Surgery and Chemotherapy Performed,” “Chemotherapy Performed Only,” and “Neither Surgery nor Chemotherapy Performed.” This derived feature serves as a key input in our study. To further streamline treatment information, rare categories within the radiation‐surgery sequence variable were consolidated: specifically, “Intraoperative radiation,” “Radiation before and after surgery,” “Sequence unknown, but both were given,” and “Surgery both before and after radiation” were grouped into a unified category labeled “Others.” Additionally, infrequent categories in the marital status variable—namely “Widowed,” “Divorced,” and “Other”—were grouped into a single category labeled “Widow/Divorced/Other.” Similarly, in the race variable, “American Indian/Alaska Native” and “Unknown” were merged into the category “Other.”

To address missing data, an iterative chained‐equations imputation algorithm (a MICE‐type implementation) was applied under a missing‐at‐random assumption. Continuous variables (Tumor Size) were imputed using Bayesian ridge regression models with 20 iterative cycles to ensure convergence. Binary variables (Laterality) were imputed using random forest classification models, and multi‐category variables (Grade, Rural–Urban status, Primary Site, and Regional Nodes Examined) were imputed using gradient boosting classification models, each with 10 iterative cycles and most‐frequent initialization. Missingness proportions were moderate overall (maximum ≈19% for Grade and Rural–Urban variables, < 5% for most others). Given the predictive modeling objective and relatively limited missingness, this procedure generated a single completed dataset that was used for subsequent machine‐learning model development and propensity‐score analyses.

Statistical Analyses

For predictive modeling in this study, the widely recognized scikit‐learn Python library, along with various supplementary model packages, was employed to train the predictive models. Before the model‐building phase, categorical variables were manually encoded to ensure compatibility with machine learning algorithms. This manual encoding approach was meticulously executed to maintain the integrity and interpretability of the data while minimizing potential biases associated with automated encoding methods. The dataset was then randomly divided into training and testing cohorts. Specifically, 70% of the patients were allocated to the training cohort, enabling the models to learn patterns and relationships from the data. The remaining 30% were assigned to the testing cohort, reserved for evaluating the performance and generalizability of the trained models. This 70/30 split is a widely adopted practice in machine learning, balancing the need for adequate training data with a robust dataset for unbiased model assessment. The predictive models utilized in this study encompassed a broad and diverse range of algorithms, including Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting Machines (GBM), XGBoost, CatBoost, and LightGBM. This comprehensive selection reflects a deliberate effort to ensure a wide comparison of modeling approaches, ranging from traditional linear methods (such as LR) to advanced ensemble‐based techniques (such as RF and GBM) and cutting‐edge gradient boosting algorithms (such as XGBoost, CatBoost, and LightGBM). By employing this extensive range of models, the study aimed to thoroughly evaluate different predictive paradigms and identify the most effective algorithm for the task at hand, thereby ensuring the robustness and reliability of the predictive modeling process.

During model training, grid search combined with ten‐fold cross‐validation was utilized to optimize hyperparameters and evaluate model performance. The dataset was partitioned into ten equally sized subsets, with nine subsets serving as the training set and the remaining one as the validation set in each iteration. This process was repeated ten times, rotating through all subsets. Grid search systematically tested various hyperparameter combinations within this ten‐fold cross‐validation framework to identify the optimal configuration, ensuring the best performance on the validation set. This approach reduced the risk of overfitting while enhancing the robustness and generalizability of the model evaluation. Subsequently, the predictive performance of all six models was evaluated using ROC curves, in addition to assessments of precision, recall, and F1 scores.

In our study, based on SHAP feature importance assessment, SHAP importance plot was employed for the global interpretation of the optimal model. Additionally, SHAP was used to explain individual predictions at a local level and to explore feature interactions, providing valuable insights into the model's behavior. SHAP dependence plots were made for this purpose. In the SHAP dependence plots, to explain the conclusions, we conducted intergroup difference tests. If all groups met the assumption of normality, we performed one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to test for intergroup differences; if the assumption of normality was not met, we used the Kruskal‐Wallis test as a nonparametric method. Additionally, to facilitate comparison, we added trend lines to some of the SHAP dependence plots.

Subsequently, we investigated the independent effect of adjuvant chemotherapy after surgery on patients' survival. To attenuate baseline imbalances across surgical groups, propensity‐score matching (PSM) was implemented. Treatment strategy (surgery only VS surgery + chemotherapy) was treated as the exposure variable; conditional on all potential confounders except diagnostic year and survival outcome, individual propensity scores were estimated via multivariable logistic regression with weighting to correct for class imbalance. Logit‐transformed scores were then used to construct a 1:1 matched cohort by nearest‐neighbor matching without replacement, employing a caliper of 0.20 logit units to constrain the maximum allowable score discrepancy between groups. This procedure yielded well‐balanced baseline characteristics, thereby furnishing a robust analytic sample for subsequent survival analyses. The entire PSM workflow was executed with PsmPy version 0.3.13 implemented in Python.

Following propensity‐score matching, baseline characteristics were systematically compared before and after adjustment to evaluate the balance achieved and confirm clinical comparability between the two surgical groups. Specifically, the surgical method (surgery alone vs. surgery combined with chemotherapy) was used as the treatment factor, and the included covariates specifically were: age, gender, race, marital status, T stage, N stage, M stage, tumor grade, histological subtype (squamous cell carcinoma), primary tumor site, and radiotherapy history. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were subsequently performed within the matched cohort, stratifying patients who underwent surgery alone (Group 0) from those who received adjuvant chemotherapy after surgery (Group 1). Between‐group survival differences were formally assessed with the log‐rank test, and a univariable Cox proportional‐hazards model was fitted to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) along with their 95% confidence intervals. Median survival times were also derived for each treatment arm to supplement the survival comparisons. The core workflow was implemented and executed utilizing lifelines version 0.30.3 within the Python programming environment.

Results

Feature Selection

Machine learning–based Recursive Feature Elimination was applied to identify the optimal feature subsets for the 1‐year, 3‐year, and 5‐year survival prediction models (Figure 2). This method iteratively eliminates the least important features based on model‐derived importance scores, continuing until the subset that yields the best validation performance is obtained.

ML‐based recursive feature elimination for 1‐year, 3‐year, and 5‐year survival prediction models.

For the 1‐year survival model, the following features were excluded due to their limited contribution (importance score ≤ 0.01): Primary Site, Grade, and Surg/Rad Sequence. For the 3‐year model, Grade, Race, and Surg/Rad Sequence were removed under the same criterion. For the 5‐year model, a broader set of features was excluded, including Primary Site, Grade, Surg/Rad Sequence, and Rural–Urban Continuum, reflecting shifts in long‐term predictive relevance. By leveraging ML‐based RFE, the final feature sets for each model achieved an effective balance between dimensionality reduction and predictive performance.

Baseline Characteristics

A total of 5470 lung cancer patients from the SEER database were included and stratified by 1‐year overall survival: 1295 (23.67%) patients survived ≤ 12 months, and 4175 (76.33%) survived > 12 months (Table 1). The mean age was significantly higher among patients with survival ≤ 12 months (73.56 vs. 71.46 years, p < 0.001). There were more male patients in the ≤ 12‐month survival group (60.69% vs. 56.86%, p = 0.016). Race, tumor laterality, and primary tumor site distributions were comparable between groups. Patients with survival ≤ 12 months had larger tumors on average (31.78 mm vs. 29.99 mm, p < 0.001), were less likely to undergo lymph node examination, and had a higher proportion of poorly differentiated tumors (Grade III).

Model Comparison

In the model development and validation stage, we first determined the optimal hyperparameters for each model. Specifically, for LightGBM, the same set of hyperparameters was identified as optimal across the 1‐year, 3‐year, and 5‐year survival prediction tasks, including learning_rate = 0.01, max_depth = 3, n_estimators = 200, and num_leaves = 31. This consistency in optimal parameter selection across both short‐ and long‐term prediction horizons indicates a stable pattern in model configuration during tuning. Final models were trained using these optimized settings.

Model performance was then evaluated using five standard metrics: AUC‐ROC, accuracy, precision, recall, and F1‐score (Table 2, Figure 2). Among all models, LightGBM consistently outperformed others across all prediction horizons, demonstrating robust classification ability and generalization. For the 1‐year survival prediction, LightGBM achieved the highest AUC (0.8343), with solid results in accuracy (0.7690), precision (0.7589), recall (0.7669), and F1‐score (0.7615). In the 3‐year prediction task, LightGBM maintained leading performance with an AUC of 0.8283 and F1‐score of 0.6873. For the 5‐year survival prediction, it achieved the best AUC (0.7995), accuracy (0.8229), and F1‐score (0.6374), further confirming its superiority in both short‐ and long‐term prediction tasks.

Global Interpretability Analysis Using SHAP

To interpret the internal logic of the predictive model, we performed a global interpretability analysis using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) on the best‐performing model, LightGBM. Two types of SHAP visualizations were used: the SHAP feature importance plot and the SHAP summary plot (Figure 5). The feature importance plot ranks predictors by their average absolute SHAP values, reflecting their overall contribution to the model's output. The summary plot further displays the direction and distribution of each feature's impact, where color represents feature value (red: high, blue: low), and position along the x‐axis indicates the SHAP value.

SHAP importance and summary plots for 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year survival predictions using LightGBM. Panels (a, c, e) show the average feature importance, while (b, d, f) display the distribution and direction of each feature's impact. Red indicates high feature values, blue indicates low.

Across all three prediction horizons, Surgery type consistently emerged as the most influential variable, followed by Age record and Regional nodes examined. In the 1‐year prediction (Figure 5a,b), the top five features were Surgery type, Age, regional nodes examined, tumor size, and radiation record. The 3‐year prediction (Figure 5c,d) maintained a similar structure, though the magnitude of importance values became slightly more balanced across the top features. In the 5‐year model (Figure 5e,f), Surgery type, Age, and regional nodes examined remained dominant, while Marital status and Sex gained slightly higher importance than tumor size and radiation variables.

From the SHAP summary plots, Surgery type and Age displayed a clear red‐to‐blue gradient from left to right, suggesting that higher values of these features are associated with lower survival probability (i.e., more negative SHAP values). In contrast, for regional nodes examined and Radiation record, a blue‐to‐red transition was observed from left to right, indicating that lower values of these features tend to negatively impact survival, whereas higher values are associated with positive contributions to model output. These patterns suggest that undergoing surgery, receiving radiation therapy, and having more lymph nodes examined are predictive of longer survival across different time points.

Overall, SHAP‐based analysis provides model‐agnostic, interpretable insights that reinforce the clinical relevance of key predictors while validating the internal consistency of the LightGBM model.

Across all time horizons, the model consistently assigned positive SHAP values to patients who underwent surgery only or surgery followed by chemotherapy, indicating a favorable contribution to survival predictions. In contrast, those who received chemotherapy only or neither treatment were associated with negative SHAP values, suggesting a detrimental effect on predicted survival. Notably, in all three models, the SHAP values for the group that received surgery followed by chemotherapy were comparable to those for the surgery‐only group – with no significant difference observed even in terms of lower‐bound SHAP values.

Following the analysis of Surgery type, we next examined the impact of Age on individual survival predictions, as well as its interaction with treatment patterns. Figure 7 illustrates both the standalone SHAP effects of Age (a, c, e) and its interactions with Surgery type (b, d, f) across the 1‐year, 3‐year, and 5‐year models.

In all timeframes, the SHAP values for Age exhibit a nonlinear negative association, with younger age contributing more positively to survival predictions. The fitted quadratic curves (panels a, c, e) reveal a clear downward trajectory: patients aged below approximately 60 years generally receive higher SHAP values, while the predictive contribution of Age declines sharply after this threshold. This trend is consistent across 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year models, with R2 values exceeding 0.87, indicating stable and strong age‐related effects on predicted survival.

The interaction plots (b, d, f) further illustrate how Surgery type modifies the marginal effect of Age. For patients receiving surgery only or surgery followed by chemotherapy, the Age–SHAP relationship is relatively smooth and follows a predictable parabolic decline. However, for those receiving chemotherapy only, SHAP values are more dispersed and less sensitive to age, particularly in older patients. Notably, in the 1‐year, 3‐year, and 5‐year plots (b, d, f), the survival benefits of surgery alone and surgery combined with chemotherapy are comparable, and both are higher than those of patients who only receive non‐surgical treatment. However, this advantage gradually decreases with age, and when patients are over approximately 70 years old, their survival benefit begins to be weaker than that of patients who only receive non‐surgical treatment.

We further explored the impact of regional nodes examined, a key clinical indicator of diagnostic thoroughness and disease staging. Figure 8 presents both the standalone SHAP effects (a, c, e) and interaction patterns with Surgery type (b, d, f) across 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year prediction models. Across all timeframes, patients with no lymph node examination (0 nodes) consistently received negative SHAP values, indicating a strong association with lower predicted survival. In contrast, those with 6–10 or more nodes examined had notably higher and more stable SHAP values, suggesting that more extensive nodal evaluation is predictive of favorable survival outcomes. This trend remained stable across the 1‐year, 3‐year, and 5‐year prognostic prediction periods, with no significant differences observed between the surgery‐only group and the post‐surgery chemotherapy group.

We further examined tumor size, a known prognostic factor in cancer survival, to understand its nonlinear contribution and interaction with surgical treatment. Figure 9 presents the SHAP main effects (a, c, e) and interaction plots (b, d, f) across the three survival models. Across all timeframes, the main effect plots (a, c, e) reveal a U‐shaped quadratic relationship between tumor size and SHAP value, with a local minimum around 30–40 mm. Specifically, SHAP values decreased with increasing tumor size up to this threshold.

In the propensity‐score‐matched cohort, Kaplan–Meier survival curves were constructed and compared by log‐rank test, and a univariable Cox proportional‐hazards model was fitted to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (Figure 10). No significant difference in overall survival was observed between patients with stage IB squamous cell carcinoma who underwent surgery alone and those who received adjuvant chemotherapy after surgery (median survival, 62 months vs. 64 months; HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.82–1.20, p = 0.893).

To further clarify the association between postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy and survival in stage IB squamous cell carcinoma, we performed a comprehensive set of subgroup analyses within the propensity‐score‐matched cohort. Patients were first stratified according to the number of regional lymph nodes examined—0, 1–5, 6–10, and 11–90—and the previously described Kaplan–Meier survival analyses, log‐rank tests, and univariable Cox proportional‐hazards models were repeated within each stratum; no statistically significant difference in overall survival was observed between those treated with surgery alone and those who additionally received adjuvant chemotherapy (Figure S2). Next, we dichotomized the cohort by tumor size (≤ 30 mm vs. > 30 mm) and again found no significant survival distinction between treatment strategies within either subgroup (Figure S3).

Subsequent analyses stratified patients by tumor grade (grades I–II vs. III–IV) and by age (< 65 years vs. ≥ 65 years) (Figures S4 and S5).; in every instance, overall survival did not differ between patients undergoing surgery alone and those receiving adjuvant chemotherapy.

Discussion

In this large‐scale, population‐based study leveraging the SEER research data, we developed and externally validated a highly discriminating and well‐calibrated prognostic framework for patients diagnosed with stage IB lung squamous cell carcinoma. Across 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year horizons, the LightGBM ensemble consistently outperformed five comparator algorithms, achieving AUCs of 0.834, 0.828, and 0.800, respectively, in the internal cohort and comparable metrics in an independent external validation set. Integration of Shapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) revealed that treatment modality—particularly the receipt of definitive surgical resection—exerted the dominant influence on predicted survival, followed by patient age at diagnosis, the number of regional lymph nodes examined, tumor size and use of radiotherapy. These relationships remained stable over time, underscoring the robustness of the model.

Subsequent propensity‐score‐matched survival analyses further demonstrated that the addition of adjuvant chemotherapy to complete surgical resection did not confer a significant overall survival benefit (median survival 62 vs. 64 months; HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.82–1.20; p = 0.893). This absence of incremental efficacy persisted across all predefined high‐risk substrata, including patients with poorly differentiated tumors, visceral pleural invasion, tumor diameter > 30 mm, extensive nodal sampling or age ≥ 65 years. Collectively, these findings affirm that surgical resection with adequate nodal assessment remains the cornerstone of curative therapy for stage IB lung squamous cell carcinoma, while routine postoperative cytotoxic chemotherapy appears to offer no discernible survival advantage in this early‐stage population.

In recent years, there have been several studies on survival prediction models for squamous cell carcinoma of the lung. Significant progress has also been made in the development of survival prediction models for squamous cell carcinoma of the lung, particularly those based on clinical and pathological factors [20, 21, 22, 23]. For instance, comprehensive models based on SEER database that integrate clinical, pathological, and demographic factors have demonstrated excellent performance in prognostic stratification and robustness across different treatment modalities and patient risk spectra [24]. But this model includes both stage IA and IB populations, making it unable to predict for stage IB patients alone. Additionally, for specific stages such as stage IB non‐small cell lung cancer patients, researchers have developed prognostic models that can more accurately predict survival rates by analyzing multiple clinical and pathological factors [25]. However, this model can only distinguish between high‐risk, medium‐risk, and low‐risk groups and is unable to predict survival outcomes for personalized treatment plans. Additionally, this model is a single‐center study, limiting the generalizability of its conclusions. A nomogram was also developed to predict survival for elderly patients with squamous cell carcinoma using SEER database data, incorporating clinical, demographic, and treatment factors, and demonstrated superior performance compared to AJCC staging in prognostic evaluation [26]. However, this model only includes the elderly population and cannot predict survival for stage IB squamous cell carcinoma patients specifically as well. Our study, based on a large amount of clinical data from the SEER database, specifically targets stage IB squamous cell carcinoma patients and combines the latest machine learning techniques to accurately predict survival outcomes for different treatment plans, offering significant advantages and practicality. In our study, the LightGBM model performed exceptionally well—not only achieving the most outstanding performance on the internal validation set but also maintaining remarkable stability and accuracy on the external validation set, fully demonstrating its strong generalization ability across time. Meanwhile, our study is also one of the few that has incorporated external validation.

In the realm of genetics and molecular markers, various prognostic models have been developed using gene expression data [27, 28, 29, 30, 31, 32]. For example, a signature model based on 12 genes can effectively distinguish between high‐risk and low‐risk groups of stage I squamous cell carcinoma patients [33]. Furthermore, immune‐related gene models have shown good predictive capabilities, allowing patients to be categorized into different risk groups [34]. Models related to m6A modification genes have also performed well in prognostic prediction [35]. Regarding the tumor microenvironment and immune cell infiltration, researchers have developed various prognostic models by analyzing the infiltration of immune cells and the characteristics of the tumor microenvironment [36, 37, 38]. These models can stratify patients into different risk groups and are associated with their response to immunotherapy [39]. Moreover, prognostic models based on tumor microenvironment scores can effectively predict patient survival [40]. Other biomarkers, such as proteins and circulating RNAs, have also shown potential in the prognostic prediction of squamous cell carcinoma of the lung [41, 42]. Compared to our prediction model, most of the above models require complex molecular biological tests, which have not yet been widely conducted in clinical settings. In contrast, this study is based on a large dataset from the SEER database, incorporating only clinical characteristics and sociodemographic factors of patients. These data are more readily available and offer greater accuracy and generalizability. Furthermore, the large volume of data and the excellent machine learning algorithms allowed our model to perform well in validation without the need for complex molecular biological testing. Regarding accuracy, most models above have AUC values between 0.7 and 0.8, performing less effectively than our model.

The application of radiomics and machine learning technologies has provided new approaches for the prognostic prediction of squamous cell carcinoma of the lung. For instance, a nomogram model constructed using radiomics artificial intelligence scores and clinical blood test data has shown high predictive accuracy in squamous cell carcinoma subtypes [43]. A prognostic model for lung squamous cell carcinoma was developed using machine learning techniques to analyze genes associated with 12 cell death modes, enabling the identification of patients likely to benefit from immunotherapy and predicting their survival based on immune microenvironment characteristics [44]. The aforementioned models utilize radiomics and blood test data, as well as genes related to programmed cell death, which are more complex in terms of data acquisition and processing, require higher computational resources, and may face challenges in data standardization and sharing in clinical applications.

The SHAP importance plot elucidates the key prognostic factors that contribute significantly to the model construction, namely: age, regional nodes examined, tumor size, and surgery type.

The role of surgery in the treatment of stage IB lung squamous cell carcinoma is indisputable. In the SHAP analysis, patients who underwent surgery alone or surgery combined with chemotherapy had significantly better survival outcomes compared to those who did not receive surgical treatment, further validating this conclusion.

However, this study observed that the survival benefit of surgery was significantly attenuated in patients aged over 70 years. It should be emphasized that age is a comprehensive surrogate marker, and this association may be confounded by factors including cardiopulmonary function, comorbidities, and frailty status, which are not fully captured in the SEER database. Therefore, this finding should not be interpreted as a recommendation to deny surgical treatment solely based on chronological age. A possible explanation for the attenuated benefit may be that increasing age is associated with elevated perioperative risks and postoperative complications. Meanwhile, existing evidence indicates that the surgical benefit for patients with stage IB lung cancer is more closely related to physiological age rather than chronological age [45]. For elderly patients, treatment decisions should be individualized through comprehensive evaluation, combined with other key prognostic factors identified in our SHAP analysis, including tumor size, the number of regional lymph nodes examined, and tumor grade. With strict preoperative screening and comprehensive assessment, even patients aged 70–80 years can still obtain survival benefits from surgery, with cancer‐specific survival rates comparable to those of younger patients [45]. In future studies, it is strongly recommended to incorporate indicators such as comprehensive geriatric assessment and frailty index, to better distinguish between chronological age and physiological frailty, and more accurately identify patients who cannot truly benefit from surgical treatment.

In our study, dissection of more than 6 lymph nodes during surgery yielded maximum benefit, while further increasing the number of resected nodes provided no significant additional benefit. It is widely believed that lymph node dissection during surgery for early‐stage lung cancer can, on one hand, more accurately stage the patient, and on the other hand, reduce the recurrence rate and improve patient survival [46]. Additionally, some researchers believe that a higher number of lymph nodes dissected is associated with better patient outcomes. A study based on the SEER database found that for patients with clinical stage I‐II non‐small cell lung cancer, the resection of at least 10 lymph nodes is associated with better long‐term survival [47]. Another study also indicated that systematic lymph node dissection (SLND) compared to limited lymph node sampling can better predict patient outcomes and improve survival rates [48]. However, in some studies, the number of lymph nodes removed and whether systematic lymph node dissection was performed had a limited impact on survival. A multicenter cohort study showed that although systematic lymph node dissection may carry higher surgical risks, it does not necessarily improve the survival rate of these patients [49]. The ACOSOG Z0030 clinical trial also indicates that if the intraoperative frozen section results of systematic hilar and mediastinal lymph node sampling are negative, further systematic lymph node dissection does not provide a survival benefit [50, 51]. These may help explain the aforementioned conclusion.

In this study, SHAP analysis revealed that the addition of adjuvant chemotherapy following surgery provided no significant survival benefit for patients with stage IB lung squamous cell carcinoma. This finding was further confirmed by propensity score matching analysis, with consistent results observed across all subgroups. These findings contrast sharply with the modest but statistically significant 5‐year overall survival improvement reported in the pooled LACE meta‐analysis (hazard ratio 0.89, 95% CI 0.82–0.96) and in the ANITA trial sub‐analysis of stage IB patients (HR 0.79, p = 0.04) [52, 53]. However, both LACE and ANITA were dominated by adenocarcinoma histology (≈55%) and pooled squamous‐cell tumors with larger T2/T3 lesions; when the latter were examined separately, the confidence bounds crossed unity, echoing our null finding. The JIPANG and TREAT prospective datasets, which were restricted to adjuvant pemetrexed–platinum in completely resected adenocarcinoma, demonstrated incremental efficacy specifically in EGFR‐wild‐type stage IB disease, reinforcing the concept that histology and molecular context modulate chemosensitivity [54]. Lung squamous‐cell carcinomas typically harbor low ERCC1 expression and a robust smoking‐related mutational signature, yet lack the high neo‐antigen burden associated with adenocarcinoma; this biological milieu may render conventional cytotoxic agents less effective in eradicating micrometastatic disease [55]. Taken together, these data question the extrapolation of adjuvant chemotherapy indications. Traditional high‐risk pathological factors (such as pleural invasion) may be insufficient to serve as indications for adjuvant chemotherapy in lung squamous cell carcinoma.

Finally, by developing a machine learning‐based predictive model, this study offers a new perspective and tool for predicting the survival of patients with early‐stage squamous cell carcinoma of the lung. Its clinical significance lies in the ability to help physicians more accurately assess patient prognosis, thereby enabling the formulation of more personalized treatment plans to improve therapeutic outcomes and patient survival rates. Additionally, this study has advanced the development of related fields, providing new ideas and methods for subsequent research.

However, the study also has certain limitations. First, despite the use of rigorous propensity‐score matching, the retrospective nature of the database cannot fully eliminate residual confounding by unmeasured peri‐operative variables. Second, SEER does not capture smoking intensity, pathological subtyping (e.g., basaloid versus keratinizing squamous‐cell carcinoma), or contemporary biomarkers including minimal residual disease (MRD) status and PD‐L1 expression, all of which may modulate both prognosis and chemosensitivity. Third, the absence of recurrence‐free survival and patient‐reported quality‐of‐life metrics precludes a comprehensive risk–benefit assessment, particularly relevant when evaluating toxic adjuvant regimens in an otherwise potentially cured population. Moreover, although our prediction model showed favorable performance in both internal validation and temporal external validation, its generalizability is still limited by the retrospective design, single database source, and potential inter observer variability in T stage assessment. Further prospective and multicenter validation in diverse populations, including Asian and European cohorts, is warranted before widespread clinical application. In the future, this model could be integrated into clinical decision support systems to assist clinicians in individualized risk stratification. Considering potential differences in race, genetics, and healthcare systems, recalibration or fine‐tuning of the model may be necessary when applied to patients from different ethnic backgrounds or healthcare settings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we leveraged a large, population‐based cohort to develop and externally validate an interpretable machine‐learning model based on the LightGBM algorithm that accurately forecasts 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐year overall survival in patients with stage IB lung squamous‐cell carcinoma. SHAP interrogation underscores definitive surgical resection and thorough nodal staging as the dominant modifiable determinants of long‐term outcome, while postoperative cisplatin‐based chemotherapy conferred no discernible survival advantage across any high‐risk substratum. Until prospective, histology‐specific trials demonstrate otherwise, routine adjuvant cytotoxic therapy should not be considered standard for completely resected stage IB LSCC; instead, efforts should focus on precise surgical quality assurance and integration of molecular risk stratifiers to further refine adjuvant decision‐making.
