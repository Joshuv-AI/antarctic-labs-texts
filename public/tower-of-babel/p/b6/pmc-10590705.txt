Exon Junction Complex Is a Molecular Compass of N6-Methyladenosine Modification
Authors: Inhong Jung, Yoon Ki Kim
Published: 2023

N6-methyladenosine (m6A) is the most prevalent internal mRNA modification in mammals and plays crucial roles in pre-mRNA splicing, mRNA translation, and mRNA stability. Recent studies from three separate research groups have brought to light the regulation of m6A installation by exon junction complexes (EJCs), which are deposited immediately upstream of each exon-exon junction following splicing. These studies have revealed that the EJCs deposited onto mRNAs act as a barrier, limiting the accessibility of METTL3/14 complex (known as m6A writer complex), thereby preventing m6A modification in regions of mRNA proximal to the exon-exon junctions. The discovery of this EJC-mediated reshaping of m6A transcriptome paves the way for new research opportunities to comprehend a wide array of molecular, cellular, and physiological events associated with the EJC and m6A.

Among the extensive repertoire of over 170 naturally occurring RNA modifications, the most prevalent internal modification within messenger RNAs (mRNAs) is the N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification (Roundtree et al., 2017). The dynamic regulation of this modification involves three types of proteins. The methyltransferase complex, comprising METTL3 and METTL14, co-transcriptionally installs m6A preferentially at DRACH motifs (where D denotes A/G/U, R denotes A/G, A denotes methylatable adenosine, and H denotes A/C/U) in the nucleus. Several accessory proteins, such as WTAP, play a role in coordinating the methylation activity and determining the specific nucleotide positions for m6A modification. The installed m6A can be erased by demethylases, ALKBH5 and FTO. Specific m6A reader proteins, including YTHDF proteins, recognize m6A present on mRNAs. Based on the molecular properties of the m6A reader proteins, m6A-containing mRNAs undergo distinct posttranscriptional regulatory processes, such as rapid mRNA degradation, increased translation, and efficient mRNA export. In addition, m6A modification exerts a profound influence on a wide range of cellular and developmental processes and human diseases, including cancer (Li et al., 2022).

An intriguing and long-standing enigma concerning m6A is its selective occurrence within the DRACH motif. Notably, not all DRACH motifs undergo m6A methylation, and the m6A is predominantly observed in the 3′-untranslated regions (3′UTR). The molecular mechanism underlying this nonuniform pattern of m6A modification has been uncovered by three independent research groups (He et al., 2023; Uzonyi et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2022). These three groups used different approaches to investigate this issue, however ultimately arrived at similar conclusions: the exon junction complex (EJC) acts as a limiting factor, restricting the accessibility of METTL3 to mRNA and thus inhibiting the installation of m6A in regions near the EJCs.

The EJC is a multiprotein complex comprising four core proteins, including eukaryotic translation initiation factor 4A3 (eIF4A3), metastatic lymph node 51 (MLN51; also known as Barentsz or CASC3), mago-nashi homolog (MAGOH), and Y14 (also known as RNA-binding motif 8A), as well as several accessory proteins (Boehm and Gehring, 2016; Le Hir et al., 2016; Woodward et al., 2017). The assembly of the EJC is tightly coupled with a splicing event. Within the nucleus, eIF4A3 interacts with CWC22, a core splicing factor, and is guided to the spliceosome. Once bound to the pre-mRNA, eIF4A3 forms a pre-EJC core complex by associating with MAGOH and Y14. Subsequently, MLN51 joins the pre-EJC to stabilize its structure. Consequently, the core EJC is positioned approximately 20-24 nucleotides upstream of each exon-exon junction (EEJ). The assembled EJC plays crucial roles in various molecular processes, such as pre-mRNA splicing, mRNA export, mRNA stability, translation, and diverse cellular and physiological events.

The versatile functions of the EJC are further highlighted by its role as an m6A suppressor (He et al., 2023; Uzonyi et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2022). He et al. (2023) conducted a study where they categorized local sequences surrounding endogenous DRACH sites into two groups: endogenous m6A-methylated sites and endogenous m6A-unmethylated DRACH sites. Subsequently, they cloned and exogenously expressed thousands of regions of each group. The analysis of the exogenously expressed DRACH-containing sequences revealed efficient m6A modification with comparable efficiency between the two groups, suggesting the presence of an inhibitory factor. Furthermore, the downregulation of an EJC core component triggered m6A methylation in endogenous m6A-unmethylated DRACH sites, providing evidence for the inhibitory role of the EJC in m6A modification.

Yang et al. (2022) compared the m6A status of chromatin-associated RNAs (enriched for nascent transcripts) and polyA-containing RNAs (mature mRNAs) from HeLa cells. Interestingly, they observed that chromatin-associated RNAs displayed a more evenly distributed m6A modification throughout the RNA molecules. In addition, they found that the downregulation of eIF4A3 resulted in increased METTL3 binding and m6A modification in regions proximal to the EEJs within the mRNA. In another independent study, Uzonyi et al. (2023) performed a massively parallel reporter assay. They cloned local sequences surrounding the DRACH sites with high-confidence m6A sites in humans and mice. The cloned sequences contained wild-type and point-mutated DRACH motifs, as well as systematic mutations and shifts in the relative position of the DRACH motifs. The results revealed a positive correlation between the number of the DRACH motifs and m6A modifications in exogenously expressed sequences. Subsequent investigations revealed that the DRACH motifs were methylated by default unless they were situated within a window of approximately 100 nucleotides from the EEJ. Furthermore, the downregulation of Y14 led to an increase in methylation in multiexonic genes with short exons, suggesting that the EJC prevents m6A methylation from occurring in regions proximal to the EEJs. All three independent studies converged on a shared conclusion: the EJC, deposited onto mRNA after splicing, acts as a barrier that inhibits m6A installation in the vicinity of the EEJs by obstructing the accessibility of METTL3 to mRNA. These observations provide valuable molecular insights that shed light on long-standing questions, such as the preferential depletion of m6A in short exons, the relative enrichment of m6A in long internal exons, and the enrichment of m6A in the last exons near the stop codon.

The discovery that m6A modification is regulated by the EJC raises intriguing questions. First, despite the EJC core components being crucial for the EJC assembly, each EJC core protein can independently participate in m6A modification. Indeed, MLN51 and eIF4A3 are involved in the translation as EJC-free forms (Choe et al., 2014; Le Hir et al., 2016). Second, not all EEJs acquire an EJC after splicing, with only 80% of the EEJs being bound by the EJCs (Singh et al., 2012). This indicates that splicing events do not always lead to EJC installation. Moreover, approximately half of the EJCs are deposited at canonical sites, approximately 20-24 nucleotides upstream of the EEJs, while the remaining half are associated with noncanonical sites. Therefore, further study is necessary to investigate whether the EJC loaded onto noncanonical sites contributes to m6A modifications. Lastly, considering the multifunctionality and involvement of the EJCs in various molecular processes, such as translation, nonsense-mediated mRNA decay, and splicing, the exploration of the potential interplay among the EJC, m6A, and EJC-related molecular processes is crucial in future studies.
