Mobilising global knowledge to strengthen the integration of community health workers (CHWs) in high-income countries with universal healthcare systems: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Audrey Steenbeek, Melissa Rothfus, Noah Doucette, Shirin ­, Alyssa Indar, Shilpi Sarkar, Fareha Khan, Seema Rani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionCommunity health workers (CHWs) are trained lay people and trusted members of communities worldwide who play crucial roles in bridging healthcare gaps in low–middle-income countries yet remain underused and not well integrated within high-income countries like Canada. The objective of this scoping review is to map out available evidence on the integration of CHWs in high-income countries with universal healthcare systems.Methods and analysisThis scoping review will include all available literature involving CHWs, or similar designations, and their integration into universal health systems within high-income countries. Literature will be excluded if it does not involve CHWs, universal healthcare systems, address integration or is conducted in low–middle-income countries. This review will include all available literature (including those that show null or negative results) that examines the integration of CHWs in high-income countries with a universal healthcare system. Documents describing integration may include, but are not limited to: tools, policies, models, frameworks, programmes or organisational features that seek to promote positive integration. Peer-reviewed and grey literature examining CHW integration in high-income countries with universal healthcare systems will be eligible for inclusion. Databases/sources to be searched (from inception until November 2025) will include: Medline (Ovid), Embase (Elsevier), Scopus (Elsevier), CINAHL (EBSCO), PsycINFO (EBSCO), Academic Search Premier (EBSCO), Business Source Complete (EBSCO), ProQuest Dissertations and Theses Global. Retrieval of full-text, all language studies (and other literature), data extraction, synthesis and mapping will be performed independently by two reviewers, following Joanna Briggs Institute methodology. Findings will be organised and presented according to the Levesque conceptual framework for healthcare access.Ethics and disseminationEthics approval is not required for this scoping review and literature search will start in October 2025 or on acceptance of this protocol. Introduction

Community health workers (CHWs) are trained lay people and trusted members of communities throughout the world, who share lived experiences with their neighbours and peers.1 They are experts in navigating complex care systems and play a crucial role in addressing gaps that result in persistent health inequities, including disease prevention (eg, vaccination, hygiene),2 3 enhancing provider–patient communication and trust,2,4 linking individuals to health and social services,2,4 managing chronic diseases (eg, diabetes, hypertension, mental illness),5 cancer screening (eg, breast, cervical and colorectal),5 improving birth outcomes,2 6 decreasing maternal mortality2 6 and enhancing child wellness2 6 (eg, nutrition), among others. In many low–middle-income countries like India, CHWs (ie, accredited social health activists, anganwadi workers and female health workers) are pivotal in connecting rural and other vulnerable populations with healthcare services, acting as nursing ‘service extensions’ and health activists.6 One of the key strengths of CHWs is their ability to connect with community members on multiple levels, prioritising interpersonal bonds over clinical expertise, which is commonly relied on by healthcare providers.2 For example, when promoting health and lifestyle recommendations, they often incorporate cultural and linguistic nuances that resonate more deeply with the communities they serve, enhancing understanding and relevance. Moreover, CHWs play essential roles in health education and patient advocacy, particularly when supporting their communities in accessing appropriate care.2,4

The WHO has consistently emphasised that health and healthcare inequities are best addressed when communities are engaged in prioritising and developing strategies to tackle their own health challenges7; CHWs are well positioned to help build, promote and empower community capacity to address these challenges.2 Additionally, there is sufficient evidence supporting CHWs’ roles in improving population health where: (1) health workforce resources are limited and access to basic needs and essential services is low (eg, low–middle-income countries),4 5 and (2) where large disparities in health outcomes exist within and among populations despite the presence of well-developed, universal healthcare systems (eg, equity seeking and rural/remote communities in high-income countries).2

During the recent COVID-19 pandemic, CHWs also provided timely, evidence-informed information to their communities (eg, vaccination, quarantine, precautions, testing, masking) and played an integral role in addressing misinformation and common fears/stigma surrounding the virus.3 8 9 As such, supporting CHWs’ ability to promote healthcare equity and health systems improvement, especially in low- and middle-income countries like India, is imperative. It is also important to consider integrating CHWs in high-income countries (eg, Canada), when possible, knowing that CHWs are pivotal in delivering culturally appropriate support, while also helping community members navigate services in under-resourced areas within these regions.2 Globally, the nursing workforce also faces a critical shortage, driving the need for innovative solutions to strengthen nursing capacity.10 In many high-income countries like Canada, this challenge has spurred substantial investments to recruit internationally educated nurses (IENs) and simplify pathways for novice nurses.11 While these workforce changes offer promise, IENS, particularly from low- to middle-income countries like India, often encounter difficulties integrating into the Canadian workforce.11 Cultural adjustments, language barriers and/or personal hardships have led some IENs to return to their home countries; these outcomes are costly and further burden the nursing workforce. Additionally, nurses in high-income countries face pressures due to a shortage of primary care providers, high rates of chronic diseases and a growing influx of immigrants and refugees.10 Integrating CHWs in high-income countries can help mitigate the impacts of primary care provider and nursing shortages, support IENs and ultimately, help improve equitable healthcare access and health outcomes in Canada and beyond. To date, literature describing CHW role integration in high-income countries with universal healthcare systems has not been comprehensively reviewed and synthesised. A 2015 scoping review examined CHWs in Canada and other high-income countries, but its primary focus was on identifying research gaps related to CHW roles.12 A scoping review with a broader focus on CHW integration (ie, including peer reviewed and grey literature) would be valuable for high income countries like Canada. Such a review could map the existing evidence (including evidence that shows null or negative integration) and where possible, highlight the level of efficacy of different models of CHW integration. A preliminary search of Epistemonikos, MEDLINE, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews and a JBI Evidence Synthesis was conducted on 27 May 2025 and no current or in-progress systematic reviews or scoping reviews on the topic were identified.

The objective of the proposed scoping review is to identify and map available evidence on CHW integration into high-income countries with a universal healthcare system like Canada. Scoping reviews are useful to assess the extent of the literature, identify knowledge gaps, clarify key concepts and examine all forms of available literature that meet the inclusion criteria.

Review questions

Primary question: how has the integration of CHWs in high-income countries with a universal healthcare system been previously explored in the available evidence base?

Subquestion: what tools, strategies, training, organisational features, policies, frameworks and programmes are available to support the integration of CHWs, and what are their key characteristics and potential outcomes for high-income countries with universal healthcare systems?

Methods and analysis

Eligibility criteria

Participants

This scoping review will consider all available, relevant literature involving the integration of CHWs into high-income countries with universal healthcare systems. This will include all types of CHWs or individuals with similar roles/titles who are trained laypersons providing health-related services of any type.

Concept

All available literature, including those that show null or negative associations, will be considered if it examines the integration of the CHW role in high-income countries with a universal healthcare system. While there are several definitions of integration, the WHO has identified six main ways to define ‘integration’ in healthcare.13 These include: (1) a package of preventive and curative health interventions for a particular population; (2) multipurpose service delivery points; (3) care over time; (4) vertical integration of different levels of service; (5) integrated policymaking and management and (6) working across sectors. For the purpose of this review, ‘integration’ will be defined as the ‘management and delivery of health services so that clients receive a continuum of preventive and curative services, according to their needs over time and across different levels of the health system’.13

Integration may include, for example, tools, strategies, training, policies, frameworks, programmes and organisational features that seek to integrate CHWs into existing (or new) healthcare services. Key characteristics of each type of integration will also include (if available): guiding principles (eg, assumptions, values and beliefs), main objectives, intended outcomes and logistical requirements (eg, human resources, financial, space, time). Any available literature that provides narrative text on these characteristics and is linked to integration of the CHW role will be considered for inclusion.

Context

This review will consider all available literature that focuses on the integration of the CHW role in high-income countries that provide universal healthcare, similar to the Canadian healthcare system and policy environment which is most familiar to the research team. In Canada, the healthcare system is publicly funded and grounded in the principles of universality, accessibility, comprehensiveness and public administration as outlined in the Canada Health Act.14 Despite a commitment to equitable access and care delivery, many high-income countries like Canada continue to face challenges in providing culturally-informed, equitable and accessible services, particularly for historically under-represented populations (eg, Indigenous, immigrants, refugees), and those living in rural and remote communities.15 Additionally, although the CHW role has been well documented to address health and healthcare disparities in low–middle-income countries like India, and subsequently, well integrated into existing healthcare systems,4 6 such integration remains fragmented, unregulated and underused in many high-income, universal healthcare systems.12

To allow for meaningful cross-country comparisons, this scoping review includes countries classified as high-income by the World Bank16 and further narrowed to those with universal (or near-universal) healthcare systems that prioritise equitable access to care through public financing and share similar structural characteristics. This includes 31 countries as follows: Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada, Czechia, Denmark, Estonia, Finland, France, Germany, Greece, Hungary, Iceland, Ireland, Israel, Italy, Japan, Korea, Latvia, Lithuania, Luxembourg, Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Portugal, Slovak Republic, Slovenia, Spain, Sweden, Switzerland and the UK.16

Cultural and subcultural factors that will be considered include: Indigenous health equity, immigrant and refugee health, gender and biological sex, supporting healthcare workforce including IENs, population homogeneity/heterogeneity, political factors and social determinants of health as these are all contexts where CHWs can help reduce systemic barriers.17 Literature from countries without universal healthcare coverage (eg, the USA) or from low–middle-income countries will be excluded, as they have different healthcare financing structures and limited generalisability to those countries with universal healthcare systems.

Types of literature

This scoping review will consider any type of available literature that examines the integration13 of the CHW role in high-income countries with a universal healthcare system. This will include both experimental and quasi-experimental study designs, including randomised controlled trials, non-randomised controlled trials, before and after studies and interrupted time-series studies. In addition, analytical observational studies including prospective and retrospective cohort studies, case–control studies, systematic reviews and analytical cross-sectional studies will be considered for inclusion. This review will also consider descriptive observational study designs including case series, individual case reports and descriptive cross-sectional studies for inclusion. Text and opinion papers, conference proceedings, newsletters, theses and dissertations and publicly available publications by the respective governments and/or healthcare organisations of the 31 countries16 will also be considered for this scoping review if they meet the inclusion criteria.

Qualitative studies will also be considered if they meet the inclusion criteria, which include, but are not limited to, designs such as phenomenology, grounded theory, ethnography, qualitative description and various critical theories (eg, feminism).

Published academic and grey literature written in any language will be considered for inclusion. Both published academic and grey literature will be searched from inception until November 2025.

Information sources

The databases to be searched will include: Medline (Ovid), Embase (Elsevier), CINAHL (EBSCO), PsycINFO (EBSCO), Academic Search Premier (EBSCO), Business Source Complete (EBSCO) and Scopus (Elsevier). Additionally, ProQuest Dissertations, Theses Global will be searched for grey literature. A Google search will identify more grey literature as well as identify organisations whose website might merit more direct searching. Searches for publicly available grey literature will also be conducted in government healthcare organisation websites with each respective country.

Search strategy

The proposed scoping review will be conducted in accordance with the JBI methodology for scoping reviews.18 The search strategy will consist of a comprehensive approach to locate both published and unpublished studies and other literature per the inclusion criteria. Following consultation, a qualified health-science librarian scientist (MR) developed a three-step search strategy. First, an initial limited search of MEDLINE (PubMed) and CINAHL (EBSCO) was undertaken to identify articles on the topic. The text words contained in the titles and abstracts of relevant articles, and the index terms used to describe the articles are then (Step 2) used to develop a full search strategy for reporting the name of the relevant databases/information sources (see online supplemental file). The search strategy, including all identified keywords and index terms, will be adapted for each included database and/or information source. This search strategy has been peer reviewed by a second qualified librarian scientist using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies guidelines (Step 3).19 In addition, the reference lists of all included articles selected for the scoping review will be screened for additional literature.

Study records

Data management

Following a comprehensive search (ie, from inception until November 2025), all selected databases and identified bibliographic citations will be collated and uploaded into the online review platform Covidence,20 with duplicates across all databases removed. Any disagreements that may arise between the reviewers at each stage of the study selection process will be resolved through discussion, or with a third reviewer if necessary. Covidence automates the record keeping process across the stages of study selection.20 We will be using the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic review and Meta-Analysis extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR)21 checklist for scoping reviews that omits discussion of items not relevant to scoping reviews, including: (1) outcomes, (2) priorities, (3) risk of bias in individual studies, (4) meta-bias and (5) confidence in cumulative evidence. The results of the search will be reported in full, keeping with the PRISMA-ScR.21

Selection process

The first stage of literature selection involves the screening of titles, abstracts and key words. These will be screened by two independent reviewers (ND and S) for assessment against the inclusion criteria. Potentially relevant studies and other literature will be retrieved in full and imported into the primary screening and data extraction tool Covidence.20 The second stage involves a review of the full text literature identified for inclusion by title, abstract and key word screening. The full text will be assessed in detail against the inclusion criteria by the same two independent reviewers. Reasons for exclusion of the full text that do not meet the inclusion criteria will be recorded and reported in the scoping review.

Data collection

Data will be extracted from papers included in the scoping review by two or more independent reviewers using a data extraction tool developed by the reviewers using a charting table (in Covidence).20 The data extraction tool22 will include specific details about the participants, concept, context, study methods (if relevant), level/type of evidence (eg, peer reviewed, grey literature) and key findings relevant to the review questions and objective. The following details will be extracted from the included literature: author(s), year of publication, type of literature (eg, research, editorial, report), aim/purpose, research design (eg, experimental, quasi-experimental, observational), CHW title (eg, CHW, patient navigator), context (geographical location and service setting), content (type of integration), characteristics of the integration (guiding principles, main objectives/outcomes and logistical resources) and main findings/author’s conclusions. Initially, two reviewers will test the extraction table with three articles, compare results and revise if necessary. Following this step, all remaining data extraction will be completed independently by two reviewers and any disagreements that arise between the reviewers will be resolved through discussion or with a third reviewer. If two or more articles describe the same integration, it will only be recorded once. Additionally, if data is missing or unclear, authors of the papers will be contacted to request further information. As data extraction continues, the charting table used will likely need adaptation; the final version will be presented in the appendix of the scoping review report.

Data synthesis

Characteristics of CHW integration including guiding principles, objectives/outcomes and logistical resources as well as both the level and source of evidence (eg, original research report (including research design), government website and all other literature will be summarised in tabular forms with accompanying text. If other characteristics emerge, they will be presented in the final report. Type and outcome of integration will be summarised thematically, using the Levesque conceptual framework of access to healthcare.23 A narrative explanation with examples (from respective studies/literature) will also be presented in accordance with the review, objective and questions.

Patient and public involvement statement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Ethics and dissemination

The authors do not anticipate any ethical and/or safety considerations occurring from this scoping review, and ethics approval is not required. The search for literature will start in October 2025 or on acceptance of this protocol, whichever comes first. Findings from the scoping review will be available in February 2026. Results from the review will be disseminated in a peer review journal and presented at local, national and international conferences if applicable.
