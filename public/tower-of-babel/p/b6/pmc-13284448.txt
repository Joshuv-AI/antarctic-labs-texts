The Association Between Personality Traits and Irritable Bowel Syndrome—Insights From an Exploratory Cohort Study
Authors: Sylvester R. Groen, Zsa Zsa R. M. Weerts, Lisa Vork, Zlatan Mujagic, Carsten Leue, Sandra Mulkens, Joanna W. Kruimel, Ad A. M. Masclee, Daisy M. A. E. Jonkers, Daniel Keszthelyi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEvidence suggests psychological factors including personality traits can have impact on the development and course of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and associated health‐related quality of life (HrQoL), with large individual heterogeneity. Main aim of this study was to examine between‐persons associations and within‐sample concurrent associations of the personality traits neuroticism, extraversion, conscientiousness, openness and agreeableness with gastrointestinal (GI) symptoms, psychological factors and HrQoL in IBS‐patients.MethodsCross‐sectional data from an observational IBS‐study (n = 194, mean age 51.36 years, 74.4% female) was used. Patients completed the Big Five Inventory (BFI)‐44 questionnaire regarding personality traits, and questionnaires on symptom severity (GSRS‐IBS), general anxiety (HADS‐A, GAD‐7), GI‐specific anxiety (VSI), and HrQoL (36‐SF). A multivariable regression model and a network correlation analysis were performed.Key ResultsNeuroticism showed the most relevant between‐persons associations, confirmed by within‐sample concurrent associations: associated with increased GI‐specific anxiety (B 7.234, p = 0.040), general anxiety (B 0.954, p = 0.040), and decreased mental HrQoL (B—3.576, p = 0.007). Extraversion showed an opposing pattern including increased mental HrQoL (B = 4.266, p = 0.001), with strong association to neuroticism. While no significant between‐person associations emerged between abdominal pain and personality traits, significantly associated to higher general anxiety (B = 6.304, p < 0.001) and GI‐specific anxiety (B = 0.034, p < 0.001) were shown.ConclusionThis study reinforces the association between personality traits and IBS, highlighting the integral connection to not only other psychological factors but also GI‐symptoms. These findings support a multifactorial, personalized approach to IBS, advocating for the integration of personality assessment—particularly neuroticism and extraversion—into the biopsychosocial model.

Introduction

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a chronic gastrointestinal (GI) disorder, which is characterized by recurrent abdominal pain and altered bowel habits [1], with a global prevalence of 4.4%–4.8% [2, 3]. In the absence of a suitable biomarker for identifying IBS, diagnosis is made by use of symptom‐based Rome criteria. Most patients experience a chronic pattern of relapse and remitting disease course, in which management mostly targets symptom control [4, 5]. These symptoms have a substantial impact on individual level, affecting work productivity, life satisfaction, well‐being and subsequently the health‐related quality of life (HrQoL) [6]. As a result, IBS is associated with an eminent economic impact on society and healthcare, concerning the ability of societal participation and healthcare utilization [7].

The pathophysiology of IBS is heterogeneous and complex. Current scientific consensus considers the contribution of a bidirectional communication between the gut and the brain referred to as the gut‐brain‐interaction, as a key factor in IBS pathogenesis. Putative etiological factors include visceral hypersensitivity, genetic predisposition, low‐grade inflammation, increased intestinal permeability and alterations in microbiota [8, 9, 10]. The biopsychosocial model of illness as applied in IBS acknowledges an interaction between both biological and environmental factors to a psychological and social context, contributing to the development of symptoms and functional disability [10, 11]. Psychosocial patterns in IBS include common extraintestinal comorbidity (e.g., generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) and depression), a higher severity of GI‐specific anxiety, insufficient resilience to stress, and coping strategies [12, 13, 14].

Personality traits are of particular interest since these can impact psychological reactions to internal and external stimuli and have a strong influence on health behavior through social cognition and associative processes. The Big Five Inventory (BFI), a generally accepted dimensional model in personality psychology, defines five general clusters of personality traits: extraversion (bold/energetic vs. shy/bashful), agreeableness (sympathetic/cooperative vs. cold/competitive), conscientiousness (efficient/organized vs. haphazard/careless), neuroticism (moody/nervous vs. relaxed/calm), and openness to experience (imaginative/philosophical vs. uncreative) [15, 16]. Neuroticism and agreeableness are generally associated with a lower emotion‐related coping and perceived lower resilience to stress [17]. Although personality traits are traditionally considered as important factors in the development and course of mental conditions and psychiatric disorders, they also appear to have relevance to IBS. A higher level of neuroticism is associated with GAD and depression—and is independently associated with a lower life‐satisfaction and HrQoL in IBS [18, 19]. A lower level of extraversion is associated with a lower capability to predict negative events, resulting in negative affect and low self‐efficacy [20]. Several other studies have previously highlighted the association between personality traits and IBS [21, 22, 23, 24], but these have hitherto not been able to conclude on the directionality of this association nor the relation to HrQoL and symptom facilitation.

Hence, the primary aim of this study was to examine both between‐person associations and within‐sample concurrent associations of personality traits with GI symptoms, psychological factors, and HrQoL in individuals with IBS. Personality traits were conceptualized as dispositional dimensions that may confer vulnerability to psychological distress and heightened symptom perception. We specifically hypothesized that neuroticism would be associated with lower HrQoL, potentially through its association with psychological comorbidity in the context of GI symptoms. Overall, our objective was to provide a systematic multilevel characterization of personality profiles and their clinical correlates within a well‐defined IBS cohort.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

Data were obtained from the Maastricht IBS (MIBS)‐cohort. Participants were recruited via the outpatient clinic of the gastroenterology department of the Maastricht University Medical Center+ (MUMC+) in Maastricht, the Netherlands, which is a secondary and tertiary referral hospital. Additionally, patients were recruited via general practitioners in South‐Limburg (the Netherlands). Patients with IBS diagnosed according to the Rome III criteria (age 18–75 years) were eligible for inclusion. Further details regarding the study design and inclusion procedure of the MIBS cohort study are described elsewhere [25]. Initial inclusion of patients in the MIBS‐cohort was initiated in 2009. A follow‐up assessment of the MIBS‐cohort study was conducted between September 2016 and September 2017, and included evaluation of the Rome III criteria, carried out by a trained clinical investigator or clinician during a telephone interview. In addition, online questionnaires were sent to the participants. In the current study, patients were included if the Big Five Inventory‐44 questionnaire (BFI‐44) was completed.

Data Collection and Questionnaires

Data was collected by self‐reported digital questionnaires and included basic demographics, body mass index (BMI), IBS subtype (according to the Rome III criteria), GI‐symptoms, HrQol, anxiety and depressive symptoms, GI‐specific anxiety, and personality traits.

Personality Traits

Personality traits were assessed and scored using the BFI‐44 questionnaire, which includes 44 items (Likert scale 1–5) divided in five sub‐scales to calculate personality dimensions: extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, neuroticism and openness to experience [16, 26]. The BFI taxonomy model is an established model to evaluate personality traits and the BFI‐44 is validated for use in a Dutch population [16, 26].

GI Symptoms

The Gastrointestinal Symptom Rating Scale, modified for use in IBS patients (GSRS‐IBS), was used to assess GI‐symptoms. This scale includes 13 items (Likert scale 1 to 7), a higher score indicates higher symptom severity [27]. A total GSRS‐IBS score was calculated of all symptom domains. Furthermore, 5 sub‐scores were calculated for predominant symptoms, including abdominal pain, abdominal bloating, constipation, diarrhea and satiety.

Psychological Factors

Anxiety and depression symptoms were assessed using the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS; 14 items, Likert scale 0–3), a validated self‐rating scale frequently used to screen for anxiety and depression in clinical practice. A score ≥ 8 indicates mild anxiety (HADS‐A) or depression (HADS‐D), 11–14 moderate and ≥ 15 severe anxiety or depression [28, 29]. Additionally, general anxiety was assessed using the Generalized Anxiety Disorder‐7 (GAD‐7; scale 0–21), with scores ≥ 5 indicating mild and ≥ 15 indicating severe anxiety [30]. Depressive symptoms were evaluated using the Patient Health Questionnaire‐9 (PHQ‐9; 9 items, scale 0–27), a validated tool for screening and monitoring depression severity, where scores ≥ 15 indicate severe depression [31]. GI‐specific symptom anxiety was scored using the Visceral Sensitivity Index (VSI), including 15 items (Likert scale 1–6), which generates a score indicating the degree of GI specific anxiety [32, 33].

HrQoL

Assessment of HrQoL was based on the 36‐item Short‐Form Health Survey (SF‐36; 36 items, scale 1–6), providing both a physical health composition score (PCS) and a mental health composition score (MCS). Higher scores indicate a better quality of life [34].

Data Visualization and Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 28.0 (SPSS statistics for Windows, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.4.0 (R Core Team, 2024. R: A language and environment for statistical computing. Vienna, Austria). The R packages Psych, Dplyr and Qgraph were used for network analysis and visualization [35, 36, 37]. Baseline characteristics were analyzed using the independent t‐test for numerical data and presented as means and standard deviation (SD). A Pearson Chi‐Square test was used for the analysis of categorical data and presented as number of patients (n, %). A two‐sided p‐value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To obtain complementary perspectives, we distinguished between between‐person associations and within‐sample concurrent associations. Between‐person analyses examine how individual differences in personality traits relate to differences in clinical outcomes across participants in order to capture trait‐like relationships across participants that can inform risk profiles and prognostic associations. In contrast, within‐sample concurrent associations reflect cross‐sectional correlations among variables measured at the same time point, without implying longitudinal intra‐individual dynamics. These analyses therefore reflect co‐occurrence patterns of symptoms and psychological variables within individuals at a single time point, offering insight into the configuration of traits and symptoms that may be clinically relevant.

A multivariable linear regression analysis was used to estimate the between‐persons association of multiple independent variables (including BFI personality traits) between various dependent variables. The following parameters were included for the multivariable linear regression analysis: BFI personality traits (extraversion, agreeableness, conscientiousness, neuroticism and openness to experience), general anxiety (GAD 7, HADS‐A) and depression (HADS‐D, PHQ‐9), GI‐specific symptom anxiety (VSI) and HrQoL (MCS and PCS). All assumptions for multivariable linear regression were assessed and met, which included: histogram and Q‐Q plot for normality; homoscedasticity; plots and variation and Cook distance for outliers (cut‐off 1); application of a variance inflation factor for multi‐collinearity. A Pearson correlation model was conducted along with a network analysis in R to analyze within‐sample concurrent associations of BFI personality traits to the variables: general anxiety (GAD‐7, HADS‐A) and depression (HADS‐D, PHQ‐9), GI‐specific symptom anxiety (VSI) and HrQoL (MCS and PCS). A p‐value of ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant. A Holm‐Bonferroni correction for multiple testing was applied in these models.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

All participants in the MIBS‐cohort study (n = 379) were approached for follow‐up assessment, of whom 194 patients completed the follow‐up questionnaires, including the BFI‐44. Additional evaluation by telephone during follow‐up was successful in 161 patients (83.0%), in which the evaluation of the Rome III criteria was conducted. At this follow‐up 112 patients (69.6%) still met the Rome III criteria for IBS, 49 patients (30.4%) did not (Figure 1). All baseline characteristics are outlined in Table 1. Patients who were reached by telephone and did meet the Rome III criteria for IBS (n = 112) were mostly diagnosed with IBS constipation subtype (IBS‐C, 46.4%). Compared to patients who did not meet the Rome III criteria for IBS during follow up, patients who still met the Rome III criteria had a significantly higher outcome regarding the following variables: GSRS‐IBS total score (p < 0.001) including all subdomain scores such as abdominal pain (p < 0.001); anxiety symptoms (HADS‐A, p = 0.003 and GAD‐7, p = 0.036); depressive symptoms (PHQ‐9, p = 0.009); GI‐specific anxiety (VSI, p < 0.001). In addition, compared to patients who did not meet the Rome III criteria during follow‐up, the patients who did meet the Rome III criteria had a significant lower mental quality of life (MCS, p = 0.045). Regarding the outcome on personality traits (according to the BFI‐44 questionnaire), no significant differences between both groups were shown.

Within‐Sample Concurrent Associations: Network Correlation Model

A correlation matrix of the within‐sample concurrent associations for personality traits and the outcomes regarding psychological factors and HrQoL are presented in Appendix S2 and visualized in Figure 2 and additionally in Appendix S3. There was a significant positive within‐person correlation of neuroticism with GI‐specific anxiety (r = 0.224, p < 0.05) and GSRS‐IBS abdominal pain (r = 0.449, p < 0.01), along with significant correlations to psychological factors including anxiety (HADS‐A, r = 0.277, p < 0.01) and depression according to the HADS‐D (r = 0.230, p < 0.05). In contrast to these findings, significant within‐sample concurrent associations were seen between extraversion and HrQoL and psychological factors, with the exception of GI‐specific anxiety (r = −0.058, p = 0.056; Appendix S2). Anxiety as measured with HADS‐A showed significant positive correlations with neuroticism (r = 0.592, p < 0.01) but with a negative correlation to agreeableness (r = −0.208, p < 0.05). These findings were comparable to the outcome of generalized anxiety as measured by GAD‐7: positive correlation with neuroticism (r = 0.678, p < 0.01) and a negative correlation with extraversion (r = −0.290, p < 0.05). Depressive symptoms (PHQ‐9) were significantly correlated with extraversion (r = −0.423, p < 0.05), neuroticism (0.532, p < 0.01) and openness (r = −0.213, p < 0.05) along with several other variables including, abdominal pain and HrQol.

Discussion

This exploratory study demonstrates that neuroticism shows the most consistent associations with psychological comorbidities in individuals with IBS. Higher neuroticism was associated with increased anxiety—particularly GI‐specific anxiety—which in turn was related to lower mental HrQoL. In addition, neuroticism was associated with GI symptoms, especially abdominal pain. While neuroticism was positively associated with extraversion, higher extraversion was linked to better mental HrQoL. The remaining personality traits showed comparatively weaker associations. Rather than implying fixed personality‐based determinants of IBS outcomes, these findings suggest that dispositional tendencies such as neuroticism may reflect a vulnerability profile characterized by heightened emotional reactivity and symptom‐related concern. Such profiles may operate through modifiable cognitive‐affective processes, including coping and symptom appraisal, which are central in contemporary health psychology models of somatic distress.

Our results corroborate previous findings suggesting neuroticism is the most frequently reported personality trait associated with IBS, with numerous studies indicating more severe neuroticism in IBS, particularly in the diarrhea‐predominant subtype [21, 22, 23]. Characterized by a tendency to worry, negative affect, and reduced emotion‐focused coping capacity, neuroticism is also linked to heightened emotional reactivity, which may influence gastrointestinal function [20, 24]. Some reports suggest that higher neuroticism may be associated with an increased vulnerability to IBS, particularly in populations exposed to psychological trauma [38] however, such associations should be interpreted within multifactorial gut–brain models rather than as deterministic pathways. Our multilevel approach extends this literature by demonstrating that the association between neuroticism and GI‐specific anxiety exceeded that with generalized anxiety at both the between‐person and within‐person level. This distinction has been less consistently highlighted in previous work and may refine existing theoretical models by suggesting that symptom‐focused anxiety plays a particularly central role in IBS. GI‐specific anxiety can intensify GI symptoms beyond the impact typically observed in generalized anxiety [12, 39, 40]. Indeed, GI‐specific anxiety was significantly more prevalent among patients who continued to meet the Rome III criteria at follow‐up, with a smaller but still significant difference observed for generalized anxiety, suggesting a greater contributory role of GI‐specific anxiety in IBS. Together, these findings are consistent with stress–vulnerability models in which dispositional emotional reactivity interacts with maladaptive appraisal processes to amplify symptom perception. Importantly, these appraisal and coping processes are considered modifiable and are directly targeted in contemporary psychological interventions.

Although a negative between‐persons association was found between neuroticism and depressive symptoms (as measured by HADS‐D), within‐person analysis revealed a positive correlation using both HADS‐D and PHQ‐9, aligning with existing literature [24, 38]. The HADS‐D was designed with an exclusion of somatic symptoms, unlike the PHQ‐9, which includes these—an important consideration given their central role in IBS. Consequently, discrepancies may arise when using both instruments. Nonetheless, growing evidence supports the use of the PHQ‐9 in IBS populations due to its clinical relevance and applicability in chronic illness. Another explanation may lie in the distinction between between‐person associations, which reflect stable, trait‐like relationships used for outcome prediction, and within‐sample concurrent associations, which capture temporal fluctuations within individuals.

The question remains what the exact directionality of the association is between neuroticism and both psychological and abdominal symptoms. Although personality traits can be considered relatively stable over time [41], IBS is increasingly conceptualized as a bidirectional disorder of gut–brain interaction, and the observed associations likely reflect reciprocal influences rather than unidirectional effects. One of the putative theories in this regard concerns the potential influence of dysfunctional coping strategies and problem‐solving skills linked to certain personality traits, leading to the accumulation of negative affect, rumination and low self‐efficacy [42]. The role of personality traits can be important in this respect as they lead to characteristic patterns of social cognition and health‐related behavior, with a specific direction toward a person's way of coping with stress or emotional triggers [15, 18, 43].

Interestingly, neuroticism was also strongly associated with the trait extraversion, which in turn was linked to better metrics of mental health. Extraversion is often viewed as the counterpart to neuroticism and has been reported at lower levels in IBS populations, likely due to the well‐developed task‐oriented coping strategies [15, 16]. The opposing patterns observed for neuroticism and extraversion further support the interpretation that affective orientation and coping style may shape psychological resilience in IBS. Extraversion, characterized by positive affect and task‐oriented coping, was associated in our within‐person analyses with lower anxiety and better mental HrQoL. Rather than suggesting that enhancing extraversion per se is a treatment target, these findings may point toward reinforcing adaptive coping strategies and positive affect regulation as clinically relevant mechanisms. It has previously been described that neuroticism and extraversion as traits have strong affective components which can coexist in the same person [20, 44]. In addition, high neuroticism and/or high extraversion has previously been shown to be accompanied by heightened affective variability, potentially reflecting positive feedback loops oriented toward negative affect for neuroticism, and toward positive affect and activation for extraversion [44]. Therefore, this could offer a therapeutic avenue in IBS patients with the aim to reinforce the personality traits (such as extraversion) being beneficial rather than detrimental in terms of increased coping and resilience.

As for the other personality traits, agreeableness was associated with poorer mental—but not physical—HrQoL in IBS patients, although this effect did not reach significance in within‐person analyses. This trait, characterized by empathy and cooperativeness, may be less prevalent in IBS populations due to its partial contrast with neuroticism [20, 21, 22]. However, its links to emotion‐focused coping and reduced resilience were evident in negative within‐sample concurrent associations with anxiety and depression. Openness showed a small but significant positive association with anxiety using the HADS‐A scale, though this was not replicated with GAD‐7. Contradictory negative within‐sample concurrent associations with psychological symptoms were also observed. No significant associations were found for conscientiousness. Its relevance to IBS appears limited, with only one study noting altered levels in constipation‐predominant IBS [21].

Importantly, these findings should not be interpreted as attributing IBS‐related distress to fixed personality characteristics. Rather, traits such as neuroticism may shape how individuals appraise visceral sensations and regulate affect, operating through modifiable mechanisms including attentional bias, catastrophic interpretation, and coping style. These processes are directly targeted in interventions such as cognitive behavioral therapy, acceptance‐based approaches, and gut‐directed hypnotherapy. Consistent with this perspective, a prospective pilot study implementing structured medical–psychiatric collaboration improves psychological symptoms and HrQoL in complex DGBI populations [45], underscoring the relevance of addressing dynamic processes rather than personality traits per se.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to examine both between‐person associations and within‐sample concurrent associations of personality traits in IBS, with specific attention to GI‐specific and generalized anxiety, GI symptoms, and HrQoL. Integrating both modalities offers a comprehensive perspective by capturing stable traits and symptom dynamics, enhancing multilevel modeling and reducing the risk of ecological fallacy. Our study also has a number of limitations. Due to its cross‐section design, conclusions regarding the development or progression of IBS in relation to personality traits cannot be drawn as we were only able to identify associations rather than causality. Our findings therefore remain descriptive and associational, providing an empirical foundation for future longitudinal and mechanistic research. Future research should incorporate longitudinal designs to assess causality and predictive value. Another limitation is the reliance on self‐report questionnaires as proxies for psychological and psychiatric parameters rather than interviews. Nonetheless, only validated instruments with established cut‐off values for the Dutch population were used. Many prior studies used various measures to assess personality (e.g., personality assessment questionnaire (PAQ) or Five Factor Inventory (FFI)), dealing with validity issues or difficulties regarding its utility or implementation due to its length. Therefore, we utilized the BFI—the most widely used and psychometrically robust tool for assessing personality traits. To enhance generalizability and minimize selection bias, participants were recruited from both secondary and tertiary care settings and general practitioners. In addition, approximately one‐third of participants no longer met Rome III IBS criteria at the time of assessment. Although inclusion of individuals with remitted IBS enhances generalizability, this clinical heterogeneity may have attenuated or obscured associations between personality traits and symptom severity. Sensitivity analyses restricted to participants meeting Rome III criteria yielded similar patterns, supporting the robustness of the primary findings.

In summary, this study provides a systematic multilevel characterization of personality traits and their associations with GI symptoms, anxiety, and HrQoL in a contemporary IBS cohort. Neuroticism appears to play a central role within a network linking dispositional vulnerability, GI‐specific anxiety, abdominal pain, and mental HrQoL. When embedded within biopsychosocial and gut–brain frameworks, these findings support a dynamic, non‐deterministic model in which personality represents one layer within a broader system of interacting psychological and physiological processes. Future longitudinal and mechanistic research is required to further disentangle these relationships and to inform stratified intervention strategies in IBS.
