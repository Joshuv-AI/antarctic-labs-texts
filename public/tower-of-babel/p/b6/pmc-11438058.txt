Association between Healthy Eating Index-2015 total and component food scores with reproductive lifespan among postmenopausal women: a population-based study from NHANES 2005–2016
Authors: Qiwang Lin, Jun Zhang, Xiuwu Liu, Qingyan Zheng, Dan Lin, Mian Pan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrior research has demonstrated that nutrition plays a crucial role in the establishment and maturation of the reproductive lifetime. Although the specific dietary components involved in preventing or postponing the reproductive lifespan are still unknown, a healthy diet can affect the reproductive lifespan. Here, the study aimed to explore the relationship between reproductive lifespan and diet quality by utilizing the Healthy Eating Index-2015 (HEI-2015).MethodsIn this study, a total of 2761 postmenopausal women were selected from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2005 to 2016. Diet quality was determined using HEI-2015 based on two 24-hour dietary recalls. Reproductive lifespan was defined as the number of years between self-reported age at menarche and menopause. Weighted linear regression and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) models were used to analyze the relationship between HEI-2015 and reproductive lifespan. Subsequently, the impact of various components of HEI-2015 on reproductive lifespan was assessed through weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression models.ResultsAmong 2761 postmenopausal women, the mean age was 63.7 years. 41.5% were obese, and 49.7% were non-Hispanic white. After adjusting for sociodemographic characteristics, lifestyle factors, and medical history, individuals in the highest tertile of HEI-2015 had a 4.81% (95% CI: 1.82–7.79%) longer reproductive time life. Higher HEI-2015 was also significantly associated with a higher likelihood of late menopause (p for trend < 0.05). Based on XGBoost models, the relative importance of HEI-2015 on reproductive lifespan was determined. Whole fruits, whole grains, total protein foods, and greens and beans significantly contributed to extending age at menopause and reproductive time life in the HEI-2015. The weights of the WQS index for age at menopause were 27.1%, 23.2%, 10.1%, and 7.5% respectively, while the weights of the WQS index for reproductive time life were 30.2%, 14.6%, 9.3%, and 14.0% respectively.ConclusionThere is a positive association between the HEI-2015 and reproductive lifespan. This underscores the significance of enhancing adherence to healthy dietary patterns in preventing a shorter reproductive lifespan.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-19902-y.

Introduction

Menopause marks the end of a woman’s reproductive cycle, characterized by the cessation of menstruation for more than 12 consecutive months [1]. The reproductive lifespan of women, also known as the fertile window, encompasses the time from the onset of menarche (the first occurrence of menstruation), which signifies the beginning of reproductive capability, to menopause (the cessation of menstruation), which marks the end of the natural reproductive phase. Reproductive lifespan is influenced by both menarche and menopause ages. Early menopause shortens reproductive lifespan, and similarly, late menarche reduces it. Studies show that each year of delay in menarche corresponds to a reduction in reproductive lifespan, significantly impacting overall reproductive health [2, 3]. Late menarche is associated with several health issues. Women with late menarche are more likely to experience decreased bone mineral density and a higher risk of osteoporosis in postmenopausal years due to shorter lifetime exposure to estrogen [4]. Additionally, late menarche is linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases, potentially due to reduced protective effects of estrogen over a shorter reproductive span [5]. Furthermore, some research indicates that late menarche is associated with higher risks of depression and anxiety in adulthood, likely due to the psychological and social adaptation challenges during adolescence [6].Studies have found that women experiencing premature menopause (before age 40) or early menopause (between ages 40–44) are at a higher risk of non-fatal cardiovascular events post-menopause, including coronary heart disease and stroke [7]. Early menopausal age and a shorter reproductive lifespan are also associated with a higher risk of aSAH (aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage) [8]. With the global trend of delayed menopausal age, understanding the changes in menopausal age and reproductive lifespan, along with related factors, becomes increasingly important [9, 10].

The duration of menopause and reproductive cycles is influenced by various factors, among which dietary factors play a significant role. An anti-inflammatory diet may enhance overall quality of life and reduce the severity of menopausal symptoms, particularly sexual problems, as indicated by previous studies on the importance of dietary components in alleviating specific quality-of-life challenges during menopause and their associated consequences [11]. Vitamin D deficiency or insufficiency may be associated with earlier menopause and could potentially shorten women’s reproductive lifespan [12]. In elderly mice, long-term consumption of a diet rich in omega-3 fatty acids can extend reproductive function. Additionally, oocyte quality was found to improve even with short dietary interventions involving diets rich in omega-3 fatty acids at the onset of the usual rapid decline in reproductive function associated with aging. For older women, omega-3 fatty acids may offer a useful and efficient way to delay ovarian aging and improve oocyte quality [13]. A study found that women who adopt healthier dietary patterns, as indicated by higher Healthy Eating Index-2015 (HEI-2015) scores, may delay their menopausal age. Specifically, higher HEI-2015 scores were associated with a later natural menopausal age [14]. Moreover, a healthy diet has likewise been shown to be significantly associated with reproductive lifespan. Some studies suggest that adopting healthier dietary patterns is associated with a longer reproductive lifespan, implying that improving dietary quality may extend women’s reproductive period [15–17]. The HEI-2015 assesses overall dietary quality, which includes dietary diversity, intake of fruits and vegetables, whole grains, protein sources, fats, and sugars. Therefore, maintaining healthy dietary habits may positively impact women’s reproductive health and menopausal age [14].

Considering the potential link between HEI-2015 and age of menopause, as well as reproductive lifespan, evidence on this association is limited and scarce, despite its biological plausibility. Thus, current study was conceived to leverage data from the extensive and representative population-based 2005–2016 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to scrutinize the associations between HEI-2015 score and reproductive lifespan among postmenopausal women. Additionally, further identifying the HEI-2015 components that play a major role.

Materials and methods

Study population

The participants in this study were from NHANES, which is an ongoing cross-sectional nationally representative survey. The NHANES is a research program conducted by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) in the United States, aiming to assess the health and nutritional status of adults and children in the country. The project measures various aspects of health through interviews and physical examinations, including chronic diseases, risk factors, nutrition, and health indicators. Data collection methods include questionnaire surveys and physical examinations, with participants undergoing body measurements, biological sample collection (such as blood and urine), and surveys on nutrition and health behaviors. All participants submitted written informed consent before the survey. The research protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Review Committee of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) [18].

Initially, a total of 4109 postmenopausal women with natural causes (i.e., women who did not experience a menstrual period within the previous year due to menopause and who did not have a hysterectomy or oophorectomy before or during their last menstrual period) who participated in the NHANES 2005–2016 physical examinations and interviews were included in the study. After further removing 470 women with incomplete dietary information, 587 women with incomplete covariate information, and 291 women with incomplete information on their age at menarche or menopause. Lastly, the current study included a total of 2761 individuals (Figure S1).

Assessment of HEI-2015

Detailed dietary intake information was obtained from NHANES participants to estimate energy, nutrients, and other food components in foods consumed in the 24 h prior to the interview. Two 24-hour dietary telephone interviews were available to all participants. The first interview was a face-to-face interview conducted at the MEC, and the second interview was a telephone interview conducted 3–10 days later [18]. This study utilized the dietary information collected from both interviews to assess dietary quality and calculate dietary quality indicators.

HEI-2015 represents a measure of overall dietary quality, assessing compliance with federal dietary guidelines. It evaluates how well a set of foods aligns with key recommendations of the Dietary Guidelines for Americans (DGA). The HEI-2015 includes components that reflect different aspects of a healthy diet, such as the consumption of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, dairy, and protein foods, as well as the intake of saturated fats, sodium, and added sugars. A higher HEI-2015 score indicates better diet quality [19]. The calculation of the HEI-2015 involves a series of steps to assess an individual’s diet quality. Here are the basic steps for calculating HEI-2015 scores. (1) Determine the food set: First, you need to identify the food set to be considered. This can be the entire U.S. food supply, choices in a specific environment, foods available in the market, or foods consumed by an individual or group over a day or longer period. (2) Determine the quantities of each relevant dietary component: Determine the quantities of each dietary component in the food set being considered. This includes energy, nutrients, certain food components (such as added sugars), and intake of food groups. (3) Calculate relevant ratios and score according to relevant standards: Calculate the score for each HEI-2015 component based on relevant standards. For example, calculate the ratio of fruit and vegetable intake to total energy intake, and similar ratios for other components. It is important to note that the HEI score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating better diet quality and greater adherence to healthy eating guidelines.

Measurement of age at menopause and reproductive lifespan

Self-reporting provided the age at menarche and menopause, which refer to the first and last menstrual periods, respectively. The following questions were used to calculate menopause age and menarche age: “How old were you when you had your first menstrual period?” “About how old were you when you had your last menstrual period?” The years between the age at menarche and the age at menopause are used to define reproductive lifespan.

Covariates

Based on previous studies [20, 21] and directed acyclic graph (DAG) (Figure S2), following potential confounding variables were selected from demographics, examination, laboratory and questionnaire data in our analysis: age (< 60, ≥ 60 years), race (non-Hispanic white, non-Hispanic black, Mexican American, others), education levels (under high school, high school, above high school), poverty income ratio (PIR) (0.00–1.30, 1.31–3.50, > 3.51), body mass index (BMI, kg/m2) (< 25, 25–30, > 30 kg/m2), marital status (married/Living with partner, widowed/divorced/separated, never married) smoking status (nonsmoking, passive smoking, active smoking), alcohol intake (non-drinking, low to moderate drinking, heavy drinking), hypertension (yes, no), diabetes (yes, no), physical activity (yes, no), total energy intake (low, adequate, high). Both hypertension and diabetes were determined based on self-reported health questionnaires. The classification of some covariates was defined in the Table S1.

Statistical analysis

In this study, frequencies of categorical variables were evaluated, and calculated the mean, standard deviation, median, and interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables in the descriptive analysis. In the general study population, we analyzed the demographic characteristics based on tertiles of HEI-2015 using unweighted Wilcoxon tests for continuous variables and unweighted chi-square analysis for categorical data. The normality test suggested a significant non-normal distribution for all variables (HEI-2015, age at menarche, age at menopause, and reproductive lifespan) (all p for Kolmogorov-Smirnov test < 0.05; Table S2). Therefore, they were ln-transformed to achieve a distribution that resembles a normal distribution [22, 23].

With the modeling prerequisites of normality, homogeneity of variance, and linearity satisfied, this study further determined the β coefficients and associated 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the relationships between the HEI-2015 score and ln-transformed reproductive parameters (such as age at menarche, age at menopause, and reproductive time life), the weighted multivariable linear regression models were utilized. The HEI-2015 score was treated as a categorical variable (tertiles) and as a continuous variable, respectively. Exponentiate the β coefficient using the natural exponential function, subtract 1 from the result, and then multiply by 100 to convert it to a percentage. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age, race/ethnicity, education levels, marital status, PIR, BMI, smoking status, physical activity, alcohol intake, diabetes, hypertension, and total energy intake. The median HEI-2015 score value within each tertile was used to calculate the p-values for the trend across tertiles. Additionally, a Generalized Additive Model (GAM) and restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression with three knots (10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles) were utilized to investigate the nonlinearity of the relationship between HEI-2015 and reproductive lifespan.

Then, the machine learning eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm model were developed to investigate the relative impact of HEI-2015 on reproductive lifespan. A sequence of weak decision tree model is generated by the XGBoost method, with each new tree trying to fix the mistakes committed by the preceding one [24]. This is achieved via XGBoost by fitting the new model iteratively to the residuals of the old model [25]. In essence, the goal of every subsequent tree in the series will be to reduce the faults of the preceding tree [24]. The final integrated model will perform better than its underlying tree model as a consequence [24]. Further determining the features’ significance using SHapley Additive Properties Interpretation (SHAP) values in order to gain a deeper understanding and interpretation of the model’s characteristics [26]. A well-liked technique for deciphering and elucidating machine learning model output is SHAP. It is possible to achieve a positive or negative SHAP value. A characteristic that contributes positively to the prediction is indicated by a positive SHAP value, while a feature that contributes negatively to the forecast is shown by a negative value. To better illustrate the significance of each feature in our prediction model, SHAP in this study were employed to assess each XGBoost feature’s contribution to the final prediction outcome (differentiating between positive and negative features). In this study, the learning rate was set to 0.05 to balance the model’s complexity and generalization capability. The maximum depth was set to 6 to prevent overfitting, and the number of trees was determined to be 500 to ensure a balance between stability and accuracy [26]. Additionally, both “subsample” and “colsample_bytree” were set to 0.8 to reduce the risk of overfitting. These adjustments ensure that the model maintains good generalizability while capturing the complexity of the data [26].

To address possible multicollinearity between variables in the regression model, a weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression were utilized to examine the health importance of individual dietary components in the overall diet with the aim to evaluate the impact of various dietary components in the HEI-2015 on reproductive lifespan [27]. An empirically-weighted index of HEI-2015 components based on their quantiles is generated by employing the WQS regression analysis, which incorporates the index as a single exposure term in a regression model and assumes linear and additive effects. Based on the quartiles of the HEI-2015 components, the WQS index of those components were constructed based on quartiles of HEI components, and 40% of the data were applied as the test set and the remaining 60% as the validation set. There were 1000 bootstrapping steps in multivariate regression models [28]. The WQS index ranges from 0 to 1, and its sum is 1. One set of WQS regression models were fitted to explore positive correlations between mixed HEI-2015 components and reproductive lifespan, with the parameters of model set as described above. Additionally, positive or negative constraints were applied in the optimization function for the weight estimation [29].

Finally, a variety of sensitivity studies were conducted to assess the reliability of our findings. First, utilizing the multivariate linear regression model as previously described, after excluding HEI-2015 values smaller than the mean − 3SD and larger than the mean + 3SD to investigate the relationship between HEI-2015 and reproductive lifespan. Second, further excluding individuals with a very low or a very high caloric intake (< 600 or > 3500 kcal/day in women) and then performed sensitivity analyses [30]. Thirdly, to further evaluate the impact of confounding bias on the results, weighted stepwise forward and backward regressions were employed to confirm the relationship between HEI-2015 and increased reproductive lifespan. Fourth, by applying linear models, the relationship between each 1-unit increase in the 13 distinct HEI-2015 components and ln-transformed reproductive lifespan was furtherly evaluated. Finally, subgroup analyses were performed to examine whether the effects of HEI-2015 and reproductive lifespan could be modified by covariates through regression models that included interaction terms for the subgroups in separate models. Wald tests were used to estimate p-values for the interaction. All analyses were weighted to account for the complex survey design of NHANES. All analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.0 (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study participants

Table 1 displays the demographic characteristics of the 2761 postmenopausal women in the study, categorized by tertile of HEI-2015 score. The individuals’ mean age was 63.69 ± 9.34 years. 49.8% of these subjects were non-Hispanic White, and 41.5% of them were obese. The range (from minimum to maximum) of HEI-2015 was 18.62–94.19 (18.62–49.32, 49.34–60.54, and 60.57–94.19 for tertile 1, tertile 2, and tertile 3, respectively). Participants who closely adhered to the HEI-2015 were more likely to be older, non-Hispanic white, non-smokers, and not living in poverty. They also had higher levels of education, a higher proportion of physical activity, reduced alcohol intake, lower daily calorie intake, lower prevalence of hypertension and diabetes, later age at menarche, and longer reproductive lifespan due to a higher age at menopause.

Association of HEI-2015 with reproductive lifespan

To examine the association between HEI-2015 and reproductive lifetime in various models, multiple regression models were used. An older menopausal age was linked to a higher quality diet (Table 2). Consistent with the results of treating HEI-2015 as a continuous variable, individuals in the highest HEI-2015 tertile had a 2.84% (1.31–4.39%) higher age at menopause after adjusting for potential covariates compared to those in the lowest HEI-2015 tertile. Age at menopause and HEI-2015 tertiles were associated in a dose-response manner (p-value for trend < 0.001). We also calculated the percentage variation in reproductive time life for each of the ten-unit increments in the HEI-2015. Table 2 also showed that all models had a positive association between HEI-2015 and reproductive time life. (Model 2: Percent difference = 1.43%, 95% CI: 0.70% 2.16%). The tertile 3 group’s HEI-2015 score was associated with a longer reproductive time life in all models when the HEI-2015 was treated as a categorical variable, with the tertile 1 group serving as the reference group (Model 2: Percent difference = -1.59%, 95% CI: -0.90% to -2.18%, p for trend = 0.002). This suggests that optimal intake was helpful in extending reproductive time life. Despite this, no discernible correlation was found between the age of menarche and HEI-2015. To assess if HEI-2015 and reproductive lifespan have a non-linear dose-response relationship, smooth curve fits based on GAM were performed. After controlling for all factors, the results demonstrated a linear relationship between HEI-2015 and reproductive lifespan (including age at menopause and reproductive time life) (Figure S3).

XGBoost algorithm models reveal HEI-2015 relative importance

In order to further evaluate the efficacy of the HEI-2015 in predicting reproductive lifespan, elucidating the effect of HEI-2015 on reproductive lifespan by using SHAP values, which aimed to delve into the “black box” of the XGBoost model. In the final XGBoost model, the Mean Squared Error (MSE) of 0.08 indicates a relatively small average squared difference between the predicted and actual observed values. Furthermore, the Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE) of 0.28 offers an intuitive understanding of the prediction error; its relatively low value implies that the model has high predictive accuracy. Based on the SHAP summary plot of the XGBoost model, the importance ranking of the independent variables indicated that the top 5 variables contributing the most to the XGBoost model for age at menopause were age, HEI-2015, education, PIR, and race, respectively. In addition, the HEI-2015 was also among the top 5 most important variables in the XGBoost model for reproductive time life were found in the study (Fig. 1 & Figure S4). In summary, HEI-2015 has a impactful positive predictive efficacy for reproductive lifespan.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were conducted in this study using weighted multivariate linear regression after excluding extreme energy intake data and extreme HEI-2015 scores. In line with the previously mentioned findings, all results demonstrated a positive correlation between HEI-2015 and both reproductive time life and age at menopause in both the fully adjusted and non-adjusted models, as shown in Tables S4-S5. Moreover, both weighted stepwise forward and backward regressions consistently showed HEI-2015 is positively associated with age at menopause and reproductive time life (p-value < 0.05) (Table S6), which further validates the robustness of the results.

Moreover, multivariate regression analysis of 13 HEI-2015 components revealed that the scores for total fruits, whole fruits, and total protein foods were significantly correlated with age at menopause after controlling for covariates. Total fruits (percent difference = 0.44%; 95% CI 0.10–0.79%), whole fruits (percent difference = 0.38%; 95% CI 0.09–0.68%), and total vegetables (percent difference = 0.33%; 95% CI 0.16–0.50%) were the components that were most closely related (Table 3). Reproductive time life increased by 0.74% (95% CI: 0.06–1.42%), 0.58% (95% CI: 0.06–1.42%), and 0.53% (95% CI: 0.28–0.77%), respectively, for every unit higher score for total fruits, whole fruits, and total protein foods among the 13 HEI-2015 components (Table 3).

Discussion

Main finding

Higher diet quality, particularly increased consumption of whole fruits, total fruits, and total protein foods, was found to be cross-sectionally associated with a later self-reported age at menopause and reproductive time life in a representative sample of postmenopausal women from the U.S. who participated in NHANES 2005–2016. Even after accounting for sociodemographic factors, lifestyle variables, and health conditions, a dose-response relationship between HEI-2015 and reproductive lifespan persisted. HEI-2015 was identified as the one of most valuable indicator for reproductive lifespan by the XGBoost model. The subgroup analysis demonstrated that the overall findings were consistent with a positive correlation between HEI-2015 and reproductive lifespan. This study is the first of its kind to assess the relationship between reproductive lifespan and diet quality in a nationally representative sample of adult US citizens.

The age at menopause in women is influenced by various factors, and dietary habits, as part of lifestyle, undoubtedly have a certain impact on the age at menopause in women. Previous studies have indicated that women who are malnourished, short in stature, and lighter in weight generally experience menopause earlier, while women with good nutritional status, taller stature, and relatively heavier weight experience menopause later. Additionally, research has shown that women who exercise regularly tend to experience menopause later than those who do not exercise [31]. Currently, there is no direct research indicating the impact of the HEI on the age at menopause in women, but dietary factors intake can affect the age at menopause in women [32, 33]. Currently, anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) is considered a relatively accurate marker that can predict ovarian aging and the age at menopause. Its association with serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] has been confirmed [34]. Among the nutritional factors, the intake of soy and serum 25(OH)D concentrations have been primarily evaluated. Soy and its products may affect reproductive health and fertility because they are a rich source of plant estrogens, which can interfere with estrogen action by altering the concentration and biological availability of estrogen as agonists or antagonists [35]. If vitamin D can regulate ovarian reserve, it will also affect the onset of menopause. After adjusting for total energy intake, a higher intake of vitamin D is associated with a delay in the onset of menopause in the Nurses’ Health Study (NHS). However, this correlation loses significance after adjusting for other covariates [36]. Some studies have found that DNA damage response genes are associated with the natural age of menopause, and these genes play a crucial role throughout a woman’s life by regulating ovarian function. A healthy diet may influence the age at menopause by providing essential nutrients such as protein, minerals, and vitamins to support the DNA repair process [37].

Antioxidant nutrients in the diet, such as vitamin C, vitamin E, and beta-carotene, can help protect the ovaries from damage caused by free radicals, slow down ovarian aging, and potentially postpone the onset of menopause [38–40]. Previous studies have indicated that a high-carbohydrate, low-protein diet can maximize the lifespan of women. Additionally, in significantly different parts of nutrient intake, women’s reproductive performance reaches its maximum [41]. Interestingly, a randomized clinical trial has provided strong evidence that long-term adherence to a low-fat, high-carbohydrate diet does not affect the timing of menopause [42]. Therefore, the impact of HEI on women’s reproductive health can provide a scientific basis for developing health interventions and personalized nutrition guidance. By further studying the relationship between HEI and the age at menopause or reproductive lifespan in women, we can better understand the mechanisms by which diet affects the reproductive system. This understanding can help provide more effective reproductive health management strategies for women, delay ovarian aging, improve reproductive health status, and thereby enhance women’s quality of life and overall health.

Elevated HEI-2015 affects the age at menopause and the ability to maintain pregnancy through its impact on endocrine signaling pathways [43]. It helps maintain normal secretion of the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis at various levels, leading to a delayed age at menopause [44]. Another potential protective mechanism involves reducing oxidative stress in the reproductive organs, which can be achieved by either decreasing ROS generation or enhancing the body’s antioxidant capacity [45]. Elevated HEI reduces oxidative stress in the reproductive tissues, which may protect against DNA damage [46]. Experiments have demonstrated that individuals who consume high-quality food exhibit lower levels of DNA damage, as measured by 8-hydroxydeoxyguanosine, the comet assay, micronucleus frequency, and epigenetic and chromosomal abnormalities [47]. Interestingly, women’s reproductive capacity declines significantly after the age of 40 due to various biological factors. These include an exponential rise in the number of age-related defective embryos [48]. Moreover, improved nutrition quality may offer genetic and epigenetic protective effects that can prolong reproductive life [49].

Interestingly, women’s reproductive capacity collapses above the age of 40 for a variety of biological reasons, including an exponential increase in the number of age-related defective embryos, and better nutrition quality may have genetic and epigenetic protective effects that extend reproductive life [50]. Among the 13 HEI-2015 components, we found that total fruits, whole fruits, and total protein meals had the strongest associations with age at menopause and reproductive time life. Fruits and protein-rich meals are rich in bioactive compounds such as vitamins and carotenoids, which possess antioxidant properties. Therefore, it is not surprising that consuming these foods regularly helps to decrease cellular damage caused by free radicals and reduce the risk of a shortened reproductive lifespan [16]. Furthermore, fruits and protein-rich meals contain a high concentration of dietary fiber and other nutrients that can reduce levels of estrogen and N-nitroso compounds while increasing levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines, thereby extending the onset and progression of reproductive lifespan [51].

Strengths and limitations

There are various advantages to our research. Firstly, this is the first study that examines the relationship between HEI-2015 and reproductive lifespan that we are aware of. In addition to the overall healthy diet index, we investigated 13 individual diets. Secondly, we eliminated the influence of certain covariates. Our findings may be applied to the general population of the United States because all analyses utilized weighted samples and considered the stratum and primary sampling units (PSU) variables of the design to generate estimates suitable for the American population. Strengths of this study include the large sample size and the high quality of NHANES sampling and methods, which provide comprehensive information for correcting various risk variables. The study’s other key strengths include the large sample size and the high quality of NHANES sampling and procedures, especially the availability of comprehensive information for correcting multiple risk variables.

It’s important to acknowledge some restrictions. First, the cross-sectional design of the investigation made it impossible to establish a causal relationship between the HEI-2015 and reproductive lifespan, emphasizing the necessity for a longer study to clarify the results. Second, in terms of dietary patterns, the data used for HEI calculation were collected from a 24-hour dietary recall, which may not necessarily be representative of an individual’s habitual diet. This could introduce recall bias. Thirdly, assessments that are made too quickly could overlook dietary habits that change over time. While using HEI makes it possible to translate dietary data into more stable dietary patterns, our findings need to be confirmed by long-term cohort studies. Fourth, although we have attempted to screen as many covariates as possible to control for confounding bias, we were unable to rule out potential confounding variables such as socioeconomic status and environmental exposures, which were either not measured or not included in the research. Lastly, it was inevitable that certain unidentified or unrecorded confounding variables, such as socioeconomic status or additional exposures that might occur in similar environmental conditions [16], would impact the outcomes. Age at first and last pregnancy, regularity of menstruation, anovulatory menstrual cycles, and markers of bone remodeling [16] are among the characteristics that were not available in NHANES and therefore could not be included in the model to address residual confounding.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings indicate that the HEI-2015 is positively associated with age at menopause and reproductive time life. The findings indicate that better adherence to the 2015–2020 DGA adherence as measured by the HEI-2015 may be beneficial for extending reproductive lifespan, especially in achieving the ideal HEI-2015 and meeting the recommended intake of vegetables and high-protein diets. These insights are valuable for guiding future prospective studies.
