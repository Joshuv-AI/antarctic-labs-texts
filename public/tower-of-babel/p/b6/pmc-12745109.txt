Simulation-guided design of leaflet height in bicuspidization of the aortic valve
Authors: Alexander D. Kaiser, Perry S. Choi, Amit Sharir, Alison L. Marsden, Michael R. Ma
Published: 2025

Central Message

In simulations of bicuspidization repair, an intermediate range of leaflet geometric height appeared to be optimal. Insufficient height caused regurgitation, while excessive height showed less regular coaptation.

Perspective

Bicuspidization repair has proven effective for severe congenital aortic valve disease; however, engineering knowledge of the optimal leaflet geometry is limited. In this study, leaflet geometric height was systematically varied in a computational model. An intermediate range of heights appeared to be optimal; insufficient height caused regurgitation, and excessive height showed lack of a “crisp” coaptation line.

Bicuspidization repair is typically applied to severe congenital aortic valve lesions. Originally developed for repair of unicuspid aortic valves,1 this repair more recently has been expanded to other pathologic aortic valve phenotypes and truncal valve repair.2, 3, 4 This repair has proven to be an effective and durable surgical approach; however, engineering studies exploring the optimal leaflet geometry in this repair are limited. In this study, the geometric height, or leaflet height along the midline, was studied with a systematic, computational approach. This work serves to highlight specific, quantitative mechanisms that influence repair performance.

Previously, we conducted a simulation-guided design study of free edge length—the leaflet length from commissure to commissure—in bicuspidization repair.5 It was found that a free edge length of 1.5 to 1.6 times the annular diameter (or one-half the annular circumference) minimized stenosis and the pressure gradient and thus appeared optimal for systolic performance. A free edge length of 1.4 times the annular diameter showed a trivially higher gradient. A shorter still free edge length <1.3 times the annular diameter exhibited increasing stenosis. This trend was confirmed in an ex vivo porcine model, which showed a similar trend toward an elevated gradient with free edge length 1.2 d and lower gradient with free edge length 1.57 d.6

The optimal leaflet geometry of a symmetric bicuspid valve appears to be relatively unstudied. One study found a rare porcine Sievers type 0 bicuspid valve and examined its hemodynamics ex vivo.7 A surgical study examined the geometric height of trileaflet and asymmetric Sievers type 1 bicuspid valves.8 In contrast, there is extensive research on the optimal geometry of the trileaflet aortic valve, including ex vivo studies9 and computed tomography studies.10 One study provided normal values of geometric and effective height as a function of body surface area.11 A simulation study modified the leaflet area including geometric height and studied changes in coaptation reserve with a trileaflet valve.12 A sequence of studies examined changes in effective height with changes in root diameter.13,14

In this work, leveraging previously developed models for the aortic valve,15 model symmetric bicuspid valves with varying geometric heights were constructed. Fluid–structure interaction simulations were performed, in which a coupled system of partial differential equations modeled the fully coupled dynamics of the valve, aorta, and blood. These simulations resulted in detailed predictions of blood flow, pressure, and valve motion through the cardiac cycle. Each geometric height was tested with 2 free edge lengths, 1.4 d and 1.57 d, where d is the annular diameter. This approach allows geometric height to be isolated as a variable and systematically changed, which would be highly challenging ex vivo and impossible in patients. Models were evaluated for stenosis, regurgitation, hemodynamics, leaflet mechanics, and gross morphology.

Methods

Modeling Methods

Model valves were constructed with 2 symmetric leaflets subtending 180 degrees of the annulus. Model construction proceeded by a first-principles approach known as “elasticity-based design,” which has been described previously.5,15 In brief, a prediction of the closed and loaded geometry was derived as the solution to a system of partial differential equations, dictated by the requirement that tension in the leaflets supports a pressure load. Model properties, including heterogeneous stiffnesses and fiber orientation and resting/reference geometry, were then derived from the predicted loaded configuration. This process produced material stiffnesses on the closed, diastolic configuration comparable to experimental data.16,17

Eight model valves were constructed. The free edge lengths at rest were set to 1.4 d, which was previously found to cause a trivial increase in pressure gradient from optimal, and 1.57 d, which was previously found to produce an optimal pressure gradient.15 For each of 2 free edge lengths, 4 geometric heights were studied. The resting geometric heights considered were 8, 11, 14, and 17 mm. The leaflets incorporate physiologic material properties and thus achieve stretch when loaded in agreement with experimentally observed values.18 Results of geometric height and free edge length incorporate this stretch as determined directly from the simulation. Thus, the emergent free edge length and geometric heights are larger than the rest lengths when loaded in diastole, approximately 12, 16, 20, and 24 mm at a free edge length of 1.4 d and 12, 16, 20, and 21 mm at a free edge length of 1.57 d.

Fluid–structure interaction simulations were performed with the model valves placed in a single patient-specific control aortic geometry. An anonymized computed tomography CT scan was obtained from a patient with no known aortic or valvular disease (Institutional Review Board [IRB] approval 39,377, approved June 27, 2023), and the control aortic geometry was segmented with SimVascular.19 Patient written consent for the publication of the study data was waived by the IRB for use of anonymized, retrospectively acquired data. To create an isolated test of valve function and avoid complications associated with modeling patient-specific, pathologic variation in root motion, we modeled the root and vessel as approximately rigid. This patient had an annular diameter of approximately 25 mm at both the virtual basal ring (minimum plane of the annulus) and the sinotubular junction (STJ). Valves were attached to a rigid scaffold, which can be understood as a virtual sewing ring or scaffold on which a bioprosthetic would be mounted. The scaffold served to precisely fix the diameter throughout the 3-dimensional aortic annulus and prevent “virtual paravalvular leak” between the aortic and leaflet geometries. Simulations were performed with the immersed boundary method20 via the solver library IBAMR (Immersed Boundary Adaptive Mesh Refinement).21

Simulations were run for 2 cardiac cycles. The results from the first cycle were discarded for initialization effects, and results from the second were analyzed. The cardiac cycle duration was fixed at 0.8 second or 75 bpm. Simulations were driven by pressures immediately upstream of the left ventricular outflow tract and at the distal ascending aorta. These pressures were determined dynamically by a lumped parameter network, with left ventricular and aortic pressures that respond adaptively to virtual left ventricular volume and aortic outflow. The ventricular pressure was determined by a “time-varying elastance” model, in which ventricular pressure is a multiple of virtual left ventricular volume, the coefficient of which varies through the cycle.22 The time-varying elastance model implicitly includes the Frank-Starling mechanism via generation of increased ventricular pressure and stroke volume in the presence of increased ventricular volume due to aortic valve dysfunction. To tune this model, experimentally measured waveforms23 were segmented, normalized to the specified cardiac cycle duration, smoothed via convolution with a normalized cosine bump, and then represented as a finite Fourier series with 600 frequencies. Mitral inflow into the model left ventricle was fixed and prescribed. Parameters were then tuned to the processed experimental data with Nelder-Mead optimization with SciPy.24 The aortic pressure was determined by a resistor-capacitor-resistor boundary condition with identical parameters as used previously.5 The code for model generation is open-source and freely available (https://github.com/alexkaiser/heart_valves).

Model Evaluation

Models were evaluated for stenosis and regurgitation. Peak gradient, mean gradient, orifice area, regurgitant fraction, regurgitant volume, and stroke volume were assessed. Gross morphology of the diastolic configuration was assessed with a variety of metrics. Additional diagrams and schematics are available in previous publications.5 In calculations, the leaflet geometric height, Gh, represents the 3-dimensional length of the leaflet midline; the effective height, Eh, is the height of the center point on the free edge measured normal to the virtual basal ring; and the billow height is the minimum height of any point on the leaflet below the virtual basal ring. The free edge length is represented as F. Nondimensional ratios of lengths were also evaluated, including the ratio of geometric height to diameter, Gh/d; the ratio of effective to geometric height, Eh/Gh; and the free edge to annular diameter ratio. The coaptation reserve height, defined as the portion of the leaflet midline above the first point <2 mm from any point on the opposing leaflet, was evaluated. Notably, owing to diffuse coupling between the fluid and structure, the simulation method leaves a small gap of approximately 1 to 2 mm, which is effectively in contact and does not allow leak. Leaflet stresses in the circumferential, or fiber direction, and radial, or cross-fiber direction, were evaluated as well.

Results

Fluid–structure interaction simulations were performed in 8 cases. The 6 cases with the largest geometric height, which had ratio of geometric height to diameter, Gh/d, of ≥0.64, showed an initial transient of backflow during valve closure followed by no regurgitation. The 2 shortest cases, which had a diastolic geometric height approximately one-half the annular diameter, showed no coaptation and severe regurgitation. All cases without regurgitation showed similar stroke volume, and cases with regurgitation had an elevated stroke volume.

Flows throughout the cardiac cycle showed unrestricted forward flow and closure without leak in a representative case (Figure 1). Waveforms of pressure and flow confirmed these qualitative observations (Figure 2). The diastolic configurations showed no regurgitation as long as geometric height was not excessively low (Figure 3). Cases with a geometric height of approximately 12 mm, which had the lowest geometric height and a Gh/d < 0.5, showed no coaptation and severe regurgitation. All other cases sealed without leak and showed no regurgitation. With increasing geometric height, the line of coaptation appeared less straight and “crisp.” Correspondingly, the appearance of excess leaflet material and billow increased, and a slight twisting or “pinwheeling” appeared. Cases with F = 1.4 d showed slightly lower values of these potentially negative leaflet kinematics. Cases with geometric height 12 mm, which had severe regurgitation with regurgitant fraction >0.5, had a Gh/d < 0.5, indicating insufficient geometric height. Cases with geometric height >21 mm and Gh/d > 0.86 had the appearance of excessive leaflet material and poor coaptation. Pressure gradients decreased with geometric height for each free edge length, with lower mean and peak gradients for 1.57 d cases compared to 1.4 d cases at each geometric height. Cases that showed severe regurgitation showed elevated gradients due to greatly increased stroke volume, as the model compensates for regurgitant volume from the previous cycle, as well as decreased leaflet excursion. Orifice area increased with geometric height and free edge length, with a larger area seen in each 1.57 d case compared to the corresponding 1.4 d case. Stroke volume was nearly constant in all competent cases, as desired with adaptive left ventricular pressures.

Stresses were computed in both the fiber and cross-fiber directions (Figure 5). In cases without regurgitation, the fiber stress magnitude appeared similar through the belly of the leaflet. With a geometric height of 16 mm, a thin region of lower fiber direction stress appeared at the free edge. As geometric height increased to 20 mm, larger regions of low stress appeared near the free edge, and similar regions were larger still in cases with a geometric height >21 mm. The lower local stress indicated that this region was not heavily loaded and that portions of the leaflet further from the free edge were bearing the load. In all cases, locally high radial stress decreased with increasing geometric height, likely because the “tighter” positions of the leaflets transferred more of the pressure force from the curved, loaded circumferential fibers to the radial direction. Coaptation was evaluated as distance from the opposing leaflet (Figure 6). In all competent cases, a clear coaptation line occurred, visible in dark red and black on the side view of the leaflets. This coaptation line appeared at the top and immediately above the regions of high fiber stress. With a geometric height 16 mm, this line was immediately below the free edge, and there was no indication of poor coaptation alignment. With a geometric height of 20 mm, the coaptation appeared slightly lower, with more excess material above, and this trend was increased in cases with a geometric height >21 mm. In regions with lower fiber stress, greater coaptation distance was seen, indicating that the leaflets must be loaded fairly close to the free edge to avoid a “floppy” closed configuration. Discussion

In simulations with a variety of geometric heights, we observed that an intermediate range, with Gh/d ranging from 0.64 to 0.78, appeared to perform best. With insufficient leaflet height, there was no coaptation and severe regurgitation, as expected. Cases with a geometric height of 16 mm and Gh/d of 0.64 showed an adequate coaptation size of approximately 4 mm. Cases with a geometric height of 20 mm and Gh/d of 0.78 to 0.79 had approximately 6 to 7 mm of coaptation reserve and showed a somewhat less regular coaptation line. Cases with a geometric height >21 mm with 9 to 10 mm of coaptation reserve showed much less alignment, more billow, and the appearance of excess material (Video 1). These geometric heights are similar to those reported in studies of healthy native trileaflet morphology, which found a normal geometric height of approximately 0.7 d.9 Trileaflet free edge length was approximately 1.2 d,9 which was observed to lead to elevated gradient in bicuspidization in simulations5 and ex vivo.6

With excessive height, billow and the appearance of less crisp coaptation occurred. Although the coaptation reserve increased with leaflet height, this might not be indicative of a better-performing valve, because it leads to a lack of alignment in the coaptation region. At the same time, the clinical significance of a less straight and crisp coaptation line secondary to increased coaptation height is unclear, especially in the context of a growing patient. Indeed, several studies have conjectured that increased coaptation height actually may be advantageous to help accommodate growth in the Ozaki procedure, in which the leaflets and their dimensions are constructed explicitly.25,26 Although this occurs in the context of a trileaflet aortic valve, the conceptual benefits of coaptation height likely apply to a bileaflet model as well, but this requires further investigation.

In clinical practice, the desired height may be obtained by removing a portion of the leaflet belly or adding patch material near the annulus2 or, in a more limited manner, may be influenced by how much native leaflet is detached from the annulus. This work illuminates the potential failure modes when the surgeon does not wish to modify the geometric height or the geometric height will change only insignificantly. This knowledge may lead the surgeon to reconsider the details of the operative plan when the height is insufficient or excessive.

This work suggests that simply creating taller leaflets is not necessarily beneficial. While in many cases the surgeon will determine the postoperative geometric height based on the constraints of native tissue, if geometric height is being augmented or remodeled, excessive height may lead to less crisp coaptation. It is possible that aortic-pressure blood may push against the small, exposed portion of the ventricular side of the leaflets, causing a retrograde jet to initiate regurgitation, although this was not observed in our simulation.

An interesting combination of patterns in leaflet stresses and coaptation emerged. Leaflets showed higher circumferential, or fiber direction, stress in similar locations on the leaflet belly at a similar distance from the annulus in all cases. The portion of the leaflets above this region was largely unloaded and increased in size with geometric height. The leaflets had the closest coaptation at the top and immediately above this loaded region. Regions of lower stress generally did not align well, showing twist and physically unstable “flopping” of tissue at the free edge. Leaflets showed generally decreasing radial stress as the size of these unloaded regions increased. Leaflet stress has been associated with degeneration of bioprosthetic valves,27,28 but in these results stresses in the belly were relatively consistent, and differences were primarily in regions of low stress, so the potential impact on subsequent valve failure is inconclusive.

This study presents many opportunities for future work but has some minor limitations. We controlled for a symmetric 180-degree commissure configuration, which might not be achievable depending on patient anatomy, including commissure and coronary position. Future work will examine systematic changes in angle as they relate to leaflet size. This study used a single control patient geometry, which allows for direct, controlled comparisons but nonetheless is not a cohort with its inherent variation in anatomy. This patient had approximately equal diameters throughout the 3-dimensional annulus from the virtual basal ring to the STJ. In future work, the interdependent effects of the diameter of the virtual basal ring and STJ and leaflet geometry will be studied systematically. While alterations to anatomy, patient physiology such as heart rate or systemic blood pressure may change specific values of parameters, we would expect the general trends predicted in this work to be consistent. Material properties do not include altered material response and dynamics of patch material or suture lines, which would be more realistic to include but are highly dependent on preoperative patient anatomy. The acute effects of portions of altered material properties can be studied in future work.

Conclusions

In this study, an intermediate range of geometric heights of 16 to 20 mm with Gh/d ranging from 0.64 to 0.78 appeared best, with coaptation heights of 4 to 7 mm. Cases with a free edge length of 1.57 d had slightly better performance that cases with a free edge length of 1.4 d in terms of gradients, lower orifice area, and wider jets of forward flow, but had slightly lower effective height, slightly larger billow, and the appearance of slightly more excess tissue. The roles of growth and oversizing should be studied in future work, as valve performance surely will change with changing annulus size.
