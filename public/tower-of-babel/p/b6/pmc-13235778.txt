Structure-functionality relationship of collagen-fibrin interpenetrating hydrogels for engineered tumor-stroma models
Authors: Sae Rome Choi, Seamus M. Mellican, Tyler J. Roberts, Sehong Kang, Bennett D. Elzey, Hyunjoon Kong, Chelsea S. Davis, Melissa L. Fishel, Matthew J. Flick, Alisa S. Wolberg, Bumsoo Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Collagen and fibrin are two major extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins involved in tumorigenesis. During cancer progression, collagen and fibrin undergo sequential and concurrent polymerization and degradation, altering ECM structure and functional properties, which impact cell behavior and drug resistance. While engineered tumor models are recently emerging, including 3D printed tissue models and microphysiological systems (MPS), the interaction of these two proteins and its impact on the structure-functionality are largely overlooked. A critical knowledge gap on the structure-functionality relationship of collagen-fibrin interpenetrating hydrogels limits reliable reconstitution of the ECM of the tumor-stroma microenvironment. To address this, we characterized the fibrillar microstructure and biomechanical properties (e.g., diffusivity, Young’s modulus, and cell-derived contraction) of collagen-fibrin interpenetrating hydrogels while varying the protein ratios and sequence of polymerization. The results show that the pore size of the composite matrices decreases as fibrin content increases. This structural change is correlated to the decreased diffusivity, which can be partially recovered after fibrin depletion. In contrast, Young’s modulus only increases at lower fibrin content and decreases with increases in fibrin content, even below collagen-only levels. Cell-derived contraction is well correlated with these Young’s modulus changes. Confocal microscopy analysis shows that collagen-fibrin gels have distinctly different microstructure depending on polymerization sequence, which highlights how collagen-fibrin interactions during polymerization shape matrix properties, with important implications for the design of engineered tumor models.

Introduction

Engineered tumor models are emerging, including microphysiological systems (MPS), organoids, and 3D printed tumor tissues, for cancer biology and oncology research [1-3]. In these models, native and synthetic materials are used to reconstitute the extracellular matrix (ECM) of the tumor-stroma tissues. Understanding how matrix composition and polymerization conditions influence the ECM microstructure and subsequent functional properties are critical to design tumor models accurately reconstituting the in vivo tumor microenvironment (TME). The microstructure of the stroma and its ECM is important because it regulates growth, adhesion, and migration of cells within the TME [4], as well as hindering drug delivery to cancer cells [5]. The recent emergence of mechanobiology has also highlighted the importance of matrix stiffness on cell processes from the nano- to macro-scale [6].

Many cancers such as pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) are characterized with desmoplastic stroma tissues, that feature stiff ECM with high collagen and proteoglycan content [3,7,8]. Collagen is continuously synthesized and degraded during tumorigenesis to create a pro-tumorigenic TME supporting proliferation, migration, and drug resistance of tumor cells. Collagen is degraded by matrix metalloproteinases which are released by a variety of cells, including fibroblasts, macrophages, and neutrophils. Synthesis of collagen primarily occurs by cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) which in cancer can become chronically activated where they over-deposit matrix creating a dense, fibrous stroma leading to tumor progression [9].

Furthermore, the leaky tumor vasculature permits strong extravascular coagulation within the TME, producing highly localized deposition of fibrin [10]. While the canonical role of fibrin is to provide a hemostatic plug and a provisional matrix for wound healing [11], in the context of cancer, it serves a different purpose [11]. Initially, fibrinogen is produced in the liver and circulates in the plasma; however, due to the abnormally leaky tumor vasculature, fibrinogen and other plasma proteins are exposed to the extravascular tumor microenvironment, where cancer and stroma cells express tissue factor that leads to conversion of prothrombin into thrombin and subsequent conversion of fibrinogen into fibrin. Deposited fibrin interacts with the pre-existing collagen matrix to alter its physical properties and influence cell behavior. Although plasmin degrades fibrin [12], this process is dysregulated within the TME, which promotes tumor growth, invasiveness, metastasis, and immune evasion [13-15].

Of the proteins present in the ECM, collagen and fibrin are the two most widely used ECM proteins in MPS and 3D printed tumor models [16-20]. Due to their significance and ease of access, both collagen and fibrin have been well studied in isolation. However, because of the chronically activated tissue state in the TME, both collagen and fibrin are continuously synthesized and degraded in parallel, creating heterogeneous tissue properties [10]. This dynamic environment creates a complex ECM composition where each protein may impact the polymerization of the other and affect the overall microstructure and functional properties (i.e. drug transport, matrix stiffness, cell-derived contraction). The structure-functionality relationship of these collagen-fibrin hydrogels is not well established and may be necessary to design new engineered tumor models reliably recapitulating the complex TME.

The objective of this study was, thus, to establish a structure-functionality relationship of collagen-fibrin interpenetrating hydrogels that is relevant to the ECM of engineered tumor-stroma models. Our underlying hypothesis is the interactions of collagen and fibrin(ogen) during polymerization and degradation determine the microstructure and subsequent functional properties of the collagen-fibrin(ogen) interpenetrating hydrogels. The rationale is that collagen and fibrin are continuously synthesized and degraded in the TME during tumorigenesis [4,9,21], and the polymerization process of each protein may be affected by the presence of the other protein and/or polymerized structure [22]. To test this hypothesis, we explored how the composition and sequence of collagen and fibrin polymerization influence the material properties of the hydrogel. We characterized the microstructure of the interpenetrating hydrogels using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and confocal microscopy. We then measured diffusivity, elastic modulus, and contraction by PDAC-derived CAFs within the interpenetrating hydrogels with varying compositions and polymerization sequences. The diffusivity and elastic modulus were measured because their relevance to drug delivery and mechanotransduction in the TME. We used a PDAC-derived CAF line to study cell contraction based on the evidence that PDAC has one of the most distinctive desmoplastic stroma with rich collagen and fibrin content [23,24]. The results presented were further discussed to establish quantitative knowledge-based for designing and analyzing engineered tumor-stroma models with desired functional properties that better recapitulate the TME and ultimately enhance clinical relevance.

Materials and methods

Histology and immunofluorescence staining of PDAC tumor tissues

To visualize the collagen and fibrin microstructure of PDAC tissues, we procured both murine normal pancreata (n = 3) and PDAC tissues (n = 7) after orthotopic injection of KPC cancer cell lines containing the following genetic modifications: KrasG12D/+, Trp53R172H/+, and ElasCreER/+ [14], and human normal adjacent pancreata (n > 3) and PDAC tissues (n = 145) were cut into a tissue microarray (TMA). For the PDAC TMA, tissues from PDAC patients (August 2010–February 2021) were provided by the Indiana University Simon Comprehensive Cancer Center Biospecimen Collection and Banking Core under institutional bio-banking protocols with patient consent for cancer research use.

Paraffin fixed samples were prepared for formalin fixed KPC mouse tumor samples. In brief, tissues were fixed in 10 % neutral buffered formalin. They were placed in a Sakura Tissue-Tek VIP6 tissue processor for dehydration through graded ethanols, clearing in xylene and infiltration with Leica Paraplast Plus paraffin. After processing, tissues were embedded in Leica Paraplast Plus paraffin. Tissue sections were taken at a thickness of 4 μm using a Themo HM355S microtome. Sections were mounted on charged slides and dried for 30-60 min in a 60 °C oven. After drying, all slides were deparaffinized through 3 changes of xylene and rehydrated through graded ethanols to water in a Leica Autostainer XL.

For immunofluorescence staining, slides were incubated in 2.5 % normal horse serum for 20 min. Excess reagent was blown off and primary antibodies were applied. Anti-collagen I antibody (Southern Biotech, 1310-01) was applied at 1:100 and anti-fibrin(ogen) antibody (Dako, A0080) at 1:1000 for 60 min. The negative control slide was stained with Rabbit IgG (Vector Labs, I-1000) at a concentration of 1 ug/mL for 60 min. Slides were rinsed twice in TBST and anti-goat Alexa 555 secondary (Invitrogen, A21432) applied at 4 μg/mL for 30 min. An anti-rabbit dylight 488 (Vector Labs, DI-1488) was then applied at 4 μg/mL for 30 min. Slides were rinsed in TBST and counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen, EN62248) at 1 μg/mL for 10 min before rinsing and cover slipping with Prolong Gold (Invitrogen, P36934).

Preparation of collagen and fibrin gel

For control collagen samples, 3 mg/mL and 6 mg/mL type I collagen matrix (TeloCol, Advanced Biomatrix, CA) was prepared with 10x PBS, 1M NaOH, 0.1M HEPES solution, FBS, Glu, P/S, and cell-culture grade distilled water and incubated for 75 min for polymerization [25,26]. Control fibrin gel was prepared with human plasma-derived fibrinogen (Enzyme Research Laboratories, IN) and diluted in 0.9 % NaCl at 37 °C to reach 6 mg/ml concentration. The diluted solution was mixed with human thrombin (Enzyme Research Laboratories, IN), 1 U/mL (for hydrogels without cells, concentration for biomaterials usage) or 0.2 U/ml (for hydrogels containing cells for contraction assay) and incubated for 60 min for fibrin polymerization.

For collagen-fibrin(ogen) composite hydrogels, collagen and fibrinogen solutions were mixed to reach target ratios. For all hydrogel formulations that are not specified in the study, the hydrogels were prepared in 1:1 ratio. The hydrogel protein composition for each collagen:fibrin ratio is shown in Table 1. For co-polymerization samples, collagen-fibrinogen samples were mixed with 1 U/mL thrombin and incubated together for 1 h. For the sequential polymerization study, collagen-fibrinogen gel was first incubated at 37 °C for 75 min for collagen polymerization. Subsequently, 1 U/mL thrombin solution prepared in PBS was overlaid on the gels and incubated for another hour.

Integrated optical imaging (IOI) method

The diffusivity of the collagen-fibrin(ogen) matrix was measured using an integrative optical imaging (IOI) method [27,28]. Briefly, collagen-fibrin(ogen) hydrogel was prepared in a Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) mold with a diameter of 12 mm. For plasmin treated samples 0.5 μM plasmin was prepared in PBS and was incubated on top of the gels for 90 min. Subsequently, a small volume (25-50 pL) of fluorescent probes with known sizes were injected into the tissue constructs using a microinjection system (MINJ-D-BDCI, Tritech).

In this study, FITC-labeled dextran with molecular weight of 4 kDa (hydrodynamic radius = 1.3 nm) and 40 kDa (5.1nm) were used. After the injection, fluorescent images were taken at 2-second intervals for 120 sec. All samples were maintained at 37 °C throughout diffusion measurements. Experimental setup is depicted in Supplementary Figure 1. The fluorescence intensity of the images was quantified using image processing software (MATLAB, Mathworks). The intensity profiles of the micrographs can be found in Supplementary Figure 2. To obtain an estimated value of diffusivity, the intensity profiles were curve-fit using MATLAB lsqcurvefit non-linear function to the analytic solution of diffusion: I(r,γi)=Ei(γi)exp[−(rγi)2]

Where I is the fluorescent intensity, Ei is amplitude term compensating the de-focused point spread function, and r is the radius of the particle cluster. From curve-fitting, term γi can be defined as a function of time is given by: γi2=4Deff(ti+to)

Where ti is real time at which concentration distributions were collected and to is virtual time compensating the uncertainty in the exact time the injected dextran infiltrates the tissue space. Deff value is extracted from the slope of linear regression of γi2∕4 against time. Calculated diffusivity values were normalized by free diffusivity, Do, at 37 °C defined by stokes-einstein equation for both 4 kDa and 40 kDa dextran particles.

Scanning electron microscopy

To characterize the fiber structures of composite hydrogels, collagen-fibrin(ogen) hydrogel was prepared in PDMS mold with a diameter of 12 mm. For plasmin-treated samples, 0.06 μM plasmin was prepared in PBS and was incubated on top of the gels overnight [29-31]. The following day, the samples were fixed in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer for at least 60 min at room temperature. Subsequently, samples were triple rinsed with distilled water. The samples were further fixed in 2 % osmium tetroxide for one hour followed by triple washing with distilled water. Sequential dehydration was performed in a graded ethanol series 50 % (10 min), 70 % (2 × 10 min), 85 % (10 min), 95 % (2 × 10 min), and 100 % (3 × 10 min), and transferred into a Tousimis 931 and critical point dried. Dried specimens were coated with platinum in a Cressington 208HR sputter coater before imaging using an FEI NOVA nanoSEM field emission scanning electron microscope (FEI Company, Hillsboro Oregon) using ET (Everhart-Thornley) detector or the high-resolution through-the lens (TLD) detector operating at 5kV accelerating voltage, spot 3, ~5.0 mm working distance and 30 mm aperture. SEM images were quantitatively analyzed in a similar manner as previously described [32]. Fiber diameter from SEM images were measured using FIJI ImageJ. Number of nodes, or branching points, calculation was performed using StructuralGT open software.

Young’s modulus characterization

Collagen-fibrin gels were prepared as previously described in PDMS molds in chamber slides and incubated overnight. Flat punch indentation testing is used to determine the elastic moduli. The apparatus and procedure used to perform this testing has been previously described in detail [33,34]. For this study, the probe used is a 1.00 mm diameter, high-speed M2 tool steel cylinder (McMaster-Carr, IL). The probe is indented into the sample to a maximum compressive load of 5 mN at a displacement rate of 15 μm s−1. A more detailed explanation and schematic of the setup can be found in the supporting information (Supplementary Figure 3).

Turbidity assay

A turbidity assay measures polymerization kinetics by monitoring the increase in light scattering that occurs as soluble monomers or oligomers convert into insoluble, crosslinked hydrogels [35,36]. The output of this analysis is the optical density measured at different timepoints which is used to calculate lag time and polymerization rates giving quantitative information on the polymerization kinetics [37]. This turbidity assay was performed by preparing different tissue constructs in a pre-warmed 96-well plate at 37 °C. Absorbance at 405 nm was measured using GloMax Discover System (Promega Corporation, WI) in 30 sec intervals for 80 min at 37 °C.

Cancer-associated fibroblast contraction assay

Human cancer-associated fibroblast cell line, CAF19, was cultured in DMEM supplemented with 2.05 mM l-glutamine (GE Healthcare Bio-Sciences Corp., MA), 100 μg/mL penicillin/streptomycin (P/S, Invitrogen, CA), and 5 % v/v fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, CA). Collagen-fibrin gels were prepared as above except with 0.2 U/mL thrombin and 0.2 × 106 cells/mL CAF19. The hydrogels were placed 80 μL per in PDMS mold with diameter 6 mm and incubated for 90 min. Then, the hydrogels were carefully peeled off the mold and placed in 24 well plate, one sample per well. 1 mL DMEM was aliquoted per well and samples were imaged every day for 3 days with FITC channel of fluorescent microscope (Olympus MVX10, Japan). For image analysis, the gel area was calculated using FIJI ImageJ and the contraction index was defined as: ContractionIndex=[HydrogelArea]Dayn[HydrogelArea]Day0

Fluorescently-labeled collagen-fibrin gel formation for confocal imaging

A total of 2.5 mL of collagen pre-gel solution was mixed with 0.25 mL of 10× DMEM, 0.25 mL of reconstitution buffer, and 1.98 mL of 1× PBS. To initiate polymerization, 20 μL of 1 N NaOH was added, mixed rapidly, and the solution was transferred onto a pre-chilled 60 mm culture dish. The hydrogel was allowed to polymerize at room temperature for 60 min. Following polymerization, 5 mL of 50 mM borate buffer (pH 9.0) was added and incubated for 15 min at room temperature.

For fluorescent labeling, 58 μL of CF-660C dye was diluted in 50 mM borate buffer to a final volume of 5 mL and vortexed briefly. The borate buffer was aspirated from the collagen hydrogel, and the dye solution was added. Hydrogels were incubated overnight at 4 °C with gentle rocking to facilitate dye conjugation. The following day, the dye solution was removed and replaced with 5 mL of 50 mM Tris buffer to quench the reaction (10 min at 4 °C with rocking). Gels were then rinsed with PBS ten times over 6 h to remove unbound dye.

To extract the labeled collagen, 500 μL of 500 mM acetic acid was added and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C with rocking. The hydrogel was then scraped, transferred to a vial, and stirred overnight at 4 °C to fully solubilize. The resulting solution was dialyzed against 4 L of 20 mM acetic acid overnight. After dialysis, the solution was centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C to remove insoluble material. The supernatant containing fluorescently labeled collagen was collected and stored at 4 °C for further analysis.

CF-660C labeled collagen (6 mg/mL) was mixed 1:10 with non-labeled collagen and constituted to a final concentration of 6 mg/mL. The total amount of labeled collagen added was calculated and the volume required for Alexa Fluor 488 labeled fibrinogen (1.5 mg/mL, ThermoFisher, MA) to be matched with the same amount of labeled collagen was calculated and was mixed with unlabeled fibrinogen. The final concentration of fibrinogen was also constituted to 6 mg/mL. Collagen and collagen-fibrin hydrogels were then prepared using the same methods as described above. The labeled polymer hydrogels were imaged with STEDYCON confocal microscope. Number of nodes, or branching points, calculation was performed using StructuralGT open software. Void space, defined as (void area)/(total area), was calculated using FIJI ImageJ.

Fibrin immunostaining

Collagen-fibrin composite hydrogel was prepared as above. The samples were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 20 min and washed with PBS three times. Samples were blocked with 5 % BSA and 0.3 % Triton-X100 in PBS at room temperature for 60 min. Mouse monoclonal anti-fibrin antibody (clone 59D8, Millipore Sigma, MA) was diluted in 1:300 in 1 % BSA and 0.1 % Triton-X100 in PBS for incubation with samples overnight at 4 °C. Negative control samples were incubated with 1 % BSA and 0.1 % Triton-X100 in PBS without addition of the primary antibody. After three washes with 0.1 % Triton-X100 in PBS, secondary antibody (Alexa Fluor 647, ThermoFisher, MA) was applied for 60 min at room temperature. Samples were washed with PBS three times and imaged with STEDYCON confocal microscope.

Statistical analysis

To compare results, groups were assessed using the student t-test, one-way ANOVA with post-hoc Tukey test, Mann-Whitney U test, or Kruskal-Wallis ANOVA, each comprising a minimum of three replicates (N≥3). Specific tests run are specified in the figure captions. Statistical significance was indicated by p-values, where significance was determined as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.005, and ****p < 0.001. The error bars indicate standard error unless otherwise mentioned in the figure legends.

Results

Dynamic collagen-fibrin matrix structure in vivo PDAC tissues

Most cancer tissues, including PDAC, exhibit a fibrotic ECM and tumor-induced vascular disruption leading to the accumulation of coagulation factors within the TME. This results in fibrin(ogen) deposition and contributes to the development of the characteristic desmoplastic stroma, which is rich in collagen and collagen-producing CAFs. As shown in Fig. 1, the human PDAC tumor demonstrates prominent expression of both collagen and fibrin(ogen) which is recapitulated in the murine model. Expression of fibrin(ogen) is especially significant compared with normal pancreata tissue (Supplementary Figure 4). A heterogeneous distribution of fibrin(ogen) and collagen is observed over several tissues (Supplementary Figure 5). Notably, in both tumor tissues, the abundance of fibrin(ogen) is comparable to that of collagen, the most prevalent ECM component in the TME [38]. The spatial distribution of fibrin(ogen) distinctly differs between these two tumor types - with more uniform distribution throughout the tissue in the KPC orthotopic murine tumor, but strong and localized expression of fibrin (ogen) around the epithelial ductal structures (noted with “*”) in the human PDAC tissue. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating both fibrin and collagen into engineered tumor models to better recapitulate the in vivo TME and highlight the need for further investigation into how each protein influences matrix structure and function.

Polymer microstructure and kinetic analysis of collagen-fibrin polymerization

To investigate the impact of the presence fibrin(ogen) on the collagen fiber structure we prepared three hydrogels – collagen only, collagen-fibrinogen, and collagen-fibrin – each with a total protein concentration of 6 mg/mL and a 1:1 ratio in the composite cases. SEM analysis of these hydrogels revealed visual differences among the three hydrogels (Fig. 2A). Compared to the collagen-only hydrogel, the collagen-fibrin hydrogel showed a higher frequency of discernible fine fibers, forming a “spider-web”-like structure likely resulting from fibrin formation. In fibrin(ogen) mixed polymers, there are noticeable depositions of small particles on the fibers. This is more noticeable for collagen-fibrin(ogen) gel, containing fibrinogen prior to thrombin treatment, likely representing unpolymerized fibrinogen aggregates [39]. This observation was consistent across SEM images of multiple samples (Supplementary Figure 6).

To investigate the polymerization kinetics of collagen, fibrin, and their composite, we monitored turbidity changes at 405 nm over time. Although the polymerization kinetics of collagen and fibrin depends on various factors including temperature, pH, and thrombin concentration [40], we focused on the typical protocol adapted in tissue engineering literatures to articulate the effects of mixing these two proteins [41-45]. At the given protein concentration of 6 mg/mL and polymerization conditions, fibrin exhibited the fastest polymerization with lag time around ~0.8 min (Fig. 2B). Collagen exhibited a significantly longer lag time (~25 min), indicating delayed onset of polymerization. Both fibrin and collagen-fibrin showed minimal lag times near zero. Interestingly, the combination of collagen and fibrin more closely matched the lag time of fibrin alone.

The maximum rate of turbidity increase (Vmax) was significantly higher for fibrin (0.200 ± 0.004 Abs/min) compared to collagen-fibrin (0.09 ± 0.024 Abs/min) and collagen alone (0.020 ± 0.003 Abs/min) (Fig. 2C). These findings suggest that the presence of collagen slows the polymerization rate of fibrin when these proteins are co-polymerized.

The peak clot absorbance for fibrin was the fastest, as predicted, with its absorbance value reaching a plateau optical density (OD) of ~1.4 within 10 min (Fig. 2D). In contrast, collagen polymerized more slowly, with a gradual increase in absorbance reaching a lower plateau (~0.6 OD) over the course of 80 min. The collagen-fibrin composite displayed intermediate behavior, with a rapid initial rise followed by a plateau at a lower OD (~0.7) than fibrin alone, indicating altered fiber network formation.

Fibrin matrix alters biomechanical properties of collagen-fibrin hydrogels

To assess the impact of fibrin(ogen) on the transport properties of the hydrogel, we measured the diffusivity of FITC-labeled dextran molecules using integrative optical imaging (IOI) method. This IOI assesses the tortuosity and diffusivity of small molecules in the extracellular space by monitoring the spatiotemporal fluorescence intensity of fluorescently- labeled dextran [27,28,46]. Five different tissue constructs were formulated: high collagen (6 mg/mL), low collagen (3 mg/mL), collagen-fibrinogen, collagen-fibrin, and collagen-fibrin with degradation of fibrin by plasmin. FITC-labeled dextrans with 4 kDa (hydrodynamic radius = 1.3 nm) and 40 kDa (5.1 nm) in size were chosen as fluorescent probes to represent commonly administered pancreatic cancer drugs gemcitabine (~1 nm) and nab-paclitaxel (~10 nm in plasma [47]).

Time-lapsed fluorescent images depicting the diffusion of 4 kDa dextran across various tissue constructs are presented in Fig. 3A. At the initial point (20s), the radial diffusion of dextran within collagen-fibrin and collagen-fibrinogen matrices was significantly diminished compared to the collagen matrix alone. This disparity is more pronounced by the conclusion of the experiment (120s). The calculated diffusivity values convey observations from the images. The final measured diffusivity value for 4 kDa dextran in 3 mg/ml collagen was 2.8 × 10−10 ± 1.6 × 10−11 m2/s, which agrees with the reported diffusivity value (2.34 × 10−10 m2/s in 4 mg/ml collagen) determined using the FRAP method [48,49]. While there was no significant difference between different collagen concentrations, the deposition of fibrinogen and fibrin demonstrated a reduction in diffusivities compared to the collagen matrix alone, by as much as 1.5-fold (P < 0.01). Notably, the reduction in diffusivity was partially restored by ~48 % upon fibrin degradation with plasmin.

Moreover, the decline in diffusivity was more pronounced when using 40 kDa dextran compared to 4 kDa dextran (Fig. 3B). Specifically, collagen concentration significantly contributed to this reduction in diffusivity by an order of magnitude, as evidenced by the values observed at 3 mg/mL (3.0 × 10−10 ± 3.2 × 10−11 m2/s) and 6 mg/mL (8.2 × 10−11 ± 4.9 × 10−11 m2/s). However, the saturation of matrix pores with 40 kDa dextran, resulting from its larger particle size, likely accounts for the lack of meaningful difference between diffusion in fibrinogen and fibrin, contrary to observations with smaller dextran sizes. It is worth noting that for larger nanoparticle-conjugated drugs, drug delivery is more influenced by advection, suggesting that future permeability measurements may offer a more comprehensive analysis of transport properties for larger particles. Conversely, the 40 kDa dextran particle exhibited a greater restoration (~87 %) of diffusivity following plasmin treatment.

To further characterize the impact of fibrin(ogen) on the mechanical properties of the tissue constructs, we measured Young’s modulus using the flat punch indentation method (Fig. 3C). This method has been extensively characterized previously for assessing the contact adhesion and elastic modulus of various materials, including tumor spheroids [16, 33,34]. The modulus values obtained revealed that different concentrations of collagen control samples do not result in significant differences in stiffness where the values for 3 mg/mL and 6 mg/mL collagen were 2.7 ± 0.2 kPa and 2.8 ± 0.2 kPa respectively. Intriguingly, collagen-fibrinogen and collagen-fibrin samples, despite having the same final protein concentrations of 6 mg/mL, exhibited significantly lower modulus values (1.5 ± 0.2 kPa and 1.9 ± 0.1 kPa respectively). Upon plasmin treatment, the modulus value decreased further, 1.4 ± 0.1 kPa, suggesting that the degradation of fibrin leads to a reduction in stiffness.

Fibrin concentration modulates collagen-fibrin microstructurefunctionality

To investigate the impact of protein ratio, SEM imaging was used to examine tissue microstructure which provided intricate details of the fiber structure within each matrix composition (Fig. 4A). The specific collagen-fibrin hydrogel mixtures investigated are reported in Table 1 of the Methods section. Consistent with previous findings, the collagenfibrin matrix exhibited a greater abundance of fine fibers compared to collagen alone likely coming from fibrin fibers [50]. This is further corroborated by quantitative measurements of fiber diameters, which revealed a decrease in average fiber diameter with fibrin deposition (Fig. 4B). Particularly noteworthy is the significant reduction in both the average and the distribution of measured fiber diameters in matrices with the highest fibrin density 0.5:1 (average diameter = 18.3 ± 1.0 nm), compared to those with higher collagen densities 1:0 (35.0 ± 3.9 nm), 1:1 (25.6 ± 2.4 nm), and 1:0.5 (28.1 ± 1.0 nm). The proximity of the average diameters observed in the 1:1 and 1:0.5 cases suggests that collagen fibers may still predominantly contribute to the average fiber characteristics within this range. However, all mixtures exhibited significantly reduced fiber diameters compared to collagen alone. Interestingly, fibrin-only samples (0:1) demonstrated higher fiber diameters compared with collagen-fibrin samples, with average diameter = 47.2 nm ± 1.3 nm (Supplementary Figure 7). In addition to changes in average fiber diameter, substantial variability in diameter distribution was observed, with the 1:1 collagen–fibrin condition displaying the broadest range of fiber sizes. This finding reflects a heterogeneous architecture of interconnected collagen–fibrin fibers throughout the hydrogel. Moreover, low-magnification SEM images demonstrate a generally uniform distribution of the heterogeneous fiber mixtures (Supplementary Figure 8).

Following plasmin-induced degradation of fibrin, while the 0.5:1 + plasmin (21.6 ± 1.8 nm) case showed a noticeable increase in diameter compared to the non-plasmin-treated case of the same ratio, the 1:1 + plasmin (22.2 ± 1.3 nm) case showed no difference compared to its nonplasmin treated case (Fig. 4C). This observation suggests that collagen is the major contributor to fiber diameter at this ratio.

The diffusivity of collagen-fibrin constructs was obtained through IOI analysis (Fig. 4D). Collagen-fibrin gels exhibited an exponential decay pattern of the diffusivity values as the fibrin concentration increases in the mixture, with 0.5:1 ratio demonstrating the lowest value (1.9 × 10−10 ± 4.1 × 10−11 m2/s). Upon fibrin degradation with plasmin, hydrogels with higher fibrin concentration experienced the greatest increase in diffusivity (Fig. 4E). None of the plasmin-treated gels reached the diffusivity of collagen alone at 6 mg/mL, indicating that even degraded fibrin matrices could contribute to the lower transport properties. Additionally, fine fibers are still present in the SEM images of plasmin treated samples showing that degradation does not restore the hydrogel to its collagen only microstructure.

We then explored the effect of fibrin concentration on polymer Young’s modulus (Fig. 4F). We hypothesized the fine fibers present in the hydrogel would augment material integrity, consequently elevating their resistance to deformation. Based on the findings this premise may only be partially valid, hinting at an optimal collagen-fibrin ratio where fibrin fibers afford maximal support to collagen fibers. This emerged at a collagen-fibrin ratio of 1:0.5. As collagen concentration decreased and fibrin concentration increased beyond this the Young’s modulus value declined. This indicated that collagen concentration may play a predominant role in maintaining hydrogel integrity. Moreover, except for the optimal collagen-fibrin ratio of 1:0.5, all collagen-fibrin hybrid gels exhibited lowered Young’s modulus values compared to that of collagen control. Furthermore, the decrease in modulus values with increasing fibrin content was consistent with previously reported study, although hydrogels in the reported study were prepared in the presence of rat aortic smooth muscle cells leading to higher measured values [43].

CAFs are highly prevalent and important cells within the tumorstroma that actively remodel the TME and interact with cancer cells. This is done through both ECM deposition and contraction which creates a stiff and ECM rich environment. These changes have implications for the ability of tumor cells to proliferate and migrate within the ECM [51-53]. To evaluate how matrix composition influences CAF-mediated matrix remodeling, we analyzed hydrogel contraction by CAF19 cells for each tissue construct. To analyze cell contraction, cell-laden hydrogels were time-lapse imaged for several days from which diameter measurements were extracted. The contraction index was defined as the change in diameter from the initial cell-laden hydrogel diameter (i.e., greater contraction index values have a smaller decrease in diameter).

Images of the CAF19-laden hydrogels demonstrate reductions in the hydrogel diameter over 3 days due to contraction induced by CAF19 (Fig. 4G). The corresponding contraction indices for each hydrogel formulation are presented in Fig. 4H. Hydrogel contractility on day 3 correlated with Young’s modulus results. The hydrogel formulation with the highest stiffness (1:0.5), at 3.5 ± 0.07 kPa, also contracted the least, with contraction index of 0.80 ± 0.04 (Fig. 4I). Moreover, a 1:1 hydrogel ratio, with the lowest stiffness, at 1.86 ± 0.06 kPa, also contracted the most, with a contraction index of 0.52 ± 0.09. SEM images of the highest and lowest contraction indices also demonstrate that CAFs cultured in 1:1 hydrogel ratio (lowest stiffness, highest CAF-induced contraction) generally demonstrated more elongated and bigger morphology (Supplementary Figure 9). However, the ability of CAFs to contract matrix of different stiffnesses via focal adhesions warrant further investigation. These findings suggest that hydrogel contractility by cells is closely related to the stiffness and the integrity of the ECM structure.

Investigation of the effect of polymerization sequence on collagenfibrin microstructure

Having shown that fibrin content influences the hydrogels structurefunctionality relationship (Fig. 4), we next investigated the effect of polymerization sequence. The order in which collagen and fibrin polymerize was examined, given its potential to alter ECM organization during progressive TME development. To reflect on a wounded TME leading to fibrin formation in spatial and temporal variations, we explored two methods of hydrogel formation: simultaneous and sequential polymerization. We postulated that both simultaneous and sequential polymerization events occur concurrently and independently in both mice and human tumors, resulting in distinct expression patterns of collagen and fibrin. The polymerization sequence was manipulated by adjusting the point of thrombin introduction so that collagen and fibrin were either polymerized simultaneously or, so collagen was polymerized and then fibrin. The case where collagen was polymerized after fibrin was not pursued due to technical difficulties of delaying collagen polymerization in a composite hydrogel. For a more comprehensive understanding of the hydrogel preparation, a schematic of the process is presented in Fig. 5A. As depicted, there are noticeable differences in the size of the monomers of each protein which may also play a role in their polymerization differences [54,55].

Although the collagen-fibrin(ogen) content remained constant across all cases, the polymerization sequence significantly influenced the fiber structure, as depicted in Fig. 5B. While both scenarios demonstrated an increase in thin fibers compared to the collagen control, the sequential polymerization case exhibited increased density of fine fibers, consequently reducing the visibility of pore structures. The increased fiber density was also depicted in measured number of nodes, or branching points, in the sequential polymerization case (Fig. 5C).

The violin plots presented in Fig. 5D provide a more comprehensive understanding of the results. In the case of simultaneous polymerization, there was a wider spread of fiber diameter with peaks clustering near a diameter of 25 nm (average diameter = 25.6 ± 2.36 nm), whereas sequential polymerization exhibited a narrower spread of diameter with peaks closer to a diameter of 17 nm (average diameter = 16.9 ± 2.39 nm). Notably, analysis of the fiber diameter distribution indicates that the reduced mean diameter observed with sequential polymerization is driven by a decrease in the prevalence of large-diameter fibers, rather than an increase in smaller-diameter fibers. Furthermore, plasmin- treated hydrogel results suggest that changes in diameter may not be attributed to alterations in collagen and fibrin diameters themselves compared to their respective control hydrogels, as the diameter of plasmin-treated gels demonstrated almost identical results for both scenarios (Fig. 5E). This leads to the conclusion that the collagen fibers themselves do not undergo significant changes depending on the polymerization sequence; rather, it is the density of fibrin fibers that plays a more significant role in determining the overall characteristics. These results were consistent with transport and mechanical characteristics of the hydrogels. As the overall fiber diameters decreased, the mean value for diffusivity of sequentially polymerized hydrogels is reduced but not statistically significant (p = 0.1596), presumably due to the heightened presence of fibers impeding particle transport (Fig. 5F). Similarly, the augmented fiber network contributed to increased modulus properties (p = 0.0917) (Fig. 5G).

Images of the CAF19-laden hydrogels demonstrate differences in the ability of CAFs to contract the surrounding matrix depending on polymerization sequence (Fig. 5H). Differences in contraction by CAF19 cells was observed over 72 h (Fig. 5I). On day 3 the co-polymerized hydrogels were significantly more contracted than the sequentially polymerized hydrogels (Fig. 5J). Consistent with the previous results, contraction correlated with modulus values. Although the modulus values between the two sequences was not statistically significant, the higher average modulus value in sequential polymerization case was associated with reduced CAF-mediated contraction.

Confocal analysis of composite gel with labeled collagen and fibrin

To resolve individual collagen and fibrin fibers, we also analyzed composite hydrogels of fluorescently-labeled collagen and fibrin(ogen) via confocal microscopy (Fig. 6). Compared to collagen-only hydrogels, which exhibited more linear and aligned fiber patterns, fibrin fibers in single-component hydrogels displayed increased curvature and a more reticulated, ‘spider web-like’ morphology, like what was observed from SEM images (Fig. 6A).

The fibers of the composite hydrogels from the confocal demonstrated that the prepared labeled hydrogels were producing an intricate interconnection of fibers (Supporting Video). Moreover, the images reveal distinct fiber architectures depending on the polymerization sequence of collagen-fibrin composite hydrogels (Fig. 6B). In the copolymerization condition (left column), collagen fibers (red) exhibited a morphology like that of the collagen-only control, while fibrin fibers (green) appeared more confined, forming in limited spaces between the collagen networks. This suggests that simultaneous polymerization may restrict the spatial distribution and network development of each component. In contrast, the sequential polymerization condition (right column), where collagen was polymerized prior to fibrin addition, showed a markedly different structure: collagen fibers appeared thicker, more elongated, and rigid, forming a robust scaffold. Fibrin fibers in this case closely resembled the structure seen in the fibrin-only gel, but with slightly more fragmented morphology. These differences highlight the critical influence of polymerization sequence on the resulting fiber microarchitecture in composite matrices.

The confocal images closely mirrored the microstructural features observed in SEM, reinforcing the distinction between collagen and fibrin fiber organization within the composite hydrogels (Supplementary Figure 10). In both imaging modalities, a network of thin fibers can be seen interwoven among thick, prominent fibers. The fluorescent labeling in the confocal images enabled clear identification of these components, providing further evidence that the fine, web-like structures correspond to fibrin, while the thick, more linear fibers are collagen. This correlation supports the interpretation that the distinct morphologies observed in SEM arise from the inherent structural differences between the two proteins.

Further, the confocal images enabled in-depth quantitative analysis of individual fibers in the composite hydrogels. Compared with single hydrogels, the number of nodes of composite polymers were significantly higher for both polymerization sequences (Fig. 6C). When comparing individual polymers in isolation, there was an increasing trend in the number of nodes of fibrin in sequential polymerization when compared with simultaneous polymerization, although not statistically significant. Void space, defined as the fraction of the total area not occupied by polymers, demonstrated an inverse relationship with the number of nodes of polymers. Compared with single hydrogels, the void spaces of composite hydrogels significantly decreased (Fig. 6D). This is consistent with previously measured diffusivity values, where collagen- fibrin(ogen) hydrogels demonstrated decreased diffusivity compared with collagen. Moreover, polymerization sequence did not result in statistically significant differences in the void space as was observed with the diffusivity results. Collectively, these findings indicate that both collagen and fibrin polymerization dynamics jointly regulate the microstructural organization and, consequently, the biomechanical properties of composite hydrogels.

To verify that the green signals observed in the composite gels represent polymerized fibrin rather than aggregates of unpolymerized fibrinogen, immunostaining with an anti-fibrin antibody was performed (Supplementary Figure 11A). Strong overlap between the anti-fibrin signal (magenta) and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated fibrin (green), with minimal overlap with collagen (red), confirms that the interwoven green structures were indeed polymerized fibrin fibers. Negative control samples stained without the anti-fibrin primary antibody showed no detectable signal, ruling out non-specific or off-target staining (Supplementary Figure 11B). Additionally, confocal imaging of collagen-fibrinogen gels revealed no detectable fibrinogen signal, further supporting that the fluorescent signal only emerges upon complete fibrin polymerization (Supplementary Figure 12).

Discussion

In this study, we examined how extracellular matrix composition and polymerization sequence regulate the structural and functional properties of collagen–fibrin(ogen) matrices, motivated by the heterogeneous distribution of collagen and fibrin observed in both murine and human PDAC tumors. We found that collagen and fibrin polymerize into interconnected networks across a range of conditions, with distinct polymerization parameters giving rise to pronounced differences in matrix architecture and functionality. These findings demonstrate that variations in ECM composition and structure can substantially influence tumor progression and drug transport.

Matrix transport properties were primarily assessed through the measurement of diffusivity, given that diffusion is anticipated to be the predominant mode of molecule transport due to elevated interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) and a dense TME [7,56]. Our diffusivity analysis revealed a decrease in diffusivity for all collagen-fibrin(ogen) hydrogels compared to the collagen control with a partial recovery upon plasmin treatment. This reduction in diffusivity could be attributed to the observed decrease in average fiber diameter in SEM images, likely stemming from the presence of thin fibrin fibers compared to collagen fibers. These results agree with collagen-fibrin composite and individual hydrogels reported in literature [57]. Interestingly, collagen-fibrinogen gel, without thrombin, also demonstrated significant reduction in diffusivity compared with collagen. This suggests that fibrinogen may also interact with existing collagen polymers, as seen from small particle depositions in SEM images of collagen-fibrin(ogen) gels, affecting their transport properties. Moreover, the diffusivities of the plasmin-treated samples never reached the diffusivity of collagen control hydrogels, suggesting the FDPs may also hinder molecule transport. The effect of FDPs requires in depth investigation as they crucially impact tumor progression, metastasis, and other inflammatory responses [58].

Young’s modulus was determined as a representative measure of the collagen-fibrin hydrogels material mechanical property. Outside of the peak Young’s modulus value at a collagen:fibrin ratio 1:0.5 all other composite hydrogels exhibited reduced Young’s modulus values compared to the collagen controls. Particularly noteworthy are the collagen-fibrinogen samples, which, despite containing 3 mg/mL collagen, displayed much lower modulus values than the 3 mg/mL collagen control gel. These findings are highly intriguing, as both hydrogels should theoretically contain similar or even higher overall protein concentrations than the collagen control gel yet exhibit reduced modulus results. This is thought to attribute to the water-absorbing capacity of collagen and fibrin hydrogels. The higher water-absorbing capability of collagen hydrogels and their ability to maintain their structure could lead to increased modulus values. While direct comparison of the water-absorbing capacities of collagen and fibrin hydrogels is lacking, a study suggests that the ability of fibrin gel to absorb water is relatively low at a thrombin concentration of 0.5 U/mL [45], which is comparable to the thrombin concentrations used in this study (0.2 – 1 U/mL). On the other hand, collagen fibers contain a fibrous, triple-helical structure with numerous hydration sites along its length. These hydration sites allow collagen to absorb water readily, contributing to its flexibility, elasticity, and mechanical properties [59]. The ability of collagen hydrogels to absorb water may be impacted when fibrin(ogen) is present and warrants further investigation.

As primary drivers of desmoplasia and stromal remodeling, CAFs were investigated to determine how matrix microstructure and Young’s modulus influence their contractile behavior. Across various collagen- fibrin(ogen) compositions, the contraction index positively correlated with Young’s modulus, highlighting the regulatory role of fibrin’s mechanical properties on cell-matrix interactions. Notably, the stiffest hydrogels exhibited the highest contraction index (i.e., minimal CAF- mediated contraction), supporting previous studies indicating reduced CAF contractility in stiff matrices [60,61]. Fibroblasts may also sense matrix stiffness, which promotes activation, increased αSMA expression, and ECM production, creating a feed-forward mechanism that again increases matrix stiffening and reduces contraction [62-64]. Moreover, both cancer cells and activated CAFs can further reorganize the stroma by secreting matrix-degrading enzymes like matrix metalloproteinase and upregulating tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases, driving fibro- genesis and tumor progression [62,65]. Importantly, CAF-collagen-fibrin(ogen) interactions presented in our research are limited to short-term culture, and the long-term impacts of fibrin deposition and altered biomechanics of remodeling tumor stroma by CAFs require further exploration.

Moreover, cancer and stromal cells directly engage with collagen, fibrin, and collagen–fibrin(ogen) matrices through distinct integrin subunits [42,66,67]. Fibrin can additionally act as a bridging molecule that facilitates interactions between collagen and fibroblasts via αVβ3 integrin [22]. Collectively, these cell–matrix interactions drive dynamic reorganization of the ECM, altering key biomechanical properties such as polymer density and matrix stiffness. Such changes activate downstream mechanotransduction pathways that modulate cellular invasion and migration [68-70]. Likewise, the capacity of cancer cells and fibroblasts to remodel ECM stiffness can be harnessed to engineer in vitro cancer models that more faithfully recapitulate in vivo microenvironments [67,71].

Diffusivity within the extracellular matrix is tightly governed by matrix porosity and microstructural organization, which together define the degree of physical confinement experienced by cells and soluble factors. Void area calculated from confocal images indeed generally demonstrated correlation with diffusivity values measured where the void areas of the composite hydrogels were significantly lower than those of the single collagen and fibrin hydrogels. In the context of cancer, this microstructural confinement and matrix plasticity have been shown to have profound functional consequences, as tumor cells dynamically sense and respond to restricted spatial and transport environments through altered mechanotransduction, metabolic reprogramming, and invasive behavior [72]. The ability for cells to remodel the surrounding matrix via secretion of various degradation enzymes and cell responses to the surrounding stiffness, or durotaxis, collectively contribute to promote or inhibit cell invasiveness and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [73-76]. Additionally, a dense, low-porosity tumor microenvironment promotes the formation of diffusion gradients that drive hypoxia, acidosis, and localized growth factor accumulation, all of which can further enhance EMT and metastatic potential [72,77,78]. Moreover, spatially heterogeneous diffusivity within a fibrotic tumor stroma can create permissive invasion paths while simultaneously shielding tumor cells from immune surveillance and drug penetration [79,80]. Thus, matrix confinements and transport properties are critical regulators of microstructural functionality that governs tumor aggressiveness, metastatic dissemination, immunosuppressive microenvironment, and limited drug delivery.

Beyond cancer, these results have implications for engineered tissue models of neuroinflammation and wound healing. In neuroinflammation, disruption of the blood-brain barrier allows large coagulation proteins to extravasate into the brain parenchyma, initiating a cascade of neuroinflammatory responses [81]. Dysregulated fibrin (ogen) deposition alters tissue transport and stiffness, potentially hindering drug delivery, promoting metabolic stress, and activating microglia [82]. These come to promote inflammation, cellular injury, and neurodegeneration. In wound healing, fibrin first forms a provisional matrix that plugs the wound and provides a scaffold for cell migration and tissue repair, before being replaced by a more permanent collagen-rich matrix [11]. Thus, the data presented here on the impact of sequence and polymerization conditions of collagen-fibrin matrices are highly relevant not only for modeling tumorigenesis but also for accurately capturing dynamic ECM remodeling in these other pathophysiological contexts.

Conclusion

Our study illustrates the intricate interplay between collagen and fibrin proteins, resulting in significant differences in their transport and mechanical properties. Tumor and stromal cells rely heavily on their external environment, where fiber alignment, stiffness, adhesion to fiber structures, and nutrient availability regulate growth and cell behavior. Previous engineered tumor models using collagen or fibrin alone have demonstrated the utility of these matrices and the power of engineered tumor models. For example, Venis et al. utilized a collagen matrix and viscous fingering to recapitulate the pancreatic acinus in which they reprogrammed pancreatic cancer cells through induction of PTF1a to revert to a healthy phenotype [83]. Both proteins have also been incorporated into bioprinting approaches to tumor modeling, enabling the generation of highly dense tumoroids that better replicate in vivo conditions by enhancing cell-cell interactions and modulating drug penetration [16,19]. By demonstrating that collagen-fibrin matrix composition and polymerization dynamics shape hydrogel structure and mechanical properties, our findings highlight the importance of integrating both proteins to enhance physiological relevance of engineered tumor models. Importantly, we show that fibrin degradation partially restores diffusivity of drug-sized molecules, suggesting that targeting fibrin or its degradation products may represent a therapeutic strategy to improve drug penetration in the TME [82]. Finally, we recognize that ECM is not merely a passive network providing structural support and future studies investigating the progressive communication between cellular components and the developing ECM are warranted.
