Does CD30 loss preclude the diagnosis of anaplastic lymphoma kinase-positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma? Insights from a rare case
Authors: Wenjie Wang, Xiaolong Sui, Guohua Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
CD30 positivity serves as a critical diagnostic hallmark for anaplastic lymphoma kinase positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALK+ ALCL); however, rare cases with partial or complete loss of CD30 expression present diagnostic challenges. We report a unique case with two morphologically and immunophenotypically distinct subpopulations within the same tumor: A typical CD30-positive component and a CD30-negative counterpart. Comparative genetic analysis revealed overlapping but divergent molecular features, including nucleophosmin 1-ALK (NPM1-ALK) fusion, notch receptor 1 mutations, and differential gene amplifications involving MYC proto-oncogene and BCL2-like 1. These findings offer insights into the regulatory mechanisms of CD30 and highlight that loss of CD30 expression alone should not preclude the diagnosis of ALK-positive ALCL, underscoring the importance of integrated histopathological and molecular evaluation in diagnostically challenging cases. Meanwhile, it provides a basis for optimizing the diagnostic process of clinically suspected cases.

INTRODUCTION

Anaplastic lymphoma kinase-positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma (ALK+ ALCL) is a distinct subtype of mature T-cell lymphoma, typically characterized by diffuse and strong CD30 expression and recurrent ALK gene rearrangements, most commonly nucleophosmin 1 ALK (NPM1-ALK) fusion.[1] CD30 is not only a key diagnostic hallmark but also an important therapeutic target, particularly with the advent of anti-CD30-directed therapies such as brentuximab vedotin.

Although CD30 expression is considered a defining feature of ALCL, rare cases with partial or complete loss of CD30 expression have been reported and may pose significant diagnostic challenges, especially in small biopsy specimens. Such cases raise critical questions regarding the diagnostic utility of CD30 negativity and the underlying mechanisms driving this phenotypic divergence. We report a rare case of ALK+ ALCL exhibiting two distinct tumor cell populations within the same lesion: One showing classic CD30-positive morphology and immunophenotype, and the other demonstrating complete loss of CD30 expression. A comparative analysis of histological, immunophenotypic, and molecular features between these two components provides insight into the heterogeneity and pathogenesis of ALCL and emphasizes the need for comprehensive diagnostic evaluation in atypical presentations.

CASE REPORT

A 45-year-old male presented with a 2-month history of a rapidly progressive mass on the upper back between the scapulae, accompanied by local pain and erythema. Physical examination revealed a 10 × 15 cm lesion that was slightly elevated above the skin surface, erythematous, tender, soft to moderately firm on palpation, and poorly mobile. Complete blood count: White blood cell count: 12.03 × 109/L, neutrophils: 8.17 × 109/L, platelets: 356 × 109/L, hemoglobin: 121 g/L. Positron emission tomography/computed tomography revealed a large hypermetabolic soft tissue mass in the upper back, accompanied by multiple hypermetabolic lymph nodes in the cervical and thoracic regions [Figure 1a-d]. The maximum standardized uptake value of the involved lesions reached ~36.5, indicating high metabolic activity. A bone marrow biopsy and peripheral blood smear were performed, which showed no evidence of tumor infiltration. The patient was referred to another hospital for further treatment after diagnosis. He received six cycles of brentuximab vedotin (anti-CD30 monoclonal antibody) combined with cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and prednisone chemotherapy regimen as first-line treatment. Detailed information regarding these medications is provided in the Supplementary Material. At the 15-month follow-up after initial diagnosis, the patient remained alive.

Pathological findings

The mass exhibited two distinct cellular morphologies microscopically, designated PART1 and PART2, respectively [Figure 2a]. PART1 consisted of diffusely proliferating small to medium-sized tumor lymphocytes with large, darkly stained, round nuclei and basophilic cytoplasm [Figure 2b]. In contrast to the classic morphology of ALK+ ALCL observed in PART1, PART2 displayed uniformly cytoplasmic, translucent tumor cells with round, darkly stained nuclei, and abundant cytoplasm [Figure 2c].

Molecular analysis

Microscopically, both CD30 and ALK manifest distinctive expressions across the subpopulations of this tumor, exhibiting well-defined demarcations. To investigate this phenomenon, we conducted a genetic sequencing analysis and a comparative study of these two subpopulations and found the following differences: the NPM1-ALK gene fusion, with an allele frequency (AF) of 16.31% in PART1 and 11.32% in PART2, and the notch receptor 1 (NOTCH1) single-nucleotide variant (SNV) variant, with a content of c.5422 G>A, showed 49.85% AF in PART1 and 46.93% in PART2; in addition, PART2 exhibited a deletion mutation at position c.179_182 of PR/SET domain 1 (PRDM1), with an AF of 2.08%, and the excision repair cross-complementation group 1 (ERCC1) SNV variant, which reads c.329A>T, with an AF of 2.08%, which were not present in PART1. Furthermore, PART1, in comparison to PART2, exhibited distinct amplifications of the MYC and BCL2L1 genes, with a multiplicity of 1.53 and 3.5 [Table 1].

DISCUSSION

The NPM1-ALK gene fusion, identified in both PART1 and PART2, plays a crucial role in the pathogenesis of ALK+ ALCL, acting as a constitutively active tyrosine kinase that promotes tumorigenesis through multiple downstream pathways, including Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription, phosphoinositide 3-kinase-AKT serine/threonine kinase, and mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling.[1] The consistent presence of this fusion in both subpopulations confirms their common clonal origin, supporting the notion that the CD30-negative PART2 is a divergent subclone derived from the CD30-positive parental clone. Similarly, NOTCH1 mutations, observed in both components, have been reported in ALCL and are associated with tumor cell proliferation, survival, and differentiation by regulating downstream targets involved in T-cell development.[2] The high AF of NOTCH1 mutation in both subpopulations suggests that this alteration occurred early in tumorigenesis and was retained during clonal divergence.

The restriction of ALK immunoreactivity to the nucleus in the small neoplastic cells observed in PART2, although unusual, has been reported in small cell-predominant ALK-positive ALCL. Falini et al.[3] suggested that although the NPM1-ALK fusion protein can accumulate within the nucleus, its oncogenic signaling activity is believed to occur predominantly in the cytoplasm. In small neoplastic cells, the cytoplasmic volume is limited, and cytoplasmic NPM1-ALK expression may be intrinsically low. Such low-level cytoplasmic expression may be sufficient at the molecular level to activate oncogenic signaling pathways yet remain below the detection threshold of routine immunohistochemistry, particularly after formalin fixation and paraffin embedding. Consequently, an apparently nuclear-restricted staining pattern does not necessarily indicate true absence of cytoplasmic ALK expression.

In contrast, a PRDM1 deletion and an ERCC1 SNV were exclusively detected in PART2 at low allele frequencies (both 2.08%). While the significance of these subclonal mutations remains uncertain, PRDM1 deletions have previously been reported to occur more frequently in ALK-negative large cell lymphoma.[4] The ERCC1 gene encodes a subunit of ERCC1-xeroderma pigmentosum group F nuclease, an enzyme that plays an important role in the repair of DNA damage and the maintenance of genome stability.[5] However, there are currently no reports of mutations of this gene in ALCL. Given their low abundance, these alterations are most consistent with subclonal events and may also reflect background sequencing noise. At present, there is insufficient evidence to support a direct causal role of these low-frequency alterations in driving the CD30-negative phenotype, and they are more likely to represent incidental passenger events. Nevertheless, their presence underscores the genetic complexity and intratumoral heterogeneity of this case; their potential biological relevance warrants further investigation.

The amplification of MYC and BCL2L1 observed in PART1, but absent in PART2, may correlate with the higher proliferative index in this region. MYC is a well-known protooncogene that acts as a non-linear amplifier in lymphomas by enhancing the expression of other active genes, thereby promoting aberrant cell proliferation.[6] Previous studies have suggested that the NPM1-ALK fusion gene protects MYC from degradation in ALCL.[7] In our case, MYC protein expression was higher in PART1 compared to PART2, consistent with the observed genetic differences. Although the observed MYC amplification is modest, our immunohistochemical findings suggest differential protein expression, possibly driven by both genetic and post-transcriptional mechanisms. Likewise, BCL2L1 encodes an anti-apoptotic protein, BCLXL, and its amplification has been implicated in resistance to cell death in other lymphoma subtypes.[8] While MYC and BCL gene amplification are frequently observed in B-cell lymphomas and have been documented to have a minimal impact on prognosis. However, these amplifications have not been reported in ALCL yet. The potential for these two amplifications to synergize and affect the expression of other proteins in ALCL remains to be investigated through further research. In this context, co-amplification of MYC and BCL2L1 may exert a cooperative effect by coupling increased proliferative drive with suppression of MYC-induced apoptotic stress, thereby creating a biologically permissive environment for sustained tumor growth. Although this association remains correlative in the setting of a single case, this synergistic mechanism is biologically plausible and may help explain the higher proliferative activity observed in PART1. Further studies are required to clarify the functional significance of these alterations in ALCL.

Regarding the loss of CD30 expression, it has been demonstrated that CD30 expression is dependent on the activity of the NPM1-ALK fusion gene.[9] Additional regulatory factors, such as MUM1 (interferon regulatory factor 4), which participates in a positive feedback loop with CD30 through nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells signaling, may further modulate CD30 expression.[10] In this case, MUM1 expression was observed in PART1 but absent in PART2, further supporting the idea of differential transcriptional environments between the two regions. Although the frequency of NPM1-ALK rearrangement was slightly lower in PART2, and MYC amplification was absent, the exact mechanism underlying CD30 loss remains unclear. Given the involvement of multiple transcriptional, epigenetic, and microenvironmental factors in CD30 regulation, this discrepancy should be interpreted as one of several possible contributing factors that warrant further investigation.

From a clinical perspective, intratumoral heterogeneity in CD30 expression may have important therapeutic implications. Recent reports have described decreased CD30 expression and disease progression in ALCL patients treated with brentuximab vedotin, suggesting that CD30 loss may contribute to therapeutic resistance. Although the patient in this case has not shown evidence of disease relapse to date, the presence of a CD30-negative subpopulation highlights the importance of close clinical follow-up and suggests that monitoring CD30 expression during treatment may be warranted.

SUMMARY

We report a rare case of ALK+ ALCL with two distinct subpopulations differing in morphology, immunophenotype, and molecular features, particularly CD30 expression status. The comparative genetic analysis reveals the common clonal origin of the two subpopulations and identifies key molecular differences that may underlie their phenotypic divergence. Our findings confirm that CD30 negativity alone does not preclude the diagnosis of ALK+ ALCL, highlighting the need for integrated histopathological and molecular evaluation in diagnostically challenging cases. Furthermore, the intratumoral heterogeneity observed in this case has important implications for CD30-targeted therapy, emphasizing the necessity of close follow-up and potential CD30 expression monitoring. This case expands our understanding of the genetic complexity and phenotypic heterogeneity of ALCL and provides a basis for optimizing the diagnosis and treatment of similar cases.
