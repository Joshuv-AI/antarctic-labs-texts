The social dimensions of fibromyalgia scientific research: an international classification of functioning, disability, and health–based perspective
Authors: André Pontes-Silva
Published: 2026

The article “Long-term examination of pain and health-related outcomes in people with fibromyalgia before, during, and after COVID-19” provides valuable longitudinal insights into pain trajectories and psychosocial correlates in individuals with fibromyalgia. Its extended follow-up period and focus on symptom persistence beyond the acute phase of the pandemic represent important contributions to the field.3

While the authors did not aim to conduct a socially oriented study, their work provides an important opportunity to reflect on how the social dimension of pain is currently conceptualized and operationalized in fibromyalgia research. Although the study refers to “psychosocial factors” and includes variables labeled as social—such as social support and social activities—the analytical emphasis remains predominantly on psychological processes. This pattern, which is common in pain research, raises broader questions about how the social domain is theoretically framed and empirically examined.

In this study, social factors are primarily operationalized through individual-level behavioral indicators derived from the West Haven–Yale Multidimensional Pain Inventory. While such measures are valid and informative, they capture only a limited portion of what the World Health Organization conceptualizes as the social domain of health. In the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health (ICF), social participation and environmental factors are not peripheral correlates, but central components of health and functioning.2

This broader perspective is particularly relevant in fibromyalgia, a condition consistently shown to be shaped by social processes such as symptom invalidation, stigmatization, work-related exclusion, and socioeconomic vulnerability. Empirical evidence suggests that perceived invalidation by family members, health professionals, and employers is strongly associated with higher pain intensity, psychological distress, and disability, often independently of biomedical disease severity.1

Furthermore, although the sample consists exclusively of women, the study does not explicitly engage with gendered social roles, caregiving demands, or cumulative social burden—factors that are widely recognized as central to the lived experience of fibromyalgia. Highlighting these dimensions does not imply that they should have been included in this particular study; rather, it illustrates how social factors are often treated as secondary or adjunctive to psychological distress, rather than as independent social determinants of pain and disability within contemporary biopsychosocial and ICF-based frameworks.2

From this perspective, the study does not fall short of its aims, but rather exemplifies a broader tendency in the field: psychological variables are typically examined in depth, whereas the social domain is more narrowly operationalized. This distinction is not merely theoretical. How the social domain is conceptualized has direct implications for interpretation, external validity, and clinical translation. When social factors are reduced to individual behaviors or secondary predictors, their capacity to shape pain trajectories through processes such as social constraint, exclusion, and invalidation may remain underexplored.2,5

Future longitudinal studies of fibromyalgia may therefore benefit from a more comprehensive and theoretically explicit incorporation of the social domain, grounded in WHO and ICF frameworks.4 Incorporating structural variables (eg, employment status and income insecurity), relational dimensions (eg, invalidation and quality of social support), and participation restrictions could offer a more balanced understanding of how social contexts interact with psychological processes to influence long-term pain outcomes.3

These considerations are intended not as a critique of the authors' methodological choices, but as a contribution to the ongoing development of conceptually robust and clinically meaningful fibromyalgia research.
