Laparoscopic identification and management of a pediatric direct inguinal hernia in a high-risk infant: A case report☆
Authors: Szu-En Chin, Ju-Bei Yen, Shih-Hsien Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Direct inguinal hernia is rare in children and may be underrecognized. We report a high-risk infant with extreme prematurity and prolonged positive-pressure ventilation who developed recurrent inguinal hernias despite anatomically appropriate laparoscopic repairs. Serial laparoscopic reassessments revealed dynamic progression from bilateral indirect hernias to pantaloon hernias and ultimately a persistent direct defect. Definitive repair was deferred until three years of age when incarceration occurred. Laparoscopic reassessment confirmed a right indirect and left direct hernia. Percutaneous internal ring closure and open Lichtenstein tension-free mesh repair achieved durable repair without recurrence at 12-month follow-up.

Introduction

Inguinal hernia is common in children and is predominantly indirect in nature. In contrast, direct inguinal hernia is exceedingly rare and remains underrecognized in pediatric surgical practice.1 Consequently, recurrence following hernia repair in children is frequently attributed to technical failure rather than to a distinct anatomical pathology.

Although laparoscopy allows accurate anatomical classification of inguinal hernias, correct classification alone does not necessarily ensure durable repair in selected high-risk patients.2,3 We report a rare case of pediatric direct inguinal hernia that developed following initial laparoscopic repair of bilateral indirect hernias in an extremely premature infant born at 25 weeks and 5 days of gestation. This case illustrates that under sustained elevated intra-abdominal pressure, the anatomical pattern of inguinal hernia in infants may be dynamic rather than static, and that surgical timing and physiological conditions may be as critical as technique selection in achieving durable repair.

Case presentation

The patient was born at 25 weeks and 5 days of gestation with a very low birth weight of 913 g and required prolonged neonatal intensive care, including extended positive-pressure ventilatory support.

At 16 months of age, bilateral inguinal hernias were identified. Laparoscopic exploration demonstrated bilateral indirect inguinal hernias, and intracorporeal internal ring closure was performed.

Early recurrence occurred shortly after surgery. At 22 months of age, repeat laparoscopic exploration revealed bilateral pantaloon hernias, with concurrent direct and indirect defects on both sides. The indirect components were treated with repeat intracorporeal internal ring closure. The direct components were repaired using the technique described by Schier,2 consisting of intracorporeal suturing reinforced by coverage with the median umbilical ligament.

Despite these interventions, bilateral groin bulging recurred again within a short period (Fig. 1). Definitive treatment was reconsidered at 3 years of age, when the patient developed right-sided incarceration requiring urgent surgical intervention.

Operative findings and management

Given the history of multiple prior repairs and recurrent symptoms, a laparoscopic approach was selected to allow precise anatomical re-evaluation, particularly to determine the relationship between the defect and the inferior epigastric vessels and to minimize additional dissection in previously operated tissue planes.

Diagnostic laparoscopy demonstrated a markedly dilated internal inguinal ring on the right side, consistent with an indirect inguinal hernia (Fig. 2a), as well as previously placed suture material away from the hernia defect (Fig. 2b). Percutaneous internal ring closure was performed for the right-sided indirect hernia. Given the recurrent nature of the left-sided direct hernia and prior failure of tissue-based repairs, open Lichtenstein tension-free mesh repair was performed for the left side. The procedure was completed without complications.

Outcome and follow-up

The postoperative course was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 1. At 12 months of follow-up, there was no evidence of recurrence or postoperative complications.

Discussion

This case highlights that pediatric direct inguinal hernia in extremely premature infants may represent a dynamic process influenced by physiological conditions rather than a static anatomical defect.

First, sustained positive-pressure ventilation likely contributed to chronically elevated intra-abdominal pressure, a well-recognized risk factor for recurrence.4,5 In such circumstances, recurrence may reflect tissue fragility and posterior wall weakness rather than purely technical failure. Repeated repair during ongoing ventilatory dependence may therefore be inherently prone to failure. Our experience suggests that, when feasible, definitive repair may be better deferred until respiratory support can be discontinued or significantly reduced.

Second, although laparoscopy enabled accurate anatomical classification at each stage, precise identification of hernia type alone did not ensure durable repair in the presence of adverse physiological stressors. The laparoscopic approach at definitive surgery was chosen primarily for diagnostic clarification and surgical planning rather than as a causative factor in prior recurrence 6, 7, 8.

Finally, direct inguinal hernia reflects posterior wall weakness rather than a discrete defect. In the present case, repeated tissue-based repair proved insufficient. Mesh reinforcement using the Lichtenstein technique—well established in adult groin hernia guidelines9—ultimately provided durable repair. While mesh use in children must be considered cautiously, it may be justified in carefully selected patients with recurrent direct hernia after failure of conventional repair.

Conclusion

Pediatric direct inguinal hernia in high-risk premature infants may represent a dynamic condition influenced by sustained elevated intra-abdominal pressure. Recurrence may reflect tissue fragility and posterior wall weakness rather than solely surgical technique. Careful consideration of timing and physiological status is essential. In carefully selected cases of recurrent direct hernia following failed tissue-based repair, mesh reinforcement may represent a durable alternative.
