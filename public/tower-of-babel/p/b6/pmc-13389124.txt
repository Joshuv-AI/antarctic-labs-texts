Lithium in the management of suicide: a formidable force in the fight against a fatal foe
Authors: Gurubhaskar Shivakumar, Kinga Szymaniak, Erica Bell, Gin S Malhi
Published: 2026

It does not belong to my subject to treat of suicide in its legal relations, nor, consequently, of its criminality. I must limit myself to showing it to be one of the most important subjects of clinical medicine.

- Jean-Étienne-Dominique Esquirol, Mental Maladies: A Treatise on Insanity (1838, English translation 1845)

Introduction

The risk of dying early is a seemingly universal human concern, but for those living with mental illness, this is not merely a possibility, but a likelihood that looms on two fronts. The first front is the illness itself, whereupon patients with severe mood disorders have an approximately 20-fold higher risk of suicide than the general population. 1 The second front is iatrogenic, and includes the cardiometabolic impact of orexigenic antipsychotic medications. The principal aim of treatment is to counter the effects of disease and improve a person’s health and possibly extend their life; and in most instances, this is indeed what transpires. Directly modulating a complex life-taking behaviour such as suicide is perhaps lithium’s most enigmatic attribute. By appearing to protect individuals from themselves, lithium performs a role that exceeds the expectations of most medications – addressing a goal that is undeniably amongst the most desirable in clinical medicine.

Lithium’s prophylactic ability to reduce suicidal risk in those with mood disorders has been recognised via retrospective analysis for over five decades. 2 However, prospective trials investigating lithium’s anti-suicide effects face significant methodological hurdles; the low base rate of completed suicide – even in the sub-population of those with severe mood disorders – necessitates randomised trials with exceptionally large sample sizes being tracked over several years. But clearly, such research is difficult to conduct, and until such evidence materialises it is imperative that we critically analyse the data we have at present, imperfect though it may be.

For example, for centuries, naturally occurring lithium-enriched water sources around the world have been lauded for their health benefits by a variety of cultures. In modern times, the average rates of suicide, as well as other forms of self-harm, had been found to be consistently lower in areas of Texas where lithium levels in drinking water were naturally high. 3 Since its publication in 1990, this ecological evidence regarding suicide has been successfully reproduced around the globe, and has also been substantiated in clinical studies. A landmark meta-analysis in 2006, for instance, found that the risk of suicide in patients on lithium for an average of 18 months was five times less than those not exposed to lithium. 4 These findings were not specific to bipolar disorder, and were consistent across both randomised and open clinical trials, even though the randomised trials were significantly shorter in duration.

Thus, the data to date provides patients and clinicians with at least two conclusions: firstly, lithium appears to possess a unique anti-suicidal signature that is to a degree distinct from other agents widely categorised as ‘mood-stabilisers’, such as valproate, olanzapine, or quetiapine, and secondly, further longitudinal and randomised research is needed to robustly establish this effect. 5 Nevertheless, acknowledging the limitations of our current knowledge, lithium appears unique in offering a degree of lifetime protection from suicide and suicide attempts. Lithium offers more than stability: it offers a degree of life-preservation that is currently unmatched in psychopharmacology, demanding its urgent re-evaluation as a first-line defence against suicide.

Suicide – An urgent need for effective intervention

The needs of patients are clear: suicide is a major global health problem with nearly 1 million people dying by suicide yearly, amongst whom 90% have a diagnosable psychiatric condition. Within this group, bipolar disorder and severe recurrent depression have the highest suicide rates, 1 and although suicide occurs across the lifespan barring perhaps early childhood, it is the leading cause of death in older adolescents and young adults. 6 While psychosocial interventions for managing suicidal crises can be helpful, few pharmacological trials have focused specifically on the prevention of suicide, and therefore clinical confidence in alleviating suicidal risk in patients with major mood disorders remains poor.

In practice, lithium is widely used to restore euthymia and maintain mood stability, and is often initiated for these reasons in acute inpatient settings. However, retrospective evidence for its anti-suicidal properties suggests that to be effective in this regard it must be administered to patients for a long time – most likely years. 4 Attempts to study this potential benefit over shorter timespans have not demonstrated efficacy of lithium over placebo. Notably, the 2022 randomised placebo-controlled trial designed to assess lithium augmentation among high-risk veterans with mood disorders and recent suicide attempts was discontinued following an interim determination of futility as no overall between-group differences in suicide-related events was likely to be established. 7 Notably, this trial had a mean treatment exposure of 38.4 weeks, compared to 79 weeks (18 months) in the study noted earlier, 4 raising the intriguing possibility that lithium accrual in cells and tissues is necessary to provide a significant anti-suicide effect. 8 Further, while lithium effectively resolves acute mania relatively quickly, and thereby counters aggressive and impulsive behaviour (including suicide), an independent anti-suicidal effect possibly requires longer term exposure.

The benefit of sustained lithium therapy is evident from lithium’s unrivalled ability to provide long-term mood stability in those with bipolar disorder. This, coupled with observational data from lithium-enriched water sources, and suicide data from long-term lithium patients, supports the hypothesis that it is cumulative lithium exposure that provides class-leading prophylaxis and unique protection against suicide. These properties clearly set lithium apart, but prompt the question: how do these effects materialise? To address this, it is perhaps useful to examine its pharmacology longitudinally and at different plasma levels.

The rationale for lithium – A function of time and dose

The treatment of mood disorders typically focuses on symptomatic improvement; however, suicide is understood to be a severe and preventable outcome that requires specific intervention above and beyond the actions of commonly used antidepressants. Thus, any interventions that modify this critical outcome could potentially be regarded as having ‘disease-modifying’ properties, akin to the role of monoclonal antibodies that transform the outcomes and prognosis of rheumatoid arthritis. 9 As suicide is aetiologically a multifactorial and complex phenomenon, its underlying mechanisms are yet to be clearly understood; specific agents to modify the drivers of suicidal behaviour have not yet been developed. However, integrated neurocognitive models such as ours (G.S.M) manage to point to potential stages within the suicide ‘process’ where clinical interventions may avert the outcome. 10 Lithium, distinct from other agents utilised in mood disorders, has a broad range of actions that span its impact on neurotransmission to changes at deeper intracellular levels. At a macro level, lithium affects clinical changes in mood, especially in the prevention of major mood episodes. However, distinct from other interventions which likely reduce suicidal behaviour through the successful treatment of mood episodes, such as antidepressants and anti-epileptic drugs, lithium’s anti-suicide efficacy is independent of its ability to improve a patient’s mood. 11 In addition, and of particular importance, this specific effect on suicide may not be dependent on the plasma levels traditionally used to treat mood disorders that lie within the so-called therapeutic window, and in this regard the value of lithium administered at ‘trace’ levels over a prolonged period may explain some of the benefits found in the aforementioned ecological studies.

Beyond its clinical effects, lithium promotes changes in brain structure, specifically the recovery of brain region volumes in suicidal patients with bipolar disorder. 12 Lithium also induces changes in neurocognition (specifically, changes in aggressive impulsivity and risk-taking) and neurocircuitry (enhancing cell proliferation and regeneration). Lastly, lithium’s neurobiological actions, such as its inhibition of GSK3β activity, are responsible for a host of changes in inflammation and oxidative stress processes, gene expression, monoaminergic (especially serotonergic) neurotransmission, neuroplasticity, and hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis function, all of which are putative anti-suicide therapeutic targets. Lithium’s benefits also extend to broader neuroprotective and mortality-reducing actions, and its effects in reducing impulsivity and aggression may prove to be some of the upstream determinants of its impact on suicidal behaviour. These plasma levels range from the micromolar concentrations as detailed within ecological studies, to supra-maximal levels utilised in early studies of lithium’s anti-manic properties. In recent decades, speculation over lithium’s hidden properties has increased, and multiple avenues for further research have been unearthed. Lithium’s role in protecting patients and populations against suicide is unique and, within the limitations of retrospective and observational data, the association is clearly favourable. This benefit is potentially independent of lithium’s efficacy as a mood stabiliser, and may be a function of lower concentrations of the element being present in the body over years. Thus, our figure indicates that exposure to even low levels of lithium may lead to protection from suicide and other sources of mortality, and this effect may be cumulative over the long-term. Also hinted at in the figure, and not falling within the scope of this paper, are further benefits of lithium in the spectrum of neuroprotection and cognition.

Within the landscape of our current understanding, lithium appears to provide transdiagnostic protection from suicide for patients, signalling a breadth of applications unmatched by comparators. For example, whilst clozapine reduces the risk of suicidal behaviour in patients with treatment-refractory schizophrenia (TRS), 14 evidence of efficacy outside of the TRS population remains sparse. Meanwhile, anti-epileptic agents commonly used in the maintenance treatment of bipolar disorder, such as sodium valproate, have mixed data in suicide prevention, 15 whilst second-generation antipsychotics do not demonstrate any clear anti-suicidal properties. Lastly, antidepressant agents – frequently ineffective or even unsafe in bipolar disorder – have been found to increase the risk of suicidal behaviour in those aged under 25, 16 as well as those presenting with mixed affective states. 17 Some data suggest antidepressants protect those over 65 from suicide, 18 yet broader evidence across demographics fails to support their efficacy for preventing suicide. 15 Thus, lithium possesses a unique clinical profile, concurrently stabilising the underlying mood disorder while specifically mitigating its most lethal outcome. A prospective perspective – ‘In’ with the new, but not ‘out’ with the old

Given what is currently known about lithium’s anti-suicidal properties, and accepting that many questions remain around its role, we must address the clinical imperative to optimise suicide prevention. Based on evidence to date, lithium likely reduces the risk of suicide when administered prospectively over long periods; however, its efficacy in the acute phase is limited.

Nonetheless, it is in this acute phase that complementary novel agents may play a pivotal role in markedly relieving depressive symptomatology, including suicidal ideation. For instance, the glutamate NMDA-receptor antagonist ketamine (and its S-enantiomer esketamine) exerts both ‘antidepressant’ and ‘anti-suicidal ideation’ effects within hours of administration. Importantly, these effects appear mechanistically independent of one another. 19 Such compounds are promising, as they may effectively intervene within the immediate-response window, or initial days, of our (GSM, EB) “Windows of Antidepressant Response Paradigm (WARP)”. Importantly, this rapid onset contrasts with the latency of conventional antidepressants, which require weeks to exert any positive impact upon mood, let alone any secondary anti-suicidal benefits. 20 Whilst further research is required to better establish the efficacy and safety of ketamine and esketamine in acutely suicidal patients with mood disorders, the WARP framework allows for a structured approach to assessing the relevance of anti-suicide interventions in this patient group, from initial presentation to long-term prevention. For the latter, we must look to lithium.

We advocate for lithium as a first-line intervention for patients with recurrent mood disorders, especially those who are clinically identified as being lithium responders, as this population is frequently characterised by a heightened risk of suicide. Whilst patients presenting in suicidal crises may benefit from acute assessment and interventions, such as ketamine administered in a hospital setting, lithium should be initiated sequentially to provide sustained mood stabilisation as well as protection from suicide over the long-term. In this later phase, the extant knowledge regarding lithium’s therapeutic and adverse effects, and its broad accessibility, all support greater widespread use to achieve the aim of better preventing suicide, the most severe outcome of mood disorders. Although the potential for synergy between ketamine and lithium remains an emerging area of inquiry, a structured, time-sensitive intervention model provides a robust foundation for future clinical research.

Conclusion

The complexity of suicidal behaviour continues to present significant methodological challenges for clinical research and medical management. At present, our field is not in a position to quantify the impact of lithium in preventing early death, especially by suicide. Much remains speculative, but this should be viewed as fertile ground for clarification. Thus far, lithium research has primarily focused on its ability to treat and prevent mood episodes; indeed, lithium was the first pharmacological agent proven to ameliorate severe affective illness in patients, and such research was (and remains) necessary to provide confidence to patients and clinicians.

However, lithium’s broad range of properties, including suicide prevention, requires renewed attention from both clinicians and researchers. Epidemiological data alone, or in combination with brief randomised trials, are insufficient for providing the translatable evidence required to guide prevention via therapeutic interventions. Longitudinal lithium therapy in patients with recurrent mood disorders provides a robust framework for multifaceted research that will more precisely define lithium’s role in suicide prevention and its broader clinical profile.

Rather than prematurely closing the door upon psychiatry’s oldest pharmacological intervention, this widely accessible, affordable, and alluring element should be allowed due respect in both laboratory and clinical settings. Newer compounds may provide adjunctive, acute relief from suicidal ideation, but lithium should not, and cannot, be forgotten in its paramount ability to treat patients most at risk for suicide across the lifespan.
