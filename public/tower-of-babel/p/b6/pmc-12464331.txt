Design and fabrication of tailored Dy2O3 PVA nanocomposites with optical characterization for advanced optoelectronic applications
Authors: Jitendra Prakash Chandra, Kiran Thakur, Dinesh Uthra, Ahmed Hashim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Recently, the integration of rare earth oxides into polymer matrices has attracted significant attention for optical applications. This study explores the effect of incorporating Dysprosium oxide (Dy2O3) nanoparticles at varying concentrations (1, 3, and 5%) into a polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) matrix. All film preparation was achieved entirely through the use of casting techniques. The impact of Dy2O3 on the structural and optical characteristics of PVA was examined. Structural phase changes resulting from Dy2O3 incorporation were analyzed using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR). The effect of adding Dy2O3 nanoparticles on the PVA surface was examined using a scanning electron microscope. Optical behaviour was studied through UV–Vis spectrophotometry. As the concentration of Dy2O3 in the PVA matrix increases, the optical absorbance also increases. Additionally, the absorption edge shifts toward longer wavelengths, indicating a reduction in the bandgap energy. Incorporating 5% Dy2O3 into PVA reduces the indirect bandgap from 4.72 to 3.85 eV. This modification also enhances the refractive index from 1.41 to 1.88. These improvements make Dy2O3-doped PVA films promising candidates for advanced optoelectronic applications.

Introduction

A fascinating area in materials science that advances both research and industry is the development of polymer materials reinforced with inorganic nanoparticles. These materials, when modified with specific additives, display unique properties that make them highly suitable for optically responsive and structural layers in advanced optoelectronic devices. Their applications include solar cells, LEDs, optical waveguides, and various photonic technologies, where their tailored characteristics considerably improve performance and functionality1–4. In nanoparticle synthesis, polymers serve a dual purpose—as both reducing and capping agents—contributing to the chemical and environmental stability of the resulting nanostructures. They assist in nanoparticle formation while providing protective encapsulation, enhancing chemical stability and ecological resistance5–7.

Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) stands out among various polymers due to its broad applicability and versatile uses8. As a non-toxic, water-soluble, and semicrystalline polymer, PVA exhibits outstanding optical properties, high mechanical strength, and exceptional film-forming ability. PVA films act as efficient host matrices for dopants like transition metals, enhancing their functional properties9–12.

In recent years, rare earth oxides have gained attention as dopants in polymer matrices for tailoring material properties. Their incorporation into polymers has sparked significant interest due to their ability to enhance various physical characteristics13–15. For instance, embedding Nd2O3 nanoparticles into a PVA matrix has been shown to modify its optical properties, with a 3.7% loading reducing the bandgap to 3.16 eV16. Further studies on PVA/Nd2O3 composites revealed improvements in dielectric behaviour and morphological properties at different doping concentrations15. Similarly, the addition of 3% Y2O3 nanoparticles to PVA decreased its bandgap energy to approximately 5.31 eV while increasing its refractive index17.

Dysprosium is considered a significant rare-earth element due to its excellent optical characteristics and strong luminescence in both visible and infrared spectral regions18. However, research has shown that the incorporation of rare-earth elements into PVA can lead to degradation of the polymer’s main chain structure19. This degradation is mainly due to the intense interactions between the dopant ions and the polymer matrix. Such structural disruption alters the intensity of infrared (IR) absorption bands, which subsequently influences the material’s optical and electrical conductivities20–25. Over the past decade, there has been a growing need for sustainable or eco-friendly materials, particularly in the field of energy storage. Composite-based polymers have emerged as promising candidates for applications in batteries and supercapacitors, offering new opportunities in sustainable energy technologies. In this context, Mostafa M.Y. et al.14 investigated the bandgap properties of PVA films doped with different weight percentages of synthesized Dy2O3 via the solution casting technique. However, their study lacked critical information on key optical parameters, such as optical conductivity and the optical dielectric function.

The present study focuses on investigating the structural and physical properties of PVA nanocomposite films doped with dysprosium (III) oxide. These films were synthesized via the solution casting technique. Structural changes in the PVA matrix due to Dy2O3 incorporation were characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. Furthermore, UV–Vis. absorption spectroscopy was employed to analyze and evaluate the modifications in the optical bandgap of the doped samples.

Experimental

PVA- Dy2O3 film preparation

The materials used in this study included polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) with a purity of 99.9%, procured from Sigma–Aldrich, and nanoscale dysprosium oxide (Dy2O3) with 99.9% purity. The PVA served as the host matrix for the fabrication of nanocomposite (NC) films using the solution casting method. Initially, the PVA powder was dissolved in distilled water at 90 °C under continuous stirring using a magnetic stirrer for three hours until a clear and homogeneous solution was achieved. Dy2O3 nanoparticles were then incorporated into the PVA solution at concentrations of 1, 3, and 5%, with vigorous stirring to ensure uniform dispersion of the nanoparticles. Here, higher concentrations are avoided due to risks of agglomeration, poor film quality, and compromised structural integrity of the films. The prepared mixtures were poured into four glass Petri dishes to form films. These films were allowed to dry at room temperature for over two weeks. Once fully dried, the films were gently detached from the Petri dishes as shown in Fig. 1. The thickness of the resulting nanocomposite films, measured using a screw gauge, was found to range between 30 and 40 μm, and the films were subsequently subjected to further analysis.

Characterization of PVA- Dy₂O₃ films

The PVA-Dy2O3 nanocomposite films were characterized using multiple analytical techniques. X-ray diffraction (Rigaku X-Ray Diffractometer) analysis was performed over a 2θ range of 10°–80° to investigate structural modifications in PVA induced by Dy2O3 incorporation. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (Bruker FTIR Alpha Spectrometer) measurements (600–4000 cm⁻¹) were conducted to examine functional group interactions between PVA and Dy₂O₃ nanoparticles. Optical properties, including absorbance, transmittance, and band gap energy, were determined using UV-Vis spectroscopy (Shimadzu UV-2450) across the 200–800 nm wavelength range.

Results and discussion

XRD analysis

The diffraction pattern of the PVA-Dy2O₃ films, as shown in Fig. 2, reveals a broad peak around 19.5° for the pristine pure PVA. The peaks are distinct from the PVA matrix and can be attributed to the presence of a hydroxyl group in the PVA side chain26. This group significantly hydrogen bonding with the matrix. The XRD analysis of the pure PVA film confirms its semicrystalline structure, consistent with previous studies27,33. The characteristic peaks of PVA were present in all polymer films, though with reduced intensity. This change indicates a structural modification in the PVA matrix, where the semicrystalline phase was disrupted due to the incorporation of Dy2O3 nanoparticles. The nanoparticles were integrated into the PVA network through hydrogen bonding, and as the Dy2O3 content increased, the semicrystalline nature of PVA diminished, reflecting greater disorder within the matrix. This trend is consistent with previous studies, which also reported a decrease in crystallinity in PVA when blended with fillers such as Er2O328, Nd2O329, CeO₂9,26, PbS30, and CuO31. The XRD pattern of the PVA-Dy2O3 composite reveals distinct diffraction peaks corresponding to Dy2O3, confirming its successful incorporation into the PVA matrix. The observed peaks at 29.0°, 33.71°, 48.28°, and 57.25° are indexed to the (222), (400), (440), and (622) crystallographic planes of the cubic Dy₂O₃ phase, with a lattice parameter of 10.67 Å (JCPDS 43-1006)32. The absence of additional peaks indicates the high purity of the synthesized material. To further analyze the structural properties, Gaussian fitting was applied to the (101) and (111) diffraction peaks of PVA to determine key XRD parameters, including the diffraction angle (2θ) and full width at half maximum (β). These values were then used to calculate the average crystallite size (D)33, dislocation density (δ)34, d spacing, and micro-strain (ε)35 in the PVA matrix using the following equations.

FTIR analysis

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy is an effective analytical technique for characterizing chemical bonding interactions in optical nanocomposite films. Figure 5 presents the FTIR spectra of pure PVA and PVA-Dy2O3 nanocomposite films. The structural analysis of pure polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and Dy2O3-doped PVA films with concentrations of 1%, 3%, and 5% was conducted using FTIR spectroscopy. A characteristic broad absorption band in the 3400–3200 cm⁻¹ range, corresponding to O–H stretching vibrations from hydroxyl groups in PVA, was observed. As Dy2O3 concentration increased, this bands intensity gradually diminished, suggesting weakened hydrogen bonding between PVA chains due to interactions between Dy³⁺ ions and hydroxyl groups. A peak near 2900 cm⁻¹, attributed to C–H stretching vibrations in PVA’s alkyl chains, exhibited minor shifts and intensity variations with doping, reflecting subtle structural adjustments in the polymer matrix40,41.

SEM analysis

Figure 6 illustrates the progressive changes in surface morphology observed in Dy2O3-doped PVA films as nanoparticle concentration rises, demonstrating a structural evolution that correlates with enhanced functional versatility. Pure PVA exhibits a homogeneous, defect-free surface, a characteristic attributed to its robust hydrogen-bonded network, which promotes cohesive film formation -blended mode42. Introducing 1% Dy2O3 results in uniform nanoparticle dispersion within the polymer matrix, preserving surface continuity. This suggests effective interfacial compatibility, likely mediated by hydrogen bonding and electrostatic attraction between the nanoparticles and polymer chains. At 3%, the increased nanoparticle presence contributes to a more textured surface, indicating stronger polymer–nanoparticle interactions and improved material responsiveness43. By 5% Dy2O3, the surface shows enhanced roughness and rich nanoparticle clustering, which may be beneficial for applications requiring high surface area or active interfaces. These morphological shifts underscore the ability to tailor PVA–Dy2O3 composites by modulating doping levels, enabling customization for optoelectronic, energy storage, or sensor technologies. The structural diversification driven by higher Dy2O3 content thus expands the material’s utility, positioning it as a versatile platform for advanced functional applications.

Optical analysis

Investigating the optical characteristics and electronic transitions in polymer nanocomposites is effectively facilitated through the application of UV–Vis absorption techniques. The UV absorbance spectrum of the pure PVA film, measured between 200 and 300 nm, displays an absorption edge, along with an absorbance band at 280 nm associated with the π-π* transition, which is a characteristic of the pure PVA matrix. As shown in Fig. 7, incorporating Dy2O3 filler into PVA samples enhances optical absorption. This effect arises due to the presence of intermediate energy levels introduced into the bandgap of the PVA system44–46. Absorption is significant at higher wavelengths, as illustrated in Fig. 7. Additionally, the absorption extends across a wide spectral range, underscoring the suitability of these samples for optoelectronic device applications. Notably, the NCP films exhibit a stronger exponential absorption behaviour compared to pure PVA.

The optical properties of the polymer films were further examined by analyzing absorbance-related parameters, with the band gap energies for indirect (Egi) transitions estimated using Tauc’s method47–49 in Fig. 8.

In this analysis, α and hυ represent the absorption coefficient and the photon energy, respectively. The pristine PVA film exhibits a band gap energy of approximately 4.72 eV. However, upon doping with 5% Dy₂O₃ nanoparticles, this value decreases to around 3.85 eV. This reduction is primarily attributed to the introduction of Dy³⁺ ions, which create impurity levels within the band gap. As the Dy2O3 concentration increases in the PVA matrix, the width of this impurity band expands50–52. At higher doping levels, the incorporation of Dy₂O nanoparticles further contributes to the formation of structural disorder, defects, and interface states, leading to the emergence of localized energy levels in the band gap. This behavior aligns with the Urbach tail phenomenon53. The growing density of these intermediate states with increasing Dy2O3 content causes a progressive narrowing of the energy separation between the valence and conduction bands. Moreover, the doping process disrupts the semi-crystalline nature of the PVA matrix, promoting the formation of amorphous regions and enhancing the overall polarizability. This alteration facilitates greater electronic delocalization and modifies the local field environment, further contributing to the observed reduction in band gap energy17,54. These observations are consistent with the findings of Abdel-Aziz et al.55, who reported a decrease in the PVA band gap to approximately 3.47 eV upon doping with 10% Tb³⁺ ions. According to Ismail et al.30, the band gap (Eg) of the PVA film decreased to around 3.18 eV as the PbS content was increased to 10%. Badawi et al.56 reported that incorporating 0.5% graphene along with 10% FeS into the PVA film reduced its band gap to approximately 4.40 eV.

Conclusions

This study successfully demonstrates the impact of Dy₂O₃ nanoparticles on the structure and optical properties of PVA nanocomposite films, prepared through the solution casting method. The XRD and FTIR analyses confirmed the interaction between Dy₂O₃ nanoparticles and PVA molecules, resulting in a modified PVA structure and the formation of a novel composite. The optical analysis highlighted a significant enhancement in optical absorption, attributed to the creation of new electronic states within the polymer’s bandgap. With increasing Dy₂O₃ concentration, the PVA’s density, refractive index, and bandgap energy all showed noticeable improvements. Specifically, the bandgap energy reduced from 4.72 eV for pure PVA to 3.85 eV for the PVA matrix doped with 5% Dy₂O₃, along with an increase in the refractive index to approximately 1.88. The effect of adding Dy2O3 nanoparticles on the PVA surface was examined using a scanning electron microscope, which shows that diversification driven by higher Dy₂O₃ content thus expands the material’s utility, positioning it as a versatile platform for advanced functional applications. Furthermore, the increase in dielectric constant and broad dispersion due to the incorporation of larger Dy₂O₃ nanoparticles pointed to a higher energy state density, offering significant potential for optoelectronic applications. These findings suggest that PVA-Dy2O3 nanocomposites hold great promise for future advancements in optical and optoelectronic technologies.
