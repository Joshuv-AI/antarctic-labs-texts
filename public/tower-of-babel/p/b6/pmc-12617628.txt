Isolation and identification of duck hepatitis a virus type III and analysis of VP1 protein variation in China, 2021-2023
Authors: Li Bing, Wang Qianqian, Zhao Saisai, Man Xinhong, Wu Mian, Lu Chengguang, Li Huihui, Mao Mingtian, Liang Suyun, Guo Zhanbao, Zhou Zhengkui, Hou Shuisheng, Tang Yi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Since mid-2021, frequent outbreaks of duck hepatitis A virus type 3 (DHAV-3) have occurred across China’s major duck-farming regions, with rising detection rates and mounting economic losses, and vaccine protection has been challenged by immune-escape variants with a potential risk of recombination.To delineate the contemporary molecular epidemiology of DHAV-3, given that VP1 determines its antigenicity, we conducted a systematic survey across major duck-farming provinces in China from 2021 to 2023. Viruses were isolated from diseased duck livers using primary duck embryo fibroblast (DEF) cultures, followed by VP1 sequencing for all isolates and whole-genome sequencing for representative strains. VP1 sequences were obtained for 23 isolates, and three complete genomes were successfully assembled. Phylogenetic analysis was then combined with VP1 three-dimensional structural modeling to characterize lineage distribution and key variation features. Phylogenetic analysis assigned 21/23 isolates (91.3 %) to subgenotype GI-a and 2/23 (8.7 %) to GI-b. Complete genome lengths varied minimally, ranging from 7 788 to 7 789 bp, with the 5’ UTR and ORF regions highly conserved and only a single-base difference observed in the 3’ UTR. VP1 amino acid alignment showed that mutations were predominantly concentrated at the C-terminus; structural predictions indicated extended α-helices, elongated β-sheets and random coil to α-helix transitions in this region.These findings define the epidemiological distribution and key VP1 variation signatures of DHAV-3 in China, and provide critical data to inform targeted control strategies, enhance pathogen surveillance, and support sustainable development of the duck industry.

Introduction

Duck viral hepatitis (DVH) is a highly acute, rapidly spreading, and often fatal infectious disease of young ducks caused by Duck hepatitis A virus (DHAV)(Niu et al., 2019; Ou et al., 2022; Zhang et al., 2020). Characterized by sudden onset, rapid transmission, and high mortality, DVH can decimate flocks within a short period, inflicting severe economic losses on the duck industry(Erfan et al., 2015). DHAV is classified into three serotypes (DHAV-1, DHAV-2, and DHAV-3), of which DHAV-1 and DHAV-3 predominate in China’s major duck-farming regions(Cha et al., 2013; Kim et al., 2024; Rajendran et al., 2025). Although large-scale vaccination against DHAV-1 has effectively controlled outbreaks of this serotype, the incidence of DHAV-3 infection has continued to rise since mid-2021, and DHAV-3 has gradually supplanted DHAV-1 as the primary cause of DVH(Gao et al., 2012; Kim et al., 2024; Lei et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023).

DHAV-3 is a non-enveloped, single-stranded, positive-sense RNA virus (family Picornaviridae, genus Avihepatovirus) with virions approximately 20-40 nm in diameter and icosahedral symmetry(Chen et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2020). Its genome spans approximately 7.1-8.5 kb and comprises a 5′ untranslated region (5′ UTR), a single open reading frame (ORF), a 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR), and a poly(A) tail(Chen et al., 2018; Sun et al., 2017). The ORF encodes a P1 polyprotein that is co- and post-translationally cleaved into three capsid proteins (VP0, VP3, and VP1)(Yang et al., 2023). These structural proteins mediate viral attachment to host cell receptors and determine antigenicity(Cao et al., 2020); high mutation rates within these proteins facilitate immune evasion through amino acid substitutions, contributing to vaccine failure(Kim et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2019). In particular, VP1 is exposed on the virion surface and represents the principal neutralizing antigen and a recognized hypervariable region among picornaviruses(Song et al., 2025). Its variability underlies viral adaptation, immune escape, and cross-serotype differences. Previous studies of DHAV-1 have demonstrated VP1’s receptor-binding activity and immunogenicity, making it a key target for vaccine development and serological assays(Chen et al., 2025; Lai et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2019).

Although DHAV-3 has emerged as the dominant serotype in northern China, systematic analyses of its molecular epidemiology—particularly genotype distribution, VP1 sequence variation, and associated structural adaptations—remain limited (Wen et al., 2018; Yang et al., 2023). In particular, little is known about the predominant circulating genotypes, key mutation sites within VP1, and their structural consequences for antigenicity and immune recognition.

To bridge these gaps, we collected liver samples from diseased ducks across multiple Chinese provinces between 2021 and 2023. We isolated DHAV-3 in primary duck embryo fibroblast (DEF) cultures, sequenced the VP1 gene of 23 isolates and the complete genomes of three representative strains, and conducted comprehensive phylogenetic, homology, and structural analyses. Our goal was to characterize the molecular epidemiology and VP1 variation patterns of DHAV-3, thereby providing critical data for surveillance, prevention strategies, and vaccine design.

Materials and methods

Sample source

The samples were collected from clinical duck flocks suspected of duck viral hepatitis. Ducks showing typical neurological symptoms or acute death were selected, and their liver tissues were collected.

Virus detection

Total RNA was extracted from homogenized liver tissue on an automated magnetic-bead platform (Nanomagbio, China) and eluted in RNase-free water, and first-strand cDNA was synthesized with a commercial kit (Agbio, China). DHAV-3 was detected by conventional PCR using a 2 × master mix (CWbio, China) with primers DHAV-3-F (5′-GGTGATTCCAATCAGCTTGGCGA-3′) and DHAV-3-R (5′-TTCAATTTCTAGATGGAGCTC-3′), yielding a 720-bp amplicon. Amplicons were resolved on a 1 % agarose gel, and products showing a 720-bp band were purified and Sanger sequenced by Tsingke (Beijing, China).

Virus isolation

Preparation of Duck Embryo Fibroblasts (DEF): Healthy 9-day-old duck embryos were selected, and their heads and internal organs were removed. The trunk tissues were excised and minced. Four volumes of 0.25 % trypsin-EDTA solution (Gibco, USA) were added, and the mixture was digested in a 37°C water bath for 15 min. The digestion was terminated by adding medium containing 10 % fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA). The cell suspension was filtered through a cell strainer, centrifuged at 1 000 r/min for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellet was resuspended in DMEM medium (Gibco, USA) containing 10 % fetal bovine serum, inoculated into cell culture flasks, and incubated in a 37°C, 5 % CO₂ incubator.

Sample Treatment: Liver tissues from diseased ducks were collected and homogenized in 4 volumes of physiological saline. The homogenate was subjected to three repeated freeze-thaw cycles, then centrifuged at 3 000 r/min for 10 min. The resulting supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm filter membrane (Millipore, USA) and stored for subsequent use.

Virus Inoculation: When DEF cells reached 80 % confluence, 0.5 mL of the processed sample homogenate was inoculated into the cells. The cells were incubated at 37°C with 5 % CO₂ and monitored daily until CPE developed, with blind passage performed for up to three rounds.

Whole genome sequencing and analysis

DEF cells harboring viral particles were washed three times with pre-cooled phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) prior to the extraction of total RNA. RNA concentration and purity were quantified using a NanoPhotometer spectrophotometer (Implen, Germany), while RNA integrity was further evaluated with the RNA 6000 Nano Assay Kit on the Bioanalyzer 2100 system.

For mRNA library preparation, 3 μg of total RNA was used per sample. A strand-specific library was constructed following the manufacturer’s protocols, utilizing the NEBNext Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina, along with its recommended ribosomal RNA depletion method. RNA was fragmented into short segments of 250-300 bp, which served as templates for cDNA synthesis. After library quality assessment on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system, sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform.

Raw sequencing data (raw reads) were analyzed using FastQC software. Reads were subjected to quality control and filtering using tools like Trimmomatic or Cutadapt to generate high-quality clean reads. The processed clean reads were imported into CLC Genomics Workbench 21.0.6 for de novo sequence assembly.

Homology analysis and phylogenetic evolutionary analysis

The complete genome and VP1 sequences of the target DHAV-3 strain and reference strains were downloaded from the NCBI (National Center for Biotechnology Information, nih.gov). These sequences were edited and aligned using MegAlign software and unified into the FASTA format.

For homology analysis, Clustal X was used to perform multiple sequence alignment on the sequences. Subsequently, DNAMAN was employed to calculate the nucleotide homology percentages between the target strain and the reference strains. Through this process, the homology results of the complete sequences and VP1 sequences were obtained respectively, which helped clarify the level of similarity.

For phylogenetic evolutionary analysis, the aligned sequences were imported into MEGA X software, and the Kimura 2-parameter model was selected. A phylogenetic tree was constructed using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method with 1 000 bootstrap replications. The clustering of the target strain was observed through the phylogenetic tree, and its genetic evolutionary relationship with the reference strains was analyzed. Meanwhile, the clustering results of the phylogenetic trees constructed based on the complete sequences and VP1 sequences were compared.

DHAV-3 has a high infection rate and wide distribution in duck flocks across China. It mainly infects ducklings within 21 days of age. Genomic Characteristic Analysis of DHAV-3 Isolates

Genomic structure analysis of three DHAV-3 isolates (CF1224, DZ0918, and XY1118) revealed that their core region characteristics were consistent with only minor differences (Table 2). The 5’UTR of all three viruses was localized at positions 1-652 of the genome (652 bp), with completely consistent location and length. The ORF of each isolate started at position 653 and terminated at position 7 408 (6 756 bp). Slight differences were observed in the 3’UTR region: CF1224 and DZ0918 had their 3’UTR localized at positions 7 409-7 774 (366 bp), while XY1118 showed a 1-base delay in the termination position (7 409-7 775), resulting in an increased length of 367 bp. All three viruses had a poly (A) tail length of 14 bp. Regarding the full-genome length, CF1224 and DZ0918 were 7 788 bp, and XY1118 was 7 789 bp. This difference falls within the normal range of natural variations in the DHAV-3 genome and did not affect the structure of core functional regions. DHAV-3 isolates from different regions showed high conservation in the 5’UTR and ORF regions, with only minor length variations in the non-coding region (3’UTR). Genetic evolution analysis of DHAV-3 Strains

Phylogenetic analysis based on the VP1 gene sequences of 23 isolated strains (Fig. 3) showed that the strains were mainly divided into three major branches: GI-a, GI-b, and GII. Among them, 21 strains belonged to the GI-a branch (accounting for 91.3 %), and 2 strains belonged to the GI-b branch (accounting for 8.7 %). Within the GI-a type, XZ1223 and XJ0926 were relatively close in genetic distance and clustered into the same sub-branch; the remaining 19 strains were genetically close to the reference strains HNAY2024 and WKX03, clustering into another sub-branch.Fig. 3Phylogenetic analysis of VP1 nucleotide sequences from 23 DHAV-3 strains.Fig 3

In the genetic evolution analysis of 3 full-length genome sequences (Fig. 4), the virus strains were clearly clustered into two major branches: GI and GII, with a larger number of GI-type strains. Analysis of amino acid mutation sites in the VP1 protein.

Discussion

This study analyzed 23 DHAV-3 isolates from nine provinces in China (2021-2023) and assembled three complete genomes. The dataset resolves a GI-dominated population (GI-a 91.3 %, GI-b 8.7 %) on a conserved 7,788-7,789 bp genome backbone and delineates VP1 C-terminal signatures—including fixed M2V and R185Q—of immediate value for lineage tracking. Together, these features define current national circulation and inform surveillance and vaccine strain selection.

The 23 isolates were obtained from nine provinces (Shandong, Inner Mongolia, Henan, Hebei, Jiangsu, Anhui, Jilin, Sichuan, and Fujian), with the highest isolation rates observed in Shandong, Inner Mongolia, and Henan provinces. This pattern highlights the high prevalence of DHAV-3 in traditional duck-farming regions and indicates that the virus has already spread extensively across China. This geography reflects sustained transmission in traditional duck-farming belts and supports the view that DHAV-3 has become entrenched nationally. Similar clustering has been reported, in line with evidence that DHAV-3 has supplanted DHAV-1 as the dominant serotype in China(Doan et al., 2016; Yan et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2023).

Genomic analysis revealed that the 5′UTR (1-652 bp) and ORF (653-7,408 bp) of the three representative isolates were highly conserved, with only a 1-bp difference in the 3′UTR; full genome length ranged from 7,788 to 7,789 bp with a 14-bp poly(A) tail. The absence of indels or recombination signals across P1/P2/P3 indicates a structurally stable backbone under current circulation, and most variability remained confined to non-coding termini, consistent with prior DHAV-1 and DHAV-3 observations(Li et al., 2013).

Phylogenetically, GI predominated, with 21/23 isolates (91.3 %) clustering in GI-a and 2/23 (8.7 %) in GI-b; whole-genome trees corroborated this GI dominance, mirroring global trends(Doan et al., 2016; Hassan et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2023). GI-a was the predominant lineage and could be subdivided into two subclusters closely related to the reference strains HNAY2024 and WKX03, suggesting that these sublineages may confer a transmission advantage. Moreover, nucleotide homology between different subtypes was markedly lower than that observed within the same subtype(Zhang et al., 2017), reinforcing clear genotype boundaries relevant to typing assays.

Homology analysis indicated that GI-a remains tightly clustered (97.5-99.2 % nt; 97.5-100 % aa), whereas their similarity with GI-b and GII subtypes was substantially lower, reflecting significant genetic divergence among subtypes. Despite its smaller sample size, GI-b also showed high internal homology (99-99.6 % nt; 99.1-100 % aa), suggesting relatively conserved evolution within this lineage.

At the protein level, a key finding is the persistence of two VP1 substitutions, M2V and R185Q, across all isolates since 2021. Their fixation over time and across locations supports their use as practical molecular markers for rapid lineage tracking and routine screening. In parallel, novel substitutions that appeared in 2022-2023 indicate ongoing diversification, warranting targeted monitoring to detect potential antigenic drift early.

Structural modeling with I-TASSER suggested that conserved and newly acquired VP1 changes contribute to extension of α-helices/β-strands at the termini and random-coil to α-helix transitions, potentially reshaping surface-exposed epitopes and altering neutralizing antibody accessibility/affinity. This structural remodeling provides a plausible mechanism for immune evasion, as similar changes have previously been shown to attenuate the efficacy of vaccine-induced antibodies (Ma et al., 2015; Ou et al., 2017). Functionally, these VP1 signatures may therefore act as both markers of circulation and mechanistic candidates underlying variable vaccine performance, underscoring the value of integrating structural inference into routine molecular surveillance.

This study focused on liver tissues from diseased ducks, without sera from healthy flocks or environmental samples, limiting inference about subclinical infection and environmental reservoirs. Although sampling spanned three years, geographic coverage remains incomplete. Future work includes developing HRM/allele-specific PCR assays for M2V and R185Q to enable rapid typing, conducting cross-neutralization on GI-a representatives and mapping antigenic distance to guide vaccine-strain choice, testing the effects of key VP1 changes by reverse genetics/site-directed mutagenesis, and broadening sampling to sera, wastewater, feed, and wild waterfowl while applying time-scaled phylogenetics to assess inter-provincial spread.

In summary, our data refine the current view of DHAV-3 circulation in China and identify VP1 C-terminal signatures, especially M2V and R185Q, as practical targets for surveillance and vaccine strain selection. Routine tracking of these residues, together with standardized antigenic assays, should strengthen early warning and support timely vaccine updates for duck production.
