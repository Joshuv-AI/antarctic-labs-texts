Sex‐Dependent Neurochemical Encoding of Acute Social Experience in Adult CD1 Mice
Authors: Garikoitz Azkona, Alina Díez‐Solinska, Oihane Saez‐Atxukarro, Luna Ochoteco‐Perales, Garikoitz Beitia‐Oyarzabal, Oscar Vegas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAcute social interaction rapidly engages physiological and neurochemical systems, yet the extent to which these responses differ between sexes remains incompletely understood. Here, we investigated behavioural, endocrine and region‐specific neurochemical responses to a brief social challenge using the social interaction test in adult male and female CD1 mice. Behavioural performance was comparable between sexes, with similar levels of social interaction and locomotor activity. In contrast, endocrine measures revealed a robust stress response, as corticosterone markedly increased after social challenge. Estradiol and progesterone levels also increased following the social challenge, whereas testosterone exhibited the expected sexual dimorphism. Neurochemical profiling revealed widespread and region‐dependent effects. The brainstem showed manipulation‐driven increases in glutamatergic and catecholaminergic markers across sexes. In the striatum, social interaction preferentially enhanced glutamatergic metabolites in males, whereas females displayed stronger monoaminergic signatures. The hippocampus exhibited the most pronounced sex‐dependent modulation, involving amino‐acid precursors, catecholaminergic turnover indices, noradrenergic metabolites and kynurenine‐pathway ratios. By contrast, neurochemical organisation in the prefrontal cortex was predominantly shaped by baseline sexual dimorphism rather than acute social exposure. Correlation analyses between plasma hormones and regional neurochemical markers revealed limited and inconsistent associations, suggesting a relative dissociation between endocrine and neurochemical responses to the social challenge. Together, these findings demonstrate that acute social interaction induces broad endocrine and neurochemical adaptations while revealing distinct, region‐specific sex signatures in neurochemical regulation.

Introduction

Human beings are inherently social, and they rely on meaningful connections and supportive relationships to sustain mental well‐being (Egaña‐Marcos et al. 2025). People find meaning and a sense of reward through their interactions with others, and even the simplest exchanges involve complex processes of information integration, decision‐making and emotional regulation (Tremblay et al. 2017). The neurobiological systems underlying social behaviours rely on a delicate balance between excitatory and inhibitory neurotransmission, together with neuromodulatory signalling across multiple brain regions. Changes in glutamatergic, GABAergic, dopaminergic, noradrenergic, serotonergic and tryptophan–kynurenine pathways have been implicated in social interaction, stress responses and dysregulated social behaviours (Beery and Kaufer 2015; Blanchard et al. 2001; Mumtaz et al. 2018). Deviations in social behaviour, and in the systems that regulate them, are central to many psychiatric disorders, including depression, anxiety and autism spectrum disorders (ASD) (Peçanha et al. 2025; Porcelli et al. 2019).

Mice, as herd animals with measurable social tendencies, have become widely used animal models for studying social behaviour. Their behavioural paradigms enable researchers to translate certain aspects of human sociality into controlled experimental settings (Azkona and Sanchez‐Pernaute 2022; Charron et al. 2024; Ellenbroek and Youn 2016; Gachomba et al. 2024). Tests such as the social interaction test are a valuable tool to dissect the neural mechanisms underlying social behaviour (Kingsbury et al. 2019; Peng et al. 2025). Despite the relative simplicity of rodent social interactions compared to humans, the homology of brain regions and neurotransmitter systems involved supports their use in modelling social behavioural disorders (Guo et al. 2019; Moy et al. 2004, 2007).

Sex differences play a critical role in shaping social behaviour in rodents, yet historically most studies have relied almost exclusively on male mice to minimise variability associated with the female oestrous cycle (Tsao et al. 2023). However, accumulating evidence demonstrates that female mice do not necessarily exhibit greater behavioural variability and, in fact, display distinct social responses compared to males. For example, sexual dimorphism has been observed in nociception (Azkona et al. 2016) and in the functioning of the locus coeruleus (Mariscal et al. 2023), which may underlie sex‐dependent differences in emotion, cognition and pain‐related behaviours in male and female mice. Moreover, fine‐grained behavioural analyses reveal sex‐ and context‐dependent differences in social decision‐making and interaction patterns, such as head movements and affiliative behaviours (McEwen and Milner 2017; Phalip et al. 2025). These findings highlight that male and female brains engage overlapping but distinct circuits in regulating social behaviour, with implications for psychiatric disorders, which often show sex‐biased prevalence and symptomatology (Beery and Zucker 2011; Kokras and Dalla 2014; Maddern et al. 2024; Murta et al. 2023). Consequently, the systematic inclusion of female mice in experimental designs is not only necessary to improve reproducibility and translational relevance but also to uncover mechanisms of psychiatric vulnerability that may differ between sexes.

The present study aims to investigate sex‐dependent neurochemical responses to an acute social challenge in adult male and female CD1 mice, analysing multiple brain regions and key metabolite ratios within the glutamatergic, GABAergic, dopaminergic, noradrenergic, serotonergic and kynurenine pathways. This comprehensive approach seeks to elucidate the regional‐ and sex‐specific dynamics of neurotransmitter metabolism following social interaction.

Methods

Subjects and Husbandry

Eight‐week‐old CD1 outbred male (n = 18) and female (n = 18) mice (Janvier, France) were housed in transparent plastic cages measuring 24.5 × 24.5 × 15 cm in groups of three upon arrival. Food and water were available ad libitum. The holding room was maintained at a constant temperature of 22°C ± 2°C with a relative humidity of 70%, under a reverse 12‐h light/dark cycle (white lights on from 19:00 to 07:00 h) to allow testing of these nocturnal animals during their active phase. All experimental procedures were conducted under dim red lighting in a room adjacent to the holding facility. All procedures involving animals were performed in accordance with the European Directive 2010/63/EU and were approved by the Animal Welfare Ethics Committee of the University of the Basque Country (CEEA‐UPV/EHU; M20/2024/201) and the Gipuzkoa Provincial Council (PRO‐AE‐SS‐340).

Experimental Procedure

All animal experiments were conducted between 08:00 and 10:00 AM (1–3 h after the onset of the dark cycle). On the fourth day of acclimatisation, animals were individually identified using ear punching. On acclimatisation day 8, when the animals were 9 weeks old (young‐adult), animals were arbitrarily assigned to one of two groups: (1) non‐manipulated (Non‐M) animals (n = 9 per sex), which were not subjected to any behavioural manipulation; (2) manipulated (M) animals (n = 9 per sex), which underwent the social interaction test.

Non‐manipulated animals were weighed and 15 μL of blood was collected from the submandibular vein using a lancet for corticosterone determination. Animals were then anaesthetised with sodium pentobarbital (200 mg/kg, diluted to 60 mg/mL for intraperitoneal administration). Once a deep anaesthetic plane was confirmed, 500 μL of intracardiac blood was collected using a 25G needle for sex hormone analysis. Finally, animals were euthanised by cervical dislocation. The brain was removed, and the brainstem, striatum, hippocampi and prefrontal cortex were dissected under cold and sterile conditions, snap‐frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C.

On the same day, animals in the M group performed the SIT, and 40 min after the end of the test, the same procedure described for the Non‐M group was conducted. Experiments in males and females were conducted in separate cohorts 2 weeks apart, with males tested first under the same experimental conditions.

Behavioural Assessment

The social interaction test (SIT) was conducted in a square open‐field arena (40 × 40 × 30 cm) made of opaque plastic, with two cylindrical perforated wire cages positioned in the corners. Habituation to the room, isolated from external sound sources and illuminated with dim red light, was carried out for approximately 1 h before testing. Each animal was recorded using an overhead video camera (GZ‐MG773; JVC, Yokohama, Japan) for subsequent assessment with the ANY‐maze computerised version 4.96 video‐tracking software program (Stoelting Europe, Dublin, Ireland).

The procedure consisted of two consecutive 150‐s phases, separated by a 30‐s interval. During the habituation phase (Pre‐test), each mouse was placed individually in the centre of the arena and allowed to explore freely for 150 s. Immediately afterwards, the mouse was removed from the arena and returned to its home cage until the second phase. During this interval, a male mouse (the ‘social stimulus’) was introduced into one of the cylindrical cages. The stimulus animal was an adult male in all conditions, including trials involving female subjects. The experimental mouse was then allowed to explore the arena for a further 150 s (Test). The stimulus animal was unfamiliar to the subject and had no previous contact with it. The wire cage allowed the exchange of olfactory, auditory and visual cues while preventing aggressive physical interactions. The arena and wire cages were thoroughly cleaned with 70% ethanol between trials to remove odour cues. The social interaction ratio (SI ratio) was calculated by dividing the time spent in the interaction zone when the subject was present by the time spent in the interaction zone when the subject was absent.

Physiological Assessments

Blood Collection, Plasma Isolation and Hormone Levels Determination

Blood samples were collected using lithium heparin tubes (cat. no. 365985; BD Microtainer), then centrifuged at 1800 × g for 15 min at 4°C. The resulting plasma was collected and stored at −80°C until further analysis. Plasma hormone determination was assessed through commercial enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) following the manufacturer's recommendations; Corticosterone ELISA Kit (cat. no. ADI‐900‐097, Enzo Life Sciences, RRID: AB_2307314), Estradiol (E2) (Estradiol ELISA Kit, cat. no. CEA461Ge, Cloud‐Clone Corp), Progesterone (P4) (Progesterone ELISA Kit, cat. no. DE1561, Demeditec Diagnostics GmbH) and Testosterone (T) (Testosterone ELISA Kit, cat. no. CEA458Ge, Cloud‐Clone Corp). A Synergy HT microplate reader (BioTek Instruments Inc., Winooski, VT, USA) was used to quantify the results.

Determination of Brain Neurochemical Metabolites by High‐Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC)

Brain structures were weighed and dissolved in a solvent volume equivalent to 10 times their mass (85% acetonitrile and 15% MilliQ water), yielding a final concentration of 0.1 mg/μL. Subsequently, the solution was sonicated (LABSONIC P, B. Braun Biotech International, Goettingen, Germany) at a frequency of 30 kHz, with a 50% duty cycle and 60% amplitude for 10 s. The sample was then centrifuged (Microfuge 22R, Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) at 15 000 g for 10 min at 4°C. 30 μL of supernatant was collected and concentrated using a DRI‐BLOCK DB.3D (Techne Inc., Princeton, NJ, USA) under a stream of compressed air at room temperature for 30 min. Following concentration, samples were frozen at −80°C for subsequent LC–MS/MS analysis. On the day of analysis, 150 μL of Dns‐Cl (4 mg/mL in ACN) and 50 μL of bicarbonate buffer (pH 11) were added to the samples. The mixture was vortexed and incubated in darkness at 35°C for 30 min. After the reaction was complete, 10 μL of formic acid (15% in MilliQ water) were added to each sample. The mixture was vortexed and transferred to inserts for injection into the chromatographic system.

HPLC analyses were performed using a Kinetex 2.6 μm F5 100 Å (2.1 × 50 mm) column. The mobile phase consisted of an aqueous solution of 20 nM ammonium formate with 0.1% formic acid and acetonitrile, applied under gradient conditions at a flow rate of 0.25 mL/min. The run time was 10 min, followed by a 2‐min post‐run equilibration. The column temperature was maintained at 40°C, and the injection volume was 1 μL. Mass spectrometric detection was carried out using an ESI‐Jetstream interface operating in positive ion mode. Data acquisition on the triple quadrupole detector was performed in dynamic multiple reaction monitoring (dMRM) mode, monitoring one to two MS/MS transitions per analyte according to their fragmentation behaviour. The final data were expressed as ng/g.

Glutamine (GLN), glutamate (GLU), γ‐aminobutyric acid (GABA), L‐phenylalanine (Phe), L‐tyrosine (Tyr), dopamine (DA), 3,4‐dihydroxyphenylacetic acid (DOPAC), homovanillic acid (HVA), noradrenaline (NA), 3‐methoxy‐4‐hydroxyphenylglycol (MHPG), vanillylmandelic acid (VMA), tryptophan (Tryp), kynurenine (Kyn), kynurenic acid (Kyna), 3‐hydroxykynurenine (3‐HK), serotonin (5‐HT) and 5‐hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5‐HIAA) were quantified. We analysed neurotransmitter turnover across the brain regions by examining metabolite‐to‐neurotransmitter ratios. For the glutamatergic and GABAergic system, GLU/GLN was used to reflect the activity of the glutamate–glutamine cycle (neuron–astrocyte), and GABA/GLU served as an index of the inhibitory–excitatory balance. In addition, the GABA/GLN ratio was calculated as an indirect marker of GABAergic metabolic activity relative to glutamine availability. In the dopaminergic system, DA/Tyr for evaluating dopamine synthesis, DOPAC/DA was calculated as a dopaminergic turnover index, and HVA/DOPAC reflected the relationship between intra‐ and extraneuronal metabolites, complementing DOPAC/DA. For the noradrenergic system, MHPG/NA indicated noradrenaline turnover, while VMA/MHPG reflected the final catabolism of noradrenaline. In the kynurenine pathway, Kyn/Tryp represented the flux of tryptophan toward the pathway, Kyna/Kyn the proportion of kynurenine converted to the neuroprotective metabolite kynurenic acid, and 3‐HK/Kyn the proportion converted to the neurotoxic metabolite 3‐hydroxykynurenine. Finally, for the serotonergic system, 5‐HT/Tryp indicated the ratio between serotonin and its precursor, reflecting synthesis and 5‐HIAA/5‐HT served as an index of serotonergic turnover.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi (version 2.3.28.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 10.3.1). Values of p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant (95% confidence), and data are expressed as mean ± standard error (SE). All datasets were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the results, including exact p‐values, are provided in Table S1. Brain metabolite data were log‐transformed to satisfy assumptions of normality. A test for outliers was conducted using the ROUT method (Q = 1%) in GraphPad Prism. No outliers were detected, and therefore no data points were excluded from the analyses. Sex differences in the SIT ratio and in the percentage of time spent interacting with the social target were analysed using unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐tests. Differences between the SIT Pre‐test and Test in mobility and immobility were analysed using two‐way ANOVA. Plasma hormone concentrations and neurochemistry were analysed using two‐way ANOVA to assess the effects of Sex (Male, Female) and Group (Non‐manipulated, Manipulated). Post hoc comparisons were performed using the Bonferroni test. ANOVA effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared (η 2 p ; small < 0.06, medium ≥ 0.06, large ≥ 0.14), and post hoc effect sizes were calculated as Cohen's d (small < 0.50, medium ≥ 0.50, large ≥ 0.80). Associations between parameters were assessed using Spearman correlations (rho), with effect sizes interpreted as < 0.30 (small), 0.30–0.49 (medium) and ≥ 0.50 (large). Correction for multiple comparisons in correlation analyses was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. Instead, the final group size (n = 9 per group) was selected based on previous neurochemical and behavioural studies in mice that employed comparable experimental designs and similar cohort sizes (Díez‐Solinska et al. 2024; Hsieh et al. 2017). Therefore, the chosen sample size was considered adequate to detect relevant neurochemical changes while adhering to ethical principles aimed at minimising animal use.

Results

Social Interaction Test

No significant sex differences were observed in the percentage of time spent in social contact, t (16) = 0.109, p = 0.915, d = 0.051 (Figure 1A) or in the SIT ratio, t (16) = 0.350, p = 0.731, d = 0.165 (Figure 1B). For distance travelled, the repeated‐measures ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Time, F (1,16) = 44.10, p < 0.001, η 2 p = 0.734 (Figure 1C). For immobility, the repeated‐measures ANOVA also revealed a significant main effect of Time, F (1,16) = 17.43, p < 0.001, η 2 p = 0.521 (Figure 1D). The main effect of Sex was not significant, and the Time × Sex interaction was not significant for either variable.

Plasma Hormone Levels

For plasma corticosterone levels, the two‐way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Group, F (1,32) = 32.42, p < 0.0001, η 2 p = 0.452, whereas the main effect of Sex was not significant. The Group × Sex interaction approached significance, F (1,32) = 3.94, p = 0.056, η 2 p = 0.055, suggesting potential sex‐dependent differences in the response to the manipulation (Figure 2A). Given this trend and our a priori interest in sex‐specific effects, we conducted post hoc comparisons to further characterise the pattern of results. In the intact condition, males and females showed comparable corticosterone levels (males: 117 ± 21.4 ng/mL; females: 114 ± 20.7 ng/mL). In contrast, corticosterone levels were significantly higher in the manipulated males (p = 0.013, d = −1.24) and manipulated females (p < 0.0001, d = −2.56). Within the manipulated groups, females exhibited markedly higher corticosterone levels (271 ± 20.7 ng/mL) than males (193 ± 17 ng/mL; p = 0.011, d = −1.27).

Regarding estradiol, the two‐way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Group, F (1,32) = 18.33, p < 0.001, η 2 p = 0.339 (Figure 2B). Manipulated males exhibited higher plasma estradiol levels than intact males (93.2 ± 7.6 vs. 61.4 ± 6.23 pg/mL, p = 0.0014, d = −1.70), and a similar pattern was observed in females (101 ± 4 vs. 78.9 ± 6.79 pg/mL, p = 0.016, d = −1.19). The main effect of Sex approached significance, F (1,32) = 4.11, p = 0.051, η 2 p = 0.076, but did not reach the conventional threshold, and the Group × Sex interaction was not significant.

For progesterone, the statistical model revealed a significant main effect of Sex, F (1,32) = 13.10, p = 0.001, η 2 p = 0.267, and Group, F (1,32) = 4.24, p = 0.048, η 2 p = 0.086 (Figure 2C). Among males, intact individuals showed a mean progesterone concentration of 4.17 ± 1.19 ng/mL, whereas manipulated males showed 5.95 ± 1.04 ng/mL. In females, intact individuals averaged 9.82 ± 2.94 ng/mL, while manipulated females exhibited markedly elevated progesterone levels (18.2 ± 3.58 ng/mL). Post hoc comparisons confirmed that all groups differed significantly from the M‐female group: Non‐M male (p = 0.003, d = −1.95), Non‐M female (p = 0.023, d = −1.17) and M‐male (p = 0.001, d = −1.70).

Finally, the two‐way ANOVA showed a significant main effect of Sex on testosterone levels, F (1,32) = 11.25, p = 0.002, η 2 p = 0.260, with males exhibiting higher concentrations than females (2039 ± 118 vs. 1568 ± 76 pg/mL, p = 0.002, d = 1.12) (Figure 2D). No correlations are observed between hormones in either sex.

Additionally, a significant Group × Sex interaction emerged (F (1,32) = 5.37, p = 0.027, η 2 p = 0.144), driven by manipulated males showing ~363% lower VMA/MHPG ratios than non‐manipulated males (t (32) = 3.98, p = 0.002, d = 1.88).

A significant Group × Sex interaction was observed for hippocampal Tryp levels (F (1,32) = 11.85, p = 0.002, η 2 p = 0.270), driven by manipulated females showing ~80% higher Tryp levels than manipulated males (t (32) = 3.87, p = 0.003, d = 1.82), and by non‐manipulated males showing ~62% higher levels than manipulated males (t (32) = 3.16, p = 0.021, d = 1.49; Figure 5F). A main effect of Sex approached significance (F (1,32) = 4.10, p = 0.051, η 2 p = 0.114), with females showing ~24% higher Tryp levels than males (t (32) = 2.02, p = 0.051, d = 0.67). For Kyn, a significant main effect of Group was observed (F (1,32) = 4.54, p = 0.041, η 2 p = 0.124), with manipulated animals showing ~9% lower Kyn levels than non‐manipulated animals (t (32) = 2.13, p = 0.041, d = 0.71; Figure 5G). For the Kyn/Tryp ratio, a significant main effect of Sex was detected (F (1,32) = 6.33, p = 0.017, η 2 p = 0.165), with males showing ~27% higher values than females (t (32) = −2.52, p = 0.017, d = −0.84). A significant Group × Sex interaction was also found (F (1,32) = 11.15, p = 0.002, η 2 p = 0.258), driven by manipulated females exhibiting ~43% lower Kyn/Tryp ratios than manipulated males (t (32) = −4.14, p = 0.001, d = −1.95). For Kyna, a significant Group × Sex interaction was observed (F (1,32) = 4.33, p = 0.045, η 2 p = 0.119), with no significant main effects of Sex or Group (Figure 5H). For the Kyna/Kyn ratio, a significant main effect of Group emerged (F (1,32) = 5.25, p = 0.029, η 2 p = 0.141), with manipulated animals showing ~19% higher ratios than non‐manipulated mice (t (32) = −2.29, p = 0.029, d = −0.76). In the serotonergic system, no significant effects were detected for 5‐HT or 5‐HIAA. Still, the 5‐HT/Tryp ratio showed a significant Group × Sex interaction (F (1,32) = 4.80, p = 0.036, η 2 p = 0.130), although none of the post hoc comparisons survived correction.

Effects of Social Interaction on Prefrontal Cortex Metabolite Levels

In the prefrontal cortex, a significant main effect of Sex was observed for GLN (F (1,32) = 10.02, p = 0.003, η 2 p = 0.238), with males exhibiting approximately 18% higher GLN levels compared to females (t (32) = −3.17, p = 0.003, d = −1.06; Figure 6A). No effects of Group or Group × Sex interaction were detected. Although GLU did not reach conventional significance, the main effect of Sex showed a trend supported by a moderate effect size (F (1,32) = 3.45, p = 0.072, η 2 p = 0.097), with males showing approximately 20% higher GLU levels than females (t (32) = −1.86, p = 0.072, d = −0.62). No significant effects or meaningful trends were found for GABA concentration itself or for Group × Sex interactions within the GABAergic system. A significant main effect of Sex was detected for the GABA/GLN ratio (F (1,32) = 8.42, p = 0.007, η 2 p = 0.208), with males showing approximately 27% lower GABA/GLN values than females (t (32) = 2.90, p = 0.007, d = 0.97). GABA/GLU showed a trend‐level Sex effect (F (1,32) = 4.07, p = 0.052, η 2 p = 0.113), supported by a moderate effect size (d = 0.67), with females exhibiting ~30% higher GABA/GLU ratios.

Several catecholaminergic markers showed significant Sex effects. DA showed a significant main effect of Sex (F (1,32) = 4.79, p = 0.036, η 2 p = 0.130), with males exhibiting ~70% higher DA levels than females (t (32) = −2.19, p = 0.036, d = −0.73; Figure 6B). Among dopamine metabolites, DOPAC was significantly higher in males (F (1,32) = 4.43, p = 0.043, η 2 p = 0.122), corresponding to ~57% elevation versus females (t (32) = −2.11, p = 0.043, d = −0.70; Figure 6C), and HVA was likewise higher in males (F (1,32) = 5.43, p = 0.026, η 2 p = 0.145), by ~38% (t (32) = −2.33, p = 0.026, d = −0.78; Figure 6D).

Within the tryptophan–kynurenine pathway, no significant or trend‐level effects were detected for Tryp or Kyn. Downstream of Kyn, Kyna showed a significant main effect of Group (F (1,32) = 4.19, p = 0.049, η 2 p = 0.116), with manipulated animals exhibiting ~8% lower Kyna levels than non‐manipulated animals (t (32) = 2.05, p = 0.049, d = 0.68; Figure 6E). Correlation Between Plasma Corticosterone and Brain Metabolite Levels by Sex

To explore the relationship between neurochemical changes and plasma corticosterone levels, we computed Spearman correlations separately for males and females. Given the large number of comparisons, these analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis‐generating. In males, corticosterone showed several nominal associations with neurochemical measures, including positive correlations with brainstem amino acids and catecholaminergic markers, and negative correlations with serotonergic indices and hippocampal metabolites. In females, corticosterone correlations were fewer and more spatially restricted, involving selected dopaminergic and kynurenine pathway markers. Discussion

Males and females displayed comparable behavioural engagement during the social interaction test, consistent with evidence that CD1 mice of both sexes exhibit robust affiliative responses under non‐aggressive conditions (Hsieh et al. 2017; Lee et al. 2025). Despite this behavioural similarity, we observed marked divergence in the underlying neurochemical architecture, indicating that equivalent behavioural outputs can emerge from distinct system‐level regulatory configurations.

A central finding is that baseline neurochemical organisation is strongly sex‐structured. Males exhibited a predominance of excitatory and catecholaminergic signatures, particularly within prefrontal circuitry, whereas females showed comparatively stronger inhibitory tone, enhanced serotonergic engagement and elevated activity along the tryptophan–kynurenine metabolic axis. These patterns support the view that male and female brains operate under partially distinct regulatory setpoints defined by differences in excitatory‐inhibitory balance, mesocorticolimbic responsivity, monoaminergic turnover and neurochemical routing (Barth et al. 2015; Bhatt and Dluzen 2005; Dalla et al. 2008; Giacometti and Barker 2020; Jones and Lucki 2005; Lafta et al. 2024; Manion et al. 2022; Tkáč et al. 2023; Wickens et al. 2018). Baseline divergence in kynurenine pathway metabolites further aligns with literature identifying this pathway as a molecular interface linking immune signalling, neuromodulation and sex‐biased affective regulation (Brown et al. 2024; de Bartolomeis et al. 2025; Milosavljevic et al. 2025; Pais et al. 2023; Perez‐Castro et al. 2024).

Acute social interaction revealed that each sex preferentially recruits distinct neurochemical regulatory axes when encoding social experience. Males showed broader mobilisation of glutamatergic and dopaminergic metabolism, consistent with enhanced recruitment of circuits mediating salience processing, motivational drive and arousal‐related network activation (Aubry et al. 2022; Gunaydin et al. 2014; Kniffin and Briand 2024; Matthews et al. 2016; Rojek‐Sito et al. 2023). In contrast, females exhibited a more selective neurochemical reconfiguration characterised by stronger noradrenergic turnover and differential routing of tryptophan metabolism. This profile suggests preferential engagement of neuromodulatory and stress‐integrative pathways rather than reduced responsiveness, aligning with evidence that female stress regulation relies more heavily on noradrenergic and serotonergic control systems than on dopaminergic mobilisation (Bangasser and Cuarenta 2021; Bangasser and Wiersielis 2018; Hodes et al. 2024; Luo et al. 2024; Scroger et al. 2025).

These sex‐dependent response modes were anatomically constrained. Regions supporting motivational valuation and stress integration, particularly the striatum and hippocampus, emerged as integrative nodes where social experience interacted with sex to shape neurochemical outcomes. This regional specificity accords with syntheses demonstrating sex differences in striatal glutamatergic transmission, dopamine regulation via local microcircuit mechanisms, and sexually differentiated social reward processing (Becker and Chartoff 2019; Chen and Hong 2018; Orsini et al. 2022; Van Zandt et al. 2024; Zachry et al. 2021). It also converges with extensive evidence that hippocampal plasticity, neuromodulatory dynamics and stress responsivity are strongly sex‐modulated and sensitive to social context (Joëls et al. 2018; McEwen and Milner 2017). Moreover, monoaminergic utilisation and kynurenine pathway flux are known to shift following social or stress exposure in a sex‐dependent and time‐sensitive manner (Abercrombie and Zigmond 1989; Audet and Anisman 2010; Haller et al. 2006; Savitz 2020; Valentino and Van Bockstaele 2008).

Endocrine measures further support a sex‐differentiated regulatory architecture. Females exhibited stronger corticosterone responses, consistent with heightened HPA‐axis reactivity (Aoki et al. 2010; Nalepa et al. 2025). Hormone–metabolite coupling patterns showed sex‐dependent differences: in males, corticosterone was associated with excitatory, catecholaminergic and serotonergic markers, whereas in females, associations were more restricted to noradrenergic and tryptophan‐related metabolism. These effects survived FDR correction, suggesting partially distinct neurochemical pathways underlying glucocorticoid signalling across sexes (Bendis et al. 2024; Goyette et al. 2023; Zuloaga et al. 2020). Regarding testosterone plasma levels, it should be noted that in females, our results (plasma samples) are substantially higher than those reported in previous studies using serum samples (Lotter et al. 2013) and plasma samples (Kontsevaya et al. 2021). In contrast, in males, we observe values comparable to those reported in other studies using serum measurements (Grífols et al. 2020). Although the assay was performed according to the manufacturer's instructions for a commercial kit validated for plasma samples, and the supplier confirmed the absence of cross‐reactivity with 5α‐dihydrotestosterone (DHT), dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) and androstenedione (ASD), the elevated values observed in females warrant cautious interpretation. Methodological and biological factors, including matrix‐specific effects and sex‐dependent differences in circulating steroid dynamics, may contribute to these discrepancies. Accordingly, these measurements were used exclusively for intra‐experimental comparisons rather than as absolute reference values.

Collectively, these results identify sex as a primary organiser of neurochemical system architecture and a major determinant of how social experience is encoded at the metabolic level. While baseline organisation shows the strongest sexual differentiation, social interaction selectively amplifies these differences within integrative corticolimbic circuitry. Together, these findings advance a systems‐level neurobiological framework for interpreting sex differences in social behaviour, stress regulation and disease‐relevant brain mechanisms, consistent with foundational models of brain sexual differentiation (Bale and Epperson 2015; Ivan et al. 2023; Shansky and Murphy 2021).

Nonetheless, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, only a single post‐interaction time point was assessed, preventing reconstruction of temporal dynamics that may include delayed or transient peaks. Second, the oestrous cycle stage was not controlled despite its known influence on glutamatergic, GABAergic and serotonergic signalling. Third, all stimulus animals were male, including in experiments involving female subjects. While this factor was consistent across all animals within each sex and did not result in detectable differences in behavioural engagement during the social interaction test, the use of a male conspecific may nonetheless differentially engage underlying neuroendocrine pathways, particularly in females. As such, the present design does not allow dissociation of sex‐specific responses to the social stimulus from potential effects driven by the sex of the conspecific. Future studies should explicitly manipulate stimulus sex to resolve these contributions. Fourth, neurochemical measures were not paired with neuronal activity readouts, limiting mechanistic resolution. Fifth, while it would have been of interest to explore correlations between metabolites and hormones, given the capacity for local synthesis of hormones such as estradiol and dihydrotestosterone in the brain, circulating levels alone may not accurately reflect the relevant neuroendocrine environment. Accordingly, meaningful interpretation of hormone–metabolite relationships would require parallel quantification of steroid levels in both blood and brain tissue, ideally using LC–MS‐based approaches. Future work should integrate these measurements with longitudinal neurochemical sampling, circuit‐level activity readouts and targeted hormone manipulation strategies to dissect causal neuroendocrine mechanisms.

Conclusion

Our results identify sex as a primary determinant of both baseline neurochemical architecture and the acute biochemical impact of social experience. Sex influences the organisation of these systems more consistently than immediate social exposure, except in regions where stress, motivation and social processing converge, such as the striatum and hippocampus. This dimorphism provides a mechanistic substrate for sex‐specific vulnerability to social stress and may help explain divergent behavioural and affective trajectories in males and females. By mapping region‐specific metabolic signatures, our study advances a more precise biological framework for interpreting sex differences in social behaviour, stress responsivity and susceptibility to neuropsychiatric disorders.
