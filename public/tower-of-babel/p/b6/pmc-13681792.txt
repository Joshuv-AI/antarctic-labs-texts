Derivation of a risk model to predict urinary incontinence during pregnancy: a research article
Authors: Alícia Carrera-Boix, Jordi Cassadó Garriga, Jordi Blanch, Lia Alves-Cabratosa, Anna Ponjoan, Ruth Martí-Lluch, Rafel Ramos Blanes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe onset of urinary incontinence (UI) during pregnancy is related to urinary incontinence persistence in the postpartum and later years. We aimed to design a prediction model of urinary incontinence that could be used at the beginning of pregnancy and included clinical and ultrasound variables.Materials and methodsDesign: Prospective cohort study.Settings: Obstetrics and Gynecology Service of Santa Caterina’s Hospital (Girona, Spain) between 2018 and 2020.Population: Women at 12–14 weeks’ gestation were invited to participate by consecutive sampling and a follow-up visit was carried out at 32–35 pregnancy weeks.Methods: During the visits, we collected clinical data, measured 2D ultrasound variables, and administered the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire (ICIQ-SF). We built a multivariable model to predict urinary incontinence, assessed its calibration and discrimination (with the area under the ROC curve), and performed an internal validation. All the analyses were carried out using the R software (version 4.1).ResultsWe recruited 174 nulliparous pregnant women during the study period and designed two models to predict urinary incontinence based on the significant variables in the univariate analysis: the urethral mobility and body mass index (BMI). Background

Urinary incontinence (UI) is a dysfunction with a strong negative impact on the daily life of the people who suffer from it, while also representing a significant socioeconomic problem that challenges the health systems [1]. This condition often appears for the first time during pregnancy, when the prevalence of UI ranges from 15 to 20% in the first trimester to 35–40% in the third trimester [2]. Moreover, women who have UI during pregnancy are more likely to suffer its persistence for months and even years after the delivery [3, 4]. Although mild cases of UI may be manageable, moderate/severe cases deserve special focus, because a higher degree of UI has been associated with a lower quality of life [3, 5].

Previous reports on the pathophysiology of UI are primarily based on clinical rather than ultrasound variables assessed during pregnancy or the postpartum period (maternal age, maternal BMI, presence of urinary incontinence during pregnancy) [6, 7]. Only a limited number of studies have evaluated ultrasound variables for the assessment of the urethra and its supporting structures and have shown a relationship between ultrasound measurements and the occurrence of UI. However, few of them have analyzed these variables during pregnancy; and they were mainly cross-sectional studies focused on the end of pregnancy and the postpartum period [8, 9]. A study conducted in 2018 described a predictive model that, similarly to ours, included several ultrasound variables in addition to clinical variables assessed during the first and second trimesters of pregnancy. Their model included the following variables: BMI, constipation, previous mode of delivery, Beta angle, and the presence of bladder neck funneling. However, the authors did not differentiate between nulliparous and multiparous women; the Beta angle had not been described in other studies and therefore could not be compared with other published data; no interobserver agreement analysis was reported; and, most importantly, the study was also based on a cross-sectional approach [10]. Longitudinal prospective studies for the analysis of relevant parameters at the beginning of the pregnancy would help identify pregnant women at risk of developing moderate/severe UI during pregnancy.

Accordingly, our main objective was to analyze whether certain clinical parameters and ultrasound variables measured in the first trimester by 2D ultrasound could help predict the onset of moderate/severe UI in the third trimester of pregnancy. We also sought to design a proof-of-concept software application for predicting moderate/severe UI in pregnant women.

Methods

We carried out a prospective cohort study at Hospital Santa Caterina (Salt, Catalonia, Spain) that lasted from 2018 to 2020. Nulliparous pregnant women attending the Obstetrics and Gynecology Department were recruited between 12 and 14 weeks’ gestation by consecutive sampling, and a follow-up visit was conducted between 32 and 35 weeks. In our healthcare area, all women assigned to our hospital undergo first- and third trimester ultrasound examination at the hospital, these assessments are not performed in primary care centers. We excluded pregnant women who were multiparous, had a multiple pregnancies, previous pelvic floor dysfunction (previous pelvic floor surgeries or treatments, incontinence, or prolapse), moderate/severe UI in the first trimester, or who were unable to complete a self-administered questionnaire on UI.

The sample size was estimated considering the main objective (construction of a model to predict the risk of moderate/severe UI in the third trimester of pregnancy [outcome] from ultrasound scan variables measured in the first trimester) and according to the Monte Carlo Simulation (based on the reliability of the regression coefficients) [11–13]. Considering that 10–15 events per variable were needed to obtain stable estimations, and assuming an event rate of 45% [3] and a maximum of six predicting factors, the minimum required sample size was 133 participants.

If the patient decided to take part, we gave her an informed consent to sign and the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire –Short Form (ICIQ-SF) to fill in. This questionnaire is designed to be self-administered and classifies UI into mild (score from 1 to 5), moderate (from 6 to 12), severe (from 13 to 18), very severe (from 19 to 21) [14]. In this study, we defined moderate/severe UI when the ICIQ-SF score was from 6 to 21. Once the first-trimester ultrasound examination was completed, we performed a 2D pelvic floor ultrasound examination to measure the study parameters and collected the clinical variables. The sonographer was blinded to the questionnaire responses and clinical variables. Finally, participants were invited to a follow-up visit between 32 and 35 weeks’ gestation, when they completed the same ICIQ-SF questionnaire.

We considered the main clinical and ultrasound variables reported in the literature as potential predictors for the onset of moderate-severe UI during pregnancy. The clinical variables assessed were age [15] (quantitative variable); origin [16] (European/other); smoking habit [17] (smoker/non-smoker); BMI [18, 19] (categorized as < 25 (reference category), 25–30, > 30 and also considered as a quantitative variable); educational attainment [19] (primary / secondary / post-secondary).

The ultrasound variables were assessed using Toshiba Xario 200 (TUS-X200) ultrasound equipment. The measurements were taken with the participants in dorsal lithotomy position. We checked that the bladder was empty and measured the study ultrasound variables, firstly, with the pregnant woman in the basal resting position; and after, during the Valsalva maneuver. We measured introital [20] and transperineal [21] urethral mobility [8] (Fig. 1); we also used transperineal ultrasound to measure, at rest and in Valsalva, the retrovesical angle [22, 23], urethral sphincter thickness[24, 25], and urethral length [26, 27]. For these transperineal measurements, we used convex transducer and the probe in the sagittal plane, sustained in between the labia against the pubic symphysis without pressure, to attain the standard sagittal sectioning that showed the pubic bone, the urethra, the vagina, rectum, and anal canal. To measure urethral mobility, we used two types of ultrasound transducers, namely, convex (transperineal mobility, measured with a11C3 Toshiba probe) and intracavitary (introital mobility, measured with a 6C1 Toshiba probe) to integrate the study measurements as much as possible into the routine consultation visit. This allowed the use of one transducer, the most optimal one, for both the pregnancy check and urethral mobility.

To calculate the transperineal urethral mobility we must draw parallel lines through the inferior margin of the symphysis pubis and the internal meatus of the urethra, perpendicular to the incident ultrasound beam and measure the distance between these lines. The difference between these distances at rest and during Valsalva is the transperineal urethral mobility [21]. The introital urethral mobility was the difference between the distance at rest from the point where the pubic symphysis intersects with the urethra –which was the distance from the intersecting point between the urethra and the pubic symphysis axis to the urethro-vesical union following the urethral length- to the bladder neck and that in Valsalva [20]. The retrovesical angle was that between the proximal urethra and the bladder trigon (one third of the bladder base proximal to the urethra) [22]. The urethral sphincter thickness was the distance perpendicular to the urethral length that includes the echogenic area that outlines the urethra on both sides; the thickest part is measured [24]. The urethral length was the (urethral) segment that goes from the contact point with the probe to the urethrovesical union [28]. Additional images to illustrate these reference points and measurements are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

All the obtained data – ultrasound and clinical data, questionnaire results - were collected in an encrypted database and analyzed later. Reliability of the ultrasound measurements was assessed by calculating inter-observer agreement between two different observers in a subsample of 42 women included in the study.

Statistical analysis

A descriptive analysis of the demographic, clinical, and ultrasound variables of the patients included in the study was carried out. Continuous variables were assessed using mean (standard deviation –SD), or median (1st -3rd quartile) where it applied; categorical variables were described as counts (%). The inter-observer agreement was assessed with the intraclass correlation coefficient [29].

The models were developed using the full analysis sample. Candidate predictors were selected based on previous literature or their association with the outcome in univariate logistic regression analyses, with P < 0.20. The multivariable logistic regression models were built using backward selection based on the Akaike Information Criterion, with the aim of deriving parsimonious prediction models. As this is a prediction model, variable retention was guided by predictive performance and model parsimony. As a sensitivity analysis, we used LASSO penalized logistic regression to evaluate whether an alternative variable selection method retained the same predictors.

Associations were reported as odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals. Model discrimination was evaluated using the area under the ROC curve (AUC). Model calibration was assessed using the calibration slope and intercept together with a smoothened (loess) calibration curve. Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling to obtain optimism-corrected performance estimates for the final fixed models [30, 31]. There were no missing data for the predictors or outcome among the participants included in the final analysis; therefore, no data were imputed. Finally, we performed sensitivity analyses of the association of potential predictors with moderate/severe UI, restricted to predicting only stress UI, because this is the most frequent subtype of UI during pregnancy.

All the analyses were carried out using the R version 4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Internal validation and optimism-corrected performance estimates were obtained using the rms package, and the AUC was computed with the pROC package. This study was reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) statement (Supplementary material).

Results

During the period between July 2018 and April 2020, a total of 204 primigravid women were recruited; thirty of them were excluded from the study because they presented moderate/severe UI in the first trimester, leaving 174 women with no UI or a mild form of it in the first trimester of pregnancy. We could not collect data from nine women in their third trimester of pregnancy due to various obstetric complications (two women had a threat of premature delivery, three suffered a miscarriage, and four had a premature deliver). Thirteen other women were also lost to follow-up due to various reasons (four changed health center, two changed their address, two had incorrect identification of data, one had incorrectly collected variables, two only completed the questionnaires partially, and two voluntarily left the study), leaving 152 pregnant women for analysis (Fig. 2).

Table 1 shows the descriptive analysis of the epidemiological variables collected in the first trimester from the group of analyzed pregnant women. Their average age was 29 years old. The majority (74.3%) were women of European origin; 11.2% of North African origin; 6.6% from the Indian subcontinent, 5.3% of sub-Saharan origin; 2% of South American origin; and 0.6% from China. Most women reported secondary education level (46.1%). In total, 20 women were smokers (13.2%). Their mean BMI in the first trimester of pregnancy was 24 and 55% of women had a BMI < 25. With regard to the ultrasound data, we observed that the mean urethral mobility assessed via introital and transperineal routes had similar values (7.9 and 8.6 mm, respectively). The mean urethral length decreased with effort and went from 35.9 to 31.6 mm. Finally, the urethral sphincter thickness and the retrovesical angle increased with effort, from 12.3 to 13.3 and 122 to 136 degrees, respectively (Table 1). We observed similar baseline characteristics between the analyzed population and women lost to follow-up (Supplementary Table S1).

The description of the clinical variables at the third trimester showed mean BMI values of 27, which was an increase of three units compared to the first trimester. Regarding UI, we observed a cumulative incidence (95% CI) of 42.8% (31.5–54.9); 21.7% mild UI and 21.1%, moderate/severe UI; we had 6 candidate predictors and 32 events for model development, which corresponded to an effective events-per-variable ratio of 5.3). As for the type of UI, 80% (52 out of 65 women with IU) of the women with UI in the third trimester had stress urinary incontinence (Supplementary Table S2).

All models were developed using the entire study cohort. Clinical candidate covariates were age, BMI (categorized into three groups), smoking status, and country of origin. Since introital and transperineal urethral mobility were correlated (the Pearson correlation coefficient was 0.54 [95% CI, 0.41–0.64]), the rest of the ultrasound measurements obtained at rest and during Valsalva capture different functional states, and the measurement of these two variables (of urethral mobility) in one person would imply changing the probe and would be impractical from a clinical point of view, we defined four candidate sets based on the ultrasound variables. Backward stepwise selection was applied to each set independently. The univariable associations of all candidate predictors are shown in Supplementary Table S3. The two models with the best performance included: (i) introital urethral mobility and BMI; and (ii) transperineal urethral mobility and BMI (Table 2). The BMI was categorized because the models showed higher predictive performance when BMI was treated as a categorical variable. The LASSO sensitivity analysis selected the same variables as the main modelling strategy; therefore, the final fixed logistic regression models were kept unchanged. The AUC was 0.72 (0.63–0.82) for the model including introital urethral mobility and 0.69 (0.58–0.79) for the model including transperineal urethral mobility. Internal validation by bootstrap resampling (B = [500]) yielded optimism-corrected AUCs of 0.70 (bootstrap 95% interval 0.60–0.80) and 0.66 (bootstrap 95% interval 0.55–0.76), with optimism-corrected calibration slopes of 0.90 (bootstrap 95% interval 0.41–1.80) and 0.88 (bootstrap 95% interval 0.42–1.63) (ideal = 1). Calibration curves are shown in Supplementary Figure S1. For each model, we identified two different cut-off risk points; one of these points sought to enhance sensitivity and the other specificity, while maintaining an acceptable value of the other factor (specificity and sensitivity, respectively). Therefore, we could identify three groups, depending on the value of these probabilities in the model: women at low, medium, or high risk of developing moderate/severe UI, respectively.

The cut-off points for the Introital Urethral Mobility Model-BMI categories were 0.14 (sensitivity 84% and specificity 51%) and 0.27 (sensitivity 50% and specificity 82%). Accordingly, the risk of developing UI for pregnant women was high, moderate, or low when the risk score was above 0.27, between 0.14 and 0.27, and below 0.14, respectively (Supplementary Figure S2); 24% of the participants were over the 0.27 threshold value (at high risk) and 57% over the 0.14 (at moderate or high risk).

Regarding the Transperineal urethral mobility model-BMI categories, the two cut-off points were 0.14 (sensitivity 75% and specificity 43%) and 0.22 (sensitivity 50% and specificity 77%). The risk of developing UI was defined as high, moderate, or low by risk scores above 0.22, between 0.14 and 0.22, and below 0.14, respectively; 30% of the participants were over the 0.22 threshold value, and 61% over the 0.14 (at moderate or high risk) (Supplementary Figure S2). The interobserver agreement was good; it was 0.87 for the transperineal urethral mobility and 0.86 for the introital urethral mobility. The sensitivity analysis to find predictors of moderate/severe stress UI showed similar associations to those in the general analysis (association with moderate/severe UI). The multivariable models were the basis to develop an online application that uses variables collected in the first trimester of pregnancy to predict moderate/severe UI in the third trimester. The online calculator of both models, Introital Urethral Mobility-BMI categories and Transperineal Urethral Mobility-BMI categories estimates an individual risk for each pregnant women, classified as high, medium, or low, based on the cut-off points defined for each model https://blanch-font.github.io/IU/index_calculadora.htmlhttps://htmlpreview.github.io/?https://github.com/Blanch-Font/IU/blob/main/index_calculadora.html (Supplementary Figure S3). This tool is a proof of concept intended for research purposes only.

Discussion

We built two models with variables commonly assessed in clinical practice at the beginning of pregnancy to identify women at high risk of developing moderate or severe UI in the third trimester. The models included ultrasound scan variables, which are crucial but seldom included in this type of predictive tool. The detection of women with high risk of UI at the beginning of pregnancy could facilitate the implementation of preventive measures to reduce its incidence.

Few published studies have explicitly studied the relationship between ultrasound imaging variables and the onset of UI during pregnancy. Most of these studies focused on the assessment of only transperineal urethral mobility and the measurement was at the end of gestation and postpartum [32]. To our knowledge, no previous analyses have measured introital urethral mobility, at any stage of the pregnancy; and no longitudinal analysis has been designed to perform the UI risk assessment at the beginning of pregnancy. Only two previous studies have built predictive models to assess this risk [6]; the design was cross-sectional and included multiparous women and women with UI during pregnancy (thus, in secondary prevention) [33]. We used a prospective cohort design, which is highly adequate for building predictive models. We also excluded multiparous women, because their risk factors are likely different from those in nulliparous women; and women with UI at the first trimester (when the risk factors were considered), to use the models in primary prevention.

Our results agree with other articles that suggest an association of increased transperineal urethral mobility with UI in pregnancy [9, 32, 34]. This increase (in urethral mobility) could be secondary to changes in the urethral support mechanisms caused by hormonal and mechanical factors inherent to pregnancy [35]. In this regard, the contractile properties of the urethral musculature may explain the (expected) shorter urethral length during Valsalva observed in our study, compared to that at rest [36]. We observed an association of introital as well as transperineal urethral mobility with UI; but no other ultrasound variables had enough predictive value to enter the model. Regarding the clinical variables, we observed a relation between the BMI of pregnant women in the first trimester and the onset of moderate/severe UI in the third trimester (OR 1.09; IC: 1,02 − 1,16). The subgroup of pregnant women with a BMI > 30 represented 15.1% of the pregnant women analyzed in the first trimester and their risk of developing a moderate/severe UI in the third trimester increased fourfold. The association between BMI and UI is a highlight in most of the analyses that have examined it [37, 38]. Some studies have suggested that increased BMI is associated with the presence of UI during pregnancy and a risk factor for its persistence in the postpartum period [3].

Strengths and limitations

One of the strengths of this study is the prospective cohort design, the most appropriate design for the analysis of predictor variables because it examines the same group of pregnant women during the first and the third trimesters of the pregnancy. Another important strength was the blinding of the sonographer to the answers of the questionnaire and the clinical variables when the ultrasound measures were taken. We also acknowledge some limitations. First, we used non-probabilistic consecutive sampling. However, 98.6% of all women invited to participate in the study accepted the invitation, reducing the likelihood of selection bias; moreover, the population served by Hospital de Santa Caterina is heterogeneous. Second, a non-negligible proportion of participants (12.6%) were lost to follow-up. However, baseline characteristics were similar between participants included in the analysis and those lost to follow-up, suggesting that attrition was unlikely to have substantially affected the results. Third, the number of events per variable was lower than expected; it was 5.3, below the commonly recommended threshold of 10–15 events-per-variable ratio which reduced the statistical power of the models, potentially limiting our ability to detect variables associated with UI, and increased the risk of unstable regression coefficients and model overfitting. The overall performance of the model should be interpreted with caution and consider both the discrimination and calibration. We acknowledge wide confidence intervals observed for the AUC estimates and the calibration slopes. The calibration results and internal validation (using bootstrap) showed similar values before and after optimism correction for both the AUC and the calibration slopes; however, the confidence intervals remained wide. Although calibration performance appeared reasonable, the small sample size limited the precision of the estimate. Finally, the generalizability of our models needs to be analyzed through external validation; clinical applicability and performance across different populations should also be examined in future multicenter studies. Consequently, the calculator should be considered a preliminary research tool and should not be used for clinical decision-making until its performance has been confirmed in independent populations.

Implications and relevance

The creation of models to predict UI is important because the incidence of this condition during pregnancy is considerable. After external validation, our models may be particularly relevant in populations with an incidence of moderate/severe UI during pregnancy comparable to that in our study population (21.1%). We found that one in five pregnant women with no moderate/severe UI at the beginning of the pregnancy will develop this condition in the third trimester. In contrast with the high number of reports on UI prevalence, studies on the incidence of this condition are scarce [39].

The models we have developed aim to optimize efforts and resources for UI prevention by targeting women at highest risk. Moreover, women who know their risk are more likely to adherence to these programs, enhancing their effectiveness [40]. Primary prevention strategies include intensive pelvic floor training directed by specialized professionals [41]; pregnancy-specific weight-control interventions supervised by nutritionists, which involve revision of dietary habits, promotion and education on physical activity, as recommended in the guidelines [42]. Supplementary Figure S4 presents a hypothetical framework illustrating how the models could be used to classify pregnant women according to their risk of UI and, potentially, guide tailored prevention programs. Risk calculation could be carried out within the first obstetrical ultrasound scan visit–at approximately 12 weeks’ gestation. Of note, any application of the calculator to guide a first-trimester primary prevention program for UI should be regarded as a proposal and would require evaluation in a clinical study before its effectiveness can be established.

Conclusions

One in five pregnant women developed moderate/severe UI in the third trimester of pregnancy. The design and building of two multivariable models (introital urethral mobility-BMI categories and transperineal urethral mobility-BMI categories) could help estimate, at the beginning of the pregnancy, the individual risk of developing moderate/severe UI in the third trimester. These results may provide a foundation for the future development of risk stratified approaches to primary prevention.
