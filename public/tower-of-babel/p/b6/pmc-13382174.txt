A stealth delivery platform for B cell-based immunity
Authors: Grant A Knappe
Published: 2026

Main text

A key goal in modern vaccine development is the design of vaccines that focus an immune response to produce broadly neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs) and minimize other non-neutralizing antibody responses.1 This goal is often achieved by using protein carriers, which multivalently display antigens on nanoparticulate scaffolds to improve vaccine trafficking and B cell activation, while also including T cell epitopes within the carrier to support germinal center reactions.2 This technology, termed protein-based virus-like particles (P-VLPs), underpins the success of multiple marketed vaccines like Gardasil (Merck). While these protein carriers provide productive T cell epitopes, they also contain repetitive arrays of B cell epitopes. The roles of these carrier-derived B cell epitopes remain unclear, despite their discovery in the 1980s3 and subsequent intensive investigation.

To study the effects of carrier-derived immune responses, Romanov et al. recently reported in Science4 on a “stealth” VLP platform that removes carrier-derived B cell and T cell responses. The platform, termed DNA-based virus-like particles (D-VLPs), multivalently scaffolds antigens on a nanoparticulate display comprised of DNA, which is a tolerized, thymus-independent antigen that is expected to generate no B cell or T cell responses (Figure 1). Previously, this technology was reported to only generate antibodies against the target antigen, not the DNA carrier, in a SARS-CoV-2 antigen system.5 Yet, it remained unclear what the removal of anti-carrier responses meant for the immune response. Romanov et al. hypothesized the carrier-directed responses diluted productive immune responses, and removing this response with DNA carriers would enhance antigen-directed B cell development. This platform and hypothesis were tested in the context of HIV vaccine design, where subdominant bnAb responses are difficult to generate, and well-developed animal models and clinical-stage P-VLPs6 exist for rigorous testing.

Comparing protein-based and DNA-based VLPs

Protein-based VLPs, a modality used in multiple marketed vaccines, produce antibody responses against the antigen (blue) and protein carrier (red). In contrast, DNA-based VLPs only produce antibodies against the antigen (blue), as DNA (gray) is a tolerized, thymus-independent antigen, while critically maintaining multivalent, nanoparticulate display of the antigen. This difference leads to germinal center reactions that are comprised of more of the desired B cells. This new technology could enable studies in fundamental immunology while opening new avenues for translation.

Romanov et al. discovered D-VLPs traffic to draining lymph nodes after subcutaneous administration but remain at subcapsular sinuses which are populated with macrophages, key gatekeepers of lymph nodes that decide which materials from the draining lymph get transferred to follicular dendritic cells (FDCs) for subsequent germinal center (GC) reactions. This contrasted with the comparator P-VLP, which robustly accumulated in FDC networks. This difference in trafficking led to a substantial decline in GC B cell responses to the D-VLP compared to the P-VLP.

Romanov et al. tested different possible mechanisms to account for this difference in trafficking, including nuclease degradation, macrophage sequestration, and complement deposition, which was ultimately identified as the key mechanism regulating trafficking. In biochemical assays, D-VLPs acquired substantially less mannose-binding lectin (MBL) and complement C3 compared to the P-VLP. These innate immune complement components were previously found to be critical for certain P-VLPs to traffic to FDCs; this previous work further demonstrated that glycan density was a key design parameter governing MBL-driven complement deposition.7,8 Romanov et al. rationally re-designed the D-VLP to increase the antigen density—by decreasing the VLP diameter and increasing the antigen valency—and found this led to complement deposition comparable to the P-VLP. The re-designed D-VLP trafficked to FDCs, and generated a substantially higher proportion of antigen-specific GC B cells than the P-VLP. This experiment provided preliminary evidence that the fraction of antigen-specific GC B cells was negatively affected by protein-based carriers.

However, the total GC size remained substantially smaller for the D-VLP group. Romanov et al. hypothesized that while removing carrier-derived B cell epitopes was a positive design principle, removing carrier-derived T cell epitopes was detrimental to the immune response. The HIV antigen was re-engineered to include a potent synthetic T cell epitope that is broadly presented across many mouse and human MHC alleles. The inclusion of this one T cell epitope in the antigen led to substantially higher GC size, and the D-VLP group generated more antigen-specific GC B cells than the clinical-stage P-VLP.

Romanov et al. then tested this optimized D-VLP formulation in a humanized mouse model that models the germline B cell population of a key class of HIV bnAbs, VRC01.9 D-VLP or P-VLP was administered and antigen-specific GC B cells were sorted for sequencing to ask: does the removal of carrier-directed B cell responses improve the priming of bnAb responses? Single-cell B cell receptor sequencing revealed D-VLPs successfully primed these bnAb precursor B cells in conditions where the clinical-stage P-VLP failed to. These data supported the conclusion that D-VLPs, which remove carrier-directed B cell responses, prime antigen-focused germinal centers that are composed of the desired precursor B cells for bnAb maturation.

Several questions remain about this new platform. When, where, and how D-VLPs biodegrade via extracellular and intracellular nucleases will guide further performance improvements and safety analyses. How antigen-focused germinal centers differentially lead to broad and durable humoral memory, compared to typical germinal centers primed by P-VLPs, remains a critical open question for eventual translation. While not observed thus far, D-VLPs have the potential to generate anti-DNA responses that could lead to autoimmunity; if, when, and how these responses may emerge must be carefully studied non-clinically and clinically. Further, additional capabilities remain to be investigated, including the potential for formulating complex antigens like glycoprotein trimers (which P-VLPs often fail to accomplish) and for formulating nucleic acid-based adjuvants against pathogen recognition receptors into the D-VLPs.

Long-term, the stealth nature of the D-VLP platform may be used by immunologists and vaccinologists to study critical basic questions surrounding carrier-induced epitope suppression3 and antibody-mediated feedback.10 Translationally, this platform could enable B cell immunity against various difficult-to-target epitopes, ranging from bnAb sites on viral antigens, to self-antigens like amyloid-beta and PCSK9, to small molecules like nicotine and nerve agents. As patient sample sequencing, AI-guided antigen design, and preventive medicine converge in coming years, the next-generation of vaccines may continue to be VLPs, harnessing this stealth delivery platform to instruct the immune system to produce the right antibody to the right target.
