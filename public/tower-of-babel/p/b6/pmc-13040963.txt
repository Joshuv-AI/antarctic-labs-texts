Frontotemporal cortical thinning and insular surface area reduction in schizophrenia with predominant negative symptoms
Authors: Jizhen Li, Kaili Zhang, Qiancheng Wang, Xiaoran Lv, Jiaxing Xie, Yumei Wan, Mingming Zhao, Xinyan Zhang, Xiaoxiao Shan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious studies have revealed brain structural deficits in patients with schizophrenia displaying predominant negative symptoms, but results remain inconsistent. The present study aimed to investigate patterns of cortical structure, including cortical thickness (CTh), grey matter volume (GMV) and surface area (SA) in schizophrenia with predominant negative symptoms and their correlations with clinical features.MethodsStructural magnetic resonance imaging was performed on 68 patients with schizophrenia displaying predominant negative symptoms and 56 healthy controls (HCs). The FreeSurfer software was used to analyze the imaging data and the support vector machine (SVM) method was used for pattern classification.ResultsCompared to HCs, patients showed reduced SA in right insular, and reduced CTh in the right parstriangularis cortex, left fusiform cortex and left precentral cortex compared to HCs. No significant correlation was observed between abnormal cortical structure of the patients with clinical parameters. SVM revealed that the CTh in the fusiform cortex could distinguish patients from HCs with values above 0.6 for sensitivity, specificity and accuracy.ConclusionsThe abnormal cortical structure in frontotemporal and insula regions might reflect the inherent pathological processes of schizophrenia, which are independent of the severity of negative symptoms. The CTh in the fusiform cortex could serve as underlying imaging markers to discriminate patients with schizophrenia from HCs.Trial registrationRegistered with ChiCTR. Introduction

Schizophrenia is a serious chronic disabling mental disorder, characterized by positive symptoms, negative symptoms and cognitive impairment. Negative symptoms have been considered a central feature of schizophrenia and are associated with poor functional outcomes [1]. Compared with positive symptoms, negative symptoms respond poorly to antipsychotic drugs [2, 3], indicating that negative symptoms have different pathological mechanisms from positive symptoms [4, 5]. However, relatively little is known about their neurobiological basis.

Most structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) studies on negative symptoms have examined gray matter volume (GMV) rather than cortical thickness (CTh). Previous studies have revealed that negative symptoms are linked to GMV alterations in the prefrontal cortex, temporal cortex, and limbic regions [6, 7]. In recent years, the surface-based morphometry method for measuring regional CTh seems to be more sensitive to detailedly evaluate the brain cortex anatomy than the voxel-based morphometry method [8, 9]. Prior studies have shown CTh alterations in schizophrenia, but the detected anomalous brain regions were inconsistent. Some studies have reported reduced CTh in the inferior frontal gyrus [10], temporal cortex [11, 12], anterior cingulate cortex [13], and insula [14] compared to healthy controls (HCs). Conversely, other studies have reported an increase in cingulate cortex thickness in schizophrenia [15, 16]. A study by Demjaha et al. revealed that predominant negative symptoms were correlated with reduced CTh in the left superior temporal gyrus (STG) and greater CTh in the left lateral orbitofrontal gyrus [17]. A meta-analysis of CTh in schizophrenia found a correlation between negative symptoms and thinning in the left orbitofrontal gyrus, medial orbitofrontal cortex, and pars opercularis [18]. However, Yuan et al. found that there was no correlation between negative symptoms and CTh [19].

These inconsistent findings may be attributed to several factors, such as clinical characteristics, sample size, and analytical methods, which differ across these studies. For example, FreeSurfer is regarded as the “gold standard” of surface-based measurement and is more accurate than the Computational Anatomy Toolbox (CAT12) used for volume-based measurement [20]. Moreover, a small sample size might limit the ability to detect CTh differences. Although some previous studies have explored the cortical conditions of patients with schizophrenia, it is still unclear whether cortical abnormalities are related to negative symptoms [21–23]. To our knowledge, few cortical exploration has been conducted in sample of patients with predominant negative symptoms. Therefore, it is necessary to further investigate the underlying brain mechanisms associated with negative symptoms.

There are various pattern recognition techniques, such as support vector machine (SVM), which can be used to classify psychiatric disorders based on neuroanatomical biomarkers. A SVM is a powerful machine learning algorithm that excels in the construction of classification models. Its goal is to effectively classify data by finding an optimal decision boundary between two classes, namely the hyperplane, and then predict labels from feature vectors. Its positioning form is as far away from the nearest data point of each class as possible, so that data can be accurately classified and empirical classification errors may be reduced. These closest points are called support vectors [24, 25]. SVM analysis has been widely used to differentiate patients with schizophrenia [26], major depressive disorder [27], and bipolar disorder [28] from HCs. However, it is not clear whether abnormal CTh can be used as a reliable underlying brain imaging biomarker to differentiate patients with schizophrenia displaying predominant negative symptoms from HCs by SVM.

In the present study, we aimed to examine abnormalities of cortical structure, including CTh, GMV and surface area (SA), in relatively large samples of patients with schizophrenia displaying predominant negative symptoms using the FreeSurfer software to analyze the imaging data. Most of the patients recruited were those with chronic schizophrenia, and the negative symptoms were relatively prominent. The duration of illness was mostly controlled within 5–8 years. A total of 68 patients with schizophrenia displaying predominant negative symptoms and 56 HCs were recruited in this study. We hypothesized that patients with schizophrenia displaying predominant negative symptoms may have significantly decreased CTh, GMV or SA in some brain regions, particularly in the frontal and temporal cortical regions. These brain structural alterations were also predicted to be correlated with negative symptoms and cognitive features in patients with schizophrenia.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study recruited a total of 68 patients with schizophrenia displaying predominant negative symptoms aged 18–49 years admitted as inpatients at the Shandong Mental Health Center (Jinan, China). Patients were diagnosed by two psychiatrists based on the Structural Clinical Interview for Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5). The inclusion criteria for patients were the following: (1) met DSM-5 criteria for schizophrenia; (2) the negative symptom subscale scores of the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) ≥ 20 points, and at least one item of the PANSS negative symptom subscale was ≥ 4 points [29]; (3) mental symptoms were stable for>4 weeks; (4) no obvious positive symptoms, agitation, or behavioral disorders; PANSS positive symptom subscale was < 20 points, and each single positive item was < 4 points [30]; (5) no history of treatment resistance. The subjects were currently on antipsychotic medications and neither drug-naïve nor had they discontinued their medications. Patients with a comorbidity of schizoaffective disorder were excluded as per the diagnostic criteria. Clinical symptoms were assessed using the PANSS, Hamilton Anxiety Scale-14 (HAMA-14) [31, 32], and Hamilton Depression Scale-17 (HAMD-17) [33, 34]. The cognitive function was assessed by the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS), a 12-component scale that assesses function in multiple cognitive areas, such as attention, speech, memory, and visual span.

A total of 56 HCs unrelated to the patients were recruited by advertisements, and were matched with patients by sex, and age. Any potential HC was not recruited if a first-degree relative had a history of psychiatric disorder.

The exclusion criteria for all participants were the following: (1) other psychiatric disorders based on DSM-5; (2) current or past serious organic brain diseases, physical illness, such as epilepsy, cardiovascular, liver and kidney diseases; (3) a history of substance abuse or psychoactive substance use; (4) mental retardation; (5) pregnancy; and (6) patients with contraindications to MRI scanning.

To minimize the impact of psychotropic medications on the brain structure, the patients recruited were all those who were taking atypical antipsychotics, including quetiapine (QTP), risperidone (RIS), amisulpride (ASP), paliperidone (PP), olanzapine (OLZ), clozapine (Cloz), and aripiprazole (ARP). Other psychotropic medications, such as mood stabilizers or antidepressants, were not allowed in the past 1 months. Moreover, patients had received mood stabilizers or antidepressants for < 6 months lifetime contact to minimize the potential impact on the brain structure. The details of the antipsychotic medications regimens were recorded. In the patient groups, 28 patients were treated with single antipsychotics (including ARP 3, RIS 3, ASP 4, PP 2, OLZ 10, and Cloz 6), The remaining 34 patients were treated with the combination of two antipsychotics (i.e., QTP + ARP 1; RIS + ARP 2; OLZ + RIS 13; ASP + OLZ 3; ASP + ARP 4; ASP + PP 2; OLZ + PP 1; Cloz + ASP 6; PP+Cloz 2).

The ethics committee of the Shandong Mental Health Center approved the study and its execution according with the Helsinki Declaration. Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects after a full explanation.

Acquisition and preprocessing of MRI data

MRI was performed using a 3.0 Tesla uMR® 790 MRI scanner (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China) to acquire images at the Shandong Mental Health Center (Jinan, China). The participants were asked to lie down, close their eyes and remain still, but awake. Noise canceling earplugs were provided to decrease noise during the scan. The scanning parameters of the structural image data were as follows: field of view (FOV) = 256 × 256 mm, repetition time (TR) = 6.7 ms, flip Angle = 8◦, matrix size = 256 × 256, voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3, echo time (TE) = 2.9 ms, slice thickness = 1 mm, number of slices = 192, gap = 0 mm.

The structural data preprocessing was executed using FreeSurfer software. FreeSurfer is a software suite widely used for processing and analyzing structural and functional neuroimaging data. It provides automated tools for cortical reconstruction, subcortical segmentation, surface-based registration, and quantitative brain mapping, enabling robust neuroanatomical studies in both research and clinical settings [35].

The detailed description of data preprocessing for CTh, GMV and SA is provided in the Supplemental Methods.

Statistical analysis

Demographic and clinical data were analyzed by a Chi-square test or two-sample t-tests when necessary.

The differences in CTh, GMV and SA between patients with schizophrenia and HCs were compared using a general linear model (GLM), with total intracranial volume, age, years of education, and sex as covariates of no interest. The significance level was set at P < 0.05 and corrected based on the Permutation Test (Vertex-wise/cluster-forming threshold: P < 0.0001, cluster-wise significance: P < 0.05, permutations:5000) for multiple comparisons with the FreeSurfer software.

Correlation analyses

Partial correlation analysis was performed to analyze the correlations between abnormal cortical structure and clinical parameters with correcting the confounding factors, such as age, gender, years of education, illness duration and chlorpromazine equivalents dose, with threshold of P < 0.05. Bonferroni correction was performed for multiple comparisons.

Classification analysis

SVM was used to test the possibility of the extracted CTh values in abnormal brain regions to discriminate patients from controls with the LIBrary for SVM (LIBSVM) software package (http://www.csie.ntu.edu.tw/~cjlin/libsvm/) in MATLAB (MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA). The “leave-one-out” cross-validation method was used in this study, which is well established method for small-sized sample studies [36]. The detailed description of the SVM analysis steps was offered in Supplemental methods.

Results

A total of 68 patients and 56 HCs were enrolled in this study. However, six patients who failed the screening were excluded (including 3 who could not persist and another 3 who showed excessive head movements). A total of 62 patients and 56 HCs were included in the final analysis. There were no significant differences in sex ratios, and age between the groups. The years of education was significantly lower in patients with schizophrenia than in HCs. Correlation analysis

The thinning CTh in the right parstriangularis (r = -0.395, p = 0.002) was negatively correlated with HAMA scores (Fig. 3). However, no significant correlations were found after the Bonferroni correction. We detected no correlations among abnormal CTh, SA and PANSS/cognition parameter scores, illness duration, and medication dosage of the patients. In addition, CTh/SA whole-brain correlations of negative symptoms were not significant based on the Desikan-Killiany atlas, which encompassed 68 brain regions.

Discussion

The findings of this study revealed that patients with schizophrenia displaying predominant negative symptoms had lower SA in right insula, reduced CTh in the right parstriangularis cortex, left fusiform cortex and left precentral cortex compared to the HCs. Moreover, the results of the SVM analyses showed that the CTh values in the fusiform cortex might serve as a potential imaging marker to distinguish patients with schizophrenia displaying predominant negative symptoms from HCs.

The frontal cortex region has explicit effects on cognitive, emotional and self-awareness mnemonic activities [37], and is one of the main regions involved in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia [38]. The thinning of the CTh in middle frontal gyrus have been reported in patients with first-episode schizophrenia, suggesting its early occurrence during the onset of the disease [39]. Norichika et al. found that compared with control group, the bilateral parstriangularis volume of ultra-high risk for psychosis and first-episode schizophrenia subjects was significantly reduced, and indicate that the reduction in local GMV in bilateral parstriangularis is more likely to lead to schizophrenia [40]. Yoichiro et al. found patients with schizophrenia had thinner cortices in the left precentral and paracentral gyri than those with schizotypal disorder, suggesting the differences in the clinical symptoms of these diseases [23]. Consistent with previous findings, our results revealed reduced CTh in the right parstriangularis, left precentral cortex in patients with schizophrenia displaying predominant negative symptoms, but no correlations were found between abnormal CTh and PANSS/cognition parameter scores of patients, suggesting that these defects might reflect the neurodevelopmental basis or the endophenotype of schizophrenia.

Numerous neuroimaging studies have revealed temporal lobe changes in schizophrenia. In this study, we also found a significant decreased in the CTh of the fusiform gyrus in patients with schizophrenia displaying predominant negative symptoms. The temporal gyrus, including the fusiform gyrus, is involved in the working memory and emotion processing, specifically negative emotions as revealed by some studies of facial emotion perception [41, 42]. Compared with non-persistent negative symptoms patients, patients with persistent negative symptoms had more significant and extensive structural changes in the prefrontal, temporal, limbic, and subcortical regions [43]. Abnormities in the fusiform gyrus are associated with deficits in emotional and visual processing of faces [44]. Moreover, we detected reduced SA of insular in the patient group, with no significant changes in the GMV or CTh of insular. This thickness-surface area dissociation pattern suggested that the pathology of the insula may mainly originate in the early stages of neural development [45]. The cortical SA was mainly determined by the proliferation and migration of neural progenitor cells during the embryonic stage, whereas the cortical thickness reflects the synaptic pruning and myelination processes from birth to adolescence [46]. As one of the earliest folded cortical regions, the SA abnormality in the insular may reflect specific disturbances in the early cortical organization program.

Previous studies directly comparing GMV and CTh in patients with chronic schizophrenia found that the patterns of cortical changes were detected differently by these two indicators. These differences may be related to the surface area, gray-white matter contrast, and cortical curvature, suggesting that CTh and GMV reflect different pathological physiological processes [47]. CTh reflects the microscopic structure of the cortical column, whereas the gray matter volume is the result of the combined effect of thickness and surface area [48]. Negative symptoms may be more specifically related to the microstructural integrity of the cortical columns represented by CTh. On the other hand, GMV, as an indicator that combines thickness and surface area, is more affected by individual variations [47]. We detected no significant GMV abnormalities in the patients’ group relative to those in the HCs, suggesting that different morphological indicators show different dimensions of the pathological basis of the disease. The brain structural basis of schizophrenia (especially negative symptoms) may require exploration through multimodal and more refined indicators.

Although our study focused on the structural correlates of predominantly negative symptoms, the literature suggests functional changes associated with predominantly negative symptoms. Evidence from a systematic review indicates relationships between negative symptoms and the functional connectivity of multiple resting-state networks, including the frontoparietal, pericentral /somatomotor, midcingulo-insular, and occipital networks [49]. Gao et al. reported that the severity of negative symptoms could be predicted through a comprehensive neural network, encompassing regions from the frontoparietal, medial frontal, sensorimotor, default mode, subcortical, and cerebellum networks [50]. In addition, fronto-temporal connectivity was significantly associated with the severity of experiential negative symptoms across both independent patient samples, suggesting that this circuit is a key neural substrate for schizophrenia [51]. Millan et al. also proposed that negative symptoms could be linked to abnormalities within medial prefrontal and temporal networks [52]. These past reports suggested that the disruption of the neural function network may reflect the complicated neural signatures underlying negative symptoms.

SVMs have been widely used in biomedical diagnosis. Previous studies have shown that specificity or sensitivity less than 0.6 might indicate that the diagnostic indicators are poor [53, 54]. Xiao et al. achieved accuracies of 85.0 and 81.8% for distinguishing drug-naïve first-episode patients with schizophrenia from controls based on surface area and CTh, respectively, using SVM analysis [55]. Koch et al. identify patients with schizophrenia with 93% accuracy and were able to predict the severity of the negative symptoms of the patients based on the patterns of ventricular striatal activation [56]. The SVM results in the present study showed that the accuracy, specificity and sensitivity of the CTh values in the left fusiform were greater than 0.6 in distinguishing patients with schizophrenia from HCs. Thus, the CTh values in left fusiform region might serve as potential imaging marker to distinguish patients with schizophrenia displaying predominant negative symptoms from control subjects.

Previous studies have shown the associations between abnormal brain structure and negative symptoms in patients with schizophrenia. In this study, we found that the decreased CTh values in the right parstriangularis were negatively correlated with HAMA scores. However, the correlation was no significant after Bonferroni correction. Besides, no correlations were revealed between abnormal CTh, SA and PANSS/cognition parameter scores of patients. A possibility is that abnormal CTh or SA may be a characteristic change in patients with schizophrenia, unrelated to clinical symptom severity or illness duration. For instance, a meta-analysis detected cortical thinning in some brain regions in clinical high-risk (CHR) cases and first episode of psychosis (FEP) individuals when compared with that in HCs. Nevertheless, the CTh of FEP did not show any further significant reduction when compared with CHR, implying possible significant cortical thinning during the high-risk stage or the first episode [57]. CTh might reflect the neurodevelopmental basis or the endophenotype of schizophrenia rather than acting as a rapidly changing state indicator with illness duration or medication dosage. Another possibility is that the sample size of this study may have limited the statistical power relating to the detection of minor effects.

Several limitations of this study need to be considered. First, this is a cross-sectional study, limiting our ability to inspect whether cortical structure could predict negative symptoms. Thus, future longitudinal studies are needed to explore this possibility. Second, previous structural MRI studies on cortical thickness for schizophrenia typically had a relatively small sample size, mostly ranging from 20 to 80 [39, 57, 58], and some of them did not distinguish among the symptoms of schizophrenia. Our study has a moderate sample size, with 62 patients of schizophrenia displaying negative symptoms. Moreover, in this study, the statistical analysis was rigorously corrected based on the Permutation Test with P < 0.0001, demonstrating a certain statistical power. Hence, the results are relatively stable and reliable. Thirdly, the patients were on antipsychotic medications, which may have contributed to a potential bias on the outcome. Fourth, the PANSS-negative subscale contains relatively few items and has limited discriminative power across different dimensions of negative symptoms. Future studies should incorporate dedicated negative‑symptom assessment tools (e.g., BNSS, CAINS) to capture clinical characteristics and changes in negative symptoms more comprehensively. Finally, no correlations were detected between abnormal CTh, SA, and negative symptoms, which may be related to several other factors, such as smoking and medication. Despite attempting to control for these confounding factors, their potential impact on the brain structure may have blurred the clarity of the association between the effects of the disease and the symptoms.

Conclusion

In summary, this study revealed reduced SA in the insula as well as thinning CTh in the frontotemporal regions, particularly in the precentral cortex, parstriangularis, and fusiform, in patients with schizophrenia showing negative symptoms. These abnormalities of the cortical structure may reflect those related to disease diagnosis, although the relationship between them and the dimensions of clinical symptoms is complex and may be modulated by other factors. The thinning CTh in the fusiform region may serve as a possible imaging marker to distinguish patients with schizophrenia displaying predominant negative symptoms from HCs. Future studies using longitudinal designs and large sample sizes as well as strict inclusion criteria are needed to further establish these brain regions as possible targets for the development of new schizophrenia treatments.
