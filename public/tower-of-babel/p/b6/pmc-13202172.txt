Association of early-life human rhinovirus and respiratory syncytial virus infections with childhood asthma: a cohort study in Suzhou, China
Authors: Longsong Li, Kaiyue Shen, Qinghui Chen, Liling Chen, Youyi Zhang, Ran Peng, Jianmei Tian, Genming Zhao, Tao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate the impact of early-life human rhinovirus (HRV) and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infections on subsequent asthma development among children with acute respiratory infections (ARI), with a focus on the timing of infection during critical developmental windows.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingTertiary paediatric hospital—Children’s Hospital of Soochow University in eastern China—with data linked to a regional health information system.ParticipantsA total of 2628 children who were hospitalised with acute respiratory infections (ARI) and received respiratory virus testing between September 2017 and December 2024 were included in this study.Primary and secondary outcome measuresThe primary outcome was incident asthma. Associations between early-life HRV or RSV infection and asthma risk were evaluated using univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models. Causal mediation analysis was applied to examine potential mediation by wheezing and bronchiolitis. Secondary outcomes were the frequency of asthma-related medical visits and number of exacerbations, analysed using multivariable negative binomial regression models.ResultsOverall, 616 (20.2%) children developed asthma. Cox regression showed that HRV-RSV (aHR=2.40, 95% CI 1.02 to 6.69) and s-HRV (aHR=1.56, 95% CI 1.10 to 2.22) were associated with asthma risk compared with negative controls, whereas s-RSV was not (aHR=1.31, 95% CI 0.89 to 1.89). Wheezing mediated 53.5% of the effect of HRV on asthma risk. Among asthma cases, both HRV and RSV were associated with increased asthma-related visits and exacerbations.ConclusionsEarly-life hospitalisation for HRV or RSV, particularly at 13–24 months of age, may be associated with increased risk of asthma and greater asthma morbidity. These findings suggest a potential role of infection timing in shaping long-term respiratory outcomes.

Introduction

Asthma is a common chronic non-communicable disease characterised by recurrent wheezing, shortness of breath, airway hyperresponsiveness and variable airflow limitation. According to the Global Asthma Network Phase I study, over 10% of school-aged children worldwide report recurrent wheezing, indicating a high and uneven global burden of asthma.1 A systematic analysis involving 220 studies from 88 countries estimated that, in 2019, the global prevalence of asthma among children aged 5–9 years was 17.79% and up to 23.35% in those aged 10–14 years.2 In recent years, the prevalence of childhood asthma in China has shown a significant upward trend as well. A 2023 meta-analysis covering 31 provinces found an overall prevalence of 2.20%, peaking at 2.98% among children aged 6–14 years. The prevalence rose from 0.69% in 1984 to 5.30% in 2021, with projections estimating 9.8% by 2050.3 4

The occurrence and development of asthma are influenced by multiple factors, including genetics, environment, and pathogen infections. Viral infections are established contributors to asthma exacerbations and have been studied for their potential role in the pathogenesis of asthma in early life.5 A nationwide cohort study in South Korea found that early childhood respiratory viral infections, particularly human rhinovirus (HRV) and respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), were associated with new-onset asthma and subsequent asthma worsening compared with uninfected children.6 However, a study indicated that the risk of asthma might be more closely linked to the frequency of respiratory infections than to the specific type of virus, suggesting that the overall infection burden and host susceptibility could be more significant factors than specific pathogens.7 Whether early-life infections impose an additional risk for asthma in children with ARI remains uncertain and requires further investigation.

To address this knowledge gap, based on the systematic surveillance study carried out at Children’s Hospital of Soochow University (SCH) during 2017 to 2022, we employed a cohort study design to assess the association between early-life HRV and RSV infection and asthma development. We enrolled children under 2 years old admitted with acute respiratory infection (ARI) and without a prior history of asthma. We investigated their HRV and RSV infections during admission and followed up on subsequent asthma development or exacerbations through the Suzhou Municipal Health Information Center (SHC). We applied survival analysis, causal mediation analysis and negative binomial regression to compare asthma risk in children with early-life HRV and RSV infections versus controls.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cohort study was based on data from the severe acute respiratory infection (SARI) surveillance system,8 conducted at the Department of Respiratory, Children’s Hospital of Soochow University (SCH). Since 2011, the SARI surveillance study has prospectively enrolled hospitalised children aged ≤5 years who presented with respiratory symptoms within 7 days prior to admission. From September 2016 onwards, more comprehensive surveillance was implemented on all acute respiratory infection (ARI) children.

We conducted a cohort study to investigate long-term respiratory outcomes among young children hospitalised with ARI between 12 September 2017 and 31 July 2022. Children were finally included in the study cohort if they met the following criteria: (1) admitted to SCH during the study period; (2) aged ≤2 years at the time of admission; (3) no ARI-related hospitalisation in the preceding 30 days; and (4) symptom onset within 7 days prior to admission. We excluded children with a prior diagnosis of asthma or those without valid pathogen testing results.

For each child, the first eligible hospital admission during the study period was defined as the index hospitalisation. Only this admission was used to classify viral exposure status and to collect baseline clinical characteristics.

Baseline information collection

Baseline clinical data on the early-life admissions were collected through questionnaires and physical examinations in the ARI surveillance study. The baseline data, measured prior to the occurrence of asthma, included (1) sociodemographic characteristics, such as sex, age, birth weight and breastfeeding status; and (2) clinical characteristics, including symptoms (such as fever, cough, wheezing, shortness of breath and respiratory rate), complications and treatment modalities (oxygen therapy, mechanical ventilation and ICU admission). Wheezing and bronchiolitis documented during the index hospitalisation were treated as baseline clinical characteristics and were not included in the outcome definition. Missing values for covariates were handled using multiple imputation with five datasets under the fully conditional specification method, implemented using the mice package in R.

We assessed the clinical severity through a composite scoring system covering five clinical dimensions: behaviour, respiratory status, oxygen saturation, oxygen therapy requirements and hospitalisation duration. Modifications to the original scoring criteria are detailed in online supplemental table S2. Each dimension was scored from 0 to 3, with higher scores indicating more severe illness.

Detection of respiratory viruses and the included cohort members

During the surveillance study, nasopharyngeal aspirates were collected within 24 hours of admission and sent to the hospital’s laboratory for detecting HRV, bocavirus (BoV) and human metapneumovirus (HMPV) by real-time PCR, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), adenovirus (ADV) and parainfluenza virus 1–3 (PIV-1–3) by direct immunofluorescence assay (DFA) using the D3 Ultra kit (Diagnostic Hybrids, Athens, Ohio, USA). Influenza A and Influenza B viruses (IFV) were detected by reverse transcription PCR.9

Only children less than 2 years old with a positive rhinovirus test were enrolled and assigned to the HRV group, while those with only RSV positive were placed in the RSV group. The HRV-RSV group included children with simultaneous detection of both HRV and RSV. The control group consisted of children who tested negative for all respiratory viruses, including HRV, BoV, HMPV, RSV, ADV, PIV-1-3 and IFV.

Follow-up and outcome measures

The enrolled children were followed up from 2017 to 2024 to identify asthma through the SHC. This system integrates medical data from nearly all healthcare institutions in Suzhou, enabling comprehensive capture of clinical diagnoses from medical visits. According to the standard clinical procedures,10 asthma was defined as either the following:

Physician-diagnosed asthma: ICD-10 codes J45.X or J46.X, or a recorded diagnosis of ‘asthma’ in the electronic medical record11.

Physician-diagnosed asthma: ICD-10 codes J45.X or J46.X, or a recorded diagnosis of ‘asthma’ in the electronic medical record11.

Recurrent wheezing: ≥3 separate wheezing diagnoses recorded at different time points. Wheezing at the index hospitalisation was not included in this definition.

Recurrent wheezing: ≥3 separate wheezing diagnoses recorded at different time points. Wheezing at the index hospitalisation was not included in this definition.

Diagnoses were identified using ICD-10 codes or corresponding diagnostic terms documented in the clinical records.

To evaluate the disease burden among children diagnosed with asthma, two indicators were used: (1) the frequency of asthma-related medical visits, encompassing both exacerbations and non-exacerbation visits; and (2) the number of asthma exacerbations. Asthma exacerbation was recorded when a patient’s asthma symptoms increased over a short period of time beyond daily variability and required additional treatment.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using R (V. 4.4.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Relationships between early-life viral infection and childhood asthma were assessed using Cox regression analyses, both in univariate models and multivariable models. The multivariable models were adjusted for relevant covariates, including age, gender, preterm birth, season of admission, sibling status, clinical severity score, wheezing and bronchiolitis. Each of these risk factors was also assessed individually via univariate Cox regression models. Subsequently, causal mediation analysis was conducted using the mediation package in R to estimate potential mediation effects within a counterfactual framework. CIs were obtained using 1000 bootstrap simulations. The frequency of asthma-related medical visits and asthma exacerbations were analysed using multivariable negative binomial regression models fitted with the glm.nb() function from the MASS package in R. A log link function was used, and the dispersion parameter (θ) was estimated from the data via maximum likelihood estimation.Results were expressed as incidence rate ratios (IRR). These models were employed to assess the associations between different infection types and the frequency of asthma-related medical visits as well as asthma exacerbations.

All calculations were performed using two-tailed tests, and a p value threshold of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To assess the robustness of the multivariable regression analyses, we applied the events-per-variable (EPV) principle. A total of 616 asthma events were observed, yielding an EPV substantially above the recommended minimum of 10, which suggests adequate statistical power and minimal risk of overfitting.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

The characteristics of study population

During the study period, a total of 5111 children hospitalised with ARI between September 2017 and July 2022 were identified and included in the surveillance. A total of 1628 children tested positive for respiratory viruses: 673 for HRV, 914 for RSV and 41 for HRV-RSV co-infection. We performed 1:1 propensity score matching stratified by sex, with nearest-neighbour matching based on age within each sex stratum and no additional calliper restriction. Children with viral infections were matched to HRV-negative and RSV-negative controls, yielding a matched cohort of 3256 children. After excluding children who were lost to follow-up (n=176), those who had a prior documented asthma diagnosis at enrolment (n=2) and those with missing critical data (n=455), a total of 2623 children were included in the final analysis. The final sample consisted of 552 HRV, 761 RSV, 34 HRV-RSV and 1276 negative cases (figure 1). Demographic and clinical characteristics varied among the four groups classified by pathogen status. The boy-to-girl ratios were 1.27:1, 2.03:1, 1.65:1 and 1.54:1 in the HRV-RSV, HRV, RSV and negative groups, respectively. Children aged 13–24 months comprised the largest age group among HRV-positive cases (32.6%), in contrast to RSV-positive cases, which were dominated by infants aged 0–3 months (40.1%). Seasonal patterns varied significantly by viral status (p<0.001). HRV-RSV co-infections peaked in autumn (52.9%). HRV infections were most common in summer (31.2%), whereas RSV infections were predominantly detected in winter (60.6%). Virus-negative cases showed a more balanced seasonal distribution, with relatively higher proportions in spring (29.0%) and autumn (24.4%). The HRV group had the highest proportion of preterm births, while the HRV-RSV group had the highest proportion of children with siblings (p<0.001). The distribution of clinical severity scores differed significantly among the four groups (p<0.001), with the highest proportion of severe cases observed in the RSV group and no severe cases in the HRV-RSV group.

The proportion of children presenting with wheezing varied significantly across groups during index hospitalisation (p<0.001), with the highest proportion observed in the RSV group, followed by the HRV-RSV, HRV and negative groups. Similarly, the clinical diagnosis of bronchiolitis at discharge differed significantly among the groups (p<0.001), with the HRV-RSV group exhibiting the highest proportion of bronchiolitis diagnoses.

Incidences of asthma in childhood and associated risk factors

Using data from SHC, a total of 178 467 medical records corresponding to the study participants were retrieved. From October 2017 to December 2024, a total of 11 438.46 person-years (py) were recorded among 2623 children, and 616 (23.5%) of them were diagnosed with asthma. During a median follow-up of 5.73 years (IQR 3.90–6.39), the 7-year cumulative incidence of asthma was highest among children with HRV-RSV infection (33.56%), followed by HRV (29.62%), RSV (27.38%) and negative (25.58%) groups. The overall incidence of asthma was 53.85 per 1000 py (95% CI 49.60 to 58.11) (table 2). Stratified by age at infection, incidence increased with age: 0–3 months (36.80 per 1000 py), 4–6 months (44.67 per 1000 py), 7–12 months (70.65 per 1000 py) and 13–24 months (77.33 per 1000 py). Boys exhibited a higher incidence than girls (62.22 vs 40.62 per 1000 py). Asthma incidence was highest in summer (60.69/1000 py, 95% CI 50.22 to 71.16) and lowest in spring (49.40/1000 py, 95% CI 41.07 to 57.74), with overlapping CIs across seasons, indicating no clear seasonal pattern. Preterm and full-term children showed similar rates (57.18 vs 53.17 per 1000 py), as did children with or without siblings (52.15 vs 56.76 per 1000 py). Notably, asthma incidence slightly declined with increasing clinical severity: mild (57.57 per 1000 py), severe (54.92 per 1000 py) and moderate (50.59 per 1000 py). Children with bronchiolitis (87.00 vs 52.68 per 1000 py) or wheezing (73.13 vs 42.47 per 1000 py) had substantially higher incidence rates compared with those without.

Early-life viral infection and risk of asthma

During the study period, asthma was diagnosed in 32.35% (11/34) of the HRV–RSV group, 24.64% (136/552) in the HRV group, 22.86% (174/761) in the RSV group and 23.12% (295/1276) in the virus-negative group. Asthma incidence was highest in the HRV-RSV group (95.59 per 1000 py), followed by HRV-only (62.14 per 1000 py) and RSV-only (53.54 per 1000 py), with the lowest in virus-negative children (50.13 per 1000 py). The probability density functions were generated using kernel density estimation to illustrate the distribution of asthma onset across time intervals following infection. The interval from infection to asthma diagnosis was the shortest for HRV. Kernel density estimation (KDE) showed that most asthma cases were diagnosed at approximately 5 months post-infection. Asthma cases were diagnosed at an average of 7 months post-RSV infection, while asthma tended to develop about 2 years later in the HRV-RSV group (figure 2a). Kaplan-Meier analysis of asthma onset stratified by age groups within each viral infection demonstrated statistically significant overall differences for both HRV and RSV infections (p<0.001 for each) (figure 2b,c). Both the HRV and RSV groups exhibited pronounced age-dependent variations in asthma risk, with the highest cumulative incidence observed in children infected at 13–24 months of age. In contrast, the HRV-RSV coinfection group showed no statistically significant differences across age groups (p=0.22) (figure 2d).

Although asthma risk appeared higher in the HRV-RSV, HRV and RSV groups compared with the virus-negative group, the differences were not statistically significant. Stratified Cox regression models were used to examine the association between HRV and RSV infection and the risk of developing asthma across different age groups (table 4). Among children aged 13–24 months, HRV-RSV was significantly associated with an increased risk of asthma (Model I: aHR=2.40, 95% CI 1.02 to 6.69), and similar associations were observed for HRV (aHR=1.56, 95% CI 1.10 to 2.22). RSV infection also showed a trend toward an increased risk, though the association did not reach statistical significance (aHR=1.31, 95% CI 0.89 to 1.89). After additional adjustment for wheezing and bronchiolitis in Model II, the HRV-RSV association remained significant (aHR=2.47, 95% CI 0.88 to 6.93), while associations for HRV and RSV were attenuated and not significant. These findings suggest that viral infections may exert an age-dependent effect on the development of asthma, with the 13–24 months window representing a particularly vulnerable period for adverse long-term respiratory outcomes following HRV or RSV infection. After stratification, no meaningful violations of the proportional hazards assumption were observed for the remaining covariates (Schoenfeld residuals, all p>0.05).

The hypothesised causal pathways were illustrated using a directed acyclic graph (DAG) (online supplemental figure S1). During this critical window, wheezing and bronchiolitis—both of which are common in young children—were each independently associated with an increased risk of subsequent asthma and may act as mediating factors linking early viral exposure to subsequent asthma development. To further explore these pathways, causal mediation analysis was conducted, revealing that HRV significantly increases asthma risk both directly and indirectly through its effect on wheezing during this critical developmental window (indirect effect RD=0.046, 95% CI 0.019 to 0.077, p<0.001; total effect RD=0.086, p=0.05) (online supplemental table S4). The indirect effect accounted for approximately 53.5% of the total effect of HRV on asthma risk, highlighting wheezing as a key intermediate phenotype in the virus–asthma pathway. Conversely, bronchiolitis did not mediate the HRV-asthma association, and RSV showed no significant total or mediated effects on asthma risk (all p>0.4).

Asthma-related medical visits and exacerbations

Of the 616 children who developed asthma, 460 (74.7%) experienced exacerbations. The proportion of boys was significantly higher among children who experienced asthma exacerbations than those who did not (70.8% vs 29.2%; p=0.02). The asthma-related medical visits peaked in autumn and winter, regardless of exacerbation status. Exacerbation rates varied by viral aetiology, reaching 100% in the HRV-RSV group, while the HRV, RSV and virus-negative groups showed comparable proportions (online supplemental table S3).

The distribution of asthma-related medical visits (figure 3a) and asthma exacerbations (figure 3b) varied markedly across different infection groups. Infants in HRV and HRV-RSV groups exhibited notably higher frequencies of asthma-related medical visits, with maximum values exceeding 60 episodes, suggesting a potential association with recurrent asthma symptoms. In contrast, children in the virus-negative group showed more concentrated distributions with lower overall frequencies, indicating relatively stable clinical courses.

Regarding asthma exacerbations (figure 3b), both the HRV and HRV-RSV groups demonstrated higher frequencies of exacerbations. Although median values across groups were similar, the broader distribution and longer upper tails in these two groups suggest that a subset of children experienced more frequent asthma exacerbations.

In multivariable negative binomial regression analyses, HRV-RSV infections were not significantly associated with the frequency of either asthma-related medical visits or asthma exacerbations, compared with virus-negative children. In contrast, both HRV and RSV infections were significantly associated with increased frequencies of asthma-related medical visits (HRV: IRR=2.20, 95% CI 1.56 to 3.11; RSV: IRR=1.91, 95% CI 1.41 to 2.59; both p<0.001), as well as with increased asthma exacerbations (HRV: IRR=2.15, 95% CI 1.55 to 2.99; RSV: IRR=1.88, 95% CI 1.39 to 2.53). Boys were associated with more asthma-related medical visits and asthma exacerbations (IRR=1.39, p=0.03; IRR=1.35, p<0.05), while wheezing and bronchiolitis increased the risk of asthma exacerbations (IRR=1.31, p<0.05 and 1.68, p=0.04). Notably, higher clinical severity was inversely associated with asthma exacerbations (IRR=0.90, p<0.001) (table 5).

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted a sensitivity analysis in which 321 participants with specific pre-existing conditions—potential confounders or competing risk factors—were systematically excluded. The exclusion criteria were defined according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) codes, including cystic fibrosis (E84), primary ciliary dyskinesia (Q34.8), congenital heart disease (Q20–Q28), primary immunodeficiencies (D80–D84), congenital respiratory tract malformations (Q30–Q34), congenital diaphragmatic hernia (Q79.0), neuromuscular disorders (G12.9, G70.2, G71.0, G80.9), trisomy 21 (Q90) and bronchopulmonary dysplasia (P27.1). Following the exclusion of these cases, analyses were repeated using Cox proportional hazards models, age-stratified Cox models and negative binomial regression models (online supplemental tables S6–S9). The results demonstrated that the primary effect estimates remained highly consistent with those from the main analyses, with no significant deviations observed. These findings provide further support for the reliability of the study conclusions, suggesting that the primary results are unlikely to be substantially influenced by the distribution of the aforementioned pre-existing conditions.

Discussion

This cohort study leveraged comprehensive medical records to investigate the association between early-life viral infections and subsequent asthma development. While initial analyses across the entire age spectrum showed a non-significant trend toward higher asthma risk in the HRV-RSV, HRV and RSV groups compared with the virus-negative group, stratification by age revealed a critical period of vulnerability. The period between 13 and 24 months emerged as a critical window, during which HRV-RSV, HRV and RSV infections were each significantly associated with an elevated risk of asthma, with the magnitude of risk decreasing in that order. Consequently, asthma incidence was highest in the HRV-RSV group (95.6 per 1000 person-years), followed by the HRV, RSV and virus-negative groups. Wheezing episodes occurring within this window likely represent an intermediate clinical phenotype linking early HRV infection to later asthma development.

In this study, the mean age at first asthma diagnosis was approximately 2.5 years. A recent cross-sectional study in Shanghai employed a multistage and multilevel sampling method to randomly select 31 kindergartens and surveyed children aged 3 to 7 years using structured questionnaires. The study found that younger age was associated with a higher prevalence of asthma (OR=0.88, 95% CI 0.81 to 0.96), which is consistent with our findings.12 HRV-related asthma tended to emerge earlier, typically within 5 months of infection, while RSV-related cases appeared around 7 months post-infection. In contrast, asthma following HRV-RSV co-infection often developed much later—up to 1 to 2 years—indicating a potentially more complex or delayed inflammatory process. In addition to viral aetiology and timing, several early-life factors present at the time of infection were independently associated with childhood asthma.

As expected, our study found that the subsequent incidence of asthma was higher in boys and those with wheezing or bronchiolitis. A systematic review on asthma prevalence among Chinese children from 1990 to 2020 reported a higher prevalence in boys than in girls.13 Consistent with our findings, multiple domestic and international studies have demonstrated that severe bronchiolitis or early-life wheezing symptoms are strongly associated with preschool wheezing and subsequent asthma development.1417

Furthermore, causal mediation analysis suggested that wheezing symptoms following HRV infection at 13–24 months of age may act as a potential mediator in this association. This finding is consistent with accumulating epidemiological evidence indicating that HRV-associated wheezing illnesses represent a particularly high-risk phenotype for later asthma development. Early-life wheezing associated with HRV, particularly by age 3, has been strongly associated with an increased risk of asthma by age 6 (OR = 25.6), with 87% of affected children developing asthma—surpassing the risk observed with early aeroallergen sensitisation (OR = 3.4).18 In a longitudinal study, early-life wheezing with HRV was associated with a fivefold increased risk of asthma at age 8 (aOR = 5.0).14 Furthermore, recent trajectory analyses from the CHILD Cohort Study identified distinct wheeze patterns, with childhood-onset and persistent wheezing being most strongly linked to later asthma and reduced lung function.19 As extensively reviewed,20 HRV-induced wheezing episodes in preschool children are associated with an increased likelihood of persistent wheezing and a later asthma diagnosis. For HRV-driven wheezing, Th2-dominant inflammation suggests that affected infants may benefit from asthma-targeted therapies, such as systemic corticosteroids.21 These findings emphasise the critical mediating role of early-life HRV-induced wheezing in shaping asthma susceptibility and highlight the need for targeted preventive strategies aimed at children experiencing such wheezing episodes.

Early-life HRV and RSV infections, particularly HRV, were both significantly associated with increased frequencies of subsequent asthma-related medical visits and exacerbations, with a stronger association observed for HRV.22 Consistent with this trajectory, a recent meta-analysis comparing viral etiologies demonstrated that infants with HRV had significantly higher odds of recurrent wheeze (OR=4.11) and childhood asthma (OR=2.72) than those with RSV.23 Previous studies have shown that respiratory viruses such as HRV and RSV can activate Toll-like receptors (TLRs), triggering a Th2-skewed immune response characterised by elevated levels of cytokines such as IL-4 and IL-5. This Th2-biased inflammation may initiate or exacerbate atopic processes, thereby contributing to increased asthma exacerbations and greater healthcare utilisation.24 The consistency of our results with prior evidence supports the use of predictive-enrichment frameworks in future trials. Such targeted follow-up enhances statistical power and clinical relevance, advancing precision prevention in asthma research.

The lack of association between early-life HRV or RSV infections (0–12 months) and the subsequent development of recurrent wheezing or asthma observed in our study is supported by findings from an Australian cohort study, which reported no increased asthma risk at ages 5–7 following HRV infection before 3 months of age.24 In contrast, a French study demonstrated that co-infections involving both HRV and RSV in infancy possibly carry a higher risk for recurrent bronchial obstruction,25 potentially due to synergistic effects on airway inflammation and epithelial damage.26 27 Discrepancies across studies may partly reflect differences in control group selection. In our study, the use of an ARI control group with elevated baseline risk may have attenuated detectable differences.28

Regarding RSV prevention, maternal vaccines are not expected to provide direct protection in this older age group. Monoclonal antibodies, such as Nirsevimab29 recommended for infants entering their first RSV season and for vulnerable children up to 24 months of age, may offer protection depending on regional advisory body guidelines.30 However, their availability and usage vary widely across settings. While paediatric RSV immunisations are under development35, none are currently licensed for routine use in this specific age group. This highlights an important gap, as children aged 13–24 months—particularly those with risk factors—may remain susceptible to severe RSV disease. Future research and policy efforts should prioritise this understudied population.

The external validity of our findings should be interpreted with caution. Our study used comprehensive real-world medical records with fine temporal resolution, enabling precise tracking of infections and asthma episodes during early life. However, as the cohort was derived from hospitalised ARI cases in a single, economically developed city in China, the generalisability of the results to community populations or regions with different socioeconomic and environmental conditions may be limited.

In this study, an association between HRV and RSV infections and subsequent asthma development was identified in ARI patients. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the asthma diagnoses relied on recorded clinical diagnoses without independent case adjudication. Diagnosing asthma in children under 2 years is inherently difficult due to the non-specific nature of early wheezing, the limited feasibility of objective lung function testing and potential diagnostic variability across institutions. However, the generally conservative diagnostic approach for paediatric asthma minimises the risk of overestimating the observed viral-asthma associations. Second, misclassification of RSV by a less sensitive test is likely non-differential with respect to the asthma. Consequently, it would generally bias the RSV-asthma association toward the null and is unlikely to alter our primary conclusion. Thirdly, our analysis was constrained by the unavailability of data on certain critical unmeasured confounders, specifically a family history of asthma, aeroallergen sensitisation and socio-economic markers. Finally, the relatively small number of children with HRV-RSV infections may also have impacted the statistical power and robustness of the findings.

Conclusion

Among children hospitalised with HRV/RSV, the period between 13 and 24 months may represent a critical vulnerable window for subsequent asthma development. Within this age window, HRV-RSV co-infection suggested a potential trend toward an elevated risk, although the estimates were imprecise and should be interpreted with caution. Wheezing during this period may act as an intermediary clinical phenotype. HRV exposure may increase susceptibility to both asthma and subsequent exacerbations.
