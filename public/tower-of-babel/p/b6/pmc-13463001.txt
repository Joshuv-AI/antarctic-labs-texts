Xanomeline-trospium reverses phencyclidine-induced cognitive deficits through modulation of the gut microbiota-brain axis in mice
Authors: Xin Ding, Rumi Murayama, Yi Cai, Yong Yue, Jian-Jun Yang, Kenji Hashimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCognitive impairment in schizophrenia remains largely unaddressed by dopamine-based antipsychotics. Xanomeline–trospium (KarXT; Cobenfy®), a combination of the muscarinic M1/M4 receptor agonist xanomeline and the peripherally restricted antagonist trospium, effectively reduces psychosis but is associated with gastrointestinal adverse effects. Here, we tested whether KarXT reverses phencyclidine (PCP)–induced cognitive deficits through microbiota-associated mechanisms in adult male mice. Mice received saline or PCP (10 mg/kg/day, s.c.) on days 1–5 and 8–12, followed by vehicle or KarXT [xanomeline 2 mg/kg/day + trospium 1 mg/kg/day, intragastric] on days 15–28. Recognition memory was evaluated using the novel object recognition test (NORT), and lung and intestinal microbiota (small intestine, cecum, and colon) were profiled by 16S rRNA sequencing. KarXT significantly rescued PCP-induced recognition-memory deficits without exacerbating PCP-related reductions in weight gain or fecal output. Microbiome analyses revealed region-specific dysbiosis after PCP exposure, most pronounced in the small intestine and cecum. Several taxa elevated by PCP—including Bacteroides fragilis, Veillonella ratti, Megamonas funiformis, Cupriavidus numazuensis, and Acetanaerobacterium elongatum—were normalized following KarXT treatment. Notably, restoration of multiple pulmonary, cecal, and colonic taxa correlated positively with the NORT recognition index. These findings demonstrate that KarXT reverses PCP-induced cognitive dysfunction while modulating microbial composition in a region-specific manner. Elucidating these relationships may help optimize cognitive efficacy and reduce gastrointestinal adverse effects of muscarinic therapies for schizophrenia.Subject terms: Schizophrenia, Diagnostic markers

Introduction

Schizophrenia is a severe and chronic psychiatric disorder characterized by positive symptoms (hallucinations and delusions), negative symptoms (amotivation, social withdrawal, and blunted affect), and cognitive impairment involving attention, working memory, executive function, and social cognition. Among these domains, cognitive dysfunction is the strongest predictor of long-term functional outcome and quality of life, yet it remains largely unaddressed by current pharmacotherapies [1–6]. Consequently, novel mechanism-based interventions targeting cognitive deficits are urgently needed. Epidemiological and clinical studies further indicate sex differences in schizophrenia, with males typically exhibiting earlier disease onset, greater cognitive impairment, and poorer functional outcomes than females [7–9], underscoring the importance of considering sex as a biological variable in both clinical and preclinical research.

Xanomeline-trospium (KarXT; brand name Cobenfy) is a first-in-class, non-dopaminergic antipsychotic that combines xanomeline, a centrally selective muscarinic M1/M4 receptor agonist, with trospium, a peripherally restricted muscarinic antagonist designed to reduce cholinergic adverse effects [10, 11]. Phase 3 EMERGENT trials demonstrated robust antipsychotic efficacy together with favorable metabolic and extrapyramidal safety profiles in patients with schizophrenia [12, 13]. On September 26, 2024, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved Cobenfy for the treatment of schizophrenia in adults [14], representing a major advance toward therapeutic strategies that extend beyond dopamine D2 receptor blockade [15].

Initial clinical analyses suggest that KarXT may also confer procognitive benefits. In pooled EMERGENT data, patients with acute schizophrenia and baseline cognitive deficits showed significant improvement with KarXT compared with placebo [16]. However, short-term cognitive improvements are also observed with existing antipsychotics and have not consistently translated into sustained functional benefit. Moreover, no head-to-head clinical comparisons have established whether KarXT provides superior cognitive efficacy relative to standard treatments [17, 18]. These uncertainties highlight the need for mechanistic studies to clarify how muscarinic modulation may influence cognition.

Preclinical findings support the procognitive potential of xanomeline. It alleviates (+)-MK-801–induced memory deficits [19], improves scopolamine-induced impairments in novel object recognition [20], and induces neocortical activation with network desynchronization in phencyclidine (PCP)–treated mice [21]. At behaviorally effective doses, however, these benefits are accompanied by peripheral cholinergic adverse effects—such as diarrhea, salivation, and hypoactivity—indicating a narrow therapeutic window in rodents.

Converging evidence implicates host microbiota, particularly the gut microbiota, in schizophrenia pathophysiology and in responses to antipsychotic treatment [22–30]. Although KarXT improves tolerability by limiting peripheral muscarinic activity, gastrointestinal (GI) adverse effects—including constipation, diarrhea, nausea, dyspepsia, and abdominal pain—remain common [12, 13]. Given the high density of muscarinic (M1–M5) receptors in the GI tract [31, 32], muscarinic signaling within the intestine may influence both peripheral adverse events and central therapeutic outcomes. Beyond the gut, emerging data suggest that lung microbiota may participate in bidirectional immune and inflammatory communication within a broader gut–lung–brain network, with potential relevance for neurobehavioral regulation [26, 33]. Although the functional significance of pulmonary microbial alterations in psychiatric disorders remains incompletely defined, simultaneous assessment of lung and intestinal microbiota may provide a systems-level perspective on peripheral microbial modulation associated with neuropsychopharmacological interventions. Accordingly, lung microbiota profiling was included in the present study as an exploratory analysis complementing the primary focus on intestinal microbial dynamics. Despite this rationale, the effects of KarXT on intestinal microbiota have not been examined in either clinical or preclinical settings.

In the present study, we tested the hypothesis that KarXT reverses PCP-induced cognitive deficits through muscarinic (M1/M4) mechanisms and that these behavioral improvements are associated with modulation of the gut microbiota. We further hypothesized that KarXT would restore novel object recognition performance while normalizing PCP-induced microbial dysbiosis, with specific taxa correlating with cognitive recovery. To evaluate these possibilities, we characterized regional microbial composition in the small intestine, cecum, colon, and lungs using 16S rRNA sequencing and examined relationships between microbial features and recognition memory performance. By integrating behavioral, physiological, and microbiome analyses, this work aims to provide mechanistic evidence consistent with a muscarinic–microbiota–brain interaction contributing to the procognitive actions of KarXT and to identify microbiota-related pathways that may inform optimization of muscarinic therapies for schizophrenia.

Materials and methods

Animals

Adult male ICR mice (8 weeks old; 35–45 g) were obtained from Japan SLC, Inc. (Hamamatsu, Shizuoka, Japan). Mice were housed in a temperature-controlled room on a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on 07:00–19:00) with unrestricted access to standard chow (CE-2; CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan) and water. Mice were randomly assigned to each group.

Ethics approval

All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the Guidelines for Animal Experimentation of Chiba university, and the study protocol was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Chiba University (approval no. 7-344), in accordance with the Japanese national guidelines for animal experimentation. Every effort was made to minimize animal suffering and reduce the number of animals used.

Compounds and treatments

Phencyclidine (PCP) was synthesized in-house by a coauthor (K.H.). Saline or PCP (10 mg/kg/day) was administered subcutaneously (s.c.) once daily on days 1–5 and 8–12, following established protocols [34–37]. From days 15–28, mice received either vehicle (0.5% carboxymethylcellulose [CMC: Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd., Osaka, Japan], 10 mL/kg/day) or KarXT—a combination of xanomeline (2 mg/kg/day) and trospium (1 mg/kg/day)—by intragastric gavage (i.g.) once daily. Xanomeline (TargetMol Chemicals Inc., Boston, MA, USA: CAS 152854-19-8; Lot 239317) and trospium (TargetMol Chemicals Inc., Boston, MA, USA: CAS 10405-02-4; Lot 145996) were prepared fresh each day in 0.5% CMC.

Measurement of body weight and fecal output

Body weight was recorded before each drug administration throughout the experiment. To assess gastrointestinal activity, mice were placed individually in clean cages, and the number of fecal pellets excreted within 60 min was counted immediately after treatment. All measurements were performed at the same time of day to minimize circadian variation.

Novel object recognition test (NORT)

NORT was performed in a square Plexiglas arena (45 × 45 × 45 cm) as described previously [34–37]. Mice were habituated to the empty arena for 10 min/day on three consecutive days (days 26–28). On day 29 (training session), two identical objects (object A1 and A2) were placed in opposite corners, and each mouse explored for 10 min. On day 30 (test session), one familiar object was replaced with a novel object, and mice were allowed to explore both objects for 5 min. Trials with a total exploration time of less than 20 s were excluded a priori (one, one, and two individuals were excluded from the Control, PCP, and PCP + KarXT groups, respectively). The recognition index (RI) was calculated as: (time investigating the novel object) / (time investigating novel + familiar objects) × 100%. Object identity and location were counterbalanced across animals; “investigation” was defined as directing the nose toward an object within ~2 cm or touching/sniffing it (sitting on or turning around the object was not counted). The arena and objects were cleaned between trials.

Collection of BALF and intestinal contents

Mice were anesthetized with a medetomidine–midazolam–butorphanol (MMB) mixture (medetomidine 0.75 mg/kg; midazolam 4 mg/kg; butorphanol 5 mg/kg; Nippon Zenyaku Kogyo, Sandoz K.K., and Meiji Seika Pharma, Japan). Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was collected by lavaging the lung with PBS (phosphate buffer saline) and immediately centrifuged at 800 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, as previously reported [38]. Supernatant was stored at −80 °C until use.

Subsequently, the intestines were excised and luminal contents were collected from the entire cecum, and from 5–7 cm segments of the small intestine and colon adjacent to the cecum [39, 40]. All samples were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C until further use.

16S rRNA sequencing and microbiome analysis

From BALF samples, bacterial DNA was extracted using the QIAamp UCP Pathogen Mini Kit (Qiagen; cat. no. 50214; lot 181025621) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples with DNA concentrations ≥ 0.3 ng/µL were submitted to BGI Japan (Kobe, Japan) for 16S rRNA gene profiling. For fecal samples, total DNA was extracted from ~250 mg of intestinal contents using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen; cat. no. 51604; Lot 181023747) according to the manufacturer’s protocol [40, 41]. DNA concentrations were normalized to 10 ng/µL and samples were submitted to BGI Japan (Kobe, Japan) for 16S rRNA gene profiling. The V3–V4 hypervariable regions were amplified using a dual-index strategy with universal bacterial primers 341 F/806 R, and amplicons were sequenced (paired-end).

Bioinformatic processing followed established procedures. Overlapping paired-end reads were merged with fastq-join (default settings). Quality control and chimera removal were performed with USEARCH v6.1, retaining reads with Q ≥ 20 across ≥ 99% of bases. Non-chimeric reads were taxonomically assigned using the Ribosomal Database Project (RDP) multiclassifier with an 80% confidence threshold at phylum–genus levels. Sequencing data are deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession PRJNA1347572.

α-diversity indices (Shannon, Chao1, Simpson, ACE, Sobs) and β-diversity metrics (Bray–Curtis, unweighted and weighted UniFrac) were computed from OTU/ASV-level tables. Ordinations included PCA, PCoA, NMDS; group separation was evaluated by ANOSIM (R, vegan v4.2.2). Between-group comparisons of diversity indices and relative abundances were tested by Kruskal–Wallis followed by Dunn’s post hoc test with FDR correction. Differential taxa were identified by LEfSe with LDA > 2.5 (or 3) and FDR < 0.05 [40, 42]. Results were visualized as taxonomic bar plots and cladograms using Metware Cloud and OmicStudio (https://cloud.metware.cn/; https://www.omicstudio.cn/) [40, 42].

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) or median [interquartile range], as appropriate. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism v9.4.1 and R v4.2.2. Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance with Bartlett’s test. Body weight (repeated measures) was analyzed using the Friedman test followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparisons test. Fecal counts and recognition index were analyzed one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test and one-way ANOVA followed by Fisher’s LSD test, respectively. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Volcano plots were generated in R (ggplot2 package), with bacterial taxa defined as upregulated when FDR < 0.05 and log₂FC > 0 between either the Control vs. PCP or PCP vs. PCP + KarXT comparisons, and as downregulated when FDR < 0.05 and log₂FC < 0. Spearman’s rank correlations between behavioral outcomes and bacterial abundances were computed and visualized using GraphPad Prism v9.4.1.

All graphs were generated directly from the original raw data using GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.1), with individual data points overlaid where appropriate.

Results

Effects of KarXT on body weight, fecal output, and cognition in PCP-treated mice

Mice received saline or PCP (10 mg/kg/day, s.c.) on days 1–5 and 8–12, followed by daily intragastric gavage (i.g.) of vehicle or KarXT [xanomeline (2 mg/kg/day) + trospium (1 mg/kg/day)] on days 15–28 (Fig. 1A). Body weight was monitored for 31 consecutive days. PCP significantly reduced weight gain relative to the Control (saline + vehicle) group, and KarXT did not alter this effect (no difference between PCP (PCP + vehicle) and PCP + KarXT groups; Fig. 1B). Analysis of fecal output showed that PCP decreased pellet number (consistent with constipation) compared with Control group, with no significant difference between PCP and PCP + KarXT groups (Fig. 1C). In the NORT, the recognition index was reduced by PCP versus Control group, indicating cognitive impairment, and KarXT significantly reversed this deficit (PCP + KarXT > PCP) (Fig. 1D).

A Experimental timeline for PCP model induction and KarXT administration. B Body-weight change relative to baseline (Friedman test with Dunn’s multiple comparisons; test statistic = 44.13, P < 0.01; n = 10 mice per group). C Number of fecal pellets produced within 1 h by male mice (one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test; F = 3.622, P = 0.04; n = 10 mice per group). D Recognition index in the novel object recognition test (NORT) (one-way ANOVA with Fisher’s LSD; F = 4.647, P = 0.02; n = 9, 9, and 8 mice for Control, PCP, and PCP + KarXT, respectively). Values are mean ± SEM. *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001.

Together, these results show that while PCP induces reduced weight gain, constipation-like changes, and cognitive dysfunction, KarXT selectively rescues cognition without exacerbating GI or weight effects.

Effects of KarXT on lung and intestinal microbiota (α- and β-diversity) in PCP-treated mice

We first assessed α-diversity (within-sample diversity) across the three treatment groups using the Shannon, Chao1, Simpson, ACE, and Sobs indices. In the lung, none of the α-diversity metrics differed significantly among groups (Fig. 2A). In the small intestine, cecum, and colon, richness/coverage indices (Chao1, ACE, Sobs) showed significant group differences, whereas diversity/evenness indices (Shannon, Simpson) did not (Fig. 2B–D).

We next examined β-diversity (between-sample community structure) using Bray–Curtis, unweighted UniFrac, and weighted UniFrac distances. In the lung, Bray–Curtis and weighted UniFrac revealed significant group differences, while unweighted UniFrac did not (Fig. 3A). In the small intestine, weighted UniFrac differed among groups, whereas Bray–Curtis and unweighted UniFrac did not (Fig. 3B). In the cecum, Bray–Curtis differed between PCP and PCP + KarXT groups, with no differences detected by weighted or unweighted UniFrac (Fig. 3C). In the colon, none of the three β-diversity metrics showed significant group differences (Fig. 3D).

Ordination analyses supported these findings: PCA (OTU/ASV abundance) showed clear separation among groups with significant ANOSIM results in the lung and colon. PCoA based on Bray–Curtis, likewise indicated significant between-group differences in the lung, cecum, and colon. NMDS (non-metric multidimensional scaling) based on the Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix plot also revealed obvious community separation among groups in the lung, cecum, and colon (Supplementary Fig. 1A–D). Collectively, these data indicate that KarXT exposure modulates microbiota composition in the lung and cecum (and, to a lesser extent, colon), while effects in the small intestine are limited at the community level.

Effects of KarXT on microbiota composition at the genus and species levels

We next compared microbiota composition across the lung, small intestine, cecum, and colon at the genus and species levels. In the lung, at the genus level, relative abundances of Brucella, Delftia, Gordonia, Aeromicrobium, Mammaliicoccus, Agrobacterium, Rhizobium, Achromobacter, Pseudomonas, and Stenotrophomonas differed among groups (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 2A). At the species level, significant differences in the relative abundance of Rhodococcus_erythropolis, Brucella_melitensis, Delftia_tsuruhatensis, Agrobacterium_radiobacter, Pseudomonas_paralactis, Rhizobium_arsenicireducens, Gordonia_polyisoprenivorans, Stenotrophomonas_maltophilia, Acinetobacter_junii and Mammaliicoccus_scicuri were observed among the three groups (Fig. 4B and Supplementary Fig. 2B).

In the small intestine, at the genus level, significant differences in the relative abundance of Euzebya, Hydrogenispora, Mesorhizobium, Ruminiclostridium, Nocardioides, Planococcus, Arboricoccus, Romboutsia, Kocuria and Turicibacter were observed among the three groups (Fig. 4C and Supplementary Fig. 2C). At the species level, significant differences in the relative abundance of Hydrogenispora_ethanolica, Ruminiclostridium_cellobioparum, Rhodologotrophos_appendicifer, Prevotellamassila_timonensis, Aggregaillinea_lenta, Romboutsia_timonensis, Halocella_celluloislytica and Arboricoccus_pini were observed among the three groups (Fig. 4D and Supplementary Fig. 2D).

In the cecum, at the genus level, significant differences in the relative abundance of Kocuria, Pseudomonas, Hydrogenispora, Guopingia, Cupriavidus, Staphylococcus, Chryseobacterium, Paracoccus, and Burkholderia were observed among the three groups (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. 2E). At the species level, significant differences in the relative abundance of Cupriavidus_numazuensis, Sporofaciens_musculi, Flintibacter_butyricus, Lacrimispora_xylanolytica, Pseudomonas_putida, Aldercreutzia_mucosicola, Enterococcus_faecium, Romboutsia_timonensis, and Dysosmobacter_acutus were observed among the three groups (Fig. 4F and Supplementary Fig. 2F).

In the colon, at the genus level, significant differences in the relative abundance of Intestinimonas, Duncaniella, Alistipes, Bacteroides, Anaeroplasma, Mucispirillum, Lacrimispora, Oscillibacter, Flintibacter and Phocaeicola were observed among the three groups (Fig. 4G and Supplementary Fig. 2G). At the species level, significant differences in the relative abundance of Intestinimonas_butyriciproducens, Bifidobacterium_adolescentis, Bifidobacterium_longum, Allomeiothermus_silvanus, Collinsella_aerofaciens, Bifidobacterium_breve, Megasphaera_massiliensis, Romboutsia_timonensis, Veillonella_ratti and Dorea_longicatena were observed among the three groups (Fig. 4H and Supplementary Fig. 2H).

Together, these results indicate that KarXT exposure reshapes microbial community composition in a region-specific manner.

For each site (lung, small intestine, cecum, colon), the top five discriminatory taxa per group are summarized in bar plots (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Predicted functional profiling and pathway enrichment (KEGG, COG, MetaCyc) were conducted on the same datasets for Control vs. PCP and PCP vs. PCP + KarXT; detailed results are shown in Supplementary Figs. 4, 5. In the colon, amino-acid metabolism was similarly increased in PCP and restored by KarXT, whereas cell motility and photosynthesis pathways were decreased in PCP and recovered with KarXT (Supplementary Fig. 5C, D).

Differentially expressed bacteria (species level) and associations with cognition

Similarity analysis and PLS-DA indicated overall microbiome differences across Control, PCP, and PCP + KarXT groups, although clear separation was observed (Supplementary Fig. 6).

To identify taxa contributing to group differences, volcano plots were generated at the species level (top 200 taxa by total abundance) using Mann–Whitney U tests with FDR correction. Species with FDR < 0.05 and log₂FC > 0 were classified as upregulated, whereas those with FDR < 0.05 and log₂FC < 0 were classified as downregulated. In the PCP vs. PCP + KarXT comparison, Romboutsia_timonensis, Dorea_longicatena, Mediterraneibacter_faecis, Faecalibacterium_longum, and Ruminococcus_bromii were increased (FDR < 0.05, log₂FC > 0), while top 5 decreased species included Segatella_copri, Veillonella_dispar, Bacteroides_fragilis, Bifidobacterium_breve, and Terrisporobacter_petrolearius (FDR < 0.05, log₂FC < 0) (Fig. 5J, L).

We next filtered taxa that were significantly altered by PCP vs. Control and reversed by KarXT vs. PCP. In total, eleven species were upregulated by PCP and normalized toward control levels by KarXT. In the lung, two species—Brucella_melitensis and Delftia_tsuruhatensis—differed markedly among the three groups, with their abundances reversed by KarXT (Fig. 6A, C); each showed a significant correlation between abundance and recognition index in the NORT (Fig. 6B, D). In the colon, three species—Bacteroides_fragilis, Veillonella_ratti, and Megamonas_funiformis—were markedly altered among the three groups, with their abundances reversed by KarXT (Fig. 6E, G, I); each showed a significant correlation between abundance and recognition index in the NORT (Fig. 6F, H, J).

In the cecum, six species—Cupriavidus_numazuensis, Acetanaerobacterium_elongatum, Aerococcus_viridans, Thermobispora_bispora, Brevibacillus_thermoruber, and Thermobacillus_composti—were significantly increased in the PCP group but nearly absent in both the Control and PCP + KarXT groups (Fig. 6K, M, O, Q, S, U). Overall, these species-level analyses highlight region-specific microbial signatures associated with PCP-induced cognitive deficits and their reversal by KarXT, with several pulmonary, cecal, and colonic taxa showing both normalization under KarXT and behavioral relevance through correlations with recognition memory performance.

Discussion

In the present study, we focused on the mechanistic and translational implications of our findings, particularly the relationship between muscarinic receptor modulation, peripheral microbial remodeling, and cognitive recovery. Repeated administration of KarXT significantly improved recognition memory impairment in PCP-treated mice, demonstrating a procognitive effect independent of dopaminergic mechanisms. Importantly, KarXT did not exacerbate PCP-induced reductions in body weight or fecal output, indicating that its behavioral benefits occurred without worsening GI or metabolic side effects.

Microbiome profiling revealed region-specific alterations in microbial α- and β-diversity, most prominently in the small intestine and cecum, with more modest effects in the lung and colon. Several taxa—including Bacteroides fragilis, Veillonella ratti, Megamonas funiformis, Cupriavidus numazuensis, and Acetanaerobacterium elongatum—were elevated in PCP-treated mice but normalized following KarXT treatment. Notably, restoration of multiple cecal and colonic species correlated positively with recognition performance in the NORT, suggesting that microbiota modulation may contribute to cognitive improvement.

Collectively, these findings are consistent with the concept of a muscarinic–microbiota–brain interaction underlying cognitive recovery. Importantly, the present results do not establish the existence of a definitive biological axis but rather provide associative and mechanistic evidence consistent with functional coupling between muscarinic signaling, peripheral microbial communities, and cognition. Accordingly, the term “muscarinic–microbiota–brain axis” is used here as a conceptual framework to integrate these observations rather than to denote a fully validated pathway.

These observations extend beyond behavioral rescue alone and suggest that KarXT may influence cognition through coordinated central and peripheral mechanisms. This study therefore provides the first preclinical evidence that KarXT—a muscarinic M1/M4 receptor agonist combined with the peripherally restricted antagonist trospium—ameliorates PCP-induced cognitive deficits, a validated model of schizophrenia-related cognitive dysfunction [34–37]. Consistent with prior work on xanomeline, the addition of trospium did not diminish central efficacy while limiting peripheral cholinergic effects [10, 11]. The NORT paradigm was selected because it represents a well-validated and widely used assay of recognition-memory impairment in subchronic PCP models [34–37], capturing a core cognitive domain relevant to schizophrenia and demonstrating sensitivity to glutamatergic and cholinergic modulation.

The inclusion of lung microbiota analysis in this study was exploratory and intended to determine whether muscarinic modulation might influence peripheral microbial ecosystems beyond the intestine. Given the low microbial biomass of pulmonary samples and the still-evolving understanding of lung microbiota in neuropsychiatric conditions, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and not as direct evidence of therapeutic mechanisms. Rather, they raise the possibility that KarXT-induced peripheral cholinergic and immune effects may extend across mucosal compartments within a broader gut–lung–brain interaction framework, warranting further targeted investigation.

Mechanistically, muscarinic receptor activation in the enteric nervous system can influence motility, secretion, and microbial composition, whereas microbial metabolites—such as short-chain fatty acids—can modulate central cholinergic and immune signaling pathways that shape cognition [32, 43, 44]. The normalization of taxa associated with inflammatory tone, mucosal immunity, and short-chain-fatty-acid metabolism further supports a biologically plausible contribution of gut microbiota to the observed procognitive effects.

Because microbiota composition and behavioral outcomes were measured concurrently, causal directionality cannot be inferred from the present data. Definitive validation of microbiota-dependent mechanisms will require future studies employing germ-free or antibiotic-treated models, fecal microbiota transplantation, or targeted manipulation of microbial metabolites to determine whether restoration of specific taxa is sufficient to reproduce KarXT-induced cognitive improvement.

Region-specific microbial alterations provide additional mechanistic insight. Across gut regions with distinct ecological characteristics [30, 45, 46], PCP disrupted community structure most prominently in the small intestine and cecum. In the small intestine, PCP increased richness-associated taxa (e.g., Hydrogenispora, Ruminiclostridium), which were partially normalized by KarXT. In the cecum, opportunistic or stress-associated taxa (e.g., Cupriavidus numazuensis, Acetanaerobacterium elongatum) were enriched by PCP and reversed by KarXT, correlating with recognition memory recovery. In the colon, PCP elevated pro-inflammatory taxa (e.g., Bacteroides fragilis, Veillonella ratti, Megamonas funiformis), whereas KarXT restored commensal organisms linked to metabolic and anti-inflammatory functions (e.g., Romboutsia timonensis, Faecalibacterium longum, Ruminococcus bromii).

Muscarinic receptor activation also influences pulmonary physiology. Although clozapine—a muscarinic agonist antipsychotic—has been associated with ileus and pneumonia [47–50], KarXT did not demonstrate such risks in phase 3 trials [12, 13], possibly reflecting the absence of sialorrhea, sedation, and neutropenia. Nevertheless, potential pulmonary microbiota alterations under muscarinic modulation warrant attention in patients with dysphagia or respiratory vulnerability during long-term treatment.

From a translational perspective, KarXT’s dual central and peripheral actions suggest that microbiota-associated pathways may contribute to both therapeutic efficacy and gastrointestinal tolerability. Longitudinal microbiome monitoring in patients with schizophrenia could therefore inform adjunctive probiotic or dietary strategies aimed at enhancing cognitive outcomes while minimizing adverse effects [32].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Accordingly, the current study should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating with respect to microbiota-mediated mechanisms, and causal relationships remain unproven. Behavioral assessment was limited to recognition memory, whereas cognitive dysfunction in schizophrenia spans multiple domains. Only male mice were examined, and sex-dependent differences in muscarinic signaling, microbiota composition, and cognition may limit generalizability. Future studies incorporating female cohorts and multidomain behavioral paradigms will be essential to determine the breadth and translational relevance of KarXT’s procognitive effects. In addition, the modest microbiome sample size and reliance on 16S rRNA sequencing restrict functional interpretation, and the biological significance of pulmonary microbiota findings requires confirmation using contamination-controlled, low-biomass sequencing approaches.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that KarXT reverses PCP-induced cognitive deficits without worsening gastrointestinal or metabolic effects and identifies peripheral microbial modulation as a potential contributor to its procognitive actions. Rather than establishing a definitive biological axis, these findings support a conceptual muscarinic–microbiota–brain interaction and highlight microbiota-targeted strategies as potential adjunctive approaches for cognitive dysfunction in schizophrenia. Further mechanistic and translational investigations will be required to determine the extent to which microbiota-dependent signaling contributes to the clinical efficacy of KarXT.
