The association between dietary iron intake and the incidence of cardiometabolic multimorbidity: the mediating role of biological age
Authors: Ningbo Chen, Dan Yang, Guiyuan Qiao, Zhilan Ke, Yudi Tang, Jing Zhou, Xinhong Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe aimed to examine whether dietary iron intake is associated with cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) incidence and the potential role of biological age in mediating this association.MethodsBased on data from the 2009 China Nutrition and Health Survey (CHNS), a national prospective cohort study of the Chinese population. Dietary intake was calculated from three consecutive 24-hour dietary recalls and household food stocks. CMM was defined as the presence of at least two cardiometabolic diseases (hypertension, diabetes, stroke, and myocardial infarction). Biological aging indicators included Klemera–Doubal method (KDM), phenotypic age (PA), homeostatic dysregulation (HD), and adaptive load (AL). Data were analyzed using COX risk regression, restricted cubic spline method (RCS) analysis and subgroup analysis. Mediation analysis was used to explore the mediating role of biological aging.ResultsHigh total iron intake (HRQ1 vs. Q5: 0.74, 95%CI: 0.57–0.97) and nonheme iron intake (HRQ1 vs. Q5: 0.69, 95%CI: 0.53–0.90) were associated with a lower risk of developing CMM. Compared to the age deceleration group, all age acceleration groups were associated with a significantly higher incidence of CMM, with hazard ratios of 3.41 (95% CI: 2.95–3.95) for KDM, 3.36 (95% CI: 2.88–3.92) for PA, 4.33 (95% CI: 3.55–5.28) for HD, and 3.42 (95% CI: 2.91–4.03) for AL. Total dietary iron intake and dietary non-heme iron intake were U-shapedly correlated with biological age (P for nonlinearity < 0.001). In the total population, significant indirect effects average causal mediation effects of total iron 0.03% (95% CI: 0.01%–0.04%) and non-heme iron 0.02% (95% CI: 0.01%–0.03%) were exhibited via the KDM algorithm, while heme iron 0.24%–0.45% (95% CI: 0.06%–1.07%) demonstrated significant indirect effects through all four biological age algorithms.ConclusionHigh dietary iron intake is associated with a lower risk of CMM, partially mediated by biological aging. Appropriate intake of total dietary iron and non-heme iron may be associated with a slowed biological aging process and a lower risk of CMM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-25256-w.

Introduction

Multimorbidity refers to the coexistence of two or more chronic medical conditions within an individual[1]. Managing patients with multimorbidity is challenging, as their complex treatment needs require more extensive healthcare resources. Consequently, enhancing prevention and optimizing care for these individuals are now key focuses in numerous countries [2]. Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), defined as the coexistence of two or more cardiometabolic diseases (CMD), including hypertension, diabetes, stroke and cardiovascular disease, is one of the most common multimorbidity patterns [3]. CMDs are a cluster of disorders centered on cardiovascular diseases and metabolic diseases. Clinically, CMD is characterized by high incidence and high disability rates; its pathological mechanisms typically involve insulin resistance, chronic inflammatory responses, and vascular endothelial dysfunction—all of which create a reciprocal interplay that exacerbates disease progression [4]. For instance, insulin resistance not only drives hyperglycemia in type 2 diabetes but also promotes vascular smooth muscle proliferation and lipid deposition, thereby increasing the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [5]. Such shared pathophysiological pathways explain why CMD often co-occurs and progresses to CMM.

The incidence of CMM has significantly increased over the past two decades with the rise in life expectancy and ongoing advancements in the management of cardiovascular diseases and diabetes [6]. In addition, developing one CMD greatly increases the risk of developing another [7], and each additional CMD, in any combination, carries an exponential mortality rate [8]. In low- and middle-income countries, the challenges of multimorbidity impose a significant burden on individuals, families, and healthcare systems, exacerbated by fragile social protections and under-resourced health infrastructures [9].

Despite growing attention to the health impacts of CMD, previous studies on dietary iron intake have mostly focused on its association with single CMD, and its influence on the progression from individual CMD to CMM [8]. Notably, compared with patients with single CMD, those with CMM face worse prognosis (e.g., higher all-cause mortality and cardiovascular event rates) and impose a heavier burden on healthcare systems—including increased hospitalizations, longer length of stay, and higher medical costs [10]. Given this, exploring the relationship between dietary iron intake and CMM is of greater clinical relevance for guiding comprehensive prevention strategies for cardiometabolic disorders.

Iron is an essential trace element that is involved in the development and progress of chronic diseases. Although some studies have shown that dietary iron intake is associated with the risk of CMDs, the results was ambiguous. Previous studies suggested that higher heme iron intake was associated with an increased risk of CVD [11–14]. Nevertheless, no association was found between CVD and dietary non-heme iron intake or total iron intake [15]. In the terms of diabetes, the dose-response curve between non-heme iron and total iron intake and diabetes was J-shaped in men and L-shaped in women, and there was no significant association between heme iron intake and diabetes risk in a Chinese cohort study of 17,026 adults [16]. However, in another cohort study from Guangzhou, no significant association was found between total dietary iron or non-heme iron intake and the risk of type 2 diabetes (T2D), while heme iron was positively associated with the risk of T2D [17]. In addition, there are inconsistent findings regarding the relationship between dietary iron intake and the risk of hypertension. In two recent cohort studies of Chinese adults [18, 19], the relationship between dietary iron and new-onset hypertension was non-linear, with a U-shaped relationship for total or non-heme iron intake and an L-shaped relationship for heme iron intake. Among 4680 participants aged 40–59 years, dietary intake of total iron and non-heme iron was associated with lower systolic and diastolic blood pressure, but there was no significant association between heme iron intake and blood pressure [20]. Two cross-sectional studies [20, 21] and one prospective cohort study [22] failed to find an association between dietary total and non-heme iron intake and the risk of elevated blood pressure. In general, the findings concerning the association between CMDs and dietary iron intake are inconsistent. Furthermore, most previous studies have only detected the effects of dietary iron intake on the incidence of CMD, little attention was paid on the influence of dietary iron intake and on CMM.

Understanding how dietary iron intake may influence the development of CMM has gained increasing scientific interest. Growing evidence suggests that various regulators of dietary iron intake play a role in organismal aging and longevity pathways, potentially contributing to disease pathogenesis [23, 24]. On the other hand, the age-related dysregulation of dietary iron intake is often associated with multiple age-related pathologies including CMDs [23]. Iron exhibits a dual role in biological systems, demonstrating toxic effects under conditions of overload. Consequently, strict maintenance of systemic iron homeostasis is crucial for preserving normal physiological functions. When present in excess, iron catalyzes excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation via Fenton chemistry, subsequently inducing oxidative damage to critical biomolecules including lipids, proteins, and nucleic acids. At the mitochondrial level, iron accumulation promotes ROS generation that exceeds antioxidant capacity, resulting in mitochondrial impairment and ultimately contributing to cellular senescence. Notably, cerebral iron deposition disrupts neural homeostasis and serves as a significant pathogenic factor in multiple age-related neurodegenerative disorders[24].. The latest research results show that dietary iron intake is negatively correlated with age acceleration in the American population [25], However, the role of aging in modifying the link between dietary iron intake and CMM development remains unclear.

Dietary iron intake critically influences physiological function at multiple biological levels, including cellular homeostasis, tissue integrity, and organ system performance [26]. Aging is a complex, progressive process characterized by the gradual decline in functional reserve, regenerative capacity, and structural integrity across cellular, tissue, and organ systems [27]. The ideal measurement strategy for biological aging should include as many system indicators as possible [28]. For predicting disease, the PhenoAge and Klemera–Doubal method Biological Age are the best-validated algorithms according to blood-chemistry–derived measures in multi-ethnic cohorts of older adults [29]. Age acceleration refers to the phenomenon where an individual’s biological age advances more rapidly than their chronological age. This concept goes beyond the mere passage of time, as it emphasizes the underlying biological processes and pathological changes, which has been shown in previous studies to be positively associated with the incidence of CMM [30].

Therefore, we conducted a cohort study in the Chinese Health and Nutrition Survey (CHNS) in people aged 18 years and older to investigate the effects of dietary iron intake and biological age on the risk of CMM, as well as the mediating role of biological age in the association of dietary iron intake with the incidence of CMM.

Method

Study population

CHNS is a large-scale, ongoing, prospective, family-based cohort study initiated in 1989—conducted every 2–4 years with 10 rounds completed by 2015—that monitors the health and nutritional status of the Chinese population during socioeconomic development by collecting household- and individual-level data (e.g., socio-demographics, diet, physical activity, and health behaviors) as well as community-level urbanization and economic changes, with detailed survey methods described elsewhere [12] [13].

Our data used in the present study were derived from CHNS covering 2009 to 2015. To ensure the validity of the cohort analysis, participants were excluded based on the following criteria: first, 3,259 participants were excluded, including those aged < 18 years, those pregnant at baseline data collection, and those with missing baseline data (3-day dietary intake data, biological age data, or CMM related information). Then, 254 participants who died or were lost to follow-up before the 2nd survey were excluded, and finally, 91 participants with confirmed CMM at baseline were excluded. Ultimately, 5945 participants were included in the study (Fig. 1).

Dietary iron intake assessment

The survey was conducted by an international team of researchers whose backgrounds include nutrition, public health, economics, sociology, Chinese studies, and demography. In every survey, trained nutritionists carry out face-to-face dietary evaluations. For individual dietary information gathering, standardized questionnaires are adopted; specifically, each participant undergoes a 24-hour dietary recall, which is administered on three randomly selected consecutive days within a week. At the same time, to measure household food consumption, a mixed approach of weighing and measuring is employed. This method calculates food usage based on the difference in household food stock between the start and end of each day, and dietary intake is presented as the average daily consumption per person. As for the amount of cooking oil and seasonings consumed by each household member, it is estimated by allocating the total household food intake in proportion to individual factors. Detailed accounts of these dietary measurement procedures have been published in earlier studies, and the reliability of the dietary assessment methods has also been verified in previous research [31, 32].

The overall dietary intake of each participant was determined by combining two parts of data: the information obtained from the individual dietary recall and the consumption data of cooking oil and condiments derived from the household food stock [33]. When assessing nutrient intake, the nutrient content of each type of food was first multiplied by its standardized serving size. Then, the resulting values for all foods were summed up in accordance with the guidelines provided in the China Food Composition Table (CFCT) to get the total nutrient intake [14, 15]. Nutrient and energy intakes were calculated by multiplying food consumption amounts by their respective nutrient values per 100 g portion from the CFCT. Heme iron was estimated to constitute 40% of total iron content in animal tissues (including red meat, poultry, fish, and organs) [16], non-heme iron was calculated by adding the iron content of all non-meat foods to 60% of the iron content of all animal tissues, logarithmically transformed and energy-adjusted using the residual method.

Biological age assessment

The Biological Age Assessment project has developed novel biological age algorithms using a well-defined panel of biomarkers. These algorithms were trained on the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey NHANES III dataset and subsequently validated in the NHANES IV dataset, enabling comparative evaluation against both internally derived and published algorithms [34]. Twelve biomarkers were selected “sbp”, “totchol”, “trig”, “hba1c”, “bun”, “lncreat”, “albumin”, “lncrp”, “rbc”, “uap”, “glucose” and “wbc” were similar to the biomarkers developed by Zuyun Liu [26] in CHNS for calculating biological age. The blood chemistry-derived metrics including Klemerae Doubal method (KDM), phenotypic age (PA), and homeostatic dysregulation (HD) were initially trained using NANES III data and the methods originally proposed by Nakazato et al. [35], Klemera et al. [29] and Levine et al. [36]. The R package “BioAge” provides the corresponding algorithms and R code and is available at https://github.com/dayoonkwon/BioAge. HD method quantifies physiological divergence from young healthy reference populations using Mahalanobis distance, while the KDM evaluates systemic integrity decline through biomarker-age regression analysis in reference cohorts [34]. PA algorithm was developed to estimate mortality risk by applying elastic net Gompertz regression to multiple mortality-associated biomarkers [37]. Adaptive load (AL) covers the combined effects of chronic stress and life events on an individual’s physical health and is determined by assessing the proportion of biomarker values indicative of an individual’s increased risk [38]. In our study, elevated risk status was defined as falling within the highest quartile for eleven biomarkers, while for the twelfth biomarker (albumin), the lowest quartile was designated as high-risk based on established inverse clinical associations. Complete quartile distributions were analyzed for all twelve biomarkers [39]. The total adaptive load calculated in this way (ranging from 0 to 1) is defined as the proportion of biomarkers considered “high risk” among the selected 12 biomarkers [40].

To quantify biological aging disparities, participants with KDM or PA estimated ages exceeding their chronological age were classified as exhibiting age acceleration [41]. Higher HD or AL that individuals were at higher risk of homeostatic disorder and physiological health load. For HD and AL, participants were divided into relative homeostasis and dysregulation according to their medians. Individuals with higher HD or AL were regarded as experiencing age acceleration [42].

Healthy diet

The dietary score in this section was constructed with reference to the dietary recommendations of the American Heart Association scoring [21]: (1) intake of no less than 4.5 servings of fruits and vegetables per day (1 serving of fruit weighs 150 g, 1 serving of vegetables weighs 75 g); (2) intake of no less than two 3.5-ounce servings of seafood per week (1 ounce = 28.35 g); (3) intake of no less than three 1-ounce servings of whole grains per day; (4) sodium intake is less than 1500 mg per day; (5) energy intake from sugar-containing beverages is less than 450 kcal per week. One point is awarded for each item that is met, and zero points are awarded for each item that is not met. Therefore, the dietary score ranges from 0 to 5 points, and the upper half of the score range (i.e., 2 to 5 points) is defined as a healthy level, and 0 to 1 point is defined as an unhealthy level. The sodium intake was based on the average per capita consumption of condiments collected by the weighing method and the individual food intake collected by the 24-hour dietary recall. The other four items were based on the individual food intake collected by the 24-hour dietary recall.

Pearson correlation analysis was employed to quantify the strength and statistical significance of the association between dietary scores and different forms of iron intake. The results showed that the correlation coefficients between dietary scores and total iron, non-heme iron, and heme iron were 0.155, 0.150, and 0.145, respectively, with all associated P-values < 0.001. This indicates a statistically significant weak positive correlation between dietary scores and dietary iron intake: as dietary scores increased, the intakes of total iron, non-heme iron, and heme iron showed a slight upward trend. However, all correlation coefficients were below 0.2, suggesting a low degree of linear association between the two and limited explanatory power of dietary scores for iron intake.

Covariates

In the current study, the selected covariates were factors known or suspected to be associated with CMM risk [17, 43–45], which were obtained through a structured questionnaire, including age, gender (male/female), region (rural/urban), region (north/south), education level (None or grad from primary/Lower middle school degree/Upper middle school degree/Above upper middle school degree), personal income level (Low/Medium/High/Very high), waistline (Normal/Increase the risk/High risk), BMI(≤ 28/>28), smoking, drinking, physical activity, healthy diet, antihypertensive drug use (yes/no), and dietary intake of total protein, carbohydrates, fat, calcium, potassium, magnesium, and zinc.

The rationale for including the selected nutrient covariates (total protein, carbohydrates, fat, calcium, potassium, magnesium, and zinc) is outlined below. Calcium, magnesium, potassium, and zinc are essential minerals for human physiological functions. Calcium deficiency or imbalance can affect multiple body systems, potentially influencing CMM risk [46]. Magnesium is involved in energy metabolism and muscle/nerve function, and has been linked to cardiovascular health [47]. Potassium is vital for maintaining fluid balance, regulating blood pressure, and ensuring proper heart function, with its dysregulation linked to an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases [48]. Zinc, as a cofactor for numerous enzymes, participates in immune function, cell growth, and repair processes, and its role in overall health might be relevant to CMM development [49]. Macronutrients, such as total protein, carbohydrates, and fat, are fundamental to energy supply and body structure maintenance. Their metabolism is directly linked to cardiometabolic disorders [50–52]. Thus, considering these potential associations, these nutrients were selected as covariates to comprehensively assess their relationships with CMM risk in the study.

CMM assessment

The primary outcome was CMM, the development of at least ≥ 2 of the following CMM: hypertension, diabetes, stroke, and myocardial infarction. At each follow-up visit, participants’ medical history was comprehensively assessed by answering the following question, “What was your doctor’s diagnosis of your disease or injury?” “Has your doctor ever told you that you have high blood pressure/diabetes/myocardial infarction/stroke?” “How old were you when you had your first accident?” “Have you had an incident like this in the last year?” “Have you used any of these therapies?” “Are you currently taking anti-hypertensive medication?” Two additional criteria were used in the diagnosis of hypertension: (1) a mean of three systolic blood pressure measurements ≥ 140 mmHg; and (2) a mean of three diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg. Blood samples were only collected for analysis in 2009, and therefore we used fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% in 2009 (48 mmol/mol) as diagnostic criteria for diabetes mellitus [53].

Ethical consideration

CHNS received ethical approval from the University of North Carolina and the National Institute of Nutrition and Food Safety of the former Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Statistical analysis

Participants were categorized into five groups (Q1-Q5) based on their total dietary iron intake by quintiles. Continuous variables in baseline characteristics were expressed as mean and standard deviation (SD) or median (IQR), and categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) or chi-square test was used to compare the differences in total iron intake between the two groups.

Three multifactorial proportional risk regression models were developed to assess the association between dietary iron intake and CMM risk. Model 1 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, waist circumference, body mass index, smoking status, drinking status, physical activity, dietary status, region, area, education, and personal income level, antihypertensive drug use status and Model 3 was adjusted for the variables in Model 2 and added dietary intake of total protein, carbohydrate, fat, calcium, potassium, magnesium, and zinc. Linear trends were tested by assigning median dietary iron intake to each group. In addition, the dose-response relationships between dietary iron intake and biological age were examined using restricted cubic spline (RCS) after adjusting for the above confounding variables. Furthermore, risk ratios derived from Cox models and absolute risks (AR) at 6-year follow-up for the associations between biological age and CMM risk were estimated and presented. We used an interaction model to test for potential effect corrections. All analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.0). All statistical tests were two-tailed and P < 0.05.

The mediating effect analysis was performed with R (version 4.5.0). The dependent variable (y) was CMM; the independent variable (x) was dietary iron intake; the mediator (m) was biological age. The mediating effect was determined according to the indirect effect and the completely standardized indirect effect of biological age results. Results are presented as standardized Beta, reporting confidence intervals of 95% (95% CI), t, and p-values (p). The alpha level was set at p = 0.05.

Two-group sensitivity analyses were performed. First, we performed data analysis on the data before performing the multiple interpolation method. Second, considering that the prevalence of CMM varied according to age and gender, we stratified the participants according to age (≤ 45 years or > 45 years) and gender (male/female).

The influence of dietary iron intake and biological age on the incidence of CMM

A total of 318 new cases of CMM occurred during the 6-year follow-up. Higher total iron intake (Q1 vs. Q5 HR: 0.74, 95% CI: 0.57–0.97), and non-heme iron intake (Q1 vs. Q5 HR: 0.69, 95% CI: 0.53–0.90) were associated with a lower risk of CMM, whereas the association between heme iron intake and the risk of developing CMM was not statistically significant (Table 2). Biological age acceleration groups including KMD (HR: 3.41, 95% CI: 2.95–3.95), PA (HR: 3.36, 95% CI: 2.88–3.92), HD_log (HR: 4.33, 95% CI: 3.55–5.28), and AL (HR: 3.42, 95% CI: 2.91–4.03) showed a significantly higher risk of CMM compared to the age deceleration groups. RCS analysis showed that KMD (p-nonlinearity < 0.0001, p-overall association < 0.0001, Fig. 2A), PA (p-nonlinearity = 0.0009, Fig. 2B), HD (p-nonlinearity < 0.0001, Fig. 2C), and AL (p-nonlinearity = 0.0009, Fig. 2D) were all associated with the risk of CMM in a J-shaped relationship. This J-shaped association can be intuitively reflected in each figure through the change curves of HR and absolute risk, and statistical tests all show significant nonlinear associations and overall associations.

Sensitivity analysis

To validate the robustness of main findings, we conducted the following sensitivity analyses. First, we recalculated the data before multiple imputation and found that the results were consistent with the primary study findings (Table S1).

Second, we performed stratified analyses by sex (male and female) to explore potential sex differences in the association between dietary iron intake and CMM incidence. The results showed that in females, highest iron intake (Q5) was significantly associated with a reduced incidence of CMM (total iron: HR = 0.64, 95% CI 0.44–0.94; non-heme iron: HR = 0.63, 95% CI 0.43–0.92), whereas no significant association was observed in males (Table S2). In terms of age stratification (Table S2), we stratified participants into two age groups (≤ 45 and > 45 years) to examine the influence of age on the relationship between iron intake and CMM. The results showed that among participants > 45 years, higher iron intake (Q5) was significantly associated with a reduced incidence of CMM (total iron: HR = 0.67, 95% CI 0.50–0.90; non-heme iron: HR = 0.63, 95% CI 0.47–0.84), whereas no significant association was observed in the ≤ 45-year group.

Finally, considering that heme iron content is not available in the CFCT, we conducted a sensitivity analysis by assuming three different proportions of heme iron in animal tissues (30%, 40%, and 50%) to test the impact of exposure misclassification on the results (Table S3). The results showed that the protective effects of total iron and non-heme iron remained stable across all three proportions: for total iron, the continuous variable was consistently associated with a 1% reduced CMM risk (all p < 0.001), and the highest quintile (Q5) showed a 31% lower risk (HR = 0.69, 95% CI 0.53–0.91, p < 0.01) in all scenarios. For non-heme iron, the continuous variable was associated with a 1% reduced risk (all p < 0.05), and the highest quintile (Q5) showed a 33%−36% lower risk (all p < 0.01). For heme iron, the continuous variable showed a weak but significant protective effect across different proportions (HR = 0.91–0.96, all p < 0.05), with no significant dose-response relationship in quintile analyses. These results confirm that the association between dietary iron intake and CMM is not affected by the assumption of heme iron proportion, further verifying the robustness of our findings.

Discussion

In this cohort study, higher dietary intake of total iron and non-heme iron, as well as age deceleration, were associated with a reduced of CMM. Total dietary iron and dietary nonheme iron intake showed U-shaped associations with biological age markers, whereas dietary heme iron intake showed an inverted L-shaped association with biological age markers. Meanwhile, sex significantly modulates the mediating association between dietary iron intake and CMM risk. In females, dietary iron intake exerts significant indirect effects on CMM via biological age. In males, the mediating effect is extremely weak and non-significant.

In this study, higher intake of dietary total iron and nonheme iron was associated with a lower risk of CMM, while heme iron was not associated with the incident of CMM. As previously reported, many studies have concluded that higher dietary total iron and nonheme iron intake is negatively associated with the risk of hypertension [54, 55], diabetes [56], cardiovascular disease [57, 58] and stroke [59]. Similarly, numerous studies have also reported that heme iron was not associated with the incidence of T2D [56, 60, 61]. Age further stratified the protective effect of dietary iron. The effect was significant in adults >45 years but absent in those ≤ 45 years. This is likely because iron absorption declines after 45 years—adequate iron intake compensates for reduced reserves and inhibits age-related metabolic disorders [62], amplifying CMM protection. No significant associations were observed in the ≤ 45-year group, possibly due to low CMM incidence and robust iron metabolism/weak biological aging in younger individuals [8], making dietary iron’s regulatory effects undetectable.

In our study, biological age is a strong positive predictor of CMM and a key mediator between dietary iron intake and CMM risk. In the total population, participants with accelerated biological aging had a 3.5–5.2-fold higher CMM risk than those with age deceleration. Previous study showed that a longitudinal study of 341,159 UK adults, with a median follow-up of 8.84 years, revealed that individuals with an advanced biological age faced an elevated risk of progressing from an initial CMD to CMM[63]. Another study of 415,147 individuals with a mean follow-up of 11 years demonstrated that biological aging follows a trajectory from CMD-free status to CMD, CMM, and ultimately dementia or death, with accelerated aging increasing CMD/CMM risk [64]. The association between biological aging and CMM arises from overlapping pathophysiological mechanisms in hypertension, dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and hyperglycemia, mediated through shared pathways including chronic inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and cellular senescence [65, 66]. Inflammation, oxidative stress, and immune disorders, which are important drivers of aging, are also mechanistically involved in the pathogenesis of insulin resistance [67, 68]. Furthermore, the structure and function of blood vessels change during the aging process, including arteriosclerosis, which promotes hypertension and increases the risk of cardiovascular disease [69]. Genetic susceptibility and age-related epigenetic changes not only influence the biological aging process, but also increase susceptibility to CMM [70]. Crucially, the impact of aging lies in reducing the functional reserve and regenerative potential of tissues and organs, thereby increasing the body’s susceptibility to metabolic disorders. This progressive functional decline constitutes an important pathological basis for the evolution from chronic physiological stress to overt cardiovascular metabolic diseases and their complications [71].

In this study, total dietary iron and dietary nonheme iron intake showed U-shaped associations with biological age markers, whereas dietary heme iron intake showed an inverted L-shaped association with biological age markers. Iron is an essential micronutrient in the diet and plays a central role in several key physiological processes. It is an important component of hemoglobin and participates in oxygen transport; it also plays an indispensable role in DNA biosynthesis, the mitochondrial electron transport chain, and muscle contraction [72]. However, free iron is toxic and causes oxidative damage to cell membranes, proteins and lipids [73]. Research shows that both iron deficiency and iron overload can promote reactive oxygen species production through different pathways, enhance oxidative stress reactions, and induce inflammation, which may ultimately lead to mitochondrial dysfunction [74, 75]. This could explain the U-shaped correlation of total dietary iron and dietary non-heme iron intake with biologic age markers, as either too low or too high dietary total iron and non-heme iron intake can cause damage.

Recent studies have confirmed that meat consumption significantly affects human metabolic homeostasis through its high saturated fatty acid and cholesterol content, and may play a potential role in age acceleration process. These components have been proven to be closely related to a variety of metabolic disorders [76]. A high-protein diet indirectly affects the body’s metabolic function and aging process by altering the structure and metabolic activity of the gut microbiota [77, 78]. A decrease in gut microbiota diversity and an increase in harmful bacteria may trigger persistent mild inflammation, which in turn promotes aging through multiple pathways [79, 80]. The high-meat diet is significantly associated with reduced the levels of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) produced by the gut microbiota, thereby affecting the body’s energy metabolism and immune function. SCFAs play an important role in maintaining metabolic health [81–83]. Notably, as the primary source of dietary heme iron, animal-based iron sources exhibit a significantly complex and even paradoxical association with adiposity and health outcomes, which further provides evidence for the inverted L-shaped relationship between heme iron and biological age. Epidemiological studies have shown that moderate intake of heme iron-rich poultry and fish can reduce the risk of central obesity by improving iron deficiency-related mitochondrial dysfunction [84]. Since central obesity is an accelerating factor for biological aging [85], its alleviation may indirectly delay the progression of biological age. However, excessive intake of heme iron from red meat leads to incomplete metabolism in the body [86], resulting in iron overload. This activates the NLRP3 inflammasome in the liver, promotes the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and thereby induces adipocyte apoptosis and ectopic lipid deposition, exacerbating adiposity disorders [84]. Collectively, these findings validate the observed association pattern between heme iron and biological age in this study, and enrich the understanding of how animal-based iron sources influence metabolic aging through multiple pathways.

A sex-specific role of biological age in linking dietary iron to CMM was observed in this study. In females, dietary iron intake exerts significant indirect effects on CMM via biological age. However, the mediating effect is extremely weak and non-significant in males. This may be related to the disruption of iron homeostasis. Disrupted iron homeostasis can induce cellular damage by generating hydroxyl radicals, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, brain aging, and even organismal aging [24]. Accelerated biological aging, in turn, increases the prevalence of CMM [63]. Due to periodic menstruation, adult females experience significant loss of iron stores in the body, requiring more dietary iron intake than males and facing greater difficulty in maintaining iron homeostasis [87]. After menopause, females have a lower basal metabolic rate compared to males [88]; improper dietary iron intake makes them more prone to iron overload, which disrupts iron homeostasis. Meanwhile, the decline in estrogen levels post-menopause reduces antioxidant capacity [89], making cells more vulnerable to iron-induced ROS-mediated damage [24], thereby accelerating biological aging. Additionally, females inherently have a higher baseline prevalence of CMM than males [90], which further amplifies this effect.

Our study has some limitations. First, the self-reported assessment of dietary factors may be subject to recall bias. Second, data on lipid-lowering drug use and family history of cardiometabolic disease were not available in CHNS. The absence of these variables may introduce residual confounding. For example, individuals using lipid-lowering drugs likely have a lower baseline cardiometabolic risk, and without adjusting for this, our estimates of the association between dietary iron intake, biological age, and CMM risk may be biased. Similarly, family history of cardiometabolic disease reflects genetic susceptibility to these conditions, and its omission could mask or exaggerate the observed associations. Third, due to the lack of heme iron content data in the CFCT, we assumed that “heme iron accounts for 40% of the total iron content in all animal tissues” to estimate heme iron intake. Although sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of our core findings, this assumption may still introduce minor exposure measurement error, as the actual heme iron proportion varies across animal tissue types. Fourth, It should be objectively noted that although components such as vitamin C and phytic acid in the diet may affect iron absorption [91], they have not been further included in the adjustment due to limitations in current data availability and the focus of the analysis. Theoretically, there is a certain risk of residual confounding. Nevertheless, based on the aforementioned correlation results, the impact of this potential confounding on the overall association trend is limited. The existing analysis can still initially reveal the characteristics of the association between the two, which is sufficient to meet the core conclusion requirements of the current research stage. Fifth, the study was conducted exclusively in Chinese adults, whose dietary patterns and genetic backgrounds differ from those of Western cohorts. This may limit the generalizability of our results to other populations, and future multicenter studies in diverse ethnic groups are needed to validate our conclusions. Finally, given the observational nature of this cohort study, we cannot establish a causal relationship between dietary iron intake, biological age, and CMM risk—residual confounding from unmeasured factors cannot be completely ruled out, so the results should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that that higher intake of total iron and non-heme iron are negatively correlated with the risk of CMM, while age acceleration is significantly associated with an increased risk of CMM. In female participants, the relationship between dietary heme iron intake and CMM risk was partially mediated by biological aging. Our research results emphasize the need for further efforts to enhance the primary prevention of CMM by consuming adequate total iron, and non-heme iron. Study individual subtypes of different genders, ages, genetic backgrounds and dietary habits, conduct large-scale and well-designed prospective cohort studies, further analyze the mediating role of biological age in the correlation between dietary iron intake and the incidence of CMM, and determine the optimal intakes of total iron and non-heme iron to prevent the risk of CMM.
