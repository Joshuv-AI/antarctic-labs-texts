Genetic dissection of plant architecture, development, and seed morphology in exotic × elite Upland cotton populations
Authors: Hrithik Mangla, Ankush Sharma, Min Liu, Tariq Shehzad, Gary J Pierce, Rahul Chandnani, Sayan Das, Sameer Khanal, Jason G Wallace, John L Snider, Don C Jones, Peng W Chee, Andrew H Paterson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) breeding has primarily emphasized fiber yield and quality, yet plant architectural, developmental, and seed morphological traits also play key roles in productivity and management. Here, we dissect the genetic basis of four such traits: plant height (HT), number of nodes with one branch (N1B), days to flowering (DTF), and average surface area per seed (ASAS) using exotic × elite Upland cotton populations. Both population‐specific and joint analyses of the populations identified 17 QTLs collectively, with ASAS having the largest number of detected QTLs. The exotic line T326 showed the highest potential for the improvement of ASAS. While HT, N1B, and DTF displayed limited genetic variance in these populations, gene ontology enrichment highlighted the CCR4–NOT complex as a potential shared regulatory hub connecting ASAS and DTF. In addition, ASAS showed a highly significant positive correlation and overlapping QTL regions with fiber strength and upper half mean length described in a companion paper, suggesting the possibility of strong linkage or pleiotropic effects of these genomic regions, which could be beneficial commercially. Overall, the data suggested no prevalence of negative allelic trade‐offs or linkage drag from these secondary traits for the improvement of fiber quality traits, underscoring the value of exotic germplasm for simultaneous enhancement of fiber quality and seed morphology in Upland cotton breeding.

INTRODUCTION

Cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.) is a major global cash crop cultivated for fiber, with seed oil as a secondary product (M. Wu et al., 2022). Breeding efforts in Upland cotton have focused on fiber yield and quality, leading to significant improvements in these commercially important traits (Baghyalakshmi et al., 2024; Sharma et al., 2025). However, plant architectural characteristics, developmental timing, and seed morphology also strongly influence yield and management (Z.‐Y. Liu et al., 2020; Sakamoto & Matsuoka, 2004). Optimizing these secondary traits can enhance how cotton plants utilize resources and fit into production systems, ultimately impacting fiber yield and harvesting efficiency. A few of these traits are plant height (HT), number of nodes with one branch (N1B), days to flowering (DTF), and average surface area per seed (ASAS), which have received relatively less attention in cotton improvement despite their importance in determining plant productivity and crop value.

Plant height and branch distribution are key components of crop architecture that affect light interception, planting density, and compatibility with mechanization (Qi et al., 2017; Reta‐Sánchez & Fowler, 2002). These traits exhibit a quantitative, polygenic mode of inheritance (Liu et al., 2020; Su et al., 2018; Z. Zhang et al., 2019), often involving complex gene interactions. Both additive and epistatic genetic effects have been reported to contribute to variation in HT (B.‐H. Wang et al., 2006), and quantitative trait locus (QTL) studies have even identified major‐effect loci with dominance contributing to heterosis for height (Shang et al., 2016). Plant height essentially reflects the combined length of all internodes and has direct and indirect implications for yield. An optimal stature is critical for mechanical harvesting (Shang et al., 2016; Su et al., 2018), and HT influences biomass allocation as taller plants tend to invest more in stem growth, whereas shorter, more compact plants can channel relatively more assimilates into reproductive structures. Notably, bushy cotton plant types with moderate height have shown higher lint yields per plant and greater yield stability across planting densities than excessively tall plants (Kaggwa‐Asiimwe et al., 2013; Su et al., 2018). Thus, breeding for moderately short, compact cultivars is expected to facilitate higher planting densities and improved lint yield per unit area.

Another architectural trait, number of nodes with one branch, represents the count of main stem nodes that bear a single fruiting branch (sympodial branch) (Figure S1). In typical cotton plants that produce a single fruiting branch at each consecutive node, N1B effectively corresponds to the total number of fruiting branches. This trait has a direct positive relationship with yield potential, since each fruiting branch carries multiple bolls and thus contributes to the fiber output (Chandnani et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2020). Prior studies have noted that N1B varies in a continuous manner in segregating populations (Liu et al., 2020), consistent with polygenic quantitative inheritance. Interestingly, several QTLs for the number of fruiting branches have been found to co‐localize with QTLs for HT (Liu et al., 2020), suggesting that common genetic factors or tightly linked genes coordinately regulate HT and branch number. In practical terms, an ideal cotton plant would balance HT and fruiting branch number to maximize boll production while maintaining a manageable plant size (X. Zhang et al., 2020).

Alongside architecture, the timing of developmental transitions such as flowering onset is vitally important in cotton cultivation. Days to flowering, the number of days from planting to first flower, is a major determinant of crop maturity. Early flowering and maturation often allow cotton to escape late‐season stresses and fit into crop rotations (Basbag et al., 2007). Quantitative genetic analyses have identified multiple genomic regions controlling flowering time in cotton, reflecting its polygenic basis (Guo et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2016; C. Li et al., 2013; L. Li et al., 2021). Many of these QTLs have individually small effects, indicating that flowering time is governed by numerous genes with modest contributions. Nevertheless, cumulative genetic variation can be exploited to adjust the flowering period. Therefore, understanding and manipulating genetic factors underlying DTF are critical for breeding cotton that can reliably complete its life cycle in shorter growing seasons or in higher latitude environments.

Average surface area per seed has been largely overlooked in cotton breeding to date. Cotton fiber, trichomes that develop from the seed coat epidermis, has a direct relationship with seed surface properties (Jan et al., 2022). A greater surface area per seed potentially provides more physical space for fiber initials to form, which could translate into more fibers and eventually higher lint yield per seed (Bednarz et al., 2007). ASAS is directly related to other seed measurements such as seed weight, average seed length, and average seed width. To our knowledge, few prior studies have specifically examined variation in these seed morphological traits or their genetic control (W. Wang et al., 2019). Incorporating seed traits, such as ASAS, into cotton breeding could open new avenues for improving yield that complement traditional selection on boll number and fiber quality (Bednarz et al., 2007; W. Wang et al., 2019).

Owing to the efforts of McCarty et al. over the past decades, exotic germplasm from wild or unadapted cotton races (often hailing from tropical Mesoamerican regions) has been converted into photoperiod insensitive varieties to facilitate their utilization in long‐day flowering conditions (McCarty & Jenkins, 2002, 2005; McCarty & Station et al., 1979). These day‐neutral converted exotic race stocks of cotton offer a valuable reservoir of underutilized genetic diversity for improving commercially important traits (McCarty et al., 1996, 1998, 2004, 2007). Although the performance for all four traits included in this study has not been reported before for this exotic germplasm, previous studies have reported some favorable estimated genetic effects for HT (McCarty et al., 1996).

Previously, we selected a small subset of exotic converted race stocks, representing most of the botanical races of G. hirsutum, to generate elite × exotic populations (Mangla, Liu, Vitrakoti, Somala, et al., 2025). These populations facilitated QTL mapping for fiber quality and yield traits, which suggested a promising high frequency of potentially beneficial exotic alleles for fiber quality (Mangla, Liu, Vitrakoti, Somala, et al., 2025). However, the potential for improving fiber yield was limited, indicating possible linkage drag arising from the detected QTL regions between fiber quality and yield (Mangla, Liu, Vitrakoti, Shehzad, et al., 2025). Along with fiber quality and yield components, these populations were also phenotyped for the four secondary traits mentioned above. In this study, we dissect the genomic regions governing these traits, along with a detailed analysis of the potential of these exotic lines to contribute favorable alleles for these secondary traits. In addition, their congruence with previously generated fiber quality and yield QTL, plus the possible candidate genes within the detected QTL regions, will also be reported.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Generation of populations and phenotyping

The current study includes five BC1F2 and one F3 populations where five selected exotic converted racestocks (T326, T281, T257, T063, T1046) were crossed with an elite representative, “DES56” (May et al., 1995; Suszkiw, 2010). For the BC1F2 populations, F1 plants were backcrossed to “DES56” followed by the selfing of four random BC1F1s to generate four BC1F2 families for each population. For the F3 or intermated (IM) population, one of the exotics (T1046) was crossed to “DES56” followed by the selfing of the F1 and further selection of four random F2 plants, which were selfed to generate the final F3 population, consisting of four F3 families.

These populations were planted in 2012 at the Plant Science farm of the University of Georgia using an unreplicated augmented block design. The final number of lines generated for each population was 168 (T0326 × DES56), 179 (T0257 × DES56), 180 (T1046 × DES56), 176 (T1046 × DES56 [IM]), 162 (T0281 × DES56), and 179 (T063 × DES56). For convenience, these populations will now be referred to as 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6, respectively. In the next year (2013), BC1F2:3 and F3:4 progeny rows were grown from the individually harvested BC1F1 and F2 plants using unreplicated augmented block design similar to the last year, respectively. These populations were phenotyped for the abovementioned four traits—HT, N1B, DTF, and ASAS in both 2012 and 2013. Plant height was measured as the length in centimeters from ground level to the top of the plant. N1B was simply counted as the number of nodes that had a single fruiting branch. DTF was measured as the number of days it took from planting to the appearance of the first flower (anthesis). ASAS was measured as the average surface area of the 30 seeds for each line using the Winseedle system (2D projected area) and was reported in square millimeters (mm2).

Phenotypic data analysis

The collected phenotypic data for all four traits in both years were subjected to various statistical analyses, including analysis of variance (ANOVA) for genotypic and environmental (year) effects. Since each genotype (line) was evaluated only once in each environment, the inclusion of genotype × environment in the ANOVA model resulted in no residual degrees of freedom; hence, the analysis was restricted to genotype and environment. However, segregating alleles were tested in multiple environments and still permit assessment of the stability of their effects.

Boxplots were generated for the performance of each population in different years for each trait, along with the Pearson's correlation coefficients among different pairs of four traits. Genotyping and variant calling

Genotyping and variant calling of these materials has been described previously (Mangla, Liu, Vitrakoti, Somala, et al., 2025). In brief, each line was subjected to DNA extraction followed by genotyping‐by‐sequencing (GBS) library preparation. These GBS libraries were sequenced using BGI's DNA nanoball sequencing technology that generated about 400–450 million paired‐end reads per single run. The sequenced files were further processed using a series of operations to generate the final variant calling files. The progenies with >90% of the sites missing, as well as the sites with greater than 30% of the missing progenies, were eliminated. This was followed by the selection of the sites with a minor allele frequency (MAF) of 0.10 or more, since each segregating family is expected to have an MAF of 0.5; thus, for a single population within that family, the minimum MAF is estimated to be 0.5/4 = 0.12 ∼ 0.10. For the joint population, we used a, MAF parameter of 0.02 (∼0.5/24). Following the imputation of the missing sites by “BEAGLE” (Browning et al., 2021), some of the sites showing inconsistency with adjacent sites were manually eliminated to generate the physical maps, since the population structure within each population hindered our ability to generate robust genetic maps. The resulting genotypic file/physical maps were employed for further QTL mapping analysis.

Association or QTL mapping

Using the above genotypic files and the phenotypic data, we used the genotype association model in TASSELv5.2 (Bradbury et al., 2007) with an additional covariate for the family to reduce spurious associations due to the family structure within the populations (Equation 1). This genotype model studies dominance effects after fitting the additive model, hence providing the p‐values for both types of effects to determine the mode of action for the detected QTL. Further, for QTLs with a dominant mode of action, the type of dominance was determined by calculating the ratio of estimated dominance effect/additive effect (d/a) and further taking the absolute (abs) of that ratio, such that if 0 < abs(d/a) < 1, it was declared partially dominant and if abs(d/a) > 1, the QTL was declared to possess a significant overdominant mode of action:

In the above equation, Y signifies the vector for the phenotypic values for different individuals; XG and BG denote the design matrix and corresponding fixed effects for the markers, respectively. Similarly, af and uf signify the family of each individual and its corresponding effect, respectively, with e as the normally distributed residual variance. For population‐specific analyses, af had values from 1 to 4 representing 4 families within each population, while for the joint population analysis, af had values from 1 to 24 representing all six populations with four families each.

The significance thresholds for population‐specific and joint analysis were determined using the Bonferroni correction (Haynes, 2013) at a p‐value of 0.05 to correct for multiple comparisons, which gave cutoff values of about 10−5 and 10−6, respectively. Moreover, the QTL likelihood intervals were established as the genomic regions adjacent to those of actual associations, where the p‐value is less than 10−3 and less than 10−4, respectively, for both approaches. The naming convention applied to the identified QTLs was q{trait name}_{population name (for analyses specific to the population)} _{environment (1 for 2012 and 2 for 2013)}_{chromosome name}_{1 to number of QTL detected on that chromosome for the same trait, population and environment (optional)}.

Candidate gene analysis

To identify candidate genes underlying the detected QTL, we first defined QTL support intervals as the genomic regions spanning the peak marker and flanking sites with significance thresholds (p < 1 × 10− 3 for population‐specific analyses and p < 1 × 10− 4 for joint analyses), with a maximum extension of 250–500 kb around the lead SNP to avoid overly wide windows. Genes located within these intervals were retrieved from the G. hirsutum UTXv3.1 reference annotation (Sreedasyam et al., 2024), and overlapping or redundant intervals were merged to generate non‐redundant gene lists. Gene Ontology enrichment was conducted in R using clusterProfiler (enrichGO; hypergeometric test, Benjamini‐Hochberg correction) across BP/MF/CC ontologies, pAdjustMethod = “BH”, minGSSize = 10, maxGSSize = 5000, and significance at FDR < 0.05; redundant terms were reduced with clusterProfiler::simplify (semantic similarity cutoff 0.7) (T. Wu et al., 2021). KEGG enrichment used enrichKEGG (T. Wu et al., 2021) with the same multiple‐testing settings.

RESULTS

Parental analysis and ANOVA

Parental values for all four traits (Table S1a) suggest that DES56 (the elite line) had lower values for HT and ASAS than most of the exotic lines in 2012 and 2013. Meanwhile, DTF was also nominally lower in 2013 for DES56 than for most of the exotics. ANOVA suggested only ASAS to be statistically different among different parental lines (p‐value < 0.05) (Table S1b).

To investigate the effect of line (genotype) and environment on the measured traits, ANOVA was carried out in each population separately (Table S2), providing a higher sample size than for parental samples. The effect of environment (year) was highly significant (p‐value < 1E‐06) for all traits and populations (Table S2). The genotypic effect was highly significant (p‐value < 0.0001) for ASAS in all populations; however, DTF was significant (p‐value < 0.05) in only populations 2 and 5 (Table S2). HT and N1B were significant in both populations 1 and 5, with HT also significant in the population 2 (Table S2).

Phenotypic distribution

Overall, all populations except 4 and 5 showed low mean values for DTF in both years (Figure 1a). For HT, populations 3 and 6 showed comparatively lower mean values (Figure 1a). There was a large difference between HT values for each population between years, which indicates a large effect of environmental factors on this trait, which is also evident in the ANOVA results for each population (Table S2).

Broad sense heritabilities and correlation analysis

Plot‐level heritability or broad‐sense heritability values (H 2) were calculated for each population as well as for the joint population (Table S3 and Figure 2). Overall, ASAS showed the highest broad‐sense H 2 values, whereas the other three traits had a very small portion of the phenotypic variation explained by genetic factors (Figure 2).

ASAS and N1B exhibited nearly normal continuous distributions; however, DTF and HT showed continuous distributions deviating slightly from normality (Figure 3). The estimation of Pearson's correlation coefficient divided the four traits into two groups—N1B and HT, and DTF and ASAS—such that the traits within the same group had a significant positive correlation with each other but a significant negative correlation with traits in the other group (Figure 3).

Correlation between the secondary traits and fiber traits

Pearson's correlation coefficients suggested highly significant correlations for these four secondary traits with two fiber yield and six HVI (High Volume Instrument) quality traits (Table 1). Again, these four traits appeared to form two groups— DTF/ASAS and HT/N1B—which had opposite correlations with the eight fiber traits. For example, both DTF and ASAS had significant negative correlations with micronaire (MIC) and short fiber content (SFC), while HT and N1B had significant positive correlations with these two fiber traits (Table 1). The strong and clear correlations among the pairs of these traits raises the possibility of common regulatory mechanisms or pleiotropic effects of some genomic regions.

Average surface area per seed

As anticipated based on the highest broad sense heritability values, the largest number of QTLs (10) were detected for ASAS, all showing an additive mode of action (Table 2). T326 (the exotic parent in population 1) contributed the favorable allele for nine QTLs, while two of these also had a favorable allele originating from T1046. For the remaining QTL (qASAS_pop5_2_D09), DES56 (the elite line) conferred the incremental allele. This superiority of T326 for multiple alleles also possibly explains the superior performance (largest mean values) of the population 1 for ASAS in both years (Figure 1b).

Most of the QTLs detected by population‐specific analyses had percent variance explained (PVE) values between 8 and 14%, while joint analyses suggested lower PVE values between 1% and 3%, consistent with our previous results for fiber quality and yield (Mangla, Liu, Vitrakoti, Somala, et al., 2025; Mangla, Liu, Vitrakoti, Shehzad, et al., 2025). Four pairs of QTLs detected by both population‐specific analysis and joint analysis had largely overlapping intervals and the incremental allele coming from the same exotic line (T326), suggesting the same possible QTL region being detected multiple times by the respective approaches or in different environments. These pairs were:(1) qASAS_pop1_1_A07, qASAS_1_A07_1, and qASAS_2_A07; (2) qASAS_pop1_1_A07_2 and qASAS_1_A07_2; (3) qASAS_pop1_1_A09 and qASAS_1_A09; and (4) qASAS_pop1_1_D07 and qASAS_1_D07 (Table 2 and Figure 4), where possibly the same QTL regions were detected in population 1 and the joint population. In addition, qASAS_1_A07_1 and qASAS_2_A07 were detected by joint analysis of 2012 and 2013 data, respectively (Table 2 and Figure 4). The largely coinciding intervals and the same incremental allele suggest the same QTL being detected in both years, further supporting the stability of this QTL in the face of changing environmental conditions.

Days to flowering

Three QTLs were detected for DTF, where two (qDTF_pop5_1_D12 and qDTF_2_D07) had an overdominant mode of action, while the third (qDTF_pop2_2_D05) showed an additive mode of action (Table 2). The dominant QTL, qDTF_pop5_1_D12, was detected in population 5 with a PVE value of 18.75% and a negative dominance effect of −0.69 units, while qDTF_2_D07 was detected in joint analysis with a PVE value of 2.54% and a positive dominance effect of 5.84 (Table 2 and Figure 4). For the additive QTL, qDTF_pop2_2_D05, DES56 or the elite allele contributed to an increase in DTF, explaining about 12.15% of the phenotypic variation (Table 2).

Plant height

Two additive QTLs, qHT_pop2_1_A11 and qHT_pop4_1_D10, were detected for HT in populations 2 and 4, respectively (Table 2 and Figure 4). qHT_pop2_1_A11 explained about 11.05% of the variance, and the T257 allele contributed to a decrease in HT (Table 2). qHT_pop4_1_D10 was detected on chromosome D10 with a PVE value of 9.87% and the incremental allele originating from T1046 (Table 2 and Figure 4).

Number of nodes with one branch

N1B, typically related to the number of fruiting branches, had relatively low heritability values in most of the populations (Figure 2). Only two QTL were detected, qN1B_pop3_1_D12 and qN1B_1_A11, in population 3 and the joint population, respectively (Table 2 and Figure 4). qN1B_pop3_1_D12 showed an additive mode of action with a PVE value of 13.02% and the incremental allele originating from the elite line DES56 (Table 2). The other QTL (qN1B_1_A11) showed a negative and partially dominant effect with a PVE value of 2.50% (Table 2).

Putative trait‐specific candidate genes in hormonal, RNA, and cytoskeletal pathways

The inspection of genome annotation suggested some genes and pathways that may be early priorities from which to empirically test hypothetical candidates that may contribute to these traits and QTLs. Most of these converged on a few conserved mechanistic axes, suggesting the additional hypothesis that cotton QTLs may largely modify the dosage and sensitivity of known pathways.

For N1B, although some genes with diverse functions were identified within the QTL intervals, enrichment analyses did not indicate any significant hits.

For HT QTLs, enrichment for signal transduction and ion‐transport terms point to genes and pathways that echo classic height biology (Table 3). GA/DELLA and BRI1–BZR1 pathways drive internode elongation, with ion homeostasis modulating cell expansion. The Green Revolution GA‐insensitive Rht‐1/DELLA alleles in wheat validated GA signaling as a height “master dial” (Pearce et al., 2011; van de Velde et al., 2021), while parallel BR evidence spans Arabidopsis, rice, and wheat (Blanco‐Touriñán et al., 2024; Nolan et al., 2023). Cotton height QTL intervals contain receptor‐like kinases, thioredoxins, and transporters consistent with hormone–redox–ion crosstalk. For example, within the qHT_pop2_1_A11 QTL, a xyloglucanendotransglucosylase/hydrolase (XTH) gene (Gohir.A11G229300) (Table 3) highlights cell wall remodeling as a mechanism for elongation (Ishida & Yokoyama, 2022). Within qHT_pop4_1_D10, genes in BR signaling (Gohir.D10G254900) and cytokinin metabolism (Gohir.D10G254400) (Table 3) further implicate hormone homeostasis in height variation. Zinc homeostasis provides an additional potential layer: zinc transporters modulate auxin‐dependent growth in Arabidopsis (Gate et al., 2024; J. Wang et al., 2021), dovetailing with enriched zinc‐transport terms (B. Liu et al., 2022; Stanton et al., 2022).

Both ASAS and DTF exhibit strong enrichment for the CCR4–NOT complex (GO:0030014) and mRNA catabolic processes (Table 3 and Figure 5), with overlapping cotton genes (Table 3). These encode core CCR4–NOT subunits such as deadenylases and scaffolding proteins. In Arabidopsis and rice, CCR4–NOT components (CAF1/CCR4/NOT1) mediate deadenylation‐coupled decay, tuning developmental timing and stress responses (Chalabi Hagkarim & Grand, 2020; Collart, 2016; Schwenk et al., 2021). Genome‐wide surveys further show that decapping/deadenylation regulates many short‐lived regulatory RNAs (Sorenson et al., 2018), while RNA surveillance via nonsense‐mediated decay (NMD) also adjusts floral transition by modulating integrator modules (Arciga‐Reyes et al., 2006; Nasim et al., 2021). This overlap between DTF and ASAS could be a possible explanation for the strong positive phenotypic correlation found above between these traits.

For DTF, the CCR4–NOT/mRNA decay axis likely regulates flowering integrators such as FT/TSF and SOC1, aligning with upstream photoperiod and sugar cues (Table S4c). Additional candidates include RIO kinases (e.g., GOHIR.D07G121500) and FLA proteins (GOHIR.D07G121700) (Table 3), which play roles in cell–wall integrity and meristem signaling (Blümel et al., 2015). Hormonal inputs also emerge: a cytokinin‐activated signaling gene on D07, a jasmonic acid biosynthesis gene on D12, and salicylic acid–related genes (Table 3), consistent with hormone integration into flowering‐time regulation (Bartrina et al., 2025; Singh et al., 2023).

For ASAS, CCR4–NOT–linked decay possibly intersects with hormone and structural regulators (Table S4b). On A09, an auxin‐responsive gene (Gohir.A09G146800) sits at the QTL peak (Table 3), consistent with auxin's role in branch angle and surface growth (Yun et al., 2025). On D07, two UDP‐glycosyltransferases (Gohir.D07G191400, GOHIR.D07G191500) could modulate hormone activity via glycosylation (Table 3), while cytochrome P450s on A07 (e.g., GOHIR.A07G192500) (Table 3) are implicated in GA/BR metabolism. Cytoskeletal enrichments (GO:0015629) map to GOHIR.A09G192600, GOHIR.D09G186800, and GOHIR.D09G229000, linking to actin/tubulin functions in fiber initiation (GhACTIN1, GhCFE1A) (Sharma et al., 2025) (Table 3). Proteostasis modules are also evident: COP9 signalosome (CSN) components (e.g., GOHIR.A07G118200) and D09 ubiquitin ligases (Table 3) support targeted protein degradation as a regulator of seed and fiber development (Dessau et al., 2008).

Noting the large structural variations found across cotton accessions, we further dissected such aberrations using a pan‐genome graph approach (Yang et al., 2026) in the 11 unique QTL regions. The overall density of 59.9 PAVs per Mb (Table S5) suggests the possible contributions of structural variants in addition to single nucleotide polymorphisms leading to variation in the studied traits. However, since none of the parents in this study were included in the referred pan‐genome, enrichment analysis was confined to the TM‐1 “UTX v3.1” reference assembly and provides only indirect indications of the possible contributions of structural variation to the studied traits.

DISCUSSION

Contributions of exotics

Among all four traits studied here, ASAS indicated the most potential for improvement by the utilization of this exotic germplasm, especially the T326 line. This exotic line was estimated to contribute the incremental (favorable) allele at nine QTLs that correspond to four unique genomic regions, as multiple QTLs were detected in different environments or by different approaches, but with highly coinciding positions (Table 2 and Figure 4). At two of these nine QTLs, which comprise a single unique region of the A07 chromosome, T326 and T1046 both conferred a favorable allele for ASAS. This QTL has been detected in both environments (qASAS_1_A07_1 and qASAS_2_A07) using joint analysis and in 2012 using population‐specific analysis (qASAS_pop1_1_A07), which lends greater credibility to its utilization in marker‐assisted selection for increasing ASAS.

For DTF, T257 contributed the favorable allele in the population 2, while the other two QTLs had an overdominant mode of action (Table 2). Only one of these two QTLs, qDTF_pop5_1_D12, exhibited a negative overdominant effect; however, this QTL was not detected in the joint population, which demands further validation before its potential utilization in hybrid production. While the T257 line was estimated to possess significant positive effects for HT in a previous study (McCarty et al., 1996), one of the two additive QTLs (q_HT_pop2_1_A11) detected in our study had the favorable allele originating from the T257 line. This discrepancy could be explained by the fact that the previous study estimated the additive genetic effects for the whole line irrespective of the individual genomic regions, while our estimates are based on the individual QTL region effects, some of which may deviate from the whole line (whole genome) effects. However, with access to high‐density genotyping technologies, we could only employ those genetic regions by targeted marker‐assisted selection. For N1B, neither of the two QTL showed promise by the utilization of the exotic lines (Table 2).

Overall, the line T326 was found to be the most promising source of beneficial alleles for ASAS. For other traits, T257 did show favorable alleles for DTF and HT, but these QTL need further validation to support their employment in marker‐assisted selection.

Congruence with fiber quality and yield traits

Since we utilized the same populations for mapping fiber quality and yield traits, the congruence between the QTLs detected for these respective traits was studied. For ASAS, the trait that showed most of the detected QTL in this study, one QTL (qASAS_2_A07) closely coincided with a fiber strength QTL (Mangla, Liu, Vitrakoti, Somala, et al., 2025), where T326 contributed the favorable allele for both traits. Another QTL, qASAS_pop5_2_D09, had a highly overlapping interval with an upper half mean length QTL and was also very close to a fiber strength QTL (Mangla, Liu, Vitrakoti, Somala, et al., 2025), where elite line DES56 contributed the favorable allele for all three traits. This overlap aligns with the pairwise correlation values found between ASAS and these two fiber quality traits (UHM and STR) (Table 1), where ASAS had highly significant positive correlations of R = 0.341 and 0.228 with STR and UHM, respectively. Since the same allele increases the phenotypic value for ASAS and fiber traits along with a significant positive correlation among them, it suggests the possibility of classic pleiotropic mode of action or the action of two linked QTLs (Meredith, 1977) between the generation of higher surface area per cotton seed and the length and strength of the fiber emerging from the epidermal cells at the seed surface. Transcriptomics studies could further shed light into the specific mode of action responsible for this overlap. Pleiotropy could be highly beneficial from the commercial point of view since it offers the potential for simultaneous improvement of multiple traits.

Inheritance and subgenomic affinity

Overall, additive gene action was prevalent for ASAS and HT in this study (Figure 2). Although HT has been found to possess some highly significant major dominant QTLs in previous studies (Shang et al., 2015), these exotic‐by‐elite crosses do not appear to contribute any such dominance or heterotic effects. Although these types of mapping populations (BC1F2) are not ideal for dissecting the dominant QTL compared to F2 populations (which have a higher percentage of heterozygous loci), we were still able to extract three QTL regions with a significant dominant mode of action for DTF and N1B.

The most significant biological pathway for ASAS (Figure 5) was suggested to be CCR4–NOT complex (GO:0030014), which overlapped with the results for DTF, possibly explaining the high positive correlation between these traits (Figure 3). Hence, this pathway motivates further exploration using fine mapping, association mapping on a comprehensive diversity panel, and differential RNA expression techniques to further investigate its involvement in the development of seed surface area and flowering time.

A previous study has suggested that HT and N1B QTL colocalize (Liu et al., 2020); in our study, two QTLs, qHT_pop2_1_A11 and qN1B_1_A11, were also found very close to each other (<10 Mbp for the significant peaks). It further suggests the possibility of shared regulation or a stronger linkage between these two architectural traits. Except for DTF, significant QTL regions were found in both subgenomes for other traits (Figure 4), indicating the absence of any subgenomic affinity; this was also not much anticipated, since, unlike for the fiber quality traits (Mangla, Liu, Vitrakoti, Somala, et al., 2025), both the diploid ancestors exhibit these four traits.

Congruence with previous QTL studies

Few studies have explored the genetic regions controlling seed morphological traits (W. Wang et al., 2019). The cited study had some QTL regions found in chromosomes A09 and D09; however, none of the QTL regions found in our study were near those QTLs, possibly due to the utilization of different parental sources of genetic variation. For HT and N1B, owing to the smaller heritability values, we only found two significant QTLs for each, which also did not overlap with QTL regions identified in previous studies, indicating possible novel genetic variants. However, a QTL found for DTF (qDTF_pop5_1_D12) did overlap with QTL identified (D12: 44–55 Mbp) in previous studies (Jia et al., 2025; X. Wang et al., 2024). Hence, this QTL region offers more credibility for further investigation of candidate genes through transcriptomics and other approaches, as well as for marker‐assisted selection.

Implications for cotton breeding

Exotic Upland cotton germplasm possesses a rich reservoir of beneficial alleles for fiber quality improvement (Mangla, Liu, Vitrakoti, Somala, et al., 2025), although it might pose a challenge of linkage drag from negative alleles for fiber yield traits (Mangla, Liu, Vitrakoti, Shehzad, et al., 2025), necessitating the utilization of marker‐assisted selection and backcross breeding techniques. The present study suggested higher genetic variance for only a single secondary trait (ASAS), for which the exotic line T326 contributed the favorable alleles mostly, that were not found to have any significant negative effects on fiber quality in the companion study using the same populations (Mangla, Liu, Vitrakoti, Somala, et al., 2025). Hence, it reduces the chances of any linkage drag for fiber quality traits but presenting additional prospects of improving ASAS simultaneously. The other three traits had very small proportions of the total variance explained by genetic factors, which might pose minimal challenges from deleterious alleles since these QTLs were not found to overlap with those of fiber quality or yield traits. In addition, since US cotton production mostly relies on cultivation of inbreds rather than hybrids, these dominant QTL regions might not be of much use, and the negative heterotic effects are expected to disappear with an increase in homozygosity from inbreeding. To summarize, our detailed analysis of these architectural, developmental, and seed morphological traits suggests few potential challenges of linkage drag with economically important fiber traits but indicate some additional opportunities, in particular for improving the ASAS. Hence, future efforts are encouraged to incorporate this exotic Upland cotton germplasm into the elite gene pool. This will potentially reduce the challenge of narrow allelic diversity and contribute to the development of a new series of superior Upland cotton cultivars.
