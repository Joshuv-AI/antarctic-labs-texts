Introduction

Proximal humeral fractures (PHFs) are among the most common upper extremity injuries in adults, particularly in the elderly [1]. When combined with anterior shoulder dislocation, these injuries are classified as Neer group VI and represent a complex clinical scenario with significant challenges in both diagnosis and management. The Neer classification remains the most widely used system for initial assessment and treatment planning, guiding both surgical decisions and prognostication [[2], [3], [4]].

However, a crucial gap persists in current orthopedic practice and literature regarding the classification of these injuries following successful reduction of the glenohumeral joint. Specifically, there is no clear guidance on whether the injury should retain its initial classification as a fracture-dislocation (F—D) (Neer group VI) or be reclassified as an isolated fracture (such as Neer group IV) if the dislocation is reduced but significant bony displacement remains.

This ambiguity is a substantial concern. The classification utilized after a reduction has direct consequences for rehabilitation protocols, the length of immobilization, risk assessment for complications like avascular necrosis, and standardized reporting across trauma centers. Furthermore, the lack of consensus on this matter results in inconsistencies in treatment approaches, follow-up strategies, and ultimately impacts patient outcomes.

This case report highlights a practical example of this clinical dilemma. We present the case of a patient with a proximal humeral fracture-dislocation (PHF-D) in which the decision of whether to maintain the original Neer classification or to reclassify post-reduction was central to the management strategy. Through this case, we discuss the broader implications for orthopedic practice and propose a practical approach to address this persistent challenge.

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE checklist (SCARE 2025) [5].

Presentation of case

A 53-year-old female presented to the emergency department after falling due to a traffic accident, reporting severe pain and loss of function in her right shoulder. She had fallen onto her outstretched right arm approximately three hours before admission. On examination, there was visible swelling, bruising, and marked tenderness over the proximal right humerus. The patient was unable to actively move her right shoulder, but the distal neurovascular status was intact.

Initial plain radiographs of the right shoulder revealed an anterior dislocation of the glenohumeral joint accompanied by a displaced fracture of the greater tuberosity of the proximal humerus (Fig. 1). Immediate closed reduction of the shoulder was performed under sedation, successfully restoring joint congruity. However, post-reduction X-rays showed that the greater tuberosity fragment remained displaced by more than 1 cm (Fig. 2).

Post-reduction X-ray image and 3D reconstruction of the right shoulder, the GH joint has been reduced, the greater tuberosity fragment remains displaced by 14.8 mm in the superolateral direction (measured with a digital caliper, green line, yellow letters). (For interpretation of the references to colour in this figure legend, the reader is referred to the web version of this article.)

Given the persistent displacement, the patient was scheduled for surgical intervention. Open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) of the greater tuberosity fracture were carried out through an anterolateral approach. The axillary nerve was identified and protected throughout the procedure. Dissecting the anterior and medial bundles of the deltoid muscle around the fracture site, we found the fracture at the greater tuberosity split into two pieces. Intra-operative inspection showed an intact supraspinatus with superficial fraying that was reinforced, corrected the fracture was corrected under the fluoroscopy screen. Anatomical reduction was achieved, and fixation was secured using a locking plate and screws (Fig. 3). The surgical wound was closed in layers, and the arm was immobilized in a Desault sling.

Postoperative radiograph of the right shoulder following ORIF: reduced greater tuberosity without step-off.

The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient reported marked pain relief on the second postoperative day and demonstrated good compliance with immobilization and early rehabilitation of the elbow, wrist, and hand. Sutures were removed at two weeks, and the shoulder immobilizer was maintained for three weeks before starting passive shoulder mobilization. At three months, the patient maintained a Constant-Murley score of 69 and expressed high satisfaction with the outcome.

Patient Perspective: “Initially, I was very worried about my injury and the possibility of permanent loss of function. Thanks to the successful surgery and careful follow-up, I am now able to use my arm almost normally and have resumed my daily activities.”

Discussion

PHF-D injuries are uncommon but carry significant clinical implications, particularly when a displaced fracture of the greater tuberosity accompanies a dislocated shoulder. The Neer classification system serves as the gold standard for the initial evaluation of proximal humeral fractures (PHFs), dividing them into six groups, with group VI specifically addressing F—D. A practical dilemma arises when the glenohumeral joint achieves anatomical reduction, yet the greater tuberosity fragment remains notably displaced. In this context, should the injury continue to be managed and rehabilitated as an F—D, or should it be reclassified and treated as a separate, isolated greater tuberosity fracture?

While the initial management of PHF-D, namely, prompt reduction and stabilization, is well established, there remains a significant lack of consensus in the literature regarding the appropriate classification of these injuries following successful reduction [6]. Most published studies continue to report outcomes based solely on the initial injury pattern, without considering whether the achievement of anatomical reduction should influence subsequent classification, rehabilitation strategies, or prognosis. For example, Marc Schnetzke et al. (2018) demonstrated that timely and accurate reduction of the humeral head in F—D is crucial for minimizing the risk of avascular necrosis. Yet, their study did not address the question of reclassification post-reduction [7]. Similarly, Robinson et al. (2012) highlighted the elevated risk of neurovascular injury associated with these complex cases, but offered no guidance on whether and how classification should be modified after reduction [8]. This persistent ambiguity underscores a notable gap in current evidence and leaves clinicians without clear recommendations for optimal post-reduction management.

This lack of clarity in post-reduction classification has direct and meaningful consequences for orthopedic clinical decision-making if the injury is managed as a persistent F—D (Neer group VI), standard protocols typically recommend a longer period of immobilization, often one to three weeks, to allow adequate healing of the joint capsule and associated soft tissues, thus necessitating a more conservative approach to early mobilization [9]. In contrast, if the injury is reclassified as an isolated displaced greater tuberosity fracture (Neer group IV) after successful joint reduction, rehabilitation protocols frequently advocate for earlier initiation of passive and active shoulder motion to minimize postoperative stiffness and optimize functional recovery [10]. A review of the literature reveals that most published case reports and series have not explicitly addressed the clinical dilemma of post-reduction classification in PHF-D. While some authors advocate for an individualized approach based on intraoperative assessment of capsular integrity and joint stability, others recommend using post-reduction imaging findings to guide both classification and subsequent rehabilitation strategies. Despite these varied perspectives, there remains a clear and pressing need for more robust evidence, particularly from large, multicenter studies, to better define optimal management pathways and to inform the development of consensus guidelines. Until such data are available, significant variability in clinical practice is likely to persist.

Drawing from our experience and a synthesis of the available evidence, we present a pragmatic, literature-informed decision-aid, not a replacement for established classifications. In all cases, post-reduction decisions should be embedded alongside established classifications. Within the scope of this article, we use the initial classification based on the Neer system to guide diagnosis and surgical planning. Following reduction, careful assessment of residual bony displacement, joint stability, and soft tissue integrity, both clinically and paraclinically, with consideration of post-reduction MRI, is essential. We chose to maintain the original diagnosis as a Neer group VI fracture-dislocation. This decision can be explained by the fact that fracture-dislocations of the proximal humerus (Neer group VI) are typically associated with concomitant joint capsule and ligament injuries. Therefore, a period of shoulder immobilization lasting 1 to 3 weeks is recommended to facilitate soft tissue healing, reduce the risk of recurrent dislocation, and minimize long-term complications [11].

Accordingly, managing PHFs associated with shoulder dislocation requires a systematic and cautious approach. If the initial closed reduction is successful, the shoulder should be immobilized using an abduction pillow or brace to minimize movement at the fracture site [6]. Strict immobilization should be maintained during the first week post-injury. At the same time, the patient is encouraged to begin active motion exercises for the elbow, wrist, and hand to prevent joint stiffness [7]. After radiographic confirmation to rule out secondary displacement, passive motion and Codman's exercises may be initiated at two weeks post-injury [7]. Active shoulder motion usually begins six weeks after an injury, and strengthening exercises can be introduced at two months, as long as the patient can achieve a pain-free active assisted range of motion or if X-rays confirm that the fracture has healed. Initially, strengthening should concentrate on isometric exercises and resistance band training [7]. For patients with a greater tuberosity fracture and irreducible shoulder dislocation, early open reduction and fixation should be performed to optimize outcomes and reduce the risk of avascular necrosis [6]. After surgery, the affected limb should be placed in an abduction brace to minimize passive tension on the fixation. Passive mobilization can be started one week postoperatively to reduce the risk of stiffness; active motion is initiated at six weeks, and strengthening exercises commence at twelve weeks [6].

This report emphasizes the pressing need for further research to identify the most effective approach to post-reduction classification in PHF-D. However, this has several limitations. First, it describes a single case and therefore cannot establish causality or generalize management recommendations; Second, soft-tissue assessment relied on clinical evaluation and intraoperative inspection; MRI/ultrasound was not performed in the acute phase, and occult labral or cuff pathology may have been underdetected. Third, follow-up is short-term to mid-term, which limits our ability to fully assess late complications such as recurrent instability, stiffness, hardware failure, or avascular necrosis of the humeral head.

Conclusion

In summary, the post-reduction classification of PHF-D remains an unresolved challenge in orthopedic practice. Until standardized guidelines are developed, orthopedic surgeons must rely on careful clinical assessment, intraoperative findings, and individualized decision-making to optimize outcomes for each patient.
