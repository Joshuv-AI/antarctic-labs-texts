Genome‐wide exploration of bacterial leaf blight resistance and fine mapping of major resistance gene (Bb13) in upland cotton (Gossypium hirsutum L.)
Authors: Spoorti S. Gandhadmath, Fred M. Bourland, S. Anjan Gowda, Navin Shrestha, Don C. Jones, Kaitlyn Bissonnette, Vasu Kuraparthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCotton bacterial blight (CBB), caused by Xanthomonas citri subsp. malvacearum (Xcm), continues to pose a significant threat to upland cotton (Gossypium spp.) production across the US Cotton Belt. To elucidate the genetic basis of resistance to race 18 of CBB and identify potential novel resistance sources, we conducted genome‐wide association studies (GWASs) using a diversity panel of 661 upland cotton accessions that included elite US germplasm, tropical landraces, and University of Arkansas germplasm releases. GWAS identified a single 3.3 Mb region on chromosome D02 corresponding to the BB13 locus as the primary determinant of race 18 resistance, with no additional major loci detected, indicating Bb13 as the predominant race 18 resistance source in upland cotton. Population genetic analyses suggested uneven distribution of the Bb13 gene across US breeding programs and landraces, likely originating from the African cultivar S295. Linkage mapping in six recombinant inbred line (RIL) populations confirmed segregation of race 18 resistance at the BB13 locus. Fine mapping using PCR allele competitive extension (PACE) markers delimited BB13 locus to a 154.28 kb interval containing nine candidate genes, six of which have homologs implicated in plant disease resistance pathways. The PACE marker CBB16, co‐segregating with resistance in both the diversity panel and RIL populations, was identified as a diagnostic marker for Bb13. Local haplotyping further revealed marker groups and haplotypes associated with CBB resistance. Results from this study provide key genomic tools for breeding CBB‐resistant cultivars and establish the foundation for positional cloning of the Bb13 gene.

INTRODUCTION

Cotton (Gossypium spp.) is the world's most important natural fiber crop and a leading source of plant oil and protein (Lee, 1984). Cotton belongs to the genus Gossypium, which includes over 50 recognized species distributed throughout tropical and subtropical climates (Viot & Wendel, 2023). Modern cultivated cotton comprises four species, two diploid species with A sub‐genome (Gossypium arboreum L. and Gossypium herbaceum L., 2n = 2x = 26) and two allotetraploid species with A and D sub‐genomes (Gossypium hirsutum L. [2n = 4x = 52, (AAD1D1)] and Gossypium barbadense L. [2n = 4x = 52, (AAD2D2)]). The world's cotton production is dominated by the two tetraploid species. G. hirsutum (upland cotton) accounts for the larger cultivated area (97% of US cotton production; USDA, 2025) due to its wider adaptability and higher productivity. Throughout the US cotton belt, cotton production is affected by both biotic and abiotic stresses (Hillocks, 2010). Cotton bacterial blight (CBB), caused by a Gram‐negative bacterium Xanthomonas citri pv. malvacearum (Xcm), is one of the destructive diseases affecting cotton (Delannoy et al., 2005). It was identified as a major limiting factor for fiber production in the United States (Jalloul et al., 2015), India (Verma, 1986), and Africa (Follin, 1983). CBB was first reported in the United States in 1891 (Atkinson, 1892). In the early to mid‐20th century, the disease led to significant yield losses, ranging from 5%–10% to over 35%–70%, depending on the cotton cultivar and the scale of outbreaks (Delannoy et al., 2005; Hillocks, 1992; Razaghi et al., 2012). The occurrence and severity of CBB depend on the environmental conditions. High rainfall and humidity with warm temperatures favor disease development (Jalloul et al., 2015). The seed‐borne pathogen, Xcm can infect cotton at all stages of growth, entering through natural openings or physical wounds (Jalloul et al., 2015; Kemerait et al., 2017). The infection initially manifests as water‐soaked lesions or spots on the lower leaf surface, which darken and become angular with a halo‐like appearance on the upper leaf surface as they progress. Severe cases result in premature defoliation, while infection of the bolls leads to lint discoloration and seed contamination, significantly reducing both yield and fiber quality (Innes, 1983; Kemerait et al., 2017; Phillips et al., 2017). Among the 22 recognized races of Xcm (Delannoy et al., 2005), race 18 has been the most prevalent and virulent race found in the United States (El‐Zik & Thaxton, 1994; Kemerait et al., 2017; Phillips et al., 2017; Zhai et al., 2013).

Historically, the disease was managed through the use of acid‐delinted seeds, field cultivation practices, and planting resistant cultivars (Jalloul et al., 2015). However, in 2011, the pathogen (Xcm) reemerged in cotton fields and became a major problem throughout the Southeastern regions of the US Cotton Belt (Allen, 2011; Kemerait et al., 2017; Phillips et al., 2017; Rothrock et al.,2012). Recent studies revealed that the percentage of susceptible upland cotton cultivars has increased dramatically since 2009 (Phillips et al., 2017). The introduction of a new virulent strain, a breakdown in cotton host resistance, and the potential shift toward disease‐conducive environmental conditions are all likely contributing factors to the reemergence of CBB in the Southeast (Phillips et al., 2017). High night temperatures coupled with native high humidity posited to increase the severity of CBB in the Southeast including in North Carolina (Kunkel et al., 2020; Reidmiller et al., 2019). Hence, it is important to identify and rapidly deploy the newer sources of CBB resistance in the cotton cultivars before the disease outbreaks spread and cause significant crop damage to cotton in the United States.

Resistance to CBB varied considerably within the genus Gossypium (Hillocks, 1992; Hunter et al., 1968; Saini et al., 2024). Early domesticated forms of G. barbadense and G. hirsutum lacked effective resistance to CBB (Innes, 1983; Knight & Clouston, 1939). Early studies on CBB resistance identified 10 major genes (ascribed as B1 – B10 ) from different Gossypium species (G. arboreum, G. herbaceum, G. hirsutum, G. hirsutum var. punctatum, G. barbadense, G. anomalum) (Knight, 1957, 1963). Most B genes reported to function synergistically to confer partial resistance to specific Xcm races (Elassbli et al., 2021b; J. Zhang et al., 2020). Most B genes were subsequently incorporated into upland cotton (G. hirsutum) (Innes, 1983), and systematic interspecific hybridization and backcrossing further accelerated the introgression of several single‐gene resistance factors into a polygenic resistance background (Bird & Blank, 1951; Simpson & Weindling, 1946; Green & Brinkerhoff, 1956; Bird, 1960, 1982; Brinkerhoff, 1984). A G. hirsutum line, 101–102B immune to CBB was first developed by crossing Empire WR (G. hirsutum) and Bar 4/16 (G. barbadense containing Knight's B2B3 genes) (Bird, 1960, 1962). These resistant genes were later transferred to other backgrounds and made available to breeders across the United States (Bird, 1982; Brinkerhoff, 1984). Building on these efforts, Dr. Fred Bourland at the University of Arkansas initiated extensive screening and selection for CBB resistance in upland cotton. Over the period from 1986 to 2024, these efforts led to the release of 114 germplasm lines and eight cultivars, all (except 12) resistant to CBB, with additional germplasm lines currently being released (F. Bourland, personal communication, October 7, 2025).

Studies on biparental mapping populations revealed that G. hirsutum cultivar S295, which is effective against all documented Xcm races (Girardot et al., 1986; Wallace & El‐Zik, 1989), carries a major gene and quantitative trait locus (QTL) on chromosome D02 (Xiao et al., 2010; Yang, 2013). Notably, chromosome D02 has been repeatedly found to be associated with multiple CBB resistance genes and QTLs (Elassbli et al., 2021a; Gowda et al., 2022; Rungis et al., 2002). Recently, we identified the BB13 resistance locus, which confers resistance to a wide range of Xcm races, including race 18, was mapped to a 371 kb region using a combined genome‐wide association study (GWAS) and high‐density linkage mapping (Gowda et al., 2022). Further, we developed diagnostic kompetitive allele‐specific PCR markers for the BB13 locus (Pettit et al., 2023), facilitating the detection of CBB resistance alleles in genetics and breeding of BB13 based CBB resistance. However, the diversity and spectrum of CBB resistance within global upland cotton germplasm remains underexplored. Further, the fine‐scale analysis of the BB13 locus is critical for its effective deployment in the breeding programs using genomic tools. In the current study, we screened a broad collection of upland cotton germplasm, including elite US diversity panel (DIV) accessions and landraces, to identify sources of CBB resistance and fine mapped the BB13 locus using GWAS, linkage mapping, and novel single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers. In addition, we delineated genomic regions associated with CBB resistance using linkage disequilibrium (LD) based local haplotyping (Marsh et al., 2023), providing insights into the complex linkage structure and variant relationships within the target region, as well as their distribution across populations.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Plant materials

A DIV of 661 G. hirsutum accessions including 382 elite DIV accessions (Tyagi et al., 2014), 185 tropical landraces (TLA) (Kaur et al., 2017), 90 accessions from the cotton breeding program at the University of Arkansas (Fred Bourland panel [FB]), and four Pima (G. barbadense) accessions were characterized for their response to Xcm race 18. This combined panel was used for the GWAS of CBB resistance in cotton. The DIV panel comprises a large part of the elite US upland cotton diversity and includes cultivars and breeding lines released between 1900 and 2005 (Tyagi et al., 2014). The TLA panel is primarily sourced from the Central American Center of Diversity (Lewis & Richmond, 1957; Waddle et al., 1961) and majorly includes G. hirsutum race latifolium, a close relative of modern‐day upland cotton. The FB includes the germplasm and cultivars developed by Dr. Fred Bourland's cotton breeding program between 1986 and 2021 (Bourland, 2004, 2013, 2018). Of the 661 accessions of the combined DIV, 145 accessions of the TLA panel, 84 accessions of the FB, and 128 accessions of the elite DIV panels were phenotyped for CBB response to race 18 in the current study. The phenotypic data for the remaining 253 DIV accessions were sourced from Gowda et al. (2022).

Six recombinant inbred line (RIL) populations were used to validate the marker–trait associations (MTAs) identified through GWAS and targeted fine mapping of the BB13 locus (Table S1). Four RIL populations (R‐28, R‐31, R‐32, and R‐33) were used for linkage mapping and consensus map construction while the remaining two populations (R‐29 and R‐30) were used to independently confirm the association between BB13 and CBB resistance across diverse genetic backgrounds. The CBB‐susceptible cultivar Acala Maxxa (PI 540885) was used as the common female parent, while the six diverse accessions BJAGL NECT (PI 529382), CABD3CABCH‐1‐89 (PI 603002), CAHUGLBBCS‐1‐88 (PI 603005), H1220 (PI 578226), LBBCDBOAKH‐1‐90 (PI 603004), and Paymaster HS200 (PI 542974) resistant to race 18 were used as male parents to develop six RIL populations, R‐28, R‐29, R‐30, R‐31, R‐32, and R‐33, respectively (Table S1). These accessions were selected based on phenotypic screening using race 18 of the accessions with core sets of allele richness identified by Tyagi et al. (2014). For RIL population, F1 plants from each cross were manually selfed and F2 and subsequent segregating populations were advanced to the F6 generation through single‐seed descent method.

Plant inoculations and CBB phenotyping

The 661 accession association mapping panel, six RIL populations (639 inbreds), along with the parental accessions were evaluated for their response to Xcm race 18 (Figure 1) at the seedling stage in the growth chambers as described in Gowda et al. (2022). Cotyledonary leaves of 7‐day old seedlings were inoculated with Xcm race 18 and disease scoring was done 7 days after inoculation. Three to four seeds from each accession were sowed in a 3.5‐in. pot and kept in a growth chamber (Model AR‐36L3, Percival Scientific) maintained at 27°C and 14 h of light. Two days before inoculations, Xcm was subcultured on the nutrient yeast glycerol agar—with peptone, yeast, glycerol, and 1% agar—medium and incubated at 30°C for 48 h. On the day of inoculation, the Xcm cells were suspended in 2 mL of 10 mM MgCl2, and OD600 of ∼0.1 was used to inoculate 7‐day‐old seedlings using a needleless syringe. The bacterium was gently injected into the abaxial surface of the cotyledon leaves using a needleless syringe. The excess inoculum was wiped, and the inoculated seedlings were placed in the growth chamber maintained at 30°C and 14 h of light. The disease response was recorded 7 days postinoculation. Plants with water‐soaked lesions at the inoculated site were categorized as “susceptible,” and hypersensitive cell death/necrosis response as “resistant.” Consistent responses across at least three plants were noted. Phenotypic segregation of the CBB resistance in the RILs was evaluated using Pearson's chi‐square statistic.

DNA isolation and genotyping

Leaf tissue from 3–4‐week‐old RIL plants were harvested into 2 mL centrifuge tubes, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C. The tissues were pulverized using the YG5 3422011 macerator (BTLab Systems), and DNA was isolated from the DNeasy Plant Mini‐Kit (Qiagen). DNA concentration and quality were assessed using the NanoDrop ND1000 spectrophotometer. DNA samples with a 260/280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 and a 260/230 ratio above 1.5 were selected for genotyping with PCR allele competitive extension (PACE) markers. The SNP‐based genotypic data of the GWAS panel (DIV + TLA + FB) genotyped using the CottonSNP63K array of Hulse‐Kemp et al. (2015) was obtained from Gowda et al. (2023).

Primer design for PACE assay

Toward identifying new SNP markers for targeted genomic mapping of the Bb13 gene, genome‐specific PACE markers were designed from the putative gene sequences of the genomic region identified in our previous study (Gowda et al., 2022). For PACE marker design, genomic sequences of target region were retrieved from the reference genome, G. hirsutum ‘TM1 CRI v1’ (www.cottongen.org), and subjected to BLAST search against other available genomes, including those from CBB‐resistant accessions (UA48, UGA230, and CSX8308) and the CBB‐susceptible accession (Coker312), using Phytozome (www.phytozome.net). Sequences with an e‐value of 0 were downloaded and aligned using Clustal Omega software (Sievers et al., 2011). The multiple sequence alignment included sequences from chromosomes D02 and A02 of the resistant and susceptible lines (Figure S1). SNP variants specific to the D02 chromosome were selected, and PACE primers were designed through the PACE assay design service (3Cr Bioscience,). The PACE markers consist of two allele‐specific primers (ASP1 and ASP2) and a common reverse primer (CRP), with the ASPs labeled with fluorophore tail sequences, FAM (GAAGGTGACCAAGTTCATGCT) and HEX (GAAGGTCGGAGTCAACGGATT). The primers were synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies, and the full sequence information of the PACE primers used for fine mapping is listed in Table 1.

PACE genotyping assay

The PACE assay involved preparing a primer mix and a master mix. The primer mix consisted of 32.2 µL of molecular‐grade water, 8.4 µL of ASP1, 8.4 µL of ASP2, and 21 µL of CRP. The master mix included 2 µL 2xPACE mix and 0.5 µL of primer mix per reaction. The final reaction volume consisted of 2.5 µL of the master mix and 2.5 µL of DNA (15–20 ng). Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) of DNA samples of RIL populations were amplified and analyzed using the Applied Biosystems real‐time PCR QuantStudio5 machine (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Following the PACE guidelines, PCR cycling was carried out in three phases: (a) enzyme activation at 94°C for 15 min; (b) 10 touchdown cycles of denaturing, annealing, and extension; and (c) 50 amplification cycles (3CR Bioscience, 2022; Makhoul et al., 2020), with a temperature drop rate of 0.8°C/s and final annealing temperature of 57°C. The QuantStudio5 machine detects the fluorescent tails attached to ASP1 and ASP2 and the allelic calls were made using QuantStudio Design and Analysis software version 1.5.2. The fluorescent tails were detected and visualized on a Cartesian plot, where allelic calls clustered into groups (Figure 2). The alleles were scored based on fluorescent endpoints, such as homozygous allele1/allele1 as “a,” homozygous allele2/allele2 as “b,” heterozygous allele1/allele2 as “h” and “u” for undetermined calls.

Data analysis

The genotypic data from the CottonSNP63K array was reprocessed and filtered according to the methods described in Gowda et al. (2023). The filtering criteria involved removing SNPs with (a) a minor allele frequency lower than 0.05, (b) more than 30% heterozygosity, and (c) more than 30% missing data. A total of 20,681 polymorphic SNP markers were retained for downstream analysis. Population structure among 661 accessions, as determined by the fastStructure algorithm (Raj et al., 2014) and the associated membership coefficients, which represents the fraction of each accessions genome assigned to the inferred subpopulations, were obtained from Gowda et al. (2023).

To explore genetic variation and relationships among the accessions of the GWAS panel, a principal component analysis (PCA) was performed and visualized using the ggplot2 package in R (Wickham, 2016). GWAS was performed in the rMVP R package (Yin et al., 2021) using a mixed linear model (MLM) incorporating both structure‐derived Q‐matrix and kinship‐based K‐matrix (Buckler et al., 2006). MTAs were identified as significant using the Bonferroni correction threshold (Hochberg, 1988), and phenotypic variance explained (PVE) was estimated using a simple linear regression model outlined by Shim et al. (2015).

LD analysis was carried out using Haploview version 4.1 (Barrett et al., 2005), and local haplotyping analysis was performed using the crosshap R package version 1.4.0 (Marsh et al., 2023) to investigate the significant SNPs identified through GWAS. In crosshap, SNPs exhibiting tight linkage and shared inheritance patterns were grouped into marker groups (MGs), and haplotypes were defined by the combination of allelic states across these groups (Marsh et al., 2023). The MGmin parameter, which specifies the minimum number of SNPs required to define a MG, was selected according to the physical size of the target region and SNP density. The epsilon parameter, which determines clustering resolution, was chosen by evaluating clustering tree structures and selecting the lowest value that generated robust, phenotypically distinct haplotype groups. All other crosshap parameters were applied using default settings.

The CBB phenotypes were coded as “a” for resistance, “b” for susceptible, and “u” for missing or unknown data. Allelic calls of each accession obtained from the QuantStudio 5 machine were scored relative to parental alleles, assigning “a” to the resistant parent cluster and “b” to the susceptible parent cluster. Linkage mapping of CBB resistance was performed in JoinMap 5.0 (Ooijen, 2018), using the maximum likelihood algorithm and Kosambi mapping function, with a logarithm of odds (LOD) score of 10.0. A consensus linkage map was generated using the LPmerge R package (Endelman & Plomion, 2014). A comprehensive literature review based on putative gene annotations was performed to explore resistance candidates previously established in model plants rice (Oryza sativa), tomato (Solanum lycopersicum), and Arabidopsis (Arabidopsis thaliana).

RESULTS

Phenotypic analyses of CBB resistance

Out of 661 accessions in the combined GWAS panel evaluated for CBB response at the seedling stage, 512 accessions showed clear “resistant” and “susceptible” phenotypes, with 133 (25.97%) identified as resistant and 379 (74.02%) as susceptible to race 18 (Table S2). The remaining accessions were classified as unknown as some of them did not germinate while others exhibited intermediate responses that could not be reliably classified.

In the elite DIV panel, 307 accessions showed clear CBB phenotypes to race 18. Of these, 67 were resistant and 240 were susceptible to CBB. Most of the resistant accessions belong to subpopulation K04 of the elite DIV panel (Table S3), which included accessions from the Bird's multi‐adversity resistance (MAR) program (Bird, 1982; Gowda et al., 2023), whereas the majority of the susceptible accessions were sampled from the Acala breeding program, the Far Western cotton breeding belt, the Pee Dee accessions, and Coker's breeding program of South Carolina (Table S3). In the TLA panel, 131 accessions showed clear CBB responses. Of these, only one accession (TX‐2367, PI 607682), sampled from Paraguay, was resistant to CBB, whereas the majority of the susceptible TLA lines were sampled from Guatemala and Mexico (Table S2). In the FB, the CBB phenotype was available for 73 accessions. Of these, 65 were resistant and were developed through breeding programs in Arkansas and Mississippi (Table S2), corresponding to the K08 cluster associated with Mid‐South cotton breeding programs (Table S3). The majority of the accessions in the FB were CBB resistant (Figure S2). Among the FB, only eight CBB susceptible lines were released over the period from 1986 to 2020 (Table S4).

Phenotypic evaluation of the six biparental RIL populations and their parents using Xcm race 18 showed clear resistant and susceptible phenotypes to CBB (Figure 1). All six RIL populations segregated in a 1:1 ratio of resistance to susceptible types. The segregation pattern, test statistic, and p‐value for each population are provided in Table S5. The segregation pattern in RILs confirmed the monogenic inheritance of CBB resistance locus (BB13) in all six resistant parental lines of the RIL populations.

Population structure and genetic diversity analysis

PCA of genome‐wide SNP data from 661 accessions revealed three distinct clusters corresponding to TLA, DIV, and FB groups, with TLA accessions clearly separated along PC1 (Figure S3). The broader spread of TLA accessions, in contrast to the compact clustering of FB and DIV groups, reflects the greater genetic diversity present within the landrace population. This wide genetic variation highlights TLA accessions as a valuable resource for exploring potential novel alleles. The FB cluster predominantly consisted of CBB resistance lines, likely due to structured breeding efforts, that have captured and maintained resistance alleles over more than four decades (Bourland, 2018). In contrast, the genetically diverse but unselected TLA lines lack such alleles, explaining their predominantly susceptible phenotype (Figure S2).

Population structure analysis grouped 326 of the 661 upland cotton accessions into 11 distinct subpopulations, while the remaining 335 accessions are unassigned to any group due to high levels of admixture (Table S3) (Gowda et al., 2023). The distribution of CBB response varied considerably across these subpopulations, with CBB resistance found in only five of the 11 subpopulations (Figure 3; Table S3). The number of CBB resistant accessions were predominant in the K04 (96.3%), followed by K08 (50.77%), K10 (11.11%), K09 (3.23%), and K05 (2.7%) cluster (Figure 3). The K04 cluster had 26 of 27 accessions resistant to CBB and included cultivars from MAR breeding program (Bird, 1982). The K08 subpopulation, with 33 of 65 accessions resistant to CBB, is predominantly composed of cultivars from the Mid‐South cotton breeding region (Gowda et al., 2023). In contrast, no resistant accessions were observed in subpopulations K01, K02, K03, K06, K07, and K11 (Table S3; Figure 3). These groups were predominantly composed of susceptible individuals, with more than 60% of susceptible accessions in each group (Figure 3). The K02 subpopulation is the PIMA outgroup. The subpopulation K03, consisting of High Plains‐type cultivars from the Southwest, included 93.3% of susceptible accessions. The subpopulation K06 consists of 73.08% of accessions susceptible to CBB, and mainly included cultivars from Southeastern cotton breeding belt, Pee‐Dee program, and Coker breeding program of South Carolina (Gowda et al., 2023). Landrace‐enriched subpopulations such as K01 (Guatemalan G. hirsutum race latifolium), K07 (G. hirsutum race punctatum), and K11 (Mexican G. hirsutum race latifolium) also lacked resistance to CBB. Among landraces, only a single resistant accession (TX‐2367, PI 607682) was identified. It was unassigned to any subpopulation due to high admixture, suggesting that CBB resistance is rare in landraces. Notably, the unassigned group included 20.6% of resistant accessions (Figure 3). These accessions were not confidently assigned to any single subpopulation due to high levels of admixture in their parentage (Gowda et al., 2023).

GWAS of CBB resistance in upland cotton

A total of 20,681 polymorphic SNP markers were used for the GWAS of CBB response to Xcm race 18 in 512 accessions. GWAS using an MLM (Q+K) with kinship and population structure as covariates identified a total of 107 MTAs above the Bonferroni threshold p‐value (Table S6; Figure 4a). All 107 associations were identified in a 3.31Mb region of the long arm of chromosome D02 (290,740–3,604,218 bp). The SNP markers (i25755Gh and i46775Gh) previously found to be strongly associated with Bb13 gene in Gowda et al. (2022) were continue to show most significant MTAs with CBB resistance in this study, with the highest PVE of 26.88% (i46775Gh) and 26.77% (i25755Gh) (Figure 4b; Table S6). No additional significant MTAs were detected in any other genomic regions in GWAS, underscoring the absence of novel resistance genes for Xcm race 18 within the broad upland cotton germplasm used in the current study. This finding further confirms that chromosome D02 harbors the major gene for CBB resistance in upland cotton and Bb13 is present in all the upland cotton accessions that showed resistance to race 18.

Allelism test for the CBB resistance in the landrace

Among the landraces of the upland cotton DIV, only one accession, TX‐2367 (PI 607682) from Paraguay showed hypersensitive resistance response to race 18 at the seedling stage. However, no additional MTAs for race 18 resistance were identified using GWAS in the DIV except at the genomic region of BB13 locus on chromosome D02. To further confirm whether TX‐2367 with race 18 resistance carries a novel resistance gene on D02 using an allelism test, an F2 population was derived from cross between resistant landrace accession TX‐2367 and resistant accession Arkot 8102, which was confirmed to carry the major dominant CBB resistance gene Bb13 (Gowda et al., 2022). Phenotyping of 94 F2 plants for Xcm race 18 response showed no susceptible plants in the segregating population suggesting the gene conferring resistance to race 18 in landrace is allelic to Bb13, providing further evidence that race 18 resistance in the landrace is due to Bb13 gene. Taken together, these results confirm that the Bb13 is the predominant resistance gene contributing to bacterial blight resistance in upland cotton in the United States

Fine mapping of the BB13 locus

Novel PACE markers: The major bacterial leaf blight resistance locus, BB13, was previously mapped to a 371 kb region on the long arm of chromosome D02 (Gowda et al., 2022). This interval harbors 30 putative genes, of which nine are implicated in disease resistance pathways. PACE markers were designed within these genes and their flanking regions for linkage mapping of BB13 locus in RIL populations (Table 1). Of the 26 markers developed, including those targeting noncoding SNPs, eight were polymorphic, while the remainder were monomorphic or showed non‐specific amplification perhaps due to the repetitive nature of the region. The eight informative markers were used to construct the linkage map (with LOD 10).

Genetic mapping using RILs: Linkage mapping was performed independently in four RIL populations (R‐28, R‐31, R‐32, and R‐33) to determine whether the same BB13 resistance locus identified in Arkot 8102 (Gowda et al., 2022) also underlies CBB resistance in four additional resistant parental accessions. In all four populations, the PACE marker CBB16, which was diagnostic for BB13 locus consistently showed co‐segregation with the CBB resistance phenotype (Figure S4). In the genetic map constructed using eight markers, the map lengths across these populations ranged from 2.6 cM in R‐32 to 4.4 cM in R‐33. A consensus map constructed from these four populations positioned the BB13 locus at 0.9 cM to a cluster of tightly linked markers (CBB3, CBB6, CBB8, CBB9, and CBB10) proximal to the telomere and 1.0 cM to the CBB15 marker distal to the telomere (Figure 5). In the R‐28 and R‐33 RIL populations, BB13 was mapped to a region between 981,030 bp and 1,135,315 bp, targeting the CBB resistance to a 154.28 kb region on the long arm of chromosome D02. To validate these findings, BB13 was further mapped in two additional RIL populations (R‐29 and R‐30), which confirmed consistent co‐segregation of the resistance phenotype with marker CBB16 (Figure S4). Although the CBB16 PACE marker showed co‐segregation with the CBB phenotypes in all the six RIL populations (Figure S4), it shows segregation distortion in F2 populations due to allele drop out in heterozygotes (data not shown). CBB16 is an excellent marker for detecting the homozygous alleles at the BB13 locus but it may not be reliable to distinguish heterozygotes from homozygous resistant alleles of the BB13 locus. Hence, we developed CBB15, a PACE marker developed based on candidate gene Gh_D02G013500 (ESD4) from the targeted genomic region of BB13 locus. Marker CBB15 showed clear differentiation of homozygotes and heterozygotes in the F2 population with expected 1 (RR):2(Rr):1(rr) segregation ratio suggesting its effectiveness in detecting heterozygotes in MAS. Further, validation of CBB15 on 93 accessions of the DIV, showed a low error rate of 1.12%, and an average error rate of 2.65% across six RIL populations, demonstrating its robustness and suitability for MAS. The allelic calls of CBB16 on a RIL population and CBB15 on DIV accessions, visualized on a Cartesian plot, are shown in Figure 2.

LD analysis and local haplotyping at the BB13 locus

LD‐based haplotype analysis was performed using Haploview across a refined genomic window, nested within the broader 3.3 Mb GWAS‐identified interval and overlapping the 154 kb linkage‐mapped region (Figure S5). The D prime‐based LD plot revealed three discrete haplotype blocks, each showing high levels of pairwise linkage, indicative of suppressed historical recombination and a conserved haplotype architecture (Figure S5a). Block 1 (30 kb; 970,931–1,001,263) comprised four haplotypes, block 2 (336 kb; 1,037,322–1,373,543) included seven, and block 3 (39 kb; 1,381,727–1,420,751) contained five haplotypes (Figure S5b). Among these, the haplotype “TGATCCGTCACTG” in block 1, “ACCGCCTTAAG” in block 2, was observed in 119 resistant lines, and “CTGTAC” in block 3 was seen in 111 resistant lines. Notably, all three haplotypes co‐occurred in 110 resistant genotypes, suggesting the presence of a major resistance associated allele or gene within this conserved LD structure (Table S7).

To resolve fine‐scale haplotype structure at the BB13 locus, we performed local haplotyping using crosshap R package. In crosshap, clustering resolution (determined by MGmin and epsilon [ε] parameters) defines how haplotypes are distinguished based on local LD structure, which is crucial for accurately representing underlying genetic relationships and their association with phenotype. Based on SNP density within the GWAS‐identified region (107 SNPs in 3.3 Mb), we set MGmin = 6 to define MGs and ε = 0.2, which produced phenotypically distinct and stable haplotype clusters (Figure S6). The analysis revealed four distinct haplotype combinations (A, B, C, and D), each defined by specific allelic combinations across three MGs (MG1, MG2, and MG3) within the GWAS targeted region for CBB resistance (Figure 6; Table S8). These MGs comprise sets of tightly linked SNPs (MG1 = 5 SNPs, MG2 = 7 SNPs, and MG3 = 6 SNPs) that inherit as a unit across the population (Table S8). Among the four haplotypes, haplotype A represented the majority of individuals (n = 371), predominantly susceptible (Figure 6a), with a mean phenotype value of 0.976 (Figure S7g). In contrast, haplotype B (n = 119) was strongly enriched for resistant individuals (Figure 6a), exhibiting a markedly reduced mean phenotype value of 0.029 (phenotype scored as 0 for resistant and 1 for susceptible). Haplotype C (n = 116) consisted primarily of susceptible individuals (mean phenotype = 0.951), while haplotype D (n = 18) was associated with resistance (mean phenotype = 0.133) despite its limited frequency (Figure S7g).

The MG and haplotype (MG/hap) matrix was used to visualize linkage patterns within the target region (Figure 6b). It displays reference (REF) and alternative (ALT) alleles for SNPs across MGs (Table S8), where REF corresponds to the allele in the reference genome (TM1 CRI–CBB susceptible) and ALT represents sequence variants relative to it. The allele label is based on the reference genome and does not reflect phenotype. Examination of allele distribution showed the presence of ALT allele for MG1 in haplotypes B, C, and D, while the ALT allele for MG2 was restricted to B and D (Figure 6b). The ALT allele for MG3 occurred exclusively in haplotype B. Notably, haplotype B, associated with CBB resistance (Figure 6a), carries ALT alleles at all three MGs, whereas haplotype A, predominantly susceptible, retains REF alleles at these loci (Figure 6b). Conversely, haplotype C, containing the ALT allele only at MG1, remains susceptible, indicating that variation at MG1 alone is insufficient to confer resistance. Haplotype D, carrying ALT alleles at MG1 and MG2 but not MG3, also exhibits resistance (Figure 6a). Collectively, these allele–phenotype patterns indicate that resistance consistently tracks with the ALT allele at MG2 (present in haplotypes B and D, absent in A and C), identifying MG2 as the key interval associated with CBB resistance. The ALT allele at MG3, although always present in the resistant haplotype B, does not independently confer resistance and is likely co‐inherited with MG2 due to linkage.

Phenotypic association analysis was conducted to evaluate the relationship between allele type (REF or ALT) at each SNP and the CBB response (Figure S7e). For each marker, accessions were grouped according to whether they carried the REF or ALT allele, and the mean resistance score (0 = resistant, 1 = susceptible) was calculated for each group. The difference between these means, termed phenodiff, indicates how the resistance outcome differs in individuals carrying the ALT allele relative to those with the REF allele. Under this scoring, a negative phenodiff indicates that accessions carrying the ALT allele are more frequently resistant than those with the REF allele. Consistent with this expectation, MG2 with phenodiff of −0.9 and mean R 2 = 1 (Figure S7b) showed a complete association with resistance. The phenodiff of −0.9 indicates that accessions carrying the ALT allele at SNPs within MG2 are linked to CBB resistance (Figure S7b). In contrast, MG1, despite a higher ALT allele frequency (0.41), showed a weaker association (phenodiff = −0.55), suggesting that MG1 alone does not explain resistance in this population. These findings identify MG2 as the high‐confidence candidate interval for further dissection of the resistance locus. Additionally, the presence of ALT alleles at all three MGs within haplotype B (Figure 6b), together with its strong enrichment for resistance (Figure 6a), highlights haplotype B as a valuable resource of favorable alleles for breeding (Table S8). Notably, haplotype D also confers resistance despite lacking the MG3 ALT allele, indicating that the combination of MG1 and MG2 ALT alleles retains key resistance‐conferring variants in certain accessions (Figure 6).

Candidate gene analysis

GWAS identified 107 significant SNPs associated with CBB resistance, spanning a 3.31 Mb region (290,740–3,604,218 bp) on chromosome D02 (Table S6). Among these, the SNP marker i46775Gh explained the highest phenotypic variation (26.88%) (Table S6). The PACE marker CBB16, designed based on i46775Gh SNP, cosegregated with the CBB resistance (BB13) locus across all six RIL populations (Figure S4) and DIV accessions (Figure 2). Linkage mapping in RIL populations further refined the BB13 locus to a 154.28 kb region on chromosome D02, between the CBB9 (981,030 bp) and CBB15 (1,135,315 bp) markers (Figure 5a; Figure S4). The cotton reference genome (TM1‐CRI v1) was analyzed in the orthologous genomic region between markers 981,030 bp and 1,135,315 bp and their flanking regions, which harbored 15 putative genes (Table S9), six of which have homologs previously implicated in disease resistance in model plant species (Table 2). Notably, two genes, RGA4 and At4g27190, encode NB‐LRR domain proteins, which are well‐established mediators of resistance responses in rice and Arabidopsis (UniProt Q9T048; UniProt, n.d.) to fungal (Cesari et al., 2013; Okuyama et al., 2011), oomycete (X. Chen et al., 2016), and bacterial pathogens (Dees et al., 2016; González et al., 2017; Hutin et al., 2016). Other candidate genes include DELLA protein, NAC domain transcription factors, ubiquitin‐like specific proteases (ULP1b, ESD4), and zinc finger BED domain proteins (Table 2). Comparative genomic sequence analysis of publicly available genome assemblies revealed that the fine‐mapped interval spans 154.3 kb in susceptible genotypes (TM1‐CRI, TM1‐UTX, and Coker312) but is contracted to 118.8 kb in resistant genotypes (UA48, UGA230, and CSX8308) (sequence retrieved from Cottongen and Phytozome). It was also observed that the fine‐mapped interval and its flanking region are complex and repetitive in nature, with few zinc‐finger domains, DELLA and noncoding sequences repeated in the region (Table S9).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we phenotyped 661 G. hirsutum accessions and 639 RILs for resistance to Xcm race 18 and genotyped them using a 63K Cotton SNP array and novel PACE markers. GWAS and linkage mapping identified a single major resistance locus, BB13, on chromosome D02, confer resistance to race 18 of Xcm in the US cotton germplasm. Fine mapping and targeted genomic mapping using RIL populations and novel PACE markers delimited this locus to a 154.3 kb interval, and two diagnostic PACE markers (CBB15 and CBB16) tightly linked with resistance, providing efficient tools for marker‐assisted selection and deployment of durable bacterial blight resistance in upland cotton.

Population genetic analyses of race 18 resistance in upland cotton

Population structure analysis grouped upland cotton accessions into 11 subpopulations (Gowda et al., 2023). The distribution of Xcm race 18 resistance varied markedly across these groups (Figure 3), reflecting both geographic origin and historical introgression of resistance alleles. (Bird, 1982; Innes, 1983; Knight, 1963). Subpopulation K04 contained 96.3% resistant accessions, largely derived from Dr. Bird's MAR program, which successfully introgressed B‐gene clusters (B2, B3, B4, and B6) from G. barbadense and G. arboreum into G. hirsutum (Bird, 1982). This targeted introgression strategy significantly broadened the resistance gene pool within upland cotton.

Subpopulation K08 included 50.8% resistant accessions, primarily from Mid‐South breeding programs such as the University of Arkansas, Stoneville Pedigreed Seed Company, Louisiana State University AgCenter, and Delta & Pine Land Company (Bourland, 2018; Gowda et al., 2023). The high frequency of resistance in this cluster reflects sustained breeding efforts for selection of bacterial blight resistance in cotton germplasm and cultivar development programs in Mid‐South (Arkansas and Mississippi) (Bourland, 2018; Phillips et al., 2017).

In contrast, six subpopulations (K01, K02, K03, K06, K07, and K11) lacked resistant accessions, underscoring the limited distribution of effective resistance alleles (Figure 3). Subpopulation K03, which includes High Plains‐type cultivars with DP10 or DP14 ancestry, was entirely susceptible, consistent with pedigree effects rather than regional adaptation (Bird, 1982; Brinkerhoff, 1984; Gowda et al., 2023). Landrace‐enriched clusters, K01 (Guatemalan race latifolium), K07 (G. hirsutum race punctatum), and K11 (Mexican race latifolium), were uniformly susceptible, except for a single resistant landrace, TX‐2367 from Paraguay. This accession was unassigned to any subpopulation in the Structure analysis (Table S3), and genome‐wide PCA separated it distinctly from other resistant breeding cultivars and DIV accessions (Figure S3), consistent with a South American ancestry rather than introgression from MAR germplasm. Although classical studies reported that CBB resistance is governed by major B gene loci (Knight, 1957, 1963; Knight & Clouston, 1939), comprehensive surveys across landraces remain limited. Collectively, these results indicate that CBB resistance is rare among landraces and that the major resistance alleles in upland cotton have been derived from a limited set of donor sources incorporated into elite US germplasm (Bird, 1960, 1962, 1982; Brinkerhoff, 1984; Innes, 1983).

Analysis of resistant parents used in RIL populations further supports the pattern of distribution of BB13 resistance among subpopulations. Of six resistant parents, two (CABD3CABCH_1_89 and H1220) were assigned to subpopulations K04 and K08 (Table S3), respectively, whereas the remaining four were admixed or unassigned. Despite originating from distinct subpopulations, both CABD3CABCH_1_89 and H1220 trace resistance back to MAR accessions, indicating independent introgressions of the same resistance source into different lineages (Bird, 1960, 1982; Bird & Blank, 1951; Green & Brinkerhoff, 1956). High admixture among accessions, common in US breeding programs (Bowman et al., 2006; Gowda et al., 2023), reflects extensive germplasm sharing and further dispersal of the Bb13 gene across breeding backgrounds. Together, these findings reveal that race 18 resistance due to Bb13 is largely concentrated in clusters enriched for MAR pedigrees (K04) and Mid‐South breeding backgrounds (K08) but also persists at lower frequency in other clusters (K05, K09, and K10) (Figure 3). The enrichment of resistance within specific clusters, yet its presence across multiple breeding backgrounds, is the expected population‐structure signature of a single major effect region. Similar structure‐based resistance signatures have been reported for the Pi‐ta locus in rice (Luo et al., 2024), the 1RS/1BL translocation in wheat (L. Zhang et al., 2011), and the chromosome 5H resistance haplotype in barley (Wonneberger et al., 2023). Consistent with such patterns, GWAS identified BB13 on chromosome D02 as the major locus underlying CBB resistance in the US upland cotton (Figure 4).

The historical dissemination of this locus reflects the impact of Bird's MAR program, which introduced a common resistance haplotype into US cultivars. Early releases such as 101–102B (carrying B2B3 genes), also tightly linked with B12 (Follin et al., 1988; Rungis et al., 2002), contributed to the spread of a D02‐linked resistance haplotype across US germplasm (Figure 3). The D02 signal identified in this study aligns with the classic B12 resistance from African accession S295 (Girardot et al., 1986; Wallace & El‐Zik, 1989; Wright et al., 1998), where S295 was derived through panmixy of 34 parents (Girardot et al., 1986). Although the precise pedigree path by which S295 entered US cultivar development remains undocumented (J. Zhang et al., 2024), marker surveys indicate that B12‐linked haplotypes are widespread among US upland cotton cultivars (Elassbli et al., 2021b). Both BB13 and B12 act as major, dominant factors on chromosome D02. Two hypotheses can therefore be considered: (1) BB13 and B12 represent the same locus, or (2) Bb13 is a tightly linked gene within the B12 region, segregating as a single Mendelian block, consistent with earlier conclusions that resistance to a US race mixture and an African isolate can be conditioned by the same gene or two tightly linked genes (Wallace & El‐Zik, 1989). Allelism tests and availability of diagnostic markers for B12, similar to the PACE markers developed for the BB13 in our current study, will be crucial to resolve these alternatives.

Temporal analysis of cultivar release further supports the above conclusions. Accessions developed in the late 1980s, and early 1990s were predominantly resistant (Table S4), reflecting intensive MAR‐derived selection, while susceptibility in later releases followed by renewed resistance deployment after 2000 (Table S4) mirrors the movement of Bb13 alleles across subpopulations. Overall, population and pedigree analyses confirm that Bb13 resistance originated from MAR germplasm and has disseminated through Mid‐South and national breeding programs.

Mapping and allelism tests

Molecular markers have facilitated the identification of more than 20 CBB resistance genes in cotton, including B2, B3, B6, B12, and BB13 (Amudha et al., 2003; Gowda et al., 2022; Rungis et al., 2002; Silva et al., 2014; Wright et al., 1998; Xiao et al., 2010; Yang, 2013; J. Zhang et al., 2020). These B genes have been genetically mapped to specific chromosomes using RFLP (restriction fragment length polymorphism), AFLP (amplified fragment length polymorphism), SSR (simple sequence repeats), and SNP markers. B2 and B3 are located on chromosome 20, linked to markers G1219 and pGH510a, respectively. Resistance conferred by B6 involves multiple loci associated with markers A1666 (unassigned linkage group), pAR1‐28 (chromosome 5), pAR827 (chromosome 4), and pAR723 (chromosome 14) (Wright et al., 1998). B12 is mapped to chromosome 14, linked to RFLP marker pAR043 at 10.4 (Rungis et al., 2002) to 11.4 cM (Wright et al., 1998), and four SNP markers spanning 3.4 cM have been identified flanking B12 (Xiao et al., 2010). On the physical map of Gossypium raimondii genome, B12 was further delineated to a 941 kb interval between SSR markers BNL1403 and CIR246 (Yang, 2013). Gowda et al. (2022) mapped BB13 locus to a 371 kb region on chromosome D02 within 0.95 cM window flanked by SNP markers i04890Gh and i04907Gh. While chromosome D02 represents key region harboring multiple resistance QTLs for CBB, additional genomic regions contributing to resistance have been reported on chromosomes A01, A05, D08, and D10 (Wright et al., 1998; Elassbli et al., 2021a). A study by Wright et al. (1998) identified three QTLs associated with CBB resistance, including two on D‐subgenome (Qb6c and Qb6d) and one on the A‐subgenome (Qb6b). Recent work by Elassbli et al. (2021a) mapped 11 QTLs, with three each on A01 (qBB‐A01‐1, qBB‐A01‐2, qBB‐A01‐3), A05 (qBB‐A05‐1, qBB‐A05‐2, qBB‐A05‐3), and D02 (qBB‐D02‐1, qBB‐D02‐2, qBB‐D02‐3), and one each on D08 (qBB‐D08‐1) and D10 (qBB‐D10‐1). Among these, B12 and BB13 are major‐effect loci on chromosome D02. To refine the BB13 region, linkage mapping across six diverse RIL populations (Figure S4) revealed that resistance to Xcm race 18 was consistently controlled by the same BB13 locus, confirming its broad effectiveness across genetic backgrounds. Variation in map length and interval resolution of the BB13 interval across the six RIL populations (Figure S4) likely reflects underlying genomic differences and recombination patterns among resistant parents, common in allopolyploid cotton (Adams & Wendel, 2005; Z. Chen, 2007). Integrating GWAS, linkage mapping, and PACE marker data narrowed the BB13 interval from 371 kb (Gowda et al., 2022) to 154 kb on chromosome D02, where BB13 found to be the sole major gene conferring resistance across the US DIV. No additional loci were detected in landraces or breeding panels.

An allelism test between the resistant landrace TX‐2367 (Paraguay) and Arkot 8102 (carrying Bb13) demonstrated that resistance in both lines is allelic (Figure S8), providing strong evidence that Bb13 governs race 18 resistance across upland cotton germplasm. This result is consistent with previous segregation studies in Paraguay that showed single dominant inheritance of CBB resistance in TX‐2367 (Follin et al., 1988). Collectively, GWAS, linkage mapping, and allelism analyses confirm that a single major gene, Bb13 on chromosome D02, confers hypersensitive resistance to Xcm race 18 in broad upland cotton germplasm.

Haplotype and candidate gene analysis

Haplotype‐based analyses provided additional resolution within the BB13 genomic region (Figure 6; Tables S7 and S9). Two complementary approaches were used: Haploview to define haplotype blocks based on pairwise LD, and Crosshap to group markers by linkage profiles. Haploview revealed distinct haplotypes differentiating resistant and susceptible accessions (Figure S5), while Crosshap identified three MGs (MG1–MG3) and four haplotypes (Figure 6). MG2 showed the strongest association with resistance, with alternate alleles linked to significantly reduced disease scores (Figure S7b). Five of the seven MG2 SNPs lie within or adjacent to NBS‐LRR genes in the 154 kb fine‐mapped interval (Table S8), indicating strong candidate genes underlying Bb13‐mediated resistance. Haplotype‐based frameworks have been used to identify significant marker alleles influencing disease resistance traits in cotton (Fang et al., 2010; Xiao et al., 2010), wheat (Tong et al., 2024), rice (Alam et al., 2024), and maize (Ding et al., 2015).

The PACE markers developed here strengthen the practical application of these findings. CBB16, derived from the significant SNP i46775Gh, consistently co‐segregated with resistance in the DIV and RILs (Figure S4), while CBB15 flanking the BB13 interval predicted CBB response with high accuracy (Table S10). Together, these markers provide robust diagnostic tools for marker‐assisted breeding and haplotype‐based selection of resistant lines.

The fine‐mapped BB13 interval (154.28 kb; 981,030–1,135,315 bp) contained 15 annotated genes (Table S9), six of which are strong candidates based on homology to known defense‐related genes: NBS‐LRR proteins (RGA4 and At4g27190), DELLA protein (GAI), ubiquitin‐like proteases (ULP1B, ESD4), NAC transcription factor (NAC014), and GRAS/HAT/BED zinc‐finger proteins (Gh_D02G013200, Gh_D02G013600, Gh_D02G013700). The NBS‐LRR proteins are well‐known mediators of hypersensitive responses and pathogen recognition (Cesari et al., 2014; Hutin et al., 2016), while DELLA proteins have been shown to enhance bacterial blight resistance through modulation of salicylic acid and jasmonic acid signaling pathways (De Vleesschauwer et al., 2016; Li et al., 2018). Similarly, zinc‐finger and NAC‐domain transcription factors are implicated in broad‐spectrum disease resistance and regulation of stress‐responsive pathways (Dong et al., 2024). These genes represent compelling candidates for functional validation and positional cloning of the Bb13 gene in cotton.

CONCLUSIONS

Over the past century, numerous studies have investigated bacterial blight resistance in cotton (Bird & Blank, 1951; Bourland, 2018; Innes, 1974; Girardot et al., 1986; Knight & Clouston, 1939). Both major genes and QTLs conferring resistance to CBB have been reported, particularly on chromosome D02, their relationships and the extent of resistance diversity within US cotton germplasm remained unclear, limiting the systematic use of allelic variation in cultivar development. Using population‐level phenotyping for race 18 resistance, genome‐wide high‐density SNPs, linkage mapping across multiple RIL populations, and novel PACE markers, we demonstrated that a single major gene, Bb13, confers resistance to Xcm (race 18) across US upland cotton germplasm. Population structure analysis revealed an uneven distribution of CBB resistance and indicated that Bb13 was likely introgressed from the African G. hirsutum cultivar S295, with subsequent dissemination into US breeding programs through accessions derived from the MAR program (Bird, 1960, 1962; Follin et al., 1988). By integrating GWAS, linkage mapping, targeted genomic, and haplotype analyses, we refined BB13 to a 154.28 kb region harboring six disease‐resistance candidate genes and developed two diagnostic PACE markers suitable for marker‐assisted selection. The phenotyped DIV and newly developed PACE markers can be directly utilized in breeding programs to accelerate the development of cultivars with durable CBB resistance, eliminating the need for labor‐intensive field‐based screening. Finally, this work established a robust foundation for positional cloning and functional characterization of BB13 to enable the use of biotechnology for developing durable resistance to CBB in cotton.
