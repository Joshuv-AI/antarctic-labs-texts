Association between ultra-short heart rate variability and risk of Parkinson’s disease: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Ruihan Wang, Kai Zhou, Nannan Li, Yingying Tang, Hui Gao, Linyuan Qin, Hanlin Cai, Feng Yang, Caimei Luo, Shiyu Feng, Mengyao Guo, Yongping Chen, Qing Gao, Qin Chen, Jay Magaziner
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCross-sectional studies have suggested that patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD) have significantly lower heart rate variability (HRV) than healthy controls. However, the role of ultra-short HRV (usHRV) as an early biomarker for PD remains unclear. The objective of this study was to investigate the association between usHRV and PD risk and its underlying mechanisms.MethodsIn a prospective cohort study based on the UK Biobank, participants without PD and dementia at baseline who had available 15-second resting electrocardiogram data (n = 48 202) were included. The participants were followed up for an average of 12.24 years, and some were diagnosed with PD (n = 307). Cox proportional hazards models were used to examine the association between usHRV parameters and PD risk. A nested case-control study was conducted within the cohort to further investigate temporal trends in HRV. Mediation analysis was used to explore the underlying mechanisms driven by brain structure, peripheral inflammation, and proteomic biomarkers.ResultsWe found that lower usHRV parameters were significantly associated with an increased PD risk. Notably, an L-shaped association was observed between the corrected root mean square of successive differences and PD risk. Temporal trend analysis suggested usHRV levels of patients with PD started to decline approximately 10 years before diagnosis. Mediation analysis revealed that thalamus-related fiber tracts, plasma inflammatory, and neuroendocrine markers mediated the association between usHRV and PD risk.ConclusionsOur findings provide evidence supporting that usHRV may serve as an early, convenient, and noninvasive biomarker of PD risk up to a decade before diagnosis.Keywords: Heart rate variability, Parkinson’s disease, Autonomic nervous system, Autonomic dysfunction

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is the second most common neurodegenerative diseases characterized by both motor and a wide range of nonmotor symptoms.1 Notably, nonmotor symptoms usually occur in the prodromal phase, years prior to the clinical diagnosis of PD.2 Autonomic disorders are particularly common among the nonmotor symptoms of PD.3 It is estimated that approximately 80% to 90% of patients with PD will experience alterations in the autonomic system,4 which may manifest as hypersalivation, swallowing difficulties, constipation, bladder disturbances, sexual dysfunction, or orthostatic hypotension.5 Assessing autonomic nervous system function in PD offers a promising approach to early diagnosis and enhancing the knowledge of disease progression.

Heart rate variability (HRV), which refers to beat-to-beat variations in heart rate, reflects the balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic nervous system modulation of cardiac activity.6 Cross-sectional studies utilizing short- or long-term electrocardiogram recordings from small sample sizes have suggested that HRV may serve as a potential objective, noninvasive marker of autonomic nervous system function in PD patients.7–9 Two longitudinal study have found lower HRV from 2 minute10 or whole night11 ECG recordings in individuals who developed PD during follow-up, but the temporal progression of HRV has not been investigated due to a small number of cases. According to established guidelines,6 short-term HRV parameters should be derived from ECG recordings lasting ≥5 minutes, which may limit its widespread application. To address this limitation, a viable alternative to standard short-term HRV measures is needed. Several studies have shown strong agreement between ultra-short HRV (usHRV) derived from 10- to 30-second ECG recordings and traditional short-term HRV metrics.12,13 Moreover, usHRV has been found to be associated with blood pressure14 and cardiovascular events,15 highlighting its potential as a valuable tool in clinical assessments. However, the longitudinal association between usHRV and PD risk remains unexplored.

Using a large prospective cohort from the UKB, we aimed to identify usHRV parameters associated with the PD risk outcomes over long-term follow-up and to explore the underlying brain structural and peripheral inflammatory mechanisms. Additionally, we investigated the temporal trends of usHRV parameters over a 12-year period prior to PD diagnosis.

Methods

Study population and design

The UK Biobank (UKB) is a large-scale prospective cohort study that enrolled over 500 000 participants aged 40 to 69 years across 22 centers distributed throughout Scotland, England, and Wales. Recruitment took place between 2006 and 2010, with all participants providing written informed consent. Details regarding the study design and population have been previously described.16

This prospective cohort study enrolled individuals who underwent a resting recording of ECG assessments during their initial visit to calculate HRV parameters. Participants with prevalent dementia, PD, or a permanent pacemaker were excluded from the analysis of HRV parameters and the risk of incident PD. A subgroup of participants who underwent laboratory blood tests or brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was further included for mediation analysis. The study was conducted using data from the UKB Resource under application number 98130, and the study design is illustrated in Figure 1.

Study design and workflow. Abbreviations: ECG, electrocardiogram; HRV, heart rate variability; PD, Parkinson’s disease.

ECG examination and HRV parameters

In the UKB, baseline 3-lead ECG recordings (leads I, II, and III) were obtained at rest (15 seconds), during exercise (6 minutes), and during recovery (1 minute) from participants who underwent cardiovascular assessments using a stationary bicycle between 2009 and 2010. ECG signals were recorded using a GE CardioSoft device (AM-USB 6.5, CardioSoft v6.51) at a sampling frequency of 500 Hz.

The 15-second resting ECG was used to calculate HRV parameters. Normal R waves were identified using the “jqrs” algorithm in the Python “pecg” package.17 We excluded recordings with RR intervals exceeding 2 seconds or below 0.428 seconds, which were indicative of ectopic beats. Noise levels were assessed by calculating the overall standard deviation (SD) of a moving SD within a 3-beat window; an overall SD close to zero indicated minimal noise, whereas recordings exceeding the 98th percentile of SD values were excluded as excessively noisy. Participants with a history of permanent pacemaker insertion prior to ECG assessment were also excluded.

Well-established time domain HRV parameters applicable to ultra-short-term analysis were calculated,18 including the root mean square of successive differences (RMSSD), the standard deviation of successive differences (SDSD), and the standard deviation of normal-to-normal intervals (SDNN). To account for the dependency of HRV on mean inter-beat intervals (IBI), corrected HRV parameters (RMSSDc and SDNNc) were also calculated using the coefficient of variation approach.19 Frequency-domain and nonlinear parameters were not included, as their reliable estimation typically requires ECG recordings longer than one minute.18 All HRV parameters were log transformed to account for skewed distributions. The mean R-R interval and resting heart rate were also calculated for each ECG recording.

Incident Parkinson’s disease

Participants with PD were identified using primary or secondary diagnoses recorded in the UKB’s inpatient hospital records, including the Hospital Episode Statistics database, the Scottish Morbidity Record, and the Patient Episode Database. Diagnoses were based on the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) coding system (332 for ICD-9 and G20 for ICD-10). Additional patients with PD were identified from death registry data where PD was recorded as an underlying or contributory cause of death.

Peripheral inflammatory and proteomic markers

Inflammatory marker levels were obtained from baseline hematological assays of UKB participants taken at recruitment, as detailed in the UKB hematology documentation. Inflammatory markers included the level of C-reactive protein and the counts of blood leukocytes, neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, and platelets.

Plasma proteomic analysis was performed by Olink Explore 1536 panel assay. The method used DNA-tagged antibodies for each analyte and utilizes amplification in a proximity extension assay to measure protein levels. These protein levels were converted into normalized protein expression (NPX) units, which represent a relative quantification on a log-2 scale. NPX data underwent rigorous quality control, incorporating various assay controls, prior to subsequent analysis. A total of 48 top-ranked proteins (Table S3) screened by the Light Gradient Boosting Machine classifier to predict PD, as shown in a previous study20 were included in the analyses.

MRI data management

High-resolution brain MRI data (1 × 1 × 1 mm) from the UKB were acquired using a Siemens Skyra 3T scanner (Siemens Healthcare) equipped with a 32-channel head coil. Detailed imaging protocols have been previously described.21 Cross-sectional brain MRI data from participants who underwent baseline scans in 2014 were included to assess brain structural measures. Preprocessing of diffusion MRI images was performed by the UKB imaging team, and subsequent diffusion modeling and tractography analyses were conducted using the FMRIB Software Library version 6.0. Diffusion MRI parameters included fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD) of 27 white matter tracts defined by AutoPtx.

Statistical analysis

Population characteristics were summarized using means (SD) or medians (interquartile ranges) for continuous variables and frequencies (proportions) for categorical variables. Cox proportional hazards models were applied to evaluate the associations between HRV parameters and the risk of developing PD. Proportional hazard assumptions were tested using Schoenfeld residuals, with no significant violations detected. Multiple imputations were performed using XGBoost to address missing covariates and minimize potential selection bias. Nonlinear associations between HRV parameters and PD risk were explored using restricted cubic spline models with 3 knots (10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles).

The Cox proportional hazards model was adjusted for sociodemographic factors (age, sex, ethnicity, education, body mass index, and Townsend Deprivation Index), lifestyle factors (smoking status, alcohol consumption, healthy sleep score, and metabolic equivalent of tasks [MET] minutes per week), and disease-related risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, ischemic heart disease, stroke, heart failure, arrhythmia, peripheral vascular disease, and polygenic risk score for PD).

A nested case-control study was conducted to evaluate temporal trends of HRV. Patients with PD identified during the follow-up period were defined as cases, whereas the controls were defined as individuals who remained free of PD and dementia at baseline and throughout the follow-up period. For each patient with PD, one control participant was selected using 1:1 propensity score matching with nearest-neighbor matching. The propensity score was estimated from a logistic regression model including sociodemographic, lifestyle, and disease-related risk factors. For each patient with PD, the time before clinical diagnosis was calculated. For each matched control, a corresponding “proxy” time was assigned to align with the index date of the matched case. Both cases and controls were then grouped into 2-year intervals according to their time to diagnosis (or proxy time). Within each interval, HRV values were standardized by z-score transformation, defined as subtracting the mean of controls and dividing by the standard deviation of controls. For each HRV parameter, temporal trajectories were modeled using locally estimated scatterplot smoothing regression across the years before PD diagnosis. The predicted temporal trends with their 95% CI were plotted for each HRV parameters. As sensitivity analyses, we additionally performed 1:2 and 1:4 matching, in which each case was matched to 2 or 4 controls using the same propensity score model and matching algorithm. This allowed us to examine the robustness of the findings under alternative matching ratios.

Mediation analyses were conducted to investigate the role of peripheral inflammatory and proteomic markers as well as brain MRI structures in the association between HRV parameters and PD risk. HRV parameters associated with PD risk were first identified using Cox proportional hazards models. Multiple linear regression models were used to assess the associations between HRV parameters and potential mediators (peripheral markers or MRI parameters), and false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied separately within peripheral markers or MRI parameters. Mediators with FDR-adjusted p < .05 were retained for further evaluation. Subsequently, logistic regression models were applied to evaluate the associations between these mediators and PD outcome, followed by an additional FDR correction across these mediators. Only mediators that remained significant after this correction were included in the final mediation analyses. A bootstrap mediation model (1000 replicates) was then applied to estimate the indirect effect (βIE) and mediation proportion, along with the corresponding 95% confidence intervals. All mediation analyses were adjusted for the confounding covariates as mentioned above. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1), and 2-sided p < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Reporting was in line with the guidelines set by the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) (Supplementary Material, STROBE Checklist).

Results

Baseline characteristics

The characteristics of the participants stratified by incident PD are summarized in Table S1. Among 48 202 participants without prevalent PD or dementia who had eligible baseline 15-second resting ECG data, 307 participants developed PD over a mean follow-up period of 12.24 ± 1.42 years. Compared to the non-PD group, participants who developed PD were more likely to be older, male, previous drinkers, non-current smokers, and to be diagnosed with hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, and other cardiovascular diseases, including ischemic heart disease, stroke, heart failure, arrhythmia, and peripheral vascular disease. Physical activity levels, as indicated by MET minutes per week, were significantly lower in participants with incident PD. Although the healthy sleep score did not differ significantly between the 2 groups, participants with incident PD were less likely to exhibit a late chronotype and more likely to report daytime sleepiness compared to the non-PD group. Baseline coffee and tea intake were not significantly different between participants with and without incident PD. Participants with incident PD were more likely to have a high-grade polygenic risk scores (PRS) for PD and less likely to have a low-grade PRS. HRV parameters were significantly lower in the PD group (Table S1).

Associations of HRV parameters with PD risk

The presentation of Cox proportional hazards analyses with hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) is shown in Figure 2. All the HRV parameters were treated as a continuous variable. Lower HRV was associated with increased PD risk. For each one-unit decrement, significant associations were observed for SDSD (HR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.04-1.34, p = .013), SDNN (HR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.04-1.39, p = .015), SDNNc (HR = 1.21, 95% CI: 1.04-1.41, p = .014), RMSSD (HR = 1.18, 95% CI: 1.04-1.35, p = .013), and RMSSDc (HR = 1.20, 95% CI: 1.05-1.39, p = .010). When HRV quartiles were analyzed, participants with the lowest HRV quartile exhibited a significantly higher PD risk compared to those in the highest quartile. Specifically, the HRs for SDSD, RMSSD, and RMSSDc were 1.51 (95% CI: 1.08-2.11, p = .015), 1.48 (95% CI: 1.07-2.05, p = 0.019), and 1.44 (95% CI: 1.04-1.99, p = .029), respectively (Figure 2).

Restricted cubic spline analyses indicated an L-shaped association between RMSSDc and the PD risk (p for nonlinearity = .0287) after adjusting for all confounding covariates) (Figure S1, see online supplementary material for a color version of this figure). The 2-piecewise Cox proportional hazards pinpointed an inflection point for PD (p for the log-likelihood ratio = .045) (Table S2). Below the inflection point of −2.6369, decrease in RMSSDc was associated with a 63% increased PD risk (HR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.18-2.24, p = .003). In contrast, when RMSSDc was greater than −2.6369, there was no association between RMSSDc and PD. We found no significant nonlinear associations between SDSD, SDNN, SDNNc, RMSSD, and PD.

The temporal trends of HRV parameters change during a period of 12 years before PD diagnosis are shown in Figure 3. The HRV parameters of patients with PD started to decrease about 10 years before diagnosis, with similar temporal patterns across different matching ratios (Figure 3, Figures S2 and S3, see online supplementary material for a color version of these figures). The windows of most pronounced group differences shifted across different matching ratios: 6-8 years prior in the 1:1 analysis, 4-6 years in the 1:2 analysis, and without significant difference in the 1:4 analysis (Tables S7-S9).

Potential biological mechanisms underlying the association between HRV and PD

All the HRV parameters were selected because they showed significant association with PD. In mediation analyses, initial screening using multiple linear regression models identified 12 peripheral markers (Table S4) and 5 MRI measures (Table S5) associated with HRV. Subsequent logistic regression further narrowed these to 3 peripheral markers and 4 MRI measures significantly linked to PD (Table S6).

Among the 7 potential mediators, mediation analyses suggested negative mediation proportions for monocyte count, plasma interleukin-13 receptor subunit alpha-1 (IL13RA1) and secretogranin-2 (SCG2) in the relationship between HRV metrics and PD risk (Figure 4A). As for brain structural measures, lower FA and higher MD of left anterior thalamic radiation, as well as higher MD of right posterior thalamic radiation and inferior longitudinal fasciculus were suggested to play a role in the association between the HRV parameters and increased PD risk (Figure 4B).

Discussion

The study provided evidence of the longitudinal association between HRV parameters and the risk of incident PD based on a large cohort of 48 202 UKB participants. We indicated that individuals with HRV in the lowest quartile had a significantly higher risk of developing PD. Furthermore, reduced HRV may potentially contribute to PD pathogenesis by impairing the integrity of thalamus-related fiber tracts while potentially attenuating the PD risk through compensatory regulation of neuroinflammation and neuroendocrine pathways. Our findings suggested that early change in HRV may serve as a preclinical biomarker about 10 years prior to PD diagnosis.

In the current study, decline in HRV parameters, particularly in the lowest quartile, was significantly associated with an increased PD risk. This finding aligned with previous studies9,10 which suggested reduced HRV markers of parasympathetic activity (RMSSD) and total variability (SDNN) in participants with PD currently or in the future. However, it’s important to note that HRV parameters in the previous studies9,10 were derived from either short- or long-term (ranging from 1 minute to 24 hours) ECG recordings, and the influence of inter-beat interval on HRV was not considered. In contrast, our prospective study utilized ultra-short-term (15 second) ECG recordings and demonstrated that 5 time-domain HRV parameters, that is, RMSSD, SDNN, and SDSD, as well as inter-beat interval corrected RMSSD and SDNN, were associated with PD risk. Furthermore, we observed an L-shaped association between inter-beat interval-corrected RMSSD and PD risk, which suggested that the risk of PD increased markedly only when inter-beat interval corrected RMSSD fell below a specific threshold. Specifically, the analysis of HRV quartiles also revealed that only participants in the lowest quartile exhibited a markedly higher risk of developing PD compared to those in the highest quartile. Taken together, these findings emphasized the potential significance of this threshold as a critical predictive cutoff point. Further studies needed to validate these findings and explore the potential of HRV as a diagnostic or prognostic neurophysiological biomarker for PD.

We found that HRV parameters in patients with incident PD were found to decline up to 10 years prior to diagnosis across different matching strategies, suggesting the presence of autonomic dysfunction in the preclinical stage of PD. This finding supports prior research indicating that Lewy body pathology accumulates in the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagal nerve of the autonomic nervous system earlier than in the substantia nigra,22 with the latter exhibiting Lewy body pathology approximately 5-6 years before clinical onset, as indicated by postmortem studies23 and striatal 18F-dopamine positron emission tomography imaging.24 Some studies provided clinical evidence suggesting that cardiac denervation may serve as a premotor biomarker of PD,8,25 even occurring up to 4 years before the emergence of abnormal dopaminergic imaging, still without Parkinsonian symptoms.26 Moreover, although the trajectories suggested a rebound in HRV about 4-6 years before diagnosis, this pattern should be interpreted with caution. The limited number of PD cases in the 0-2 and 2-4 year windows increased uncertainty and may exaggerate fluctuations at the curves’ edges, making the upturn more likely a methodological artifact than a real biological rebound. The time window of the most pronounced group differences varied by matching ratio, with 6-8 years before diagnosis in the 1:1 analysis, 4-6 years in the 1:2 analysis, and absent in the 1:4 analysis. This pattern likely reflects that adding more controls stabilized estimates but also introduced heterogeneity, which reduced sensitivity to small differences. Accordingly, we suggested a potential temporal pattern of HRV alterations preceding the clinical onset of PD. Although impaired HRV has been reported to significantly correlate with the duration of PD,27 evidence regarding the trajectory of HRV changes prior to the clinical PD onset remains limited. Future studies could further investigate dynamic changes in autonomic function across different stages of PD.

Our results indicated that thalamus-related fiber tracts mainly mediate the association between HRV and the PD risk. Previous studies have found microstructural changes of the anterior thalamic radiation,28 posterior thalamic radiation,29 and inferior longitudinal fasciculus30 in patients with PD, and those alterations were associated with motor30 or cognitive28 dysfunction. Thalamus was considered a central hub in the central baroreflex‐mediated autonomic regulation.31 A reduction in thalamic volume was associated with slower orthostatic heart rate recovery in individuals aged ≥50 years.32 Moreover, loss of thalamic volume and decreased functional connectivity were associated with high‐risk phenotypes for sudden cardiac death in epilepsy33 and schizophrenia.34 These evidences suggested that thalamic volume, integrity, and connectivity may be crucial to autonomic function. In addition, the activity of about 70% of the thalamic neurons was modulated by either the heartbeat, the cardiac inter-beat interval, or the respiration.35 Consistently, we further emphasized the critical role of thalamic fiber tract integrity in the association between autonomic function indicated by HRV and PD risk.

We observed negative mediation effects for monocyte count, plasma level of IL13RA1 and SCG2 in the relationship between HRV and PD risk. These findings might reflect compensatory mechanisms triggered by autonomic dysfunction. Reduced HRV has been linked to heightened systemic inflammation, including increased IL-6, CRP, and leukocyte counts.36 However, autonomic imbalance may also activate neuroimmune reflexes, such as the vagal cholinergic anti-inflammatory pathway, which inhibits macrophage-derived TNF release via α7nAChR,37 and sympathetic β2-adrenergic signaling, which induces IL-10 production and suppresses pro-inflammatory cytokines.38 These mechanisms may account for the association of higher monocyte counts with reduced PD risk,39 and lower HRV.

Moreover, IL13RA1 may also play a protective role. As part of the IL-4/IL-13 receptor complex, IL13RA1 mediates alternative macrophage activation, promoting anti-inflammatory and tissue-repair responses.40 Although IL13RA1 expression in dopaminergic neurons has been associated with oxidative vulnerability,41 its increased levels in our analysis likely reflect early compensatory anti-inflammatory signaling. Similarly, SCG2, a member of the granin family, is crucial for dense-core vesicle formation and is co-stored with catecholamines.42 Upon sympathetic stimulation, SCG2-derived secretoneurin is released,43 which facilitates striatal dopamine release and exerts neuroprotective effects.44 Previous study has reported decreased CSF levels of SCG2 in PD patients,45 supporting our observation that its upregulation may represent a compensatory attempt to maintain dopaminergic neurotransmission during early disease stages.

Overall, these results underscored the complex interaction between autonomic dysfunction, immune regulation, and neurosecretory pathways in shaping PD risk. Our findings suggest that reduced HRV may not only indicate autonomic imbalance but also trigger compensatory mechanisms through monocyte activation, and IL13RA1 and SCG2 upregulation, highlighting biological links between autonomic function and neurodegeneration.

This study has several strengths. Firstly, it was based on a large sample size of 48 202 participants, employed a prospective design, included a long-term follow-up, and explored the temporal trends of HRV parameters before PD diagnosis. Secondly, we explored the association between HRV parameters derived from 15-second ultrashort ECG recordings and PD risk, as well as the temporal trend of HRV prior to PD diagnosis. usHRV could be easily captured by wearable devices such as smartwatches,46 offering a practical and low-burden approach for large-scale community screening. This feasibility enabled early risk stratification of PD, whereby individuals with persistently reduced usHRV might be flagged as higher-risk and prioritized for closer neurological follow-up or confirmatory biomarker testing in clinical settings. Third, we investigate both brain and peripheral mechanisms between HRV and PD risk, which provided a comprehensive insight into the autonomic dysfunction of PD.

Our study has some limitations. First, this study relied exclusively on time-domain HRV parameters, which are the most robustly derived from ultrashort recordings. Frequency-domain and nonlinear parameters can provide additional insights into autonomic regulation; however, their reliable estimation typically requires more than 1 minute of recording.18 Thus, future studies with extended ECG recordings may help to overcome this gap. Second, the PD outcome was identified based on ICD codes but lacked the status of biological evidence.47 Third, the study sample in the UKB cohort is limited in representativeness, as participants tend to be healthier than the general population and predominantly of White ethnicity.48,49 These characteristics may restrict the number of participants who develop PD and limit the generalizability of our findings to more diverse populations or individuals with more severe disease. Future validation in cohorts with greater ethnic and clinical heterogeneity will be important.

In conclusion, this study provides evidence for a longitudinal association between usHRV and PD in the general population. Given the wide availability, noninvasiveness, and efficiency of ultrashort-term ECG compared to neuroimaging and CSF testing, it could serve as a practical tool for widespread screening of populations at high risk for PD. Collectively, our findings support usHRV as an early and convenient biomarker for assessing PD risk up to a decade prior to diagnosis.
