Mettl3 promotes reprogramming and axonogenesis of induced retinal ganglion cells
Authors: Ke Zhang, Huilin Liang, Shuyi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryDirect somatic cell-to-neuronal fate reprogramming (induced neurons, iNs) is valuable for translational and basic research. N6-Methyladenosine (m6A), the most prevalent mRNA epitranscriptomic modification, is critical for neural biology, but its role in iN reprogramming remains elusive. Using our induced retinal ganglion cell-like neuron (iRGC) system, we found dynamic m6A epitranscriptomic adjustments during iRGC reprogramming. Mettl3, the core component of the m6A methyltransferase complex, promoted iRGC fate reprogramming and axon development. Integrated RNA-seq/MeRIP-seq analyses and gene function interrogations identified three m6A-modified genes (Prokr1, Rspo1, and Fmo2) as key mediators of Mettl3 effects. Collectively, our study elucidated the essential roles and molecular mechanisms of the m6A epitranscriptomic modification in neuronal fate reprogramming and axon development. These findings could aid future investigations designed to improve neuronal fate and axon regeneration outcomes for therapeutic purposes to treat neurodegenerative diseases.Keywords: Mettl3, m⁶A modification, neural fate reprogramming, axonogenesis, induced retinal ganglion cells, somatic cell reprogramming

Introduction

Direct somatic cell reprogramming is an excellent strategy for producing precious cells from easily accessible source cells, such as fibroblasts and blood cells, for regenerative medicine, drug discovery and basic research (Morris and Daley, 2013; Wang et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2015). In 2010, the Wernig group pioneered the direct reprogramming of fibroblasts into functional generic neurons (induced neurons, iNs) via overexpression of three neurogenic transcription factors (TFs), Ascl1, Brn2, and Myt1l (Vierbuchen et al., 2010). Since then, various protocols have been developed to generate subtype-specific neurons by utilizing lineage-restricted TFs (Blanchard et al., 2015; Caiazzo et al., 2011; Kim et al., 2011; Mertens et al., 2016; Pang et al., 2011; Wainger et al., 2015; Yoo et al., 2011). iNs generated through direct somatic cell reprogramming not only provide donor cells for transplantation to treat neurodegenerative diseases but also represent excellent model systems for studying neural development, physiology, aging, and pathology (Aversano et al., 2022; Legault et al., 2022; Mertens et al., 2018; Pitrez et al., 2024). Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying iN reprogramming holds significant theoretical and translational value.

Retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) are the projection neurons in the retina that transmit visual information collected by the eye to the visual center of the brain via axons that coalesce into the optic nerve (Sernagor et al., 2001). RGC axons are exceptionally vulnerable to damages under various conditions, such as ocular hypertension and aging, leading to glaucoma, which is the most prevalent retinal degeneration disease causing blindness (Jonas et al., 2017). All RGCs are generated by retinal progenitor cells during development, and the adult mammalian retina lacks intrinsic ability to regenerate RGCs or their axons once these cells (and/or axons) are lost, resulting in poor prognosis for glaucoma patients (Laha et al., 2017; Nguyen-Ba-Charvet and Rebsam, 2020; Pernet and Schwab, 2014). In an effort to produce sufficient numbers of RGCs for translational and theoretical applications, we previously developed an efficient iN reprogramming method to convert fibroblasts into RGC-like neurons (iRGCs) via overexpression of three TFs, Ascl1, Brn3b, and Islet1 (Wang et al., 2020). Under induction of these three TFs, mouse fibroblasts can be quickly and highly efficiently converted into iRGCs in as short as 3 days; further culture of these iNs yields mature iRGCs that resemble native RGCs at both the transcriptomic and functional levels (Wang et al., 2020). We have further demonstrated that this system represents an excellent model for studying RGC axon development and maturation (Zhang et al., 2024).

N6-Methyladenosine (m6A), the most abundant internal mRNA modification in eukaryotes, functions as a multifaceted regulator of gene expression by modulating critical RNA dynamics, including translation, degradation, subcellular localization, and alternative splicing (Meyer and Jaffrey, 2014; Roundtree et al., 2017). This modification is catalyzed by a protein complex composed of methyltransferase-like 3 (Mettl3) and several other auxiliary components. m6A modifications are recognized by YTH domain-containing reader proteins, while demethylation is mediated by Fto and Alkbh5 erasers (Patil et al., 2018; Shi et al., 2019; Zaccara et al., 2019). m6A plays essential roles in various biological processes, including stem cell self-renewal and differentiation, tissue development and function, and tumorigenesis (Frye and Blanco, 2016; Liu et al., 2019; Stojkovic and Fujimori, 2017). Studies have also revealed essential roles for m6A in neural development, physiology, and pathology (Livneh et al., 2020; Shen et al., 2025; Xin et al., 2022; Yoon et al., 2017). However, the roles and underlying mechanisms of m6A modification during iN reprogramming remain elusive.

In this study, exploiting the convenient and efficient iRGC reprogramming system, we investigated the role and underlying molecular mechanisms of Mettl3, the core component of the m6A-methyltransferase complex, during iRGC reprogramming. Our study demonstrated that Mettl3 promotes iRGC reprogramming and axon development by promoting the degradation of its m6A-modified downstream targets.

Results

m6A epitranscriptome dynamics during iRGC reprogramming

We previously developed an efficient iRGC reprogramming protocol, wherein mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs) are transfected with doxycycline-controlled Ascl1-Brn3b-Islet1-overexpressing (ABI-OE) polycistronic lentiviruses. Upon ABI induction, neuron-like cells with growing neurites become apparent by day 5 after induction (day 5), and mature iRGCs at day 13 resemble native RGCs at both transcriptomic and functional level (Wang et al., 2020) (Figure 1A). To study the role of m6A in iRGC reprogramming, we first profiled the m6A epitranscriptomes of cells undergoing iRGC reprogramming, and parental MEFs. For this, we constructed an ABI-2A-GFP lentiviral vector and isolated reprogramming cells via fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) of GFP+ cells. Due to the challenge of obtaining sufficient GFP+-iRGCs for conventional MeRIP-seq, we adopted a modified MeRIP-seq protocol integrated with the SMART-seq amplification strategy (Lee et al., 2019). We performed sequencing experiments at three time points: parental MEFs, early reprogramming cells at day 5, and mature iRGCs at day 13 (Figure 1A). Sequencing data processing and peak calling analyses revealed 5439 m6A-modified genes in MEFs, 6,031 in day 5 iRGCs, and 4,640 in day 13 iRGCs (fold-enrichment cutoff ≥2) (Table S1). Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analyses revealed that many genes retained m6A modification across all stages (Figure 1B), and the m6A epitranscriptomes of both day 5 and day 13 iRGCs, as well as that of MEFs, were predominantly enriched in genes involved in RNA metabolism and function, such as mRNA processing, RNA splicing, and ribosome biogenesis (Figures 1C, 1D, andS1A). Consistently, the common m6A-modified genes in cells at all three time points were also significantly enriched for RNA metabolism and functional genes (Figure 1E). However, there were also a large number of genes adjusted their m6A modification status during reprogramming (Figure 1B). Specifically, a comparison of the m6A epitranscriptome of day 5 iRGCs with that of the starting MEFs showed that day 5 iRGC unique m6A epitranscriptome was more enriched in cellular pathways such as “mRNA processing” and “chromatin remodeling.” Additionally, genes involved in “axonogenesis,” including Nefl, Ephb2, Efnb1, and Unc5c, were enriched in day 5 iRGC unique m6A epitranscriptome (Figure 1F; Table S1), in line with the active neurite growth activity observed for the reprogramming cells at this stage. On the other hand, the lost m6A-modified genes during this period were enriched in biological processes related to protein dynamics, such as “tRNA aminoacylation” and “macroautophagy” (Figure S1B). Comparing day 13 iRGC m6A-epitranscriptome with MEF m6A-epitranscriptome showed that cellular pathways involved in visual sensory system development were most prominently enriched in the mature iRGC m6A epitranscriptome (Figure 1G), in line with the RGC identity of the iRGCs at this stage. Comparing day 13 iRGC m6A-epitranscriptome with day 5 iRGC m6A-epitranscriptome and MEF m6A-epitranscriptome showed that newly acquired m6A-modified genes were enriched in biological processes related to protein secretion, including many genes involved in synapse formation and function such as Cavin1, Rab34, Efna5, and Syt4 (Figure S1C; Table S1). For those m6A-modified genes that were lost at day 5 but reappeared at day 13, they were enriched in biological processes related to protein synthesis, such as “tRNA aminoacylation” (Figure S1D).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the m6A epitranscriptome of the reprogramming cells undergo dynamic changes during iRGC reprogramming, which correlates with active cellular activities during the process.

Mettl3 promotes iRGC axon development

The MeRIP-seq analyses showed that the group of day 5 iRGC unique m6A-modified genes was enriched for genes involved in axonogenesis (Figure 1F). Previously, we have demonstrated that the iRGC system is an excellent model for studying RGC axon development (Zhang et al., 2024). Exploiting this property, we investigated whether m6A modification regulates iRGC axon development. For this purpose, we focused on Mettl3, the core catalytic component of the m6A methyltransferase complex. Immunofluorescence (IF) staining showed that METTL3 was abundantly expressed in the nuclei of both parental MEFs and reprogramming iRGCs (Figure 2A). We then designed two shRNAs targeting Mettl3 and cloned them into an shRNA-expressing lentiviral vector. We cotransfected MEFs with ABI-OE lentiviruses and Mettl3-shRNA lentiviruses. Mettl3-shRNA lentiviruses infection significantly decreased METTL3 expression (Figure 2B, arrows point to GFP+-shRNA expressing cells; shRNA-lentiviral vectors also carry a GFP reporter), demonstrating the efficiency of the shRNA-mediated knockdown. We then examined the axon length of reprogramming cells at day 5, when the cells were at the stage of active axon development. Measurement and quantification of the length of the iRGC axons revealed that iRGCs in the shRNA groups exhibited shorter axons than those in the control group (Figure 2C). In the control group, axon lengths ranged from 33.80 to 152.18 μm (interquartile range), with an average of 108.26 ± 4.42 μm (n = 581 cells from 4 independent experiments). In contrast, axon lengths of the Mettl3-shRNA #1 group ranged from 18.84 to 57.76 μm (interquartile range), with an average of 50.20 ± 2.24 μm (n = 611 cells from 4 independent experiments), which was significantly shorter than that of the control group (p < 0.001), whereas axon lengths of the Mettl3-shRNA #2 group ranged from 18.31 to 48.57 μm (interquartile range), with an average of 50.50 ± 3.22 μm (n = 468 cells from 4 independent experiments), which was also significantly shorter than that of the control group (p < 0.001) (Figure 2D), demonstrating that Mettl3 is required for iRGC axon development. To independently validate the functional impact of Mettl3 loss-of-function on iRGC axon development, we derived MEFs from Mettl3 conditional knockout mice (Mettl3flox/flox). We co-transduced these MEFs with ABI-OE lentiviruses and Cre recombinase-expressing lentiviruses, which will convert Mettl3flox/flox cells into Mettl3-knockout (Mettl3-KO) cells (Figures S2A and S2B). Quantification of iRGC axon length at day 5 showed that axon lengths of Mettl3-KO iRGCs ranged from 30.74 to 106.70 μm (interquartile range), with an average of 91.81 ± 9.69 μm (n = 169 cells from 3 independent experiments), significantly shorter than the control group, which had a mean length of 137.50 ± 7.94 μm (p < 0.001; 3 independent experiments) (Figures S2C and S2D). These data further confirmed that Mettl3 is indispensable for iRGC axon development. We then tested whether overexpressing Mettl3 could promote iRGC axon development. To this end, we constructed a Flag-tagged Mettl3-overexpressing (Mettl3-OE) lentivirus and cotransfected MEFs with ABI-OE and Mettl3-OE lentiviruses (Figure 2E). Measurements and quantification of the axon length revealed that the length of the axons of iRGCs overexpressing Mettl3 ranged from 38.82 to 154.5 μm (interquartile range), with an average of 124.5 ± 4.97 μm (n = 884 cells from 4 independent experiments), which was significantly longer than that of control group (which ranged from 25.27 to 109.5 μm and averaged at 93.95 ± 4.16 μm; n = 809 cells; p < 0.001; 4 independent experiments) (Figures 2F and 2G).

The observed effects of Mettl3 manipulation on iRGC axon growth might be secondary to altered iRGC neuronal reprogramming and maturation rather than direct regulation of axonogenesis. To rule out this possibility, we conducted a refined set of experiments in which Mettl3-shRNA or Mettl3-OE lentivirus transduction was delayed until day 4, a time point at which iRGC fate specification is stably established (Wang et al., 2020). Axon lengths were subsequently quantified at days 6, 7, 8, and 9. The results showed that delayed Mettl3 knockdown via either shRNA also resulted in profound impairment of axon outgrowth, with iRGCs showing arrested axon elongation at as early as day 7 (Figure 2H). Conversely, delayed Mettl3 overexpression moderately accelerated iRGC axon growth at days 6 and 7, although this promotional effect diminished at later time points (Figure 2H). These data strongly support that Mettl3 most likely exerts a direct regulatory role in iRGC axon development.

In summary, combined loss- and gain-of-function analyses demonstrate that Mettl3 acts as a positive regulator of iRGC axon development.

Mettl3 promotes iRGC reprogramming

We next investigate the role of Mettl3 on iRGC fate reprogramming. We quantified reprogramming efficiency at day 13 by calculating the ratio of successfully reprogrammed TUJ1+ cells at day 13 to successfully transfected ASCL1+ cells at day 2 following a previously established protocol (Wang et al., 2020). Quantification showed that, in the control group, 13.32% ± 0.63% of the MEFs were converted into iRGCs, whereas this percentage decreased to 5.31% ± 1.50% and 7.70% ± 1.01% in the two Mettl3-shRNA groups, respectively, both significantly lower than the control groups (both p < 0.01; n = 4 experiments) (Figures 3A–3C). These results demonstrate that Mettl3 is required for iRGC fate reprogramming. We next sought to validate the functional impact of Mettl3 loss-of-function on iRGC reprogramming by quantifying the iRGC induction efficiency of Mettl3-KO MEFs. The results showed that Mettl3 knockout drastically reduced the iRGC induction efficiency to as low as 1.127%, in stark contrast to the 13.71% efficiency observed for the control group (Figures S2E and S2F). These data further confirm that Mettl3 is indispensable for iRGC reprogramming. We then tested whether Mettl3 overexpression promotes iRGC fate reprogramming. Quantifying the iRGC induction efficiency at day 13 showed that 22.43% ± 1.06% of the MEFs were converted into iRGCs in the Mettl3-OE group, significantly higher than the control group (p = 0.02; n = 3 experiments) (Figures 3D–3F). Taken together, these results demonstrate that Mettl3 promotes iRGC fate reprogramming.

In the iRGC reprogramming system, MEF-to-iRGC fate induction occurs quickly and early (Wang et al., 2020). The altered iRGC reprogramming efficiency observed at day 13 might be attributed to changes in iRGC survival, given Mettl3’s role in iRGC axon development (Figure 2), and the critical importance of neurite integrity for neuronal survival. We thus examined the iRGC induction efficiency at day 5. The results showed that compared with the control groups, the Mettl3-shRNA groups at day 5 exhibited a trend of reduced induction efficiency (Figure 3G), while the Mettl3-OE group at day 5 exhibited a trend of increased induction efficiency (Figure 3H), though neither reached statistical significance. We next directly assessed cellular survival using TUNEL assays on control and Mettl3-shRNA iRGCs at days 5 and 9. Under normal conditions, only a small fraction of reprogramming iRGCs underwent cell death during the assay period, whereas Mettl3 knockdown significantly increased the proportion of dying iRGCs at both time points (Figure 3I). These results indicate that the altered reprogramming efficiency in Mettl3-shRNA/OE groups at day 13 is, at least partially, attributed to changes in the survival capacity of reprogramming cells.

Mettl3 regulates the transcriptomes of reprogramming cells

To investigate how Mettl3 regulates the transcriptome of the reprogramming cells, we performed RNA-seq on day 5 iRGCs. We first compared the transcriptomes of Mettl3-knockdown (Mettl3-KD, shRNA #1) reprogramming cells with those of control reprogramming cells. The sample distance correlation analysis showed that the Mettl3-KD groups were closely clustered together and were well separated from the control groups (Figure 4A), suggesting that Mettl3 knockdown significantly altered the transcriptome of reprogramming cells. Differential gene expression (DEG) analyses revealed that at the cutoff thresholds of an adjusted p value < 0.05 and a fold change >2, 118 genes were upregulated and 205 genes were downregulated in Mettl3-KD cells (Figure 4B). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) showed that the top three most significantly affected cellular processes were all related to neural synaptic communication, and all exhibited downregulation (Figure 4C), consistent with the significantly disturbed axon development phenotype observed for Mettl3-KD reprogramming cells at day 5.

Next, we compared the transcriptomes of reprogramming cells overexpressing Mettl3 with those of control reprogramming cells. Sample distance correlation analyses showed that the Mettl3-OE groups clustered together and were well separated from control groups (Figure 4D), suggesting that Mettl3 overexpression significantly altered the transcriptome of reprogramming cells. DEG analyses revealed that at the cutoff thresholds of an adjusted p value <0.05 and a fold change >2, 37 genes were upregulated and 197 genes were downregulated in Mettl3-OE cells (Figure 4E). GSEA showed that the top three most significantly affected cellular processes were related to mitosis (upregulated) and muscle cell function (downregulated) (Figure 4F), indicating that cell proliferation was promoted and muscle cell fate was repressed by Mettl3 overexpression. These biological pathway changes are consistent with the increased iRGC induction efficiency in Mettl3-OE cells, as these two biological terms have been implicated in regulating iRGC/iN fate induction (see “discussion”).

Taken together, the results of the RNA-Seq transcriptome analyses demonstrate that Mettl3 regulates the transcriptome of reprogramming cells.

Mettl3 regulates iRGC axon development through m6A-modified downstream target genes

To identify direct downstream molecules that mediate the function of Mettl3 during iRGC reprogramming, we performed integrative analyses of RNA-seq and MeRIP-seq data. Given that the primary and most prominent effect of m6A modification is to promote mRNA degradation (Zaccara et al., 2019), we established the following criteria for downstream target selection: (1) upregulated in Mettl3-shRNA iRGCs; (2) downregulated in Mettl3-OE iRGCs; (3) harboring m6A modification at the time of RNA-seq (day 5). Intersection of the two RNA-seq DEG gene sets identified 28 genes that were significantly upregulated upon Mettl3 knockdown (adjusted p value < 0.01 and log2(fold change) > 0.75) and significantly downregulated upon Mettl3 overexpression (adjusted p value < 0.01 and log2(fold change) < −0.75). Among these, 3 genes, Prokr1, Rspo1, and Fmo2, also harbor m6A modifications according to MeRIP-seq (Figure 5A), and thus were selected for further investigation.

qPCR validation confirmed that Prokr1, Rspo1, and Fmo2 were upregulated upon Mettl3 knockdown and downregulated upon Mettl3 overexpression in reprogramming cells (Figure S3A), consistent with RNA-seq results. MeRIP-qPCR further confirmed that all three genes carry m6A modification (Figure 5B). Furthermore, MeRIP-qPCR demonstrated that the m6A modification levels on these three genes were significantly reduced upon Mettl3 knockdown in reprogramming cells (Figure 5B). To determine how Mettl3 regulates the metabolism of the three genes in reprogramming cells, we performed an RNA stability assay by measuring the half-lives of mRNA transcripts after transcription was inhibited by actinomycin D treatment. The results revealed that in Mettl3-KD reprogramming cells, the half-lives of the transcripts of Prokr1, Rspo1, and Fmo2 were significantly extended (Figure 5C). Collectively, these experiments demonstrated that Mettl3 catalyzes m6A modification on Prokr1, Rspo1, and Fmo2, and thereby promotes their degradation.

We next investigated whether these three genes mediate Mettl3’s regulatory effects on iRGC axon development. For this purpose, we first constructed lentiviral vectors overexpressing each gene (Figure S3B) and examined the effects of overexpressing these genes on iRGC axon development. Measurement and quantification of the iRGC axon length at day 5 revealed that iRGCs overexpressing Prokr1, Rspo1, or Fmo2 exhibited significantly shorter axons than control cells (Figure 5D), recapitulating the phenotype observed for Mettl3-KD iRGCs. These results suggest that these three genes may contribute to the axon developmental defects observed in Mettl3-KD iRGCs. We then tested whether downregulation of these genes could rescue the axon development defects caused by Mettl3 deficiency. For this purpose, we constructed lentiviruses expressing shRNAs targeting each gene and validated their silencing efficiency by qPCR (Figure S3C). We coinfected MEFs with ABI-OE lentiviruses and lentiviruses expressing shRNAs targeting each gene. Measurements and quantification of axon length at day 5 revealed that compared with control cells, iRGCs deficient in Prokr1, Rspo1, or Fmo2 exhibited significantly longer axons (Figure 5E). Importantly, when Mettl3-KD reprogramming cells were coinfected with Prokr1/Rspo1/Fmo2-shRNA expressing lentiviruses, the shortened axon length defects of Mettl3-KD iRGCs were partially rescued (Figures 6F and 6G).

METTL3 regulates human iRGC reprogramming and axonogenesis

Finally, we investigated whether METTL3 plays conserved roles in human iRGC (hiRGC) reprogramming and axon development. We previously established a protocol to reprogram human fibroblasts into hiRGCs by overexpressing ABI combined with Sox4 (ABI_S-OE) (Wang et al., 2020). To explore the role of METTL3 in human iRGC reprogramming, we first designed a shRNA targeting human METTL3 and constructed lentiviral vectors expressing the shRNA (which also harbored a GFP reporter). We cotransfected human neonatal fibroblasts (HNFs) with ABI_S-OE lentiviruses and hMETTL3-shRNA lentiviruses. IF staining confirmed that hMETTL3-shRNA efficiently knocked down METTL3 expression in HNFs (Figure 6A). We then examined the axons of reprogramming hiRGC at day 9, a time point when reprogramming hiRGC were in the stage of active axon development. Measurement and quantification of hiRGC axon length showed that, in the control group, axon lengths of iRGCs at day 9 ranged from 35.66 to 84.25 μm (interquartile range), with an average of 69.82 ± 2.35 μm (n = 537 cells from 3 independent experiments). In contrast, in the hMETTL3-shRNA knockdown group, axon lengths ranged from 23.61 to 59.76 μm (interquartile range), with an average of 47.71 ± 2.45 μm (n = 240 cells from 3 independent experiments), significantly shorter than that of the control group (p < 0.001; Figures 6B and 6C), indicating that METTL3 knockdown impairs hiRGC axon development. We then tested the effect of METTL3 overexpression on hiRGC axon development. To this end, we constructed a lentiviral vector encoding Flag-tagged human METTL3 (hMETTL3-OE). IF staining confirmed the successful overexpression of hMETTL3 (Figure 6D). We then cotransfected HNFs with ABI_S-OE and hMETTL3-OE lentiviruses. Measurements and quantification of the axon length at day 9 showed that in hMETTL3-OE group, axon lengths ranged from 46.79 to 113.4 μm (interquartile range), with an average of 97.91 ± 5.41 μm (n = 365 cells, from 3 independent experiments), significantly longer than that of the control group (ranging from 35.37 to 83.83 μm, with a mean of 69.92 ± 2.62 μm; n = 460 cells from 3 independent experiments; p < 0.001; Figures 6E and 6F), indicating that METTL3 overexpression promotes hiRGC axon development. We next assessed the role of METTL3 in hiRGC fate reprogramming by quantifying induction efficiency at day 13, following the same protocol used for mouse iRGCs. The results showed that the hiRGC induction efficiency in the hMETTL3-shRNA group was 0.6200% ± 0.1815%, significantly lower than the control group (5.31% ± 0.54%; 3 experiments; p < 0.01; Figures 6G and 6H). In contrast, the hiRGC induction efficiency in the hMETTL3-OE group was 8.41 ± 3.10% (3 experiments), comparable to the control group (8.26 ± 3.23%; 3 experiments, p = 0.976) (Figures 6I and 6J), indicating that METTL3 overexpression does not affect hiRGC fate reprogramming.

Collectively, these experiments demonstrate that METTL3 is both essential and sufficient for promoting hiRGC axon development, and is required for efficient hiRGC fate reprogramming.

Discussion

In recent years, the m6A modification, a major form of epitranscriptomic regulation, has been reported to play critical roles in regulating neural development, physiology, and pathology (Broix et al., 2025; Livneh et al., 2020; Shen et al., 2025; Xin et al., 2022; Yoon et al., 2017; Zhao et al., 2025). However, the role and underlying molecular mechanisms of the m6A epitranscriptome during neural fate reprogramming and axon development remain elusive. In this study, exploiting an efficient iRGC somatic cell direct reprogramming system, we investigated these questions by challenging the reprogramming cells with Mettl3 knockdown or overexpression. Our study demonstrates that Mettl3 promotes iRGC reprogramming and axonogenesis by facilitating the degradation of m6A-modified transcripts.

RNA-seq analyses showed that the cell cycle biological pathway was upregulated, while muscle cell-related biological pathways were downregulated in Mettl3-OE reprogramming cells. Previous studies have demonstrated that cell cycle status is an important factor determining efficient iRGC reprogramming (Wang et al., 2020) and that muscle cell fate needs to be repressed in iN reprogramming systems when Ascl1 is included in the reprogramming TF combination (Wapinski et al., 2013). These results suggest that Mettl3 promotes iRGC reprogramming by regulating the expression of cell cycle regulators and muscle cell fate regulators. On the other hand, when we examined the reprogramming efficiency at day 5, similar trends of induction efficiency change were observed; however, the changes were far less significant than those observed at day 13 (Figure 3). Given the important role of Mettl3 in iRGC axon development (Figure 2) and the significance of neurite integrity for neuronal survival (Almasieh et al., 2012; Goldberg and Barres, 2000), and the increased apoptosis in Mettl3-knockdown iRGCs, the altered iRGC reprogramming efficiency observed at day 13 might be partially attributed to the changes in the survival ability of reprogramming cells.

Integrated RNA-seq and MeRIP-seq analyses suggested that three m6A-modified genes, Prokr1, Rspo1, and Fmo2, were responsible for mediating the function of Mettl3 during iRGC axon development. MeRIP-qPCR and RNA stability assay experiments demonstrated that Mettl3 catalyzed m6A modification on these three genes, and thereby promoted their degradation. Furthermore, downregulation of these three genes rescued the detrimental effects of Mettl3-shRNA on iRGC axonogenesis, demonstrating that these three genes play key mediating roles in Mettl3-regulated iRGC axonogenesis. Prokr1 is a G protein-coupled receptor that binds to Prokineticins and activates MAPK and STAT signaling pathways. Studies have linked Prokr1 to angiogenesis, muscle development, and diabetes (Mok et al., 2021, 2024). Rspo1 belongs to the R-spondin family of secreted proteins that bind to the LGR family of G-protein-coupled receptors and mediate both canonical and noncanonical Wnt signaling (de Lau et al., 2012; Glinka et al., 2011). Studies have shown that Rspo1 plays important roles in regulating cell proliferation and sex determination (Kim et al., 2005; Parma et al., 2006). Fmo2 is among the most important noncytochrome enzymes involved in the metabolism of endogenous substances and xenobiotics containing nitrogen and sulfur (Krueger and Williams, 2005). Studies have linked Fmo2 to aging, neuroinflammation, and neurodegenerative diseases (Leiser et al., 2015; Li et al., 2021; Rossner et al., 2017). However, no studies thus far have linked Prokr1, Rspo1, and Fmo2 with neural axon development. In the future, characterizing the precise m6A modification sites on these three genes and testing the rescue ability of m6A-site mutant form of these three genes would provide more definitive mechanistic link between them and Mettl3-controlled iRGC axonogenesis, and it will also be interesting to investigate the signaling pathways downstream of these three genes that control axon development.

Beyond these three genes, other m6A-modified genes may also mediate Mettl3’s regulatory effects on iRGC reprogramming. Our MeRIP-seq examination of the reprogramming iRGCs at different stages showed that the m6A epitranscriptomes exhibited dynamic changes during the reprogramming process. What caused the dynamic changes in the m6A epitranscriptome during iRGC reprogramming? We propose two major contributing factors: (1) transcriptome dynamics: mRNAs must be present to be m6A-methylated; the transcriptome undergoes dramatic remodeling during iRGC reprogramming (Wang et al., 2020), which would profoundly affect the composition of the epitranscriptomes (in turn, m6A modification would also regulates transcriptome dynamics). (2) m6A methylation and demethylation activities, controlled by m6A-writer (Mettl3-complex) and erasers (Alkdh5 and Fto). Thus, the gain and loss of m6A-modified transcripts during iRGC reprogramming are likely the combined result of active m6A methylation/demethylation and passive transcriptome dynamics. Then how does the m6A-epitranscriptome dynamics relate to reprogramming versus maturation. Dynamically m6A-modified genes during iRGC reprogramming are enriched in diverse biological pathways, including RNA and protein metabolism and activity, axonogenesis, and nervous system development, all of which play important roles in neuron fate determination and maturation, and exhibit distinct expression patterns and activities between MEFs and iRGCs. Thus, the dynamic expression of m6A-modified genes in these pathways is likely the combined result of transcriptome remodeling and active m6A methylation/demethylation. The presence of m6A modification on these genes suggests that m6A may regulate iRGC reprogramming by modulating these processes through regulating the metabolism or function (degradation, translation, splicing, etc.) of modified transcripts. However, the precise mechanisms by which these dynamically m6A-modified genes and pathways contribute to iRGC reprogramming and maturation require further investigation using advanced and integrated multi-omics analyses (m6A epitranscriptome, transcriptome, proteosome, translation, etc.) and functional interrogation of m6A writer/eraser/readers and candidate downstream targets.

Taken together, we elucidated the essential roles and underlying molecular mechanisms of the m6A epitranscriptome during neuronal fate reprogramming and axon development. These findings may help future investigations to improve in vitro iN reprogramming protocols. More importantly, the findings of this study indicate that manipulating the m6A epitranscriptome might help to improve the outcomes of neuronal fate and axonal regeneration in vivo to treat neurodegenerative diseases, such as glaucoma and Parkinson’s disease, and are thus worthy of future investigations.

Methods

iRGC reprogramming

iRGC reprogramming was done following our previously published protocol (Wang et al., 2020) and detailed in supplemental information section.

Molecular cloning

Short hairpin RNAs (shRNAs) targeting the genes of interest were cloned into the pSicoR lentiviral vector (Addgene, Watertown, MA, USA). The coding sequences (CDS) of the target genes were amplified by PCR from a mouse brain cDNA pool and cloned into the family-modified pSicoR-TetON-3xflag vector using restriction enzyme digestion and ligation. All constructs were validated by Sanger sequencing, and all primers used are listed in Table S2.

Immunofluorescence staining

Cultured cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, Sigma) for 10 min at room temperature and then washed with PBS (PBST) containing 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma). Samples were blocked with 5% normal serum in PBST (Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA) for at least 30 min, incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibody, rinsed with PBST, and incubated with secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. After three PBST washes, nuclei were stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Sigma, 1 μg/mL) for 5 min at room temperature, followed by three more PBST washes. Stained cells were imaged using a Zeiss Axio Observer Z7 inverted microscope. Antibodies used included mouse anti-TUJ1 (G7121, Promega, Madison, WI, USA, 1:1000), rabbit anti-TUJ1 (802001; BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA, 1:1000), rabbit anti-METTL3 (ab195352, Abcam, 1:500), rabbit anti-ASCL1 (ab74065, Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA, 1:500), mouse anti-cMYC (13–2500, Thermo Fisher, 1:500), and Alexa 488, 568, or 647-conjugated secondary antibodies (Thermo Fisher, 1:1000).

Neurite length measurement

On day 5 (or day 9 for hiRGCs), reprogramming cells were immunostained with anti-TUJ1 antibodies for neurite length measurement. Stained cells were imaged using a Zeiss Axio Observer Z7 inverted microscope. For each sample, 5 random non-overlapping 10x objective fields were captured, and all TUJ1+-iRGCs within these fields were measured. Axon length was measured using ImageJ software, following predefined criteria: an axon was defined as the longest neurite of TUJ1+ cells, and its length was quantified as the linear distance from the geometric center of the cell soma to the distal tip of this neurite. To minimize batch variation, data were pooled from 3 to 4 independent biological experiments.

TUNEL assay

Cell samples were prepared as described for immunofluorescence staining. After TUJ1 immunofluorescence staining, the TUNEL assay was performed using the One-Step TUNEL Apoptosis Assay Kit (Cat. No. C1088, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Apoptosis efficiency was assessed by capturing five random 10× fields per sample and calculating the percentage of TUJ1+TUNEL+ double-positive cells among all TUJ1+ cells. Data were collected from three independent biological replicates.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

Target cells were harvested at 5 days post-induction of doxycycline and purified via FACS using a BD FACSAria Fusion system (BD Biosciences). For each experimental group, 106 sorted cells (n = 3 independent replicate experiments) underwent RNA library preparation with the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit, followed by 150-bp paired-end sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq platform. Raw sequencing reads were first processed through Trimmomatic for adapter trimming and quality filtering. Clean reads were then aligned to the mouse reference genome (mm10 assembly) using HISAT2, with gene-level quantification accomplished through FeatureCounts. Differential gene expression analysis employing DESeq2 identified statistically significant genes, followed by functional annotation using clusterProfiler for both GO enrichment and GSEA.

MeRIP-seq

Five micrograms of total RNA were extracted respectively from MEFs, day 5 iRGCs, or day 13 iRGCs (iRGCs samples are isolated via FACS) using Trizol (R0016, Beyotime) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. 10 ng was saved to record the input, and the remaining RNA was used for immunoprecipitation using an m6A antibody (mouse mAb anti-m6A, Synaptic Systems, 202011) as previously reported. Samples were then resuspended with 5 μL RNAase-free water. Single Cell Full-Length mRNA-Amplification Kit-Box 2 (N712, Vazyme) was used to amplify the RNA. Libraries were prepared with TruePrep DNA Library Prep Kit V2 for Illumina (TD503; Vazyme) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq platform on a 150 bp paired-end run. Raw data were filtered to remove low-quality reads to obtain clean reads. Clean reads were mapped to the mouse genome (mm10) using Bowtie2. MACS2 was used to call m6A peaks.

MeRIP-qPCR

MeRIP for qPCR was performed as described for MeRIP-seq. m6A pull-down and 10% reserved input RNAs were reverse transcribed into cDNAs using the SuperScript II First-strand Synthesis System for RT-PCR (Thermo Fisher). qPCR was performed using SYBR Green on a LightCycler 480 (Roche). Primer sequences of qPCR are listed in Table S2. Enrichment of m6A-tagged genes was calculated based on ΔCt values between pull-down and input samples, normalized to the internal negative control Rsp14, which lacks m6A modification.

RNA stability assay

MEFs were transfected with ABI-OE and GFP or Mettl3-shRNA #1 lentiviruses, followed by doxycycline (Dox) induction for reprogramming on the next day. At day 5, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 5 mM actinomycin D (Act D) to inhibit mRNA transcription. Total RNA was collected at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 h post-treatment by Act D using Trizol and extracted for qRT-PCR analysis. The mRNA half-life was calculated as follows: relative mRNA levels at each time point were normalized to time point 0, and the natural logarithm of normalized mRNA concentrations at 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, and 12 h were plotted against time to perform linear regression analysis. The slope of the regression line was defined as the decay rate (k), and the half-life was calculated using the formula: t1/2 = ln2/k.

Statistical analysis

All experiments, except for m6A-seq, were performed with at least three independent replicate experiments and subjected to statistical analysis. Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software. Quantitative results are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical significance was determined by independent Student’s t tests (two-tailed) or one-way ANOVA, with a p value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.
