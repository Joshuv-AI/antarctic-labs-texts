Association between multiple pregnancy with peripartum cardiomyopathy: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Triwedya Indra Dewi, Azizah Muthiah, Chaerul Achmad, Firman Fuad Wirakusumah, Ruswana Anwar, Annisa Dewi Nugrahani, Delvac Oceandi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhile the diagnostic criteria for peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM) have been better established than other forms of cardiomyopathies in pregnancy, its pathogenesis and association with multiple pregnancies are still poorly understood, and there has never been an established meta-analysis regarding this study. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the association between multiple pregnancies and the development of PPCM.MethodsWe conducted a comprehensive literature search using several databases, including PubMed, Scopus, and ScienceDirect, until May 31th, 2025. Studies were eligible if they investigated the association between multiple pregnancies and the probability of PPCM. Data were analyzed using a random effects model of pooled odds ratios (ORs) with the Mantel-Haenszel method.ResultsThis meta-analysis included one prospective and ten retrospective observational studies, with a total of 11,523,332 participants from eight different countries. Our study revealed that multiple pregnancies were significantly associated with an increased risk of developing PPCM (OR = 2.59 (95% CI = 2.17–3.10); P = < 0.00001; I2 = 36%, P-heterogeneity = 0.03).ConclusionMultiple pregnancies demonstrated a significant association with the risk of PPCM development; hence, screening in this high-risk group is required.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05229-y.Keywords: Peripartum cardiomyopathy, Multiple pregnancy, Multiple gestation

Introduction

Peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM) has been identified as a rare yet potentially life-threatening condition that poses a challenge for physicians due to its diverse manifestations, ranging from subtle signs and symptoms to acute heart failure, pulmonary edema, and cardiogenic shock [1]. PPCM is idiopathic cardiomyopathy occurring towards the end of pregnancy or within the five months following delivery [2] in a previously healthy woman in the absence of other identifiable causes of heart failure (HF) characterized by an acute or gradually progressing decrease in the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of less than 45% [1–3]. Regardless of variable occurrence and fatality rates amid studies, PPCM constitutes one of the primary causes of pregnancy-related morbidity and mortality across borders [4–6]. Several possible predisposing factors for PPCM encompassing age above 35 years old, multiparity, multiple pregnancies, gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, smoking, obesity, family history, and prolonged use of tocolytic beta-agonist, which differs by geographical region and ethnic background of the given study [7–23].

The etiology of PPCM remains uncertain. A combined well-established model including systemic angiogenic imbalance and host predisposing factors [24, 25]. Several recent investigations have found that PPCM is closely linked to endothelial dysfunction. Some experimental evidence also shows a strong association between genetic predisposition, an increase in serum levels of prolactin and prolactin cleavage products, low selenium levels, immunological response, stress-activated cytokines, and imbalanced oxidative stress during pregnancy, which activates multiple cardio-vasotoxic, inflammatory, and angiogenic-antiangiogenic imbalances that cause PPCM [26–34]. Recently, it has been proposed that PPCM is linked to increased production of placental soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (sFlt-1). This potent vascular endothelial growth factor inhibitor has been implicated in the pathogenesis of pre-eclampsia, suggesting an overlap between these conditions [35–38]. Despite advances in comprehending the pathophysiological mechanisms of this entity, the established correlation between other predisposing factors and the development of PPCM is still lacking, and considerable fundamental gaps in knowledge still prevail. Thus, our meta-analysis sought to evaluate the association between multiple pregnancies as one of the predisposing factors of PPCM and the risk of development of PPCM..

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and Reported based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) [39]. The protocol was registered at the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), under identification number CRD42024560616.

Search strategy

We conducted a systematic search of PubMed, Scopus, and ScienceDirect from inception to May 31th, 2025 with restriction to English only. The full search strategy is further described in Supplementary Tables 1 and the PECO structure described in Supplementary Table 2. The PRISMA criteria were implemented in our search and Fig. 1 outline the search and screening processes.

Flow chart of study selection

Study selection

In this meta-analysis, we considered studies on the association of multiple pregnancies and the development of PPCM. The detailed inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) prospective or retrospective observational studies (case-control or cohort) or randomized controlled trial; (2) women with livebirth delivery in a given period that includes a total of PPCM and not PPCM cases; (3) reported the gestation profile either single or multiple. Studies are excluded when they report only the incidence or prevalence of PPCM, PPCM criteria are not met; they evaluate other types of cardiomyopathies, and do not mention the gestation profile. Our meta-analysis excluded case reports/series, review papers, editorials, comments, meta-analyses, and conference abstracts. Independent evaluations of the pre-selected studies that meet the inclusion criteria were conducted by TID, AM, and ADN. Any differences of opinion were worked out through conversation with the other author (CA, FFA, RA, DO).

Data extraction and risk of bias assessment

We extracted the data from the eligible studies, including baseline characteristics of the included studies (first author’s name, the country in which the study was conducted, study design, study participants and control population characteristics, PPCM diagnostic criteria, exclusion criteria, total participants, maternal age, race, multiparity, multiple pregnancies, gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, and obesity).

The Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) was implemented by the authors to independently assess the possibility of bias in each study, further described in Supplementary Table 3. A study with a total score of seven or above was deemed bias-free. Research with a total score of six or less was considered to be biased and thus excluded from the research. All included studies scored between 8 and 10 points, indicating moderate to high methodological quality. The selection and outcome domains were consistently strong across studies, with minor variations in the comparability all domains. Therefore, the included studies were deemed appropriate for synthesis in the meta-analysis. Author discussion was utilized to settle quality rating disagreements [41].

Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis, Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4 was used. The effect estimates in this meta-analysis were expressed as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CI with statistical significance attained by a P-value < 0.05. The I2 index was used to examine inter-study heterogeneity and an I2 statistic of 30% to 60% disclosed as modest homogeneity [41]. And also given the likely clinical and methodological diversity across the included studies (e.g., population, diagnostic criteria, and geographic location). The odds ratios described in each study are pooled using the generic invariance method with a random effects model. The random-effects models were used to pool the overall effect magnitude, given that the inter-study heterogeneity was moderate.

Results

Study selection and baseline characteristics

The primary search yielded 2,040 articles; after manual exclusion of case reports/series and review papers and removing duplicates, 1,789 were screened. Following screening titles and abstracts, we identified full papers for further assessment, adhering to the established inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 11 observational studies were included in our analysis, with a total of 11,523,332 participants and 3,997 PPCM cases (0.00034%) from eight different countries [7–17]. Multiple pregnancy and peripartum cardiomyopathy

A total of one prospective and ten retrospective observational studies evaluated the association between multiple pregnancy and PPCM [7–17]. Our merged analysis suggested that population with multiple pregnancies had a significant increased risk of PPCM (OR = 2.59 (95% CI = 2.17–3.10); P = < 0.00001; I2 = 36%, P-heterogeneity = 0.03) (Fig. 2).

Forest plot showing an association between multiple pregnancy and PPCM

Publication bias

To address potential publication bias, we constructed a funnel plot based on the effect sizes and their standard errors. Funnel plot analysis revealed some asymmetry (Fig. 3), which may indicate potential publication bias. However, due to the small number of included studies and methodological variability, this observation should be interpreted with caution. Funnel plots are widely accepted as a qualitative method for assessing publication bias, particularly when the number of studies is limited, as in our case. Given the observational nature of the included studies and the moderate heterogeneity observed, we believe that visual inspection of the funnel plot provides a more context-appropriate and cautious approach to identifying potential bias, without overinterpreting results from underpowered statistical tests such as Egger’s.

Discussion

This is the first meta-analysis evaluating the association between multiple pregnancies and PPCM. Our studies showed that those with multiple pregnancies had a significantly higher risk of PPCM, 2.59 times greater than those with single pregnancy. The pathophysiology of PPCM is intricate and difficult to understand. It involves several pathophysiological pathways, including inflammation, genetic mutations , hormonal abnormalities, abnormal immune responses, and oxidative stress [32, 42]. The cleavage of the hormone prolactin into its smaller, physiologically active form, the N-terminal part of prolactin, 16 kDa prolactin (16 kDa-PRL or 16 K PRL), induced by oxidative stress, is the principal cause of PPCM. The vascular-hormonal theory suggests that 16 K PRL elevates the level of micro ribonucleic acid-146a (miR-146a), resulting in several detrimental consequences on the endothelium. The interplay of this and sFlt-1 diminishes many proangiogenic factors, resulting in heart failure throughout the postpartum and late pregnancy phases. This complex process entails the placenta’s production of sFlt-1, which injures vascular endothelial cells, disrupts the action of placental growth factor (PlGF), and reduces trophoblastic invasion into the decidua [35, 43, 44].

Clinical studies have shown that multiple pregnancies are considered a risk factor for PPCM. The prevailing theory concerning the pathophysiology of adverse cardiovascular outcomes proposes that excessive placentation or hyperplacentosis leads to abnormal secretion of angiogenic factors, notably sFlt-1, soluble endoglin (sEng), and placental growth factor (PlGF), culminating in endothelial dysfunction [45–48]. Patients with PPCM exhibited higher sFlt-1 levels [48, 49]. Following delivery, sFlt-1 levels decline swiftly. In the absence of pregnancy, exogenous sFlt-1 alone was adequate to provoke significant systolic dysfunction in an invivo research [50].

A prior study reveals that in multiple pregnancies, from late gestation to 5–6 months postpartum, there is an elevation in sFlt-1 levels and the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio compared to singleton pregnancies. Satorres et al. [50], reported that the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio was 26 in multiple pregnancies, as opposed to 3 in singleton pregnancies. The results aligned with those of Dröge et al. [51] revealing a median sFlt-1/PlGF ratio of 13.29 ± 319.64 in multiple pregnancies, contrasted with 4.94 ± 4.72 in the control group (P < 0.001). The reference ranges for the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio are similar for women with twins and singletons until 29 weeks of gestation, after which they seem elevated in multiple pregnancies [52, 53]. Moreover, Binder et al. [54] proposed that the sFlt-1/PlGF ratio exhibited superior performance in multiple pregnancies compared to PlGF levels alone. It has been established that decreases in radial, circumferential, and longitudinal strain occur before a loss of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and increased sFLT-1 levels are associated with left ventricular concentric remodeling [49, 50]. Previous research also revealed that LVEF tends to decrease towards the end of pregnancy in twin patients, particularly those with preeclampsia [45].

Another proposed idea suggests that the 16 K PRL-miR-146a circuit is associated with the pathophysiology of PPCM. The miR-146a caused endothelial damage and reduced the metabolic activity of cardiomyocytes. The 16 K PRL–miR-146a circuit appears to play a crucial role in the progression of PPCM, as the inhibition of miR-146a in conditional knockout (cKO) mice using locked nucleic acid–modified antisense oligonucleotides or antago-miRs significantly halted the manifestation of the PPCM phenotype [55]. Nonetheless, this proposed theory remains dependent on an in vivo methodology for singleton pregnancies and necessitates additional confirmation for multiple pregnancies. Dr. Ricke-Hoch et al [56] conducted a study demonstrating that plasma levels of miR-146a in PPCM patients were 3.6 times more than those in healthy postpartum controls, with a positive association shown between these levels and the concentration of circulating plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 (PAI-1). The research indicated that the co-precipitation of 16 K PRL with PAI-1 correlated with increased uPA receptor (uPAR)-mediated nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-kB) activation in endothelial cells, subsequently leading to endothelial dysfunction. Circulating PAI-1 levels, which correlate with plasma miR-146a levels alongside uPAR-NF-kB, may serve as valuable diagnostic indicators for PPCM [56, 57]. Notwithstanding numerous limitations, the research conducted by Dr. Ricke-Hoch et [56]. significantly enhances our comprehension of the pathomechanism of PPCM. Additional research is necessary to prospectively assess these putative novel PPCM biomarkers.

Clinical implications

The discovery of clinical stratification for PPCM in pregnancy holds a crucial role. This meta-analysis study offers insight into the increased risk of peripartum cardiomyopathy in pregnant women having multiple pregnancies. This study’s findings will enhance understanding of current recommendations for high-risk pregnancies, offering guidance on early detection through the assessment of PPCM prognostic significance from late pregnancy to postpartum management.

Strength

This study highlights the potential association between multiple gestations and an increased risk of peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM), which is an important finding with several clinical implications. Recognizing this link enhances risk stratification by identifying women with multiple pregnancies as a higher-risk group, thereby enabling more targeted prenatal monitoring and early detection of cardiac complications. This knowledge can improve prenatal counseling by informing expectant mothers about specific risks related to their pregnancy type, promoting informed decision-making and preparation. Furthermore, understanding this association supports tailored postpartum management strategies, including closer cardiac follow-up and timely interventions, which may ultimately improve maternal outcomes. Overall, these insights contribute to advancing personalized obstetric care and highlight the need for heightened vigilance in multiple gestation pregnancies.

Limitations

Despite promising results, this study has several limitations that should be considered. Firstly, the majority of included studies were retrospective observational analyses, which are inherently subject to selection bias and rely on existing medical records that may lack comprehensive or standardized data. This design limits our ability to establish causal relationships and may affect the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Therefore, our conclusions should be interpreted with caution, and further prospective, high-quality studies are needed to confirm these findings. Secondly, although we identified an association between multiple gestations and peripartum cardiomyopathy (PPCM), the included studies did not fully account for various potential confounding factors that could influence outcomes. These additional contributors may include genetic predisposition, socioeconomic status, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, and environmental influences, all of which warrant further investigation. Lastly, our analysis did not differentiate between multiple pregnancies and higher-order gestations due to insufficient detailed data in the original studies. Subgroup analyses based on the number of fetuses would provide valuable insight into the gradient of risk but were not possible here due to limitations of data from available studies. Future studies should aim to stratify risks according to the exact number of gestations to better inform clinical management.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study indicates that multiple pregnancies were associated with a higher likelihood of PPCM. This presents opportunities for future studies to specifically tackle this issue; moreover, additional multicenter collaborative efforts are necessary to enhance these findings.
