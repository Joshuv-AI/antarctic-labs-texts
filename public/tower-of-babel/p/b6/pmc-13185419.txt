Does laser delivery mode matter? Corneal sensitivity and ocular surface changes after single-spot and multispot PRP in proliferative diabetic retinopathy
Authors: Yusuf Cem Yilmaz, Serife Ciloglu Hayat, Merve Kelebek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPanretinal photocoagulation (PRP) is the standard treatment for proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR), but it may alter corneal sensitivity and tear film stability through thermal and neuroinflammatory mechanisms. The recent introduction of pattern-scanning multispot systems allows shorter pulse durations and lower cumulative energy delivery. This study aimed to evaluate pre- and post-treatment changes in corneal sensitivity and ocular surface parameters following single-spot and multispot PRP in patients with PDR.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included 90 eyes from 45 patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus who underwent PRP using either single-spot (n = 44) or multispot (n = 46) techniques. Corneal sensitivity (Cochet–Bonnet esthesiometer), tear breakup time (TBUT), Schirmer I test, and corneal fluorescein staining (Oxford scale) were measured at baseline, and at 1 and 3 months after PRP. Repeated measures were analyzed using generalized estimating equations to account for inter-eye correlation, and multivariate analysis was performed to adjust for potential confounding factors.ResultsIn the single-spot PRP group, TBUT, Schirmer, and esthesiometry values significantly decreased at both 1 and 3 months compared with baseline (p < 0.05), while Oxford grading scores increased significantly (p < 0.05). In the multispot PRP group, TBUT values decreased at 1 month but showed no significant difference from baseline at 3 months, and esthesiometry values increased significantly at 3 months (p < 0.05). Total delivered energy was lower in the multispot group despite a higher number of laser burns (p < 0.001). Diabetes duration was independently associated with Schirmer test values at month 1 and Oxford grading scores at month 3 (p < 0.05).ConclusionsBoth PRP techniques effectively treated PDR, but their effects on ocular surface parameters differed. Single-spot PRP resulted in a sustained reduction in corneal sensitivity and tear film stability, whereas changes after multispot PRP were transient and less pronounced. The multispot technique, characterized by shorter pulses and reduced cumulative energy, may therefore minimize anterior segment alterations associated with PRP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-026-04806-9.

Introduction

Diabetic retinopathy (DR) is a leading cause of vision loss worldwide, with proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR) representing its most advanced and vision-threatening stage. Panretinal photocoagulation (PRP) remains the gold standard treatment for PDR, aiming to induce regression of neovascularization and reduce the risk of severe vision-threatening complications [1]. Although PRP is highly effective in halting disease progression, it may disrupt ocular surface homeostasis by altering blink reflexes or reducing tear film stability, thereby indirectly affecting corneal nerve integrity [2, 3].

Corneal sensitivity, which reflects the functional integrity of the trigeminal sensory pathways, is often diminished in diabetic patients due to peripheral neuropathy [4]. Several studies have suggested that PRP may further reduce corneal sensitivity through a combination of inflammatory, neural, and thermal mechanisms. Although the retina and cornea are innervated by distinct branches of the trigeminal nerve, intense retinal stimulation can trigger central neural modulation that suppresses corneal sensory input via reflex pathways [5]. The long posterior ciliary nerves, which contribute to corneal innervation, course near the 3 and 9 o’clock meridians; therefore, inadvertent or excessive laser application near these regions may theoretically cause direct neural injury [6]. In addition, thermal diffusion of laser energy may lead to collateral tissue stress.

Previous studies have also demonstrated elevated levels of inflammatory mediators in the tears and conjunctival samples of PDR patients following PRP [7]. Retinal laser photocoagulation has been shown to increase anterior segment vascular permeability and induce the release of inflammatory cytokines that may diffuse into the aqueous humor, resulting in subclinical anterior segment inflammation [8]. Furthermore, procedural factors such as prolonged eyelid opening, contact lens pressure, and exposure to intense illumination may further compromise tear film stability.

Recent advances in laser delivery technology have led to the widespread adoption of both single-spot and multi-spot techniques. The conventional approach delivers one burn at a time, whereas modern pattern-scanning laser systems use short pulse durations (10–30 ms) to deliver multiple burns in a single activation [9]. This innovation reduces total procedure time, enhances patient comfort, and minimizes collateral damage to non-retinal tissues. Indeed, previous studies have reported that multispot PRP is associated with less pain and tissue injury compared with conventional single-spot delivery [10, 11].

However, the thermal and biological effects of these two delivery modes on corneal nerve integrity have not yet been directly compared. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has evaluated the differential impact of single-spot and multi-spot PRP on corneal sensitivity. Therefore, the present study aimed to assess and compare pre- and post-treatment corneal sensitivity and ocular surface parameters in patients undergoing either single-spot or multi-spot PRP for PDR, in order to determine whether the mode of laser delivery influences anterior segment neurophysiology.

Methods

This retrospective observational study was conducted at the Department of Ophthalmology, a tertiary care center in Turkey, between January 2025 and August 2025. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB No: 2025 − 309) and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) who presented for routine ophthalmologic examination and had a prior diagnosis of PDR were consecutively enrolled. Glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels were recorded for all participants. Exclusion criteria included any pre-existing corneal pathology, severe dry eye, use of topical medications within three months prior to PRP, history of ocular surgery, contact lens wear, pregnancy, high refractive error (defined as > 3.00 D of myopia, > 3.00 D of hyperopia, or > 1.50 D of astigmatism), previous PRP, tractional retinal detachment, vitreous hemorrhage, and macular edema.

All participants underwent a comprehensive ophthalmologic examination, including corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA) using a Snellen chart, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, intraocular pressure (IOP) measurement, and dilated fundus examination. Imaging included optical coherence tomography (OCT; DRI OCT Triton, Topcon, Japan), fundus photography, and fluorescein angiography (FA; Xephilio OCT-S1, Canon, Tokyo, Japan).

Tear film parameters were evaluated using tear breakup time (TBUT) and corneal fluorescein staining graded according to the Oxford scale. Corneal sensitivity was assessed with the Cochet–Bonnet esthesiometer (12/100 mm; Luneau Ophthalmologie, Prunay Le Gilon, France) prior to any topical instillation to avoid pharmacologic interference. Subsequently, the Schirmer I test (without anesthesia) was performed to assess tear production. Applanation tonometry was conducted using the Goldmann applanation tonometer (KAT; Keeler, UK) mounted on a slit lamp with a cobalt blue filter.

All PRP treatments were performed using a pattern-scanning laser system (EasyRet; Quantel Medical, Cournon-d’Auvergne, France) with a 577-nm yellow laser source capable of both single-spot and multispot delivery modes. Laser parameters, including spot size, duration, and power, were titrated to achieve moderate gray retinal burns, with adjustments made according to retinal pigmentation and disease severity. PRP was completed over three consecutive sessions at one-week intervals. In all cases, a standardized wide-field contact laser lens was used with the same coupling material to ensure procedural consistency and minimize potential variability in ocular surface measurements.

A total of 90 eyes were included in the study: 44 eyes received single-spot PRP and 46 eyes underwent multispot PRP. Both groups were comparable in terms of age and sex distribution. Corneal sensitivity and tear film parameters were recorded at baseline (prior to PRP) and reassessed at 1 and 3 months post-treatment (i.e., 4 months after baseline).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range, IQR) values, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the independent samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate.

Pre- and post-treatment comparisons of CDVA and intraocular pressure (IOP) were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test when applicable. For repeated measurements of corneal sensitivity, TBUT, Schirmer test values, and corneal staining scores (Oxford grading), generalized estimating equations (GEE) were applied to account for inter-eye correlation within the same participant. Additionally, multivariate GEE models were constructed to evaluate the independent effects of age, duration of diabetes, HbA1c level, total number of laser shots, and total delivered energy on postoperative ocular surface parameters.

Relationships between categorical variables were analyzed using the Pearson chi-square test, while correlations between continuous variables were assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Data visualization was performed using line graphs.

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A two-tailed p value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 90 eyes from 45 patients were included in the study. Of these, 44 eyes of 22 patients (10 males, 12 females; mean age, 58.0 ± 11.6 years) underwent single-spot PRP, and 46 eyes of 23 patients (14 males, 9 females; mean age, 59.7 ± 12.6 years) underwent multispot PRP.

Table 1 summarizes the baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of both groups. The two groups were comparable in terms of age and sex distribution (p > 0.05). The mean total number of laser shots was significantly higher in the multispot group than in the single-spot group, whereas the mean total delivered energy was significantly lower (p < 0.05). No statistically significant differences were observed between the groups with respect to lens status, HbA1c level, duration of diabetes, preoperative CDVA (LogMAR), preoperative IOP, postoperative CDVA (LogMAR), or postoperative IOP (p > 0.05).

When comparing between treatment modalities, patients who underwent multispot PRP demonstrated significantly higher mean values for TBUT at month 3, Schirmer test at month 1, and esthesiometry at both month 1 and month 3, compared with those who underwent single-spot PRP (p < 0.05). Conversely, baseline Schirmer values and month 3 Oxford grading scores were significantly lower in the multispot group (p < 0.05).

A GEE model was applied to account for the correlation between fellow eyes. When all patients were analyzed together, postoperative TBUT, Schirmer test, and esthesiometry values showed a statistically significant decrease compared to baseline measurements (p < 0.05). The subgroup analyses for single-spot and multispot PRP eyes are shown in Tables 3.1 and 3.2, respectively.

In the single-spot PRP group, similar to the overall cohort, TBUT, Schirmer, and esthesiometry values significantly decreased postoperatively compared to baseline (p < 0.05). Conversely, in the multispot PRP group, the mean TBUT decreased at postoperative month 1, whereas the mean esthesiometry value increased significantly at month 3 compared to baseline (p < 0.05) (Table 3).

Table 4 summarizes the correlations between TBUT, Schirmer, Oxford grading score, and esthesiometry parameters with clinical variables including age, duration of diabetes, HbA1c level, total energy. A moderate positive correlation was observed between diabetes duration and Oxford grading score at postoperative month 3 (p < 0.05). Total delivered energy was weakly negatively correlated with TBUT at postoperative month 3, weakly positively correlated with Oxford grading scores at postoperative months 1 and 3, and moderately negatively correlated with esthesiometry at postoperative months 1 and 3 (p < 0.05).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the effects of single-spot and multi-spot PRP on corneal sensitivity and ocular surface parameters in patients with PDR. Our results suggest that multi-spot PRP is associated with fewer adverse effects on the ocular surface compared to the single-spot technique at three-month follow-up. These findings highlight the potential protective role of newer laser delivery technologies on anterior segment structures.

Traditional single-spot PRP is typically performed over multiple sessions, during which approximately 500–1000 burns are applied per session, with a total of 1500–2000 burns as recommended in the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study (ETDRS) protocol [1]. Although the ETDRS regimen remains the historical reference for PRP, modern pattern-scanning laser systems have adopted shorter pulse durations (10–30 ms) and smaller spot sizes to achieve comparable efficacy with less collateral retinal and choroidal damage [9]. Although shorter pulses require higher power, each burn delivers less total energy due to the shorter exposure time. As a result, patterned lasers induce less lateral and axial heat diffusion, thereby minimizing collateral retinal and choroidal damage.

In a comparative study, Takamura et al. demonstrated that short-pulse (20 ms) multispot PRP induced less inflammation and macular thickening than conventional long-duration PRP [12]. In that study of high-risk NPDR patients, aqueous flare levels were significantly lower in the short-pulse group, and the increase in central macular thickness was markedly smaller, indicating that short, high-power pulses produce less secondary inflammation in non-retinal tissues. Furthermore, because multispot systems substantially shorten treatment duration, the contact lens remains on the cornea for a shorter period, potentially reducing surface desiccation. Faster completion of treatment in fewer sessions may also improve patient compliance and decrease treatment-related ocular surface stress [10].

However, because short-pulse burns are smaller and shallower, a higher number of spots may be required to achieve an equivalent therapeutic effect. One study reported that approximately 1,500 burns delivered with a multispot laser were insufficient, and more than 2,000 burns were necessary to obtain comparable efficacy. In high-risk PDR cases, up to 5,000–7,000 burns may be required [9]. Thus, while patterned lasers reduce per-burn energy and minimize collateral damage, the total number of applications must be increased to ensure complete therapeutic coverage [13]. Nevertheless, in our study, despite the higher total number of burns applied in the multi-spot group, the overall energy delivered was still lower than in the single-spot group. Consequently, the three-month postoperative parameters demonstrated less adverse alteration in the multi-spot group compared with the single-spot group, suggesting a relative preservation of anterior segment structures due to reduced cumulative energy exposure.

It is well known that diabetes mellitus causes ocular surface alterations, including decreased corneal sensitivity, reduced tear secretion, tear film dysfunction, progressive epithelial loss, and degeneration of corneal nerve fibers, irrespective of PRP [14]. Similar to our findings, Ruben et al. reported corneal hypoesthesia in both insulin-dependent and non–insulin-dependent diabetic patients following single-spot PRP [15]. Conversely, Misra et al. compared 19 diabetic eyes treated with multi-spot PRP to 19 untreated diabetic eyes and found no significant differences in subbasal nerve density or corneal sensitivity between groups, concluding that multi-spot PRP does not cause long-term nerve loss [16]. We observed a transient decrease in TBUT at one month, whereas they did not. A possible explanation could be the difference in the severity of thermal injury to the underlying nerves, depending on the total number and energy of laser burns applied in each study. Unfortunately, these data were not available for direct comparison.

Several investigations have also focused on the short-term effects of PRP and have reported significant but transient ocular surface changes [7, 17]. Jamali et al. also evaluated ocular surface parameters in 50 patients with type 2 diabetes two months after multi-spot PRP. In contrast to our findings, they observed significant reductions in esthesiometry values and tear film stability, suggesting that multi-spot retinal laser therapy may adversely affect corneal innervation and ocular surface function [2]. This discrepancy may be related to differences in total delivered energy, baseline anterior segment parameters, and esthesiometry values, which were notably lower in their cohort. Moreover, we could not identify any HbA1c data in their report, despite this parameter being a crucial indicator of glycemic control in patients with chronic diabetes. Previous research has demonstrated that corneal nerve fiber damage can occur even in the early stages of diabetes, and the extent of this damage correlates with the severity of diabetic retinopathy [3]. Additionally, higher grades of dry eye disease are more frequently observed in patients with poor glycemic control. Dutta et al. reported a positive correlation between the prevalence of dry eye and poor glycemic control in diabetic patients [18].

Furthermore, laser-related corneal nerve damage has also been documented outside diabetic contexts. In a prospective cohort of patients undergoing pars plana vitrectomy with circumferential (360°) endolaser retinopexy, Bouheraoua et al. observed a marked reduction in subbasal corneal nerve density, accompanied by decreased esthesiometry scores and epithelial thinning several months after treatment [19]. As 360° retinopexy is typically more confluent and destructive than multi-spot PRP, it may induce greater nerve injury. This finding aligns with our observation that the single-spot laser group exhibited not transient but persistent changes compared with baseline at three months, likely reflecting the higher cumulative energy and secondary inflammatory response associated with single-spot delivery compared with multispot PRP. In addition to laser-related thermal effects, pars plana vitrectomy itself may contribute to postoperative ocular surface dysfunction through several mechanisms, including conjunctival manipulation, prolonged surgical exposure, continuous intraoperative irrigation, and the use of antiseptic agents such as povidone–iodine, all of which may induce epithelial stress or disrupt tear film homeostasis. These factors have been associated with postoperative reductions in corneal sensitivity and increased dry eye symptoms in vitrectomized eyes.

Another strength of our study is the inclusion of systemic parameters, such as diabetes duration and HbA1c levels, enabling a more comprehensive interpretation of corneal responses relative to metabolic control. Poor metabolic regulation has been associated with increased ocular surface instability and corneal nerve dysfunction in large diabetic cohorts [4, 18]. Although we found no correlation between HbA1c and ocular surface parameters, diabetes duration was significantly associated with ocular surface parameters in both the correlation and multivariate analyses, particularly with Schirmer test values at postoperative month 1 and Oxford grading scores at month 3. This suggests that the duration of type 2 diabetes is an important risk factor for developing ocular surface disorders, similar to other diabetes-related complications [3, 20]. We hypothesize that HbA1c reflects only the average glycemic status over the preceding three months, whereas blood glucose levels may fluctuate over time, and measurements taken at different intervals can yield variable results.

Additionally, the potential contribution of contact lens–related mechanical effects to ocular surface alterations has been investigated in previous studies comparing contact and non-contact laser delivery systems. In a randomized study of patients with PDR undergoing PRP, ocular surface parameters including Schirmer test values, TBUT, and Oxford grading scores were evaluated following laser treatment delivered either via slit-lamp–based contact lens systems or non-contact binocular indirect ophthalmoscopy [21]. Although contact lens–assisted laser delivery was associated with a significant reduction in TBUT and increased Oxford grading scores at three months, the overall changes in tear film parameters between the contact and non-contact groups were largely comparable at follow-up. These findings suggest that ocular surface alterations observed after PRP may not be solely attributable to mechanical contact lens application. In the present study, although we were unable to directly compare contact lens–related mechanical effects due to the lack of recorded procedure duration, the presence of differential changes between the single-spot and multispot groups at three months further supports the notion that longer-term postoperative alterations are unlikely to be explained by contact lens–related effects alone.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, its retrospective design and relatively limited sample size may restrict the generalizability of the findings. Second, the duration of PRP application was not routinely recorded due to the retrospective nature of the study, and therefore could not be analyzed. Differences in procedure time between single-spot and multispot techniques may influence ocular surface parameters, particularly considering the direct and prolonged contact of the laser lens with the corneal surface during treatment. Although prolonged contact lens application may have contributed to early postoperative changes—particularly at the one-month follow-up—the persistence of certain alterations at three months suggests that factors beyond mechanical contact alone may also be involved. Third, although all procedures were performed using the same type of wide-field contact lens and coupling material in a standardized outpatient setting, the potential mechanical impact of contact lens application itself on tear film stability and corneal sensitivity cannot be fully excluded. Additionally, a control condition involving prolonged contact lens placement without laser application was not evaluated, which may have helped to differentiate between procedure-related and laser-induced effects. Finally, corneal sensitivity was assessed using the Cochet–Bonnet esthesiometer, which primarily evaluates mechanical sensitivity and may not fully capture the functional complexity of corneal nerve activity. The study also did not include corneal confocal microscopy, which could have provided detailed structural information on subbasal nerve plexus alterations.

In conclusion, both single-spot and multispot panretinal photocoagulation effectively treated proliferative diabetic retinopathy; however, the multispot technique demonstrated a more favorable profile regarding corneal sensitivity and ocular surface preservation. Despite requiring a higher number of laser applications, multispot PRP delivered lower cumulative energy, resulting in reduced collateral effects on anterior segment structures. These findings suggest that the use of short-pulse, pattern-scanning laser systems may minimize PRP-related ocular surface alterations and provide a safer therapeutic option for patients with PDR. Prospective, large-scale studies with longer follow-up and adjunct imaging of corneal nerves are warranted to confirm these results and further elucidate the mechanisms underlying these differences.
