Exploring novel indenoquinoxaline Schiff bases as corrosion inhibitors for conversion coating on mild steel: ultrasonication-assisted synthesis and electrochemical and computational studies
Authors: Hamdy Khamees Thabet, Moustafa S. Abusaif, Ghalia A. Gaber, Ahmed Ragab, Yousry A. Ammar, Amira M. Hyba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Three new organic inhibitors, IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC, based on indenoquinoxaline Schiff bases, were synthesized via a rapid, highly efficient, and ultrasonication-assisted protocol, with excellent yields of 99%. The electrochemical behavior and weight-loss measurements of bare mild steel and coated mild steel with organic inhibitors were evaluated in both 0.5 M H2SO4 and 3.5% NaCl solutions. The electrochemical results indicated that the Icorr of the mild steel coated with the novel organic inhibitors decreased by more than half compared with that of the uncoated mild steel. Demonstrating their efficacy as mixed-type inhibitors, 200 mg L−1 of the IQ-SSC, IQ-HSC, and IQ-BSC inhibitors achieved inhibition efficiencies of 89.02%, 92.08%, and 92.33%, respectively. The computational studies exhibited an acceptable fit of the calculated data with the experimental data and concluded that the efficiency of the coated MS was IQ-BSC > IQ-HSC > IQ-SSC. Through a combination of surface and solution analyses with theoretical modeling, we demonstrate that IQ-BSC achieves its excellent inhibition performance by forming a protective organic layer that acts as a barrier against corrosive species on the steel surface. The high nitrogen content and benzene rings within the inhibitor molecules act as the primary active sites, facilitating strong physicochemical adsorption that ensures durable protection of the mild steel surface. Corroborated by potentiodynamic polarization data and highly negative standard Gibbs free energy of the adsorption values () ranging from −34.59 to −35.68 kJ mol−1, the adsorption process was confirmed to be spontaneous and of a mixed physicochemical nature.

Introduction

Mild steel (MS) is an economical engineering material used for diverse applications in many settings, including mildly acidic situations, with certain safeguards.1 However, metallic material failures brought on by corrosion pose a serious problem for many different businesses, resulting in significant financial losses, environmental damage, and even possible safety risks.2 Organic coatings enhance the look and corrosion resistance of metallic substrates. Implementing this coating involves pretreatment to alter the metal surface, enhancing the adhesion of the organic coating and augmenting corrosion protection.3 The coating operates as a protective barrier for the substrate material in industrial applications. Also, the utilization of conversion films on metallic substrates is widely recognized for enhancing the corrosion resistance of metal surfaces and improving the adhesion performance of the subsequently applied organic coatings.4,5 This technology is poised to supplant conventional use of phosphate in the automotive industry, offering advantages such as reduced costs, improved process control, and enhanced wastewater treatment.6–8 This process typically produces conversion films following immersion in a hexafluorozirconic or hexafluorotitanic acid solution. In the initial moments, the free fluorides in the solution effectively dissolve the oxide layer of the substrate.9,10 Several researchers have previously investigated the many characteristics of film development in stationary hexafluorozirconic acid solutions on various substrates, such as magnesium, cold-rolled steel, and hot-dip galvanized steel.11–13 However, translating these conclusions to the properties of the pre-treatment process on a macroscopic scale, such as in automobile body lines, is challenging. The effect of functional additions, such as copper, under varying bath agitation settings remains poorly understood.14,15

Consequently, creating innovative coatings that exhibit enhanced and durable anti-corrosion characteristics is essential. In recent years, the incorporation of micro/nanofillers into organic coatings has been developed as an effective method for reducing corrosion due to their capacity to improve the protective characteristics of coatings.16 Implementing micro/nanofillers can extend the diffusion path of corrosive species, which can delay the onset of metal corrosion. However, incorporating micro and nanofillers into coatings is essential because they do not provide a complete solution for long-term protection against corrosion. This is because the corrosive agents may penetrate the coating and damage the metal substrate underneath.17

As a result, discovering uncomplicated organic molecules that are easy to synthesize, cost-effective, and exhibit high coverage efficiency is essential. Also, as environmental concerns grow among the scientific community, there is a heightened emphasis on developing greener methods that minimize environmental hazards, are economically viable, and yield end products with enhanced properties. Recently, the quinoxaline acceptor has been shown to exhibit a stable quinoid structure and superior electron-accepting capacity owing to the presence of two internal nitrogen atoms, which confer enhanced electron-transporting capabilities to its derivatives.18–20 Quinoxaline-based acceptors are commonly found in D–A molecular structures, which are utilized in a range of optoelectronic devices, such as electrochromic devices21,22 polymer solar cells, organic light-emitting diodes (OLEDs)23,24 and organic photodetectors.25,26 In reality, the quinoxaline unit has a readily ornamented structure. In addition to the benefits above, it may increase diversity by easily inserting substituents in its second and third positions.27 Luckily, in 2023, Abusaif and his research team demonstrated the construction of novel water-soluble quinoxaline materials as corrosion inhibitors that reached an IE of 95%.28 Phenanthrenequinoxaline and acenaphthenequinoxaline are examples of typical quinoxaline derivatives; these derivatives insert phenanthrene and acenaphthene into the quinoxaline, respectively, which enables two acceptors (quinoxaline and phenanthrene/acenaphthene) to be present in the same structure.29,30

Several approaches for synthesizing quinoxalines have been documented, including microwave irradiation31 ultrasound irradiation32 and ball milling techniques.33 Limited reports exist regarding the highly efficient, ultrasonication-assisted synthesis of indenoquinoxaline through the reaction of ninhydrin with diaminobenzene34 and the reaction of indenoquinoxaline with hydrazones has not been previously documented. Several documented approaches for synthesizing the title heterocycles exhibit limitations, such as severe reaction conditions, extended reaction durations, costly catalysts, hazardous solvents, or laborious workup procedures. Developing simple, energy-efficient and environmentally friendly technologies for synthesizing these heterocyclic molecules is essential. By adopting this strategy, the authors intend to open a new approach to the development of new indenoquinoxaline hydrazones that are expected to be efficient conversion coating materials through the use of a rapid and reduced solvent toxicity protocol. In this study, the rational design of the inhibitors included the use of ninhydrin to intentionally build an indenoquinoxaline core, distinguishing these novel derivatives from standard quinoxalines. This specific fusion creates a highly planar, rigid structural backbone with enhanced π-conjugation. Such increased planarity is critical for corrosion inhibition, as it promotes strong, flat adsorption structures parallel to the metal substrate, thereby more effectively preventing the penetration of corrosive species.35

In this paper, we focus on the design and construction of three novel organic coatings, IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC, based on the indenoquinoxaline moiety, using a rapid and efficient ultrasound-assisted protocol. This work also describes the results of our methodical investigations on the use of the title materials as conversion coating materials for corrosion inhibition. We focus on the organic conversion coating of mild steel to modify the surface properties of the steel and enhance its corrosion resistance. The corrosion behavior of organic-coated MS samples in various corrosive solutions (0.5 M H2SO4 and 3.5% NaCl) was investigated using open-circuit potential (OCP) analysis, potentiodynamic polarization (PDP) tests, and long-term corrosion assessments, including weight loss calculations, to determine the equivalent corrosion rates. Computational studies, including optimized structure, energy gap, molecular electrostatic potential (MEP), and global reactivity parameters, were also conducted to evaluate and confirm the obtained electrochemical measurements.

While previous studies, including our own earlier work, established standard quinoxaline derivatives as effective corrosion inhibitors (often reaching efficiencies of around 95%), they require prolonged, energy-intensive synthetic routes in toxic organic solvents, and their structures limit further performance enhancement. A major advance in this work is the highly efficient, ultrasonication-assisted synthetic protocol. Achieving the core framework in just 50 seconds at room temperature, with an exceptionally high atom economy, represents a significant operational and environmental advance over earlier methodologies. Also notable is the fusion of the rigid indeno-quinoxaline core with highly functionalized Schiff base linkages. This unique design aims to maximize both the planar surface coverage and the density of localized electronegative anchoring sites, creating a more advanced barrier than previously reported systems.

Experimental section

Materials and instruments

1,2-Diaminobenzene (99%) and ninhydrin (98%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, 2-hydroxybenzaldehyde (SC, 99%) was obtained from Merck (Germany), 2-hydroxy-5-nitrobenzaldehyde (BSC, 98%) was obtained from PLDpharm USA, and 2-hydroxy-1-naphthaldehyde (HSC, 96%) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. The solvent used for purification was supplied as HPLC-grade from Fischer.

Infrared spectra (FT-IR) were obtained with a Shimadzu 440 spectrophotometer across the 400–4000 cm−1 range, corroborating the essential functional groups in the suggested chemical structure. NMR spectra were acquired with a JOEL spectrometer operating within the 400 to 101 MHz frequency range; DMSO-d6 was utilized as the solvent, and the chemical shifts were recorded as ppm relative to TMS, which acted as the internal standard (0 ppm). Melting points of the synthesized organic inhibitors were measured using open capillaries with a digital Gallenkamp MFB-595 device.

Mild steel (MS) sheets with a composition (wt%) C 0.04, Si 0.23, Mn 1.3, Al 0.33, Cu 0.44, Zn 0.21, and Fe (balance) were used. All the samples were ultrasonically cleaned in ethanol for five minutes and dried with air. The pH of this solution was adjusted to 4.0 with ammonium bicarbonate at 15 vol%. Finally, the samples were rinsed in deionized water and dried with air. The organic material conversion coatings were applied by immersing the specimens in a beaker (diameter = 8 cm) containing 20 mL of the investigated coating (room temperature, conc. 200 mg L−1, pH 4) for 24 h.

Chemical preparation of the target structures

Chemical preparation of (Z)-11-hydrazineylidene-11H-indeno[1,2-b]quinoxaline (IQ)

The target material IQ was utilized as a starting compound to create the newly organic materials. It was chemically synthesized using an ultrasonication method. Equimolar amounts of the corresponding indeno-quinoxaline IA (1.0 mmol, 232 mg) and the appropriate hydrazine hydrate (1.0 mmol, 50 mg) were mixed in 7.0 mL of deionized H2O. The mixture solution was irradiated under ultrasonication at 300 W power, 500 J, and 20% amplitude at 25 °C for 50 s. The progress of the reaction was monitored using TLC (ethyl acetate : CH3OH, 8 : 2). After the reaction was completed, the precipitated solid was separated by filtration and washed with ethanol to afford the target material IQ (Scheme 1). The recrystallized pure material was obtained as pale-yellow crystals with a defined melting point (mp 195 °C)36 and a high yield of 92% (226 mg).

Chemical preparation of the target Schiff base derivatives (IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC)

Equimolar amounts of the key intermediate IQ (1.0 mmol, 246 mg) and the corresponding salicylaldehyde derivatives (1.0 mmol) were mixed in 10.0 mL of deionized H2O. The mixture solution was irradiated under ultrasonication at 600 W power, 100 J, 20% amplitude at 25 °C for 40 s. The reaction was monitored using TLC (ethyl acetate : n-hexane, 1 : 2). Weight loss approach and electrochemical measurements

The gravimetric approach is the simplest way to evaluate corrosion losses. Mild steel coupons used in weight-loss and electrochemical measurements were cut into dimensions of (1 cm × 2 cm × 0.2 cm). Prior to each experiment, MS coupons were mechanically polished using successive grades of silicon carbide emery paper ranging from 400 to 1200 grit to obtain a smooth and uniform surface. The specimens were subsequently rinsed thoroughly with distilled water to remove abrasive residues, followed by degreasing in ethanol to eliminate organic impurities. After cleaning, the samples were washed again with deionized water, dried at room temperature, and stored in a desiccator prior to use to prevent surface oxidation. For all measurements, independent, freshly prepared specimens were used for weight-loss and electrochemical experiments to ensure reproducibility and avoid cross-contamination between tests. The chemical composition of the mild steel was obtained from the supplier specification sheet and is listed as follows: Fe (balance), C 0.4310, Mn 0.0335, Si 0.1500, P 0.0151.

Weight-loss analyses of the mild steel (MS) samples after coating were conducted in 0.5 M H2SO4 and 3.5% NaCl solution for 24 h. Weight-loss experiments were performed in triplicate. On the other hand, open-circuit potential measurements (OCP) were performed while dipping the coated mild steel in both acidic and chloride solutions to study the electrochemical response of the substrates in situ and as a function of time. For all base substrates, except for the first 3 seconds, the deviation obtained in three different measurements was 7 mV.

Potentiodynamic polarization of mild steel was carried out at a range of potentials from +1.0 to 3.0 V vs. Ag/AgCl, and a scan rate of 2 mV s−1 in all solutions; the Tafel lines were plotted, and the corrosion rate (CR) and corrosion potential (Ecorr) values were calculated. Potentiostatic measurement applies a constant potential to the metal solution interface and measures its electrochemical behavior as a function of time. Potentiostatic measurement can be used to determine the diffusion coefficients of dissolved materials in a solution and measure passivation or repassivation potentials and rates, allowing the anodic and cathodic protection techniques to be evaluated.39 From the potentiodynamic curves, a particular potential was selected (in the passive region), and samples of the tested alloys were potentiostatically polarized to this potential and kept at this potential for 1 h.

Morphology surface characterization

The surface morphology of the coated MS with organic materials in 3.5% NaCl was investigated before and after the corrosion test. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was conducted with a FEI Inspect S (Netherlands), coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS). The thickness of the conversion coating in different media with time was determined with a coating thickness gauge by following the guidelines outlined in the user manual UT343D.

Density functional theory calculations

The computational calculations for the novel synthesized organic materials, IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC, were performed using the Gaussian 09 and Gaussian View 6.0 software. We applied the methodologies previously described40–43 to optimize the molecular structures, and conducted the calculations utilizing the widely recognized hybrid B3LYP functional in conjunction with the double zeta basis set 6-31G′(d).

Results and discussion

Chemistry and characterization of the organic coating

Chemistry

In the present work, we aim to design and construct three novel organic materials using an efficient ultrasonication-assisted protocol. The synthetic route used to assess the materials IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC is shown in Scheme 1. Initially, the target compound (Z)-11-hydrazineylidene-11H-indeno[1,2-b]quinoxaline IQ was used as a key intermediate to create the final target organic structures IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC. The key intermediate IQ was synthesized via the condensation reaction between indeno-quinoxaline IA and hydrazine monohydrate in deionized H2O under ultrasonication at 300 W power, 500 J, and 20% amplitude at 25 °C for a short time, as a rapid and safe protocol.36 The recrystallized pure material was obtained in a high yield of up to 92% (226 mg) as pale-yellow crystals with a defined melting point (mp.) of 195 °C.44 The final reaction between the key intermediate IQ and the selected salicylaldehyde derivatives proceeded in deionized H2O under ultrasonication due to the fascinating properties of deionized H2O as a solvent and the beneficial effects of ultrasound. This study reports, for the first time, the reaction of the hydrazone derivative IQ and selected substituted salicylaldehydes proceeded in water under ultrasonic irradiation, without organic solvent or catalyst. For conditions optimization, the reaction between hydrazone derivative IQ and 5-nitrosalicylaldehyde was used as a model for synthesizing the target organic materials (Scheme 1). Thus, the reaction was conducted in multiple solvents under conventional and ultrasound irradiation techniques. Without any catalyst, the reaction was carried out at room temperature using 1.0 mmol of IQ and 1.0 mmol of 4-nitrosalicylaldehyde in 10.0 mL of solvent, and the reaction was monitored using TLC (Table 1). Conventional thermal syntheses of highly substituted quinoxalines and Schiff base materials typically require prolonged refluxing, harsh organic solvents, and acid catalysis; however, such conditions often lead to thermal degradation and lower yields. To overcome these limitations, we deliberately employed an ultrasonication-assisted, aqueous-phase methodology. This approach was strategically chosen because the acoustic cavitation provides the intense localized energy required to overcome the steric hindrance of the indeno-fused system at room temperature, facilitating a rapid, highly efficient, and environmentally benign synthesis.45,46

It was found that the reaction under ultrasonic irradiation was completed in shorter periods, with a highly distinguished yield. Within the solvents evaluated, water demonstrated the highest efficacy, achieving a yield of 99% in just 40 seconds (Table 1, entry 4). Due to the high purity of the product, no further purification was necessary. Acetic acid yielded the second-highest product after water, but is classified as a hazardous solvent and catalyst. Generally, ultrasound results in a faster rate of reaction. This is attributed to cavitation phenomena that occur during sonication. Cavitation leads to the formation of microbubbles and adiabatic collapse, resulting in the generation of localized hotspots.47 The hotspots produce elevated temperatures and pressures reaching several thousand atmospheres, facilitating a swift reaction. In conclusion, we established a highly efficient, ultrasonication-assisted methodology for synthesizing three Schiff base materials in water at room temperature, reduced the solvent toxicity, and eliminated the need for any catalyst.

NMR spectral analysis

The chemical structure of the title compounds was elucidated using 1H and 13C NMR spectral analyses; the spectra are shown in Fig. 1. The 1H NMR spectrum of IQ-SSC confirmed the presence of indeno-quinoxaline protons, salicylaldehyde protons, and a new azo-methine proton (CHN), as depicted in Fig. 1a. Two significant singlet signals in the down-field region at δ 9.21 and 12.40 ppm are observed owing to the new Schiff base formation (CHN) and exchangeable phenolic-OH, respectively.48 The other signals that appeared are related to the aromatic protons of the compound, ranging between δ 7.06 and 8.23 ppm, equivalent to 12 protons. Five triplet signals detected at δ 7.06, 7.54, 7.75, 7.91, and 7.95 ppm with coupling constants J = 6.4, 8.4, 3.8, 6.0, and 7.2 Hz, respectively, owing to five aromatic protons, are observed. In addition, doublet signals related to five aromatic protons at δ 7.15, 7.71, 8.07, 8.12, and 8.23 ppm with a mean coupling constant J of 7.2 Hz were observed, and multiplet signals equivalent to two aromatic protons appear at 8.14–8.16 ppm.49

The proton NMR spectra of IQ-BSC and IQ-HSC revealed singlet signals due to the azo-methine proton at δ 8.93 and 8.66 ppm, respectively, besides the exchangeable OH proton displayed at δ 10.95 and 12.88 ppm, respectively (Fig. 1b and c). For the IQ-BSC structure, the aromatic protons of the quinoxaline moiety were established as two doublet signals at δ 7.63 and 7.72 ppm with the same coupling constant, J = 6.4 Hz, and two triplet signals at δ 7.14 and 7.51 ppm with coupling constants, J = 8.4 and 6.8 Hz, respectively. The aromatic protons of the indeno nucleus appeared as triplet, doublet, and multiplet signals at δ 7.57, 8.15, and 7.85–7.87 ppm, respectively. Finally, the phenyl aromatic protons appeared as two doublet signals at δ 6.94 and 8.33 ppm with coupling constants, J = 9.2 and 8.0 Hz, respectively, and a singlet signal at 8.44 ppm equivalent to one aromatic proton.

Furthermore, the 13C NMR spectra confirmed that the reaction successfully formed the three title compounds IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC, as shown in Fig. 1d–f, respectively. For example, the 13C NMR spectrum of IQ-BSC, revealed singlet signals related to significant functions in the structure, at δ 159.91, 164.05, and 166.44 ppm owing to the carbon of the Schiff base function, the carbon attached to the phenolic-OH group, and the carbon of the hydrazone function (CN–N, hydrazone)50 respectively (Fig. 1e). Also, 18 singlet signals due to the aromatic carbons of the IQ-BSC chemical structure were observed at δ 119.22, 120.03, 123.64, 124.85, 129.76, 130.19, 130.56, 131.27, 131.66, 132.73, 133.51, 134.52, 134.84, 135.65, 136.31, 136.89, 139.73, and 142.36 ppm. In conclusion, the 1H and 13C NMR spectra indicate the successful construction of the suggested structures for the newly synthesized compounds, IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC.

FT-IR spectral analysis

The present and newly formed functional groups in the target prepared compounds were analyzed using the FT-IR spectroscopy; the spectra are displayed in Fig. 2. The FT-IR spectrum of the key intermediate IQ revealed absorption peaks at υ 3445, 3338, and 3220 cm−1 corresponding to the amino (–NH2) and NH functions, respectively (Fig. 2a). The stretching bands appeared at νmax 3063, 1564, 1435 cm−1, assigned to the sp2-carbon, the CN function of the hydrazone linker, and the CC group, respectively.51 The IR spectrum of the first Schiff base compound, IQ-SSC, lacked the absorption bands of the amino group, while new absorption frequencies were observed at νmax 3399 and 1636 cm−1 assigned to the phenolic-OH and CHN Schiff base group, respectively52 (Fig. 2b). Additionally, other significant peaks related to the sp2-carbon, CN function of the hydrazone linker, and CC group were detected at νmax 3045, 1607, and 1516 cm−1, respectively, and a bending vibration was observed at νmax 1043 cm−1 owing to the C–OH group.

The FT-IR spectrum of compound IQ-BSC displayed sufficient peaks to support its chemical structure. For instance, a broad absorption peak at νmax 3407 cm−1 due to the phenolic-OH group and the NH function of the hydrazone linker was observed. Significant peaks were displayed at νmax 1638, 1605, 1514 and 1265 cm−1, owing to the azo-methine function (CHN), CN group, and asymmetric N–O of the nitro group (–NO2)53 respectively (Fig. 2c). Changes in the intensity and nature of the presented peaks confirmed the interaction between the key intermediate IQ and 5-nitrosalicaldehyde BSC. Other absorption frequencies at νmax 3061 and 1023 cm−1 confirmed the presence of sp2-carbons and a bending OH group, respectively. The FT-IR spectrum shown in Fig. 2d for IQ-HSC revealed sharp absorption peaks at νmax 3436, 3126, and 1640 cm−1 due to the phenolic-OH, aromatic-CH, and azo-methine groups, respectively. Other significant absorption frequencies at νmax 1593, 1453, and 1005 cm−1 proved the suggested chemical structure of the title compound. Finally, the unequivocal variation in all the absorption peaks in the FT-IR spectrum confirms the proposed structure of the target materials IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC.

Open-circuit potential (OCP)

A material's open-circuit potential (OCP) is a recognized quantity that serves as a thermodynamic predictor of its electrochemical oxidation propensity in a corrosive environment.57 Without applied potential, the steel electrode's potential could change over time due to changes in the surface cathodic and anodic reaction rates, which happen naturally. The OCP may alter over time due to immune development, passive layer creation, or oxidation.58

The variation of the open-circuit potential (EOCP) as a function with time during immersion of the bare MS and coated MS with the title organic materials (IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC) in different corrosion solutions (0.5 M H2SO4 and 3.5% NaCl) are depicted in Fig. 5. Two OCPs will be addressed: EOC1 for the steel electrode inserted into the solution straight from the air (during the first periods of submersion), and after the electrode's potential settles around a constant value, EOC2. Also, the EOC2 represents the steel electrode's corrosion potential when exposed to the corrosion medium; the values of EOC1 and EOC2 are depicted in Table 2. Throughout the immersion period, it has been shown that the OCPs of the bare MS and the MS coated with IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC in 0.5 M H2SO4 solution exhibit stable potentials. This is characterized by the fact that EOC1 is approximately equal to EOC2. In detail, the EOC1 and EOC2 are recorded to be −0.456 and −0.469 V/SCE, respectively, while the MS coated with IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC materials exhibit EOC1 values of −0.430, −0.381, and −0.413 V/SCE, respectively, and the EOC2 are −0.447, −0.394, and −0.426 V/SCE, respectively (Fig. 5a and Table 2). The observed data reveal that the OCPs of the MS coated with the prepared organic materials exhibit the same characteristics but have a reduced negative value of the EOC2 compared with the uncoated counterpart. In addition, the MS coated with IQ-BSC organic material revealed a significantly more negative EOC2 value, reaching −0.394 V/SCE. This value indicates that the IQ-BSC material can be considered an organic coating that is sufficient to prevent corrosion in dilute H2SO4 solution. Moreover, the effectiveness of the three prepared materials as organic coatings can be arranged as follows: IQ-BSC > IQ-HSC > IQ-SSC.

High corrosion potential, high resistance to polarization and low corrosion rate usually indicate good coating corrosion resistance.62

The corrosion protection properties of the layer correspond to the formation of an oxidized layer that could reduce the available surface area of the samples. The conversion coating forms a barrier layer over the steel surface that inhibits its direct contact with the electrolyte solution. Moreover, the title organic coatings that form on the steel surface could limit the corrosive electrolyte attack, weakening the corrosion strength.63 Among the tested compounds, IQ-BSC exhibited the highest corrosion inhibition performance, proving to be a highly effective corrosion inhibitor for mild steel (MS) in both H2SO4 and NaCl solutions. A lower cathodic current density may explain the more negative potential of the impregnated sample, suggesting a lower reaction rate of the cathodic reaction for the impregnated sample. In addition, the corrosion potential rating must consider the shape of the polarization curves, which corresponds to the behavior of an ideally polarizable electrode in the anodic range. Moreover, the inhibition efficiencies of the title organic coatings IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC in 0.5 M H2SO4 solution were 63.68%, 81.13%, and 74.13%, respectively, while in 3.5% NaCl solution they were 89.02%, 92.33%, and 92.08%, respectively. The obtained results demonstrate that the IQ-BSC organic coating exhibited higher inhibition efficiency in different corrosive solutions than the other titled organic coatings, IQ-SSC, and IQ-HSC. The chemical structure of the best organic coating, IQ-BSC, included a greater number of electron-rich functional group, such as –NO2, –OH, and N-containing groups, which functioned as a barrier. The electrochemical polarization results indicate that the investigated inhibitors influence both anodic and cathodic reactions. The corrosion current density (Icorr) decreases significantly in the presence of all the inhibitors, while noticeable changes are observed in both anodic (βa) and cathodic (βc) Tafel slopes compared to the blank solution, confirming the simultaneous effect on metal dissolution and hydrogen evolution reactions. In addition, the corrosion potential (Ecorr) shows only a limited shift (less than approximately 85 mV in both acidic and chloride media), which suggests that the inhibitors do not act predominantly on either the anodic or cathodic process alone.

On the other hand, the data clearly establish an inhibition efficiency trend of IQ-BSC > IQ-HSC > IQ-SSC. To elucidate this trend, correlations between the molecular structure of the substituents and their corresponding adsorption behaviors were analyzed, focusing on conjugation, electronic effects, and molecular polarity. Firstly, comparing IQ-SSC to IQ-HSC highlights the role of extended π-conjugation and molecular size. IQ-HSC features a naphthyl moiety, whereas IQ-SSC contains a smaller phenyl ring. The extended aromatic system in IQ-HSC provides a larger planar surface area. When adopting a flat adsorption configuration, IQ-HSC blankets a larger portion of the metal substrate per molecule, structurally blocking more active dissolution sites. Additionally, the richer π-electron cloud in the naphthyl ring enhances the probability of forward electron donation (π–d) to the metal.28 Secondly, the superior performance of IQ-BSC demonstrates the dominant influence of molecular polarity and electronic effects. IQ-BSC incorporates a strong electron-withdrawing nitro group (NO2), which drastically increases the molecular dipole moment. In the highly polar, acidic aqueous environment, this elevated polarity strengthens the initial physical adsorption (physisorption) through robust dipole–dipole and electrostatic interactions with the charged steel surface.64 Moreover, the two highly electronegative oxygen atoms of the nitro group serve as additional, potent coordinating centers, effectively increasing the number of bonds the molecule can form with the iron surface. The strong electron-withdrawing nature of the nitro group significantly lowers the energy of the molecule's lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (ELUMO). A lower ELUMO makes the molecule a superior electron acceptor, thereby highly facilitating d–π* retro-donation from the metal's populated d-orbitals back into the inhibitor. This “push–pull” dynamic creates an exceptionally stable chemisorbed film.

Surface morphology analysis of the corroded MS

The secondary electron imaging technique in a scanning electron microscope (SEM) and EDX analysis were utilized to characterize the corroded surface features, demonstrating the protective performance of the prepared IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC organic coatings in 3.5% NaCl solution. SEM-EDX images were acquired for the uncoated and coated steel after WL investigations, and the results are displayed in Fig. 8. As presented in Fig. 8a, the SEM image shows the nature of the surface of the bare MS following 4 days of immersion in 3.5% NaCl solution. It shows that the surface exhibits pitting and localized corrosion triggered by barrier layer porosity and violent ion attacks. Corrosion tends to be more prevalent along the steel grain boundaries. Minute depressions are apparent on the corroded bare steel in the corroded solution, as shown in Fig. 8a. The pitting of steel is a recognized indicator of localized corrosion. Consequently, exposed mild steel undergoes localized corrosion in the corrosion solution due to chloride ions (Cl−).68

SEM-EDX images and elemental mapping spectra in 3.5% NaCl corrosive solutions of the (a) bare MS, (b) MS coated with IQ-SSC, (c) MS coated with IQ-BSC, and (d) MS coated with IQ-HSC.

In contrast, by coating the MS with the target organic materials IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC and immersing it in the corrosive solution, the layer is clearly insoluble in the aqueous NaCl solution (Fig. 8b–d). It is noted that the coating on the mild steel has a homogeneous surface because the barrier layer does not allow corrosive ions into the coating surface. Also, the heavy physisorption of the coating on the surface prevents the corrosive ions from penetrating the coating surface. As indicated in the electrochemical characterization, the MS dissolution was regulated by the impermeable coat of organic materials on the surface. Moreover, depending on the surface conditioning treatment parameters prior to the application of the conversion coating, the oxide film might be modified. This change in oxide might have an important effect on the first step of the formation of the film, and consequently on the surface characteristics of the resulting conversion layer. Moreover, the SEM figures shown in Fig. 8c displayed a highly protective layer with the IQ-BSC organic coating that more effectively prevented the corrosion process than the other layers with IQ-SSC and IQ-HSC organic coatings. The effectiveness of the coatings can be arranged as follows: IQ-BSC > IQ-HSC > IQ-SSC.

The EDX elemental percentages for the bare MS surface are 8.9% O, 0.6% Cl, 0.7% Cu, 0.1% Zn, and 86.0% Fe, as viewed in Fig. 8a. The EDX elemental percentages for MS coated with IQ-SSC materials is 25.7% O, 0.1% N, 0.6% C, 0.9% Cl, 0.3% Cu, and 0.2% Zn (Fig. 8b), and for MS coated with IQ-BSC it is 18.3% O, 0.4% N, 2.7% C, 0.7% Cl, 0.4% Cu, 0.3% Zn, 0.8% Br, and 74.0% Fe (Fig. 8c), and finally the MS coated with IQ-HSC is 27.6% O, 0.2% N, 0.8% C, 0.4% Cl, 0.1% Cu, 0.5% Zn, and 67.7% Fe (Fig. 8d). In more detail, by coating the MS with the title organic materials, the EDX analysis revealed a minimal nitrogen content by weight, and the peaks corresponding to salts and oxides formed due to corrosion process are diminished. The MS coated with IQ-HSC contained the highest intensity peak of iron, while the MS coated with IQ-SSC and IQ-HSC exhibited the lowest intensity peak of iron. These results indicate that the IQ-HSC material can create a more protective layer on the surface of the metal steel than the other organic coatings.

Generally, at the initial immersion, the untreated MS suffered from a high corrosion rate, and the surface was rapidly covered with corrosion products (Fig. 8a), which slowed down further corrosion attack. It could be concluded that, at least in the initial immersion, samples coated with quinoxaline were significantly more corrosion-resistant than the untreated ones. This is consistent with the results of the electrochemical tests, which measured short-term corrosion rates. Furthermore, it must be pointed out that, for MS, it is not easy to accurately determine the mass loss of the substrate due to corrosion because it is not easy to remove the corrosion product completely without affecting the substrate, and thus a large error might be present. From the results of the electrochemical tests and the immersion test, it might be stated that the protectiveness provided by the coating is high in the initial immersion, but as time increases, it becomes less effective.69 Both the electrochemical and immersion tests in different media showed an appreciable increase in corrosion resistance resulting from the coating of the MS with quinoxaline, in terms of both corrosion rate and damage morphology.70

Chemisorption is driven by the various and numerous heteroatoms (O and N) present in the inhibitor structures, especially IQ-BSC, as their lone-pair electrons coordinate with the metal's vacant orbitals. Conversely, physisorption is primarily attributed to the abundance of aromatic rings within the molecular framework, a feature that is particularly pronounced in the IQ-BSC and IQ-HSC inhibitors. Upon exposure to the corrosive solution (0.5 M H2SO4 or 3.5% NaCl), the steel undergoes oxidation, generating a positively charged surface that facilitates the pre-adsorption of chloride ions (Cl−). Simultaneously, the inhibitor molecules become protonated in the acidic environment. This leads to physisorption, driven by electrostatic attraction between the protonated inhibitors and the pre-adsorbed Cl− layer. This interaction draws the inhibitor molecules in close proximity to the steel surface, initiating charge transfer.74,75 As the protonated inhibitors transfer electrons to the metal, they revert to their neutral state, evolving hydrogen gas and forming coordinate bonds. Next, robust chemisorption is achieved by the donation of electrons from the heteroatom lone pairs and the benzene ring π-system into the unoccupied d-orbitals of the mild steel.76 Finally, the adsorption of these inhibitors was found to follow a Langmuir isotherm, with a mixed-type mechanism. While all synthesized compounds proved to be effective organic inhibitors, the data highlight IQ-BSC as the most efficient, followed by IQ-HSC.

Adsorption and inhibition mechanism

The inhibition of mild steel corrosion in 0.5 M H2SO4 by the synthesized indenoquinoxaline derivatives proceeds via a comprehensive, mixed-type adsorption mechanism. As illustrated in Fig. 12, using the highly efficient IQ-BSC as a representative model, the adsorption cannot be strictly isolated to either physisorption or chemisorption; rather, it manifests as a synergistic combination of both processes. Crucially, the adsorption configuration of IQ-BSC on the metal surface is dictated by its rigid, indeno-fused molecular architecture. This highly planar framework thermodynamically favors a parallel (flat) orientation relative to the steel substrate. This flat configuration maximizes the contact area between the inhibitor's extended π-system and the metal, effectively blanketing the surface and creating a dense steric barrier that restricts the diffusion of corrosive species.

Adsorption and inhibition mechanism of the IQ-BSC material on the mild steel surface in a 0.5 M H2SO4 solution.

• Physical adsorption (physisorption): in the highly acidic corrosive medium, the heteroatoms of the IQ-BSC inhibitor (particularly the basic nitrogen atoms) readily undergo protonation. Concurrently, the steel surface becomes positively charged, leading to the rapid adsorption of H2SO4 ions. The protonated IQ-BSC molecules are subsequently drawn to the interface via strong electrostatic attractions to these pre-adsorbed, negatively charged sulfate bridges.

• Chemical adsorption (chemisorption): as the physically adsorbed inhibitor molecules approach the metal substrate, strong coordinate covalent bonds are formed. This occurs via the forward donation of lone-pair electrons from the unprotonated heteroatoms (n–d) and delocalized π-electrons from the aromatic rings (π–d) into the unoccupied 3d atomic orbitals of the surface iron atoms.

• Synergistic retro-donation: to alleviate the accumulation of negative charge on the iron atoms caused by the forward electron donation, a synergistic retro-donation occurs. Electrons from the populated d-orbitals of the iron surface are back-donated into the unoccupied antibonding π-orbitals (π*) of the inhibitor's aromatic rings (d–π*). This “push–pull” electronic interaction heavily stabilizes the flat adsorption configuration, resulting in a highly robust and resilient protective film.

Computational studies

To provide a theoretical rationale supporting the experimental findings, quantum chemical calculations were performed using density functional theory (DFT) with the widely recognized hybrid B3LYP functional in conjunction with the double zeta basis set 6-31G′(d)/gas phase model. These idealized calculations evaluate the intrinsic electronic properties of the isolated inhibitor molecules to propose potential interactions at the metal interface. The optimized geometrical structures of the three inhibitor molecules (IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC) are illustrated in Fig. 13. The structural optimizations indicate an intrinsic non-planar conformation where the salicylaldehyde-derived moieties are twisted almost perpendicularly relative to the rigid indenoquinoxaline core. While dynamic surface adsorption can alter these conformations, this 3D molecular architecture distributes hydrophilic and polar functional groups (such as the nitro and hydroxyl groups in IQ-BSC) across multiple planes, offering numerous potential anchoring sites for interaction with the metal surface.78,79

The molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) maps visually estimate the electrophilic and nucleophilic zones of the isolated molecules, offering qualitative predictive insights into potential metal-inhibitor interactions (Fig. 13). The electrostatic potential increases from red (most negative/nucleophilic) to blue (most positive/electrophilic). For IQ-SSC, strong nucleophilic regions localize around the hydrazone linkage and the terminal hydroxyl group, suggesting these are primary sites for proposed electron donation to the metal surface. In contrast, IQ-BSC, featuring the strongly electron-withdrawing nitro group (-NO2), exhibits more pronounced and highly localized electron-rich (red) and electron-deficient (blue) domains. This enhanced molecular polarity theoretically supports a stronger initial electrostatic attraction to the charged steel surface compared to IQ-SSC. The naphthyl moiety in IQ-HSC presents a moderate distribution of these reactive zones.80 All these results confirmed the successful binding and coating of the organic materials on the surface of mild steel, especially the IQ-BSC organic coating.

Furthermore, the energies of the frontier molecular orbitals (HOMO and LUMO) and the energy gap (ΔE) were calculated (Fig. 14, Table 9). The ELUMO value for IQ-BSC (−2.669 eV) is notably more negative than those of IQ-HSC (−2.588 eV) and IQ-SSC (−2.404 eV). A lower ELUMO suggests an enhanced theoretical capacity for the molecule to accept back-donated electrons from the metal d-orbitals (d → π* retro-donation). Consequently, the calculated energy gap (ΔE) reveals that IQ-BSC possesses the lowest gap at 3.211 eV (compared to 3.389 eV for IQ-SSC and 3.413 eV for IQ-HSC). The notably lower energy gap of IQ-BSC implies a higher intrinsic chemical reactivity and a more facile electron transfer from the HOMO to the LUMO level.81,82 Global reactivity parameters further support these electronic trends (Table 9). The low ionization energies (I) of the title compounds (5.793, 6.004, and 5.601 eV, respectively) indicate a favorable thermodynamic tendency to donate electrons to the unoccupied orbitals of the metal. The electrophilicity index (ω), which quantifies a molecule's energetic propensity to accept electrons, increases in the order: IQ-SSC < IQ-HSC < IQ-BSC. This trend indicates that IQ-BSC possesses the strongest potential to interact with electron-rich surface sites via retro-donation. Additionally, IQ-BSC exhibits the lowest chemical hardness (η = 1.467 eV), classifying it as the “softest” and most easily polarizable molecule in the series, a trait highly advantageous for forming coordinate bonds with transition metals.

Conclusion

The present study successfully designed, synthesized, and evaluated three indenoquinoxaline Schiff base derivatives (IQ-SSC, IQ-BSC, and IQ-HSC) as corrosion inhibitors for mild steel. Based on the empirical and theoretical data obtained under the specified testing conditions, the main supported findings are succinctly described as follows:

• Synthesis and characterization: the three novel indenoquinoxaline Schiff bases were successfully synthesized using an operationally efficient, ultrasound-assisted aqueous protocol, achieving the core structures in short reaction times (∼40 seconds) with excellent yields (∼99%). The structural integrity and purity of the synthesized compounds were rigorously confirmed via FT-IR, 1H NMR, and 13C NMR spectroscopy, and CHN elemental analysis.

• Electrochemical performance: electrochemical measurements demonstrated that the addition of these inhibitors to 0.5 M H2SO4 and 3.5% NaCl solutions significantly reduced the corrosion current density compared to the bare mild steel. The IQ-BSC derivative exhibited the strongest performance, lowering the current density to −0.010 mA cm−2.

• Inhibition trends: prolonged weight loss measurements corroborated the electrochemical data, revealing a significant reduction in the corrosion rate. Treating the steel with these organic materials reduced the corrosion rate by approximately 91%, establishing a clear, consistent inhibition efficiency trend of: IQ-BSC > IQ-HSC > IQ-SSC.

• Surface protection: surface morphology analysis confirmed that the bare mild steel suffered from extensive pitting and oxide layer formation (primarily manganese and iron oxides). In contrast, the application of the inhibitors, particularly IQ-BSC, effectively suppressed the development of these corrosive features, indicating the successful adsorption of a protective molecular film.

• Computational support: idealized quantum chemical calculations (DFT) provided a qualitative theoretical rationale that aligns with the experimental trends. IQ-BSC exhibited the lowest energy gap (3.211 eV) and the highest molecular polarity, theoretically supporting its superior capacity for electron transfer and surface adsorption.

• Future perspectives: while these findings firmly establish IQ-BSC as a highly effective corrosion inhibitor under the tested laboratory conditions, further validation is required. Future studies incorporating advanced in situ surface spectroscopy (e.g., XPS) and long-term, pilot-scale testing will be necessary to definitively confirm the proposed atomic-level adsorption mechanisms and assess the ultimate industrial viability of these compounds.
