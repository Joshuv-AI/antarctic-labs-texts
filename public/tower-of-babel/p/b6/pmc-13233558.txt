The impact of COVID-19 on the knowledge, attitudes and practices of end-of-life care and decision making amongst doctors in South African Emergency Departments
Authors: Aniefiok Oluwatomisin Edem, Lucy Hindle, Jana du Plessis, Kelly Jacobs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe COVID-19 pandemic placed unprecedented strain on emergency departments (EDs), altering approaches to end-of-life (EOL) care. This study assessed its impact on emergency clinicians’ knowledge, decision making and attitudes towards EOL care in Johannesburg, South Africa.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study among doctors working in five state-funded Johannesburg EDs between July 2024 and January 2025. A self-administered questionnaire assessed knowledge, attitudes, and practices across three peripandemic time periods: pre-COVID-19, during COVID-19 and post-COVID-19. Participants included doctors with ED experience both before and after the pandemic, and ED/ critical care experience during the pandemic. The survey included 10 knowledge items and Likert-scale assessments of attitudes and practices. Changes over time and associations with demographics were analysed, with p<0.05 considered statistically significant.ResultsNinety-three doctors participated (median ED experience 5 years). Only 11.8% reported formal EOL training, although median knowledge scores were high (9/10, IQR9-10). Attitudes towards EOL decision-making shifted significantly during the COVID-19 pandemic, with clinical prognostic factors (e.g., likelihood of survival, poor neurological outcomes, quality of life) increasing in importance, while medicolegal and religious considerations remained stable. Reported practices of withholding and withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment increased significantly during the COVID-19 pandemic and declined thereafter, suggesting an association with pandemic related pressures. In contrast, symptom management improved across all time points and EOL discussions became more frequent during and after the pandemic, particularly regarding prognosis and family involvement.ConclusionThe COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the ethical complexity importance of EOL care in South African EDs, alongside sustained improvements in communication and symptom management. These findings support the need for targeted EOL care training and system-level policy development to strengthen care in resourced constrained environments.Keywords: End-of-life care, Emergency medicine, Palliative care, South Africa, COVID-19

Introduction

Palliative care, as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), is a process that “improves quality of life of patients and their families’ facing problems associated with life threatening illness”. It upholds the right to a dignified death, and should be provided by all health care providers [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. While palliative and end-of-life (EOL) care are at times used synonymously, the terms should rather be viewed as a continuum, with palliative care initiated early in the diagnosis of a life-threatening illness, while EOL care specifically refers to health care providers’ practices when death is imminent [5,6].

Lowery & Quest’s [7] research describes the ‘twelve palliative care skills critical to the practice of the emergency provider’ which include, among others, “the ability to withhold and withdraw life sustaining treatments, the ability to manage imminently dying patients and the understanding of the ethical and legal issues pertinent to end-of-life care”. In a study done by Taylor et al. [8], 29.4% of patients known to palliative care presented to the Emergency Department (ED) with acute symptoms. EOL decision making is therefore a core skill needed for emergency providers, particularly in managing distressing symptoms and determining the need to withhold or withdraw life sustaining treatment [3,[9], [10], [11]].

The COVID-19 pandemic placed immense strain on EDs globally. During the three peak waves in South Africa, large numbers of acutely ill patients presented to EDs, resulting in severe health system resource constraints [12,13] These challenges intensified the need for EOL care decisions in emergency settings, and may have prompted growth in knowledge and changes in attitudes and clinical practices regarding EOL care [7,[14], [15], [16]] In South Africa, little is known about how the pandemic influenced these domains among ED doctors.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on the knowledge, attitudes and practices surrounding EOL decision making among doctors working in emergency departments in Johannesburg, South Africa.

Methods

This cross-sectional, descriptive study explored changes across three peri‑pandemic periods: pre-COVID-19, during COVID-19 (defined by the time period of the peak COVID-19 waves in South Africa, June 2020 – September 2021), and post-COVID-19 [17].

A self-administered questionnaire (Appendix A) with convenience sampling was used to enable pragmatic data collection and assess self-reported attitudes and recalled behaviours across time. Data were collected from July 2024 to January 2025. Participants were recruited at academic meetings, provided informed consent, and completed the survey anonymously. The study was conducted across five state-funded hospitals in Johannesburg. Doctors were eligible for inclusion if they had ED work experience prior to and after the COVID-19 pandemic, or if they had worked in an ED or acute COVID-19 critical care unit during the pandemic. Doctors who were not yet qualified during the pandemic were excluded.

The questionnaire comprised three sections: knowledge, attitudes, and practices regarding EOL care. The knowledge section included ten true-or-false items derived from the Health Professions Council of South Africa’s booklets on Ethical Palliative care and Withholding and Withdrawing of Treatment [4,11]. The attitudes and practices sections were informed by previously published studies on COVID-19 and EOL care [[18], [19], [20]]. Specifically, items assessing attitudes towards resource allocation and decision-making, as well as practices related to withholding and withdrawing life-sustaining treatment and end-of-life discussions, were adapted for relevance to the South African emergency department context. Content validity was supported through expert review by three specialist emergency physicians, who assessed the questionnaire for clarity, relevance, and clinical applicability. Minor revisions were made based on their feedback prior to data collection; the questionnaire was not formally pilot tested.

Attitudes were assessed using an inverted Likert scale, rating the importance of 11 criteria regarding resource allocation and withholding and withdrawing life-sustaining treatment. The Practices domain assessed self-reported behaviours related to the delivery and enactment of EOL care, rather than primary decision making alone, based on recalled involvement. A similar Likert scale format was used in the subsections: Reasons for withholding and withdrawing life-sustaining treatment, practices around pain and symptom relief, and practices around conducting end-of-life discussions.

Involvement in end-of-life care was not restricted to primary decision makers, as end-of-life care in the ED is inherently team-based and clinicians at all levels participate in implementing decision-making. Therefore, all participants who contributed were considered to have sufficient clinical exposure to report on practices. Missing data were handled using pairwise deletion, with all available responses included in each analysis.

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 30 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and RStudio (Version 2024.12.0; Posit PBC, Boston, MA, USA). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize participant characteristics. Friedman tests were performed to assess changes over time across repeated measures and, where significant (p < 0.05), Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were conducted as post hoc pairwise analyses to identify specific time point differences (e.g., pre- vs. during COVID, during vs. post-COVID). Bonferroni correction was applied, with the adjusted significance threshold at p < 0.0167. Associations between participant demographics and attitudes and practices were analysed using median Likert scores per participant, with Kruskal–Wallis tests and Mann–Whitney U tests used for secondary analyses. The study was approved by the University of the Witwatersrand Human Research Ethics Committee (Medical) (Reference number: M231160)

Demographic characteristics of particpants.

Ten knowledge questions were answered, with a median knowledge score of 9/10 (IQR 9.0–10.0, SD 1.6). Eighty four percent (n=78) of doctors understood that withholding or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment can be ethical. Of note, only 75% (n=70) of doctors correctly identified that palliative care does not only involve providing relief from pain and distressing symptoms. Overall, there were no statistically significant associations between demographic characteristics and knowledge scores.

When assessing attitudes towards EOL care, six factors (Fig. 1) showed increased self-reported importance during the COVID-19 pandemic, with lower median scores (inverted Likert scale) reflecting stronger perceived influence on decision-making.

Proportion of “Very important” responses to factors influencing EOL decisions.

The proportion of respondents rating “patient unlikely to survive despite medical treatment” as very important increased from 58.4% pre-COVID-19 to 81.5% during the pandemic, and remained elevated at 72.8% post-COVID-19. Similar increases from the pre- to during COVID-19 period were observed for “poor quality of life despite survival” (47.7% to 59.8%), “poor neurological outcome” (54.5% to 69.6%) and "ED critical care bed availability" (30.7% to 53.3%). Post hoc testing revealed statistical significance for these factors in the period before to during the pandemic (p < 0.0167), indicating these clinical prognostic factors to be more central to decision-making during the pandemic.

Four of these factors, as well as "xpected quality of life as viewed by the patient", revealed statistical significance in the period comparing before to after the pandemic (p < 0.0167), highlighting that the pandemic may have influenced the perceived importance of these factors with a sustained post-pandemic effect.

In contrast, factors such as fear of litigation, patient nationality, religious views of the patient, expected quality of life as viewed by the family, and religious views of the treating physician did not show significant shifts across the three periods, with median scores remaining stable.Practices around EOL care

The most frequently cited reason during the COVID-19 pandemic for withholding treatment was ‘poor prognosis’ (Fig. 2), with 55.9% (n = 52) of respondents indicating that they “very often” withheld treatment (median = 1, IQR 1–2). Withholding due to ‘poor prognosis, patient age, treatment being a limited resource, lack of critical care beds and formal hospital protocol’ all increased significantly during the pandemic (p < 0.0167).

Perroportion of “Very often” responses to factors influencing withholding of treatment.

During the pandemic, resource constraints became a dominant driver of care withdrawal (Fig. 3), with all shortage related factors as well as futility of treatment seeing a significant increase in reason for withdrawal (before to during, p < 0.0167). The proportion of doctors who reported “very often” withdrawing treatment due to mechanical ventilator shortages rose by 37.4% from pre-pandemic (median = 3, IQR 2.25–3) to during COVID-19 (median = 2, IQR 1–2.75). Similar peaks were noted for shortages of nurses (4.4% to 28.0%), essential equipment (4.4% to 25.8%), and critical care beds (10.0% to 45.2%). The self-reported importance of many of these factors declined post-pandemic, although levels remained above pre-pandemic baseline.

Pain and symptom management

Overall, symptom management practices improved over time. Comparing pre and post COVID-19 periods, notable gains were seen in all domains apart from nutrition support. The self-reported importance of managing nausea and vomiting increased, with 37.5% (n = 35) of respondents rating it as “very important” after the pandemic compared to 32.2% (n = 29) before COVID-19. Dehydration management improved, with “very often” responses increasing from 38.9% (n = 35) before COVID-19 to 46.2% (n = 43) during and after the pandemic (p = 0.009). Delirium management also showed an upward trend. Dyspnoea management demonstrated a statistically significant improvement during the pandemic (median = 1, IQR 1–2; p < 0.001) and remained above baseline levels thereafter.

End-of-life discussions

End-of-life communication practices demonstrated significant increase in self-reported importance across all seven measured items. Notable increases were observed in discussions of prognosis with patients and families, rising from 37.8% (n = 34) reporting “very often” before the pandemic to 58.1% (n = 54) after COVID-19 (p < 0.001 for pre to post-COVID-19 comparison). Support for last rites showed significant increased importance both during and after the pandemic. Similar positive trends were noted in family presence during resuscitation and in post-event debriefing. The only domain showing a temporary decline during the pandemic was visitor access, which decreased to 34.4% (n = 32) during COVID-19 but rebounded significantly after restrictions eased, reaching 66.7% (n = 62) post pandemic and remaining substantially higher than baseline levels.

Discussion

The COVID-19 pandemic is defined as a biological natural disaster [21]. During disasters, EOL care is delivered across a range of acute settings including emergency departments, where services are strained by increased demand [21]. Kelly et al. emphasize the continued importance of symptom management and maintaining EOL discussions with patients and families during these disasters, particularly around prognosis and treatment futility [21]. This need was further recognised by the Association of Palliative Care Practitioners of South Africa (PALPRAC), which issued national guidance in April 2020, recommending that palliative care be provided within hospitals and emergency departments to address reduced access to specialist palliative services, shortages of beds and ventilators, and the ethical complexity of triage during the pandemic [22].

Pegg et al. [23] describe how emergency departments in the African context, historically less aligned with providing palliative care, were compelled during the COVID-19 pandemic to integrate EOL decision-making into routine emergency workflows, particularly for patients presenting in extremis. Against this backdrop, our study explored the impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on emergency providers’ knowledge, attitudes, and practices in EOL care. Encouragingly, participants in our study achieved high knowledge scores regarding palliative care principles, echoing Lowey & Quest’s [7] framework of twelve core skills for emergency physicians—including illness assessment, prognostication, advance care planning, communication of bad news, and symptom management.

Attitudinal findings revealed consistent prioritisation of clinical factors such as medical futility, neurological outcome, and quality of life in EOL decision-making, with relatively little influence from non-clinical considerations. Notably, the elevated importance of these clinical factors persisted beyond the pandemic period, suggesting a perceived shift in provider priorities towards patient-centred outcomes.

Practice data reflected more dynamic changes. During the pandemic, clinicians reported increased withholding and withdrawal of treatment, largely driven by concerns about prognosis and futility, as well as by systemic pressures such as shortages of ventilators, critical care beds, staff, and equipment.

An important contextual consideration is the composition of the study population, which included a small proportion of specialist-level clinicians compared to medical officers. While EOL care in the ED is often a team-based approach, in South African EDs, senior clinicians are typically responsible for final decisions regarding withholding or withdrawing care. As such, our findings may reflect the perspectives of clinicians who are involved in implementing or observing these decisions, rather primary decision-makers. This has implications for interpretation, as reported attitudes and practices may represent shared experiences of care delivery rather than individual decision-making authority.

While these resource-driven practices largely reverted to baseline as the acute crisis subsided, the temporary increase in importance during the pandemic underscores the extent to which capacity strain shaped EOL decision-making. In South Africa, ICU and ventilator scarcity was a known concern at this time. As early as April 2020, local news reports warned of a looming ventilator shortfall in the context of global supply chain disruption [24]. As Naidoo & Naidoo [25] highlighted in their South African study, pre-existing resource limitations and staff shortages reveal the ethical tension inherent to EOL care in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where resource limitations are a persistent reality rather than a crisis-specific phenomenon. Clinicians across all levels are frequently required to balance individual patient-centred care with broader considerations of distributive justice. The COVID-19 pandemic intensified these challenges, but also made visible the ethical complexity that characterises routine emergency care in resource-constrained environments.

By contrast, practices grounded in palliative principles—such as treatment futility and patient or family request—remained elevated post-pandemic, suggesting the crisis may have influenced clinicians’ implementation of EOL decision-making and reinforced a focus on clinical appropriateness.

Importantly, participants reported improvements in symptom management during and after the pandemic, with more proactive approaches to pain and comfort care. These findings were similarly observed by Heung et al. [26], who found that integrated palliative approaches during COVID-19 improved symptom control, communication, and family satisfaction. Rowe et al. similarly also described positive reception to palliative care integration into emergency departments during the pandemic [27]. These changes likely reflect the heightened focus on comfort measures during a period of high mortality and limited curative options, and the sustained increase post-pandemic suggests a perceived enduring change in practice.

EOL communication also showed encouraging recovery. Clinicians reported greater frequency of prognosis discussions and more regular involvement of families in care planning, reflecting growing recognition of the holistic dimensions of EOL care.

Taken together, our findings suggest that EOL care in the ED is shaped both by external pressures, such as pandemics and resource constraints, and by internal factors including level of experience and clinical exposure. The COVID-19 pandemic may have contributed to changes in practice, reinforcing the ethical imperative for patient-centred, clinically appropriate care. Institutions now have an opportunity to embed these lessons into policy and training, ensuring that EOL principles are not reserved for times of crisis but form a routine and integral part of emergency medicine practice. Future multi-centre studies may help validate these trends and inform national policy to strengthen ethically grounded, patient-centred and holistic emergency care.

Limitations

This study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to recall bias and hindsight reconstitution. This may limit the interpretation of any observed changes in attitudes and practices. Additionally, there is no validated measurement tool for this context. The study population was limited to ED in one South African city, Johannesburg, limiting the external validity.

The use of a structured quantitative questionnaire, while useful for identifying trends, did not allow for deeper qualitative exploration of factors that may influence decision-making. Furthermore, the study did not control for confounding factors such as institutional protocols, staffing levels, or hospital-specific resource constraints, which may have influenced reported practices.

Conclusion

The COVID-19 pandemic highlighted the unique ethical tension in delivering EOL care in LMICs, while also acting as a potential catalyst for improving care in South African emergency departments. Future efforts should build on these findings to sustain compassionate, ethically sound care beyond crisis settings.
