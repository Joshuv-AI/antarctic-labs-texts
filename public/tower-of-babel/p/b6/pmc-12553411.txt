Clinical Prediction Score for Patients With Initial Nonshockable Rhythm Receiving Extracorporeal Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation for Out‐of‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest: Development and Internal Validation
Authors: Akira Kawauchi, Yohei Okada, Makoto Aoki, Akihiko Inoue, Toru Hifumi, Tetsuya Sakamoto, Yasuhiro Kuroda, Mitsunobu Nakamura, the SAVE‐J II Study Group
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSelecting appropriate patients for extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) in cases of out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest remains challenging, particularly for those with initial nonshockable rhythms. Our aim is to develop a prediction score to identify suitable ECPR candidates in patients with initial nonshockable rhythm.MethodsThe data were sourced from the SAVE‐J II (Study of Advanced Life Support for Ventricular Fibrillation With Extracorporeal Circulation in Japan II) study, a retrospective multicenter observational study in Japan. Included were adult patients with out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest who underwent ECPR with initial pulseless electrical activity or asystole. The primary outcome was survival to hospital discharge. We developed a prediction score, employing logistic regression analysis and internally validating it with 1000 bootstrap samples. The performance of the score in predicting a favorable neurological outcome at discharge was also evaluated.ResultsAmong 648 eligible patients, 86 (13.3%) survived to hospital discharge. The median age was 60.5 years, 75.9% (492) were male, and 74.4% (482) had pulseless electrical activity as the initial rhythm. Three clinical predictors for the START‐ECPR Score (Signs of Life, Transient ROSC, Not Asystole Rhythm to ECPR Score) were identified: shockable rhythm or pulseless electrical activity at hospital arrival, transient return of spontaneous circulation before hospital arrival, and signs of life at hospital arrival. Survival rates were 4.4% (7/159) for a score of 0, 10.7% (38/356) for a score of 1, and 30.8% (39/130) for scores of 2 to –3. The bias‐corrected C‐index for the score was 0.696. For predicting favorable neurologic outcomes at discharge, the C‐index was 0.761.ConclusionsWe developed a straightforward 3‐factor prediction score for predicting survival to hospital discharge and favorable neurologic outcomes in patients with out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest with initial nonshockable rhythms receiving ECPR.

Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ECPR) is an advanced life‐saving technique for patients experiencing refractory cardiac arrest. 1 Recent studies have demonstrated that ECPR can improve outcomes following cardiac arrest, 2 , 3 , 4 prompting its increased adoption globally. 5 , 6 However, selecting the appropriate patients for ECPR is particularly challenging, especially in out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) scenarios where time and data to evaluate eligibility are scarce. Clinicians must consider various factors from the patient's background and cardiac arrest situations when assessing ECPR eligibility. 1 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 Of these, the initial cardiac rhythm is crucial, as an initial shockable rhythm is associated with better neurological outcomes. 8 , 9 , 10 Thus, ECPR in OHCA settings has predominantly been directed at patients presenting with initial shockable rhythms, 8 , 9 , 14 , 15 and prediction models for selecting ECPR candidates have mainly been tailored to this group. 13 , 16

Nevertheless, certain patients with initial nonshockable rhythms have also shown potential for favorable neurological recovery. 17 , 18 , 19 Current guidelines from the Extracorporeal Life Support Organization now recognize pulseless electrical activity (PEA) as a qualifying criterion for ECPR. 1 This inclusion presents a clinical challenge: despite the typically poor prognosis for initial nonshockable rhythms, there is a need to pinpoint which patients within this group might benefit from ECPR. Existing prediction models, however, offer limited guidance for this demographic. 20 , 21 To address this gap, we aimed to develop and internally validate a straightforward clinical prediction score that can effectively identify ECPR candidates among patients with OHCA with initial nonshockable rhythms, using a comprehensive nationwide registry.

METHODS

This study followed the guidelines of the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis + Artificial Intelligence statement, published in 2024 (Table S1). 22 The protocol for the SAVE‐J II (Study of Advanced Life Support for Ventricular Fibrillation With Extracorporeal Circulation in Japan II) study received ethical approval from the Japanese Red Cross Maebashi Hospital's ethics committee (approval number: 2023‐53). The requirement for written informed consent from participants was waived. Data used in this study are available from the SAVE‐J II Study Group upon reasonable request and with permission.

Data Source and Data Preparation

We conducted a secondary analysis of the SAVE‐J II study, a retrospective nationwide multicenter observational study in Japan. 14 The SAVE‐J II study collected data from adult patients (≥18 years old) who underwent ECPR for OHCA across 36 intensive care units in Japan from 2013 to 2018. The participating facilities were major emergency hospitals located in urban and suburban areas throughout the country. 23 Approximately 60% of the facilities had their own protocols for ECPR, and ECPR was consistently performed under the supervision of emergency physicians. ECPR was defined as resuscitation with venoarterial ECMO during cardiac arrest. Prehospital and in‐hospital data were recorded by treating physicians at each participating institution. The SAVE‐J II study group committee subsequently reviewed and cleaned the data. Following the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis+Artificial Intelligence statement and the expert's recommendation, 22 , 24 , 25 we used all collected data for development and internal validation without data splitting to maximize the predictive performance.

Participants

We included all patients presenting with initial nonshockable rhythms (PEA and asystole) from the SAVE‐J II study. We excluded those with external causes such as accidental hypothermia, drug overdose, heatstroke, trauma, drowning, or suffocation. Additionally, patients who exhibited a return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) at hospital arrival or ECMO cannulation were also excluded. In this registry, ROSC was defined as the presence of a sustained palpable pulse for more than 1 minute. Prehospital ROSC was evaluated by emergency medical services in Japan, who strictly follow standard protocols to provide advanced airway interventions and administer adrenaline to patients experiencing cardiac arrest during transport, as clinically indicated. We also excluded patients who received venoarterial‐ECMO after intensive care unit admission, those withdrawn after cannulation due to ROSC, patients transferred from other hospitals, and those with unknown initial cardiac rhythm or outcomes. ECPR implementation varied at the discretion of the clinician or facility protocol, as there were no standardized criteria for ECPR or ECMO management among the study groups.

Outcome

The primary outcome was survival to hospital discharge, which was used in development of a prediction model. The secondary outcome was a favorable neurological outcome at discharge, defined as a Cerebral Performance Category of 1 or 2. 26 We assessed the performance of the score in predicting primary and secondary outcomes.

Patient Characteristics and Potential Predictors

As we aimed to develop a prediction score to support ECPR decision‐making at hospital arrival for patients with OHCA with initial nonshockable rhythms, potential predictors were selected from those that clinicians can use at patient hospital arrival. We collected patient characteristics from the SAVE‐J II registry as potential predictors at hospital arrival, selected through expert opinion from our study group and based on previous studies. 1 , 8 , 11 , 12 , 13 , 27 These included age, sex, body mass index, past medical history (hypertension, diabetes, cardiac disease, stroke, chronic kidney disease), performance status, 28 location of cardiac arrest, witness, bystander, initial cardiac rhythm, cardiac rhythm at hospital arrival, arrest to hospital arrival time, signs of life (SOL) at hospital arrival, transient ROSC before hospital arrival, location of cannulation, pH, and lactate levels at hospital arrival. Signs of life were defined as the presence of gasping, pupillary light reaction, or Glasgow Coma Scale‐Motor Score≥2. 12 Transient ROSC was defined as the ROSC that lasted for more than 1 minute before hospital arrival but was not sustained upon arrival at the hospital. Additionally, we collected other patient characteristics that could not be used as potential predictors because they were not available at the time of hospital arrival, such as low‐flow time (from arrest to ECMO initiation) and the cause of cardiac arrest. This is because low‐flow time is determined at the start of ECMO, and the cause of cardiac arrest is diagnosed only after several diagnostic interventions following ECMO initiation.

Sample Size and Missing Data

We did not perform a formal sample size estimation during the protocol's development; instead, we followed guidance on calculating sample sizes, 29 with further details provided in the Data S1. Missing data were imputed using a random forest based imputation method with the missForest package. 30

Statistical Analysis

Selection of Predictors

From the clinically relevant predictor candidates mentioned previously, univariate logistic regression analysis was performed to calculate odds ratios (ORs) with 95% CIs for survival to hospital discharge. 16 , 31 Continuous variables were converted into binary factors based on clinically relevant cutoff values reported in previous studies. 1 , 13 , 32 , 33 , 34 Factors with a p value <0.05, significant β coefficients, and clinical relevance were selected to enhance the model's utility, following guidance from the literature. 24 , 25

Development of the Prediction Model

Using the selected variables, we developed a prediction model using a logistic regression model. The performance of the model was assessed by several metrics: the C‐index, Somers' Dxy correlation, Nagelkerke R2, calibration intercept and slope, and the Brier score. Calibration of the model was visualized through calibration plots. Internal validation was performed using a bootstrapping procedure with 1000 samples drawn with replacement from the original data set to assess potential overfitting and optimize model parameters. 31

Development, Grouping, and Evaluation of the Prediction Scoring System

We developed a simplified scoring system by converting the β coefficients from the prediction model into integer points, following methods used in previous literature. 16 , 31 The point values were assigned based on the magnitude of each variable's β coefficient, with rounding to the nearest integer to enhance clinical usability. To facilitate clinical interpretation and decision‐making, we stratified patients into 3 groups based on the total score distribution and outcome rates. We performed a decision curve analysis to evaluate the clinical utility of the scoring system across various threshold probabilities, comparing the net benefit of using the score versus treating all or no patients. The same evaluative approach was applied to the secondary outcomes using our scoring system. Data were presented as medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables and as numbers with percentages for categorical variables. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (version 4.4.1) with the “rms” and “rmda” packages (R Project for Statistical Computing).

RESULTS

Participants

Among 2157 patients recorded in the SAVE‐J II registry, 648 were eligible for this study (Figure 1). A total of 1331 patients (61.7%) were excluded due to an initial shockable rhythm, and 178 patients were excluded for other reasons. Of these, 86 patients survived to hospital discharge, and 562 experienced in‐hospital mortality (Table 1; Table S2). Across the overall cohort, the median age was 62.0 years (interquartile range, 49.0–69.0), and 492 patients (75.9%) were male. The most common location of cardiac arrests was at home (38.4%), followed by public places or streets (24.3%), and in ambulances (21.6%). The median ages for the survival to hospital discharge and in‐hospital mortality groups were 60.5 years (interquartile range, 49.0–69.0) and 62.0 years (interquartile range, 49.0–69.0), respectively. Witnessed arrests occurred in 83.5% and 81.0% of the cases, whereas bystander CPR was performed in 64.3% and 60.9% of cases in the survival and mortality groups, respectively. An initial cardiac rhythm of PEA was 83.7% and 73.0%, a shockable rhythm at hospital arrival was 25.6% and 16.2%, PEA at hospital arrival were 64.0% and 52.8%, SOL at hospital arrival were 41.8% and 18.4%, and transient ROSC before hospital arrival were 25.6% and 9.1% in the survival and in‐hospital mortality groups, respectively.

Model Development, Performance, and Evaluation

The crude ORs with 95% CIs for each potential predictor of the primary outcome are detailed in Table S3. After evaluating the statistical significance of various predictors, we selected three key variables for the prediction model (Tables 2 and 3): shockable rhythm or PEA at hospital arrival, transient ROSC before hospital arrival, and SOL at hospital arrival. C‐index of the prediction model was 0.701 (95% CI, 0.645–0.756), and the bias‐corrected C‐index by bootstrapping procedure was 0.696 (Table S4). The calibration plot indicated the model was well calibrated (Figure S1). Further details on the model's performance in the bootstrap procedure are described in Table S4.

We developed a simplified scoring system named the START‐ECPR Score, derived from a mnemonic of the selected factors (Signs of Life, Transient ROSC, Not Asystole Rhythm to ECPR). The bias‐corrected C‐index of the START‐ECPR Score was 0.696 (Table S4). Patients were stratified into 3 groups based on their probability of survival: low probability (score 0), moderate probability (score 1), and high probability (scores 2–3). The observed frequencies of survival in each group were score 0, 4.4% (7/159); score 1, 10.7% (38/356); and scores 2 to 3, 30.8% (39/130) (Tables S5 and S6; Figures 2; Figure S2). Decision‐curve analysis indicated that the net benefit of the START‐ECPR Score was higher than that of the all‐or‐none strategy at threshold probability of 0.05 and 0.2, indicating that the score is effective and useful in supporting ECPR implementation decisions when clinicians have a threshold probability between 0.05 and 0.2 (Figure S3).

DISCUSSION

Key Findings and Strengths

This study developed and internally validated a prediction scoring system (START‐ECPR Score) designed to support decision‐making for ECPR implementation in patients with OHCA with an initial nonshockable rhythm. The score provides a simple and practical tool for rapid assessment at hospital arrival. According to the START‐ECPR Score, patients with scores of 2 to 3 showed a higher survival rate to hospital discharge and more favorable neurological outcomes. In contrast, those with a score of 0 had an extremely low probability of survival and favorable neurological outcomes. Our study has several strengths. First, this scoring system exclusively focused on patients with OHCA with an initial nonshockable rhythm, a population that has not been prioritized as ECPR candidates due to their generally poorer outcomes and the limited evidence available for patient selection compared with those with initial shockable rhythms. Consequently, this study may offer novel and original insights for ECPR practices. Second, the 3 factors included in our scoring system—cardiac rhythm at hospital arrival, ROSC, and SOL—can be quickly assessed without the need for laboratory results. This feature makes our prediction score both practical for clinical decision‐making and easily interpretable when determining whether to initiate ECPR.

Comparing Previous Literature

Previous studies on patient selection scoring or criteria for ECPR in OHCA have primarily used basic information based on the Utstein template and laboratory data such as pH and lactate. 1 , 35 , 36 For example, the TiPs65 score, which includes 4 factors (age, time from arrest to hospital arrival, cardiac rhythm at hospital arrival, and pH value), has demonstrated good discrimination and calibration in selecting appropriate candidates for ECPR in patients with OHCA. 16 , 20 Furthermore, the validation study of the Extracorporeal Life Support Organization guidelines showed that patients who closely adhered to the 4 selection criteria (age, witness presence, bystander CPR, and low‐flow time) experienced better neurological outcomes. 1 , 15 However, these scoring systems and criteria, although effective for patients with an initial shockable rhythm, have shown limited utility for patients with an initial nonshockable rhythm. 15 , 21 Through our model development, we identified 3 factors that were more strongly associated with hospital survival in this population and demonstrated superior predictive ability compared with conventional prognostic indicators such as transport time. This may be because the variables incorporated into the SMART‐ECPR score not only reflect the overall quality of prehospital resuscitation and its physiological impact but also implicitly capture prognostic elements considered in previous literature. It is notable that although conventional predictive factors such as basic characteristics and laboratory data used in previous studies were not selected in our model development process, our model achieved reasonably good performance in predicting outcomes for patients with initial nonshockable rhythm, who require different approaches for identifying suitable ECPR candidates.

Regarding model performance, the START‐ECPR Score appears reasonable, especially when considering the intrinsically poor prognosis of initial nonshockable OHCA and when comparing prediction scores among similar patient populations. The LACT‐8 score was developed for use in patients undergoing venoarterial ECMO for refractory cardiogenic shock; however, the target population differs from that of ECPR. 37 In the context of prediction models for ECPR other than the TiPs65 score, the Resuscitation Using ECPR During In‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest score and the Lee's score have been reported. 38 , 39 , 40 Although both scores showed comparable or even superior performance to the START‐ECPR Score, their specific predictive value in patients with initial nonshockable rhythms remains unclear. We believe that the current performance of the START‐ECPR score is acceptable, particularly for patients with OHCA and an initial nonshockable rhythm who may still be considered as potential candidates for ECPR.

Clinical Implication

Our results demonstrate that the START‐ECPR Score could support in making decisions about ECPR implementation or patients with initial nonshockable rhythm who could benefit from survival and favorable neurological outcome at discharge. To facilitate timely ECPR activation, conventional prehospital criteria should be used to initiate team preparation, whereas the SMART‐ECPR score may support the final eligibility assessment upon hospital arrival. A score of 0 may serve as a clear indicator to withhold ECPR, whereas a score of 2 or 3 may support proceeding with ECPR. For more stringent patient selection, incorporating additional factors such as sustained ROSC at hospital arrival could provide stronger justification for ECPR implementation beyond the START‐ECPR Score alone.

In clinical practice, the use of SOL may present a barrier to applying this score, as the SOL variable had a relatively high rate of missing data in our study. Although SOL is included as a criterion in current guidelines, 1 research on SOL in the context of ECPR is relatively new and lacks a standardized definition across the literature. 12 Additionally, the absence of SOL in the current Utstein registry may contribute to this issue. The use of the START‐ECPR Score may require changes in clinical practice, particularly regarding the routine assessment and documentation of SOL. Decision curve analysis indicates that the START‐ECPR Score provides a net benefit within a threshold probability range of 0.05 to 0.20. This implies that the score could be clinically useful for clinicians who share the clinical values that implementing ECPR is reasonable for patients with OHCA with initial nonshockable rhythm when the estimated survival probability is between 5% and 20%. The decision threshold for performing ECPR involves balancing the probability of favorable outcomes, the invasiveness of the procedure, and its cost‐effectiveness. 7 , 41 As ECPR is a resource‐intensive intervention, acceptable thresholds for its use vary significantly across different health care systems and countries. 42 , 43 Given that the cost‐effectiveness of ECPR is generally lower for patients with initial nonshockable rhythms compared with those with shockable rhythms, 44 careful consideration of resource allocation is crucial. Implementing ECPR for patients with initial nonshockable rhythms who have survival probabilities between 5% and 20% might be seen as aggressive; therefore, this approach could be acceptable for clinical teams and health care systems that pursue an active resuscitation strategy in OHCA.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, we did not conduct external validation of the START‐ECPR Score due to the absence of an available database, which could lead to potential overfitting and optimism. External validation with independent data sets for the target population is necessary. Second, there was likely selection bias in ECPR implementation across participating institutions, which may have affected patient outcomes and limit the validity of our prediction score. This is because the decision‐making process was based on institutional protocols or the clinical judgment of individual physicians. Additionally, variations in the personnel performing ECMO cannulation might have influenced outcomes, though our data on this factor were not available. Third, our database from the SAVE‐J II study included a limited number of patients with initial nonshockable rhythms, resulting in a small sample size for outcome analysis. Therefore, we used survival to hospital discharge as the primary outcome, whereas the validity of the model was assessed based on favorable neurologic outcomes. Ideally, a larger sample would be required to develop a more robust model with better discrimination and calibration; however, data on patients with initial nonshockable rhythms treated with ECPR are scarce worldwide. Given this limited evidence base, our study provides valuable insights into this challenging clinical scenario. Fourth, the C‐index of our prediction score was 0.695 for survival to hospital discharge and 0.761 for favorable neurological outcome, which is not sufficiently high to always inform the best decision‐making. However, this reflects our focus on developing a practical 3‐factor scoring system that enables rapid assessment at the time of hospital arrival. Given the poor outcomes of patients with initial nonshockable rhythms and the challenges in identifying suitable ECPR candidates within this group, this level of performance may be acceptable. Therefore, clinical decisions regarding ECPR implementation should consider this prediction score alongside other relevant clinical factors, circumstances, values, and resources. Fifth, regarding model fairness, our study was based solely on Japanese data, limiting its generalizability to other populations with OHCA. Although evaluation of this prediction model in diverse health care settings is necessary to confirm model fairness, the scoring system uses internationally standardized definitions for both the target population and outcomes, which may support its applicability across different health care systems and medical settings.

CONCLUSIONS

This study developed and internally validated the START‐ECPR Score to support clinical decision‐making regarding ECPR implementation in OHCA patients with initial nonshockable rhythms. The score provides a straightforward and practical tool for rapid assessment at hospital arrival. Future studies should focus on external validation and evaluate the clinical utility of the score in diverse health care settings.
