Optimization of polyhydroxyalkanoate biopolymer production from lignocellulosic wood waste using statistical experimental designs
Authors: Mukesh Kumar, Amar Yasser Jassim, Rohan Samir Kumar Sachan, Deepak Kumar, Manickam Selvaraj, Gaurav Kumar, Manoj Kumar Sarangi, Anand Mohan, Arun Karnwal, Natalia Nesterova
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPlastic pollution, resulting from the persistence of conventional polymers, remains a critical environmental challenge that necessitates the development of biodegradable alternatives. Polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) represent an attractive solution, being naturally synthesized by microorganisms under nutrient-limited conditions. This study investigates the production of PHAs using lignocellulosic wood waste, specifically sal and teak residues, as an economical carbon source. Fermentable sugars were obtained via dilute sulfuric acid hydrolysis (10% w/v biomass with 4% v/v H2SO4), incubated at 120 °C for 1 h, and filtered to yield a hydrolysate containing approximately ~ 36 mg/mL total reducing sugars (DNS assay). The hydrolysate served as the carbon source in bioprocess optimization (optimal carbon concentration: 2.50%, equivalent to 25 g/L). Potential PHA-producing isolates were screened using Nile Blue and Sudan Black staining. The most efficient producer, Klebsiella pneumoniae strain DSM 30,104 (MK2023), confirmed through 16 S rRNA sequencing, demonstrated notable PHA accumulation. Process parameters—including carbon and nitrogen concentrations and Temperature—were optimized through Plackett–Burman Design (PBD) followed by Response Surface Methodology (RSM) using a face-centered central composite design. Optimal production was achieved at 2.50% carbon, 0.105% nitrogen, and 34 °C, yielding 5.7 mg/mL PHA after 72 h with 10% (v/v) inoculum. UV–Vis and FTIR analyses confirmed the polymer’s identity as polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB). The study highlights wood waste as a viable, low-cost substrate for PHA synthesis, promoting sustainable biopolymer production while advancing circular bioeconomy practices.Keywords: Biodegradable; Lignocellulose; Nile blue, Optimization; Polyhydroxyalkanoates, Sustainability, WastewaterSubject terms: Biotechnology, Chemistry, Environmental sciences, Microbiology

Introduction

Plastic waste has become a huge worldwide environmental problem because the common plastics are everlasting and undegradable; these plastics greatly contribute to the imbalance in nature and waste accumulation1. Many studies have been carried out in the search for a biodegradable solution to this issue so that long-lasting effects on the environment can be reduced. Polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) are among these materials, and their attention as potentially useful biodegradable polymers has increased2. PHAs, unlike regular synthetic plastics, are biologically degradable under certain ecological conditions due to microbial action and therefore do not leave an ecological footprint behind. These polymers are formed naturally by different microorganisms as intracellular carbon and energy storage materials when there is excess carbon available but limited essential nutrients3.

PHA production has been studied with diverse substrates, such as soil, urban sewage, sludge, and water bodies4. Agricultural residues and industrial by-products have been investigated as cheap feedstocks for microbial PHA production. The possibility of using wood waste as substrate has not yet been studied thoroughly, even though wood waste is known to contain a high amount of Carbon and is readily available. Wood waste mainly comprises lignocellulosic biomass, which contains high cellulose and hemicellulose; therefore, it can be hydrolyzed into fermentable sugars that can serve as a carbon source for microbial processes5,6. These sugars may then be used for the biosynthesis of value-added products like biodegradable polymers.

PHA production from lignocellulosic biomass has been limited due to its structural complexity and low fermentability. However, recent developments in pretreatment and hydrolysis technologies have improved sugar recovery from lignocellulose, making it possible to convert this biomass into precursors for biofuels and biopolymers, including PHAs7,8. The optimization of PHA biosynthesis from such substrates is very much dependent on the selection of microbial strain as well as process parameters like nutrient composition, pH, Temperature, aeration, inoculum age, etc. The traditional method of optimization, particularly one-factor-at-a-time (OFAT), is laborious. Hence, inefficient; therefore, statistical optimization techniques, factorial design, and response surface methodology (RSM) have become more important tools for systematically studying variable interactions toward establishing optimal conditions for PHA production8,9.

The study at hand is an exploration into the potential of using wood waste as a sustainable and cost-effective resource for producing PHA. It aims to tap into the abundant cellulose and hemicellulose fractions found in wood waste by transforming complex lignocellulosic materials into fermentable sugars that can be utilized for microbial biopolymer production. Additionally, this research addresses the challenge of the intrinsic limitations of lignocellulosic substrates through optimized hydrolysis and fermentation strategies. Advanced statistical techniques such as factorial design and RSM are applied here to find out the best combinations of process parameters. This study aims to develop an environmentally friendly and economical process for biosynthesizing PHAs from wood waste. Thus, it contributes toward managing plastic waste sustainably while fostering a circular bioeconomy.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Sewage water samples were collected using grab sampling technique from four locations of Jalandhar, Punjab (Sample 1 (L-1) location: latitude 31° 16.1126’ N, longitude 75° 35.7164’ E, Sample 2 (L-2) location: latitude 31° 20.2469’ N, and longitude 75° 40.1239 E, Sample 3 (L-3) location: latitude 31° 20.4499’ N, and longitude 75° 31.7189’ E, Sample 4 (L-4) location: latitude 31° 13.6414’ N, and longitude 75° 38.2179 E). All the samples were serially diluted and then plated on MSM agar containing 10 g/L sucrose, 0.225 mg/L Nile blue, and 15 g/L agar. Incubation at 30 °C for 48 h facilitated primary screening, where Nile blue was incorporated into bacterial cytoplasm during growth. Under UV light, plates exhibited fluorescence, allowing the identification of fluorescent colonies as potential PHA producers10. Secondary staining was performed using Sudan black B. Smears were heat-fixed, flooded with Sudan black B for 30 min, washed with distilled water, and air-dried. Smears were examined under a 100× objective to detect blue-black intracellular granules11.

Characterisation of PHB-producing isolates

Morphological and Biochemical characteristics

The morphology of the selected bacterial colony was examined using Gram staining. The isolate was identified as Klebsiella pneumoniae strain DSM 30,104 MK2023 through 16 S rRNA sequencing (GenBank accession OR362761)12.

PHA production

Production of PHA using MSM media

The isolates and positive control strains were subjected to triplicate inoculation in test tubes containing 10 mL of mineral salts medium (MSM) formulated explicitly for polyhydroxyalkanoate (PHA) production. These inoculated tubes were then incubated at 37 °C and agitated at 150 rpm for 72 h to ensure optimal growth and PHA biosynthesis. The MSM was composed of ammonium chloride (1.5 g/L), yeast extract (0.16 g/L), KH2PO4 (1.52 g/L), Na2HPO4 (4.0 g/L), MgSO4∙7H2O (0.52 g/L), CaCl2 (0.02 g/L), glucose (20 g/L), and a trace element solution (0.1 mL). The trace element solution consisted of ZnSO4∙7H2O (0.13 g/L), FeSO4∙7H2O (0.02 g/L), (NH4)6MO7O24∙4H2O (0.06 g/L), and H3BO3 (0.06 g/L). Both the glucose and trace element solutions were autoclaved separately, ensuring sterility, before being added to the medium.

Following the 72-hour incubation, the production medium was subjected to centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 15 min to separate the biomass. The resulting cell pellets were treated with 13% sodium hypochlorite for cellular digestion, followed by incubation at 50 °C for 2 h to ensure complete cell lysis. The cell extract was centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 15–20 min, followed by multiple washes with distilled water, acetone, and methanol to purify the PHA. The residual white precipitates were identified as PHA granules, confirming the successful production and extraction of polyhydroxyalkanoates.

For the synthesis of PHA film

The PHA content was dissolved in chloroform and then heated to its boiling point to facilitate efficient dissolution. The heated chloroform was carefully transferred to a sterile Petri plate or watch glass, ensuring thorough dissolution of PHA within the solution. After incubating the mixture overnight, a thin, transparent, light-colored PHA film formed, confirming the successful dissolution and precipitation of the polymer13.

The dry biomass of the extracted PHB was quantified in g/L, and the residual biomass was calculated as the difference between the total dry cell biomass and the dry biomass of PHB. The intracellular PHB accumulation percentage was determined by expressing the PHB content as a proportion of the dry cell weight (DCW = Total dry cell weight)14.

Hydrolysis of wood waste

Sal (Shorea robusta) and teak (Tectona grandis) wood-processing residues (offcuts/sawdust) were collected from licensed sawmills/wood workshops located in Phagwara and Jalandhar, Punjab, India. The wastes were obtained directly from freshly generated sawmill stockpiles (not from municipal dumps/roadside litter) to minimize soil, plastic, and other extraneous contamination. On collection, the material was transported in clean polyethylene bags to the laboratory, manually sorted to remove visible non-wood impurities, washed with distilled water, and oven-dried at °C to constant weight. Dried samples were milled and sieved, and the particle-size fraction of (0.5–1.0 mm) was used for acid hydrolysis to ensure uniform heat/mass transfer and reproducibility. The processed biomass was stored in airtight containers at room temperature until use.

The hydrolysate (filtrate) was subjected to preliminary carbohydrate confirmation using the Molisch test. Reducing sugars in the hydrolysate were quantified by the DNS method using a glucose calibration curve, and results are reported as glucose equivalents. The sugar utilization test was not used as a hydrolysate characterization assay; rather, it was performed separately as a fermentation/bioutilization indicator test to confirm that the selected isolate could metabolize the wood-waste hydrolysate as a carbon source15,16. Because the DNS assay responds only to reducing sugars and exhibits differential response to different mono-/disaccharides, it was used here solely to estimate total reducing sugar equivalents rather than to quantify individual sugars or total carbohydrates. Non-reducing sugars (e.g., sucrose-type) were therefore not directly quantified by DNS, and the reported values represent reducing sugars expressed as glucose equivalents. The Molisch test was used only as a qualitative confirmation of carbohydrate presence (purple/violet ring), not for quantification17,18.

Optimization using statistical experimental design

The Design of Experiments (DOE) or Statistical Experimental Design (SED) provides a robust and systematic framework for conducting experiments with multiple potential causal factors. Leveraging a screening design approach efficiently identifies the most significant factors, thus reducing the number of variables to be further tested in subsequent stages. The Plackett and Burman (P.B.) orthogonal arrays are particularly advantageous for this screening process, offering precise estimations of direct effects within a minimal design structure. In all experimental designs, factor levels are reported with explicit units. Concentrations expressed as percent are defined as % (w/v) for solid solutes (g per 100 mL medium) and % (v/v) for liquid additions (mL per 100 mL medium). Inoculum size is expressed as % (v/v) of an actively growing culture, standardized to an optical density at 600 nm (OD600 = 0.8). Incubation period is expressed in hours (h), and Temperature is expressed in °C8. This makes the P.B. design ideal for two-level factorial screening, especially when evaluating 11 variables impacting PHA production, as demonstrated in Table 1. Using PBD, the independent variables are coded into High (+ 1) and Low (-1) levels, ensuring that trials are conducted at a 95% significance level. This approach facilitates the careful selection of the most pertinent factors, the determination of appropriate factor ranges, and the avoidance of experimental conditions that could lead to failure, ensuring the reliability and validity of the results.

A quadratic polynomial model was fitted to characterize the response surface and find the optimum, and the design included replicated center points (A = 2.5, B = 0.105, C = 34) to estimate pure error and evaluate model adequacy20.

Qualitative analysis of PHA using UV-Vis spectroscopy

A precise and well-established protocol was employed for the qualitative analysis of Polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA). Initially, the PHA granules or films were dissolved in 10 ml of concentrated sulfuric acid and subjected to a controlled heating process in a water bath for 15 min at 100 °C. This thermal treatment facilitated the conversion of the polymer into a brown-colored crotonic acid, which is a key indicator of the presence of PHA. After cooling, the resulting solution was carefully analyzed using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 235 nm, with the sulfuric acid blank serving as the reference. No neutralization step was performed following acid hydrolysis; the cooled hydrolyzate was directly scanned against a sulfuric acid blank to detect the characteristic absorbance peak of crotonic acid at 235 nm22. The spectrophotometric analysis quantitatively determined the PHA content, specifically the Polyhydroxybutyrate (PHB) fraction, by comparing the absorbance at 235 nm to a standard calibration curve derived from known concentrations of 3-hydroxybutyric acid (Fig. 9)22,23. This approach provides a reliable method for assessing PHB in the extracted samples with accuracy and reproducibility22,23.

UV–Vis quantification of PHA (P3HB) via crotonic acid formation, measured spectrophotometrically at 235 nm using a standard curve.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) analysis

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) was employed to characterize further the PHA extracted from the microorganism. The FT-IR analysis was conducted using a JASCO FT/IR spectrophotometer, covering a broad spectral range of 4000 –400 cm⁻¹. This technique identifies specific functional groups in the polymer by analyzing the absorption peaks corresponding to various chemical bonds. FT-IR serves as a crucial method for confirming the extracted PHA’s molecular structure and functional group composition, providing valuable insight into its chemical characteristics24. This complementary analysis ensures the successful extraction of PHA and validates its purity and composition, offering a deeper understanding of the polymer’s properties.

Results and discussion

Screening of PHA-producing isolates

Based on fluorescence under UV light, 26 isolates (S-1 to S-26) from sewage samples were identified after primary screening with Nile blue staining, suggesting possible PHA accumulation. 19 positive isolates (S-1 to S-5, S-7, S-9, S-11 to S-14, S-16, S-18 to S-21, S-24, S-25, S-26) with blue-black intracellular granules were found by secondary confirmation using Sudan Black B staining11. With PHA accumulation exceeding 60% of dry cell weight (DCW), isolates S-3 (67% PHA content) and S-5 (60.6% PHA content) from Table 2 stood out as the most promising, significantly outperforming isolates S-9 (28.8%) and S-11 (21.8%)10,11.

These findings show that the sequential Nile blue-Sudan Black screening protocol is effective in quickly identifying high-PHA producers from environmental sources10,11. The nutrient-limited sewage environment that favors PHA-storing bacteria under carbon-excess conditions is reflected in the high incidence of positives (73% of Nile blue fluorescents). Trends indicate localized enrichment as top performers (e.g., S-3, S-5) cluster from initial isolates (S-1 to S-5); S-3 was deprioritized as a result of earlier exploration, advancing S-5 (Klebsiella pneumoniae DSM 30104 MK2023) for wood waste optimization. Scalable isolate selection for the production of lignocellulosic PHA is supported by this screening efficiency10.

Workflow for Isolation: Of the 26 isolates that tested positive for Nile blue (S1–S26), 19 tested positive for Sudan black. Isolate S-5 outperformed S-3 (6.75 mg/mL PHA but chosen for earlier work) with the highest PHA accumulation (6.05 mg/mL, 60.6% DCW; Table 2). S-5 (Klebsiella pneumoniae DSM 30104 MK2023, GenBank OR362761), sometimes referred to as M3/no. 3, was advanced for PHA film synthesis, wood waste optimization (PBD/RSM), and all downstream analyses14.

Extraction and quantification of PHA

A white precipitate of purified PHA was obtained and measured gravimetrically as dry weight per culture volume after extraction using sodium hypochlorite digestion (1–3%) at 50 °C for two hours, followed by successive washes with water, acetone, and methanol (Table 2). Isolate S-5 showed the highest PHA accumulation among the 19 Sudan Black B-positive isolates screened in triplicate (MSM with 20 g/L glucose, 72 h, 37 °C), surpassing isolate S-3 (6.75 mg/mL PHA but 67% DCW; previously characterized) and others ranging from 21.8 to 60.2% DCW. Klebsiella pneumoniae DSM 30,104 MK2023 (S-5; GenBank OR362761) was chosen for downstream wood waste hydrolysate optimization due to its superior yield, as its PHA content surpassed typical values for environmental Klebsiella isolates (e.g., 0.6–1.1 g/L or ~ 20–50% DCW under glucose/ammonium conditions). These findings highlight efficient primary screening using Nile Blue/Sudan Black, where PHA granules showed up as fluorescent/dark intracellular inclusions, allowing for quick identification of high-accumulators appropriate for inexpensive lignocellulosic substrates. The isolate was identified by 16s rRNA sequencing12. PHA film formation via chloroform casting further confirmed polymer integrity (Fig. 1)14, with transparency and uniformity indicative of high molecular weight PHB-like PHA13,14.

Molisch test

The purple ring in the centre of the test shows the presence of carbohydrates, as shown in Fig. 317.

Molisch test for wood-waste hydrolysates: (A) sal, (B) teak (Sangwan), and (C) sal+teak mixture; the violet ring indicates the presence of carbohydrates.

DNS test for reducing sugar

During the reaction of DNS with glucose under a water bath, the reducing sugar undergoes oxidation while DNS is reduced, resulting in a color change from yellow to orange/red. The absorbance was quantified using a spectrophotometer to determine the initial concentration of glucose/reducing sugar. The recorded readings were as follows: for sal wood, the absorbance was 0.722 at 540 nm; for teak wood, it was 0.741 at 540 nm; and for a mixture of sal and teak wood, the absorbance was 0.973 at 540 nm after one-third dilution. These values were compared to a standard DNS assay graph prepared using glucose as the sugar. Based on the readings, hydrolyzed sal+teak wood contained approximately 36 mg per ml of sugar content18.

Interpretation of wood waste supernatant analysis

These qualitative and quantitative assays altogether confirm that the acid-hydrolyzed sal and teak wood residues gave a fermentable, carbohydrate-rich supernatant which can be used as a carbon source for PHA production. The positive Molisch reaction indicates that carbohydrate moieties are present in the hydrolysate; DNS-based measurements show that mixed sal+teak hydrolysate has about 36 mg/mL of reducing sugars (as glucose equivalents), much more than either sal or teak fractions alone. Since this high sugar content is found in the combined hydrolysate, it was chosen to serve as the main carbon feed during further fermentation and optimization experiments, so that the availability of Carbon rather than limitation by carbohydrates would be what constrained biosynthesis of PHA. Also, this concentration of reducing sugars matches the carbon range being targeted by statistical designs (e.g., 2.50% carbon setting). This means there is a similar physical-chemical basis for understanding how changing levels of biomass growth and intracellular accumulation affect PHA in later parts of the study6,16.

Sugar utilization

The chosen isolates underwent a fermentation test to validate sugar utilization. A media solution was prepared with the following concentrations per 100 ml: 1 g peptone, 0.5 g sodium chloride, and 0.02 g bromocerol. This solution was then combined with wood waste supernatant in equal proportions. Initially purple, the solution changed to a light brownish color after 24 h of incubation, affirming the utilization of wood waste supernatant as a substrate, as depicted in Fig. 425.

Statistical analysis

To optimize the production of polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHAs) from a diverse group of 19 microbial isolates, a robust comparative analysis was conducted using the influential Design Expert 12 software. Among the isolates, isolate no. 3 emerged as the top performer, achieving a remarkable production rate exceeding 60%. The optimization strategy was carried out in two well-defined phases: the Plackett-Burman Design (PBD) and the Response Surface Methodology (RSM) modules, both integral software components. The initial phase employed PBD to systematically evaluate the impact of 11 critical factors on PHA production. These factors included Carbon and nitrogen sources, ferric citrate, Temperature, pH, inoculum size, MgSO4.7H2O, Na2HPO4, K2HPO4, incubation period, and trace elements. The software carefully defined the higher and lower limits for each factor, and based on these variations, it generated 12 distinct experimental runs for each isolate. This step ensured that the optimization process was thorough and well-structured, providing valuable insights into the relative contributions of each factor.

In the subsequent phase, optimized conditions were tested using a substrate medium derived from wastewater and wood waste. For each experimental run, 50 ml of this medium was prepared with precise concentrations of the factors identified in the PBD phase. The samples were then incubated under the defined conditions, enabling the evaluation of PHA production efficiency and validation of the optimized parameters.

This systematic approach, leveraging advanced software tools and carefully designed experiments, identified the optimal conditions for PHA production and demonstrated the potential of specific isolates like no 3 to outperform others significantly. The combination of PBD and RSM ensured the optimization process was efficient and scientifically rigorous, providing a clear pathway to enhancing PHA yield for potential industrial applications.

Fit summary

In the context of Response Surface Methodology (RSM), assessing the fit summary is paramount in gauging the model’s effectiveness. It is vital to ascertain how well the model captures the intricate relationships among variables. The optimization using RSM was carried out according to a face-centered central composite design (CCF-CCD) with three-level experimental conditions and replicated center runs (Table 2). This variant of CCD allows the fitting of a second-order (quadratic) model without needing any axial points outside the selected limits for factors. Thus, linear, interaction, and quadratic effects can be estimated efficiently within practical operating ranges27. As revealed in Table 7, the software opted for the quadratic mode, signifying the identification of a substantial quadratic effect within the model. This implies that the association between the variables is not strictly linear but exhibits curvature. Including quadratic terms enhances the model’s capacity to comprehend complex interactions, refining prediction accuracy. This automated selection streamlines the modeling process, ensuring critical non-linear relationships are appropriately accounted for in RSM20.

Point prediction values

In Response Surface Methodology (RSM), point prediction is a crucial technique for estimating the response value based on a specific set of input variable values. This approach is indispensable for achieving precise and reliable predictions, allowing researchers to evaluate the significance of various factors and optimize the response for either cost minimization or performance maximization. Point prediction facilitates process optimization by identifying the optimal settings for input variables and enhances the understanding of the complex relationships between factors and responses. It is instrumental in identifying influential variables and assessing their impact on the overall outcome. Informed decision-making, central to RSM, relies heavily on accurate point prediction. In numerical optimization, the model predicted maximum PHA production at 2.50% (w/v) carbon, 0.105% (w/v) nitrogen, and 34 °C, with a predicted yield of approximately 5.9–6.0 mg/mL. Under these conditions, confirmatory experiments yielded 5.7 mg/mL PHA, closely matching the model prediction and confirming the adequacy of the optimization21. These values indicate that the conditions outlined are optimal for maximizing PHA yield, highlighting the importance of precise point prediction in process optimization.

Validation of RSM-optimized conditions

To validate the predictions of the RSM model experimentally, three confirmatory experiments were performed at the optimal point (2.50% (w/v) carbon from wood waste hydrolysate, 0.105% (w/v) Nitrogen, 34 °C). The medium was prepared by mixing 347 mL of wood waste hydrolysate (containing reducing sugars at a concentration of 36 mg/mL as determined by the DNS method) with 153 mL of distilled water and then adding salt components to achieve a pH of 7.0 and autoclaving it. This included an additional supplement of ammonium chloride at a final concentration adjusted to provide Nitrogen at 0.105%, other salts (KH2PO4, Na2HPO4, MgSO4·7H2O), and trace elements according to MSM requirements. Flasks with a working volume of 500 mL were inoculated with Klebsiella pneumoniae DSM 30,104 MK2023 culture at an OD₆₀₀ equal to 0.8 in a ratio of 10% (v/v) and incubated under agitation at 150 rpm for 72 h14,19.

After incubation, cultures were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min and at a temperature of 4 °C, washed the cells, then extracted PHA with 1% sodium hypochlorite solution at a temperature of 50 °C for two hours, followed by centrifugation at 8,000 × g and several washings in sequence, starting from water through acetone ending up with methanol. The yield of PHA extracted was gravimetrically measured as 5.887 g/500 mL (5.7 mg/mL), which is very close to the model prediction value between 5.9 and 6.0 mg/mL (error < 5%), thus proving the reliability of this model; Fig. 10 shows a film made from PHA characterized by UV-Vis at235 nm and FTIR that confirmed its identity as PHB. This is an improvement by a factor of about two point three over runs that have not been optimized, which gave results equal to two point five mg/mL, indicating how well RSM can be applied practically for potential scale-up7,21.

In this study, Response Surface Methodology (RSM) was employed to optimize the composition of a microbial growth medium. Through rigorous analysis, RSM predictions identified the optimal concentrations of Carbon, Nitrogen, and Temperature for enhanced microbial growth. Specifically, the ideal conditions were determined to be 2.5% (25 g/L) carbon, 0.1% (1 g/L) nitrogen, and a temperature of 34 °C. These optimized parameters formed the basis for the subsequent production run.

For the medium preparation, a precise formulation was followed, carefully blending 347 mL of hydrolyzed wood wastewater with 153 mL of distilled water. The hydrolyzed wood waste, rich in sugar content (approximately 36 mg/mL), was quantified using the DNS test standard graph to ensure accurate sugar concentration. 1 g of ammonium chloride was added to the mixture to provide an adequate nitrogen source. Following the preparation, the medium was inoculated with a specific microbial isolate, and incubation was carried out at the optimized Temperature of 34 °C for 72 h. This incubation period allowed for the proliferation of the microbial community and facilitated efficient nutrient metabolism. After incubation, the microbial culture was centrifuged to separate the cellular biomass from the liquid supernatant. The isolated cells were then processed to extract Polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA). As illustrated in Fig. 8, the results demonstrate a successful outcome, with a final PHA yield of approximately 5887 mg per 500 mL of the medium, corresponding to a concentration of 5.7 mg/mL. The RSM-derived optimum (2.50% carbon, 0.105% nitrogen, 34 °C) was validated using a 10% (v/v) inoculum and a 72 h incubation period, yielding 5.7 mg/mL PHA21. This high PHA yield underscores the effectiveness of the optimized medium and conditions, providing a valuable contribution to the biotechnological production of this biopolymer.

The absorbance peak observed at 235 nm for both crotonic acid and PHB is primarily due to the presence of the conjugated double bonds in the molecular structure. In the case of PHB, this absorbance arises when the polymer is subjected to acid digestion, where PHB degrades into crotonic acid, which has a UV absorbance maximum at 235 nm due to its double bond in the conjugated system.

Fourier transform-infrared spectroscopy(FTIR)

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy is a powerful analytical tool to examine the functional groups within isolated Polyhydroxyalkanoate (PHA) granules. This technique works by measuring the absorption of infrared radiation by the sample, capturing the vibrational frequencies of the chemical bonds present within it. The FTIR spectrum provides critical insights into the chemical structure, composition, degree of crystallinity, and molecular weight of the polymer, among other properties24.

In the case of PHA, the FTIR spectrum typically showcases distinct absorption peaks that correspond to key functional groups, such as the carbonyl (C = O) and ester (C-O) bonds—hallmarks of the polymer’s backbone structure. These absorption peaks provide valuable information regarding the polymer’s molecular characteristics. Additionally, peaks associated with the polymer’s C-H, C-O, CH3, and O-H groups occur at wave numbers of 725, 1018, 1106, 1270, 1461, 1724, and 2938 cm-1, respectively (Fig. 10).

Analyzing these peaks offers essential insights into PHA’s chemical composition and structure. Notably, the presence of ester and methyl groups confirms that the polymer in question is poly(3-hydroxybutyrate) (PHB), a member of the broader PHA family. PHB stands out within the PHA family due to its unique chemical composition, which includes a methyl group (CH3) and an ester linkage (-COOR). These structural features are directly responsible for PHB’s thermoplastic nature, hydrophobic properties, high crystallinity, and its inherent brittleness. FTIR analysis, therefore, provides an invaluable, non-destructive method to characterize the PHA polymer, offering a clear understanding of its molecular architecture and functional attributes.

Conclusion

This study introduces an innovative approach to producing polyhydroxyalkanoates (PHA) using wood waste and wastewater as carbon sources, facilitated by the bacterium Klebsiella sp. This strategy presents a cost-effective alternative to conventional production methods and significantly reduces dependence on petroleum-based plastics, aligning with broader environmental sustainability objectives. The research successfully optimized key factors affecting PHA production using advanced statistical methods such as the Plackett-Burman Design (PBD) and Response Surface Methodology (RSM). PBD identified critical parameters—Temperature, carbon concentration, and nitrogen concentration—that most influenced PHA yield, and RSM further refined these factors to determine optimal production conditions. Under the optimized conditions of 2.50% carbon, 0.105% nitrogen, and 34 °C, the highest PHA production rate of 12.8 mg/ml was achieved, utilizing wood waste and wastewater as the substrate. This optimized method presents significant potential to lower production costs and promote an environmentally sustainable manufacturing process. For future research, it is recommended to explore the scalability of this process, focusing on large-scale production and its integration into industrial systems.

Additionally, investigating the use of other lignocellulosic waste materials and wastewater sources could further enhance the feasibility of this approach. Future studies should also focus on improving the efficiency of PHA extraction and polymerization processes, as well as evaluating the environmental impact of large-scale PHA production. By advancing these areas, this approach could become a key player in the transition to a more sustainable, circular economy, further reducing the reliance on fossil fuel-derived plastics.
