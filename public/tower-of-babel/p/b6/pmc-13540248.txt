Ligand-based directed differentiation to produce granulosa-like cells expressing steroidogenic enzyme genes
Authors: Hana Kubo, Lina Lu, Maya VanZanten, Janavi Thyagraj, Diane C. Saunders, Ruli Gao, Monica M. Laronda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ovarian granulosa cells are responsible for producing hormones and supporting oocytes through maturation and meiotic resumption. There is a need to generate granulosa-like cells (GLCs) from human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) to better model human gonadal development and to test the effects of exogenous or pharmaceutical compounds on the ovary. Here we report a rapid ligand-based protocol for differentiating hiPSCs into cells that express markers of the transient developmental lineages and steroidogenic pathway genes. Single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis identified canonical granulosa cell genes were expressed in a subset of cells and identified new genes of interest that were significantly associated with computationally modeled pseudotime. HSD17B1 was expressed in resulting GLCs but at low levels, suggesting an immature granulosa cell phenotype. The GLCs were produced using a simple culture method that could be augmented for granulosa cell functions such as sustaining oocyte growth. Producing GLCs through protocols such as this one is a first step toward designing large-scale ovarian endocrinology assays and developing personalized cell-based fertility and hormone restoration technologies in the future.This rapid protocol produced cells that express steroidogenic enzyme genes etoc blurb.Kubo and colleagues present a 5-day rapid protocol to generate immature granulosa-like cells from hiPSCs. Cells differentiated with inhibition of DKK1, a WNT signaling target gene, expressed gonadal ridge markers and FOXL2 transcripts and protein. Additionally, steroidogenic enzyme genes were expressed. A small population of differentiated cells were identified as expressing early-stage granulosa cell genes by single-cell RNA-seq.

Introduction

Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI), which is the loss of ovarian function prior to the age of 40, may result from iatrogenic, genetic, or idiopathic causes. Individuals with ovaries that undergo gonadotoxic treatments that put them at a high increased risk for developing POI are offered fertility preservation procedures such as egg cryopreservation or ovarian tissue cryopreservation (Wallace et al., 2003; Meacham et al., 2020). However, these technologies either provide only short-term hormone restoration or no hormone restoration at all (Dolmans et al., 2021; Tsui et al., 2024). While hormone replacement therapies support systemic restoration of progesterone and/or estradiol, they do not restore the comprehensive ovarian hormone milieu in a manner that mimics functional ovaries (Yu et al., 1987; Perrien et al., 2007; Chidi-Ogbolu and Baar, 2019; Roh et al., 2019). Granulosa cells, the largest steroidogenic cell population in the ovary, are a major target for patient-derived human induced pluripotent stem cell (hiPSC)-based treatment (Lan et al., 2013; Zhang et al., 2013; Lipskind et al., 2018; Gonen et al., 2023; Piechota et al., 2023; Smela et al., 2023). Gonadotropin-responsive granulosa-like cells (GLCs) would mark an improvement over existing hormone replacement therapies by more closely mimicking dynamic and responsive endocrine function in vivo. In addition to hormone replacement, there is a need for alternative methods to restore fertility through a bioprosthetic ovary transplant for patients that undergo ovarian tissue cryopreservation but are not candidates for autologous transplant due to risk of malignant cell reintroduction (Dolmans et al., 2013; Bak et al., 2024). For these patients, the follicle microenvironment would need to be constructed in vitro to support isolated oocytes. Production of GLCs at an early stage could support oocytes in the development of such an ovarian transplant.

Beyond fertility and hormone restoration, there is also a demand for ovarian cells for high throughput toxicity or pharmaceutical drug screening. Since non-diseased ovaries from young patients that represent the diversity required for such studies are not reliably available (Valle et al., 2023), a simple but robust GLC protocol that works on an array of hiPSCs representing diverse patient demographics would support these efforts. Platforms utilizing GLCs could help to identify therapeutics or environmental compounds that alter ovarian hormone production.

Some existing GLC protocols utilize overexpression of transcription factors to direct hiPSCs toward a steroidogenic state (Piechota et al., 2023; Smela et al., 2023), but this approach carries some degree of clinical risk because it introduces viral vectors (Fowler et al., 2020). Prior sequential and directed growth factor approaches follow all or some in vivo developmental stages, respectively, yet often require a more complex workflows using through 3D culture techniques and longer periods for differentiation (e.g., 12 days) (Lan et al., 2013; Kim et al., 2025; Liu et al., 2016; Hart et al., 2022). Here we aimed to build on a ligand-based differentiation protocol of human embryonic stem cells into GLCs by establishing a simple but robust ligand-based hiPSC differentiation protocol. The generated cells expressed the expected developmental lineage markers, FOXL2 mRNA transcript, and FOXL2 protein. Several genes were identified using single cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) at later points in the differentiation protocol that correspond to granulosa cells. Additionally, the differentiated cells expressed steroidogenic enzyme genes. This ligand-based protocol provides a starting point for future development for GLCs that may support oocytes, act as a toxicity or drug array screening tool and create cell-based hormone therapeutics.

Results

DKK1 inhibition effectively maintains FOXL2 expression in a ligand-based, GLC differentiation protocol using hiPSCs

Granulosa cells arise within the fetal gonad, which develops from the intermediate mesoderm lineage. A previous study that generated endometrial stromal fibroblast cells from pluripotent stem cells demonstrated treatment with CHIR99021, a WNT-agonist that works by inhibiting glycogen synthase kinase 3 (GSK3) and is hereafter referred to as ‘GSK3i,’ is highly effective in inducing the intermediate mesoderm (IM) lineage (Cheung et al., 2021). Additionally, a study using human embryonic stem cells (ESCs) revealed that the treatment of cells already in the IM-like state with a combination of bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4) and follistatin (FST) allowed for the induction of steroidogenically active GLCs after 12 days (Lan et al., 2013). Therefore, we sought to develop a 5-day protocol that combined these two previously published protocols for the generation of GLCs in a format (Fig. 1A).

Another study aimed to differentiate murine ESCs into fetal ovarian somatic cell-like cells (FOSLCs) that can support primordial germ cell-like cells (PGCLCs). The group first induced the epiblast-like state, then utilized retinoic acid, Sonic hedgehog (SHH), MEK inhibitor PD0325901 (referred to here as ‘MEKi'), BMP4, and epidermal growth factor (EGF) for two days, followed by a higher dose of BMP4 and FGF9 for two days to generate FOSLCs that express first Nr5a1 and then Foxl2 (Hayashi et al., 2011; Yoshino et al., 2021). The FOSLCs self-organized into a reconstituted ovarioid with PGCLCs and produced competent ova that gave rise to murine pups (Yoshino et al., 2021). We sought to test the latter half of this protocol with adjustments for the higher doubling time of human cells along with the aforementioned IM induction step, resulting in a 10-day long protocol (Fig. 1A). We removed the FGF9 from our cocktail as the authors of the original report (Hayashi et al., 2011) noted that this component did not alter the differentiation efficiency (personal communication).

Beyond these known factors for driving pluripotent stem cells towards the granulosa cell lineage, we sought to test the effects of inhibiting DKK1, which has been shown in mice to dampen Foxl2 expression (Manuylov et al., 2008). To do this, we added a treatment group in which gallocyanine (Li et al., 2012), a DKK1 inhibitor hereafter referred to as ‘DKK1i’, was included after IM induction. We also tested the addition of fibroblast growth factor 2 (FGF2) along with DKK1i as many of the other ligand-based GLC differentiation protocols included FGF2 (Lan et al., 2013; Gonen et al., 2023; Yoshino et al., 2021) (Fig. 1A).

Transcripts for POU5F1, a marker of pluripotency, are expressed in undifferentiated IISH2i-BM9 (BM9) cells (hiPSCs, 46,XX), decreased after treatment with GSK3i and were minimally expressed throughout the differentiation protocol (Fig. 1B). Interestingly, DKK1 expression peaked at day 2 and remained low for the remainder of the culture; this is likely because DKK1 is a WNT signaling target gene and the treatment of GSK3i amplifies WNT signaling (Fig. 1B). IM, which gives rise to urogenital structures, expresses PAX2, LHX1, and WT1. PAX2 and LHX1 were induced at day 2 of the protocol consistent with previous differentiation protocols that induced IM with GSK3i (CHIR99021) (Cheung et al., 2021; Takasato et al., 2014; Yucer et al., 2017; Morizane et al., 2015) (Fig. 1C). However, WT1 was not expressed until after day 6 in the extended culture. The addition of FGF2 did not affect these expression patterns. GATA4 is expressed in biopotential gonads and was elevated in cells that underwent differentiation by the end of both protocols (Fig. 1D). NR5A1 expression also approached significance by the end of the protocol with the addition of DKK1i but was variable across replicates (Fig. 1D). Lastly, we observed significant induction of FOXL2 on day 2 of the protocols, and this was best maintained in the 5-day long protocol with the addition of DKK1i (Fig. 1E).

To confirm nuclear localization of FOXL2, assess the percentage of FOXL2-expressing cells, and to quantify FOXL2 protein expression across timepoints for both differentiation protocols, we performed immunocytochemistry and Jess Western. FOXL2 was localized within 31% of the cell nuclei on day 5 with the addition of DKK1i alone (Fig. 1F–G, S1A–C). Additionally, FOXL2 was detected in cell lysates, most abundantly at day 2, and was best maintained in the presence of DKK1i (Fig. 1H–I). Based on the above data, we proceeded with the 5-day long protocol with DKK1i as our GLC differentiation protocol (Fig. 2A).

The 5-day protocol with DKK1i supplementation induced differentiation of other hiPSC lines into GLCs

To ensure that our GLC differentiation protocol was robust, we tested it on two additional hiPSC lines, STiPS-A3 (46,XX) and AN1.1 (46,XX). The gene expression patterns in these lines tested mimicked the expression pattern of BM9 cell differentiation using the 5-day protocol with DKK1i supplementation (Fig. 2B–E). Expression of POU5F1 decreased by day 2 of culture and genes important for intermediate mesoderm lineage designation (PAX2, LHX1, WT1) increased, though not all reached significant levels. GATA4 expression was greater at day 5 in STiPS-A3 compared to day 0, and NR5A1 expression was variable, though STiPS-A3 expression patterns matched the BM9 cell differentiation better than that of AN1.1 (Fig. 2C–D). Most importantly, FOXL2 expression peaked at day 2, as was shown in the BM9 line (Fig. 2E). Additionally, FOXL2 protein expression was observed in both STiPS-A3 and AN1.1 lines during differentiation (Fig. 2F–I).

GLCs were identified in scRNA-seq analysis of the differentiated hiPSCs

To further assess the transcriptional profile of the cells during differentiation, we conducted scRNA-seq on BM9 cells at days 0, 2, and 5 of the protocol (Fig. S2A). Following integration using Harmony (Korsunsky et al., 2019) with sample as the covariate, the cells from all three days clustered together but were grouped by day in the dimension reduction analysis, suggesting shifts in transcriptional profile during the differentiation (Fig. 3A). Although we detected FOXL2 in only a small subset of cells within this dataset (1.4% of total; Fig. 3A–B), this may be explained by the fact that FOXL2 is a transcription factor expressed at lower levels relative to other genes and therefore subject to high dropout rate in scRNA-seq data (Pokhilko et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2024) and is consistent with a reduced percentage of granulosa cells expressing FOXL2 during early differentiation (Wamaitha et al., 2023). Additionally, expression of FOXL2 target genes SERPINE2 and FST in more than half of cells at day 2 and still in over 10% of day 5 cells suggests that FOXL2 is expressed during the differentiation (Luo et al., 2024; Georges et al., 2014; Kashimada et al., 2011) (Fig. 3A–B, Table S2). Additionally, we confirmed the expression of several key genes that are known to be expressed in mature granulosa cells, such as PWRN1, GATA4, NPY, and PRXL2A, in our dataset (Luo et al., 2024; Urata et al., 2020; Kyrönlahti et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2019) (Fig. 3B). To ensure we did not have other cell populations that could arise from this protocol, we confirmed the absence of testis and gonadotroph markers (Fig. S2B).

Because the low number of FOXL2-expressing cells precluded further statistical comparison, we instead investigated potentially genes associated with GLC differentiation by focusing on the subset of cells that expressed granulosa cell markers PWRN1 and/or NPY. NPY is a neuronal protein expressed in the early antral follicles of rats, and PWRN1 is a long non-coding RNA whose shRNA-mediated knockdown resulted in lower estradiol and progesterone production by human granulosa cells (Urata et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2024). Since expression of these markers is linked to steroidogenic function in granulosa cells, we were curious to know which genes were co-expressed and therefore may contribute to functional maturation. At the single cell level with unsupervised clustering, PWRN1 and NPY were significantly overexpressed in day 5 cells compared to day 0 cells, with adjusted p-values of 1.0074E-93 and 2.132E-149 respectively. There were 151 genes that were significantly enriched by ≥1.5 fold change in NPY+ compared to NPY− cells and 108 genes that were significantly enriched by the same cutoff in PWRN1+ compared to PWRN1− cells, of which 72 genes overlapped between NPY+ and PWRN1+ populations (Fig. S2C–E and Table S3–S4). Of these overlapping genes, we noted several possible markers of interest: FGFR2, involved in sex determination; ZFPM2, which forms a complex with GATA4 to drive gonadal development; CD99, which was reported as a novel granulosa cell surface marker; and PITX2, which his involved in gonadal asymmetry in female chicks (Manuylov et al., 2008; Kyrönlahti et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2019; Trapnell et al., 2014; Shao et al., 2019). Interestingly, PITX2 is a target of canonical Wnt signaling, suggesting that the addition of DKK1i may be driving Wnt signaling between days 2 to 5 of differentiation (Shao et al., 2019).

To explore the dataset further, we conducted a pseudotime analysis of the sequenced cells using monocle3 (Trapnell et al., 2014), which approximately correlated to the time course of the differentiation protocol (Fig. 3C). Genes whose expression was significantly associated with pseudotime were identified and categorized into 7 different groups based on expression patterns (Fig. 3C, Table S5). Groups 1 and 3 were comprised of genes that were highly expressed early in the pseudotime continuum and then decreased in expression, while group 2 was comprised of genes that significantly increased in expression across pseudotime (Fig. 3C). Pathway enrichment for each gene set was conducted using Metascape (Zhou et al., 2019) (Figs. 3C, S3A–G). The top gene ontology (GO) term associated with Group 1 genes was ‘negative regulation of canonical Wnt signaling pathway,’ likely due to the GSK3i treatment between days 0 and 2 causing upregulation in Wnt signaling (Figs. 3C, S3A). Interestingly, along with the ‘Naba Core Matrisome’ gene set (Shao et al., 2019), we also saw enrichment of the term ‘reproductive structure development’ associated with Group 2 genes (Fig. 3C, S3B). Groups 4 through 7 were all comprised of genes that significantly decreased in expression across pseudotime at variable rates (Fig. 3C). Terms associated with RNA and protein metabolism, chromosome organization, and the cell cycle were enriched based on the genes in Groups 4 through 7 (Figs. 3C, S3D–G). Of note, ‘transcriptional regulation of pluripotent stem cells’ was enriched in Group 7 genes, suggesting loss of pluripotency during differentiation (Figs. 3C, S3G).

Differentiated cells express steroidogenic enzyme genes and peptide hormone transcripts

To predict whether GLCs produced by our protocol were of the mature granulosa cell phenotype that would make them responsive to follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and androstenedione, the expression of steroidogenic enzyme genes was assessed throughout the differentiation protocol. Out of the three hiPSC lines tested, two showed trends of increasing CYP19A1 expression, and all three lines expressed increasing relative amounts of HSD17B1. We also captured expression of AMH, with two cell lines achieving significance (Fig. 4). Additional comparisons between the three lines are shown in Fig. S4. Together, the data suggests that the differentiated cells have an immature phenotype that would require additional maturation and enrichment to achieve steroidogenesis.

Discussion

Here, we present a rapid differentiation protocol for generating GLCs from hiPSCs. The resulting cells express the appropriate lineage genes during differentiation, and express both transcript and protein FOXL2, a well-established marker for granulosa cells (Schmidt et al., 2004; Uhlenhaut et al., 2009).

Our goal was to utilize aspects of protocols in the literature that demonstrated the production of GLCs from hiPSCs to develop a simple culture. For the first step, we implemented an effective strategy for generating the intermediate mesoderm from an endometrium stromal fibroblast differentiation protocol (Cheung et al., 2021). We then developed two different protocols influenced by Lan et al., who utilized human ESCs to generate GLCs that were steroidogenic, and Yoshino et al., who utilized murine ESCs to generate FOSLCs that supported PGCLCs in culture, resulting in live pups (Lan et al., 2013; Yoshino et al., 2021). This narrowed down our experiments to a 10-day protocol and a 5-day protocol with differing ligands.

Our next step was to induce and maintain FOXL2 expression, a key driver of ovarian development. A previous study identified that knocking out DKK1 in mice resulted in FOXL2 overexpression in the fetal ovaries (Manuylov et al., 2008). Based on this, we tested the addition of gallocyanine, a small molecule inhibitor of DKK1, to our differentiation protocol. Our data demonstrated that the addition of DKK1i was particularly effective for the 5-day protocol in maintaining FOXL2 at the transcript and protein levels. We also tested this version of the protocol on two additional hiPSC lines, STiPS-A3 and AN1.1, to ensure robustness in differentiation across biologically unique cells (Fig. S4). While AN1.1 overall showed lower expression of target genes compared to the other lines in this study, it still showed increased FOXL2 protein expression at days 2 and 5. Finally, while levels of estradiol in our culture media were undetectable even after the supplementation with FSH and androstenedione (data not shown), we did detect increased expression of steroidogenic enzyme genes, albeit at low fold change, in all three lines tested.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis of BM9 cells at days 0, 2, and 5 of differentiation provided a detailed look at cell heterogeneity. The cells organized by the protocol timepoints in the dimensional reduction projection, and several key genes expressed by native human granulosa cells were present at days 2 and 5 of the differentiation protocol (Fan et al., 2019). Notably, FOXL2 target genes SERPINE2 and FST were expressed at day 2, suggesting that Wnt agonist GSK3i effectively induced FOXL2 expression. This is likely mediated by the stabilization of β-catenin, as it has been shown previously that Wnt proteins are dispensable for FOXL2 expression while β-catenin is required and sufficient to induce FOXL2 expression in murine gonads (Manuylov et al., 2008; Maatouk et al., 2008; Li et al., 2017; Habara et al., 2021). More broadly, expression of distinct groups of genes were found to be significantly associated with a pseudotime trajectory that paralleled protocol timepoints, suggesting coordinated cell differentiation. This analysis also revealed potentially novel signaling pathways and candidates for future study in granulosa cells. The variability in relative gene expression across the hiPSC lines can be mainly attributed to epigenetic. Epigenetic changes can significantly modify the characteristics of stem cells. Chromatin modifiers known as Polycomb group (PcG) proteins (PRC1 and PRC2) function as gene repressors to regulate embryonic development and cell fate transitions in stem cells (Aloia et al., 2013). When specific sections of the genome are hypomethylated, there is a smaller amount of H3K27me3 residues for the appropriate binding of PcG proteins, resulting in unwanted activation of those genes (Boyer et al., 2006; Surface et al., 2010). Kumar and colleagues reported a higher variability in the expression of lineage regulator genes (Neurod1, Otx2) than pluripotency regulator genes (Nanog, Nr0b1) across different colonies of a mouse embryonic stem cell line (Kumar et al., 2014). On average, Polycomb target genes had higher levels of H3K27me3 than non-Polycomb target genes. Moreover, reprogramming and excessive passaging can alter the extent of DNA methylation at specific imprinted loci, resulting in differential gene expression patterns in iPSC lines. Consequently, gene expression variability was observed in GLCs derived from the three stem cell lines (IISh2i-BM9, AN1.1, and STIPS-A3), despite the application of a standardized differentiation protocol.

Despite the lack of detectable hormone production, key steroidogenic genes were expressed in a subset of day 5 cells. Based on this, there may be GLCs that are immature at the end of the protocol but could produce estradiol given the appropriate maturation conditions. Additionally, both RNA and protein expression patterns suggest that GLCs make up a smaller population of cells within our heterogeneous population and that sorting to enrich for a desired cell type will also improve our ability to produce estradiol following this GLC differentiation protocol.

There are several GLC differentiation protocols in the literature, with some using ligand-based approaches and others employing a transcription factor overexpression approach (Lan et al., 2013; Yoshino et al., 2021; Gonen et al., 2023; Piechota et al., 2023; Smela et al., 2023). Many of these methods were successful in generating steroidogenic GLCs, ovarian somatic cells that support gametogenesis or oogenesis, or in some cases both cell populations. Many of these protocols for human pluripotent stem cells generate phenotypically very mature cells that are responsive to FSH stimulation and can aromatize androgens into estrogens (Lan et al., 2013; Piechota et al., 2023; Smela et al., 2023). We believe that GLCs reported in this study may provide functionality needed from a more nascent phenotype, such as supporting primordial germ cells or primordial germ cell-like cells (PGCLCs) through oogenesis and subsequent folliculogenesis (Smela et al., 2023; Chang et al., 2025). Currently there are robust protocols to generate PGCLCs from pluripotent stem cells, with some success in inducing oogenesis with the use of murine fetal ovarian somatic cells (Sasaki et al., 2015; Yokobayashi et al., 2017; Murase et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2019; Irie et al., 2015; Yamashiro et al., 2018). If the protocol reported here produces GLCs capable of supporting human PGCLCs, it would have large implications in advancing in vitro gametogenesis. Exploration of GLC steroidogenic potential along with future work on generating other cell populations such as theca-like cells will also move us closer to cell-based hormone replacement therapy that comprehensively covers the ovarian hormonal milieu and recapitulates cyclicity.

Experimental procedures

Culture and expansion of hiPSCs

All hiPSC lines were acquired through Material Transfer Agreements. STiPS-A3 cells were received at passage 12, AN1.1 cells were received at passage 21, and IISH2i-BM9 cells were received at passage 26. All cell lines were expanded and cultured with mTeSR1 media (STEMCELL Technologies, #85850) on Vitronectin XF (STEMCELL Technologies, #100–0763) as previously described (Schwartz et al., 2021; Siebert et al., 2023). Cells were passaged in 50–200 μm clumps using Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (STEMCELL Technologies, #07174) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and media was supplemented with ROCK Inhibitor Y27632 (ATCC, # ACS-3030) for 24-hours post plating at 5 μM. Split ratio for maintenance and preparation for differentiation was 1:12. Any freeze was performed using CryoStor CS10 (STEMCELL Technologies, #07930) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All cell lines were confirmed to be negative for mycoplasma using the Mycoplasma PCR Detection Kit (Abcam, #ab289834) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Differentiation of hiPSCs

Cells were passaged 24-hours prior to the start of differentiation at 10–20% confluence. The cells were differentiated into the intermediate mesoderm lineage using Advanced RPMI 1640 Medium (Thermo Scientific, #12633012) supplemented with 1x Glutamax (Thermo Scientific, # 35050061) and 8 μM GSK-3 Inhibitor XVI CHIR99021 (EMD Millipore, #361559–5MG; referred to as ‘GSK3i’ in text) for two days as previously described (Cheung et al., 2021). After this, the cells were further differentiated into the ovarian lineage using Advanced RPMI 1640 Medium supplemented with 1x Glutamax, 5 μM gallocyanine (Abcam, #ab145230; referred to as ‘DKK1i’ in text), 10 ng/uL BMP4 (R&D Systems, #314-BP-050/CF), and 25 ng/uL FST (R&D Systems, #669-FO-025/CF) for three days, with a media refresh two days in. STiPS-A3 cells between passages 16–17, AN1.1 cells between passages 27–28, and IISH2i-BM9 cells between passages 30–31 were used for differentiation.

RT-PCR

RNA was extracted from cell lysates collected at days 0, 2, and 5 of the differentiation protocol using the Quick RNA MiniPrep Kit (Zymo Research, #R1055) following the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was synthesized from the RNA using the Superscript™ IV VILO™ Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, #11756500) following the manufacturer’s instructions. RT-PCR was performed using the QuantStudio 6 Flex (Thermo Fisher Scientific, # 4485691). Primer information provided in Key Resources Table and Table S1. RPL19 was used as housekeeping gene. All data shown are normalized to day 0 as control within each cell line. Experimental replicate refers to each differentiation experiment, while technical replicates refer to replicates collected from the same experiment.

Immunocytochemistry

Cells were fixed at days 0, 2, and 5 of the differentiation protocol using Fix Mix and subsequently washed as previously described (Laronda et al., 2017). Cells were stored at 4C in ICC blocking buffer until ready for staining as previously described. The cells were incubated overnight in 4°C in the FOXL2 primary antibody (Abcam, #ab246511) at 1:250. The next day, the cells were washed three times in 1X phosphate-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (PBST) and then incubated with 1:500 anti-rabbit-568 secondary antibody (Fisher Scientific, #A10042) at room temperature for 1 h. Cells were washed twice in PBST, and counterstained with Hoechst (Sigma-Aldrich, #B2261–25MG) for 15–20 min at room temperature. The cells were washed once in PBST prior to either storing at 4°C covered until ready to image or were imaged immediately after. Of note, cells positive for FOXL2 tended to occur in clusters; to show positive cells, captured fields of view in Fig. 1F and S1A–C do not necessarily reflect the overall percentage of positive cells. Images were captured on the Keyence BZ-X700 and analyzed using Fiji (Schindelin et al., 2012). Any adjustments to the red channel were applied to all images as a flattened layer on Adobe Photoshop.

Jess Western

Cell lysates were collected at days 0, 2, and 5 of the differentiation protocol using RIPA buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, #R0278–50ML). Protein abundance of samples collected throughout the differentiation protocol was measured using a BCA assay according to the manufacturer's protocol (ThermoFisher Scientific). Using the sample with the lowest total protein abundance, protein input was normalized to 626.22 ng per sample. Protein separation and antigen detection were performed using an automated capillary protein electrophoresis system (Jess™, ProteinSimple, USA) using the 66–440 kDa separation module and 25-capillary column (SMW008) according to supplier’s recommendations. Proprietary in-capillary normalization reagent was included in assay plates. Signals for FOXL2 (ab246511, 1:50 dilution) were quantified as area under the curve using the Compass for SW software (Bio-Techne), which provides chemiluminescence peaks at computed molecular weights (McDowell et al., 2024).

Long-read single cell sequencing of time-series cells

BM9 cells from a single experiment were collected at days 0, 2, and 5 of the differentiation protocol using Accutase (Sigma-Aldrich, #A6964–500ML) to generate single cell suspensions. The single cell transcriptome sequencing on long read sequencer was performed by following scNanoRNAseq protocol (Shiau et al., 2023). Single cell suspensions were centrifuged at 450 g for 5 min, resuspended in cold PBS (Sigma #D8537) + 0.04%BSA solution (Ambion #AM2616) and passed through 40um flowmi filter (Bel-Art #h13680–0040). Next, single cell suspensions were sent for single cell capturing, barcoding and library preparation by following the 10X Genomics Single Cell Chromium 3′ protocol (PN-120237) using V3 chemistry reagents (10X Genomics). The barcoded cDNAs were amplified, purified and sent for library construction using SQK-LSK114 ligation sequencing kit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies) to generate PromethION long-read cDNA libraries. The final sequencing was run on PromethION flow cell (FLO-PRO114M) with one sample per flow cell at Northwestern University. The output data was base-called using Guppy (v6.5.7) under the super-accurate base-calling model. Raw sequencing data was processed using scNanoGPS. The resulting single-cell gene expression matrix was used for this study.

Single cell gene expression analysis

Single cell gene expression analysis was performed using ‘Seurat’ (v4.0). The quality control, dimension reduction with UMAP, unsupervised clustering and differential gene expression and plotting were performed using Seurat functions. After quality control, 6,896 cells remained (1280, 2512, and 3104 for days 0, 2, and 5, respectively). The data from cells at different timepoints were integrated using ‘Harmony’ method (Korsunsky et al., 2019) with sample as the covariate to remove batch effects. The reported values are adjusted p-values values.

Construction of single cell trajectories with scRNAseq data

We performed pseudo-time analysis using R package ‘monocle3’ (Cao et al., 2019) to construct single cell trajectories from gene expression data. We converted ‘Seurat’ object into a ‘SingleCellExperiment’ object to run ‘monocle3′. We then used the cluster_cells, learn_Graph, and order_Cells functions with default parameters to order cells. DEGs were selected by q_value == 0 & morans_I > 0.25 and plotted by R package ‘ComplexHeatmap’. Gene ontology enrichment analyses were performed with Metascape40 using the default parameters.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted on Prism version 10.1.1–10.6.1 (GraphPad). For data within each cell line, means were compared by ordinary one-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparisons test, with a single pooled variance. For Figs. 2, 4, and S4 showing data from all three cell lines, means were compared by two-way ANOVA with with Tukey’s multiple comparisons test, with a single pooled variance. In all graphs, asterisks indicate p-values: *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, ****p ≤ 0.0001.
