Nonclinical toxicity study duration in AAV gene therapy development: Evidence from industry survey supports adequacy of short-term assessments
Authors: Basel T Assaf, David Edwards, Shawn Jay Berens, Brian Lauritzen, Christelle Pierrot, Francis Fonyuy Tukov, Cristina Bertinetti-Lapatki, Manuela Braun, Kai Schaefer, Jessica L Lynch
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdeno-associated virus (AAV)-based gene therapies are emerging as transformative treatments for serious diseases; however, determining the optimal duration of nonclinical toxicity studies remains a key regulatory and scientific question. To address this, the EFPIA Gene Therapy Working Group surveyed 24 AAV gene therapy programs across 13 companies to assess current practices and the value of long-term (≥6 months) toxicity studies. Results showed that ≤3-month studies were sufficient to characterize the toxicology profile in 87.5% of programs that completed a toxicity assessment in a ≥6-month long-term chronic studies, with only one program identifying new toxicities in longer chronic studies with impact on clinical development. Common AAV-related toxicities, such as liver and dorsal root ganglia effects, were observed within the first 6 weeks post-administration. Longer studies were often driven by sponsor’s perception based on internal experience or need to assess durability, rather than regulatory requirements. These findings aligned with regulatory reviews of approved AAV products (e.g., Zolgensma, Luxturna, Roctavian) that consistently demonstrated the adequacy of ≤3-month studies for approved and marketed products. The outcome of this survey supports a risk-based, science-driven approach to in vivo study duration, emphasizing that shorter-term studies are generally sufficient for identifying relevant toxicities associated with AAV-based gene therapies. Embracing this approach can reduce animal use, accelerate development timelines, and support harmonized regulatory expectations for AAV gene therapy products.Keywords: AAV gene therapy, chronic toxicity, study duration, risk assessment, platform-related toxicity, nonclinical toxicology, regulatory

Introduction

Gene therapy (GT) continues to transform modern medicine, delivering tangible cures for untreatable conditions. Once considered a novel and experimental modality, adeno-associated virus (AAV) GT is now transitioning into a foundational platform within the GT landscape. To fully realize the potential of AAV GT, product development must transition from a case-by-case model to a more harmonized platform-based approach. A previous survey from the European Federation of Pharmaceutical Industries and Associates (EFPIA) Gene Therapy Working Group identified some trends as well as key development challenges in the current practices for designing and implementing nonclinical toxicology studies to support the development of viral vector-delivered in vivo gene therapies.1 One key challenge is determining the adequate duration of nonclinical toxicity study and the necessity to perform nonclinical studies with long-term follow-up (i.e., 6-month or longer studies).

Nonclinical toxicity studies are essential for evaluating the safety and potential risks of new drugs before they enter human clinical trials. The duration of these studies depends on the intended duration of human exposure, the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of the drug, and the occurrence of any adverse effects in animals.2,3,4 Long-term nonclinical studies may provide more information about the potential chronic and cumulative toxicity of the drug, as well as the reversibility and progression of any toxic effects.5 However, longer nonclinical studies have some drawbacks, such as increased animal use, costs, and overall development timelines. Therefore, the optimal duration of nonclinical toxicity studies should balance the need for adequate safety data generation with the ethical and practical considerations of animal testing. The ICH M3(R2) guideline provides harmonized recommendations for the duration of nonclinical studies largely for investigational small molecules based on the phase and duration of human clinical trials.2

For biotechnology-derived products, such as monoclonal antibodies, cytokines, and growth factors, the ICH M3(R2) only provides guidance with regard to timing of nonclinical studies relative to clinical development,2 while the appropriate nonclinical safety studies are determined in accordance with ICH S6(R1).3 According to ICH S6(R1), the duration of nonclinical studies for biotechnology-derived products should be determined by the intended duration of human exposure, the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic properties of the product, the occurrence of any adverse effects in animals, and the availability of relevant clinical data.3 The ICH S6(R1) guideline also states that repeat-dose toxicity studies of 6 months duration in rodents or non-rodents are considered sufficient for chronic use products, and that “studies of longer duration have not generally provided useful information that changed the clinical course of development.”3 However, the guideline also acknowledges that the nonclinical testing strategy should be tailored to the specific characteristics and intended use of each product, and that alternative approaches may be acceptable if scientifically justified.3 In a recent survey, no new toxicities or no toxicities of concern were observed in 71% of chronic toxicity studies from 142 unique monoclonal antibody (mAb) programs from 11 companies when compared to the toxicity findings observed in the short-term first-in-human (FIH)-enabling studies. More importantly, new toxicities were considered critical only in 7% of programs and led to program termination in 2%, strongly suggesting that a 3-month toxicity study may be sufficient for some mAbs based on a risk assessment model and concordant with the outcome previously reported on 23 approved biotechnology-derived pharmaceuticals, including mAbs and other recombinant human proteins.6,7

GT products pose some unique challenges for nonclinical testing, such as the complexity of the vector design, the biodistribution (BD) and persistence of the vector and the transgene expression, the potential for germline transmission, and the immunogenicity and tumorigenicity of the modified cells.8,9,10 Therefore, the nonclinical toxicity studies for GT products may require additional or different considerations than those for conventional pharmaceuticals provided in ICH M3(R2) and S6(R1) guidelines, the details of which are limited in the existing GT guidelines.9,10,11,12,13,14 The testing of small molecules and biotherapeutics in humans involves repeated dosing in clinical studies of increasing duration that are supported by a succession of nonclinical toxicity studies covering the length of the clinical trial. In contrast, for AAV GT products, the therapeutic premise is mostly associated with a single-dose administration that provides lifelong therapeutic effects through persistent transgene expression.

The study duration for AAV GT products is impacted by the peak and persistent exposure to the vector and the transgene expression, as these factors may influence the safety and efficacy of the GT.9,10 Peak exposure refers to the highest concentration of the vector and the transgene expression in the target tissue or the systemic circulation after administration, whereas persistent exposure refers to the long-term presence of the vector and expression of the transgene in target and non-target tissues or the systemic circulation.14,15 The program-specific BD in terms of peak and persistent exposure may affect the durability of the treatment as well as the risk of toxicity, immunogenicity, and tumorigenicity and may thus require different or additional nonclinical studies to assess the potential risks and benefits.14,16

The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and European Medicines Agency (EMA) guidance documents provide more detailed recommendations on the design and duration of nonclinical studies for AAV GT products, taking into account the peak and persistent exposure to the vector and the transgene expression and its potential clearance, as well as the characteristics and intended use of each product.9,10,14 The peak and persistent exposures to the vector and the expressed transgene are determined by nonclinical BD studies performed as stand-alone or in conjunction with nonclinical pharmacology and toxicology studies.14 BD assessment should provide sufficient characterization of the time-related changes in GT product levels to capture the time period of peak exposure and an estimate of the steady-state period or persistence.9,10,14 Subsequently, the duration of nonclinical toxicity studies for GT products are determined on a case-by-case basis to capture peak and persistent exposures for each AAV-based GT product.9,10,16 While determining the time of peak exposure can be readily identified, persistent exposure is more challenging to determine and may require a continuous process as more data accumulate.16 Defining the precise duration of persistent exposure is not always necessary (and may not be feasible for a durable lifelong transgene-expressing GT product) to understand the risk of delayed adverse events for GT products. It may be sufficient to determine the time point beyond which no new toxicities of critical concern to human safety occur in nonclinical toxicity studies. Taking this approach would help ensure efficient use of animals incorporating the 3Rs principle, reduce other resources, and potentially accelerate clinical development timelines, speeding potential therapies to patients. Ultimately, this effort will contribute to further harmonization of AAV GT product development.

Toward these goals and to identify common practices in determining an adequate duration of toxicity studies supporting AAV GT products, the EFPIA Gene Therapy Working Group developed and conducted a survey of pharmaceutical and biotechnology companies developing AAV GT products. The survey identified patterns of toxicology study durations supporting FIH dosing, factors that may have influenced study durations, and assessed the impact and added value of conducting long-term toxicity studies for AAV GT development. The survey outcome revealed that toxicology assessments in ≤3-month toxicity studies were adequate to assess the toxicology profile of the vast majority of candidate AAV gene therapies and that ≥6-month studies did not identify any new toxicities that were not previously identified (if any) in shorter-duration studies, particularly for the detection of AAV-platform-related toxicities such as liver and dorsal root ganglia (DRG) toxicities. These findings were concordant with regulatory documents on approved commercial AAV products.

Results

Company/institution demographics

The majority of the programs were from biopharmaceutical companies (15/24 responses) of which most (14 programs) had more than 10,000 employees and one had less than 1,000 employees (Figure 1). The remaining programs (9/24 responses) were from small- to medium-sized biotechnology companies, with between 1,000 and 10,000 employees (5/24 responses) or less than 1,000 employees (4/24 responses). No responses were received from academic institutions in the survey.

Summary of company demographics of survey respondents

Most survey respondents are from large biopharmaceutical companies with a gene therapy portfolio larger than 2 programs, and predominantly more than 5 programs, including 25% of respondents from companies exclusively focused on gene therapy product development.

Most of survey responses came from companies that have multiple AAV programs only in their portfolio, between 2 and 5 (10/24 responses) or >5 (11/24 responses) programs, and the remaining responses came from two companies each having 1 or 2 programs.

A quarter of the programs (6/24 responses) represented companies whose entire portfolio is focused on GT. These were primarily representing small companies with less than 1,000 employees (5 programs), with a split between companies having few (1–2) programs or more than 5 programs, and one large company with more than 10,000 employees having more than 5 programs. The remaining programs (18/24 responses) were received from companies with broad portfolios, encompassing modalities other than GT.

General program information

Most survey responses related to programs in early clinical development (16/24 responses). Investigational new drug (IND)-enabling programs represented the next largest group (5 responses). Three responses were for programs at the phase 3 stage of development. No data from registered products were submitted; however, such data are captured and discussed separately using publicly available information later in this manuscript (Figure 2).

Program features frequently captured in the survey

Most survey responses reflected programs in early clinical development with gene replacement mode of action predominantly by systemic or intra-cerebrospinal fluid routes of administration.

Twelve of the survey responses were for programs focused on serious and life-threatening diseases. Most responses (7/24 responses) were intended to treat neurodegenerative disease, followed by ocular (4/24 responses), otic (2/24 responses), and metabolic diseases (2/24 responses). Muscular degeneration was the focus of 1 response. The intended therapeutic indication for the remaining responses was not disclosed. None of the responses were intended for use in advanced cancer patients. Two responses were focused on pediatric disease (one neurodegenerative and the other was not specified).

Gene replacement represented the most common mechanism of action (16/24 responses), consistent with this modality representing the most mature AAV GT technology. Gene addition was the next most represented response (4/24 responses), as well as gene editing and gene suppression (2/24 responses each). Finally, one response indicated it was intended to modify secreted protein and another indicated dual gene suppression and gene addition (Figure 2).

Ten survey responses (10/24 responses) used the intravenous (i.v.) route of administration, two of which were dually administered with a second intra-CSF (cerebrospinal fluid) or intracranial/intraparenchymal route of administration (Figure 2). Four responses (4/24 responses) were using intra-CSF (intracisterna magna, intrathecal [IT], intracerebroventricular [ICV]) administration, and 2/24 responses were using intraparenchymal administration. Intraocular dosing was reported in 6/24 responses, and dosing in the ear (otic indications) was reported in 2/24 responses. Two respondents declined to reveal the intended route of administration.

A single animal species was used to conduct IND/clinical trial application (CTA)-enabling toxicity studies in 11/24 programs: non-human primate (NHP) in 8 programs, mouse in 2 programs, and pig in 1 program. In 13/24 programs, two or more species were used as detailed below. NHPs were the most common animal species used in the IND/CTA-enabling studies in 18/24 of the responses; cynomolgus monkeys were specified in 6/24 responses (Figure 3A). NHPs were exclusively used in 8/24 responses, and mice were used as a second species in another 8/24 responses. Rat was the second species in one survey response. In one response, mouse, rat, sheep and pig in addition to NHPs were all used. In the 6 programs that did not use NHPs, mouse was the exclusive test species in 2/24 responses and mouse and rabbit were used in another 2/24 responses. One program used pig alone and one used pig, rat, and dog. Overall, NHPs were the most used test species (18/24 responses) followed by mice (13/24 responses), rat and pig (3/24 responses each), rabbit (2/24 responses), and finally dog and sheep (1/24 response each), consistent with previously reported data.1

Species selection for IND/CTA-enabling toxicology

Species selected for IND/CTA-enabling toxicology (A) and pharmacology studies (B). Mouse was predominantly used in pharmacology studies, while NHPs and mouse were the species of choice for toxicology studies.

Mouse was the most used pharmacology species (23/24 responses) and was the exclusive animal species in 14/24 of these responses (Figure 3B). The predominant use of mouse is most likely since it is most amenable to developing animal models of disease compared with larger, more long-lived animal species. One respondent indicated cynomolgus monkey as the only pharmacology model used. Where two pharmacology animal models were used, a mouse model was accompanied with NHPs in 3/24 responses. For the remaining respondents, mouse was used with a second pharmacological animal model species, NHP in 4/24 responses, and rabbit, dog, pig, and sheep in one response each.

Chronic toxicity testing strategy

Survey respondents were queried to gain an understanding of the chronic toxicity assessment strategies used in nonclinical packages supporting programs to IND/CTA. The in vivo nonclinical packages contained both pharmacology studies with toxicity endpoints and/or dedicated toxicity studies (Figure 4).

Survey responses on the study duration for the toxicology assessment of AAV-based gene therapy candidates

Longest toxicity assessment included any toxicology assessment performed as a component of pharmacology studies, while IND/CTA-enabling toxicology assessment were studies primarily designed for toxicology assessment.

Most studies (20/24 responses) capturing the shortest duration toxicity assessment (Good Laboratory Practice [GLP] or non-GLP) were up to 6 weeks long (Figure 4). Seven of 24 programs leveraged toxicity assessments from studies of less than 4 weeks, and 13/24 programs used studies 4–6 weeks long. The observation of peak AAV platform-related toxicity, such as liver and DRG toxicity, is anticipated to occur at these durations.17,18,19,20 A small number of programs (4/24 responses) utilized 13-week study data as the shortest toxicity assessment. All four programs were for serious and life-threatening conditions outside the context of ICH S9. In 3 of the 4 programs, 13 weeks was also the longest toxicity assessment conducted. The data suggests that a single toxicity study may have been conducted to enable an IND/CTA for these programs.

The duration of the longest toxicity assessment ranged evenly among 13 weeks or less (8/24 responses), 26 weeks (9/24 responses), and longer than 26 weeks (7/24 responses) (Figure 4). Full to partial resolution of AAV platform-related toxicity would be expected by 26 weeks and longer.17,19,20 The longest IND/CTA-enabling toxicity assessment was split between 13 weeks (9/24 responses), 26 weeks (9/24 responses), and longer than 26 weeks (6/24 responses) (Figure 4). Many of these assessments occurred in the toxicology model (18/24 responses).

In two instances, the longest toxicity assessment was captured in the pharmacology model only, and in four instances, both the pharmacology and toxicology models included the longest assessments. In the four programs, where the pharmacology model was used for the longest toxicity assessment, the studies were conducted under non-GLP conditions. Two of the studies were positioned as pilot/dose-ranging studies, and one was positioned as a definitive toxicology study.

The assessment of chronic toxicity caused by persistent protein expression can be compromised by host immunogenic responses that can lead to loss of meaningful GT expression and/or tolerability. Prophylactic immunosuppression can be used to limit the impact of host-mediated immunogenicity. Most programs, however, did not use an immunosuppressive regimen (18/24 responses) during the chronic toxicity assessment. Of the 6/24 programs that did use an immunosuppressive regimen, 4 did so to allow a longer duration study and 2 did so without having an impact on the decision for study duration.

Survey respondents were asked what external factors influenced the duration of the longest toxicity assessment for these programs. The 2 most common external factors given were previous organization internal development experience (13/24 responses) and scientific advice given by a regulatory authority (10/24 responses). Other external factors cited included historical precedent from the literature (7/24 responses) and other factors (6/24 responses). Other factors shared by respondents were a novel route of administration, toxicologic findings observed at shorter duration toxicity assessments, and one answer was uncertain. One respondent noted that their organization typically defaults to a 3-month toxicity assessment unless specifically requested to perform a longer duration study by the FDA during the pre-IND meeting. Importantly, among the six programs that had toxicity assessments of longer than 26 weeks, only 1/6 of the responses cited scientific advice given by a regulatory authority as an external factor influencing the duration. These factors were also cited among these 6 programs, with previous experience being the most common.

Longer chronic toxicity assessment

Respondents were asked about factors that drove the decision to perform a toxicity assessment of 6 months or longer duration and kept the answer open for more than one answer per program. Most respondents indicated multiple answers, supporting the complex nature for determining the need for longer chronic toxicity assessment (Figure 5). The primary factors included the necessity to evaluate long-term toxicity not identified in shorter-duration studies (8/24 responses), assessing toxicity in studies primarily designed to assess long-term GT durability of effect (9/24 responses), and assessing recovery/reversibility identified in shorter-duration (<6-month) studies (6/24 responses), with an additional response specifically indicating assessing lack of progression of DRG toxicity. Transgene mode of action played a role in the decision of 5/24 programs as the kinetics of some transgene expressions or their effects of the pharmacodynamic parameter (e.g., gene editing) may require assessment in longer-duration studies. In certain instances, longer chronic toxicity studies of 6 months or longer were specifically requested by regulatory authorities (4/24 responses), of which 1 program specifically indicated such assessment in a non-GLP study. Additional less-frequent reasons provided by respondents included the use of novel capsids (2/24 responses), the need to assess toxicity in long-term pharmacology studies (2/24 responses), and the immunogenicity of the test compound (3/24 responses). Finally, 1 respondent each indicated uncertainty about their decision or based their approach on publicly available literature, or simply following the regulatory ICH M3 guidance.

Factors influencing the decision to perform ≥6 months toxicity assessment

Most importantly, our survey found out that no new toxicities were identified in such long-term studies that were not already identified (if any) in 3-month-duration studies in the vast majority (14/16; 87.5% of responses) of the 16 programs that had completed toxicity assessment in ≥6-month long-term chronic studies. Two programs indicated otherwise, in which both programs identified new toxicity in their 6-month studies that were ≤3-month-duration studies. Both programs involved a gene addition modality (e.g., AAV-expressing antibody). One of these programs is intended for pediatric neurodegenerative condition with intracranial route of administration and indicated that a central nervous system (CNS) finding of uncertain cause was identified in the 6-month toxicity study. This respondent indicated that this long-term chronic toxicity assessment was in fact combined with assessing pharmacology and durability of the AAV GT candidate in a non-GLP study that was eventually considered the definitive toxicology study, while the original IND/CTA-enabling study was performed for a 3-month duration only. The other program was an ocular program that observed ocular toxicity findings in their 3-month NHP toxicity study, and more prominent ocular toxicity findings were seen in their 6-month NHP toxicity assessment. However, the respondent provided additional details that animals were immunosuppressed only in the first 3 months of the study and that the more prominent ocular toxicity findings started to appear after cessation of immunosuppression regimen. Hence, one may hypothesize that if immunosuppression was not used at all, a 3-month toxicity studies would have identified the same findings and that the immunosuppression essentially only delayed what could have been seen earlier.

Of these two programs, the AAV program with intracranial administration for pediatric neurodegeneration indicated that the newly identified CNS toxicity of uncertain cause observed in the 6-month toxicity study was critical to human safety and resulted in modification of the clinical plan. Conversely, the toxicity findings observed in the 3-month study with more prominence in the 6-month study ocular program did not have any impact on or change to the clinical plan. Twelve respondents (12/24 responses) indicated that ≥6-month long-term chronic studies did not identify new information and 7/24 responses were not applicable since no such chronic studies were performed. One respondent indicated that cardiotoxicity was observed in a 3-month study but without significance to the clinical program because the findings were shown to be driven by species-specific immunogenicity shown to be lacking in the definitive 6-months study. One respondent indicated that the impact of the long-term toxicity assessment is pending. There was one case of conflicting answers, in which the respondent initially indicated that their >6-month chronic toxicity assessment did not identify novel toxicities that were not identified in ≤3-month studies but subsequently stated that their >6-month chronic toxicity assessment identified new information that was critical for human safety.

Critically considering the adequacy of detecting common AAV platform-related toxicities (namely, liver and DRG toxicity) in ≤3-month toxicity studies, 11/24 responses indicated that such toxicities were not observed for their candidate AAV GT product, of which 9/11 responses were associated with localized route of administration (6/11 ocular, 2/11 otic, and 1/11 intracranial) (Figure 6). Only 1/11 was for a program associated with i.v. route of administration, but this program was conducted in mouse that is known to be less sensitive to liver and DRG toxicities compared with rats and NHPs,21 supporting the lack of observing common AAV-platform-related toxicities in such program. The last 1/11 respondent did not share the route of administration for their program.

Assessing the ability for the detection of common AAV platform-related toxicities such as dorsal root ganglia toxicity and hepatotoxicity in ≤3-month toxicity studies

Ten programs (10/24 responses) identified common AAV-platform-related toxicities in their ≤3-month toxicity studies, and all of these programs were associated with systemic i.v. or intra-CSF (e.g., intrathecal or intracisternal magna) routes of administration, two of which (2/10 responses) indicated the detection of such toxicities within the first month post-vector administration. One respondent indicated that DRG toxicity has rarely been seen in their program with intracranial route of administration. One respondent did not provide an answer; another indicated that common AAV-platform-related toxicities were not identified in their ≤3-month toxicity studies. These responses support that a ≤3-month toxicity study is adequate in assessing common AAV-platform-related toxicities, particularly for programs with route of administrations that result in systemic exposure to the AAV GT product (namely, i.v. and intra-CSF routes).

If ≤ 3-month toxicity studies are adequate to identify common AAV-platform-related toxicities associated with the capsid or the vector genome, we sought to capture if the lack of pharmacological effect of the test item in the test species (e.g., lack of guide RNA homology in gene editing or lack of sequence homology for miRNA) influenced study duration (i.e., assessment was limited to platform-related effects and thus was shorter). Seven respondents indicated that their transgene lacked a pharmacological effect, of which only 1 respondent indicated an effect on the decision on study duration, while the remaining 6 respondents disagreed. For other 17/24 respondents, the product was active in the test species and hence the question was not applicable.

Commercial AAV-based gene therapy products

There are several commercially available AAV-based GT products that had completed the entire regulatory review and approval processes for marketing their products, and/or with publicly available documents from the FDA (e.g., Summary Basis of Regulatory Action [SBRA]) and from the (EMA; e.g., European Public Assessment Report [EPAR]). Information about these commercial products was not captured in our survey, and therefore we sought to capture information related to study duration through the review of SBRA and/or EPAR documents or any publicly available regulatory document, whenever such documents are available.

Glybera (alipogene tiparvovec) is an AAV1 expressing the S447X variant of the human lipoprotein lipase (LPL) gene, administered intramuscularly, and indicated for the treatment of LPL deficiency. Glybera was approved in Europe in 2012, and therefore, publicly available data are only available in the EPAR document.22 Toxicology assessment was conducted in wild-type mice in 3 studies of 90-, 105-, or 180-day duration following the administration of Glybera under two different manufacturing conditions.22 The 6-month duration was justified to assess the long-term expression of the therapeutic gene. Decreased body weight gains and slightly increased incidence of splenic lymphoid hyperplasia were noted, with the later reversing 3-months post dose. Glybera-related inflammation and muscle degeneration at the site of injection were noted and remained for the entire study duration; however, regression of muscle lesion was observed by 6 months post-dose. Additional reproductive toxicology and breeding study was conducted without clear statement on study duration. Nonetheless, no findings were reported in this study. Taken together, 3-month study duration was adequate for toxicology assessment of Glybera, with the 6-month study not identifying any novel toxicities.

Luxturna (voretigene neparvovec) is an AAV2 expressing normal human retinal pigment epithelial 65-kDa protein gene, administered subretinally, and indicated for the treatment of Leber congenital amaurosis. Luxturna was approved by FDA and EMA in 2017 and 2018, respectively. Several single-dose and repeat-dose toxicology studies were conducted in normal-sighted dogs, Briard (RPE65−/−) mutant dogs, and normal-sighted NHPs following intra-ocular (subretinal or intravitreal) vector administration.23,24 Intraocular inflammation was the predominant finding mainly at the injection site, with limited to no findings systemically. Two GLP-compliant toxicity studies were conducted at 3-week or 3-month duration in dogs. Ocular inflammation and perivascular infiltration in the brain were observed at both the 3-week and 3-month studies. Additional longer-duration non-GLP toxicity studies were conducted in NHPs and dogs primarily to assess dose re-administration within the same eye or to the contralateral eye up to ∼7 months post-vector administration. No significant adverse ocular effects were observed in this study, and findings were generally consistent with those observed in shorter-duration studies.

Zolgensma (onasemnogene abeparvovec) is an AAV9-expressing human survival of motor neuron 1 (SMN1) gene, administered i.v., and indicated for the treatment of pediatric patients with infantile-onset spinal muscular atrophy with confirmed biallelic mutations in SMN1 gene. It was approved in the United States in 2019 and in the European Union in 2020. Two 12-week GLP-compliant toxicity studies were conducted in neonatal FVB/NJ mice following i.v. systemic administration of clinical vector lots with 3- and 6/7-week interim necropsy time points.25,26 Test article-related findings primarily in the heart and liver were observed as early as week 3 and generally decreased in severity by week 12. Atrial thrombi were observed in highest dose groups, and mice died prematurely at weeks 6/7. Hepatocellular degeneration was observed microscopically and was associated with increased alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and/or creatine kinase through 12 weeks. Three additional supporting ∼6-month toxicity studies in neonatal and adult FVB/NJ mice as well as in neonatal cynomolgus monkeys using scAAV9.CBA.SMN vector lot manufactured using a different process from that of clinical vector presented with a different toxicology profile as animals lacked the toxicity findings observed in the GLP toxicity studies with the clinical vector lots despite the long-term ∼6-month study duration. Additional non-GLP studies in the EPAR document are described, in which research-grade vector lot was administered via ICV or IT routes in mice and NHPs, respectively, and animals were followed for 12 or 24 weeks. No adverse findings were identified in the mouse study, and transient changes in ALT and platelets were reported earlier and resolved by 6-month post-vector administration in the NHP study. Taken together, toxicity study durations of 3 and/or 6 weeks were adequate to identify the toxicology profile of onasemnogene abeparvovec and studies of >3-month duration did not identify any novel toxicity.

Elevidys (delandistrogene moxeparvovec) is an rAAVrh74 expressing human micro-dystrophin gene, administered i.v., and indicated for the treatment of ambulatory patients with Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) with a confirmed mutation in the DMD gene. Elevidys was approved in the United States in 2023. Four primary studies and 1 supporting toxicology study of 4-, 12/13-, or 24-week duration were conducted in C57BL/6J and mdx mice to support delandistrogene moxeparvovec.27 Possible test article findings were observed in the liver and/or the brain of several mice in several studies; however, all findings were identified at the 12/13-week time points, and no new findings were reported in the long-term 24-week study.

Roctavian (valoctocogene roxaparvovec) is an AAV5-expressing factor VIII gene, administered i.v., and indicated for the treatment of adults with severe hemophilia A. It was approved in the European Union in 2022 and in the United States in 2023. A 6-month and a 3-month toxicology study in adult mice and adult NHPs, respectively, supported valoctocogene roxaparvovec application.28,29 In the 6-month mouse study, adverse Roctavian-related findings related to hemorrhage mainly in the heart, lungs, and epididymis as well as mortality were observed through the 6 months duration. However, these findings were considered mouse specific with no clinical impact on hemophilia patients as they were attributed to anti-hFVIII antibodies cross-reacting and inhibiting the murine endogenous FVIII and resulting in bleeding phenotype and subsequent visceral lesions.29 In the 3-month monkey study, findings were limited to prolongation of activated partial thromboplastin time and minimal to mild mononuclear/mixed infiltration in the lung at 3 months. Germline transmission was evaluated in a 50-day study, and no Roctavian-related adverse changes on mating rates, maternal behavior, and fertility indices were observed. Vector genome integration was assessed in a dedicated 13- and 26-week study in NHPs, and liver was examined for integration site (IS) analysis. No evidence of clonal expansion or enrichment of insertion sites was observed at either time points, and the IS profiles were stable between the 13- and 26-week sacrifice time points, with acceptable sponsor’s justification “for the study duration based on time of assembly/presence of linear and episomal AAV DNA with respect to time of transduction/vector administration” (from FDA Pharmacology-Toxicology Review document).28 Overall, a 3-month duration was adequate to assess the risk that might be associated with Roctavian, and the longer-term 6-month toxicity study in mice did not identify novel toxicities.

Beqvez/Durveqtix (fidanacogene elaparvovec) is an AAVRh74var-expressing factor IX gene, administered i.v., and indicated for the treatment of adults with moderate to severe hemophilia B. It was approved in the United States and the European Union in 2024, and several pharmacology and toxicology studies are reported in FDA and EMA documents.30,31 In the EPAR, 3-month GLP toxicity study in cynomolgus monkeys with AAVRh74var-hFIX19-R338L (a closely related predecessor product) was used to support clinical trials and registration. However, a 2-year vector integration study with fidanacogene elaparvovec was conducted in cynomolgus monkeys (as well as in neonatal and juvenile hemophilia B dogs with ≥12-month follow-up) to evaluate the integration profile and risk of hepatocellular carcinoma. No test article-related toxicities were observed in any of these studies. Toxicity assessment was also conducted in a 12-month male mouse study using AAVRh74var-FIX-WT or AAVRh74var-FIX19-R338L, and unscheduled deaths occurred in 14 animals with likely test article-related anatomic pathology findings in the brain and/or skin due to exacerbations following tissue/vascular injury. These findings were considered mouse-specific due to repeat submandibular blood collection procedures, and administration of the product to healthy animals with endogenous factor IX (FIX) activity and had no impact on human risk assessment. Taken together, the 3-month toxicity study in monkeys was adequate to support fidanacogene elaparvovec, and the additional 12-month mouse study, ≥12-month dog studies, and 2-year monkey study did not demonstrate any added value in the detection of novel toxicities that were not addressed in the shorter 3-month study.30,31

Hemgenix (etranacogene dezaparvovec) is an AAV-expressing factor IX gene, administered i.v., and indicated for the treatment of adults with hemophilia B. Hemgenix was approved in the United States and the European Union in 2022. Two studies in healthy adult mice and NHPs assessed the toxicology of Hemgenix.32,33 Study durations were not clearly stated in the SBRA, but any study outcome was reported in the context of 13-week time point.32 In the EPAR, the same studies were specified with 6-month duration. One of 10 mice developed pulmonary thrombi likely due to the expression of FIX in healthy mice (i.e., not relevant to hemophilia B patient), and transient elevation of AST and ALT on days 2–4 that fully returned to normal from day 8 onward with no adverse findings was reported in the monkey study.33 The risk of germline transmission of the Hemgenix predecessor was evaluated in healthy male mice. These mice were then mated with healthy naive females on day 6 post-dose and subsequently euthanized 20 days post-dose. Vector genome was detected in male reproductive tissues and sperm, but with no adverse effects on mating or fertility indices. IS analysis was completed in the livers of healthy mice and NHPs 6 months post-Hemgenix predecessor administration, and the IS profile indicated no predilection to specific ISs or genes associated with malignant transformation in humans. Consequently, general toxicology of Hemgenix was adequately assessed within 3-month duration and the longer 6-month studies did not demonstrate any novel toxicities.

Upstaza/Kebilidi (eladocagene exuparvovec) is an AAV2-expressing human dopa decarboxylase gene, administered intraparenchymally (IP) into the putamen of the brain and indicated for the treatment of adult and pediatric patients with aromatic L-amino acid decarboxylase deficiency. It was approved in the European Union in 2022 and in the United States in 2024. In the SBRA document, toxicology studies of 6-month durations were conducted in rats with no significant adverse findings noted.34 A 30-day toxicology study in NHPs was specifically tailored to evaluate the safety of 0.001% poloxamer 188 without identifying any findings unique to this variable.34 More details are provided in the EPAR, in which the details of another 30-day toxicity study in rat was shared, as well as a rat study (without stating study duration) designed to assess the effect of poloxamer 188 alongside the NHP study already mentioned in the SBRA.35 In the 30-day and 6-month rat studies shared in the EPAR, eladocagene exuparvovec was tolerated with no obvious signs of toxicity. Finally, a 4-week study with multiple routes of administration (IP, ICV, and IT) was conducted in juvenile monkeys as part of a pediatric investigation plan, and eladocagene exuparvovec was well tolerated. The good tolerance and lack of outcomes difference in both 30-day and 6-month studies in rats, with the subsequent request for a 4-week study in juvenile monkeys, likely indicate that the short-term ∼1-month study durations were adequate to identify any novel toxicities associated with eladocagene exuparvovec, without the need for a long-term 6-month follow-up.

Discussion

The goal of this survey was to capture the current practice in AAV-based GT product development in relation to determining study duration for nonclinical toxicology assessment. We also explored factors influencing the determination of toxicology study duration and the outcomes and impacts of such decision on human risk assessment and clinical study design, with a major emphasis on the value of chronic toxicology studies that are 6 months or longer in duration. The ultimate goal is to bring harmonization for regulators and GT product developers on the expectations for study durations adequate for assessing the toxicology profiles of AAV GT products, which in turn would reduce the use of animals, resources and financial burdens, and product development life cycles to bring promising medicines to patients with urgent needs. The primary finding gained from this survey is that toxicology assessment in ≤3-month toxicity studies was adequate to assess the toxicology profile of the vast majority of candidate AAV gene therapies for programs (87.5% of responses; 14/16 programs that had completed toxicity assessment in ≥6-month long-term chronic studies) and that ≥6-month longer-term chronic studies did not identify any new toxicities that were not previously identified (if any) in ≤3-month-duration studies. This was particularly evident for the detection of AAV-platform-related toxicities such as liver and DRG toxicities in nonclinical species, suggesting that shorter studies may be sufficient for detecting common AAV-related toxicities in most cases.

Insights from this survey were drawn from 24 unique industry-sponsored programs equally represented by small biotechnology (40%) and large biopharmaceutical (60%) companies, of which 25% of programs came from companies that identified as a GT-only company with the entire portfolio focus on GT; highlighting both the diversity and convergence of strategies used across the field. Approximately 46% of the programs came from companies that have more than 5 programs in their portfolio, and an additional 42% from companies with 2–5 programs in their portfolio, making up 88% response rate from companies experienced with AAV-based GT product development.

Toxicity assessments of ≤6 weeks captured peak AAV-related toxicities for the majority of programs (20/24; 83%), consistent with published data of early onset of AAV platform-related toxicities such as liver and DRG toxicities, to occur during such period. A minority of programs (4/24) used 13-week studies as their shortest duration, typically in the context of serious diseases outside ICH S9 guidance. The longest toxicity assessments were evenly distributed across 13 weeks, 26 weeks, and >26 weeks, indicating no clear consensus on optimal toxicology study duration. This variability most likely reflected differing previous internal knowledge and experience (13/24) and regulatory expectations (10/24). Notably, longer studies were often conducted in toxicology models and under GLP conditions, reinforcing their role in regulatory submissions.

The decision to conduct longer-term studies (≥6 months) was complex and multifactorial. The sponsor’s perception, likely influenced from developing other therapeutic modalities such as small molecules and monoclonal antibodies, for the need of longer-term toxicity studies particularly in association with demonstrated durable transgene expression was the most cited rationale (two-thirds of responses). The need to assess reversibility of findings and transgene-specific effects were prominent drivers. Surprisingly, specific request by regulatory authorities for longer chronic toxicity studies of 6 months or longer was infrequently cited (4/24 responses).

There were only two instances of programs (2/24 programs surveyed) that indicated identifying novel toxicities in longer-term studies and involved a gene addition modality (e.g., AAV-expressing antibody). However, one program indicated that the observed CNS findings were of uncertain relationship to the GT tested and the other program shared that ocular toxicity did in fact occur within the first 3 months in immunosuppressed monkeys but the inflammation exacerbated 6 months post-dose primarily due to cessation of immunosuppression in these animals, arguably highlighting that ≤3-month toxicity study without immunosuppression would have been adequate in identifying the toxicology profile of such candidate. Most critically, only in the program with CNS findings (one program among all 24 programs surveyed) led to a modification of the clinical plan, underscoring the potential value of extended assessments in specific contexts.

The data strongly support the adequacy of ≤3-month toxicity studies for identifying common AAV platform-related toxicities, particularly in programs with routes of administration associated with higher systemic exposures to the AAV vectors (i.v. or intra-CSF route). The absence of such toxicities in localized administration routes (ocular, otic, intracranial) further supports a risk-based approach to study design. The limited use of immunosuppressive regimens (6/24 programs) during chronic toxicity assessments suggests a general preference for evaluating the intrinsic safety profile of the GT product. However, the impact of immunogenicity on study outcomes, particularly in longer-term studies, remains a critical consideration. The ocular program that observed delayed toxicity following cessation of immunosuppression illustrates the complex interplay between immune modulation and toxicity manifestation.

The survey outcome is concordant and reinforced with information from publicly available regulatory documents for approved AAV-based GT commercial products, and shorter-term toxicity studies are generally sufficient to support regulatory approval. For example, Glybera, Luxturna, and Zolgensma all underwent 3-month or shorter GLP-compliant studies, with longer-term studies (up to 6 months) not revealing novel toxicities. In the case of Zolgensma, toxicities were observed early and diminished over time, and longer studies using different vector lots did not replicate earlier findings, suggesting lot-specific effects rather than duration-dependent findings.

Similarly, Elevidys and Roctavian were supported by 12- to 13-week studies, with no new findings in 24- or 26-week studies. Roctavian’s longer-term mouse study revealed findings attributed to species-specific responses, not relevant to human risk. Beqvez/Durveqtix and Hemgenix also demonstrated that 3-month studies in NHPs were sufficient, with longer-term rodent studies offering no additional safety insights. Upstaza/Kebilidi, despite conducting 6-month studies, showed no added value over shorter 30-day studies, and subsequent pediatric studies were limited to 4 weeks.

These regulatory precedents provide strong external validation for the survey findings. Across multiple approved products across the United States and/or Europe, longer-term studies rarely identified new toxicities, and when they did, the findings were often species specific or attributable to study design variables rather than intrinsic product risk. This supports a risk-based, indication- and route-specific approach to study duration, with ≤3-month studies being generally adequate for most AAV GT programs. These observations are additionally consistent with the clinical experience observed in clinical trials and post-marketing monitoring, in which most safety signals (elevated liver enzymes and hepatoxicities, cardiotoxicity and elevated cardiac troponin, thrombotic microangiopathy and acute hemolytic uremic syndrome, intraocular inflammation, acute respiratory distress syndrome, and fatalities) associated with AAV products are observed within the first 3 months following vector administration.17,36,37

Conclusion

This study underscores the importance of a flexible, science-driven approach to chronic toxicity assessment in AAV GT development. Toxicology assessment in ≤3-month studies appears sufficient for detecting common toxicities in most cases, but longer-term assessments may be warranted in select instances. Continued dialog between industry, regulators, and the scientific community will be essential to refine best practices and ensure patient safety while enabling innovation.

Material and methods

The survey was developed by the EFPIA Gene and Cell Therapy Working Group to obtain details on AAV-based GT product properties and identify factors that may influence decisions on study duration, key toxicities identified in each nonclinical species, and the impact of the data on clinical study designs in order to identify common practices in determining the duration of nonclinical toxicity studies supporting AAV-based GT products. The survey was designed to capture insights from individual unique AAV-based GT program currently in the IND/CTA-enabling GLP-compliant toxicity studies phase or advanced beyond that point into IND/CTA submission or clinical trials. These criteria were chosen to ensure that the duration of the IND/CTA-enabling study was adequately reviewed and approved at the pre-IND/CTA and/or IND/CTA stage of the program by the health authorities. Therefore, each company had the opportunity to complete the survey multiple times if they had several programs that have reached that stage and met the survey inclusion criteria.

The survey was compatible with desktop or mobile viewing using MS Forms, electronically distributed by emails, and was completed anonymously with no specific information collected on the identity of the company completing the survey. The survey consisted of 23 questions subdivided into 4 main sections: (1) “General program information” was composed of 6 questions designed to obtain general information that may indirectly influence the determination of study duration, (2) “Chronic toxicity testing strategy” was composed of 7 questions designed to capture additional program details on longest duration for toxicity assessment performed and factors that may have influenced such a decision, (3) “Longer chronic toxicity assessment” was composed of 6 questions designed to capture specific factors that may have influenced the decision to perform toxicity assessment 6 months or longer in duration, and (4) “Company/institution demographics” to obtain general information on the company/institution that could influence the determination of study duration. Three additional questions were included as optional questions (Questions #24, #25, and #26). Question #24 requested companies that provided responses to more than one survey to provide a unique code (e.g., ABC123) to permit identification of responses submitted by the same company while maintaining their anonymity. Questions #25 and #26 were to rate satisfaction with the survey and to give space for companies to provide any additional feedback they would like to share.

The survey was distributed to companies through the EFPIA Gene and Cell Therapy Working Group and Biotechnology Innovation Organization (BIO) Cell and Gene Therapy Nonclinical Task Force networks in November 2024 with survey conclusion in March 2025. A total of 24 survey responses representing 24 unique AAV programs in the GLP toxicity studies phase or that had advanced beyond that point into IND submission or clinical trials were received from 13 unique companies. Eight companies provided responses for a single AAV program, 1 company provided responses for 2 AAV programs, 3 companies provided responses for 3 AAV programs, and 1 company provided responses for 5 AAV programs.

Data availability

The data supporting the findings of this study were obtained through a confidential survey of proprietary development programs. Due to the sensitive and non-public nature of these data, they are not available for public sharing.
