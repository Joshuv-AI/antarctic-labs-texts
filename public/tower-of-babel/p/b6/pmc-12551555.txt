Wearables Integrated Technology to support healthy behaviours in general practice patients with type 2 diabetes (WEAR-IT): study protocol for a cluster randomised controlled trial
Authors: Breanne Hobden, Kristy Fakes, Elizabeth J Halcomb, Katharine Wallis, Glen Maberly, Kean Seng-Lim, Christopher Doran, Christopher Oldmeadow, Gideon Meyerowitz-Katz, Stijn Soenen, Nicholas Zwar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionType 2 diabetes is a prevalent chronic disease, associated with health complications, premature morbidity and significant healthcare costs. Optimal lifestyle behaviour control and patient self-management are crucial for improving diabetes control; however, they are difficult to achieve in primary care. There is limited research on the use of information from wearable devices to encourage behaviour change. This study will examine the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of a multi-component health behaviour intervention in achieving clinically significant reductions in haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) among general practice patients with type 2 diabetes.Methods and analysisThe study uses a cluster-randomised controlled design, with general practices randomly assigned to either the Wearables Integrated Technology (WEAR-IT) intervention (n=15) or usual care (n=15). To achieve a sample size of 375 participants, 12–13 patients per practice will be recruited. Patients diagnosed with type 2 diabetes will be eligible to participate if they are aged 18–75 years; have had poorly controlled diabetes (HbA1c≥7.5%), with the cognitive capacity and ability to access the intervention application via an iOS or Android smart device. The WEAR-IT self-management intervention combines information from wearable devices (physical activity, blood glucose and blood pressure) and the electronic medical record, with goal setting and coaching support. The intervention will be primarily delivered by the general practice nurse, with review and confirmation of goals by the general practitioner. Participants attending the usual care practices will receive standard care. Outcome measures, including HbA1c, lipids, blood pressure, quality of life, dietary and exercise behaviours and cost-effectiveness, will be collected at baseline, 6-month (primary endpoint) and 12-month post-randomisation. The primary analysis will compare the change in HbA1c between the intervention and control groups at 6-month follow-up, with long-term outcomes assessed at 12-month post-randomisation.Ethics and disseminationThe study was approved by Bond University (BH00137). Introduction

Approximately 1.2 million Australians, nearly 5% of the total population, are living with diabetes.1 Type 2 diabetes accounts for 85% of all diabetes in Australia.2 Complications associated with diabetes, such as increased risks of stroke, heart, kidney and eye disease; reduced quality of life, higher morbidity and premature mortality.3 In 2020–2021, the direct cost of diabetes was $A3.4 billion, representing 2.3% of total Australian health system disease expenditure.4 The true cost of diabetes, however, is significantly larger through indirect costs, government subsidies and links with other conditions.5 For example, direct healthcare costs are 2.5 times higher among individuals with diabetes experiencing micro or macrovascular complications compared with those without complications.5 Achieving blood glucose, cholesterol and blood pressure targets for people with diabetes can reduce the likelihood of such complications6 and has been associated with improved mortality and reduced cardiovascular events.7 Regular exercise and physical activity have been shown to improve blood glucose control, reduce cardiovascular risk and improve well-being for patients with type 2 diabetes.8 9 Dietary intake also plays a key role in blood glucose modulation.10

Primary care providers, including general practitioners (GPs) and general practice nurses (GPNs), play an important role in supporting people living with diabetes to manage their health. Patient–provider collaboration and sharing of knowledge represent critical factors in improving health outcomes for people in the community.11 However, diabetes care requires complex lifestyle management, including increasing physical activity, making healthy dietary choices and ensuring medication adherence.8 Despite recognising the importance of promoting self-management, GPs and GPNs face a range of challenges in promoting lifestyle risk reduction within primary care.12 13 A meta-analysis of data from 71 clinical trials and 44 observational studies found that less than 40% of patients with diabetes had haemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels below or equal to the target of 7%.14 Further, a more recent study conducted across the USA and Europe found that among 8794 patients with diabetes, 60.9% had an HbA1c greater than 6.8%.15 In Australia, it is estimated that less than 50% of people with diabetes achieve their HbA1c target levels, while less than 40% reach target cholesterol and blood pressure levels.16 Nevertheless, multidisciplinary and nurse-led intervention approaches have demonstrated effectiveness in improving outcomes, including reducing HbA1c levels, among patients with diabetes.17,20 Therefore, these interventions represent an important strategy for continuing to address the burden associated with diabetes.

The potential for digital technology to shape and improve the health landscape has been widely identified.21 22 Digital interventions can provide access to a person’s health data to help the care team support and encourage behaviour change, while efficiently minimising provider burden through automated prompts and targeted education.23 While data on wearable technology is still emerging, meta-analysis of data from general population groups indicates that mobile interventions with wearable devices are effective in achieving significant improvements in lifestyle behaviours such as physical activity, diet, smoking and alcohol consumption.24 25 Additionally, web-based digital interventions have demonstrated effectiveness in reducing blood glucose levels for people with poorly controlled diabetes.26 A recent systematic review and meta-analysis explored wearable devices for patients with diabetes and demonstrated effectiveness in reducing HbA1c when the duration of the intervention was 12 weeks or less.27 Meta-analysis of clinical and observational trials for flash glucose monitoring has also demonstrated effectiveness in reducing HbA1c for patients with diabetes.28 Exploratory analysis suggests that the use of automated data, rather than user-reported data, increases the effectiveness of mobile health-focused interventions.25

While this indicates promise for wearable technologies, these studies are predominantly less robust study designs with small sample sizes, reducing the quality of the evidence and its generalisability.27 28 Additionally, few studies have explored wearable devices for patients with type 2 diabetes in a primary care setting.29 Moreover, no previous study has assessed whether a multi-component wearable technology intervention, embedded within primary care and administered via a multidisciplinary healthcare team, improves patient outcomes compared with standard care.

Aims

Primary aim

To examine the effectiveness of the multi-component Wearables Integrated Technology (WEAR-IT) intervention, compared with usual care, in reducing mean HbA1c levels among general practice patients with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes (HbA1c≥7.5%) after 6 months.

Secondary aim

To examine the cost-effectiveness of the intervention and its effects on macrovascular risk factors (low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and blood pressure) and quality of life after 12 months.

Hypotheses

When compared with usual care, at 6 months and 12 months, the intervention will result in: (1) ≥0.5% lower mean HbA1c level (primary outcome); (2) a cost-effective improvement in quality-adjusted life years; (3) an increase in the proportion of patients with controlled macrovascular risk factors (ie, LDL-C<2.0 mmol/L and blood pressure ≤140/90 mm Hg); and (4) an increase in average quality of life scores.

Methods and analysis

Study design

The study will be a two-arm cluster randomised controlled trial with 30 general practices (12–13 patients per practice (n=375 total)) (figure 1). The first patient consented on 23 October 2024. Patient recruitment is expected to continue until 1 March 2026. The study period (from the date of first patient recruitment to the last patient follow-up) is expected to be 23 October 2024 to 30 April 2027.

Setting

General practices across Australia will be recruited for the study. In Australia, general practices are typically the initial point of care for generalist health management and assessment prior to any specialist or tertiary care referral. Generally, general practices are staffed by GPs, GPNs and administrative staff, and may also include some allied health professionals.

General practice eligibility and recruitment

Eligible practices are required to have at least: one consenting GPN (registered nurse); one consenting GP; and 20 patients with type 2 diabetes who have visited the practice at least three times within the last 2 years (ie, active patients). Participating practices must have electronic medical records and either have or be willing to instal the Pen Computing Software (Pen CS), which is required for extracting medical record data.

Recruitment

The study will be advertised through partnering Primary Health Networks, Pen CS’ GP Network and a national primary care clinician education provider. Practices will complete a brief online expression of interest if they are interested in hearing more about the study. The research team will email practices with the eligibility criteria and then email eligible practices to complete a practice consent form and data sharing agreement. Evidence-based strategies to increase recruitment and retention will be adopted, including: personalised invitations; financial reimbursements for participating practices; engaging a practice champion; study newsletters; and the ability for participating GPs and GPNs to claim continuing professional development for their involvement. Higher than anticipated levels of interest have resulted in 40 practices consenting to the study to date.

Patient eligibility and recruitment

Eligibility

Participants will be: (1) active patients (ie, visited the practice at least three times within the last 2 years); (2) diagnosed with type 2 diabetes; (3) those with poorly controlled diabetes (ie, HbA1c≥7.5%); (4) aged 18–75 years; and (5) able to access the online intervention application via an iOS or Android smart device.

Recruitment

A list of patients meeting the first three eligibility criteria will be generated using a Pen CS tailored software tool. These data will be managed at a practice level by Pen CS, and no patient data will be shared with the research team until the patient consents to receive further study details. The patients identified through the medical record search will then be randomly ordered using an online random number generator. Practices will be asked to contact the first 30 participants on the list or all participants if the list has fewer than 30 patients. Potential participants will be sent an SMS or email from their general practice, inviting them to click on a link to confirm their eligibility, view the information statement and sign the consent form (see online supplemental file 1). A reminder SMS or email will be sent to non-responders, with further participants from the randomised list invited till the target number is reached. Reimbursements will be provided to all participants for completing the study data collection. For participants in the intervention group, all wearables will be provided to participants, and the study will cover any out-of-pocket consultation costs associated with the intervention. These factors are hoped to reduce socioeconomic barriers to participation in the study.

Data collection

Once consent is attained, participants will complete an online baseline survey including sociodemographic characteristics; health behaviours; patient knowledge, skill and confidence for diabetes self-management, quality of life (36-item Short Form (SF-36 version 2).30 All participants will be mailed a pathology referral form for HbA1c and lipids (total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, LDL-C and triglycerides) collection. Patients will be able to attend any service location of the participating pathology organisation. Height, weight and blood pressure measurements will be recorded during practice visits. This process will be repeated at the 6- and 12-month follow-ups. Intervention participants will be asked about their experience with the intervention at the 6-month follow-up. To minimise attrition, participants who have not completed their survey and/or pathology collection will receive one reminder via email, followed by one follow-up call from the study research team. Participants will receive a $10 gift card on completing each study survey and a $20 gift card on completing each pathology collection.

Randomisation

Following baseline data collection at each consenting general practice, practices will be randomly allocated to either the intervention or usual care group (figure 1). Consenting practices will be randomised (1:1) using permuted block randomisation with stratification by location (rural vs urban). For pragmatic purposes, randomisation of practices will be undertaken in blocks of 6 following baseline data collection. An independent statistician will generate the allocation sequence. Members of the research team involved in quantitative data analysis will be blinded to group allocation. It will not be possible to blind the GPs, GPNs or practices to group allocation due to the nature of the intervention.

Practice nurse and GP education

Participating practices will have access to a study website with basic study information. GPNs and GPs from intervention practices will also have access to a password-protected section of the website, which hosts the resources needed for the WEAR-IT intervention. GPNs will also receive a hard copy intervention manual containing a compilation of resources. A training webinar will be provided for GPNs and GPs covering the core features of the patient–provider care platform (ie, CareMonitor) and continuous glucose monitoring apps, connecting wearables to the apps, creating a care plan and how to access data from the clinician dashboard. Key intervention resources, including interpreting continuous glucose monitoring data, care plan template and study checklists, will be provided to intervention sites.

WEAR-IT intervention

Participants in the intervention group will receive standard care, plus the WEAR-IT intervention. In Australia, standard care consists of a combination of medical and lifestyle management. GPs may refer patients on for further assessment by specialist services or prescribe medications and other interventions to prompt behaviour change and lifestyle risk reduction. Practices may have adopted technological innovations to manage diabetes, but their use is infrequent.

The WEAR-IT intervention is an 8-week self-management care plan that incorporates wearable devices. The 8-week intervention period will be used to motivate patients to make ongoing lifestyle changes in active collaboration with their care team to reduce their lifestyle risks. This timeframe allows the participants sufficient time to incorporate wearable device feedback and make lifestyle changes, while reducing the likelihood of device fatigue. Exploring the effectiveness of novel shorter-term behavioural change interventions that can be feasibly incorporated within general practice is an important consideration. For instance, previous meta-analytic data have demonstrated that even very brief smoking cessation advice, delivered by GPs, can be effective in improving longer-term outcomes.31 The intervention components are described as follows.

CareMonitor platform

CareMonitor is an established remote patient monitoring application (iOS and Android compatible) that incorporates complex linking of patient self-monitoring data with general practice medical records.32 The patient app interface has the capability to connect to both the physical activity monitor and blood pressure monitor, displaying patient data. Patient data will be fed back to clinicians via a dashboard to enhance the impact of wearables through enabling patient–provider health collaboration and supporting self-management. The continuous glucose monitor (Freestyle Libre) cannot be integrated with the CareMonitor platform; however, the existing patient app and provider dashboard provided by Abbott33 for this device will be used.

Initial consultation (care plan and goal setting through CareMonitor)

Prior to their first appointment, participants will receive an information pack, with intervention resources including a study checklist and calendar. GPNs will invite consenting patients to attend a face-to-face 45-minute initial consultation. During this visit, participants will work with the GPN and GP to develop a care plan, including individualised target ranges for diet, exercise, body mass index, smoking, alcohol consumption, HbA1c, lipids and blood pressure. This will enable patients to monitor their own targets and behaviours through their wearable devices. The following will be tracked using wearable devices:

Physical activity and exercise: individual patient activity goals may include reducing sedentary behaviour and increasing anaerobic or resistance exercise, in line with best-evidence guidelines.34 Patients will be able to access daily and weekly summaries of their physical activity and exercise goal progress. While some patients may already use a physical activity monitor, they will be encouraged to wear the study-provided monitor to standardise data.

Follow-up consultation (review and update of care plan if needed)

The patient will be invited to return for a 30-minute consultation with the GPN and review by their GP. This consultation will involve reviewing the data from the wearable devices and assessing progress towards goals. Goals will be reviewed and updated as needed to assist patients in achieving change. The care team will discuss potential ways to improve their physical activity, glucose levels and blood pressure. This may include a medication review if required.

Weeks 5–8 (remote patient intervention)

The patient will repeat the intervention as per weeks 1–4, with the implemented changes as discussed in their follow-up consultation. Patients will be able to see how these changes impact their wearable outputs. Patients will be informed that they can book a 30-minute follow-up appointment with the GPN/GP if they have further questions about the changes to their care plan and want to discuss the sustainability of the lifestyle changes they have implemented.

Intervention fidelity

Intervention fidelity will be considered met if the participant attends a minimum of two of the three coaching and review consultations with their GPN/GP and has continuous glucose monitoring data available. GPNs will maintain a tracking document of whether the participant has booked their consultations and received their wearable devices. A member of the research team will hold a virtual meeting with any practice staff not adhering to the intervention protocol to discuss the barriers and identify strategies to overcome these issues. Patient adherence to the intervention will be encouraged through practices, contacting participants to book their initial and follow-up consultations and following up on missed appointments. Further, clear instructions and guidelines on wearable use will be provided by the study team, study contacts will be available for issues or troubleshooting, and healthcare providers will assist and encourage the use of intervention components.

Measures

Procedure

Data will be collected via pathology testing, data extraction from medical records and a participant survey at baseline, 6- and 12-month post-randomisation. Pathology referrals will be sent via post and the study survey link will be sent via email to participants. A 2-week reminder will be sent via email, if no response is received. Where participants do not respond to the initial or reminder mailouts, a member of the study team will telephone participants to follow-up. Monetary incentives to provide study data will also be used. These methods have been previously demonstrated to strengthen participant retention.36

Diabetes control (primary outcome). HbA1c level for all participants will be collected via pathology testing at baseline, 6 and 12 months. The 6-month follow-up is the primary endpoint, with long-term outcomes assessed at 12 months. A change in HbA1c of at least 0.5% is considered clinically relevant.37 The change in mean HbA1c level has been selected as individual target ranges may vary for some populations. A decrease in HbA1c levels across all participants is, therefore, a more practical approach rather than assessing the proportion of participants meeting one specified target.

Quality of Life. Quality of life will be prospectively assessed at baseline, and 6- and 12-month follow-up, using the SF-36 version 2.30 The SF-36 measures how often respondents have experienced problems over the last 4 weeks, across eight domains of well-being. It provides two summary scores: the Physical Component and the Mental Component. Scores from 0 to 100 are calculated for each subscale, with higher scores representing better health. There is evidence of internal consistency (α=0.81–0.92), test-retest reliability (Spearman’s ρ=0.36–0.83)38 and discriminant validity,39 and a minimally important difference of 0.3 SD has been recommended.40

Data for cost-effectiveness analysis

Healthcare utilisation will be determined through routinely collected clinical data. The frequency and mode of consultations, Medicare Benefits Scheme item codes and prescribed medicines (where possible) will be extracted from practice medical records by Pen CS. The valuation of healthcare services and items will be based on published data. Resources (including time) used to implement the intervention, as well as the costs associated with wearables and CareMonitor, will be monitored throughout the trial. Resources will be valued at appropriate market rates or price proxies, as applicable. Quality-adjusted life years (QALY) will be the primary outcome used in the cost-effectiveness analysis, derived from the SF-6D utility index score obtained from the SF-36 using standard methods.47

Process measures

Patient uptake of wearables and CareMonitor

The proportion of eligible and invited patients who: (1) engage in a consultation with the GPN in the month following the invitation; (2) download CareMonitor and (3) retain use of the wearables will be recorded.

Patient acceptability

Participant views about the ease of use, relevance and quality of the intervention will be assessed by a study-specific checklist for intervention participants administered at the 6- and 12-month follow-up surveys. While it will not be possible to isolate specific components of the intervention, the intervention checklist will allow patients to indicate which components of the intervention they found the most useful. A random sample of 20 patients will also be invited to participate in a qualitative telephone interview (see online supplemental file 2), aiming to gain an in-depth understanding of their experiences with the intervention. Patients will be reimbursed for their time to participate in the interviews.

Provider acceptability

Within intervention practices, GP and GPN views about their experience with using and delivering the intervention will be assessed by a brief survey at the conclusion of the intervention (see online supplemental file 3). A random sample of 10 GPs and 10 GPNs will also be invited via email to participate in a qualitative telephone interview (see online supplemental file 4), aiming to gain an in-depth understanding of their views on the feasibility, acceptability, impact and sustainability of the intervention. GPs and GPNs will be reimbursed for their time to participate in the interviews.

Data monitoring

Data monitoring will occur weekly throughout the study’s data collection period. Practices, GPs/GPNs and patients will have access to a study telephone number where any adverse events can be reported. The investigator team will take responsibility for monitoring adverse events and, in consultation with relevant ethics committees, determine the steps needed to minimise harm or further adverse events. The likelihood of adverse circumstances arising from the intervention is low, given the minimally invasive nature of the WEAR-IT programme. There are some risks for patients in undertaking pathology testing, but these are aligned with standard care practices for those with type 2 diabetes.

Data management

Survey data collected at baseline and follow-up will be captured using the Qualtrics survey software platform. These survey data from baseline and data extracted from medical records will be linked for each participant using a unique identifier. Interview recordings will be stored on secure, password-protected servers within the university.

Statistical analysis

The analysis will follow intention-to-treat principles, where participants will be analysed according to the general practice they were attending at randomisation. Missing data will be imputed using multiple imputation. The intention-to-treat population will include all eligible patients who have completed baseline data collection. Participants who have completed baseline will be retained if their practice drops out. Baseline characteristics will be summarised between the groups using means and SD for continuous variables and frequencies/counts for categorical variables. The difference between the intervention and control arms will be assessed using linear mixed-effects regression models. Regression models will incorporate random effects for practices, GPs nested within practices, patients nested within GPs (for repeated measures on the same patient), as well as fixed effects for the practice level, mean baseline value of the outcome, treatment group and stratification variables. The same model will be used to assess differences in the continuous secondary outcome variables (ie, macro-vascular risk and quality of life). A transformation will be applied if the distributional assumptions are not met. Interviews will be transcribed and then coded using inductive thematic analysis.48 An estimated 10 GPs and 10 GPNs are required to reach data saturation. However, should saturation not be reached, further clinicians will be recruited to achieve saturation.

Sample size

15 practices per arm, with an average of 10 patients per practice (n=300 total) will give the study 90% power to detect an absolute decrease of 0.5% in mean % HbA1c levels between arms, with a type 1 error rate of 5%. This calculation assumes an SD of 1.32 for the % and an intra-class correlation of 0.04 (derived from data in a previous study).49 Assuming an 80% retention rate by 6 months, approximately 12–13 patients per practice (n=375 total) will be required at baseline. Currently, 38 practices have been recruited to allow for potential practice dropout.

Cost-effectiveness analysis

Cost-effectiveness analyses will adopt international best practice and include the adoption of a healthcare perspective; transparent and scientific methods to identify, measure and value both costs and outcomes from the trial; modelling and uncertainty testing of epidemiological and costing input parameters.50 The analysis will model costs and outcomes for the duration of the trial and for 10 years, discounting future costs and health outcomes at a rate of 3% per year. Healthcare utilisation, including frequency and mode of consultation, will be extracted from practice medical records by Pen CS. Resources (including time) used to implement the intervention, including the cost associated with wearables and CareMonitor, will be monitored throughout the trial. Where possible, we will explore potential cost savings from improved type 2 diabetes outcomes. Resources (and cost savings) will be valued at appropriate market rates or price proxies where appropriate. QALYs will be the primary outcome used in the cost-effectiveness analysis, derived from the SF-6D utility index score obtained from the SF-36 using standard methods.47 Costs and health outcomes will be summed to determine the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER), in dollars per QALY gained. Monte Carlo analysis will be used to derive 95% uncertainty intervals for all outcomes and to determine the probability of intervention cost-effectiveness against an appropriate cost-effectiveness threshold. ICER results will be displayed on a cost-effectiveness plane with affordability issues addressed in an acceptability curve.

Patient and public involvement

The CareMonitor platform has been tested for acceptability and feasibility with patients and GPs in a Primary Health Network, where it is used by several early adopters who supported its development, including general practice beta testing in diabetes management. Qualitative research with four consumers and six clinicians using the platform indicated positive views regarding CareMonitor’s (1) facilitation of patient–provider communication, (2) automated feedback, (3) data integration and (4) risk stratification to enable action before exacerbation of their condition. Study authors, GM and GK-M have also explored the clinical use of a wearable glucose monitoring device (Freestyle Libre flash glucose monitor) in 59 patients with complex type 2 diabetes. This evaluation indicated that 83% of patients found the device ‘very useful’ and 83% (n=49) demonstrated an HbA1c reduction. KF is also a wearables consumer and has experience using the Freestyle Libre for management of her type 1 diabetes.

Research ethics

The study was approved by the Bond University Human Ethics Committees (BH00137). Confidentiality and privacy of information. All participant survey and outcome data will be linked using a unique patient identifier (ie, practice name, mobile number, gender, date of birth). Electronic patient consent forms, which will link the participant’s name to their unique study identification number, will be stored separately from survey data. Electronic files containing data will be stored on the Bond University and University of Newcastle servers in a password-protected file. Only the chief investigators and staff employed to work directly on the study will have access to raw data.

Dissemination

The proposed study will contribute level two evidence11 on the effectiveness of a multi-component wearable device intervention in improving patient outcomes and reducing health costs. This will develop new knowledge that is applicable to the Australian healthcare system and provide policy-relevant information regarding the benefit of the intervention and its potential for national up-scaling and implementation to address type 2 diabetes. In addition, by conducting a robust costing and cost-effectiveness analysis, we will link our results directly to action. Governments, including those in Australia and internationally, will be able to use these results to drive policy on wearables and technological innovations for diabetes. The size of the trial and robustness of the measures will allow for useful inferences to immediately improve healthcare for people with diabetes. Study findings will be disseminated through: summaries to study sites, participants and partners; conference presentations; and publications in peer-reviewed journals. Opportunities will also be sought to present the study findings through media outlets and to state and federal government departments.
