Structure-guided engineering of AI-derived adenine base editors for nuclear and mitochondrial DNA editing
Authors: Hye-Yeon Hwang, Nabukenya Mariam, Jiyeon Kweon, Hwalin Yi, Gayoung Jang, Jiyoo Roh, Kayeong Lim, Jae-Hyun Park, Yongsub Kim, Daesik Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional adenine base editors (ABEs), primarily derived from TadA, suffer from bystander editing and limited diversity, while artificial intelligence (AI)-designed deaminases such as Deam-P32 exhibit lower efficiency and precision than state-of-the-art editors such as ABE8e. Here, we report the OpenABE variants through the structure-guided evolution of Deam-P32 by optimizing DNA engagement and the base-contacting pocket and incorporating C-terminal extensions from ABE8e. These variants achieved a 16–36-fold increase in A-to-G conversion efficiency over Deam-P32, matching the editing efficiency of ABE8e across 29 endogenous nuclear loci while mitigating bystander cytosine editing. OpenABEs also reduced ATC motif bystander editing and produced fewer guide RNA-independent off-target effects on DNA and RNA. Further, we adapted these variants to mitochondrial DNA editing by designing OpenABE-TALEDs, which yielded editing efficiencies comparable or superior to those of ABE8e-TALEDs. Delivery through engineered virus-like particles further enhanced specificity and product purity. These results demonstrate that structure-guided refinement of AI-designed deaminases can produce precise, versatile base editors for nuclear and mitochondrial genomes, expanding the research and therapeutic applications of genome editing toolkits.

Introduction

Base editors are powerful genome-editing tools that can introduce precise single-base substitutions without cleaving double-stranded DNA [1–3]. Prominent examples include adenine base editors (ABEs), which convert A-T base pairs to G-C [1, 4–9]; cytosine base editors (CBEs), which convert C-G base pairs to T-A [2, 4, 10–20]; and thymine base editors, which convert thymine to alternative bases (T-to-V, where V represents A, C, or G) [21–24]. Recently, advanced CBEs have also been developed that can catalyze novel C-to-G or C-to-A conversions [3, 25]. Given that C-to-T transitions account for ∼48% of pathogenic human single nucleotide polymorphisms [1], ABEs carry considerable therapeutic potential for correcting several of these clinically relevant mutations [1].

Cytosine deaminases, which catalyze the deamination of cytosine to uracil (eventually resulting in C-to-T transitions in DNA), are widely distributed among mammals and microbial species. Notable examples of cytosine deaminases include members of the APOBEC family [2, 4, 26], CDA1 [10, 12], FERNY [12], DddA [27], Sdd [17, 18], and DYW-like deaminase [28]. Diverse, target-specific CBEs have been engineered by fusing cytosine deaminases to Cas9 nickase. By contrast, no naturally occurring enzyme has been identified that directly catalyzes adenosine deamination in DNA. To address this gap, researchers developed a DNA-targeting adenosine deaminase through the directed evolution of TadA, an RNA-specific transfer RNA (tRNA) adenosine deaminase from Escherichia coli that converts adenosine to inosine [1]. When fused to Cas9 nickase, this engineered enzyme forms the basis of ABE systems [1]. While the natural diversity of cytosine deaminases allows for the selective use of CBEs tailored to specific DNA targets, most ABEs developed to date rely on engineered TadA derivatives, such as ABE7.10 [1], ABEmax [4], ABE8e [5], ABE8.20 [7] ABE8eWQ [6], ABE8e-N108Q [8], ABE9 [8], and hyABE [9]. As a result, the development of ABEs with novel biochemical properties has been limited till fairly recently.

Recent advances in large language model-driven protein engineering have opened new possibilities for the design of entirely novel adenosine deaminases. Ruffolo et al. used large language model technology to create two new ABEs, PF-DEAM-1 and PF-DEAM-2 [29]. Using proseLM, a protein structure-encoded language model, they generated two more novel ABEs, Deam-A22 and Deam-P32, from Deam-parent [30]. Deam-A22 and Deam-P32 demonstrated a base-editing activity that was ∼1.5-fold higher than that of Deam-parent and comparable to the activities of widely used ABEs, such as ABE7.10 and ABE8.20 [30].

Here, we improve these artificial intelligence (AI)-designed base editors by addressing three persistent challenges: submaximal activity, indel formation, and cytosine bystander editing. We first found that Deam-parent and its two derivatives (Deam-A22 and Deam-P32) exhibited lower editing efficiencies, higher cytosine bystander editing, and higher indel rates compared with ABE8e. Guided by structural analysis, we applied structure-guided engineering to Deam-P32 to generate OpenABE1.1 and OpenABE1.2, which achieve A-to-G editing efficiencies comparable to that of ABE8e while reducing bystander C-to-T conversions and indels. Collectively, our results demonstrate that these OpenABE variants enable efficient and programmable base editing at both nuclear and mitochondrial DNA targets, thereby expanding the applicability of ABEs in research and therapeutic development.

Materials and methods

Plasmid construction

The plasmids ABE8e (Addgene plasmid #138 489), dSaCas9 (Addgene plasmid #138 162), and pCMV-MMLVgag-3xNES-ABE8e (Addgene plasmid #181 751) were a gift from David Liu. The deaminases used in this study, Deam-parent, Deam-A22, and Deam-P32, were previously reported AI-derived deaminases originally described by the group that developed OpenCRISPR-1 [30]. To construct base editor variants, the coding sequences of DEAM-parent, Deam-A22, and Deam-P32 were synthesized as gBlocks (Integrated DNA Technologies) and cloned into the ABE8e plasmid backbone by replacing the original ABE8e coding sequence via Gibson Assembly (New England Biolabs). P32-based variants were further engineered through site-directed mutagenesis (New England Biolabs; Supplementary Table S1). Plasmids encoding pCMV-MMLVgag-3xNES-OpenABE1.1 and pCMV-MMLVgag-3xNES-OpenABE1.2 for eVLP generation were constructed by Gibson Assembly with pCMV-MMLVgag-3xNES-ABE8e backbone. All plasmids used for downstream transfection were prepared using the NucleoBond Xtra Midi Prep Kit (Macherey-Nagel).

Cell culture and transfection

HEK293T (ATCC, CRL-11268), HeLa (ATCC, CCL-2), SKOV3 cells (ATCC, HTB-77), and Gesicle Producer 293T cells (Takara Bio, 632 617) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM; Welgene). DMEM media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Welgene) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Welgene). All cell cultures were incubated under the standard conditions of 37°C and 5% CO₂ in a humidified environment.

For base editing experiments, HEK293T cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1.5 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured overnight before being cotransfected with 1500 ng of base editor and 500 ng of guide RNA (gRNA)-encoding plasmid using Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the supplier’s instructions. The seeding densities per well for HeLa and SKOV3 cells were 4.0 × 104 cells and 2.5 × 104 cells, respectively, and transfection was conducted under the same conditions.

Production and purification of OpenABE-eVLPs

eVLPs packaging OpenABE variants and their corresponding gRNAs were produced in Gesicle Producer 293T cells. Cells were seeded in 150-mm dishes at a density of 1 × 107 cells per dish. After 24 h, the cells were transfected using polyethyleneimine (Sigma–Aldrich) with the following plasmid amounts (per dish): VSV-G, 800 ng; MMLVgag–pro–pol, 6750 ng; gRNA vector, 8800 ng; and pCMV-MMLVgag–3xNES–ABE expression plasmid, 2250 ng (OpenABE1.1 or OpenABE1.2).

At 72 h post-transfection, the culture medium was collected, clarified by centrifugation at 500 × g for 5 min, and filtered through a 0.45-µm polyvinylidene difluoride membrane (Millipore). The filtrate was concentrated by overnight precipitation at 4°C using 5 × PEG-it Virus Precipitation Solution (System Biosciences). The eVLPs in the concentrate were pelleted by centrifugation at 1500 × g for 1 h at 4°C and resuspended in 1 × HIV-Safe Manager buffer (Lugen Sci). The purified eVLPs were stored at −80°C and thawed on ice prior to use.

Targeted deep sequencing

Target regions within the genomic DNA were amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase (New England Biolabs) and site-specific primers (Supplementary Tables S2 and S3 Supporting Information). After incorporating Illumina TruSeq HT dual index adapter sequences, the PCR products were purified and subjected to 150-bp paired-end sequencing on the Illumina iSeq 100 platform. A sequencing depth of over 5000 reads was achieved. A-to-G base-editing efficiency was calculated as the percentage of total analyzed reads containing the desired mutation within the defined editing window (positions 1–20 of the protospacer), as determined by MAUND bioinformatics tool (https://github.com/ibs-cge/maund). Specifically, A-to-G conversion (%) was defined as the fraction of reads harboring at least one A-to-G substitution within the target region relative to the total number of reads. Accordingly, when multiple adenines were present within a given target site, reads containing one or more A-to-G conversions were classified as edited reads. Heatmaps representing editing frequencies at each position were calculated using MAUND and subsequently generated using GraphPad Prism.

Orthogonal R-loop assay

To quantify gRNA-independent off-target editing, orthogonal R-loop assays were performed in HEK293T cells. For plasmid-based delivery, cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 1.0 × 10⁵ cells per well and co-transfected the following day with 600 ng base editor plasmid, 400 ng gRNA plasmid, 600 ng dSaCas9 plasmid, and 400 ng SaCas9 gRNA plasmid using 3 μl Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For eVLP-based delivery, cells were first transfected with 600 ng dSaCas9 plasmid and 400 ng SaCas9 gRNA plasmid, and then treated 24 h later with eVLPs carrying the corresponding base editor and gRNA cargo. Cells were harvested 4 d after the initial transfection, and genomic DNA was isolated using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Editing frequencies at orthogonal R-loop sites were subsequently quantified by targeted amplicon sequencing.

Transcriptome sequencing

Total RNA was isolated 48 h after transfection using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Strand-specific libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Gold Kit (Illumina), and library integrity was verified on an Agilent 2200 TapeStation system using the D1000 ScreenTape assay. Pooled libraries were sequenced by Macrogen on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate 2 × 100 bp paired-end reads.

RNA sequencing data were processed using a previously validated pipeline for detecting off-target RNA base editing [31]. Briefly, raw paired-end RNA-seq FASTQ files were computationally downsampled to 50 million read pairs per sample prior to reference mapping using BBTools reformat.sh (v.39.81), with read pairing preserved and a fixed random seed (seed = 100) before alignment to the human reference genome GRCh38 (Ensembl release 105) using the STAR aligner, v2.7.10a. Post-alignment processing and RNA variant calling were performed using the GATK toolkit, v4.2.4.1 (MarkDuplicates, BaseRecalibrator, ApplyBQSR, HaplotypeCaller), with default parameters unless otherwise specified. To control for background variation, the variants were filtered against matched replicate controls. In replicate set #1, untreated replicate #2 served as the control, and vice versa for set #2. Variant loci were retained if they met both the following criteria: minimum variant count ≥2 and total depth ≥10 reads. Variant loci were excluded if they were present in the corresponding control or were indeterminate due to insufficient control depth. A-to-G editing was quantified as A-to-G on the positive strand or T-to-C on the negative strand, each expressed relative to total RNA editing calls.

Construction of TALEDs

The plasmids TALED_Left-ND1-AD-E1347A (Addgene plasmids #183 894), TALED_Left-ND1-1397C-AD (Addgene plasmids #183 892), and TALED_Right-ND1-1397N (Addgene plasmids #183 898) were gifts from Jin-Soo Kim. The coding sequences of OpenABE variants were amplified and cloned into these TALED plasmids to construct OpenABE-TALEDs targeting the mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) ND1-1. The coding sequences of nuclear export signal were also added to the C-terminal region of each construct to reduce the off-target effectors of nuclear DNA. To construct OpenABE-TALEDs targeting other mtDNA loci, TALE arrays were obtained from TALE-SRE [28]. The TALE arrays were digested with the restriction enzymes BamHI and XbaI (New England Biolabs) and cloned into the OpenABE-TALEDs plasmids targeting mtDNA ND1-1 using T4 DNA ligase (New England Biolabs).

Statistics and reproducibility

Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean unless otherwise indicated. Statistical analyses were performed in GraphPad Prism (v9.1.1). Pairwise comparisons were performed using two-sided Student’s t-tests, as specified in the figure legends. Sample sizes were not predetermined by statistical methods; all data points were included and no exclusions were made. Experiments were not randomized, and investigators were not blinded to group allocation during data collection or analysis.

Code availability

Editing efficiencies were calculated by MAUND, an analysis tool available at GitHub (https://github.com/ibs-cge/maund), which is distributed by BotBot Inc. No modifications were made.

Results

Comparison of AI-designed ABEs with ABE8e

First, we evaluated the base editing efficiencies of three AI-designed ABEs, Deam-parent (engineered using protein language models), Deam-A22 (optimized using proseLM with an active site-focused design), and Deam-P32 (optimized using proseLM with a nonactive site-focused design), which were reported by the same group that developed OpenCRISPR-1 [30], relative to the widely used ABE8e at 29 endogenous genomic loci in HEK293T cells using plasmid DNA-mediated transfection and targeted deep-sequencing. On average, Deam-A22 and Deam-P32 achieved higher A-to-G editing efficiency than Deam-parent (22.62% ± 4.16% and 13.02% ± 2.92% versus 9.28% ± 2.50%, respectively; Fig. 1A and B). However, the three AI-designed ABE variants exhibited significantly lower A-to-G editing activity compared with ABE8e (49.47% ± 2.95%), although their activity was comparable at several loci (NPR2-2, MAP3K3, CKM, SSTR5, VEGFA, and HEK2; Fig. 1A). All three AI-designed ABEs also showed increased bystander editing, including indel formation and bystander C-to-D (D represents A, G, or T) editing (Fig. 1C and D). Notably, Deam-A22 produced higher levels of bystander C-to-D conversions than the other variants (Fig. 1D). Taken together, these results indicate that while Deam-A22 and Deam-P32 exhibit improved A-to-G editing compared with Deam-parent, they do not yet match the performance of ABE8e.

Structure-based rational engineering of Deam-P32

Focusing on Deam-P32, we sought to enhance its A-to-G editing efficiency and precision using a structure-guided approach, targeting key functional regions identified through comparative structural analysis with ABE8e (PDB: 6VPC). Drawing on the structural framework of adenine deaminases and insights from the engineering of ABE7.10 and ABE8e [1, 5], we selected 11 sites for systematic mutagenesis, spanning three functional regions: (i) DNA engagement interface, (ii) base-contacting pocket, and (iii) catalytic environment (Fig. 2A–C).

DNA engagement interface

The stability of the deaminase-substrate DNA complex can be enhanced by introducing additional DNA binding interfaces. The residues P103 and A105 of Deam-P32 were identified as positions that could form additional interactions with substrate DNA (Fig. 2B). The P103S mutation introduces a hydroxyl group capable of forming hydrogen bonds with the phosphate groups in the DNA backbone, potentially increasing binding affinity and deaminase–DNA complex stability. The A105R substitution provides an opportunity to establish electrostatic interactions between the positively charged arginine and the negatively charged DNA backbone (Fig. 2B).

Base-contacting pocket

The V22F, V22R, and L78F mutations promote π-stacking or cation–π interactions with the edited base and the +1 base (Fig. 2C). The R146Y mutation was designed to replace electrostatic interactions with aromatic interactions with the objective of fine-tuning the stability of the deaminase–DNA complex (Fig. 2C).

Catalytic environment

We specifically targeted positions in the zinc coordination sphere and catalytic core of Deam-P32, which represent the most critical regions for deaminase activity. Our strategy aimed to modulate the electronic environment and potentially alter substrate positioning within these regions while preserving the essential catalytic machinery. We designed I42T and I42Q mutations, which introduce polar functionality near the zinc coordination sphere (Fig. 2A). The H51C substitution explores noncanonical zinc coordination, while E53D represents a conservative modification to the catalytic carboxylate (Fig. 2A). These modifications, while potentially affecting enzyme stability or activity, may confer unique catalytic properties. Accordingly, we generated 11 single or double-site variants of Deam-P32 as follows: Y17R, V22F, V22R, I42T, I42Q, H51C, E53D, L78F, P103S/A105R (paired change), F143Y, and R146Y. The editing efficiencies of the variants were measured at the HEK1, HEK3, and HEK4 loci in HEK293T cells. Targeted amplicon sequencing revealed that the L78F and P103S/A105R mutations substantially increased the A-to-G editing efficiency of Deam-P32 across all loci, nominating these substitutions for incorporation into next-generation Deam-P32 derivatives (Fig. 2D–F).

A major structural difference between Deam-P32 and ABE8e is the absence of a C-terminal extension in Deam-P32 (Fig. 2G). Previous studies have reported that the C-terminal domain of ABE8e contributes prominently to its DNA deamination activity as well as to DNA binding and processivity [32]. To determine whether modifications to the Deam-P32 C-terminal region could enhance its editing activity, we engineered the following two variants: (i) C-ext1, with a 15-amino acid extension (RQVFNAQKKAQSSIN) derived from the C-terminal sequence of ABE8e; and (ii) C-ext2, generated by deleting two amino acids (ER) from the C-terminal region of Deam-P32 and subsequently adding a 17-amino acid sequence (MPRQVFNAQKKAQSSIN), also sourced from the ABE8e C-terminus (Fig. 2G). HEK293T cells were cotransfected with plasmids encoding either Deam-parent, Deam-P32, Deam-P32 C-ext1, or Deam-P32 C-ext2, along with corresponding gRNAs targeting HEK1, HEK3, or HEK4. Targeted amplicon sequencing revealed a significant enhancement in A-to-G editing efficiencies for both the C-ext1 and C-ext2 variants across all three target loci compared with Deam-P32 (Fig. 2D–F).

These experiments resulted in the identification of four Deam-P32 variants that exhibited significantly enhanced A-to-G base editing efficiencies, namely L78F, P103S/A105R, C-ext1, and C-ext2 (Fig. 2D–F). To explore potential synergistic effects, we generated a panel of Deam-P32 derivatives encompassing all possible combinations of these double and triple mutations. Following cotransfection with gRNAs into HEK293T cells, we evaluated editing activities using targeted deep sequencing at three endogenous loci (HEK1, HEK3, and HEK4). Two of the double mutants exhibited increased A-to-G editing activity, one harboring P103S/A105R and C-ext1 and the other harboring P103S/A105R and C-ext2 (Fig. 2H–J). However, both these double mutants also induced elevated frequencies of bystander C-to-D conversions (Fig. 2K–P and Supplementary Fig. S1A–C). Interestingly, incorporating an additional L78F mutation into these double mutants not only enhanced A-to-G editing efficiencies, but also substantially reduced off-target bystander C-to-D conversions and indel formation (Fig. 2H–P and Supplementary Fig. S1A–I). The resulting triple mutants L78F + P103S/A105R + C-ext1 (hereafter referred to as OpenABE1.1) and L78F + P103S/A105R + C-ext2 (hereafter referred to as OpenABE1.2) exhibited editing activity comparable to that of ABE8e (Fig. 2H–J). Compared with Deam-P32, OpenABE1.1 and OpenABE1.2 demonstrated A-to-G conversion efficiencies that were greater by 16.3–34.4-fold and 17.5–36.1-fold (Fig. 2H–J). These results showcase the potential of structure-guided engineering to improve the efficacy and product purity of ABEs.

Comparison of OpenABE variants and ABE8e

Next, we compared the base editing efficiencies of OpenABE variants and ABE8e by transfecting HEK293T cells with ABE8e, OpenABE1.1, or OpenABE1.2, together with gRNAs targeting 29 endogenous loci, and measuring adenosine and cytosine base editing frequencies via targeted deep sequencing (Fig. 3A and B). The average A-to-G editing frequencies of OpenABE1.1 (41.73% ± 1.90%) and OpenABE1.2 (41.03% ± 1.99%) were comparable to that of ABE8e (42.61% ± 2.36%), indicating similar on-target activity across a broad range of target loci (Fig. 3A and B). By contrast, the average C-to-D editing frequencies of OpenABE1.1 (1.03% ± 0.24%) and OpenABE1.2 (0.71% ± 0.15%) were lower than that of ABE8e (1.81% ± 0.81%), indicating attenuation of cytosine bystander formation (Fig. 3B). The indel frequencies for OpenABE1.1 (0.54% ± 0.08%) and OpenABE1.2 (0.63% ± 0.16%) were similar to that for ABE8e (0.49% ± 0.12%; Fig. 3B). Collectively, these results show that OpenABE variants retain A-to-G editing efficiency while improving product purity under matched conditions. Moreover, we found that the editing windows of OpenABE1.1 and OpenABE1.2 were broadly comparable to that of ABE8e (Fig. 3C). We also assessed the two OpenABE variants in other cell types by transfecting HeLa and SKOV3 cells with ABE8e, OpenABE1.1, or OpenABE1.2 and quantifying editing efficiencies using targeted deep sequencing. The mean on-target A-to-G conversion exhibited by OpenABE1.1 (HeLa: 43.56% ± 3.91%; SKOV3: 62.82% ± 5.24%) and OpenABE1.2 (HeLa: 44.78% ± 3.15%; SKOV3: 60.24% ± 5.44%) was comparable to that exhibited by ABE8e (HeLa: 47.68% ± 5.89%; SKOV3: 62.46% ± 4.42%). Moreover, the frequencies of C-to-D bystander formation and indels were lower for both OpenABE variants in both cell lines (Fig. 3D–G). These results demonstrate that OpenABE1.1 and OpenABE1.2 achieve reduced bystander editing and improved product purity compared with ABE8e in HeLa and SKOV3 cells, while maintaining comparable on-target A-to-G editing activity.

Bystander editing in the ATC motif

ABE8e is known to produce elevated C-to-D conversion in the ATC motif [6]. Therefore, we used targeted deep sequencing to compare the A-to-G and C-to-D editing efficiencies of OpenABE1.1, OpenABE1.2, and ABE8e specifically at ATC-containing targets. The on-target A-to-G conversion efficiencies of OpenABE1.1 (53.97% ± 3.81%) and OpenABE1.2 (54.34% ± 4.43%) were comparable to that of ABE8e (48.90% ± 7.32%), whereas the C-to-D bystander frequency was markedly reduced for both OpenABE variants (Fig. 4A and B). For example, at ATC sites in FANCF, the A-to-G editing efficiency of OpenABE1.1 (60.51% ± 0.47%) and OpenABE1.2 (62.69% ± 0.91%) was comparable to that of ABE8e (57.64% ± 0.54%), but the C-to-D conversion frequency of OpenABE1.1 (2.79% ± 0.12%) and OpenABE1.2 (4.89% ± 0.18%) was 7.0–12.2-fold lower than that of ABE8e (34.09% ± 0.84%; Fig. 4A). Similarly, at ATC sites in NOS2, the two OpenABE variants demonstrated enhanced A-to-G conversion efficiency in conjunction with reduced C-to-D bystander editing (Fig. 4A). Next, we compared the OpenABE variants with ABE8eWQ, an ABE8e derivative designed to mitigate ATC-dependent bystanders [6]. Although the C-to-D:A-to-G ratio varied by site, the average A-to-G conversion efficiencies of OpenABE variants (OpenABE1.1: 53.97% ± 3.81%; OpenABE1.2: 54.34% ± 4.43%) exceeded that of ABE8eWQ (34.54% ± 9.64%). For example, at the NOS2, CSRNP3, CA9, GRIK5, and PTGER1 sites, the OpenABE variants exhibited significantly higher activity than ABE8eWQ (Fig. 4A and B). These results demonstrate the capacity of OpenABE variants to attenuate ATC-dependent C-to-D bystanders while preserving on-target A-to-G editing activity relative to ABE8e and ABE8eWQ.

Off-target effects of OpenABE variants and ABE8e

Next, we evaluated the gRNA-dependent off-target activity of OpenABE variants and ABE8e at single- or double-mismatch sites identified using the Cas-OFFinder algorithm [33] and quantified A-to-G base editing efficiency through targeted deep sequencing (Fig. 4C). The OpenABE variants exhibited site-dependent, bidirectional off-target propensities, higher than ABE8e at some sites and lower at others (Fig. 4C). To evaluate gRNA-independent effects, we used an orthogonal dSaCas9 R-loop assay [34], in which OpenABE1.1 reduced unintended A-to-G editing relative to ABE8e at R-loop site 5 and site 6 (Fig. 4D). Next, we used comprehensive RNA sequencing analysis to evaluate gRNA-independent transcriptome-wide RNA off-target activity. The OpenABE variants exhibited a lower number of RNA off-target sites compared with ABE8e (Fig. 4E). Taken together, these results indicate that the OpenABE variants exhibit mixed, locus-dependent gRNA-dependent off-target activity, while attenuating gRNA-independent DNA and RNA off-target effects relative to ABE8e.

Mitochondrial DNA base editing using OpenABE variants

To evaluate A-to-G editing in double-stranded mtDNA, we constructed transcription activator-like effector (TALE)-linked deaminases (TALEDs) by changing ABE8e to OpenABE variants together with a mitochondrial targeting sequence [35]. TALE arrays for ND1-1 were assembled by Golden Gate cloning [35], and a monomer versus split TALED comparison targeting ND1-1 confirmed that split architectures yielded higher editing efficiencies across both ABE8e-TALEDs and OpenABE-TALEDs (Fig. 5A and B, and Supplementary Fig. S2A and B). Therefore, the split architecture was used in subsequent experiments. We constructed split-TALEDs for ND1-2, CO3, CYB, and ATP6 and transfected HEK293T cells. Targeted mtDNA amplicon sequencing revealed robust, locus-dependent A-to-G conversion by OpenABE-TALEDs with efficiencies comparable to those of ABE8e-TALEDs, reaching up to 32.7% at CYB A9. The editing efficiencies were site- and position-dependent (Fig. 5A and B). At CO3, A2 favored ABE8e-TALEDs, whereas A6 and A9 favored OpenABE-TALEDs (Fig. 5A and B). Across CYB and ATP6, OpenABE-TALED activity was comparable to or higher than ABE8e-TALED activity (Fig. 5A and B), and OpenABE-TALEDs showed greater efficiency in the AA context (Supplementary Fig. S2C). Collectively, these data demonstrate that OpenABE-TALEDs enable efficient, programmable mitochondrial A-to-G editing, and their activity can match or surpass that of ABE8e-TALEDs at selected positions.

We next evaluated OpenABE-TALED variants alongside ABE8e-TALEDs in HeLa and HAP1 cells by targeting the mtDNAs ND1-1 and ND1-2. A-to-G editing efficiencies were measured through targeted mtDNA amplicon sequencing. In both cell types, OpenABE-TALEDs achieved efficiencies comparable to or exceeding those of ABE8e-TALEDs at both ND1-1 and ND1-2 target sites (Supplementary Fig. S3A and B). These results demonstrate that OpenABE-TALED variants sustain robust mitochondrial base editing across multiple cell backgrounds, indicating broad cellular compatibility.

Delivery using engineered virus-like particles enhances specificity of OpenABE variants

To improve specificity and limit bystander editing, we delivered OpenABE1.1 and OpenABE1.2 as ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complexes using engineered virus-like particles (eVLPs). ABE8e, OpenABE1.1, and OpenABE1.2 were packaged along with gRNAs targeting SSTR5, ITGA6, GCK, and FANCF and transduced into HEK293T cells. Under matching conditions, eVLP delivery sustained or increased on-target A-to-G editing relative to plasmid transfection across all targets and both OpenABE variants except for OpenABE1.1 at FANCF (Fig. 6A and Supplementary Fig. S4A). To quantify product purity, we enumerated per-allele edit multiplicity (number of adenosines converted within the protospacer; 1–6 edits per allele). Compared with plasmid DNA transfection, eVLP-mediated RNP delivery enriched single- and double-nucleotide outcomes (Fig. 6B–D and Supplementary Fig. 4B–D), consistent with editor peak activity at positions 5–6 and the presence of adenines at both positions in SSTR5 and ITGA6. In SSTR5, the fraction of alleles with ≤2 A-to-G conversions reached 68.61% ± 0.94% for OpenABE1.1-eVLP and 43.12% ± 0.83% for OpenABE1.2-eVLP, exceeding the corresponding values for ABE8e-eVLP (12.15% ± 0.49%) and plasmid-delivered OpenABE counterparts (OpenABE1.1: 9.74% ± 0.42%, OpenABE1.2: 35.60% ± 0.80%; Fig. 6B and D). Similarly, single-nucleotide outcomes were enriched at the GCK and FANCF sites following eVLP delivery compared with plasmid transfection (Supplementary Fig. S4B and D). We next compared gRNA-dependent off-target effects between plasmid DNA and eVLP-mediated RNP delivery. eVLP-mediated delivery yielded significantly higher specificity, with specificity ratios as high as 181.26 for OpenABE1.1 and 69.59 for OpenABE1.2 (Fig. 6E). To determine whether this improvement extended to gRNA-independent off-targeting, we performed the orthogonal R-loop assay. At Sa-site 5, which exhibited relatively high gRNA-independent off-target editing under plasmid delivery, editing was markedly reduced following eVLP treatment (Supplementary Fig. S4E). Collectively, these data indicate that eVLP delivery maintains or improves on-target efficiency while enhancing allelic specificity by preferentially producing one- to two-base edits. Additionally, eVLP delivery reduces gRNA-dependent and gRNA-independent DNA off-target effects of the two OpenABE variants.

Discussion

In this study, we demonstrate that structure-guided refinement of an AI-designed adenine deaminase can yield base editors with performance comparable to that of the best TadA-derived editors [30]. As a starting point, we chose the AI-generated deaminase Deam-P32 [30], which showed only modest activity and elevated byproduct formation compared with evolved editors such as ABE8e. We sought to overcome the limitations of Deam-P32 through rational protein engineering. The resulting OpenABE1.1 and OpenABE1.2 editors demonstrated adenine base-editing efficiencies comparable to that of ABE8e across numerous loci, while markedly lowering the frequency of undesired edits (Fig. 3A and B). By introducing targeted mutations to enhance DNA engagement and optimize the base-contacting pocket and by grafting a critical C-terminal extension sourced from ABE8e, we enhanced the A-to-G conversion rate of the Deam-P32 parent by over an order of magnitude (Fig. 2D–M).

A particularly noteworthy feature of OpenABEs is the mitigation of ATC motif cytosine deamination editing, a known and unwanted bystander effect of ABE8e [6]. The hyperactivity of ABE8e often comes at the cost of promiscuous cytosine deamination when the target adenine is followed by a T and C in the protospacer (the “ATC” context), leading to undesired C-to-D substitutions [6]. OpenABE1.1 and OpenABE1.2 reduced C-to-D conversion at ATC-containing sites while preserving their high A-to-G editing activity comparable to ABE8e (Fig. 4A and B). These findings show that our structure-guided modifications successfully rebalanced the activity and specificity of the deaminases, creating editors that process their target base selectively without incurring excessive bystander mutations. Consistent with this, we also observed attenuated gRNA-independent off-target effects (Fig. 4D and E). OpenABE1.1 exhibited lower Cas9-independent DNA deamination in an R-loop assay (Fig. 4D) and generated fewer transcriptome-wide RNA edits than ABE8e (Fig. 4E). These improvements associated with higher-fidelity base editing may boost the therapeutic applicability of OpenABEs.

Beyond nuclear DNA editing, our study shows that OpenABE-TALEDs perform robust mtDNA editing across multiple loci (ND1, CO3, CYB, ATP6), matching or even surpassing the performance of ABE8e-TALEDs in certain sequence contexts (Fig. 5, and Supplementary Figs S2 and S3). Our findings demonstrate the validity of entirely new deaminase scaffolds, engineered for high efficiency, in organellar genome editing. Moreover, the strong performance of OpenABE in several cell lines (HEK293T, HeLa, and HAP1) suggests that these editors are broadly active and not restricted to a single cell type (Fig. 3D–G and Supplementary Fig. S3). Together, these results illustrate that our OpenABE variants are versatile tools, operating effectively in both the nucleus and mitochondria. Moreover, they highlight the generality of our engineering strategy for enhancing enzyme function in diverse genomic compartments.

Finally, we demonstrate that pairing our optimized editors with advanced delivery methods can further improve editing outcomes. When packaged and delivered as RNPs via eVLPs, OpenABE1.1 and 1.2 achieved efficient on-target editing while increasing product purity and specificity (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. S4). For targets with multiple editable adenines, eVLP delivery enriched single or dual edits per allele and reduced higher-order editing events (Fig. 6A–D and Supplementary Fig. S4A–D). In parallel, eVLP delivery mitigated gRNA-dependent and gRNA-independent off-target DNA editing (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. S4E). A likely explanation is that transient RNP delivery limits the duration of intracellular editor exposure, thereby reducing opportunities for sequential bystander and off-target deamination. This interpretation is consistent with prior studies showing that transient RNP delivery can improve editing purity relative to plasmid-based expression [36]. Such a reduction in bystander and off-target editing may be particularly valuable for therapeutic applications, in which cleaner allelic outcomes are central to both safety and efficacy.

While our structure-guided approach successfully enhanced Deam-P32 activity, our mutation screening was limited by practical constraints. We primarily evaluated single mutations and selected rational combinations at three representative loci, but could not test all possible combinations of the 11 variants generated. This is a notable limitation because synergistic effects between mutations are frequently observed in protein engineering. Individual substitutions with minimal standalone activity can exhibit substantial improvements when combined, as exemplified by L78F, which showed moderate enhancement alone but dramatically reduced bystander editing when combined with P103S/A105R and C-terminal extensions (Fig. 2H–P). Alternative combinations involving mutations classified as low-performers such as Y17R, I42T, or R146Y might yield improved editors in multi-mutant configurations. Future high-throughput screening and machine learning approaches could systematically explore these combinatorial possibilities, potentially uncovering OpenABE variants with further enhanced activity and precision.

In summary, our work showcases how integrating AI-driven protein design with structure-guided engineering can unlock new capabilities in genome editing. We took an AI-designed adenine deaminase that was initially underperforming and transformed it into two OpenABE editors that rival the editing efficiency of the best evolved TadA variant (ABE8e) while offering cleaner editing outcomes. These new editors alleviate the longstanding issues of bystander mutations and off-target activity, and their efficacy in mtDNA editing further broadens the scope of base editing to include previously challenging genomic targets. The success of OpenABEs suggests that the landscape of ABEs need not be limited to a single enzyme lineage; rather, computationally generated enzymes can be harnessed and honed to diversify and improve our editing toolkit. Our results thus provide a framework for base editor development, leveraging AI-generated scaffolds as a starting point and elevating them through structure-guided design, ultimately expanding the enzyme toolkit beyond the canonical base editors. Collectively, OpenABE variants and the design principles behind them could enable the development of more precise and versatile base editing technologies, accelerating both research applications and the advancement of safer gene therapies.
