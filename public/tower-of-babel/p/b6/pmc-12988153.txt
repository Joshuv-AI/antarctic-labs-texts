First abundance estimate for greater Caribbean manatees (Trichechus Manatus Manatus) in Belize
Authors: Holly H Edwards, Jennifer F Moore, Nicole Auil Gomez, Jamal A Galves, Angeline Valentine, Celeshia Guy Galves, Armando J Ubeda, Anmari Álvarez-Alemán
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPopulation status of the Greater Caribbean manatee (Trichechus manatus manatus) is unknown across most of its geographic range, including the western part of the Caribbean in Central America. No statistically derived estimates of abundance are available for this region, but the population appears to be low and possibly stable or declining. To assess abundance and distribution of manatees in Belize, we conducted aerial surveys and estimated abundance by accounting for detection using a framework designed for marine mammals. Using a generalized additive model (GAM), we predicted abundance of manatees in 2014 and 2022 and how it varied across space as a function of spatial habitat covariates. We estimated that in the sampled area (within 500 m of flight path) there were 479 (95% CI: 275–857) manatees in 2014 and 555 (95% CI: 316–998) in 2022. Manatee abundance was higher closer to the coast, freshwater, and shallow waters. These estimates are the first for Greater Caribbean manatees in the western Caribbean Sea that account for sources of error such as observer perception bias, availability bias and spatial variation. Introduction

The Greater Caribbean manatee (Trichechus manatus manatus), also referred to as the Antillean manatee1, a sub-species of the West Indian or America manatee (Trichechus manatus), is found throughout the Wider Caribbean region in at least 20 countries2. The species’ distribution is patchy and fragmented3; and local abundance indices vary across its range. Listed as an endangered species by the International Union for Conservation of Nature and Natural Resources (IUCN) Red List of Threatened Species3 and proposed to be listed as endangered by the United States Fish and Wildlife Service4, the Greater Caribbean manatee population status is not well known in most of its geographic range. No statistically derived estimates of abundance are available for populations in the western part of the Caribbean in Central American, but its abundance appears to be low, and its status is thought to be stable, declining, or unknown3, 5. Although the sub-species is protected, it is still subjected to anthropogenic threats that likely contribute to the fragmented and patchy nature of its distribution and status3, 6–9. Poaching, pollution, loss of habitat, collision with watercraft, and entanglement in fishing gear and other man-made objects are known to negatively impact the well-being of Greater Caribbean manatees in Belize and throughout its range6, 10.

The manatee population along Belize and the Mexican Caribbean coast in Central America has historically been considered the largest in the Caribbean region11, 12. In Mexico, Belize, Guatemala, and Honduras a few studies have provided counts or minimum estimates of the numbers of manatees at local levels through aerial surveys or boat counts or expert opinion5, 12–17. The manatee population in Belize is believed to be about 1000 individuals5, however, no statistically derived estimates of population size are available. Although these studies have helped put the size of local meta-populations into perspective by providing minimum counts of manatees in each of those countries, no statistically robust population estimates that account for uncertainty are available for those regions. As such, the status of regional populations has been difficult to gauge. For example, Auil15 noted that counts obtained from aerial surveys in Belize varied seasonally, and because of high variation in indices of abundance adjusted for effort, the degree to which the results reflected the actual status of the population was undetermined15.

Surveys of manatee populations can be both expensive to conduct and logistically challenging, especially in areas that are remote or have little infrastructure. As a consequence, surveys of manatees are often conducted infrequently, and survey coverage is limited. In most cases, manatee censuses, for national or regional management, have historically relied on raw or uncorrected aerial survey count data to act as an index for population size. These numbers are usually considered to be undercounts of an unknown proportion because they don’t account for two important sources of error: (1) spatial variation from not covering all areas potentially occupied by the species, and (2) imperfect detection or not accounting for animals missed by observers during a survey (observer perception bias and availability bias)18–22. Thus, most raw or uncorrected aerial survey counts represent the minimum number of animals known to be alive on the day of the survey and present in the area23. Unfortunately, uncorrected counts, in some cases, can be misleading or of limited value since the amount of error in these measures and how close those estimates are to the true abundance are unknown23–26.

To assess the abundance of manatees in Belize and how it varies across space in relation to habitat covariates, we conducted aerial surveys and estimated abundance while accounting for detection using an established framework designed for marine mammals27. This was the first application of this framework to the Greater Caribbean manatee population. The estimates derived here are the first abundance estimates for Greater Caribbean manatees in the western part of the Caribbean Sea that account for sources of error such as observer perception bias, availability bias, and spatial variation23, 28–30. These surveys provide an assessment of the distribution (i.e. abundance across space) and population size for a country believed to have the largest population of manatees in the Caribbean. This information will help local resource managers assess the status and distribution of the manatee population in Belize to inform decisions concerning the protection of the species and its habitat (e.g., placement of protected areas, passing and implementing legislation to address conservation issues such as boat speed zones, and approving applications for development).

Methods

We estimated manatee abundance as a function of spatial habitat covariates using generalized additive models (GAMs) fit to data from aerial surveys. We included ancillary estimates of detection probability as an offset in these models. Total detection probability was estimated as the product of perception probability, based on double observer data, and probability of availability, based on a separate study combining manatee diving data, water visibility, and distance from the aircraft (details on each step are included below).

Aerial surveys

Surveys were conducted to count manatees along the coastline of Belize including coastal lagoons, rivers, and some offshore islands including Turneffe Atoll (Figs. 1 and 2). These surveys were part of a wider effort to assess manatee distribution and abundance in Mexico, Belize, and Guatemala. Two aerial surveys were conducted, one from a Cessna 206 fixed-wing aircraft (operated by Lighthawk, Inc.) on May 5–8, 2014, and one from a Cessna Skymaster fixed-wing aircraft (operated by Technology Service Corporation) on May 4, 6, and 7, 2022. The surveys were flown at an approximate altitude of 229 m and an approximate speed of 130 km/hr. Two observers independently collected the data. In 2014, one observer was seated in the middle seat of the aircraft, and the other in the right rear seat directly behind the first observer. In 2022, one observer was seated in the right front seat (co-pilot position) and one directly behind in the right middle seat. The flight route followed flight paths used in the past for this manatee population15, 16 and was recorded with a GPS at a 3-sec interval. In the 2022 survey, the iOS and iPadOS application Foreflight (Foreflight LLC, https://foreflight.com/products/foreflight-mobile/) was used to assist the pilot and observers in preparing for the survey and recording and navigating the route.

It is widely accepted that not all animals are or can be seen during surveys because of poor environmental conditions that obstruct the observer’s view or because animals are not available to be seen by the observer, for example, because they are submerged or because water visibility is poor19, 21, 22, 24. To account for this, dual observer data were used to estimate the probability of an observer detecting manatees given availability (see Martin et al. 2015 for details)23. In the Cessna 206, the rear door was off, and the primary observer acted as the data recorder and counted manatees from the open door at the back of the aircraft behind the secondary observer. In the Skymaster the front-seat observer was the primary observer and data recorder for the surveys. The secondary observer worked independently of the primary observer and conveyed to the primary observer all the manatees they sighted. The primary and secondary observers were the same for all survey days within each year and the primary observer was the same in both years. Once a group of manatees was encountered and both observers had the opportunity to see and mark the sighting (individual or group of manatees), the secondary observer would communicate to the primary observer the location and size of the group sighted. The primary observer confirmed the sightings and recorded, on a paper map, all the groups and numbers of manatees seen by both observers. A GPS was not used to record manatee sighting locations because it only marks the location of the GPS unit and not the actual location of the animals. Manatees can be seen up to approximately 500 m from the aircraft11 (at 228.6 m) and can be in small, narrow or irregularly shaped waterbodies where accurate coordinates can be difficult to capture with a GPS. When an individual or group of manatees was sighted observers recorded the total number of adults and calves seen. In a few cases (6) where group size could not be determined on the first pass (e.g., view was obscured by glare but known to be a manatee sighting) the aircraft would slow and circle if needed, and the total number of manatees seen on each additional pass was recorded. Individual manatees were not identified and tracked between passes. In this study, the count from the first additional pass was used in the analyses and was assumed to be accurate without error and was corrected for uncertainty in the analyses.

Observers recorded visibility along the flight paths during the survey using the ERSI product QuickCapture (http://quickcapture.ersi.com)31. The App interface was configured so that the observers could quickly and easily record those data values on the iPad instantaneously along the entire length of the flight path (including areas with and without manatees) using radio buttons. Visibility or turbidity of the water was measured on a scale from 1 to 4 based on the clarity of the water, category 1: clear water, bottom visible and manatees can easily be seen; category 2: bottom is visible, but detail is unclear. A manatee may be visible on the bottom, but it cannot be clearly seen; category 3: water may be clear, but the bottom is not visible due to depth; and category 4: water is turbid with less than 1 m of visibility. Preferred survey conditions included sunny days with light winds (< 28 km/hr with gusts < 46 km/hr); no conditions exceeded these limits.

Data processing

Data processing for analyses was done in ArcGIS Pro31. First, the flight paths from the aerial surveys were combined with the visibility categories collected with QuickCapture (categories 1–4). Next, buffers of three different sizes: 200, 300, and 500 m, were created on the right-hand side of the flight paths. Because the observers sat on the right-hand side of the plane and only looked out the right windows, the surveyed areas were only on the right side of the flight paths. We assumed that manatees more than 500 m away from the airplane were not visible to observers. These buffer distances were chosen following Martin et al. (2015), who estimated different availability probabilities based on these distance categories23. Next, a grid of cells (1 km2) was overlaid onto the sampled area. We calculated the area sampled in each grid cell for each distance and visibility category, as well as habitat covariates describing each grid cell. These covariates included the distance from each grid cell to the nearest land (mainland and islands), the distance from each grid cell to the nearest seagrass area, the distance from each grid cell to the nearest freshwater source, the proportion of seagrass in each grid cell (dense seagrass coverage, standing crop 80 g.m-2, cover > 70%; medium seagrass coverage, standing crop 11–80 g.m-2, cover 30–70%; or sparse seagrass coverage, standing crop 1–10 g.m-2, cover < 30%), and the average bathymetry value within each grid cell.

Manatee availability

We estimated the probability of availability (probability that an animal is close enough to the surface to be detected)27 of a manatee based on: (1) water clarity, from the visibility categories recorded during the surveys; and (2) the availability of a manatee to be seen by an observer (i.e., the amount of time the manatee was above a certain depth and thus visible to an observer). Manatee depth data, collected from multisensory digital acoustic tags (DTAG) placed on 18 manatees from a study conducted in Florida32 were used here to estimate the probability that a manatee would be above a given depth threshold. The probability that a manatee would be detected at different depths in each of the four visibility categories and at different distances from the aircraft was estimated using a manatee replica placed in the water and circled by observers in an aircraft to determine under what conditions the replica was visible or not23, 33. Observers flew over the replica to determine the maximum water depth and distance at which the manatee replica could be detected23. The probabilities determined from the Florida studies were combined with the visibility categories from the surveys in Belize to estimate the probability of manatee availability for each grid cell. If a grid cell had multiple visibility or distance categories, a weighted average for availability was used for the grid cell.

Observer perception probability

We fit the multinomial-Poisson mixture model34 to our data to estimate the perception probability (probability of detecting a manatee group, given it is available to be detected) for each observer. For this model, the manatee group sighted is considered the sample unit, with the response variable being a multinomial outcome representing whether the group was sighted by the primary observer, the secondary observer, or both observers. We assumed that perception probability did not vary with manatee group size. We fit this model to the data collected from both the 2014 and 2022 surveys and retained the perception probability for the primary observer for each year in subsequent analyses. This model was only used to estimate perception probability, and its uncertainty, for the primary observer. The model was fit to our data using R statistical software in the package ‘unmarked’35.

Statistical methods

Using a generalized additive model (GAM) with a Tweedie distribution, we estimated abundance of manatees and how abundance varied across space as a function of spatial habitat covariates (distance to nearest land, freshwater source, and seagrass, proportion of area covered in seagrass, and bathymetry; Figs. 3, 4, 5 and 6) for the two years of sampling, 2014 and 202227. Manatee counts () in each grid cell () were modeled with a log-link function comprised of smooth functions () of covariates in each grid cell () and an intercept (): . Spatial covariates were tested for correlation prior to model fitting to avoid multicollinearity issues using Pearson’s product-moment correlation and were included in the model as smooth terms using the thin plate regression spline. Only grid cells that were surveyed during the flights were used to fit the model. We used the estimate of total detection probability for each year calculated as the product of the observer perception probability and probability of availability as an offset in the GAM36 on the log-link scale. We then fit a series of models starting with the full model (including all five spatial habitat covariates) and removed covariates one by one using backward selection. The covariate with the highest p-value was removed and the new model was compared to the previous model using Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). Backward selection stopped when the previous model had a lower AIC than the new model with one less covariate. GAMs were fit using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) and a basis dimension of three to minimize overfitting, with the Tweedie power parameter (p) estimated during model fitting. The Tweedie distribution is an exponential distribution, where variance is given by the mean to the power p. Models were fit in R statistical software using the package ‘mgcv’37. Model fit was evaluated using a combination of diagnostic tests and visual inspection of the residuals using the gam.check and plot.gam functions in the mgcv37 package as well as the testResiduals function in the DHARMa package38. In addition, we used 10-fold cross-validation to assess predictive performance of the model and calculated root mean squared error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) based on predictions for held-out data.

Uncertainty was propagated from both the spatial model and the detection model using a bootstrapping approach. Beta distributions were constructed using the estimates of perception probability and availability probability36. A pair of random values were drawn in each bootstrap iteration for perception probability and availability from their corresponding beta distributions27. The pairs were used to calculate total detection probability for each grid cell, the GAM was then refit using the total detection probability as an offset, and then abundance and its variance were predicted for the water area within each grid cell. Mean abundance in each grid cell was estimated across the bootstrap iterations and the variance was calculated as the variance of the estimates plus the mean of their variances. Total abundance was the sum of the mean abundance estimates across all grid sampling units36.

Results

Detection estimates

The survey path varied slightly between years, and conditions were fair to very good during both survey years. The 2014 survey occurred over four days and covered 2779.3 km. Observers counted 297 manatees (Fig. 1) of which 236 were detected by the primary observer, with a perception probability (probability a manatee group was detected by the observer given that it was available) of 0.780 (95% CI: 0.678–0.857). The 2022 survey occurred over three days and covered 2507.8 km. Observers counted 287 manatees (Fig. 2), of which 200 were sighted by the primary observer with a perception probability of 0.554 (95% CI: 0.457–0.648). The mean estimated probability of availability for manatees across all sites was 0.747 (95% CI: 0.500–0.954) for 2014 and 0.805 (95% CI: 0.507–0.966) for 2022. In 2014, 60% of sightings were of individual animals with an average groups size of 1.9 manatees and a maximum group size of 20. In 2022, 65% of sightings were of single individuals with a mean group size of 2.0 manatees and a maximum groups size of 9.

Abundance estimates

We fit a single generalized additive model (GAM) with a Tweedie distribution (p = 1.284) to the data from both years (2014, 2022) using only the observations from the primary observer (Table S1). The most parsimonious model included year, and the amount of water area sampled within each grid cell, as well as the 5 spatial habitat covariates: distance to the nearest coastline, distance to the nearest freshwater source, distance to the nearest seagrass, proportion of grid cell with seagrass, and the average bathymetry for each grid cell (Table S1, S2). Evaluation of pairwise correlations among spatial covariates indicated no problems with multicollinearity (all correlations < 0.418, Table S3). The amount of water area sampled within each grid cell was included to account for differences in how much of a grid cell was sampled across the study area. The model converged successfully and explained 28.1% of deviance in manatee abundance, with an adjusted R2 of 0.04. Cross-validation showed low absolute prediction error (RMSE = 0.57; MAE = 0.16). Residual diagnostics (Figure S1) suggest no significant deviation from the expected distribution (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test p = 0.43) indicating no strong patterns in the residuals, but there is evidence of overdispersion (dispersion = 1.52). There was no strong evidence of temporal (ACF within bounds; Figure S2) or spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I = 0.027 for 2014; I = 0.0175 for 2022).

We estimated that in the sampled area (within 500 m of the flight path) there were 479 (95% CI: 275–857) manatees in 2014 and 555 (95% CI: 316–998) in 2022. Figures 3, 4, 5 and 6 show the distribution of manatees across the country with areas in green and blue predicted to have higher manatee abundance than areas in yellow. For the spatial covariates, the model showed that manatee abundance was higher closer to the coast and a freshwater source, as well as, in shallow waters. Manatee abundance increased in sites from 0 to 12 km from seagrass and then declined in sites further than 12 km from seagrass. In addition, manatee abundance increased in sites with 0 to approximately 70% coverage of seagrass and then slightly decreased in sites with 70–100% of seagrass (Fig. 7).

Discussion

This study provides the first abundance estimate for Greater Caribbean manatees in the Western Caribbean in Central America, specifically in Belize, that account for detectability. Unlike other surveys in the region, the methods used here provided an estimate of the number of manatees in Belize while accounting for uncertainty or bias (spatial variation and manatees missed during the survey from imperfect detection), an important component for assessing the population over time. Past studies only provided locations and minimum counts of manatees13, 15, 16.

We integrated sources of data about the probability of availability from studies in Florida to fill gaps in information for the Greater Caribbean manatee. As Martin et al. 2015 stated, estimates from one study can be “borrowed” and applied to other studies23. This was necessary because data on the probability of availability for this population do not exist and obtaining those data would require a separate study which would be time and cost prohibitive. We recognize that there could be differences in behavior between the two subspecies that could affect availability; however, we believe the data on Florida manatees is an appropriate surrogate for those in Belize given the similarities in habitat and the effects of visibility and sea state on availability. This model is amenable to improvements and once data are available for this region or subspecies it can be integrated into the model to reflect any differences in availability there may be between Florida and Greater Caribbean manatees.

For each survey we reported a point estimate and 95% confidence interval for manatee abundance within the sampled area. These estimates are similar between years with an overlap in the confidence intervals. This means that there is no significant difference in manatee abundance between the two years. The modeling approach here can also be used to extrapolate abundance estimates across a larger area than was sampled based on the spatial covariates (Gowan et al. )27; however, there are some caveats with this extrapolation. For this study, manatee experts in the region estimate there is a total of 9803.935 km2 of possible manatee habitat, of which 7% was sampled in 2014 and 8% was sampled in 2022. If we extrapolate across the entire area based on the spatial covariates in our top model, we estimate an abundance of 2074 (95% CI: 1002–4461) in 2014 and 2045 (95% CI: 1983–8751) in 2022. The estimate is slightly lower in 2022, but the confidence intervals again overlap showing no significant difference in the estimates between years. However, one of the caveats with this extrapolation is that the sampled area was not chosen at random across the total area. In this situation, almost all the sampling was along the coastlines and islands in what is likely the most optimal manatee habitat, while sampling at random would have included sampling both optimal and sub-optimal manatee habitat. Therefore, these extrapolated abundance estimates are likely an overestimation of the true abundance. In addition, the relationship between covariates and abundance would be more accurate if habitat had been sampled across a wide range for each of the covariate values. In this situation, this was not the case as the sampled area was primarily along the coastline of the country near freshwater sources. This again could lead to overestimating manatee abundance, especially in sites further away from the coastline and freshwater sources.

Results from the two surveys appear to show little change in abundance between 2014 and 2022; however, these trends are confounded by a few factors. Belize has complex habitats, for instance, ranging from clear, deep offshore reefs to muddy lagoons and rivers. Availability of manatees may not be the same as for Florida manatees, therefore the estimate used here may not reflect the actual measure of availability of manatees in this region. Furthermore, manatees in Belize can move freely into and out of adjacent countries which may also increase bias since the number of manatees in the Belize survey area may change from one survey to the next although it is likely not significant. For example, one telemetry tagged female manatee from Mexico, travelled 240 km between Chetumal Bay, Mexico to the south of Placencia Lagoon in Belize. Another manatee travelled to and from Mexico to the Southern Lagoon in Belize39. A genetic study has shown that manatees in Belize likely have low migration in the Northern Caribbean40. Surveys in both Mexico and Guatemala conducted just before and after the Belize surveys in 2014 counted 83 and 45 manatees respectively (B. Morales and E. Quintana per. comm.). The amount of transboundary movement is not known, but it is possible that manatees in this region share a home range that may include more than one country. More information from satellite telemetry and genetics are needed to better understand movement behavior in the region and additional region-wide surveys that include adjacent countries should be considered. Other data sources, when available, such as survival and mortality can be integrated with aerial survey data to better assess population trends for the region as Hostetler et al. 2021 did in an integrated population model for Florida manatees41.

Anthropogenic pressure on the manatee population in Belize is increasing. Historically, poaching was believed to have the greatest impact on the population. Since 1997 when documenting manatee strandings began, watercraft collisions have been the main cause of manatee deaths in the country increasing from an annual mean of 10 detected deaths a year before 2010 to an annual mean of 25 a year from 2010 to 20196. According to the International Monetary Fund (2019), Belize’s tourism has grown rapidly since 2016 and the potential for future growth of that industry is high, from 2010 to 2018 tourist arrivals increased at a rate of 8.6% per year (https://www.imf.org/-/media/Files/Publications/WP/2019/wpiea2019267-print-pdf.ashx). Much of Belize’s tourism focuses on its marine resources and according to the Belize Tourism Industry Association the top three most popular tourist attractions are marine related, including visits to the barrier reef system, marine protected areas and offshore islands (https://marfund.org/en/wp-content/uploads/2022/08/Theme-4-The-Importance-of-Belizes-Marine-Resources-t-oOur-Tourism-Product-Linette-Canto-BTIA.pdf). As tourism in Belize grows and use of its marine resources increases, monitoring the manatee population and assessing its distribution becomes increasing more important for resource managers who are tasked with developing effective management strategies to balance the use of marine habitats with conservation. Additionally, strengthening management to maintain the country’s manatee population given the uncertainties of climate change is warranted42–44. According to the Fisheries Department, encouraging sustainable conservation through scientific research and stakeholder knowledge is a key objective for them. This study provides critical information to help coastal zone managers and other groups make sound decisions concerning the protection of the manatee population in Belize, considered by many since the first national survey to be the last manatee stronghold of the region12.
