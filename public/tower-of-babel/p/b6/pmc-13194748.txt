Clinical impact of proteinuria and blood pressure variability on long-term outcomes after percutaneous coronary intervention
Authors: Jongheon Jeong, Byung Sik Kim, Woohyeun Kim, Hyungdon Kook, Yonggu Lee, Jin-Kyu Park, Jeong-Hun Shin, Hwan-Cheol Park, Jinho Shin, Young-Hyo Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Proteinuria and blood pressure variability (BPV) are associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes in coronary artery disease (CAD). However, their combined prognostic impact after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is unknown. To investigate the association of proteinuria and BPV with long-term outcomes after PCI, we analyzed 2,539 patients from an observational PCI registry. Proteinuria was defined as ≥ 1 + on dipstick urinalysis at index hospitalization. BPV was calculated as the standard deviation of systolic blood pressure at follow-up visits and dichotomized at the median. The patients were categorized into four groups based on proteinuria and BPV. The primary endpoint was net adverse clinical events (NACE; all-cause death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, any revascularization, or major bleeding) over 5 years. During a median 5.64-year follow-up period, NACE occurred in 689 patients (27.1%). Both proteinuria and high BPV were independently associated with higher NACE risk. Concomitant proteinuria and high BPV had the highest risk of NACE (hazard ratio, 1.696; 95% confidence interval, 1.341–2.147 vs. no proteinuria and low BPV). The addition of proteinuria and BPV to conventional risk models significantly improved the risk discrimination and reclassification of NACE. Simple dipstick urinalysis and routine BPV assessment may provide complementary prognostic information in patients undergoing PCI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47258-3.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality globally1. Among various modifiable risk factors, high blood pressure (BP) is recognized as a pivotal determinant of CAD progression and prognosis2,3. Recent evidence suggests that beyond absolute BP values, blood pressure variability (BPV), the fluctuation in BP, has independent prognostic implications for cardiovascular events and mortality in patients with CAD4. BPV likely reflects hemodynamic stress and vascular dysfunction that are not captured by single BP measurements, making it increasingly relevant for secondary prevention of CAD5.

Proteinuria, an established marker of renal dysfunction and microvascular injury6, is a well-recognized independent predictor of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, regardless of the presence of chronic kidney disease (CKD)7,8. It also serves as an indicator of systemic vascular injury and heightened cardiovascular risk, a relationship consistently observed in patients with CAD9,10. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests a potential interplay between proteinuria and increased BPV11, raising the possibility of a synergistic contribution to cardiovascular risk. However, the combined prognostic effect of proteinuria and BPV in patients with established CAD remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the synergistic effects of proteinuria and BPV on the long-term clinical outcomes in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) to address the critical gap in optimizing secondary prevention strategies for CAD.

Methods

Study participants

This study utilized data from the Hanyang University Medical Center (HYUMC) registry (NCT05935397), an observational registry database that consecutively enrolled patients who underwent PCI for CAD between January 2012 and December 2016 at Hanyang University Seoul Hospital and Guri Hospital in Korea5,12. During the study period, 3,525 consecutive patients were included in the registry. After applying the exclusion criteria (repeat admissions, n = 288; in-hospital death, n = 110; use of first-generation DES, n = 7; loss to follow-up within 6 months, n = 244; insufficient baseline medical records, n = 31; and absence of dipstick proteinuria data, n = 306), 2,539 patients were eligible for the study. Patients were categorized into four groups according to proteinuria status (≥ 1 + or < 1 + on dipstick testing)13,14 and systolic BP (SBP) variability, which was classified as low or high based on the median value of the standard deviation (SD) (Fig. 1).

Data collection and patient management

Baseline demographic characteristics, cardiovascular risk factors, medical history, laboratory findings, procedural details, and discharge medications were systematically collected by trained research coordinators, under the supervision of the principal investigators, using standardized case report forms. Cardiovascular risk profiles included hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, CKD, smoking status, and previous history of myocardial infarction, stroke, or PCI. The left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was assessed using transthoracic echocardiography, and detailed angiographic and procedural characteristics were recorded during the index hospitalization. Medication use at discharge was recorded and included antiplatelet agents, anticoagulants, statins, nitrates, renin–angiotensin system blockers, β-blockers, calcium channel blockers, diuretics, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists. All patients were treated according to contemporary clinical guidelines, and medication regimens, including dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin combined with clopidogrel, ticagrelor, or prasugrel), were prescribed at the discretion of the treating physician15,16. Routine clinical follow-up occurred 1 month after the procedure, followed by subsequent visits every 3 months or more as clinically necessary.

Blood pressure measurement and variability

Blood pressure was measured, at each scheduled outpatient follow-up visit, using an automated oscillometric device (OMRON HEM-907XL, Kyoto, Japan). The patients were seated comfortably and allowed to rest quietly for at least 5 min before BP measurement. For each patient, visit-to-visit SBP variability was assessed as the SD of SBP across follow-up visits. Only patients with at least three outpatient SBP measurements available during follow-up were included for BPV calculation. To ensure an appropriate temporal relationship, only SBP measurements obtained prior to the occurrence of any clinical endpoint were used for BPV assessment. According to the registry design, SBP measurements obtained during follow-up were compiled for up to 5 years after discharge or until a maximum of 20 outpatient readings were available per patient. Patients were then categorized into low or high BPV groups according to the median SD value. For sensitivity analyses, alternative BPV metrics were assessed, including the average real variability (ARV; the mean absolute difference between consecutive BP readings) and the coefficient of variation (CV; SD divided by the mean SBP)17.

Proteinuria measurement

Proteinuria was assessed as part of routine clinical care using a dipstick urinalysis performed once during index hospitalization. Urine protein was measured using the AUTION dipstick test (ARKRAY Inc., Kyoto, Japan), and analyzed with an automated urine chemistry analyzer (AX-4030, ARKRAY Inc.). The performance of the assay was supported by external quality control data from the Korean Association of External Quality Assessment Services. Dipstick proteinuria measurements were graded according to the manufacturer’s instructions as negative, trace (approximately 15 mg/dL), 1+ (30 mg/dL), 2+ (100 mg/dL), 3+ (300 mg/dL), and 4+ (1000 mg/dL). In this study, proteinuria status was classified as present (≥ 1+) or absent (< 1+)18.

Outcome definitions

The primary outcome assessed in this study was net adverse clinical events (NACE) evaluated up to 5 years after the index procedure. NACE was defined as the occurrence of major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) or major bleeding. MACCE included all-cause death, nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI), nonfatal stroke, or any revascularization. Secondary outcomes comprised the five individual components of NACE. The clinical endpoint definitions followed the recommendations of the Academic Research Consortium19. MI was diagnosed when typical ischemic symptoms were accompanied by new electrocardiographic changes or imaging evidence of myocardial injury, and with elevated cardiac biomarkers exceeding the 99th percentile upper-reference limit. Peri-procedural MIs were not included. Stroke was defined as a new-onset neurological deficit that was attributable to an acute focal vascular event in the central nervous system, confirmed by neuroimaging and neurological evaluation, and required hospitalization. Any revascularization was defined as PCI or coronary artery bypass grafting of either the target or nontarget vessels. Major bleeding was defined as type 3 or type 5 bleeding according to the Bleeding Academic Research Consortium (BARC) criteria19,20.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables, and as means ± SDs for continuous variables. The distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and visual inspection of the Q–Q plots. Between-group comparisons were conducted using the chi-square test for categorical variables, and either a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for normally distributed continuous variables or the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables.

The cumulative incidence of clinical events was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were generated to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the association between the combined proteinuria and SBP variability categories and clinical outcomes. The models were adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, presentation with MI, current smoking, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, CKD, previous PCI, previous stroke, LVEF, troponin-I, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), multivessel disease, chronic total occlusion, the use of anticoagulants, statins, angiotensin blockers, or β-blockers, and the average SBP during follow-up. As a sensitivity analysis, additional Cox regression models were fitted in which baseline SBP was used as an adjustment variable instead of average SBP during follow-up. Furthermore, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was applied as a sensitivity analysis to address potential confounding among the four study groups. Propensity scores were estimated using a multinomial logistic regression model including the same covariates as those used in the multivariable Cox regression. Stabilized weights were calculated and applied to weighted Cox proportional hazards models, and covariate balance before and after weighting was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs).

The predictive performance of the combined classification for NACE was assessed using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (time-ROC) curve analysis and calculation of the area under the curve (AUC). Reclassification improvement was evaluated using net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) metrics. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with RStudio (version 2024.12.0; RStudio, PBC, Boston, MA, USA), and a two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics according to proteinuria status and SBP variability are summarized in Table 1. Patients with proteinuria and high BPV were older (67.3 ± 12.4 years) than those without proteinuria and low BPV (62.2 ± 11.6 years, p < 0.001). The proportion of men ranged from 77.8% in the proteinuria-and-low-BPV group to 59.9% in the no-proteinuria-and-high-BPV group (p < 0.001). The BMI was comparable across the groups. The prevalence of acute MI at index presentation was greatest in the proteinuria-and-low-BPV group (66.5%). Hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and CKD were more frequently observed in the proteinuria-and high-BPV group (all p < 0.001), followed by hypertension in the no-proteinuria-and-high-BPV group, and diabetes mellitus and CKD in the proteinuria-and-low-BPV group. Patients with proteinuria and high BPV also showed the lowest eGFR and the highest troponin-I and HbA1c levels (all p < 0.001). Multivessel disease and chronic total occlusion were least frequent in the no-proteinuria-and-low BPV group (p = 0.006 and p = 0.012, respectively).

At the time of hospital discharge, angiotensin receptor blockers, calcium channel blockers, diuretics, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists were most frequently prescribed in the proteinuria-and high-BPV-group, whereas beta-blockers were used more often in the proteinuria-and-low-BPV group. The median number of outpatient SBP measurements per patient during follow-up was 18 (interquartile range, 7–20). The mean SBP at follow-up was highest in the proteinuria-and high-BPV group, followed by the no-proteinuria-and-high BPV group (p < 0.001). There was no significant difference in diastolic BP (DBP) between the groups (p = 0.329). The BPV indices (SD, ARV, and CV) were significantly greater in the high BPV groups compared with those in the low BPV groups (all p < 0.001).

Relationship of proteinuria severity with SBP variability

The relationship between dipstick proteinuria severity and BPV indices, as well as the mean follow-up BP, is presented in Fig. 2 and Supplementary TableS1. The BPV indices, including SD, ARV, and CV of SBP, were significantly higher in patients with greater proteinuria severity (all p < 0.001). The BPV indices increased significantly in the 1 + proteinuria category compared with those in the negative or trace proteinuria category. The mean follow-up SBP progressively increased with the degree of proteinuria, whereas the mean DBP did not differ significantly across the proteinuria categories (p = 0.293).

Association of proteinuria status and SBP variability with clinical outcomes

The median follow-up duration was 5.64 years (interquartile range: 2.09–8.48), during which NACE occurred in 689 patients (27.1%). Table 2 presents the associations among proteinuria status, SBP variability (per 1-SD increase), and adverse clinical outcomes. In multivariable analyses adjusted for demographic, clinical, and procedural covariates, the presence of proteinuria (≥ 1 + on dipstick) was significantly associated with a higher risk of NACE (HR 1.530, 95% CI 1.279–1.830), MACCE (HR 1.326, 95% CI 1.074–1.637), major bleeding (HR 1.806, 95% CI 1.408–2.315), and all-cause mortality (HR 1.946, 95% CI 1.351–2.803). Higher SBP variability, regardless of the metric used (SD, ARV, and CV), was independently associated with an increased risk of NACE, MACCE, major bleeding, and several individual outcome components. For example, each 1-SD increase in the SD of SBP was associated with an 18.8%, 12.2%, and 21.8% higher risk of NACE, MACCE, and major bleeding, respectively. To assess effect modification, an interaction term between proteinuria status and SBP variability (per 1-SD increase) was added to the multivariable Cox models. No statistically significant interactions were observed for any clinical outcomes (all p > 0.05; Supplementary Table S2). In additional analyses stratified by dipstick proteinuria severity, a graded increase in the risks of NACE, MACCE, major bleeding, and all-cause mortality was observed with increasing proteinuria categories, whereas no clear dose–response relationship was noted for nonfatal myocardial infarction or repeat revascularization (Supplementary Table S3).

Clinical outcomes according to combined proteinuria status and SBP variability

The Kaplan–Meier curves for the cumulative incidence rates of NACE, MACCE, and major bleeding, stratified by combined proteinuria status and SBP variability, are shown in Fig. 3. Across all three endpoints, patients with both proteinuria and high BPV experienced the highest event rates, followed by those with proteinuria and low BPV and those without proteinuria but with high BPV (overall log-rank p < 0.001 for all comparisons).

Table 3 summarizes the incidence rates and HRs for primary and secondary outcomes across the four groups. After adjusting for covariates, the proteinuria-and-high-BPV group exhibited a significantly higher risk of NACE (adjusted HR 1.696, 95% CI 1.341–2.147), MACCE (adjusted HR 1.503, 95% CI 1.142–1.979), and major bleeding (adjusted HR 2.264, 95% CI 1.623–3.158) than the reference group (no proteinuria and low BPV). This group also exhibited a significantly increased risk of all-cause mortality (adjusted HR 1.765, 95% CI 1.064–2.926). Patients with proteinuria and low BPV also had elevated risks of NACE (adjusted HR 1.532, 95% CI, 1.161–2.022), MACCE (adjusted HR 1.392, 95% CI, 1.006–1.926), and major bleeding (adjusted HR 1.812, 95% CI, 1.200–2.736). In contrast, no significant associations were observed between the no-proteinuria and high BPV group.

For sensitivity analyses, SBP variability was quantified using ARV and CV instead of SD to define the high and low BPV categories (Supplementary Tables S4 and S5). Overall, the associations among combined proteinuria status, BPV categories, and clinical outcomes were consistent with the primary analysis. In an additional sensitivity analysis, baseline SBP was used as an adjustment variable instead of average SBP during follow-up. The direction and magnitude of the associations remained similar to those observed in the primary analysis (Supplementary Table S6). Furthermore, an IPTW analysis was conducted to further address potential confounding. After weighting, covariate balance was substantially improved, as indicated by reduced standardized mean differences (Supplementary Table S7 and Supplementary Figure S1). The results of the IPTW-weighted Cox model were generally consistent with those of the primary multivariable analysis, without material changes in the direction or magnitude of the associations (Supplementary Table S8).

Incremental prognostic value of proteinuria and SBP variability

The prognostic values of proteinuria and SBP variability for predicting 5-year NACE are illustrated in Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table S9. The base clinical model (Model 1) demonstrated an AUC of 0.688 (95% CI 0.662–0.713). Inclusion of proteinuria in the model (Model 2) increased the AUC to 0.691 (95% CI 0.666–0.717), without statistical significance (p = 0.374 vs. Model 1). Further addition of SBP variability (Model 3) improved the AUC to 0.698 (95% CI, 0.673–0.723), which was significantly higher than that of both Models 1 (p = 0.024) and 2 (p = 0.034). The reclassification analysis revealed consistent findings. Compared with Model 1, the addition of proteinuria significantly improved the NRI (0.080, p = 0.013) and IDI (0.009, p < 0.001). Adding SBP variability to Model 2 yielded further improvements in the NRI (0.103, p < 0.001) and IDI (0.014, p < 0.001). These results indicate that proteinuria and SBP variability provide modest incremental prognostic information beyond conventional risk factors.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to evaluate the combined impact of proteinuria and visit-to-visit SBP variability on the long-term clinical outcomes of patients with CAD who underwent PCI. The key findings of our study were: (1) both proteinuria and high BPV were independently associated with an increased risk of NACE, MACCE, major bleeding, and all-cause death; (2) patients with concomitant proteinuria and high BPV exhibited the highest risk of adverse clinical outcomes; these associations remained consistent across multiple sensitivity analyses, including alternative BPV metrics and an IPTW approach, supporting the robustness of the observed relationships; and (3) the addition of proteinuria and BPV to a clinical model with conventional cardiovascular risk factors a modest but statistically significantly improved risk discrimination and reclassification for NACE. These results suggest that proteinuria and BPV each provide independent and additive prognostic information on cardiovascular prognosis, underscoring the importance of assessing both proteinuria and hemodynamic variability parameters as part of comprehensive secondary prevention strategies for patients after PCI.

Proteinuria is widely recognized as a clinical indicator of vascular and renal injury and serves as a proxy for increased cardiovascular risk. Its presence is consistently linked to the development of cardiovascular disease and unfavorable cardiovascular outcomes, and this association persists even when kidney function is preserved6–9. In patients with CAD, proteinuria is independently associated with adverse prognosis. For example, Ota et al. reported that in patients with acute myocardial infarction, the presence of dipstick proteinuria on admission predicted a more than four-fold increase in long-term all-cause mortality, independent of eGFR21. Recently, Funakubo et al. demonstrated that among patients undergoing PCI, proteinuria (≥ 1 + on dipstick) was a strong predictor of cardiovascular and all-cause death regardless of eGFR or diabetes status9. Consistent with these findings, our study also demonstrated that proteinuria was independently associated with adverse long-term clinical outcomes in patients with CAD who underwent PCI, even after adjusting for conventional risk factors and kidney function.

In our study, proteinuria was associated with a higher SBP variability, suggesting a potential link between renal injury and hemodynamic instability. This finding is consistent with previous observations that proteinuria often coexists with increased BPV22,23, potentially reflecting shared underlying vascular mechanisms10,17. However, our findings also indicated that isolated high BPV in the absence of proteinuria was not associated with worse outcomes. This pattern aligns with prior PCI studies showing that hemodynamic abnormalities may confer limited prognostic impact without concomitant organ damage24. Given the interrelationship and individual prognostic implications of BPV and proteinuria, the coexistence of these two factors may reflect a greater cumulative burden of vascular and target organ injury, thereby conferring a higher risk of adverse outcomes. Our study evaluates the combined prognostic significance of proteinuria and BPV in patients with CAD who underwent PCI, highlighting their independent and additive contributions to long-term outcomes.

Although the precise mechanisms underlying the adverse associations of proteinuria and high BPV remain unclear, they are likely to involve shared pathophysiological pathways. Proteinuria reflects renal and systemic vascular injury, often accompanied by endothelial dysfunction and increased vascular stiffness, which reduces arterial compliance and may exacerbate BP fluctuations10,17,25. High BPV has been associated with autonomic dysregulation and systemic inflammation, both of which can aggravate vascular injury, particularly in the presence of proteinuria25–27. Importantly, both proteinuria and BPV are regarded as markers of target organ injury, and their coexistence may indicate a more extensive and severe burden of injury involving multiple organ systems2,3,28. These interrelated processes may partially explain the increased risk of adverse cardiovascular outcomes observed in this study. Further research on these mechanisms could clarify their precise roles and inform targeted interventions.

The findings from our study have significant clinical implications. First, the results underscore the need for a more comprehensive risk assessment in patients undergoing PCI by incorporating both proteinuria and visit-to-visit SBP variability into the prognostic evaluation. This suggests that beyond conventional BP control targets, clinicians should consider assessing proteinuria and monitoring BPV to better stratify risk and guide secondary prevention strategies. Second, proteinuria can be detected using a simple, inexpensive, and widely available dipstick test, making it a practical tool in routine clinical practice, especially for patients undergoing PCI. Despite its lower sensitivity than quantitative assays29, prior studies and our results indicate that dipstick proteinuria has significant prognostic value30,31. Identifying such patients early offers the opportunity for timely interventions, including optimization of BP management, renin–angiotensin system blockade, and lifestyle modifications, which may help attenuate both proteinuria and BPV. Finally, given the growing availability of frequent BP measurements during clinic visits, home monitoring, and wearable devices, a more precise evaluation of BPV is increasingly feasible32. Integrating these measures into regular follow-ups may facilitate early identification of patients at high risk for future events, especially in those with proteinuria at index hospitalization. Taken together, our results support the inclusion of proteinuria assessment and BPV monitoring as complementary components of long-term risk management in patients with CAD after PCI.

Our study had several limitations. First, as an observational analysis, it remains subject to inherent design limitations, including the possibility of residual confounding and reverse causality, despite extensive adjustments for demographic, clinical, and procedural variables. Selection bias may also have been present, even though consecutive patients from a prospective PCI registry were included. In addition, patients with concomitant proteinuria and high BPV were older than those in other groups. Although age was included in the multivariable models, residual confounding related to older age and cumulative disease burden cannot be completely excluded. Second, we were unable to account for several potentially important factors, including information on whether complete revascularization was achieved, adherence to post-PCI medications, changes in treatment regimens during follow-up, socioeconomic status, and lifestyle modifications after discharge. Third, home and ambulatory BP measurements were not systematically collected in the registry; therefore, BPV was derived only from office BP recordings, which may not fully capture out-of-office BP variability. In addition, proteinuria was assessed using a single dipstick urinalysis obtained at index hospitalization, and repeated measurements to distinguish persistent from transient proteinuria were not available. This may have resulted in non-differential misclassification, which would be expected to attenuate the observed associations. Fourth, adverse events related to BP-lowering therapy, including hypotension, bradycardia, syncope, electrolyte imbalance, and acute kidney injury, were not evaluated, and may have influenced the outcomes. In addition, the incidence of major bleeding in our cohort was higher than that reported in recent randomized trials or meta-analyses. This difference likely reflects the real-world nature of our registry, which included unselected PCI patients with heterogeneous bleeding risk profiles, longer follow-up duration, and contemporary antithrombotic practices. Similar bleeding rates have been reported in recent nationwide real-world studies33, supporting the clinical plausibility of our findings. Fifth, because the data were derived from two PCI centers within a single university hospital in Korea, the generalizability of our findings to other populations may be limited. Lastly, patient dropouts during the long follow-up period may have introduced bias. Future multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate our findings and clarify the causal relationships.

In conclusion, in this large PCI registry, both proteinuria and visit-to-visit SBP variability were independent predictors of long-term adverse clinical outcomes, including NACE, MACCE, major bleeding, and all-cause death. Patients with concomitant proteinuria and high BPV were at the greatest risk, and the combined assessment of these factors provided additional prognostic information beyond conventional risk factors. Given the simplicity and accessibility of dipstick proteinuria testing and the growing feasibility of BPV monitoring, incorporating these measures into routine follow-ups may assist with early identification of high-risk patients and guide targeted secondary prevention strategies in patients with CAD who undergo PCI.
