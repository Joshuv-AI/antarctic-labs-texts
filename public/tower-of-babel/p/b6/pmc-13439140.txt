Extrafascial out-of-plane continuous interscalene block using the C6 transverse process as a bony landmark -A case series-
Authors: Keisuke Nakazawa, Panramon Vanichvithya, Genki Iwama, Takefumi Kamiya, Osamu Kitajima, Shunichi Takagi, Kazuyoshi Nakanishi, Takahiro Suzuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExtrafascial interscalene block has been proposed to reduce phrenic nerve involvement while maintaining analgesic efficacy for shoulder surgery. However, a reproducible continuous catheter technique using a clear anatomical landmark has not been well described.CaseWe describe a case series of six patients undergoing shoulder surgery who received a continuous extrafascial interscalene block using the C6 transverse process as a bony landmark. Under ultrasound guidance, an 18-gauge Tuohy needle was advanced in a cranial-to-caudal out-of-plane trajectory toward the posterior tubercle of C6, and a catheter was placed within the middle scalene muscle. Continuous levobupivacaine infusion was maintained for 24 hours as part of multimodal analgesia.ConclusionsThis technique was associated with favorable postoperative analgesic profiles within a standardized multimodal regimen, without clinically apparent respiratory compromise. Given the absence of a control group, findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. The C6 bony endpoint may improve reproducibility.

Ultrasound-guided interscalene brachial plexus block is recommended by procedure-specific postoperative pain management (PROSPECT) guidelines as an effective analgesic strategy for shoulder surgery, including continuous catheter techniques when prolonged analgesia is required [1]. Conventional intrafascial interscalene block has historically been associated with a high incidence of hemidiaphragmatic paresis due to phrenic nerve involvement, which may limit its use in patients with reduced respiratory reserve [2,3]. Extrafascial techniques have therefore been investigated as strategies to preserve analgesia while reducing hemidiaphragmatic paresis and respiratory impairment compared with conventional intrafascial approaches [4–7]. These approaches have been described using a lateral-to-medial trajectory, positioning the needle or catheter tip immediately outside the brachial plexus sheath at the C5–6 level [4,5]. Extraplexus injection outside the brachial plexus sheath has been reported to provide effective analgesia comparable to an intraplexus approach, supporting further evaluation of targets outside the immediate perineural space for shoulder surgery [8].

In the context of multimodal analgesia for shoulder surgery, a reliable catheter placement method is needed that can be integrated into routine perioperative practice without requiring advanced expertise in fascial plane identification. The middle scalene muscle (MSM) is a substantially larger anatomical target than the narrow scalene groove; targeting the MSM body rather than the immediate perineural space may therefore offer technical simplicity and reproducibility while still allowing effective local anesthetic delivery. However, detailed technical guidance on achieving stable intramuscular catheter positioning within the MSM is limited. Continuous perineural infusion has been reported to prolong analgesia and reduce opioid requirements compared with single-shot techniques in a range of surgical settings [9]. A technique that targets a large, clearly identifiable anatomical structure while avoiding direct proximity to neural elements would therefore represent a valuable addition to the multimodal analgesic armamentarium across different levels of operator experience. Furthermore, with a lateral-to-medial approach, the catheter must traverse the MSM transversely before reaching the scalene groove at its tip; partial postoperative withdrawal could theoretically move the tip into the muscle belly and away from the target plexus. With our cranial-to-caudal approach, the catheter is advanced along the MSM parallel to the transverse process; partial withdrawal would theoretically be more likely to keep the tip within the groove region. To our knowledge, the influence of catheter insertion direction within the MSM on catheter tip stability relative to the scalene groove has not been evaluated.

Therefore, we developed a novel cranial-to-caudal out-of-plane technique using the C6 transverse process as a reliable bony landmark, based on the anatomical study of Martinoli et al. [10]. Unlike conventional lateral-to-medial extrafascial approaches, our technique advances the needle in a cranial-to-caudal direction toward the acoustic shadow of the C6 posterior tubercle, positioning the catheter tip within the MSM in close proximity to the scalene groove, as illustrated in Fig. 1. We hypothesised that this trajectory would keep the catheter more parallel to the scalene groove during cervical movement, potentially improving tip stability and the consistency of local anesthetic delivery. The present case series describes the feasibility of this novel technique and the analgesic outcomes observed in patients undergoing overnight-admission shoulder surgery within a standardized multimodal analgesic regimen.

Case Reports

We report a case series of six consecutive patients scheduled for shoulder surgery at Nihon University Itabashi Hospital. Written informed consent for case report publication and academic presentation was obtained from all patients in accordance with the institutional policy, and all consent forms were submitted to the Institutional Review Board of Nihon University Itabashi Hospital. This case series was approved by the Institutional Review Board (approval number: SH-0012, approved: 13 March 2026). This study was performed in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. This case series was prepared in accordance with the CARE guidelines for reporting of clinical cases.

All peripheral nerve blocks were performed after induction of general anesthesia under ultrasound guidance, by staff regional anesthesiologists or regional anesthesia fellows under direct supervision. A high-frequency linear probe was placed over the interscalene groove in transverse view. The C5, C6, and C7 roots were identified according to the method described by Martinoli et al. [10] (Figs. 2A–C). Representative ultrasound images demonstrate needle-tip contact with the posterior tubercle of the C6 transverse process, intramuscular spread of local anesthetic within the MSM, and catheter-tip visualization within the local anesthetic pool after placement (Figs. 2D–F). Patient positioning, pre-procedural ultrasound scanning, and planned needle trajectory are illustrated in Figs. 3A and B.

For all blocks, an 18-gauge Contiplex® Tuohy Ultra 360 100-mm needle (B. Braun Melsungen AG) was inserted approximately 2–3 cm cephalad to Erb’s point using an out-of-plane approach (Fig. 3C), with the ultrasound beam directed cephalad-to-caudad. The needle was then advanced through the MSM toward the acoustic shadow of the C6 transverse process. Upon contact with the posterior tubercle of C6, the needle was slightly withdrawn and 0.25% levobupivacaine (20 mL) was injected after negative aspiration (Fig. 3D). The catheter was then advanced 1 cm beyond the needle tip within the MSM. Under real-time ultrasound guidance, the catheter was subsequently withdrawn 5 mm to confirm that the tip was positioned within the MSM superficial to the C6 posterior tubercle (Fig. 3E). The catheter was secured using the fixation method shown in Fig. 3F. The bolus volume and subsequent infusion rate were based on institutional practice for continuous interscalene brachial plexus block analgesia.

General anesthesia was induced with propofol 1.0–2.0 mg/kg, rocuronium 0.6 mg/kg, and remifentanil 0.25–0.4 μg/kg/min, and maintained with inhaled sevoflurane targeting a bispectral index of 50–60. Dexamethasone 6.6 mg was administered intravenously at induction of anesthesia for multimodal analgesia and postoperative nausea and vomiting prophylaxis. Fentanyl 100 μg was administered approximately 60 min before the anticipated end of surgery as transitional analgesia. Intravenous acetaminophen 1000 mg (or 15 mg/kg in patients weighing less than 50 kg) was administered approximately 30 min before the end of surgery. Ondansetron 4 mg intravenously was given for postoperative nausea and vomiting prophylaxis in all cases. Neuromuscular blockade was reversed with sugammadex 2–4 mg/kg, and extubation was performed after confirming a train-of-four ratio of 100%.

Following extubation, respiratory function was assessed by visual inspection of thoracoabdominal movement for paradoxical breathing patterns. A postoperative chest radiograph was obtained on the day of surgery to evaluate for hemidiaphragmatic elevation. Formal assessment of diaphragmatic function using ultrasound or spirometry was not performed, as respiratory adequacy was evaluated clinically by standard post-extubation criteria: bilateral auscultation of breath sounds, visual inspection of thoracic and abdominal respiratory excursion, maintenance of SpO2 within normal limits in the operating room, assessment of conscious respiratory response, and postoperative chest radiography confirming the absence of hemidiaphragmatic elevation or other pulmonary complications. Dedicated diaphragmatic ultrasound to quantify excursion or fractional thickening change was not considered standard of care and was therefore not performed. Catheters were maintained with a continuous infusion of 0.25% levobupivacaine 6 mL/h, with a patient-controlled bolus dose of 0.25% levobupivacaine 3 mL via the peripheral nerve catheter (CPNB-PCA) and a lockout time of 30 min, supplemented by intravenous patient-controlled analgesia (IV-PCA) with fentanyl 10 μg/h baseline infusion, bolus dose 10 μg, and lockout time 10 min.

All patients received a standardized multimodal analgesic regimen. Once oral intake was permitted, patients received acetaminophen 400 mg (two 200-mg tablets) every 6 h, celecoxib 200 mg twice daily, and mirogabalin 5 mg twice daily, regardless of pain scores. Rescue analgesia was defined as any additional analgesic medication administered upon patient request beyond this scheduled regimen, including tramadol 37.5 mg/acetaminophen 325 mg as required; in addition, patient-activated IV-PCA bolus doses and CPNB-PCA bolus activations, as recorded by nursing staff, were also classified as rescue analgesia. Rescue analgesia was provided at the discretion of the attending clinicians.

Pain scores were assessed using the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS, 0–10) at extubation and at five postoperative time points: 2 h, 6 h, bedtime (21:00), 07:00 on the following morning, and between 10:00 and 12:00 on the day after surgery before discharge. The NRS assessment at extubation was performed only after the patient demonstrated sustained eye opening to verbal command, purposeful movement, and the ability to respond to the NRS verbally or by finger counting. Because residual effects of anesthetic agents may attenuate pain perception at this time point, extubation NRS scores should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Motor function of the distal upper extremity (wrist flexion and extension) was assessed using Manual Muscle Testing (MMT) at each time point, as the operative limb was immobilized in an arm sling, precluding proximal motor assessment.

In this case series, the patients were aged 22 to 82 years (four male and two female). The body mass index (BMI) ranged from 21.4 to 31.3 kg/m2. American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification was 1 or 2 for all patients. Procedures included arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (Cases 1, 3, 5), total shoulder arthroplasty (Case 2), and arthroscopic Bankart repair (Cases 4, 6). Full patient characteristics are presented in Table 1. Pain scores at extubation were 0 in every case. Five patients maintained NRS scores of ≤ 2 throughout the first 24 h without requiring rescue analgesia. One patient (Case 5: a 49-year-old male with a left rotator cuff tear) reported an NRS of 5 at 2 h postoperatively and received diclofenac sodium 25 mg intramuscularly, two fentanyl IV-PCA bolus doses, and one CPNB-PCA levobupivacaine 3 mL bolus via the peripheral nerve catheter as rescue analgesia. Pain scores subsequently remained at 0–2 from 6 h postoperatively onward.

Total postoperative fentanyl consumption (IV-PCA basal infusion plus bolus doses) ranged from 160 to 240 μg during the observation period (median, 190 μg). Case 5 had the highest total fentanyl consumption and was the only patient to require IV-PCA bolus activation.

Assessment of distal upper extremity motor function revealed complete recovery in all patients, with MMT grade 5 for wrist flexion and extension throughout the 24-h observation period. All patients received supplemental oxygen at 5 L/min via a facemask postoperatively, which was discontinued after 2 h in all cases. Continuous pulse oximetry monitoring was maintained overnight until the following morning, with no episodes of oxygen desaturation or respiratory compromise observed in any patient, including a patient aged 82 years (Case 1) and one with a BMI exceeding 30 kg/m2 (Case 5). Chest radiographs revealed no hemidiaphragmatic elevation in any patient. Following confirmation of respiratory stability and discontinuation of supplemental oxygen, oral fluid intake was commenced and scheduled analgesics were initiated from the evening dose onward. No catheter dislodgement occurred, and in all six cases, catheter tip position within the MSM was confirmed by linear-probe ultrasonography immediately prior to removal. All catheters were removed prior to discharge. All patients were discharged the following day.

Discussion

In this case series, we describe a novel cranial-to-caudal out-of-plane technique for continuous extrafascial interscalene block using the C6 transverse process as a consistent bony landmark. This approach enabled reproducible catheter placement within the MSM and was associated with favorable postoperative analgesic outcomes in most patients undergoing shoulder surgery as part of a standardized multimodal analgesic regimen.

The principal clinical finding of this study is that five of six patients maintained low postoperative pain scores (NRS ≤ 2) throughout the first 24 h without requiring rescue analgesia, while the remaining patient demonstrated transient early postoperative pain that responded promptly to rescue treatment. As this series lacks a control group, the analgesic outcomes observed reflect the integrated effect of the catheter technique within a standardized multimodal analgesic regimen; attribution of benefit to catheter infusion alone cannot be established from these data. These findings should therefore be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating.

The C6 transverse process was chosen as the needle endpoint because it provides a consistent, easily identifiable acoustic shadow under ultrasound that serves as both a haptic and an imaging target, independent of fascial boundary recognition. Fascial identification in the interscalene groove is acknowledged as technically demanding; the fascial layers may be incomplete, poorly echogenic, or misidentified under ultrasound, rendering extrafascial positioning less certain when the sheath itself is used as the reference [10–12]. By advancing the needle in an out-of-plane trajectory until the tip contacts the posterior tubercle of C6, the operator obtains a definitive, reproducible endpoint that does not depend on tissue contrast or operator experience in fascial identification. This bony landmark approach may therefore reduce technique variability and facilitate training, potentially broadening access to effective catheter-based multimodal analgesia. Furthermore, slow injection of local anesthetic within the MSM permits real-time visualization of intramuscular spread, which may help identify and avoid inadvertent intraneural injection before the full volume is administered.

Positioning the catheter tip within the body of the MSM using a cranial-to-caudal trajectory may theoretically maintain its proximity to the scalene groove during postoperative cervical movement; however, it is important to emphasise that this proposed advantage remains theoretical. The present study did not include serial ultrasonographic confirmation of catheter tip position during the postoperative period, and therefore cannot provide objective evidence of improved catheter stability. In the present series, no catheter dislodgement occurred over the 24-h observation period, and in all six cases, catheter tip position within the MSM was confirmed by linear-probe ultrasonography immediately prior to removal, confirming that the catheter remained within the MSM at the end of the infusion period. However, this single-timepoint confirmation immediately prior to removal does not exclude the possibility of transient tip displacement during the intervening period. Therefore, the observed analgesic outcomes cannot be attributed to catheter stability in the present study. This concept should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and will require prospective evaluation with serial imaging. The cadaveric study by Franco et al. demonstrated that the brachial plexus sheath membrane is only 1–2 mm thick and is traversed by loose connective tissue septa internally [11], supporting the hypothesis that local anesthetic deposited within the MSM can diffuse across the sheath in a volume-dependent fashion. The effective distance for local anesthetic spread from needle tip to nerve has been quantified in ultrasound-guided interscalene block, suggesting that placement within a few millimetres of the sheath is sufficient for block success [13].

The choice of continuous infusion rather than a single-injection technique is central to this approach. Local anesthetic deposited at an extrafascial site is subject to more rapid systemic absorption than intrafascial injection, due to the higher vascular density at the external surface of the brachial plexus sheath [12], which shortens the effective duration of sensory blockade. In the overnight-admission setting of our institution, continuous catheter-based delivery mitigates the inherent time-limitation of single extrafascial injection and allows titration of infusion rate according to patient response [14]. Furthermore, rebound pain following block offset has been reported to affect approximately half of patients after single-injection interscalene block for shoulder surgery [9,15]; continuous catheter delivery may mitigate this phenomenon by maintaining local anesthetic delivery beyond the offset period of the initial bolus.

Systemic opioid administration represents a potential confounder when interpreting analgesic outcomes in the context of multimodal analgesia. In the present study, all patients received a standardized IV-PCA regimen with a basal infusion of fentanyl 10 μg/h. Total postoperative fentanyl consumption ranged from 160 to 240 μg (median, 190 μg), indicating relatively limited inter-patient variability. While the contribution of systemic opioids to overall analgesia cannot be excluded, the relatively narrow range of fentanyl consumption (range, 80 μg) across patients suggests that differences in postoperative pain scores were unlikely to be driven primarily by variability in systemic opioid administration alone. Nevertheless, formal attribution of analgesic outcomes to the catheter technique cannot be made in the absence of a control group.

Block procedure time was not routinely recorded in this case series, and therefore formal assessment of procedural efficiency or technical difficulty was not possible. This represents an acknowledged limitation of the present study. Regarding body habitus, Case 5, the only patient requiring rescue analgesia, had the highest BMI in the cohort (31.3 kg/m2). This observation may reflect either increased technical difficulty associated with larger body habitus or a relatively lower effective local anesthetic dose per unit body weight, as a fixed volume of 20 mL was administered to all patients irrespective of body weight. These observations highlight the need for prospective evaluation of technique performance and dose optimisation in patients with higher BMI.

No clinically apparent respiratory compromise was observed in any patient, including one aged 82 years and one with BMI exceeding 30 kg/m2, within the context of routine clinical assessment. While respiratory safety was not a primary endpoint of this case series, this observation is consistent with prior evidence that extrafascial injection may reduce hemidiaphragmatic paresis compared with intrafascial techniques [4,5]. However, objective assessment using diaphragmatic ultrasound or spirometry was not performed, and prospective controlled studies are required to confirm the respiratory effects of our approach.

There are several limitations to this case series. First, this series comprised only six patients, limiting generalizability and statistical inference. Second, there was no control group, and therefore the analgesic contribution of the catheter technique cannot be isolated from that of the multimodal analgesic regimen. Third, respiratory function was assessed by chest radiography and visual inspection alone; subclinical hemidiaphragmatic paresis cannot be excluded. Fourth, catheter tip position was confirmed ultrasonographically only at removal, and serial confirmation during the postoperative period was not performed. Fifth, motor assessment was limited to the distal upper extremity owing to arm sling immobilization. Sixth, the series included heterogeneous surgical procedures, which may have contributed to the variability in postoperative pain intensity. Seventh, the block procedure times were not formally recorded. The anatomical spread of local anesthetics following injection near the posterior tubercle of the C6 transverse process has not been evaluated in cadaveric or imaging studies, and the precise diffusion pathways toward the brachial plexus sheath remain uncertain and warrant further investigation.

In this preliminary case series of six patients undergoing shoulder surgery, the novel out-of-plane C6 transverse process landmark technique for continuous extrafascial interscalene block was associated with favorable postoperative analgesic profiles as part of a standardized multimodal analgesic regimen, without clinically apparent respiratory complications. Given the absence of a control group, causal inference cannot be established, and these findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. The use of the C6 bony endpoint may represent a reproducible and operator-friendly approach to catheter placement within the MSM, independent of fascial boundary recognition.
