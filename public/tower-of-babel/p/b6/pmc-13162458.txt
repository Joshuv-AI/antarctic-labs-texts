RNA modification-mediated multidimensional crosstalk: a novel perspective on gene expression regulation
Authors: Yan Wang, Chunxu Yang, Rongjie Lin, Huan Ling, Yong-Zhen Xu, Qiuji Wu, Yunfeng Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRNA modifications play pivotal roles in regulating RNA metabolism. Notably, they do not function in isolation, but crosstalk with DNA/protein modifications and other RNA modifications to form multi-layered regulatory networks that cooperatively fine-tune gene expression. In this review, we propose a two-dimensional, mutually exclusive “cis/trans” and “direct/indirect” framework to delineate the mechanisms and functions of four reciprocal modification axes including RNA-DNA, RNA-histone, RNA-non-histone, and RNA-RNA interactions. Furthermore, this RNA modification-centered crosstalk provides groundbreaking insights into translational applications across disease therapy, tissue engineering, and plant biotechnology. We also address current technical challenges and outline future directions to accelerate translational research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13059-026-04037-7.Keywords: RNA modifications, DNA modifications, Post-translational modifications, Crosstalk

Introduction

RNA modifications have emerged as a fundamental, dynamic layer of post-transcriptional gene regulation. To date, over 170 modifications have been identified [1], including N6-methyladenosine (m6A) [2], 5-methylcytidine (m5C) [3], N1-methyladenosine (m1A) [4]. The deposition, removal, and recognition of these sequence-unaltered marks on RNA bases or ribose moieties are mediated by specialized writer, eraser, and reader proteins, respectively, enabling precise spatiotemporal control of RNA metabolism [5–8].

Emerging evidence reveals that RNA modifications do not act in isolation. Instead, they engage in extensive cross-regulation not only with DNA modifications, histone modifications, and non-histone post-translational modifications (PTMs), but also among different RNA modifications themselves. Such multi-layered crosstalk constitutes additional layers of gene expression regulation and plays fundamental roles in a wide range of physiological and pathological processes.

Here, RNA modification-centered interactions are classified using a two-dimensional, mutually exclusive framework: (1) “cis/trans” is determined by molecular association—cis describes molecules within a single functionally linked (spatially proximal) cluster, while trans describes molecules in unrelated, spatially segregated clusters; (2) “direct/indirect” is determined by signal transduction—direct interactions require no intermediates (occurring via direct binding or catalysis), while indirect interactions depend on intermediates and are further divided into two subclasses: quantity-dependent (intermediate levels fluctuate, with signaling dependent on this concentration shift) and non-quantity-dependent type (intermediates remain quantitatively stable). Together, this framework generates four primary categories: cis-direct, cis-indirect, trans-direct, and trans-indirect (Fig. 1). Among these four interaction modes, cis-direct and trans-indirect interactions (dominated by the quantity-dependent subclass) are the two predominant core modes. Cis-direct interactions act as the fundamental local regulatory mode, characterized by direct molecular binding that mediates diverse biological effects; trans-indirect interactions act as the major global regulatory mode, relying on intermediates for cross-molecular or cross-pathway crosstalk. Cis-indirect interactions constitute another crucial regulatory layer in eukaryotes, enabling synergistic/antagonistic crosstalk via intermediates. By contrast, trans-direct interactions are relatively rare in eukaryotes under physiological conditions, though exemplified by interchromosomal transcriptional regulation, and are predominantly observed in prokaryotes or eukaryotic pathological states.

In this review, guided by the “cis/trans” and “direct/indirect” classification, we systematically summarize the mechanisms and functions of RNA modification-centered multidimensional crosstalk in physiological/pathological contexts, while emphasizing its translational potential in disease therapy, developmental tissue engineering, and plant yield improvement. We also address current challenges and outline future directions anchored in utilizing single-cell/live-cell-based spatiotemporal single-molecule long-read sequencing, coupled with advanced bioinformatics analysis, to expedite translational progress.

The interplay between RNA modifications and DNA modifications

The crosstalk between RNA and DNA enables bidirectional information flow between modifications: RNA modification signals can be transmitted to DNA to fine-tune the complex epigenome, while DNA modification signals can conversely guide RNA modification to regulate the intricate epitranscriptome. Beyond individual nucleic acid modification, this bidirectional exchange creates a coordinated regulatory network that broadens the regulatory dimensions of biological information. The cis-direct mode, cis-indirect (non-quantity-dependent) mode, cis-indirect (quantity-dependent) mode, and trans-indirect (quantity-dependent) modes are the main forms of RNA–DNA modification crosstalk. Each of these modes has distinct molecular mechanisms that support its particular biological functions.

Cis-direct mode

There are three major subtypes in the cis-direct mode that mediates crosstalk between RNA modification and DNA modification: interaction of a single RNA modification regulator with both RNA and DNA modifications via physical binding, direct physical synergistic interactions between RNA modification regulator and DNA modification regulator, and direct competitive binding between RNA modification regulator and DNA modification regulator. These subtypes collectively underpin context-dependent regulation of nucleic acid modifications, with molecular evidence increasingly highlighting their biological significance.

A typical example of the first subtype (interaction of a single RNA modification regulator with both RNA and DNA modifications via physical binding) is exemplified by the viral invasion context (Fig. 2A (1)): YTHDF2 acts as an m⁶A reader to bind m⁶A on host RNA via its C-terminal YTH domain, while its N-terminal flexible loop (residues 1–120) forms hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions with 6 mA-DNA, and m⁶A-RNA binding stabilizes this loop to enhance YTHDF2-6 mA-DNA binding. This YTHDF2-mediated interaction boosts exogenous DNA phase separation to activate the cytosolic DNA sensor cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS), underpinning the DINA-EHIS (discriminative incorporation of nucleotide analogues into pathogenic nucleic acids to elevate host immune surveillance) strategy via prodrug QKY-613 for selective 6 mA incorporation into pathogenic nucleic acids to safely enhance immunity, with potential for cancer immunotherapy [9].

Direct physical interactions between RNA modification regulator and DNA modification regulator drive a crucial subtype of cis-direct crosstalk between RNA and DNA modifications that depends on conserved synergistic effects (Fig. 2A (2)). In particular, m⁶A readers recognize m⁶A-modified RNAs and recruit the DNA 5mC demethylase TET1 to target genomic loci, where TET1 catalyzes the removal of 5mC, controlling chromatin accessibility and downstream transcriptional activity [10, 11]. Transposable elements (TEs) are mobile genomic DNA sequences that alter positions or generate copies, thereby impacting genome structure, expression, and evolution. In human embryonic stem cells (hESCs), the m⁶A reader YTHDC2 binds m⁶A-modified RNAs transcribed from the primate-specific TEs LTR7/HERV-H. This interaction guides TET1 to demethylate 5mC at LTR7/HERV-H loci, maintaining their hypomethylation and transcriptional activity; this process further inhibits differentiation of hESCs via downstream effectors such as LINC-ROR [10]. By contrast, in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) cells, the m⁶A reader FXR1 interacts with m⁶A-modified RNAs and recruits TET1 to demethylate 5mC at the promoters or enhancers of cancer-related genes such as WNT7B, BCL6 rather than TE loci. This enhances the chromatin accessibility and transcription of these oncogenes, ultimately driving ESCC development [11].

Besides, the antagonistic crosstalk can be mediated by direct competitive binding between RNA modification regulator and DNA modification regulator (Fig. 2A (3)). The insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding proteins (IGF2BPs) specifically recognize m⁶A-modified R-loops via their KH domains, mediate m⁶A-5mC at crosstalk by precluding DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) from binding the semaphorin 3 F (SEMA3F) promoter to induce its CpG hypomethylation, and this hypomethylation-upregulated SEMA3F inhibits cell growth, reduces migration, and enhances docetaxel chemosensitivity in advanced prostate cancer via the Hippo pathway [12].

Cis-indirect (non-quantity-dependent) mode

The crosstalk between RNA modification and DNA modification can also be mediated by intermediates that remain quantitatively stable but reposition spatially to link RNA modification regulator and DNA modification regulator. The chromatin remodeler ZmDDM1 physically interacts with the RNA m6A methyltransferase ZmMTA, and together they recruit the DNA 5mC methyltransferase DRM2 to deposit 5mC on the CHH sites in the 5' regions of m⁶A-modified genes via the RNA-directed DNA methylation (RdDM) pathway. This m⁶A-5mC crosstalk mechanism is indispensable for embryogenesis and endosperm development in maize (Fig. 2B) [13].

Cis-indirect (quantity-dependent) mode

This crosstalk mode is featured by RNA–DNA modifications cooperatively regulating the same biological process via quantity-dependent intermediates (Fig. 2C). In Arabidopsis, when DNA 6 mA coexists with RNA m⁶A on the same R-loops, it exerts dual effects—on one hand, it produces an additive effect to synergistically enhance the intensity of the intermediate R-loops with m⁶A; on the other hand, it antagonizes R-loop m⁶A-mediated transcriptional activation by recruiting repressive histone marks (another quantity-dependent intermediate, whereas R-loop m⁶A alone recruits active histone marks to promote transcription). Collectively, m⁶A and 6 mA interact with R-loops to coordinately modulate chromatin state and gene transcription by competitively recruiting distinct histone marks, ultimately determining the transcriptional status of target genes [14].

Trans-indirect (quantity-dependent) mode

Nucleic acid modification regulators are a special class of intermediates that mediate this crosstalk mode. In particular, DNA modifications at genomic loci encoding RNA modification regulators or their upstream signaling factors regulate the transcription of these regulators, thereby affecting global or locus-specific RNA modification profiles; on the other hand, RNA modifications on mRNAs encoding DNA modification regulators or their upstream signaling factors change the expression/stability of these regulators, indirectly reshaping the DNA modification landscape (Fig. 2D). This interaction has been conserved throughout evolution and is involved in a number of biological processes, including liver fibrosis, cancer progression and plant development.

For example, the DNA 5mC-STAT3-HIF-1α-YTHDF1-m6A axis coordinates the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) in liver fibrosis [15]. DNA 5mC hypermethylation on SOCS3/PPARγ promoters suppresses their expression to activate STAT3 during the HSC activation-initiation stage. Elevated STAT3 then transactivates HIF-1α, which further stimulates YTHDF1 expression. In the perpetuation stage, YTHDF1 recognizes m⁶A on major collagen transcripts to increase mRNA stability and promote the overproduction of extracellular matrix [15].

In pancreatic duct epithelial cells, cigarette smoke condensate induces 5mC hypomethylation in the METTL3 (encoding RNA m⁶A methyltransferase) promoter, which upregulates METTL3 expression; elevated METTL3 then enhances m⁶A deposition on primary microRNA (miR)−25, thereby promoting excessive miR-25-3p maturation and ultimately pancreatic cancer progression [16]. In osteosarcoma cells, elevated DNMT1 enhances 5mC modification at the gene locus of NSun2 (encoding RNA m5C methyltransferase), which downregulates NSun2 expression and impairs its m5C-modifying function on mRNAs of anti-apoptotic genes AXL, NOTCH2, and YAP1 to inhibit their translation. Concurrently, DNMT1 directly hypermethylates the promoters of these genes to suppress their transcription; together, these dual changes repress anti-apoptotic gene expression and reduce the resistance of osteosarcoma cells to chemotherapy-induced apoptosis [17].

Notably, this RNA–DNA modification interplay mode forms a reciprocal feedback loop. In climactic fruit ripening, 5mC on the SlALKBH2 promoter is removed by the DNA demethylase SlDML2 [18]. In turn, increased SlALKBH2 erases the m⁶A modification on SlDML2 mRNA to improve its stability and expression, and SlDML2 further removes DNA methylation from the promoters of important ripening regulators like SlACS4 and SlFUL1 to activate their transcription [19]. The DNA methylation on ripening-related genes is dynamically controlled by this SlDML2-5mC-SlALKBH2-m6A-SlDML2-5mC feedback loop, which in turn controls tomato fruit ripening [18, 19].

Collectively, RNA–DNA modification crosstalk, with its diverse patterns of interaction, plays a critical role in maintaining the equilibrium of nucleic acid function. Its fundamental biological significance is highlighted by the fact that it supports both normal biological processes such as plant embryogenesis, fruit ripening, hESC differentiation, and disease progression like cancer, immune surveillance, and liver fibrosis. This interplay not only expands our knowledge of nucleic acid regulation but also provides potential targets for disease treatment.

The interplay between RNA modifications and histone modifications

Beyond RNA–DNA modification crosstalk, RNA-histone modification interplay constitutes another prominent type of dynamic crosstalk between the epitranscriptome and epigenome, facilitating mutual information transmission between RNA and chromatin. Core interaction modes include cis-direct, cis-indirect (non-quantity-dependent), trans-direct, and trans-indirect (quantity-dependent) modes, each with distinct molecular underpinnings.

Cis-direct mode

m⁶A modification regulators recruit histone modification regulators through direct physical interaction to alter histone modification marks, thereby regulating chromatin state and transcription. Chromosome-associated regulatory RNAs (carRNAs), including promoter-associated RNAs (paRNAs), enhancer/super-enhancer RNAs (eRNAs/seRNAs), and repeat RNAs, are typical targets exploiting this modulation mode [20–22].

On one hand, m⁶A modification on carRNAs exerts a negative regulatory role in chromatin state and transcription by promoting the deposition of repressive histone marks (Fig. 3A (1)) [23–25]. For instance, in mouse embryonic stem cells (mESCs), YTHDC1 recognizes m⁶A on IAP/ERVK/LINE1 transcripts and directly interacts with SETDB1, recruiting SETDB1 to retrotransposon-linked chromatin. This promotes histone H3 lysine 9 trimethylation (H3K9me3) deposition, silences retrotransposons, and represses the Dux-driven 2C-like program, ultimately maintaining mESC identity, preserving mESC self-renewal and pluripotency, and ensuring normal early embryonic development [20].

On the other hand, m⁶A modification on specific carRNAs—such as human γ satellite DNA II (GSATII) RNA and certain nascent RNAs—exerts a positive regulatory role by either facilitating the deposition of active histone marks (Fig. 3A (2)) or directly removing repressive histone marks (Fig. 3A (3)). For example, Huang et al. found that YTHDC1 recognizes m⁶A on human GSATII RNA, a repeat RNA, and recruits bromodomain protein 4 (BRD4, the reader of histone H3 lysine 27 acetylation, H3K27ac) to H3K27ac-marked satellites in human and mouse cells. Via an m⁶A-YTHDC1-BRD4-H3K27ac axis, this activates transcription of GSATII itself and other cis (intrachromosome) -associated centromeric/pericentromeric satellites (CenSats), ensuring proper chromosome segregation during mitosis [21]. Additionally, YTHDC1 interacts with KDM3B, histone H3 lysine 9 dimethylation (H3K9me2) demethylase, to recruit it to m⁶A-associated chromatin after recognizing m⁶A on nascent RNA, which is co-transcriptionally installed by METTL3/METTL14. In turn, KDM3B catalyzes the demethylation of H3K9me2, which relieves transcriptional repression, promotes Pol II deposition, and maintains the balance of gene expression essential for normal cell development and health, while preserving mESC pluripotency [22].

Intriguingly, histone modifications regulate m⁶A targeting and deposition on specific RNAs by altering chromatin status and accessibility, forming a complete bidirectional regulatory loop that finely tunes chromatin dynamics and transcriptional output, with implications for metabolic reprogramming and cell fate determination [26–30]. Specifically, active histone marks—such as H3K27ac coupled with H3K4me1 (marking active enhancers) and H3K4me3 (marking active promoters), as well as the H3K36 methylation family (H3K36me2/me3)—promote m⁶A deposition by enhancing recruitment of m⁶A regulators (Fig. 3B). In contrast, repressive marks (e.g., H3K9me2/H3K9me3/H3K27me3) block binding of these regulators to restrict m⁶A deposition (Fig. 3B) [26, 27]. Notably, this regulation process directly determines the presence or absence of m⁶A on RNAs, rather than its intensity [26]. Among these active histone marks, one prominent example of promoting m⁶A deposition is the H3K36 methylation family (H3K36me2/me3), which exhibits distinct regulatory patterns in different species. In animals, H3K36me3 directly interacts with METTL14 (a key component of the m⁶A methyltransferase complex, MTC), allowing MTC binding to elongating pSer2-Pol II and specific m⁶A deposition on adjacent nascent RNAs, such as pluripotency genes Oct4 and Sox2 mRNAs, accelerating degradation of these mRNAs and maintaining the balance between mESC pluripotency and differentiation [28]. While plants rely on H3K36me2 (instead of H3K36me3) for m⁶A guidance, the H3K36me2 is enriched at the 3′ ends of plant gene bodies, and the physical interaction between SDG8 (H3K36me2 writer) and FIP37 (core component of plant m⁶A MTC) mediates precise MTC recruitment [30]. This H3K36me2-guided m⁶A modification supports plant protein metabolism and regulates shoot apical meristem stem cell fate (via controlling STM/WUS mRNA stability), ensuring orderly aerial organ formation [29, 30].

Cis-indirect (non-quantity-dependent) mode

In the cis-indirect (non-quantity-dependent) mode, m⁶A modification regulators either crosstalk with histone modification regulators via stable intermediate-mediated interactions or compete with them for binding to the same intermediates, thereby altering histone modification marks to regulate chromatin state and transcription. This cross-kingdom evolutionarily conserved regulatory mode is critical for sustaining mESC identity and plant growth homeostasis, as well as promoting cancer progression (Fig. 3C (1)-(3)). Notably, LINE1 subfamilies serve as one of the key factors determining distinct crosstalk modes, and these varied interplay patterns cooperatively maintain ESC pluripotency, genome stability, and normal early embryonic development [20, 31, 32]. L1Md_T, a 2C-related retrotransposon with METTL3-preferred DRACH motifs, depends entirely on METTL3 for m⁶A. YTHDC1 recognizes m⁶A-L1Md_T, directly interacts with and recruits SETDB1 to inhibit the Dux-driven 2C-like program [20, 31]. In contrast, global LINE1 acts as an RNA scaffold, with m⁶A modification partially dependent on METTL3 and METTL16. YTHDC1 binds m⁶A-global LINE1, interacts with NCL to form the LINE1-NCL-KAP1 complex, and recruits SETDB1 via KAP1 to silence associated transposons and maintain mESC self-renewal [31]. Evolutionarily young subtypes like L1Md_A and L1Md_Tf show high basal m⁶A in mESCs, with greater elevation upon FTO knockout. FTO demethylates them to prevent YTHDC1-mediated degradation; stabilized RNAs recruit YY1/EP300, boosting active histone modifications to enhance chromatin accessibility and support ESC pluripotency and early embryonic development [32]. Similarly, depending on distinct functional needs, different IAP subfamilies use two distinct SETDB1-recruiting mechanisms in mESCs: For IAP subtypes such as IAPA_MM-int and IAPEz with activation propensity or requiring enhanced silencing, YTHDC1 reads m⁶A-modified IAP RNA and directly interacts with SETDB1 [20]; for initial silencing or steady-state repression of the IAPEz subtype, METTL3 directly interacts with SETDB1, independent of m⁶A and YTHDC1 [33].

Trans-direct mode

To date, well-documented instances of trans-direct mode linking RNA methylation to histone modification remain relatively rare. As previously noted, m⁶A on GSATII RNA utilizes the cis-direct mode to activate transcription of GSATII itself and other cis (intrachromosomal) targets. Notably, this regulatory m⁶A-YTHDC1-BRD4-H3K27ac axis also activates transcription of trans (interchromosomal)-associated CenSats, such as the trans-associated chr3 centromeric higher-order repeat (HOR) α satellite. These actions collectively ensure proper chromosome segregation during mitosis (Fig. 3A (2)) [21].

Trans-indirect (quantity-dependent) mode

This highly prevalent mode is primarily mediated by reciprocal modifications on molecules encoding regulators of the counterpart modification class: RNA modifications on transcripts encoding histone modification regulators, and histone modifications at loci encoding RNA modification regulators. Relevant concrete examples of these interactions are detailed in Table 1 [37–62].

Interestingly, RNA modifications on mRNAs that encode histone modification regulators alter the abundance of histone modification regulators, which in turn regulates histone modifications at the promoters of genes encoding RNA modification regulators, thereby changing the abundance of RNA modification regulators and further modulating RNA modifications on transcripts that encode histone modification regulators to form a positive feedback pathway (Fig. 3D (1)). For example, METTL3-catalyzed EZH2 mRNA m⁶A increases EZH2 levels, and EZH2 cooperates with SOX4 to promote H3K27ac modification at the METTL3 gene promoter, which elevates METTL3 expression, forming positive feedback to enhance temozolomide resistance in glioblastoma [46].

Additionally, a lactate-driven self-reinforcing circuit mediates reciprocal reinforcement between histone lactylation at the promoters of genes encoding RNA modification regulators and RNA modifications of glycolysis-associated RNAs (Fig. 3D (2)). For instance, H3K9la at the METTL1 promoter upregulates METTL1, which enhances PKM mRNA m⁷G to increase PKM2 and boost lactic acid production, ultimately promoting H3K9la at the METTL1 promoter; while H3K18la at the NSUN2 promoter increases NSUN2, which cooperates with YBX1 to mediate ENO1 mRNA m5C, upregulating ENO1 and enhancing lactic acid production to further strengthen H3K18la at the NSUN2 promoter, and both reciprocal loops drive colorectal cancer (CRC) progression via metabolic reprogramming [47, 54]. Moreover, lactic acid also mediates the crosstalk between non-coding RNA modifications and histone lactylation. The long non-coding RNA (lncRNA) protein disulfide isomerase family A member 3 pseudogene 1 (PDIA3P1) is stabilized through insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 1 (IGF2BP1) recognition of its m⁶A modification, which promotes glycolysis and lactate production by competing with miR-152-3p to protect glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) mRNA from degradation, and interacting with hexokinase 2 (HK2) to protect it from ubiquitination. Elevated lactate then upregulates histone H4 lysine 8 lactylation (H4K8la) on the promoters of bone morphogenic protein 7 (BMP7), ultimately promoting BMP7 transcription and ESCC progression [43].

Taken together, RNA structural and functional subtypes, interacting modification enzymes, and various physiological/pathological demands collectively determine the regulatory modes and effects of RNA-histone modification crosstalk. Furthermore, a single biological process may simultaneously employ distinct regulatory modes, contributing to the intricate regulatory landscape that includes stem cell biology, development and senescence, tissue degeneration and fibrosis, infection immunity, and cancer.

The interplay between RNA modifications and non-histone PTMs

Non-histone PTMs critically regulate their biological activity, structural stability, subcellular localization, and protein–protein interactions [63]. As shown in Fig. 4, a range of non-histone PTMs (including O-GlcNAcylation, methylation, acetylation, lactylation, SUMOylation, phosphorylation, S-nitrosylation, ubiquitination, and 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation) engage in crosstalk with various RNA modifications (including m⁶A, m5C, m⁷G, ac4C, adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I), and m⁶Am), which collectively regulate multiple biological processes including RNA/cellular metabolism, DNA damage and repair, telomere damage and senescence, immune defense, inflammation regulation, neuroprotection, stem cell maintenance, as well as tumorigenesis and progression. Details of crosstalk regarding site-specific non-histone PTMs, relevant RNA modifications, crosstalk modes, underlying mechanisms, and functions are provided in Additional file 1: Table S1 [48, 49, 54, 64–143]. Overall, trans-modes are far more prevalent than cis-modes in this regulatory crosstalk. Noteworthily, RNA-modification regulators stand out as the most prevalent non-histone class in this crosstalk.

Cis-direct mode

This mode is rare in such crosstalk and primarily occurs at DNA damage sites induced by double-strand breaks (DSBs). Specifically, ATM-catalyzed phosphorylation of METTL3 at S43 primarily directs its recruitment to DSBs loci, where METTL3 directly mediates m⁶A modification of DNA damage-associated RNAs. YTHDC1 then binds to these m⁶A-modified RNAs, promoting the accumulation of DNA-RNA hybrids and the recruitment of RAD51/BRCA1 for homologous recombination (HR)-mediated repair. Targeting this p-METTL3 (S43)-m⁶A crosstalk sensitizes cancers to chemo- and/or radiotherapy that elicit DSBs [105].

Cis-indirect (quantity-dependent) mode

This interplay mode is also scarce and mainly involves m⁶A-modified circular RNAs (circRNAs) mediating protein complex assembly. For instance, m⁶A modification of circNDUFB2 enables it to act as a scaffold, enhancing the interaction between TRIM25 and IGF2BPs to form the TRIM25/circNDUFB2/IGF2BPs ternary complex. This interaction promotes ubiquitination and proteasome-dependent degradation of IGF2BPs, thereby inhibiting the growth and metastasis of non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cells [101].

Trans-direct mode

This crosstalk mode is relatively common, primarily involving PTMs of RNA modification regulators that alter their catalytic activity or binding capacity to directly change RNA modification levels. Notably, this PTM-RNA modification crosstalk can synergize with histone and RNA modifications to coordinately regulate the identical biological process. In tumor infiltrating myeloid cells (TIMs), two lactylation sites (K281 and K345) on METTL3 directly enhance its ability to capture target RNAs. Meanwhile, H3K18 lactylation at the METTL3 promoter increases its abundance by promoting transcriptional expression. These two lactylation-mediated effects work synergistically to enhance m⁶A modification of JAK1 mRNA, which is recognized by YTHDF1, promoting its translation efficiency to increase JAK1 protein levels, ultimately activating the JAK1/STAT3 pathway and strengthening the immunosuppressive functions of TIMs [49].

Trans-indirect (non-quantity-dependent) mode

This interaction type is primarily involved in viral immune evasion, which is mediated by the impaired ability of retinoic acid-induced gene I (RIG-I) to sense viral RNA. For example, in myeloid cells, RIG-I, a cytosolic RNA sensor that binds to unmodified viral RNA and mediates the phosphorylation of IRF3 (Ser396) and IRF7 (Ser471/Ser472), as well as their subsequent nuclear translocation, is effectively impeded in its recognition by m⁶A modification of HIV-1 RNA. HIV-1 is able to evade innate immunity, because reduced phosphorylation of these two factors compromises the expression of the interferon (IFN)-I gene [125]. Significantly, the m⁶A-dependent immune evasion mechanism is also utilized by m⁶A-modified nonsegmented negative-sense (NNS) RNA viruses such as human respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), human metapneumovirus (HMPV), vesicular stomatitis virus (VSV), and members of the Pneumoviridae, Paramyxoviridae, and Rhabdoviridae families [144–147].

Trans-indirect (quantity-dependent) mode

This is another prevalent interaction mode characterized by the modulation of protein abundance and/or subcellular localization through non-histone PTMs, the levels of which may also be regulated by upstream PTM events.

As summarized in Additional file 1: Table S1, a major subgroup of these proteins are the RNA-modification regulators, whose PTMs engage in reciprocal interactions with RNA modifications to form positive feedback loops. For instance, METTL3-mediated m⁶A modification of IL-6 mRNA activates the IL-6-AMPK/SIRT1 axis to deacetylate METTL3 at K177, thereby promoting METTL3 nuclear translocation and enhancing m⁶A levels to drive cancer metastasis [138]. Intriguingly, RNA modification can also employ this crosstalk mode to interact with both histone and non-histone PTMs, and this interaction cooperatively constitutes a sophisticated positive feedback circuit to orchestrate identical biological processes, as exemplified by NSUN2-mediated m5C modification of ENO1 mRNA. This m5C modification boosts ENO1 abundance and lactic acid production; accumulated lactic acid then induces NSUN2 K356 lactylation and activates NSUN2 transcription via histone H3K18 lactylation at its promoter, further elevating NSUN2 to enhance ENO1 mRNA m5C and promote CRC progression [54].

Particularly, beyond covalent interactions that regulate non-histone PTMs, noncovalent modification of RNA modification regulators can also alter their stability and subcellular localization. For instance, SUMO-2/3 binds directly to the SUMO-interaction motif (SIM, 236–240 aa) of the m5C RNA methyltransferase NSUN2 through noncovalent bonds, modestly stabilizing NSUN2 and inducing its nuclear transport; this interaction further enables NSUN2 to catalyze m5C modification of its putative target genes PIK3R1 and PCYT1A, thereby promoting gastric cancer (GC) progression [75].

In conclusion, the crosstalk between RNA modifications and non-histone PTMs is predominantly mediated by trans-modes, whereas cis-modes are rare and confined to specific biological contexts such as DNA damage repair foci and scaffold structures. Notably, trans-modes drive reciprocal interactions between RNA modifications and PTMs of RNA modification regulators, forming positive feedback loops, and frequently coexist with histone modifications at promoters encoding RNA modification regulators to coordinately modulate biological processes. Collectively, this site-specific non-histone PTM-RNA modification crosstalk orchestrates critical physiological and pathological processes such as RNA metabolism, genomic stability, viral infection and tumorigenesis, and provides insights for targeted disease therapies and advanced tissue engineering.

The interplay and coordination between modifications of RNA species

Accumulating evidence highlights potential crosstalk across RNA species. The deep neural network method MultiRM identifies overlapping sequence preferences shared by multiple types of RNA modifications, among which same-nucleotide modification pairs show particularly strong associations, such as a correlation coefficient (ρ) of 0.895 for m5C/Cm, 0.931 for I/m1A, and 0.908 for Ψ/m5U [148]. Distinct RNA modifications further share regulators, for instance: m6A readers YTHDF1-3—particularly YTHDF2—interact with m5C-modified RNA bait, and genetic ablation of YTHDF2 leads to elevated m5C levels at multiple rRNA loci alongside impaired rRNA maturation in human cells; noteworthy, YTHDF1-3 and YTHDC1 bind m1A-modified RNAs, with YTHDF1 potentially enhancing m1A-mRNA translation while YTHDF2 modulates the stability of these mRNAs; additionally, ALKBH3 (an m1A eraser) can also remove m6A and m3C on tRNAs [149–152].

All of these offer a potential foundation for the interaction between RNA modifications, which can take place either in cis (where modifications are coordinated within the same RNA species) or in trans (where modifications are coordinated across different RNA species) (Fig. 5).

Cis-direct mode

As a well-known example of this crosstalk, tRNA modification circuits, where the deposition of one modification (or cluster) influences another in the identical tRNA molecule, are described in Additional file 2: Table S2 [153–176]. Such interplays occur in both the anticodon loop and main body of prokaryotes (e.g., Thermus thermophilus, Saccharomyces cerevisiae) and eukaryotes (e.g., Homo sapiens, Mus musculus). Frequently, these modifications follow well-defined sequential order, governed by inter-event cross-talk. Human tRNAPhe exemplifies this: in vivo modifications at positions 32, 34, and 37 follow a hierarchy—m1G37 first, then Cm32 and Gm34, and o2yW37 last—precisely sustaining nervous system-related gene expression, supporting neural development, and preventing intellectual dysfunction [156, 177]. Additionally, some tRNA modifications crosstalk lacks strict sequence dependence, instead relying on functional synergy/antagonism or structure-driven dependency. For example, in Thermus thermophilus, class I tRNA m⁷G46 (TrmB-catalyzed) positively regulates Gm18/m1G37/m1A58 to maintain tRNA stability and support ≥ 70 °C survival, while Ψ55 (TruB-catalyzed) inhibits Gm18/m1A58 to avoid excessive thermal stability and enable 50 °C adaptation [172, 173, 175]. In Saccharomyces cerevisiae, Ψ55 (PUS4-catalyzed) shapes the tRNA T-loop’s stable tertiary structure (not sequence-dependent), recruiting methyltransferases for m1A58/m5U54 modifications to stabilize overall tRNA conformation and ensure translational fidelity [174].

These tRNA modification crosstalk plays pivotal biological roles: First, it upholds foundational functions—maintaining tRNA structural stability and core translation processes (e.g., accurate codon decoding, translation efficiency) [153, 157, 166, 178]; second, it supports cellular and organismal homeostasis, including tissue/subtype-specific translation, stress tolerance, and basic growth/survival [160, 161, 175]; third, it mediates environmental and evolutionary connections, such as microbiome-regulated metabolic balance, responses to environmental signals (e.g., queuine), and conserved modification networks [165, 168–170].

Beyond tRNA, crosstalk between RNA modifications, including m5C-m⁶A, A-to-I editing-m⁶A, and m⁷G-m⁶Am, also occurs via the cis-direct mode within individual mRNA or snRNA molecule. For instance, in p21 mRNA, NSUN2-mediated m5C (C2079) and METTL3/METTL14-mediated m⁶A (A2044/A2061) synergistically enhance its translation, thereby driving cellular senescence under oxidative stress [179]. Additionally, in hESCs (H1/H9), HEK293FT, and HeLa cells, m⁶A negatively regulates A-to-I editing by altering the RNA secondary structure from duplex to single-stranded regions (enriched in the RRACH motif), which impedes the ability of ADAR enzymes to bind their target transcripts [180]. Moreover, the m⁷G cap is a prerequisite for m⁶Am modification: PCIF1 (an m⁶Am methyltransferase) selectively converts 2′-O-methyladenosine (Am) to m⁶Am at the first nucleotide downstream of the m⁷G cap in mRNA, thereby increasing the stability of a subset of mRNAs [181]. Conversely, FTO (an m⁶Am eraser) exerts demethylase activity at the same position (downstream of the m⁷G cap) in snRNAs to regulate splicing [182].

Cis-indirect (non-quantity-dependent) mode

The aforementioned crosstalk between distinct RNA modification types within a single RNA molecule requires intermediates in some cases. For example, in tRNASer (CGA/UGA/GCU), intermediate Ses1 interacts with Trm140, enhancing its efficiency in converting i⁶A37 (MOD5-catalyzed)/t⁶A37 (SUA5-catalyzed) to m3C32, thereby ensuring tRNASer-specific m3C32 [155]. In the 3'UTR of Sox9 mRNA, m5C (Nsun4-mediated) and m⁶A (Mettl3-mediated) recruit intermediates YTHDF2 and eEF1α−1 to form the NMYE complex, co-regulating Sox9 translational reprogramming and promoting chondrogenic differentiation of bone-marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) [183]. Moreover, intermediate HRSP12, a binding partner of m⁶A reader YTHDF2, preferentially recognizes m1A and the consensus GGUUC motif. m1A and m⁶A then act cooperatively to enhance the HRSP12-YTHDF2 interaction, which recruits the RNase P/MRP complex to trigger endoribonucleolytic cleavage of mRNAs and circRNAs bearing both modifications, accelerating their degradation [184].

Trans-direct mode

Trans-direct regulation, where modifications of RNAs are coordinated across distinct RNA species via direct physical binding or catalysis, is not common. A classic example of this model is that mammalian ac4C within Kozak sequences at the −1 position adjacent to an AUG start codon disrupts the hydrogen bond interaction between cytidine (C) and the hypermodified t6A at nucleotide 37 of the initiator tRNAiMet, and generates repressive structures to inhibit mammalian translation initiation [185].

Trans -indirect (quantity-dependent) mode

This mode is primarily mediated by “RNA modifications targeting mRNAs encoding RNA modification regulators”. For example, m⁶A modifications at the conserved site in interferon-inducible ADAR1p150 mRNA are recognized by YTHDF1, which mediates ADAR1 translation, thus inducing ADAR1-mediated A-to-I editing and preventing excessive activation of IFN responses [186]. Moreover, in glioma stem-like cells (GSCs), METTL3 depletion downregulates the expression of A-to-I editing enzymes ADAR by reducing m⁶A modifications on their transcripts and concurrently upregulates their inhibitory counterpart ADARB2 via non-m⁶A-mediated indirect regulation, and these two effects together reduce A-to-I editing and impair the oncogenic signaling of GSCs [187]. In addition to m⁶A- A-to-I editing crosstalk, this mode is also utilized by m⁶A-ac4C, and m1A-m⁶A RNA modifications crosstalk. The m⁶A modifications on NAT10 mRNA are recognized by IGF2BP1, which elevates NAT10 mRNA translation and subsequent ac4C modification of ACOT7 mRNA, driving tumorigenesis in ovarian cancer [188]. In hypertrophic scars (HTS), the upregulated m1A demethylase ALKBH3 demethylates the c.52-m1A site in METTL3 mRNAs 5′ UTR, impeding YTHDF2 (m1A reader)-dependent degradation of METTL3 transcripts. Elevated METTL3 catalyzes m⁶A modification on mRNAs of key extracellular matrix components COL1A1 and FN1; these m⁶A-modified mRNAs are then recognized by m⁶A reader YTHDF1, boosting their stability and ultimately driving HTS pathological transformation [189].

The other well-characterized instance of the trans-indirect (quantity-dependent) mode is bidirectional m⁶A interplays in virus-host crosstalk. Upon viral infection, the host employs its m⁶A machinery to modulate viral RNA m⁶A status, either restricting infection or supporting viral lifecycles. Conversely, viruses reprogram the host’s m⁶A epitranscriptome primarily for self-benefit, with rare virus-specific exceptions.

For the RNA virus SARS-CoV-2, infection relocalizes host METTL14/ALKBH5 to the cytoplasm, aiding METTL3/METTL14 in catalyzing m⁶A on viral RNA strands, and host YTHDF2 recognizes these modifications to suppress replication; In turn, SARS-CoV-2 triggers global elevation of host mRNA m⁶A (with altered localization and motif shifts) to dampen innate immunity, as upregulated m⁶A aids viral entry via “membrane trafficking/apoptosis” genes while downregulated m⁶A weakens antiviral responses [190].

For RNA viruses of the Flaviviridae family (e.g., DENV, HCV, ZIKV), the host METTL3/METTL14 MTC deposits m⁶A on viral RNAs, and YTHDF family proteins (YTHDF1-3) bind to these m⁶A-modified viral RNAs to restrict viral assembly and infectious particle production [191]. Reduced m⁶A on host CIRBP mRNA inhibits intron retention of the long isoform (CIRBP-L), maintaining the dominant short isoform (CIRBP-S) and supporting DENV/ZIKV/HCV replication; elevated m⁶A on host RIOK3 mRNA promotes its YTHDF1-dependent translation. Noteworthy, RIOK3 promotes DENV/ZIKV but inhibits HCV, a virus-specific difference [192].

For the DNA virus Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus (KSHV), during lytic reactivation (the initiation of lytic cycle), host-mediated m⁶A modification on multiple kinetic classes of viral transcripts supports lytic progression, while YTHDF2 degrades these m⁶A-modified transcripts to inhibit lytic viral gene expression (cell-type specific) [193]; this phase is also accompanied by KSHV-driven reduction in m⁶A levels across a significant portion of the host transcriptome [193]. As lytic reactivation proceeds to lytic replication, KSHV further uses intermediates (viral ORF50/RTA, PAN RNA; host ERK/MAPK) to reprogram cellular pathways for sustained lytic activation [194]. During latency, KSHV latent proteins (e.g., LANA) and host factors (e.g., YY1) drive 5′UTR hypomethylation and 3′UTR hypermethylation in the cellular epitranscriptome, maintaining viral latency and supporting KSHV-induced cellular transformation via regulating oncogenic pathways and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [194].

In summary, crosstalk between different RNA modifications is characterized by four specific interaction modes: cis-direct, cis-indirect (non-quantity-dependent), trans-direct and trans-indirect (quantity-dependent). This interplay involves diverse RNA species (tRNA, mRNA, snRNA, viral RNA), modulates crucial biological processes (e.g., RNA metabolism, translation, cellular/organismal homeostasis, and differentiation/senescence), and pathological diseases (e.g., cancer, viral infection, and scar formation). Overall, this crosstalk is critical for gene regulation, holds great therapeutic promise, and may advance the development of vaccines and antiviral drugs.

Application of targeting RNA modifications-centered multidimensional crosstalk

As aforementioned, RNA modification-centered multidimensional crosstalk plays a significant role in regulating a number of physiological functions and pathological progression. Thus, targeting this multifaceted crosstalk centered on RNA modifications holds significant translational potential in disease therapy, developmental tissue engineering, and plant biotechnology. Currently, the major strategies for targeting this crosstalk include bioactive molecules and RNA therapeutics.

As shown in Table 2, bioactive molecules targeting RNA modification-centered crosstalk mainly include small-molecule inhibitors, bioactive prodrugs, and antibodies, with several (e.g., erlotinib, lapatinib, panobinostat) FDA-approved or under clinical investigation, holding great therapeutic potential in cancers, fibrotic diseases, and viral infections, as well as promoting ESC pluripotency.

Bioactive Molecules of Targeting RNA Modification-Centered Multidimensional Crosstalk

Abbreviations: 5-aza 5-Aza-2'deoxycytidine, LiCl Lithium chloride, 5S-G acetyl-5S-GlcNAc, 3-MPA 3-Mercaptopicolinic acid, 2-DG 2-Deoxy-D-glucose, 2,4-PDCA Pyridine-2,4-dicarboxylate, BSO L-S,R-buthionine sulfoximine, OGT O-GlcNAc transferase, PRV pseudorabies virus, OC ovarian cancer

Targeting modification enzymes (e.g., METTL3, DNMTs, ALKBH5), blocking protein–protein complexes (e.g., Elvitegravir inhibiting METTL3-STUB1 interaction), and controlling particular modification sites (e.g., #7586–3507 targeting NAT10 K823 crotonylation) are the main action modes of bioactive molecules. Notably, a combined intervention that targets various kinds of modifications offers new insights into improving therapeutic efficacy. For example, in preclinical glioblastoma studies, combination treatment with ALKBH5 inhibitor ALKBH5i and the EGFR inhibitor erlotinib hampers global m⁶A modification and ALKBH5 Y71 phosphorylation, respectively, which synergistically reduces tumor volumes and prolongs survival compared to either monotherapy [122]. However, currently, research on drug-mediated interference with crosstalk between different RNA modifications themselves remains scarce; thus, greater efforts are required to expand this area of study in the future.

In addition, RNA therapeutics represents another key application area leveraging RNA modification-centered crosstalk, where synergistic nucleobase-modified mRNAs drive advancements in somatic cell reprogramming and cell fate regulation. Specifically, synthetic mRNAs with uridine substituted by pseudouridine (Ψ) and cytidine by m5C exert synergistic effects: they significantly suppress innate immune activation, enhance protein expression, and enable efficient, non-integrating reprogramming of multiple human cell types into RNA-induced pluripotent stem cells (RiPSCs)—which faithfully recapitulate hESC properties—and facilitate the effective directed differentiation of RiPSCs into terminally differentiated myogenic cells [195]. Notably, the current state-of-the-art m5C/Ψ platforms are outperformed by the combination of N1-methylpseudouridine (m1Ψ)-based modification and m5C, increasing gene expression by up to ~ 44-fold while decreasing intracellular innate immunogenicity and enhancing cellular viability [196]. Thus, this improvement provides a safe and efficient approach for somatic cell reprogramming and cell fate regulation, with wide-ranging applications in basic research, disease modeling, and regenerative medicine.

Furthermore, by enabling site-specific epitranscriptomic modulation, RNA-guided RNA-targeting CRISPR systems, such as deactivated Cas13 (dCas13), are advancing modern precision RNA therapeutics. Specifically, these platforms facilitate precise deposition or ablation of RNA modifications at discrete RNA loci via guide RNAs and dCas13-conjugated modifying enzyme complexes [197]. They exhibit negligible off-target activity while elucidating epistatic relationships within RNA modification-centered multidimensional crosstalk, which refers to the hierarchical upstream–downstream regulatory networks that clarify the precise mechanisms of their interplay [198]. Two distinct systems enable spatiotemporal modulation of RNA modifications: the optimized PAMECR system leverages tissue-penetrant near-infrared (NIR) irradiation for robust, reversible, and concurrent modulation of RNA modifications across multiple transcripts, while a chemically inducible platform (CIP) integrated with the CRISPR-dCas13 toolkit achieves temporal control of site-specific RNA modification imprinting at specific transcripts via the inducer abscisic acid (ABA) [199, 200]. Notably, both systems possess broad application prospects: for instance, in plants, the PAMECR system leverages NIR-driven multi-transcript regulatory capability to align with yield-related growth stages (such as grain filling stages and stress response stages) for enhancing crop productivity, while the CIP platform achieves site-specific regulation induced by ABA, which suits the precise tuning of key genes during seed germination or floral development; in the field of stem cell-based tissue engineering, the reversible regulatory property of the PAMECR system enables the maintenance of stem cell pluripotency and directed differentiation, while the targeted regulatory function of the CIP platform refines critical differentiation checkpoints, thereby enhancing the quality of engineered tissues. Furthermore, the CRISPR-Cas inspired RNA targeting system (CIRTS), which is composed entirely of human-derived protein components to circumvent bacterial-derived dCas and attenuate immunogenicity risks [201], can be further enhanced by integrating spatiotemporal regulatory modules such as optogenetic or chemical induction to augment its in vivo precision, and is a promising precision therapy for various human diseases.

In conclusion, advancing strategies including bioactive molecules, nucleobase-modified mRNAs, and CRISPR-based RNA-targeting systems with safety and spatiotemporal control highlight the great translational potential of targeting RNA modifications-centered multidimensional crosstalk. Further refinement of biomarkers for evaluating treatment efficacy will help identify individuals most likely to gain sustained benefits.

Conclusions and perspectives

RNA modifications each have well-characterized individual functions; however, accumulating evidence underscores that RNA modifications-centered multidimensional crosstalk is pivotal for regulating gene expression. These interplays are extensively involved in the biological processes underlying physiology and pathology across both prokaryotes and eukaryotes, and hold broad application potential in various fields.

Herein, we first propose a mutually exclusive two-dimensional framework for classifying such crosstalk: “cis/trans” is defined by spatial association (cis: functionally linked, proximal molecules; trans: spatially segregated, unrelated ones), and “direct/indirect” by signal transduction (direct: binding/catalysis without intermediates; indirect: intermediate-dependent, subdivided into quantity-fluctuating and stable subtypes). Leveraging this framework, we more clearly delineate the flow of gene regulatory information, not only between the epitranscriptome and either the epigenome or the epiproteome, but also within the epitranscriptome itself, and further elucidate the underlying interaction mechanisms, thereby deepening our understanding of gene regulatory networks.

From these RNA modifications-centered multidimensional interplays, two key points merit our highest prioritized attention. First, most interactions preferentially target specific chromatin regions or transcription units, with functional effects varying by gene locus and context (cell-, tissue-, temporal stage-, and environment-specificity). Notably, this multi-dimensional context-dependence directly drives divergent functional outcomes [202]. However, many existing research models do not mimic real-world natural conditions and lack detection methods that account for specificity such as site-specificity and cell-specificity. Second, multiple RNA modification-centric crosstalk events frequently co-regulate a single biological process. Apart from the aforementioned evidence that RNA modifications can crosstalk with either DNA modifications and histone marks [14] or histone marks and non-histone PTMs to coordinately regulate the same biological process [49, 54], another paradigmatic example involving all four modification types is the DNA methylation/histone acetylation-Circ_0001187-miR-499a-5p-RNF113A-METTL3 polyubiquitination-m⁶A axis in acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [77]. Circ_0001187, a tumor suppressor, is downregulated by the combined action of promoter DNA methylation and histone acetylation. It sequesters miR-499a-5p, thereby upregulating the E3 ubiquitin ligase RNF113A, which reduces METTL3 levels via K48-linked polyubiquitination, ultimately decreasing m⁶A modification on target mRNAs and suppressing AML progression [77]. However, few studies currently simultaneously detect multiple interactions centered on RNA modifications to more accurately dissect the mechanisms of these interactions and their regulatory hierarchical relationships.

Thus, to address the challenge of context-specific and multi-dimensional dissection of RNA modification-centered crosstalk, single-molecule sequencing technologies with high-resolution analytical capabilities should first be recommended for application. Single-molecule real-time (SMRT) enables base-level resolution mapping and phasing of diverse RNA modifications without motif-based inference [203]. Complementing these, novel single-nucleotide resolution assays target specific modifications: such as m6A-label-seq [204], m6A-SAC-seq [205, 206] and eTAM-seq [207] for m6A, m1A-quant-seq for m1A [208], m7G-quant-seq for m7G [209], HAC-seq for m3C [210], BID-seq for Ψ[211], Nm-Mut-seq for Nm [212]. Integrating these with RNA in situ conformation sequencing (RIC-seq) [213, 214] —which profiles global RNA-RNA spatial interactions—will be pivotal for identifying RNA modification-centered multidimensional crosstalk. Notably, single-base resolution methods for plant RNA modifications remain unavailable, a critical gap that highlights the urgent need to develop plant-adapted single-base resolution technologies for their profiling.

Second, full-length direct RNA long-read sequencing and novel bioinformatic analytical approaches should be utilized. For example, combining full-length direct RNA long-read sequencing with meRIP-seq enhances m⁶A detection accuracy by resolving both nucleotide and transcript-level modifications [215]. Supporting this, specialized bioinformatic tools have been developed: ELIGOS infers native RNA modifications at single-base resolution from Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) signals [216], while a machine learning algorithm trained on synthetic sequences predicts m⁶A with ~ 90% accuracy [217].

Third, single-cell resolution technologies enable the study of cell-type-specific modification crosstalk and also open new avenues for dissecting spatiotemporal regulatory coordination. Emerging tools such as sn-m6A-CT and scm6A-seq profile cell-type-specific m⁶A methylomes and transcriptomes [218, 219], while scCUT&Tag and EpiDamID enable simultaneous detection of histone PTMs and transcription [220–222]. Multimodal assays (MulTI-Tag, nano-CT [223, 224]) allow mapping of up to three epigenomic modalities in single cells. Integrating these approaches will improve our comprehension of RNA modification-centered multidimensional crosstalk in complex biological systems. Concurrently, live-cell imaging captures real-time dynamics of such crosstalk, shedding light on how they evolve during cellular transitions or pathology [225].

Fourth, to overcome limitation of vitro cell lines and mouse models, in-depth investigations using clinically relevant systems such as patient-derived xenografts (PDX) or organoids (PDO)will greatly improve the biological validity and clinical relevance of identified crosstalk mechanisms, as they more faithfully recapitulate the in vivo microenvironment and intercellular communication networks.

In the future, targeting key nodes of RNA modification-centered interactions or simultaneously modulating these interacting modifications could pave the way for novel therapeutic and biotechnological strategies. Clinically, this strategy is especially promising for patients who have acquired drug resistance or a poor response to the single-agent medications. Precise targeting of these crosstalk nodes may improve ESC differentiation protocols for tissue engineering applications in developmental biology and regenerative medicine, enabling the production of functional tissues for transplantation. Manipulating RNA modification-centered crosstalk in plants could help create advanced plant biotechnology products and stress-resistant crops.

In conclusion, RNA modification-centered multidimensional crosstalk has significant potential in tissue engineering, plant biotechnology, developmental biology, and the treatment of human diseases. This review emphasizes their transdisciplinary significance and aims to motivate researchers to dissect their underlying mechanisms and translate basic discoveries into practical applications.
