Quantitative proteomic analysis of PK-15 cells infected with porcine circovirus type 3 using 4D-DIA approach
Authors: Dan Wang, Jiangwei Song, Jing Wang, Rong Quan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPorcine circovirus type 3 (PCV3) infection is clinically related to various diseases, including porcine dermatitis and nephrotic syndrome (PDNS)-like disease, respiratory disease, reproductive disorders, and gastrointestinal and neurological diseases. Since PCV3 infection was discovered in 2016, it has developed rapidly and has attracted much attention worldwide. However, specific preventive and therapeutic interventions are currently lacking. In this study, four-dimensional (4D) data-independent acquisition (DIA)-based quantitative proteomics detection combined with bioinformatics analysis were employed to quantitatively identify the differentially expressed proteins in PK-15 cells from the PCV3-infected group compared with those from the uninfected control group. A total of 194 cellular proteins were significantly altered in response to PCV3 infection, including 58 upregulated proteins and 136 downregulated proteins. In our Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis, these differentially expressed proteins were mostly associated with cellular anatomical entities, binding, cellular processes, biological regulation, catalytic activity, metabolic processes, developmental processes, protein-containing complexes and responses to stimuli. Our Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis revealed that the DEPs were predominantly involved in metabolic pathways, the cAMP signaling pathway, protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and the calcium signaling pathway. For the experiments, Western blotting (WB) was used to confirm the changes in important molecules. The differentially expressed proteins identified should contribute to a greater understanding of the mechanism of PCV3 replication and pathogenesis, as well as the host response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11259-024-10501-1.Keywords: PCV3, PK-15 cells, 4D-DIA proteomic analysis, Host response

Introduction

Porcine circovirus (PCV) is a nonenveloped virus with single-stranded circular DNA. Currently, four types of PCVs are known, namely, PCV1 to PCV4. Among these strains, PCV1 was identified in the pig kidney cell line, which was proven to be nonpathogenic to pigs in subsequent studies. PCV2 is the primary threat to the global swine industry and the cause of pig postweaning multisystemic wasting syndrome (PMWS) (Meng 2012), which is associated with severe lymphocyte depletion and multiple organ inflammation (Oh et al. 2022). Many extensive studies have been conducted on PCV2 pathogenesis and immune regulatory mechanisms in recent years. PCV3 was first reported in North America in 2016 (Phan et al. 2016) and is a newly identified pathogen associated with symptoms of porcine dermatitis and nephropathy syndrome (PDNS) and reproductive problems. In addition, PCV4 was recently discovered in 2019 (Chen et al. 2021). Only limited information is available about PCV4 pathogenesis and its clinical implications.

Although PCVs share similar genome morphologies, the sequence of PCV3 is likely only 40% homologous with that of other PCVs (Yuzhakov et al. 2018; Guo et al. 2019). Since its discovery, PCV3 has been reported in Asia, Europe, South America, and North America around the world and has garnered widespread attention in the swine industry. Several studies have further demonstrated that PCV3 infection is related to respiratory, digestive and reproductive disorders and multisystem diseases (Jiang et al. 2019; Kroeger et al. 2022; Sirisereewan et al. 2022). Some groups have reported that coinfection with PCV3 and other pathogens (such as PEDV, PCV2, PRRSV, or PRV) is a common condition in healthy or sick pigs on some farms (Zhang et al. 2022; Burrai et al. 2023; Amoroso et al. 2021). All the evidence shows that PCV3 may be another important pathogen in the swine industry.

The difficulty of isolation and the limited cultured cells limit the in vitro propagation and research of PCV3. Therefore, in our previous study, PCV3 rescued by reverse genetics was used to study the pathogenic mechanism of PCV3 (Jiang et al. 2019). PCV3 alone can cause PDNS-like disease in SPF piglets, and the results provided much information on quantitative proteomics and the gut microbiota community in PCV3-inoculated piglets (Hou et al. 2020; Jiang et al. 2020). The above research revealed that PCV3 may infect cells, including neutrophils, macrophages, eosinophils, and epithelial cells, in various tissues and organs. The changes in host cells induced by PCV3 infection lead to disease progression. However, how host cell proteins affect the proteome of PCV3-infected PK-15 cells is not well understood. Thus, using a proteomic analysis to examine the cellular responses and clarify the infection and pathogenesis of PCV3 is essential.

In this study, we applied an advanced 4D-DIA quantitative approach to reveal the proteomic pattern of PCV3-infected PK-15 cells. The function, distribution and signaling pathways associated with the differentially expressed proteins were explored via a high-throughput analysis combined with bioinformatics methods. The goal of this integrated proteomic analysis was to thoroughly examine the differentially regulated proteins in host cells infected with PCV3.

Materials and methods

Cells and virus

Porcine kidney epithelial PK-15 cells free of PCVs were originally obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. The cells were routinely cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium supplemented with 5% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 g/mL streptomycin.

The PCV3 strain rescued from the infectious PCV3 genome clone PCV3/CHN/Hebei-LY/2015 (MF318451) was used in this study (Wang et al. 2021). This PCV3 strain was successfully propagated fifteen times in PK15 cells and stored at -80 °C. The PCV3 titer was determined as the 50% tissue culture infective dose (TCID50)/ml. PK-15 cells were cultured for nearly 24 h until reaching 70% confluence and were infected with PCV3. After 18 h of adsorption, infected cells were treated with 300 mM D-glucosamine and then maintained in DMEM supplemented with 2% FBS. Uninfected PK-15 cells were used as the mock-infected group. Viral propagation was confirmed via western blotting. Three replicates of virus- and mock-infected cultures were prepared at each time point.

Protein extraction and trypsin digestion

The collected cells were suspended in protein lysis buffer (8 M urea, 1% SDS) supplemented with protease inhibitors and disrupted with a high-flux tissue grinding machine 3 times for 40 s each. The mixture was subsequently incubated on ice for 30 min. After centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, the protein concentration in the collected supernatant was determined with a BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific), and SDS‒PAGE was performed.

One hundred micrograms of protein were resuspended in trimethylammonium bicarbonate buffer (TEAB), which had a final concentration of 100 mM. The mixture was reduced with Tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP) at a final concentration of 10 mM at 37 °C for 60 min and then alkylated with iodoacetamide (IAM) at a final concentration of 40 mM at room temperature for 40 min in the dark. After centrifugation at 10,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, the pellet was collected and resuspended in 100 μl of trimethylammonium bicarbonate buffer (TEAB), which had a final concentration of 100 mM. Trypsin was added at a 1:50 trypsin-to-protein mass ratio, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C overnight.

Spectra library generation

Equal amounts of peptide samples were mixed and concentrated via vacuum centrifugation and then resuspended in UPLC loading buffer (Phase A: 2% acetonitrile, pH 10; Phase B: 80% acetonitrile, pH 10). The mixed peptides were fractionated via vanquish Flex binary UHPLC chromatography (Thermo, USA) with an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 column (1.7 µm, 2.1 mm × 150 mm, Waters, USA) to increase the proteomic depth. Briefly, peptides were first separated with a gradient of elution over 47 min at a flow rate of 200 μl/min. The elution gradient was as follows: 0–16 min, 0%–0% B; 16–17 min, 0%–3.8% B; 17–34 min, 3.8%–24% B; 34–37 min, 24%–30% B; 37–38 min, 30%–43% B; 38–39 min, 43%–100% B; 39–44 min, 100%–0% B; and 44–47 min, maintaining 0% B. Six fractions were collected from the mixed sample.

Based on peptide quantification results, the peptides were redissolved in spectrometry loading buffer (2% ACN with 0.1% formic acid) including the appropriate iRT peptide, which was used to calibrate the retention time and was analyzed using an EASY-nLC system (Thermo, USA) coupled with a timsTOF Pro2 mass spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Briefly, a C18 reverse-phase column (75 μm × 25 cm, Ionopticks, USA) was equilibrated with solvent A (2% ACN with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (80% ACN with 0.1% formic acid). The peptides were eluted using the following gradient: 0–45 min, 3–28% B; 45–50 min, 28–44% B; 50–55 min, 44–90% B; and 55–60 min, 90% B. The tryptic peptides were separated at a flow rate of 300 nL/min.

The timsTOF Pro2 mass spectrometer was operated in parallel accumulation serial fragmentation (PASEF) mode. The positive ion detection method was used, the ion source voltage was set to 1.5 kV, and MS and MS/MS were both detected and analyzed by TOF. MS data were collected over the m/z range of 100 to–1700. Both the accumulation time and the ramp time were set to 117 ms, and the ion mobility coefficient (1/K0) value was set from 0 to 5 Vs·cm2. During MS/MS data collection, each tims cycle included ten PASEF MS/MS scans. The exclusion criterion was activity after 0.24 min.

The DDA-PASEF data files were analyzed using Spectronaut software (version 2.4) to build a digital library of spectra as a qualitative basis for the subsequent DIA analysis.

DIA mass detection and protein identification

The samples were digested with trypsin, and equal amounts of peptides were drained in a vacuum centrifuge concentrator. The enzymatically drained peptides were subsequently resolubilized with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), and the peptides were desalted with HLB and drained with a vacuum concentrator. Finally, the peptides were quantified using the Thermo Fisher Scientific Peptide Quantification Kit (item #23,275).

Based on peptide quantification results, the peptides were redissolved in spectrometry loading buffer (2% ACN with 0.1% formic acid) including the appropriate iRT peptide, which was used to calibrate the retention time and was analyzed using an EASY-nLC system (Thermo, USA) coupled with a timsTOF Pro2 mass spectrometer (Bruker, Germany) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Briefly, a C18 reverse-phase column (75 μm × 25 cm, Ionopticks, USA) was equilibrated with solvent A (2% ACN with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (80% ACN with 0.1% formic acid). The peptides were eluted using the previous gradient. The tryptic peptides were separated at a flow rate of 300 nL/min.

Data-independent acquisition (DIA) data were acquired using a timsTOF Pro2 mass spectrometer operated in DIA-PASEF mode. MS data were collected over an m/z range of 400 to–1200 and an ion mobility range of 0.75 to–1.28 Vs·cm2. Both the accumulation time and ramp time were set to 100 ms. During MS/MS data collection, each tims cycle contained one MS scan and ten PASEF MS/MS scans. The exclusion criterion was activity after 0.4 min.

Spectronaut software (version 14) was used to search the raw DIA-PASEF data based on the spectral library generated from the DDA-PASEF data. The retention times were corrected by iRT, and 6 peptides per protein and 3 daughter ions per peptide were selected for quantitative analysis. The following parameters were used: protein FDR ≤ 0.01, peptide FDR ≤ 0.01, peptide confidence ≥ 99%, and XIC width ≤ 75 ppm. The shared peptides and modified peptides were excluded, and the peak areas were calculated and summed to obtain the quantitative results.

Bioinformatics methods

The bioinformatics analysis of the proteomic data was performed with the free online Majorbio Cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). P values and fold changes (FCs) in the proteins between the two groups were calculated using the R package “t test”. The thresholds of a fold change (> 1.5 or < 0.5) and a P value < 0.05 were used to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). The functional annotation of all the identified proteins was performed using GO (http://geneontology.org/) and KEGG pathway analyses (http://www.genome.jp/kegg/). DEPs were further used for GO and KEGG enrichment analyses. A protein‒protein interaction analysis was performed using String v11.5.

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software, and the results are presented as the means ± standard errors of the means (SEMs). The significance level was set at p < 0.05, and the outcomes are reported as the means ± SEMs. Three biological replicates were utilized for all experiments.

Immunofluorescence assay

PCV3-infected PK-15 cells were fixed with cold ethanol at different time points, incubated with a polyclonal antibody against PCV3 for 1 h at 37 °C, and then washed three times with PBS before further incubation with a FITC-conjugated goat anti-swine antibody (Southern Biotech, 614–1202) in the dark for 45 min at 37 °C. After the samples were washed three times with PBS, images were captured under a fluorescence microscope. Swine polyclonal antibody against PCV3 Cap prepared in our laboratory was used in the immunofluorescence assay.

Western blot analysis

Mock- and PCV3-infected cells without supernatants were collected for western blotting. Equivalent amounts of cell lysates from each sample were mixed with 6 × sample loading buffer, boiled for 10 min, and then separated on 12% SDS‒PAGE gels. The proteins were electrotransferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore). The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk–TBST overnight at 4 °C and then incubated with primary antibodies. The membranes were washed with TBST three times and then incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse antibody at 37 °C for 1 h. The signals were obtained using the clarity-enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA), after which images were captured. A mouse monoclonal antibody against PCV3 Cap prepared in our laboratory was used for Western blot analysis. Other antibodies, including the anti-RUNX2 antibody (Abcam, ab114133), anti-FOSL1 antibody (ABclonal, A5372), anti-EphA5 antibody (Santa sc-927), anti-β-actin antibody (ABclonal, AC038), goat anti-rabbit IgG (horseradish peroxidase-conjugated, HRP) (Abcam, ab205718), and goat anti-mouse IgG (HRP) (Abcam, ab205719), were purchased commercially.

Results

Confirmation of PCV3 replication in PK-15 cells

Viral protein expression was monitored at different time points after infection to investigate the kinetics of PCV3 propagation in PK-15 cells, as shown in Fig. 1A. According to the PCV3 growth curve in PK-15 cells, PCV3 propagation peaked at 72 hpi and then gradually declined. The IFA results indicated that the infected cells were stained at 72 hpi (Fig. 1B). At 96 hpi, almost all the cells had detached. Generally, no significant host cell cytoskeletal collapse or membrane rearrangement was observed, but a high virus yield was the optimal time point for the proteomic analysis following PCV3 infection. Therefore, PCV3- and mock-infected cells were harvested at 72 hpi for subsequent proteomic analysis.

Identification of the protein profile by 4D-DIA analysis

The protein extracts were prepared from PCV3- and mock-infected PK-15 cells, as described in the Methods section. The host response to PCV3 infection at 72 hpi was analyzed by examining differences in protein expression. Clustering analysis revealed dramatically different proteomic profiles in PCV3-infected PK-15 cells (Fig. 2A). A total of 122,313 peptides and 9546 proteins were detected and quantified, among which 194 proteins presented significant differences in expression between PCV3- and mock-infected cells at 72 hpi. A total of 58 and 136 proteins were significantly up- and downregulated, respectively, during PCV3 infection according to the criteria (p values < 0.05 and fold changes > 1.5 or < 0.67), as shown in Fig. 2B (and supporting information). Three replicates were performed in this experiment.

Validation of the differentially expressed proteins

Three selected proteins from PK-15 cells with and without PCV3 infection were analyzed to confirm the differentially expressed proteins identified via the 4D-DIA proteomic analysis. Based on interest and different ratios, FOSL1, RUNX2 and EPHA5 were selected for western blot analysis, as shown in Fig. 3. Among the three representative proteins, FOSL1 and RUNX2 expression increased by 2.035- and 1.86-fold, respectively, and EPHA5 expression decreased by 0.417-fold in PCV3-infected PK-15 cells at 72 hpi. Although the number of verified proteins was relatively limited, the ratios from the immunoblot analysis were similar to those obtained from the 4D-DIA approach.

Bioinformatics analysis of the cellular proteome

All the proteins that were differentially regulated upon PCV3 infection were subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and might be associated with virulence and pathogenicity. Three major annotation categories were obtained from the GO consortium website: cellular components, molecular functions, and biological processes (Fig. 4A). For the biological process annotation, the DEPs were involved in many biological processes, such as cellular processes, biological regulation, metabolic processes, developmental process in response to stimuli, and immune system processes. The molecular function annotation revealed proteins related to binding, catalytic activity, transcriptional regulatory activity, molecular transducer activity, and molecular function regulators. In terms of the cellular component annotation, the DEPs were well distributed within the cellular anatomical entity and protein-containing complexes (128 and 28 proteins, respectively). Collectively, the GO annotations and analysis of the DEPs inferred that PCV3 infection may perturb several processes, such as protein binding, transcription, metabolism, and the innate immune response.

A Comparison of GO term annotations of the DEPs in PK-15 cells infected with PCV3 at level 2, including biological process, molecular function, and cellular component terms. B Analysis of the KEGG pathways associated with the DEPs in PCV3-infected PK-15 cells

KEGG pathway enrichment analysis was performed to further investigate the cellular pathways in which these proteins are involved (Fig. 4B), and the top 20 enriched pathways with the highest representation of each term were identified (Table 1). Interestingly, the majority of the DEPs were involved in metabolic pathways, human diseases (such as cancer, viral infections and neurodegenerative disease), protein processing in the endoplasmic reticulum, signal transduction (cAMP, MAPK, PI3K-Akt and calcium signaling pathways), regeneration and the immune system. The DEPs were further analyzed using the STRING database and a protein‒protein network was constructed to clarify the mechanisms underlying the interactions of PCV3 with proteins of PK-15 cells and the consequent effects on cell function. In total, 70 protein networks were mapped based on these DEPs upon PCV3 infection, as shown in Fig. 5A. Four main functional categories of interest are depicted here to investigate the underlying differences in biological functions that may be related to viral infection: (1) cellular anatomical entities (56 molecules); (2) signal transduction mechanisms (46 molecules); (3) biological regulation (42 molecules); (4) binding (40 molecules); and (5) developmental processes (27 molecules) (Fig. 5B). The pathway annotation analysis revealed strongly interacting proteins involved in signal transduction, such as the cAMP, MAPK, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways. These proteins may have important functions in the innate immune response and nuclear pore transport.

Discussion

Virus‒cell interactions are associated with alterations in protein expression, signaling and immune response pathways during viral infection. To date, no research has focused on the protein profile of host cells infected with PCV3. In this study, a 4D-DIA quantitative proteomic analysis was employed to reveal substantial alterations in cellular proteins during PCV3 infection. The data revealed a larger proteomic shift at 72 hpi. In total, 194 (58 upregulated and 136 downregulated) host proteins were identified as differentially expressed relative to mock-infected cells based on a fold change > 1.5 and < 0.67 and a p value < 0.05. The western blot results were also in accordance with the proteomic analysis. The differences in cellular responses were associated mainly with protein synthesis, immune regulation, cellular assembly and organization, signal transduction, and apoptosis. These data provide comprehensive information to reveal the pathogenic mechanisms of PCV3 infection.

Type I interferon (IFN) plays a critical role in the clearance of many viruses by the innate immune system. Several viruses have been shown to inhibit IFN production through diverse strategies to cause immune escape. Many studies have shown that PCV2 can reduce the IFN response both in vivo and in vitro by interfering with the activation of IFN or decreasing ISG transcription (Wang et al. 2021, 2022; Wu et al. 2021), whereas recent studies have reported that the PCV3 cap protein can inhibit IFN signaling in vitro (Shen et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2020a, b). In our previous itraq studies, PCV3 infection upregulated the expression of IFN-induced proteins, including OAS1, Mx1, Mx2, ISG15, and IFIT3, in pigs. The proteomic results from this study are not consistent with those of the in vivo study. The research model and method used in our proteomic study may be the main reasons for the different results. The dose and time course account for the difference. Proteomics at the cellular level is more focused on the in-depth study of gene functions and molecular mechanisms.

The FOS family has four members, including FOS, FOSB, FOS-like antigen 1 (FOSL1), and FOSL2. FOSL1 is a component of the activator protein 1 (AP-1) complex, which contains Jun, Fos, activating transcription factor (ATF), and musculoaponeurotic fibrosarcoma (MAF). Previous reports have shown that FOSL1 can promote the epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition, tumor invasion, and metastasis of various cancers and contributes to different cellular processes, such as proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. Recently, FOSL1 was suggested to impair the association of TRAF3, TRIF, and TBK1, leading to the inhibition of IFN-I signaling during viral infection (Cai et al. 2017). FOSL1 is upregulated by PCV3, and the detailed interactions among PCV3, FOSL1 and IFN-I signaling are still unclear and need further research.

Our proteomic study revealed that RUNX2 expression changed significantly after PCV3 infection. The RUNX family of transcription factors includes RUNX1, RUNX2, and RUNX3 and shares a common Runt homology domain, which functions as both a DNA-binding domain and a protein-binding domain. RUNX and CBFβ form a small family of heterodimeric transcription factors (TFs) referred to as core-binding factors to activate or repress transcription at several loci important for hematopoiesis and osteogenesis. Increasing evidence has shown that RUNX2 is a master regulator of cell development and differentiation, cancer biology, the persistence of T-cell-mediated responses during chronic viral infection, and PI3k-Akt signaling (Pilotti et al. 2022; Ito 2008). Furthermore, RUNX2 has been demonstrated to facilitate the response to viral infection through the control of interferon regulatory factor 7 (IRF7) (Chopin et al. 2016). Therefore, the influence of RUNX2 on PCV3 infection in PK-15 cells should be considered in the future.

The Eph receptor family constitutes the largest family of receptor tyrosine kinases. Eph receptors and their ligands form a complex signaling network and play roles in inflammation and cardiovascular development and diseases. Some Eph receptors have been shown to function as entry receptors for various types of viruses (Foo et al. 2016; Grosskopf et al. 2019; TerBush et al. 2018). EPHA5 has been shown to be an important regulatory factor for the Wnt signaling pathway and its downstream target genes (Katoh and Katoh. 2006; Zhang et al. 2020a, b). EPHA5 plays a role not only in regulating the cell structure but also in regulating cellular immune-related signaling pathways. The effects of PCV3 inhibition of EPHA5 and its role in virus infection and invasion need to be further explored.

In our study, we reported for the first time that PCV3 infection obviously upregulated FOSL1 and RUNX2 expression and decreased the expression level of EPHA5 in PK-15 cells. Overall, we inferred that PCV3 infection might disrupt the interferon (IFN) response and impair the cellular network structure to facilitate its replication.

Taken together, this study is the first attempt to explore the whole protein profile of PCV3-infected PK-15 cells in vitro. Additionally, these findings provide valuable information for a better understanding of the mechanism of virus‒host interactions during PCV3 infection. Substantial in-depth studies will help elucidate viral pathogenesis.
