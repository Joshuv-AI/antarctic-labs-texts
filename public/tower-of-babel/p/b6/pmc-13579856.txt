Development and validation of the subjective financial distress scale for cancer patients
Authors: Tianying Yao, Linglong Liu, Xiaoxuan Li, Mengxia Yu, Shuo Zhang, Shanshan Zhou, Lihua Lu, Mingxia Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeGrounded in Chinese healthcare and cultural background, this study aimed to develop and validate the Subjective Financial Distress Scale (SFDS) for cancer patients.MethodsBetween July and November 2022, cancer patients were recruited from the oncology department of a Grade A hospital in Jiangsu Province by the convenience sampling method. This research was conducted in three stages: Phase I (Item development) included domain identification and item generation based on a systematic literature review and preliminary qualitative interview results. Phase Ⅱ (Scale development) reduced items through Delphi expert consultation, a pilot test, item analysis and exploratory factor analysis. Phase III (Scale validation) evaluated the reliability and stability of the scale.ResultsA total of 452 patients were recruited. The final scale consisted of 24 items with 6 dimensions, including financial risk perception, changes in daily consumption patterns, changes in work status, changes in treatment plans, individual spontaneous emotional experiences and externally mediated interactive reactions. The scale’s Cronbach’s alpha, split-half reliability, test-retest reliability and content validity were 0.949, 0.902, 0.953 and 0.970, respectively. Comparison with the Comprehensive Scores for Financial Toxicity Based on the Patient-Reported Outcome Measures showed high calibration correlation validity (coefficient = 0.805). Confirmatory factor analysis showed that the first-order and second-order models both fit well, respectively.ConclusionThe SFDS is reliable and valid to assess the degree of the subjective financial distress in cancer patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05071-7.Keywords: Cancer, Subjective financial distress, Scale, Psychology

Introduction

In recent years, the global incidence of cancer has been on an upward trend [1]. Advancements in medical technology have markedly enhanced the survival rate of cancer patients [2], leading to a substantial increase in the number of cancer survivors. However, this also implies an extended treatment duration and increased in treatment costs. From 2020 to 2050, the worldwide expenditure on cancer treatment is projected to reach $25.2 trillion dollars (at constant 2017 prices). Notably, China is expected to account for 24.1% of this total, leading the worldwide rankings [3]. The growing financial burden has adversely affected patients’ physiology and psychology [4]. This phenomenon is defined as Financial Toxicity (FT), including Objective Financial Burden (OFB) and Subjective Financial Distress (SFD) [5].

The OFB refers to the direct or indirect expenses caused by cancer treatment, functioning as an objective measure of consumption of an individual or family [6]. The SFD is a negative psychological response caused by disease-related financial risk perception and coping behaviors [7]. Currently, the 3-dimensional and 6-sub-dimensional conceptual models of the SFD proposed by Witte et al. have been widely adopted. It consists of material conditions (financial spending and financial resources), psychological response and coping behaviors (support seeking, coping care, and coping lifestyle) [8]. In comparison to the OFB, the SFD was more common and had a more detrimental effect on patients [9]. The prevalence of SFD among cancer patients in the United States fluctuates between 16% to 73% [10, 11]. Meanwhile, it ranges from 52% to 83.7% in China [12–14]. Both are at a relatively high level. Patients with the SFD often also suffer from the OFB such as reduced income and debt [15]. Struggling with the current financial distress, patients’ treatment compliance has decreased significantly [16], accompanied by an increased burden of symptoms like insomnia and headache [17, 18]. This vicious cycle leads to a drastic reduction in patients’ quality of life [19] or even a shortened lifespan [20]. Consequently, there is an imperative to place greater emphasis on the SFD faced by cancer patients and implement timely interventions.

Accurate assessment of patients’ SFD levels was a fundamental prerequisite for adopting effective interventions. Existing specific instruments include (1) the Breast Cancer Finances Survey (BCFS) [21], (2) the Social Economic Well-Being Scale (SWBS) [22], (3) the Comprehensive Scores for Financial Toxicity Based on the Patient-Reported Outcome Measures (COST) [23], (4) the Financial Index of Toxicity Questionnaire (FIT) [24], (5) the Patient-Reported Outcome for Fighting Financial Toxicity (PROFFIT) [25]; and (6) the Subjective Financial Distress Questionnaire (SFDQ) [26]. Upon evaluation by Witte et al. of these instruments, it was discovered that the majority of the scales are currently incapable of conducting a comprehensive assessment across all relevant dimensions of SFD [8].

Although the SFDQ was firmly anchored in the conceptual framework proposed by Witte et al., its cross-cultural applicability remains limited due to the distinctive healthcare system in which it was developed. India, where it was developed, has a relatively low medical insurance coverage and a comparatively high burden of out-of-pocket expenses on patients [27, 28]. This healthcare system is markedly different from that of China, which has basically achieved universal medical insurance coverage. Such differences may lead to significant disparities in patients’ experiences of the SFD. Furthermore, the SFDQ has only been applied in patients receiving radiotherapy in India [26], so its applicability in Chinese cancer patients remains to be verified.

The availability of external resources such as family or social support are key factors in the occurrence of the SFD. While some instruments have evaluated SFD from the family perspective, the social dimension has often been overlooked. However, patients face various challenges in obtaining social support. Many patients know little about government subsidies, charitable funds, or health insurance coverage [15]. This information gap prevents them from effectively utilizing social resources to mitigate SFD. Notably, following through on overly specialized paperwork and dealing with complicated eligibility criteria or complex application procedures can be challenging for patients who are already struggling with the physical and emotional toll of cancer [29]. Therefore, it is urgent to pay more attention to such a phenomenon. The exploration of the underlying causes is warranted and objective and meticulous assessments must be carried out. Only by doing so can we carry out more targeted and effective interventions.

In summary, this study aimed to explore and enrich SDF’s connotations and dimensions, grounded in Chinese healthcare and cultural background. Through the rigorous process, the Subjective Financial Distress Scale (SFDS) for cancer patients will be developed and validated. Consequently, it could serve as a reference for early clinical detection and prompt intervention, ultimately improving the overall well-being and treatment outcomes of cancer patients.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study developed and validated the SFDS for cancer patients in three steps with reference to the Best Practices for Developing and Validating of Scales for Health, Social and Behavioral Research [30]. Phase Ⅰ (Item development) involved dimension identification and item generation based on a systematic literature review and preliminary qualitative interview results. Phase Ⅱ (Scale development) reduced items through Delphi expert consultation, a pilot test, and a pretest. Phase Ⅲ (Scale validation) evaluated the reliability and stability of the scale.

A total of 16 experts participated in the Delphi expert consultation. The inclusion criteria for these experts included: (1) having ≥ 10 years of clinical nursing experience or ≥ 5 years of research in cancer care; (2) having an intermediate title or above; (3) being familiar with the field of SFD; (4) being will to provide informed consent and voluntarily participating.

The convenience sampling method was used to recruit cancer patients admitted to the oncology department of a Grade A hospital in Jiangsu Province from July 2022 to November 2022. The sample size for exploratory factor analysis (EFA) should be 5 to 10 times the number of variables, and the sample size for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) should be 5 to 20 times the number of variables, and not less than 200 cases. Therefore, 15 patients participated in the pilot test, 214 in the pretest (subsample 1), and 238 in the Phase Ⅲ (subsample 2).

The variables collected in this study included socio-demographic variables, and clinical variables. Socio-demographic variables included age, gender, education level, marital status, residence, insurance type, post-cancer employment status, and personal monthly income. Clinical variables included travel time, total medical expenses, number of hospitalizations, number of comorbidities, cancer type, cancer stage, metastasis status, type of treatment received, and time since diagnosis.

The inclusion criteria for patients included: (1) cancer diagnosis confirmed through clinicopathological examination; (2) age of 18 years or older; (3) being willing to provide informed consent and voluntarily participating. Criteria for excluding patients: (1) having language communication disorder; (2) having mental or other significant disorders that influenced communication; (3) randomly checking the questionnaires and showing regular responses (wave or linear); (4) giving up during the process of filling out the questionnaires.

Phase Ⅰ item development: domain identification and item generation

A systematic literature review was performed to identify studies and assessment tools related to the SFD for cancer patients, as described in Appendix S1. Databases included PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CINAHL, Embase, CNKI and Wanfang. The retrieval time was from the establishment of the database to October 16, 2022. The search terms were used such as “neoplasm*”, “cancer*”, “oncolog*”, “tumor*”, “financial hardship*”, “financial distress”, and “financial toxicit*”. Based on the review results, the research term analyzed the concept and dimensions and combined with the results of later semi-structured qualitative interview, constructing the scale framework and item pool.

Phase Ⅱ scale development

Two rounds of Delphi expert consultation via e-mail were conducted to modify the scale dimensions and items. Items were reduced by the results of the expert positive coefficient, the authority coefficient (Cr), the opinion concentration and Kendall’s coefficients of coefficients (Kendall’s W). During the pilot test, 15 patients were instructed face-to-face to fill in the pretest version of the scale and point out something that was not understood. Then item analysis and EFA were adopted to reduce items to form the formal scale in the pretest.

Phase Ⅲ Scale validation

The scale reliability was assessed through internal consistency reliability, split-half reliability, and test-retest reliability. The scale validity was assessed through content validity, construct validity, convergent validity, discriminant validity and criterion validity. The COST has good reliability and validity and has been widely used in clinical practice. Therefore, this study chose it to assess the criterion validity of the scale developed in this study. Finally, CFA was adopted and the model fit indicators included the chi-square minimum/degree of freedom (X2/df), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Normed Fit Index (NFI), Incremental Fit Index (IFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), and Comparative Fit Index (CFI).

Statistical analysis

Software Nvivo 12, Excel 2019, SPSS 27.0 and Amos 27.0 were used for statistical analysis.

During the Delphi expert consultation, the expert positive coefficient was assessed by the effective return of the questionnaires. If ≥ 70%, it indicated good positivity. When Cr ≥ 0.70, the expert authority could be considered to be high. The degree of expert opinion concentration was assessed by the mean importance rating, coefficient of variation (CV) and frequency of full scores for each item. Items were considered for deletion if mean < 4.00 or CV > 0.25. When P < 0.05, a higher Kendall’s W indicated a greater level of expert consensus.

In the item analysis phase, critical ratio (CR), item-total correlations (ITCs) and internal consistency were used as the evaluation methods. Items with CR < 3.00 or no statistically significant difference between high and low groups (P < 0.05) were considered for deletion. Consideration was also given to deleting items with ITCs < 0.40 or P > 0.05. If the scale’s Cronbach’s alpha (α) increased after deleting an item, then consider deleting this item.

The dimensions were extracted by the principal components analysis with varimax rotation in EFA. The following conditions need to be met: (1) Bartlett’s spherical test for significant difference (P < 0.05), and Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin Measure of Sampling Adequacy (KMO) ≥ 0.80; (2) cumulative variance contribution of factors higher than 50%; (3) extracted factor eigenvalue > 1.00 and each primary factor contained ≥ 2 items; (4) deleting items with double factor loadings > 0.40 or with factor loadings > 0.40 but difference < 0.20 between their primary and alternative factor loadings [31].

Twenty-five patients refilled the questionnaires again 2 weeks after the last round of questionnaire collection. When the test-retest coefficient was > 0.70, Cronbach’s α > 0.80 for the total scale, Cronbach’s α > 0.60 for the subscales, and the higher split-half reliability, the better the reliability of the scale was indicated.

The content validity index (CVI) was used as the evaluation index of content validity, including I-CVI and S-CVI. Each expert was invited to assess the relevance of each item to the corresponding dimensions during Delphi consultation. A 5-point scale was used, where (1 = very unimportant, 2 = unimportant, 3 = general, 4 = important, 5 = very important). Items with a score of 4 or 5 were considered relevant to what was being measured [32]. The I-CVI value of more than 0.83 and the S-CVI/Ave value of more than 0.90 were considered as appropriate and acceptable [33].

Construct validity examined the degree of match between the scale structure and the preset structure, which could be measured by the results of correlation analysis and EFA. The correlation coefficients between the dimensions need to be smaller than that between the dimensions and the total scale. The higher the degree of agreement between the structure derived from EFA and the preset structure, the better the construct validity of the scale.

When average variance extracted (AVE) was > 0.50 and composite reliability (CR) was > 0.70, the scale’s convergent validity was considered excellent. The scale’s discriminant validity was considered good if the square root of the AVE for each factor was greater than the square of its correlation coefficient with other factors [34]. The degree of correlation between the COST and the scale developed in this study was measured, with higher coefficient indicating better its criterion validity.

In CFA, a better model fit needs to fulfill the following conditions: (1) X2/df ≤ 3.00; (2) RMSEA ≤ 0.08; (1) NFI, IFI, TLI and CFI were all ≥ 0.90. When the model fit is poor, a covariance relationship could be established among different observed variables with strong correlation in the same domain based on the theoretical basis through the model Modification Index (MI). The COSMIN checklist is provided as Appendix S7.

Instrument scoring

A 5-point Likert scale is used, where (1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = neutral, 4 = agree, 5 = strongly agree). All items are positively scored with a total score ranged from 24 to 120 scores. A greater total score correlates with an increased degree of SFD.

Ethical considerations

This study has been approved by the Scientific and Ethical Committee of Nanjing Medical University [review number: (2022) 792]. The purpose and significance of the study was explained to the patients by the researcher. After obtaining the written informed consent, the patients were instructed to fill in the questionnaire. The patients were also informed that the data were confidential and would be used only for this study.

Item generation

Based on the results of the literature review and the qualitative research, the research team identified three dimensions and nine sub-dimensions of the scale after discussion, including financial risk perception (increasing objective financial burden and decreasing financial affordability), coping behaviors (changes in daily consumption patterns, changes in work status, changes in treatment plans, and seeking support), and negative psychological response (personal dimension, family dimension, and social dimension). Then the research team compiled and analyzed items from relevant assessment tools to identify a pool of 41 items (Appendix S2).

Item reduction

Sixteen experts participated in the first round of consultation and 15 experts in the second round. After group discussion based on the first-round experts’ opinions, the research group deleted 4 items, modified the expressions of 23 items, added 5 items, and adjusted the dimensions of 2 items. In the second round, 4 items were deleted and 3 items were modified to form the pretest version of the scale with 3 dimensions, 9 sub- dimensions and 37 items (Appendix S5). The specific modifications can be seen in Table Appendix S3-4.

The expert authority coefficients of the two rounds were 0.892 and 0.906 respectively. The expert positive coefficient was 100% and 93.75% respectively. The mean importance rating of each item was > 4.00, and CV was < 0.25 (except deleted items). Kendall’s W were 0.401 (P < 0.05) and 0.473 (P < 0.05), indicating that experts had good authority and consistency. The number and expression of items did not change during the pilot test.

According to the results of item analysis, 5 items’ (item 3, 5, 7, 22, and 23) adjusted ITCs were < 0.40 and the Cronbach’s α of the total scale increased after deletion. Therefore, the above 5 items were deleted, and the remaining 33 items were reordered for EFA. The KMO value of 0.923 and X2 = 7422.744 (P < 0.001) in the Bartlett’s spherical test indicated the appropriateness of the data in phase Ⅱ for factor analysis. After the first-round EFA, items 3, 23, 27, and 29 with factor loadings > 0.40 but differences < 0.20 on both dimensions and items 4 and 28 with obvious dimension misclassification were deleted. Items 20, 21, and 22 were deleted due to their factor loadings > 0.40 but differences < 0.20 on both dimensions in the second round. After the third round, the six factors with eigenvalues > 1.00 were extracted and named as follows: financial risk perception, changes in daily consumption patterns, changes in work status, changes in treatment plans, individual spontaneous emotional experiences and externally mediated interactive reactions. The cumulative variance contribution rate was 82.132% and a total of 24 items’ factor loadings ranged from 0.694 to 0.948 (Appendix S6).

Discussion

This study developed and verified the SFDS for cancer patients considering Chinese healthcare and cultural background. This 24-item measure assessed six dimensions of SFD: financial risk perception, changes in daily consumption patterns, changes in work status, changes in treatment plans, individual spontaneous emotional experiences, and externally mediated interactive reactions. The study results also showed that the scale’s reliability and validity were good. The breadth and depth of the items were improved through systematic literature review, semi-structured interviews, and Delphi expert consultation. Furthermore, the scientificalness of the scale was ensured by the objective data acquired from item analysis, EFA and CFA.

The dimensions of the SFD ultimately differ from those anticipated in this study and proposed by Witte et al. [8]. In this regard, the research team subsequently further explored and then discovered that both active financial spending and passive consumption of financial resources involve an evaluation of patients’ current or future financial situations as well as treatment-related costs. Lueckmann et al. [15] also observed that patients consider the feasibility and potential burden associated with financial adjustments. These “hidden” evaluation processes may exacerbate patients’ sense of financial uncertainty, thereby becoming key factors in the development of SFD. Underlying the material conditions is the patient’s awareness and attention of financial risk. Thus, it may be more appropriate to name this dimension as financial risk perception, aligning with recent studies emphasizing that subjective appraisal of financial threat often precedes actual material hardship [15].

Patients who perceive financial risks tend to adopt various preventive or coping behaviors. Among them, changes in lifestyle and treatment plans are the ones most mentioned in relevant studies [16, 35]. In addition to the two behaviors above, this study discovered that patients’ work status is also readily affected negatively. In recent years, there has been a discernible and concerning trend of the cancer population growing younger [36]. However, patients in this age group usually serve as the main source of income for their families. 72.8% of the participants in this study remained in the working age stage. Owing to the exigencies of treatment, their professional lives were inevitably disrupted, manifesting as diminished work capacity, curtailed working hours, or even job loss [37]. Consistent with findings by Zafar et al. [5], who highlighted the profound impact of employment disruption on financial toxicity. The work intensity beyond patients’ tolerance, strained interpersonal relationships in the workplace and the bleak career prospects are significantly associated with the occurrence of the SFD [38, 39]. In light of these findings, to enhance the comprehensiveness of the assessment, relevant assessment contents were added to this scale.

Notably, the domain of “seeking support” was ultimately excluded from the final framework. This may be largely attributed to the profound influence of traditional Chinese cultural values. Specifically, an ingrained sense of self-reliance and the shame associated with soliciting help often deter patients from seeking assistance, as disclosing financial vulnerability is frequently stigmatized as a personal or familial failure [40]. During the interviews, some patients disclosed that they tried to seek support. While doing so, they either faced rejection or failed to obtain effective help. Instead, their requests seemingly imposed a burden on others. Consequently, these patients became reluctant to expose their vulnerabilities, opting to withhold their need for further support.

Unpredictable financial risks and forced coping behaviors may result in negative emotions. Through the analysis of the genesis of such emotions, two novel dimensions, namely “individual spontaneous emotional experiences” and “externally mediated interactive reactions”, have been incorporated.

The former refers to the emotions generated by patients after assessing their current or futurefinancial conditions. The latter pertains to emotions triggered by external factors. Families are important resources for patient care, emotional and financial support [41, 42]. As the disease advances, the protracted and extensive support provided by families inevitably adversely affect the lives or work of other family members. Their physical and mental burdens have been aggravated, and eventually, the whole family relationship has even become estranged [40]. This reflects the family-centric care model prevalent in China, where individual illness is perceived as a collective family crisis. Moreover, administrative hurdles should not be ignored. It refers to additional paperwork or actions a patient or their provider must take to access benefits and spans across medical, insurance, and employment factors [43]. Common issues such as the opacity of financial assistance programs, the complicated and time-consuming help-seeking procedures, or the lack of sufficient professional personnel have hindered patients' access to effective support, thereby contributing to SFD [15]. Consequently, this scale is also supplemented with relevant assessment.

In addition, some research findings indicate that reverse-scored items may function differently across groups with varying educational levels [44]. Notably, within the cancer patient population, there exists a substantial diversity in educational backgrounds. What’s more, a considerable number of items were compiled in this scale. However, it was observed that the average time for participants to complete reverse-scored items exceeded that for positive-scored items [45].With the aim of enhancing the scale’s applicability and popularization, all positive-scored items were set in this study.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the convenience sampling method was adopted, with most participants being recruited from the Yangtze River Delta region of China. Consequently, the representativeness of the sample might be compromised. Secondly, constrained by time, manpower, and material resources, all the participants in this study were from the oncology department of a Grade A hospital in Nanjing. To some extent, this may affect the generalization of the conclusions. To further validate the scale’s applicability and stability, future research could be oriented towards multi-centre studies with large sample sizes.

Conclusion

In this study, a 6-dimension, 24-item SFDS for cancer patients was developed, adhering stringently to the standardized scale compilation procedures. The reliability and validity of SFDS are good, indicating that SFDS can sever as an excellent instrument for evaluating the extent of SFD in cancer patients. It can be applied in the early stages of clinical practice for screening purposes, thereby providing valuable references to facilitate timely and targeted interventions.
