Genome-wide CRISPR screen identifies splicing factor SF3B4 in driving hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Yue Guo, Mingjing Xu, Hua Xue, Xiaofan Ding, Alissa M. Wong, Na Lin, Dandan Pu, Aikha M. Wong, Xin Wang, Hui Zhao, Nathalie Wong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Although genome sequencings have recognized many cancer-associated genes in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), distinguishing their functional effect remains challenging. Leveraging on a genome-wide CRISPR knockout (KO) screening, we uncovered spliceosome factors as major survival essential genes in HCC and up-regulations of ferroptosis suppressors [particularly glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLC)] in lenvatinib resistance. Our KO screen in patient-derived HCC organoid showed splicing factor 3b subunit 4 (SF3B4) to be top-ranked, conferring prosurvival signal in HCC organoid and driving tumorigenic potentials in both hepatic progenitor organoids and hydrodynamic tail vein injection HCC murine model. The combined RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing, long-read isoform sequencing, and transcriptome revealed characteristic splicing landscape regulated by SF3B4 and identified T-box transcription factor 3 (TBX3) variant TBX3+2a as a potent downstream effector. Our findings highlighted vital roles of SF3B4 in HCC cell survival and tumor progression, and the phenomenon of ferroptosis resistance in patients unresponsive to first-line agent lenvatinib.

INTRODUCTION

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is a leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide due to its asymptomatic nature and rapid progression (1, 2). Surgical resection is an effective therapy but is only amenable to a small fraction of patients with early-stage tumor. For advanced inoperable patients, systemic therapies represent the mainstay treatment option. Tyrosine kinase inhibitor lenvatinib is the Food and Drug Administration (FDA)–approved first-line agent for patients with unresectable HCC. Although lenvatinib monotherapy has shown good clinical effect, the problem of drug resistance is nevertheless apparent (3). Understanding the biology underscoring lenvatinib resistance and identifying relevant biomarkers are hence particularly important to aid further development of lenvatinib combinatory therapies in patients with HCC.

High-throughput sequencings have unveiled the genomic and transcriptomic blueprints of HCC and thereof recognition of cancer-associated genes (4, 5). However, elucidating the myriad of genes identified for their causal role in carcinogenesis remains a daunting task. Genome-wide screening serves as an invaluable strategy for phenotypic analysis and identification of essential genes in driving tumorigenesis and drug resistance (6, 7). CRISPR-based screens have been widely applied to the elucidation of causal connections between genotype and phenotype (8, 9). In this study, we undertook a loss-of-function CRISPR screen focusing on the identification of (i) survival-essential genes in HCC and (ii) modulator genes in resistance to lenvatinib.

Studies have accentuated on the clinical relevance of cancer-derived organoids for their genetic similarities to patients and phenocopy of many biological features (10–12). Our CRISPR-Cas9 knockout (KO) screen was hence conducted in a bona fide HCC patient-derived organoid (PDO), which is also lenvatinib resistant. We first identified the splicing factor SF3B4 (splicing factor 3b subunit 4) as a critical driver of HCC progression. Given the lenvatinib-resistant nature of the PDO model, we further applied the screening strategy to explore mechanisms of drug resistance and found glutamate-cysteine ligase catalytic subunit (GCLC)–mediated antiferroptosis pathway. Together, these results highlight two functionally important and clinically actionable pathways in HCC. They also demonstrate the versatility and translational potential of organoid-based CRISPR screening to uncover therapeutic vulnerabilities in HCC.

RESULTS

Genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 KO screen in patient-derived HCC organoid

Directly cultured from patient’s HCC tissue, we established PDO H813Torg that preserved the histopathology and genetic makeup of the originating tumor. Important driver mutations of HCC, such as TP53R175H and pTERTC228T, were captured in H813Torg alongside histological architecture of parental tumor in xenotransplantation (Fig. 1A and fig. S1, A and B). Using the human GeCKO (Genome-Scale CRISPR Knockout) pooled libraries, screening was performed in H813Torg with two half-libraries, each targeting 19,050 genes and 3 single guide RNAs (sgRNAs) per gene. Multiplicity of infection (MOI) at ~0.4 resulted in more than 80% of cells receiving a single sgRNA-containing lentiviral integrant, which is in accord with published threshold (6). A portion of cells was kept from day 0 as control, while the remaining was cultured for another 7 days before harvesting to assess genes affecting H813Torg growth. The sgRNA abundance on days 0 and 7 was quantified by next-generation sequencing and bioinformatic interpretations as described in Materials and Methods. A schematic workflow of our CRISPR KO screen in H813Torg is shown in Fig. 1B.

To ensure quality findings, we obtained confirmatory metrics on a high mapping ratio (79 to 84%), near full coverage of detected sgRNAs (~100% of total sgRNAs) and good reproducibility (fig. S1, C to E). We further validated the reliability of screening results by examining the behavior of predefined high-confidence core essential genes (625 genes) and nonessential genes (350 genes) as reported in previous studies (13, 14). Given that the disruption of essential genes is lethal to cells, our finding of substantial underrepresentation of sgRNAs targeting core genes compared to nonessential genes at day 7 was encouraging (fig. S1F). Most essential genes presented a negative selection at log fold change < 0, while nonessential genes displayed log fold changes near zero (Fig. 1C).

Spliceosome factor SF3B4 is essential for HCC survival

We identified 1470 essential genes that were consistently negatively selected across four replicates of KO screens with a robust statistical significance [RobustRankAggreg (RRA) ranking P value < 0.05] (Fig. 1D and table. S1). The top essential genes were significantly enriched in biological processes that underscored tumor dependence on DNA replication, protein synthesis, and cell cycle (Fig. 1E). The involvement of Spliceosome was prominent with 29 splicing regulators demonstrating marked selection from the CRISPR screen (Fig. 1, E and F). These genes were shown to be consistently up-regulated in HCC transcriptome datasets from both in-house (4) and TCGA-LIHC (Fig. 1G). Expression levels of few splicing factors, particularly SF3B4, correlated with inferior patient survival (Fig. 1H).

SF3B4 is an important subunit of the spliceosome complex and involved in pre-mRNA splicing, regulation of cell signaling, transcription and translation. Among the spliceosome genes identified, SF3B4 displayed distinct overexpressions in HCC (Fig. 1G). Up-regulation of SF3B4 in RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data (Fig. 1I) was confirmed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) in an independent cohort of 60 paired HCC and nontumoral liver, which showed near 60% of tumors to exhibit an increased level of SF3B4 mRNA (Fig. 1J). We further affirmed fortified protein expression of SF3B4 in tumors by immunoblotting (Fig. 1K). As an independent functional validation of the KO screen, SF3B4 knockdown induced spontaneous cell death in HCC PDOs, H813Torg, and H688Torg (Fig. 2, A and B, and fig. S2A).

SF3B4 promotes premalignant features in hepatic organoids and HCC formation in vivo

Next, we overexpressed SF3B4 in two normal liver organoids (Liver.Org1 and Liver.Org2) to evaluate its oncogenicity (fig. S2B). The method of Liver.Org1 and Liver.Org2 establishments from human liver tissues was as previously described (15). Notably, stably overexpressed SF3B4 in both Liver.Org1 and Liver.Org2 induced morphological transformation with time, where the organoids became less cystic and more irregular, developing thick walls composed of multiple cell layers and exhibiting a number of premalignant features (Fig. 2, C to E). In particular, SF3B4-overexpressed organoids could markedly prolong in vitro life span to more than 35 weeks, whereas vector controls ceased to grow after 18 weeks or so (Fig. 2, C and F). In addition to growth advantages, immunohistochemical staining for Ki67 further revealed an increased proliferative index in SF3B4-overexpressing organoids (Fig. 2G). Transcriptomic analysis using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further revealed that genes altered by SF3B4 were significantly enriched in the stem cell–type subclass of HCC (Fig. 2H). Consistently, the elevated expression of stemness markers SRY-Box Transcription Factor 9 (SOX9) and Epithelial Cell Adhesion Molecule (EpCAM) was observed, indicating enhanced stem-like features (Fig. 2I).

To further examine the effect of SF3B4 in vivo, we deployed the murine model of hydrodynamic tail vein injection (HDTVi) to mimic HCC formation in situ. Several HDTVi models are commonly used in HCC studies, including sgTrp53/MYC (16), MYC/CTNNB1 (17), and NRAS/AKT (18). To guide our model selection, we analyzed the TCGA-LIHC dataset and observed a significant co-occurrence of SF3B4 amplification with TP53 aberrations (19). Furthermore, previous genomic analysis revealed that TP53 alterations frequently co-occur with MYC amplification in HCC (4). We therefore selected the sgTrp53/MYC HDTVi model to examine the functional role of SF3B4 in hepatocarcinogenesis. A transposon-based MYC overexpression and CRISPR-mediated Trp53 KO were used as background control, and a cocktail of Trp53 KO/MYC overexpression/SF3B4 overexpression engaged to exam the SF3B4 effect in vivo (fig. S3A). We found that while the control group showed 50% of tumor formation, the presence of SF3B4 greatly augment HCC incidence to all mice (100%) (Fig. 2J). The SF3B4-induced HCC nodules were also much enlarged in tumor volume (Fig. 2K). The HDTVi model maintained baseline endogenous SF3B4 expression levels comparable to those observed in normal human liver (fig. S3B), and HDTVi-mediated SF3B4 overexpression remained within the physiological range found in primary human HCC samples (fig. S3C). Together, these results underscore the oncogenic role of SF3B4 in promoting HCC development under physiologically relevant expression levels.

SF3B4 alters mRNA splicing pattern

To understand how SF3B4 stimulates liver oncogenicity, we performed immunoprecipitation-coupled liquid chromatography/mass spectrometry (IP-LC/MS), RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (RIP-seq), long-read Iso-seq (PacBio SMRT), and short-read transcriptome in Liver.Org1 with or without SF3B4 overexpression. LC/MS was performed on IP product from SF3B4 pull-down to define those interacting partners. A total of 166 proteins showed notable enrichment in the SF3B4 pull-down group compared to immunoglobulin G (IgG) control (table S2). These SF3B4-binding proteins were mostly involved in RNA splicing processes and metabolism (Fig. 3A) with top-ranked proteins being spliceosome components or RNA splicing regulators (Fig. 3B) that are in accord with the canonical function of SF3B4. Notably, 12 splicing modulators that interact with SF3B4 were also identified as hits in the CRISPR screen (fig. S4A). Furthermore, RNA-seq and qPCR analysis revealed that four of these interacting splicing factors—SNRPA1, SF3B1, SF3A2, and SRSF2—are up-regulated upon SF3B4 overexpression (fig. S4B). These findings suggest that SF3B4 functions within a coregulated splicing network, where splicing factors exhibit functional interdependence.

RIP-sequencing further probed the SF3B4-RNA interactions. Using PureCLIP (v1.3.1) for peak identification and HOMER (v5.1) on annotation, we identified a total of 114,286 annotated RNA peaks that were indicative of SF3B4 binding regions (table S3). A clear enrichment of SF3B4-associated RNA reads was localized to these sites, whereas minimal representation shown from control input (Fig. 3C). The binding peaks were predominantly mapped to protein-coding genes and, to a lesser extent, noncoding RNAs (fig. S5A). Further analysis of SF3B4 peaks revealed a predominant localization to intronic regions (36.02%), followed by 3′ untranslated regions (29.03%) and exonic regions (24.40%) (Fig. 3D).

To investigate the influence of SF3B4 on the alternative splicing landscape, we performed long-read Iso-seq for isoform identifications in Liver.Org1 SF3B4OE and vector control supplemented with short-read transcriptome for more accurate quantification. The combined analytics termed Hybrid RNA-seq was adopted for robust interpretations (20, 21). Mapped full-length transcripts (annotated and novel) could be obtained in ~75% of unique isoforms detected in both SF3B4OE and vector control (fig. S5B). Hybrid RNA-seq defined the mRNA splicing pattern, which accentuated on SF3B4 principally regulating splicing events of the cassette exon, involving both the exon exclusion (40.98%) and exon inclusion (31.26%) of the mature mRNA (Fig. 3E). Notably, a strong positive correlation (r = 0.5316, P < 0.0001) was found between increased transcript percent spliced-in (PSI) and enhanced transcript expression, suggesting a direct regulatory role of SF3B4 on transcript isoform generation (Fig. 3F). Our analysis identified 748 transcripts with considerable increase in splicing dPSI (>0.1) in SF3B4OE group compared to control (table. S4). These transcripts were enriched for transcription factor binding and DNA binding activities (Fig. 3G).

TBX3+2a, a downstream effector of SF3B4

Our data suggested the likely involvement of SF3B4 in augmenting spliced variants of transcription factors. TBX3+2a showed marked expression in the presence of SF3B4OE and amid the top ranked (fig. S5C). TBX3 is a transcription factor of the T-box family. This gene has an isoform TBX3+2a resultant from an exon inclusion and a canonical form TBX3. Our finding suggested TBX3+2a to be preferentially expressed in the presence of SF3B4. qPCR verification in a cohort of primary HCC tumors (n = 41) corroborated a significant positive correlation between TBX3+2a and SF3B4 expressions (P = 0.0017). This association, however, was not apparent for TBX3 (P = 0.095) (Fig. 4A).

Detailed analysis on RIP-seq data showed enriched binding of SF3B4 on the TBX3 gene, particularly distinct occupancy could be localized to the proximity of exon 2a (Fig. 4B). The strong affinity to TBX3 could be readily demonstrated by RIP-qPCR, which showed a marked enrichment of TBX3 mRNA to SF3B4 protein in pull-down product compared to IgG or random control EZH2 (Fig. 4C). We were able to further affirm the increased presence of variant TBX3+2a by semi-qPCR. In both Liver.Org1 and Liver.Org2, SF3B4OE prompted an elevated TBX3+2a:TBX3 ratio by more than twofold (Fig. 4D), which corresponded to an apparent increase in TBX3+2a mRNA in qPCR assay (Fig. 4E). A direct link between SF3B4 and TBX3+2a was also demonstrated in the knockdown experiment, in which silencing of SF3B4 expression in HCC PDO H688Torg resulted in a decrease in TBX3+2a levels (Fig. 4F). To further support the regulatory role of SF3B4 in mediating TBX3+2a splicing, we used antisense oligonucleotide (ASO) to disrupt the interaction between SF3B4 and TBX3+2a pre-mRNA. This disruption led to a notable decrease in TBX3+2a:TBX3 ratio (Fig. 4G), indicating that SF3B4 binding is required for the proper expression of TBX3+2a. Furthermore, results from minigene reporter assay showed that knockdown of SF3B4 substantially reduced the efficiency of TBX3 exon 2a inclusion, resulting in decreased long form of minigene (Fig. 4H). To assess the functional contribution from TBX3+2a, we used shRNAs designed to specifically target TBX3+2a while maintaining canonical TBX3 expression (fig. S6). This targeted inhibition of TBX3+2a resulted in a noticeable growth arrest in SF3B4OE liver organoids, suggesting a critical role for this isoform in SF3B4-mediated cell growth (Fig. 4I).

To explore the translational impact of manipulating the splicing machinery in HCC treatment, we investigated the relationship between SF3B4 and lenvatinib resistance, a major clinical challenge in HCC management. Notably, SF3B4 knockdown sensitized the lenvatinib-resistant PDO model H813Torg to the drug (fig. S7A). Moreover, lenvatinib treatment induced a dose-dependent up-regulation of SF3B4 across multiple PDO lines (fig. S7, B and C), suggesting a possible positive feedback loop. To further assess therapeutic potential, we performed in vivo xenograft experiments using H3B-8800, a modulator of the SF3B complex. H3B-8800 was tested both as a monotherapy and in combination with lenvatinib. Our results showed that H3B-8800 notably suppressed tumor growth and synergized the effect of lenvatinib in organoid-derived xenografts (Fig. 4J), suggesting an effective combinatorial strategy with current standard therapy.

CRISPR screen revealed GCLC-driven antiferroptosis in lenvatinib resistance

In addition to core essential genes, we also explored genes contributory to lenvatinib resistance, a common phenomenon in patients with HCC receiving this first-line agent (22). We adapted our CRISPR-Cas9 genome-wide screen in H813Torg, which has limited sensitivity to lenvatinib (fig. S8A), to including drug-treated or nontreated control groups throughout the 7-day screening period (Fig. 5A). We found 423 genes to be preferentially and negatively selected in the lenvatinib-treated group (table. S5), among which the highest ranked were ferroptosis suppressors (Fig. 5B and fig. S8B). A prominent negative selection of sgRNAs targeting GCLC, FURIN, BRD3, NQO1, and FTL was observed from our KO screen (fig. S8C). Increased expressions of these ferroptosis suppressors have been linked to the lack of lenvatinib response in patients with HCC ensuing progressive disease (Fig. 5C). As an independent validation method for our KO screen, we stably knockdown expression of these five genes in H813Torg (fig. S8D) and reassessed PDO response to lenvatinib. Consistent with the screening data, down-regulating GCLC, FURIN, BRD3, NQO1, and FTL resensitized H813Torg to lenvatinib (Fig. 5D and fig. S8E). Intriguingly, resensitization to lenvatinib following SF3B4 knockdown was accompanied by ferroptosis activation (figs. S7A and S8, F to H), supporting a mechanistic link between ferroptosis and lenvatinib responsiveness in HCC.

Given that GCLC ranked as the top hit, we further investigated its role in lenvatinib resistance. In an additional lenvatinib-resistant HCC PDO, H688Torg, we consolidated that abolishing GCLC expression readily lowered IC50 value to lenvatinib compared to control (Fig. 5E and fig. S9A). Conversely, GCLC overexpression in drug-responding PDO H744Torg boosted resistance to lenvatinib (Fig. 5F and fig. S9B). To investigate the effect of GCLC in vivo, organoid-derived xenograft of H813Torg that lacked GCLC expression showed tumor diminution following lenvatinib treatment, while empty vector controls remained resistant (Fig. 5G). The shGCLC effect on H813Torg and H688Torg corresponded to an up-regulation of ferroptosis indicators CHAC1 and SLC7A11, and lipid peroxidation, in the presence of lenvatinib (Fig. 5, H-J and fig. S9, C-D). Furthermore, a dose-dependent induction on GCLC expression from lenvatinib was observed in H813Torg and H688Torg (Fig. 5K and fig. S9E), suggesting a feedback up-regulation of GCLC in limiting HCC response to lenvatinib.

Having established that GCLC-mediated suppression of ferroptosis contributes to lenvatinib resistance, we next assessed whether ferroptosis inducers could restore drug sensitivity. Treatment with imidazole ketone erastin (IKE), a well-characterized ferroptosis inducer, led to marked resensitization of HCC tumors to lenvatinib in vivo (Fig. 5L). Consistently, l-buthionine-(S,R)-sulfoximine (BSO), a GCLC inhibitor, also enhanced lenvatinib sensitivity in organoid-derived xenografts (Fig. 5L), further supporting our conclusion that antiferroptosis pathway mediates the lenvatinib resistance in HCC.

DISCUSSION

Genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 functional screening has emerged as a powerful technology in accelerating previously unidentified cancer discoveries (23). Through the utilization of sgRNA libraries, loss-of-function negative screens have enabled the finding of genotype-specific vulnerabilities (8, 9, 23) and underlying mechanisms of drug action, particularly in identifying genes that can work in synergy with a drug or develop resistance against it (24). Reports on CRISPR-Cas9 screening have, however, been largely conducted in cell lines (7, 24), which somehow would render presence of artifacts from the long-term in vitro propagation. PDOs, on the other hand, recapitulate the genetic and histological features of the originating tumor and can better simulate tumor in vivo through structure mimicry in three-dimensional organization. In this study, we undertook the first of its kind CRISPR-Cas9 screen in authentic HCC organoid H813Torg that was directly established from patient’s tumoral tissue. To minimize potential off-target effects in our CRISPR-Cas9 KO screen, we used two independent sgRNA libraries (library A and library B), each containing three distinct sgRNAs targeting the same gene, resulting in six sgRNAs per gene. For each half library, we also performed as two biological replicates. This design allowed us to enhance the reproducibility and specificity of gene-level effects across independently designed guides. We also used shRNA-mediated knockdown as an orthogonal gene-silencing approach to confirm the screen hits.

Our genome-wide negative selection enabled us the identification of essential factors in liver tumorigenesis, and genes in lenvatinib resistance by carrying out two sets of genome-wide CRISPR screens. The first screen identified the SF3B4-TBX3+2a axis as a key oncogenic pathway in HCC progression. Given that H813Torg exhibited intrinsic resistance to lenvatinib, we extended our CRISPR screening approach to investigate genes that modulate lenvatinib resistance. This led to a second screen, which identified the antiferroptosis pathway as a critical contributor to lenvatinib resistance, with GCLC emerging as the top-ranked hit. Although these findings originated from two distinct screening efforts, subsequent experiments revealed a mechanistic connection between SF3B4 function and ferroptosis regulation. Specifically, SF3B4 not only promoted tumor growth but also contributed to lenvatinib resistance by supporting ferroptosis evasion. These findings highlight the pivotal role of SF3B4 in HCC pathogenesis and support the therapeutic potential of targeting ferroptosis pathways to overcome lenvatinib resistance.

Abnormalities in alternative splicing have been implicated in the initiation and progression of human cancers (25, 26). Components of the spliceosome prompt assembly of splicing factors, such as the SF3B family, to initiate pre-mRNA splicing. Our screening data underscored the importance of spliceosome components in HCC, emphasizing SF3B4 as a vital core gene. Functionally, SF3B4 promoted precancerous changes in hepatic organoids, bestowed growth advantages in HCC organoids, and augmented tumorigenicity in HDTVi mouse model. Together with common SF3B4 up-regulations detected in ~60% of HCC tumors, it is plausible that SF3B4 supports the stepwise progression of HCC from the instigation of premalignant features in normal liver to upholding the continued oncogenic pressure in tumor progression. SF3B4 appears to influence a broad range of pathways in cancer, including the splicing machinery (27, 28). In our organoid-based CRISPR KO screen, we identified 29 spliceosome genes that were significantly enriched among the negatively selected hits. Notably, 12 of them were found to interact with SF3B4 based on our pull-down mass spectrometry analysis (fig. S4A). Further analysis revealed that four of these interacting splicing factors—SNRPA1, SF3B1, SF3A2, and SRSF2—are up-regulated upon SF3B4 overexpression (fig. S4, A and S4B). These findings suggest that SF3B4 functions as part of a coregulated network of splicing factors that collectively promote the oncogenic process in HCC. The observed interdependence indicates that perturbing one splicing factor may influence the expression and activity of other components within this network.

Further detailed investigations into SF3B4 interacting proteins and its pre-mRNA bindings showed distinct mRNA splicing pattern conferred within the cassette exon. Notably, the finding of both exon inclusion and exclusion within the cassette exon was in line with reported splicing factor modifications in tumor progression (29–31). Our SF3B4-altered variants were significantly enriched in transcription factors that are known to be master regulators of gene expression. In particular, transcription factor TBX3+2a, an isoform of canonical TBX3, showed prominent expression in the presence of SF3B4OE. We found TBX3+2a could functionally support SF3B4-driven organoid growth, and together with a strong positive link between TBX3+2a occurrence and SF3B4 expression in primary HCC tumors, enforced TBX3+2a as one of the SF3B4 downstream effectors.

Ferroptosis is a regulated cell death that is triggered by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation of the cell membrane. Resistance to ferroptosis is a major obstacle to effective cancer therapy, limiting drug response in multiple cancer types (32, 33). Although lenvatinib has demonstrated improved efficacy over sorafenib in HCC, a major proportion of patients exhibit drug resistance (3, 22). Our results indicated that ferroptosis regulators are key factors in lenvatinib resistance. Ferroptosis suppressors such as GCLC, FURIN, BRD3, NQO1, and FTL were highly ranked contributors to lenvatinib resistance in our CRISPR/Cas9 genotoxic screen. Notably, reducing the expression of GCLC restored sensitivity to lenvatinib with a corresponding increase in ferroptosis markers and lipid peroxidation. Collectively, our data proposed lenvatinib’s ability to trigger ferroptosis in HCC is contingent upon the loss of GCLC-mediated protection.

To conclude, CRISPR-Cas9 screening has accelerated progress in many areas of contemporary cancer research. Our genome-wide KO screen has uncovered SF3B4-driven oncogenic activities that perturbed primarily the alternative splicing events. The SF3B complex is a representative target for drugs such as spliceostatin A, pladienolide B, E7107, and H3B-8800. These small molecules are attracting attention as agents to manipulate splicing and for use as anticancer drugs. Our results showed that H3B-8800 substantially suppressed tumor growth and enhanced the efficacy of lenvatinib in organoid-derived xenografts, supporting the therapeutic potential of splicing modulators as both monotherapies and combination treatments in HCC. Through a second CRISPR screen conducted under lenvatinib treatment, we identified the GCLC-mediated antiferroptosis pathway as a key mechanism of resistance. Inducing ferroptosis using two distinct molecules, IKE and BSO, consistently sensitized HCC xenografts to lenvatinib. Similarly, ferroptosis induction was observed in SF3B4-supressing organoids, which also showed increased sensitivity to lenvatinib. Collectively, these findings highlight two functionally essential and clinically actionable pathways in HCC. They also demonstrate the versatility and translational potential of our organoid-based CRISPR screening platform.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Clinical specimens

The use of clinical specimens for research was approved by the Joint Chinese University of Hong Kong–New Territories East Cluster Clinical Research Ethics Committee (reference no 2020.420). Informed consent was obtained from each patient before collecting specimens.

Paired HCC tumor and adjacent nontumoral liver tissues were collected from patients who underwent curative surgery at the Prince of Wales Hospital, Hong Kong. Following surgical dissection, specimens were immediately snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C until DNA, RNA, and protein extraction. The diagnosis of HCC was confirmed by histology.

Culture for patient-derived tumor organoids and nontumoral liver organoids

HCC tumor organoids and nontumoral liver organoids were derived from surgically excised HCC tumors and adjacent nontumoral liver tissues collected from the resection margin, as previously described (15, 34). Freshly excised tissues were minced and dissociated using a combination of collagenase type 2 and dispase at a ratio of 2:1 (w/w) and filtered with 100-μm cell strainers. The digested cell clusters were embedded in Matrigel (Corning) and seeded in prewarmed culture plates. Tumor organoids were cultured in basal medium, which is AIM-V medium supplemented with 16% fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin/streptomycin (0.35 U/ml), 1% l-glutamine, 1% MEM nonessential amino acids (NEAA), and 0.2% insulin-transferrin-selenium. Nontumoral liver organoids were maintained in basal medium supplemented with wnt3a, R-spondin-1, 2% B-27 supplement, 1% N2 supplement, 1 mM N-acetyl-l-cysteine, 10 mM nicotinamide, Noggin (25 μg/ml), epidermal growth factor (50 ng/ml), fibroblast growth factor 10 (100 ng/ml), HGF (25 ng/ml), 10 nM human [Leu15] Gastrin I, 10 μM forskolin, and the transforming growth factor–β inhibitor A 83-01. All cultures were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2.

Lentiviral production and infection on organoids

Lentivirus was produced by transfecting 293FT cells with psPAX2, pMG2.G, and lentiviral plasmids using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent. Viral particle–containing supernatants were collected and concentrated using Lenti-X Concentrator and resuspended in AIM-V medium for storage at −80°C or immediate use. For viral transduction, dissociated organoid cells were mixed with lentivirus, polybrene (8 μg/ml), and culture medium, followed by centrifugation at 600g for 1 hour and subsequent incubation at 37°C for 6 hours. Transduced cells were then embedded in Matrigel and maintained in culture medium. Antibiotic selection using puromycin or hygromycin was initiated 48 hours posttransduction. Additional experimental details are described in Supplementary Methods.

Genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 KO screen

The organoid-based genome-wide CRISPR screen was conducted using the Human CRISPR Knockout Pooled Library (GeCKOv2 library), which comprises two half-libraries (library A and library B). Each library targets 19,050 genes with three sgRNAs per gene. Independent lentiviral pools were generated from libraries A and B and used to infect PDO H813Torg separately, ensuring that each gene was targeted by a total of six sgRNAs. The screening was conducted in four biological replicates to enhance accuracy and reproducibility. The workflows of KO screens are illustrated in Figs. 1B and 5A. Briefly, dissociated H813Torg was transduced with GeCKOv2 library at a low MOI to ensure effective barcoding of individual cells. The transduced cells were embedded in Matrigel at a 2000 cell/μl density and selected with puromycin (2.5 μg/ml) for 5 days. A portion of cells was harvested as a day 0 control, while the remaining cells cultured for additional 7 days with or without 30 μM lenvatinib. After that, at least 3.5 × 107 cells per condition were collected for genomic DNA extraction to ensure sufficient coverage of the GeCKOv2 library. Integrated sgRNA sequences were amplified using NEBNext High-Fidelity 2X PCR Master Mix as described (6) and subjected to next-generation sequencing by Novogene Technology (Beijing, China). For each experiment, using nontargeting control sgRNAs as normalization method, the sgRNA read count and hits calling were analyzed using the MAGeCK v0.5.9 algorithm resulting a ranked gene list reporting positively and negatively selected genes (13, 35). Ranked gene lists were integrated using the RRA (v1.2.1) method with default settings (36). Genes with P value < 0.05 were considered consistently selected across replicates and classified as significantly selected. The fold change for each gene was calculated as the average across all experiments.

Organoid cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using CellTiter-Glo 3D Cell Viability Reagent. Briefly, organoids embedded in 2 μl of Matrigel were seeded in 96-well plates at 1500 to 5000 cells per well. Growth was monitored by measuring cell viability at defined time points and normalized against initial values measured on day 0. To assess response to lenvatinib, varying drug concentrations were added on day 2 postseeding and incubated for 5 days. End-point cell viability was measured and normalized to untreated controls. Drug response curves were generated and analyzed using GraphPad Prism with nonlinear regression curve fitting. Each experiment was performed at least three times to compare the mean of lenvatinib IC50s.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were conducted under the ethical approval of the Animal Experimentation Ethics Committee of The Chinese University of Hong Kong and in full compliance with the Animals (Control of Experiments) Ordinance of Hong Kong. All animal handling and care procedures were performed according to the guidelines outlined in the NIH Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.

To establish HDTVi HCC model, male C57BL/6 mice were intravenously injected with a saline solution at a volume equivalent to 10% of their body weight within 8 s, as previously described (37). The saline solution contains a mixture of plasmids, including 30 μg of pX330-based CRISPR-Cas9 vectors targeting Trp53 (pX330-sgTrp53), 4 μg of a Sleeping Beauty transposase vector (SB100X), and 10 μg of Myc overexpression transposon vector (pT3-EF1α-Myc). For simultaneous expression of SF3B4 or empty vector (EV) control, 5 μg of the pT3-EF1α-SF3B4 or pT3-EF1α-EV was co-injected with the plasmid cocktail. Mice were monitored daily for the development of palpable tumors and euthanized after 5 weeks postinjection. Additional experimental details about the PDO-derived xenografts are provided in Supplementary Methods.

RIP-seq and RIP-qPCR

RIP was conducted using the EZ-Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Sigma-Aldrich) with slight modifications. Liver.Org 1 overexpression Flag-tagged SF3B4 was cross-linked with formaldehyde, and the reaction was quenched with glycine. Cells were lysed in RIP lysis buffer containing protease and ribonuclease (RNase) inhibitors, and the lysate was gently sonicated. A portion of the lysate was saved as the input control, while the rest was incubated with anti-Flag, anti-EZH2, or mouse IgG antibodies for 4 hours 4°C, followed by incubation with magnetic beads for 1 hour. The beads were washed sequentially with lysis buffer, high-salt buffer, and LiCl wash buffer and then resuspended in RIP wash buffer. Protein-RNA complexes were eluted by reversing cross-links at 60°C and treated with proteinase K. RNA was extracted and purified using TRIzol-LS reagent, followed by deoxyribonuclease I digestion to remove DNA contaminants. The purified RNA was resuspended in RNase-free water for RIP-seq or RIP-qPCR after reverse transcription. Additional experimental details are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Repository datasets

The HCC mutational profile from the Prince of Wales Hospital (PWH)-HCC cohort (113 cases) and the paired HCC tumor and nontumor tissue dataset (27 pairs) used for transcriptomic analysis of essential spliceosome genes have been previously described (4, 5). RNA expression data with known patient response to lenvatinib treatment were from previously deposited data (NGDC database, GSA: HRA006499) (12).

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed in at least triplicate. Data were presented as means ± SD. Statistical analysis was conducted using GraphPad Prism (version 8.4.0) and R on RStudio. Statistical significance was determined using specific tests, including unpaired or paired Student’s t tests, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and two-way ANOVA. A P value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with significance levels denoted as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001. Additional experimental assays to support validations and functions are described in Supplementary Methods.
