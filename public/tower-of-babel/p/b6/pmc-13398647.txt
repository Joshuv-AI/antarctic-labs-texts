Assessing pediatric gastroenterologists' use of artificial intelligence in clinical and professional tasks
Authors: Ashwin Agrawal, Vineeta Guntupalli, Jonathan E Teitelbaum, Mohamed Elrais
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesArtificial intelligence (AI) has demonstrated potential to enhance clinical efficiency. However, its real‐world adoption among pediatric gastroenterologists (GIs) remains poorly characterized. The primary objective of this study was to assess how pediatric GIs are currently utilizing AI. Secondary objectives included estimating time saved, identifying commonly used tools, and evaluating the impact on burnout.MethodsAn institutional review board‐approved anonymous survey was distributed via the international pediatric gastroenterology listserv. The questionnaire assessed demographics, types of AI technologies utilized, and estimated time saved through their use. The survey was open from September 30, 2025, to October 28, 2025.ResultsOne‐hundred and twenty‐nine completed surveys were included in the analysis. Majority of respondents were from the United States (85.3%). AI was most frequently used for ambient AI scribes, dictation, answering clinical questions, preparing letters of medical necessity, and insurance appeals, with many reporting time savings with use of AI. In addition, 58.1% of respondents had utilized AI for some research‐related purpose. AI utilization did not vary significantly across years of practice or practice type. Clinicians who utilized AI technology had higher frequency of self‐reported reduced burnout. Few (28.7%) received training in AI, while the majority (84.4%) would like more education in the use of AI in practice.ConclusionsThe use of AI tools by pediatric GIs is variable. Those who adopted AI reported reduced burnout and increased time savings, reflecting a positive impact on efficiency and clinician well‐being. Despite limited formal training, there was a strong interest in AI education and clinical integration.Keywords: burnout, documentation, efficiency, information technology, scribes

INTRODUCTION

Physicians have been incorporating artificial intelligence (AI) applications into their workflow for more than a decade. One of the earliest applications of AI in medicine was INTERNIST‐1, developed in the 1970s, which laid the foundation for computer‐assisted clinical decision‐making by simulating a physician's diagnostic reasoning. 1 Over the last decade, AI use has been substantially incorporated into healthcare practice, particularly for clinical documentation and clinical decision‐making. Despite these advances, physician burnout remains a persistent challenge. Burnout rates for physicians have remained relatively constant over the past decade despite its growing awareness and healthcare organizations' attempts to combat physician burnout. Overall, about 45.2% of physicians experienced burnout in 2023, which is similar to burnout of 38.2% in 2020 and 43.9% in 2017. 2 This illustrates a need to implement technologies that may help physicians with the ongoing challenges of work‐life integration.

Optimistically, there is evidence that utilizing advanced information technologies for clinician workflows may improve provider (physicians, etc.) burnout rates while also improving patient satisfaction scores. A scoping literature review established the effectiveness of AI‐driven workflow tools, including natural language processing (NLP), electronic health record (EHR) integration, and clinical decision support systems (CDSS), in reducing burnout and mental strain. These benefits were observed among physicians, nurses, and allied clinical staff. 3 In addition, a systematic review exploring EHR‐embedded AI applications such as ambient AI scribes, CDSS, large language models (LLM), and NLP tools reported consistently reduced workload for physicians. 4 Studies performed to assess the efficacy of AI scribe tools showed drastic improvement in physician burnout and reduced after‐hours clinical work documentation. 5 Although AI‐driven innovations have exhibited potential in various medical specialties, studies assessing real‐world adoption of AI are still limited, especially in pediatric subspecialties like pediatric gastroenterology (GI).

Pediatric GI is a highly demanding specialty requiring high cognitive demand, longitudinal care of complex chronic conditions, and extensive documentation. Combined with high clinical volumes, these pressures significantly increase provider stress and risk of burnout. The use of AI tools shows promise in improving the workflow efficiency of pediatric GIs to help clinicians save time and potentially reduce burnout.

This is the first study assessing the real‐world usage of AI technologies by pediatric GIs. The primary objective of this study was to assess how pediatric GIs are currently utilizing AI. Secondary objectives included estimating time saved, identifying commonly used tools, and evaluating the impact on provider burnout.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study underwent expedited review (institutional review board [IRB] Registration 00003104) by the Monmouth Medical Center's IRB and was determined to be IRB‐exempt.

Study design

The survey instrument was designed in Rutgers University's REDCap, a secure, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)‐compliant, web platform for building and maintaining surveys. The survey was designed to be anonymous, and no identifiable information was collected.

The survey was designed to assess how pediatric GIs utilized AI and if that use of AI saved time. The survey consisted of 29 questions (Data S1). It assessed if pediatric GIs utilized voice recognition dictation, ambient listening AI, AI to help answer clinical questions, AI to help write letters of medical necessity (LOMN), AI to help write letters for insurance appeals (LOIA), AI in endoscopy/colonoscopy, and AI in research tasks. For clinical‐related tasks, questions were asked to assess if time was saved by use of these technologies. The survey also assessed interest in learning how to utilize AI and about burnout. Questions were also included to assess general demographics.

Prior to finalizing the survey, the survey was assessed by three practicing GIs for readability, clarity, and understandability. Their feedback was incorporated into the final survey.

Study distribution

The survey instrument was distributed via the international listserv for pediatric GI providers maintained by the University of Vermont (pedgi@list.uvm.edu). The survey was sent to 3617 recipients, which includes physicians, trainees, nurses, advanced practitioners, and others who are interested in pediatric GI worldwide.

The survey was open for responses from September 30, 2025, to October 28, 2025. A total of three emails were sent to members of the listserv over the study period.

Statistical analysis

Survey data were stored in REDCap. Survey responses were evaluated to remove fraudulent responses by evaluating if answers matched what was asked in each question. For questions asking about AI technology utilized, respondents who utilized multiple names of the same technology product were grouped to the parent (main) technology product. Microsoft Excel and IBM SPSS V31 were used to perform data analysis. Descriptive statistics were calculated for all study variables. Chi‐squared analysis was performed to determine between‐group differences. Only complete surveys were utilized in data analysis.

RESULTS

At the completion of the study period, a total of 169 responses were received, of which 129 were fully completed. The completed response rate was 3.6%, representing the proportion of fully completed surveys (n = 129) out of the total number of surveys distributed via the listserv (n = 3617). One respondent was excluded from data analysis because their response was identified as fraudulent, as this respondent would answer that they do not use specific technology but then choose the most minutes saved by that technology, and in a free text answer wrote: “stop using the PEDGI board for this.”

Completed surveys represented 13 unique countries. The majority of the responses were from the United States (n = 110, 85.3% of respondents). Other countries with multiple responses included Canada (n = 4), Hungary (n = 3), and Mexico (n = 3). Countries with a single response included the United Kingdom, Turkey, Slovenia, Saudi Arabia, Portugal, Philippines, Greece, Chile, and Australia.

Demographic data indicated that 11.6% of respondents were fellow/trainees, 83.7% attending physicians, and 4.7% advanced practice providers (i.e., nurse practitioners or physician assistants). The respondents represented different years of experience, with 21.7% having less than 5 years of experience, 20.2% having 6 to less than 10 years of experience, 17.1% having 11 to less than 15 years of experience, 10.1% having 16 to less than 20 years of experience, and 19.4% having more than 20 years of experience. Most of the respondents practiced in quaternary care centers (42.6%), then tertiary care centers (34.9%), community medical centers (14.0%), private practice (7.8%), and military medical centers (0.8%).

In regard to research related purposes, AI was utilized during many research activities including performing a literature review (52.7%), to help improve research ideas (17.8%), to help write a research article (16.3%), to help write a research protocol (13.2%), to help generate research ideas (12.4%), and to help with data analysis (8.5%).

How often pediatric GIs utilize AI varies. Moreover, 39.5% utilize AI daily, 31.0% utilize AI a few times a week, 15.5% utilize AI a few times a month, and 5.4% utilize AI a few times a year. Only 8.5% did not use any AI tools. There was no significant difference in the frequency of use of AI by years of experience (p = 0.66) or by practice setting (p = 0.37).

Pediatric GIs utilized a wide range of AI products to improve their clinical workflow. Table 3 shows the different AI products utilized by respondents of the survey. The most common AI products for note writing were Dragon® (61 respondents) and Abridge® (31 respondents). The two most common tools to help answer questions or write letters included OpenEvidence® (79 respondents) and ChatGPT® (83 respondents).

This study also asked respondents if the use of AI tools in providers' clinical/professional work impacted their feelings about burnout. The results showed 14.7% had significantly reduced feelings of burnout and 40.3% had somewhat reduced feelings of burnout. About 43.4% saw no noticeable impact on burnout. Only 1.6% had somewhat increased feelings of burnout, and no one had significantly increased burnout. Subgroup analysis shows that providers who utilized AI more frequently had higher rates of reduced burnout (p < 0.001). Subgroup analysis showed that 74.5% of respondents who utilized ambient listening AI reported reduced burnout. Those who utilized ambient AI had increased frequency of reduced burnout compared to those who did not use ambient AI (p < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

This study provides the first description of real‐world AI utilization by pediatric GIs. Our findings demonstrate that AI tools are already widely integrated into both clinical and professional activities within pediatric GI. A substantial portion of respondents reported frequent AI use, with perceived gains in efficiency across several practice domains. Notably, clinicians who reported more frequent AI use also described lower levels of burnout. Reduced burnout was also true in clinicians who utilized ambient AI scribes.

There are many AI tools clinicians can utilize in their practice. These include LLM which synthesize and generate human‐like text to assist with clinical question answering, documentation, and patient communication. NLP technologies extract and structure clinically relevant information from the EHR. Ambient AI scribe systems use speech recognition and contextual understanding to generate clinical notes in real time from patient‐clinician encounters. CDSS apply algorithmic models to provide care recommendations at the point of care. Generative AI extends these capabilities by producing new text or content based on learned patterns. The emergence of novel agentic AI systems may, in the future, be able to autonomously execute multi‐step tasks such as information retrieval, documentation, and workflow coordination under clinical oversight. Collectively, these AI technologies are designed to augment clinical efficiency, reduce administrative burden, and support clinical decision‐making without replacing physician judgment. 2 , 3 , 4 , 5

AI was most applied to tasks that are time‐intensive and administratively burdensome. The most reported applications of AI in this survey included using AI to help answer clinical questions, drafting LOMN and insurance appeals, and supporting clinical documentation by either dictation or ambient listening AI scribes. These findings are consistent with emerging evidence that clinicians preferentially adopt AI for documentation, administrative support, and to improve their workflows. For example, surveys of general practitioners found that many physicians reported using LLM tools to generate documentation, support differential diagnosis, and create patient summarizations. 6 Similarly, a survey of physician sentiments found that the most valuable functions of generative AI were information gathering and documentation assistance. 7 In addition, studies of implementation of ambient AI scribes show reductions in documentation burden and clinician‐perceived cognitive load. 5 , 8

Time savings associated with the use of AI were reported across multiple applications, with ambient AI scribe tools yielding the greatest perceived benefit. Nearly half of the clinicians utilizing ambient AI reported saving more than 1 h per day. These findings parallel recent evaluations of ambient AI scribe systems, which have demonstrated reductions in after‐hours charting and improvements in professional satisfaction. 3 , 5 , 9 Given that documentation burden is a well‐established contributor to physician burnout, these efficiencies may have important implications for both individual clinicians and the broader pediatric GI workforce. 2

While both voice recognition dictation and ambient AI technologies aim to reduce documentation burden, ambient AI systems may offer greater efficiency by passively capturing and structuring clinical encounters without requiring active physician input. As this technology evolves, it may lead to greater time savings. The observed differences in perceived benefit between technologies may also reflect varying levels of familiarity, institutional support, and individual clinician preference. These were not assessed in this study but likely influence adoption patterns and perceived effectiveness.

The association between AI use and reduced burnout observed warrants particular attention given the high effect of burnout on physicians and projected physician shortage. More than half of respondents reported some improvement in burnout. Those who utilized AI tools more frequently had more frequent improvements in burnout. In addition, those respondents who utilized ambient AI scribes also had a higher frequency of reduced burnout. These findings are consistent with a growing body of research suggesting that AI‐enabled workflow support may alleviate administrative burden and improve clinician well‐being. 2 , 3 , 4 This association observed with ambient AI scribe supports the concept that benefits of AI for physicians are more likely to arise from automation of clerical tasks rather than from substitution of clinical decision‐making.

These findings about burnout are particularly relevant in pediatric GI, where prior studies have demonstrated high workload demands and significant burnout rates among providers, largely driven by documentation burden and practice inefficiencies. 10 , 11 Given the association between AI use and reduced burnout observed in this study, increased access through thoughtful implementation of these technologies may represent a potential strategy to mitigate clinician burnout.

AI adoption did not vary significantly by years of experience or practice setting, suggesting broad acceptance across career stages and institutional environments. The relatively uniform adoption observed may reflect increasing familiarity with AI tools, improved usability, and shared documentation pressures that affect pediatric GIs regardless of seniority or practice.

In addition to clinical applications, more than half of the respondents reported using AI for research‐related activities. Most common uses were for literature review, improving research ideas, and manuscript preparation. While AI‐assisted research, specifically writing of manuscripts, has prompted concerns of authorship, accuracy, and transparency, evolving editorial guidance emphasizes responsible use and disclosure rather than strict prohibition. 12 , 13 As AI tools become more prevalent in academic medicine, pediatric subspecialties require clear institutional and journal‐level standards to ensure ethical and methodologically sound use of AI.

Table 3 highlights the range of AI technologies currently being used by pediatric GIs in clinical practice. Respondents reported use of multiple commercial and general‐purpose platforms across documentation, ambient AI, answering clinical questions and administrative tasks, without reliance on a single dominant product. The heterogeneity likely represents the competitive marketplace for AI technologies for clinicians, as well as differences in institutional infrastructure, EHR integration, local availability, product cost, and individual preferences.

Survey responses also highlighted a strong interest in formal training on the use of AI tools by pediatric GIs. This shows that there is a need for structured educational activities. Incorporating AI‐focused training into continuing medication education and fellowship curricula may facilitate responsible adoption of these technologies and help clinicians maximize the potential benefits of these technologies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first survey study on the use of AI by pediatric GIs, and its modest response rate raises the possibility of selection bias as clinicians with greater interest or experience using AI may have been more likely to participate. Measurements of time savings and burnout were self‐reported and could result in recall or perception bias. In addition, most respondents were from the United States, which may limit generalizability to healthcare systems with different regulatory and documentation structures. The cross‐sectional design and reliance on self‐reported measures limit the ability to make causal inferences. In addition, this study assessed perceived impact rather than objective workflow metrics, which underscores the need for future prospective and interventional studies. Finally, given the rapidly evolving nature of AI technologies, patterns of use, availability, and clinician perceptions may change over time, potentially limiting the long‐term generalizability of these findings. Despite these limitations, this study provides important early insights into how pediatric GIs are engaging with AI in routine practice.

This study is intended to assess current utilization patterns of AI among pediatric gastroenterologists and does not advocate for or against its use given ongoing debate and variability in evidence regarding its role in clinical practice.

CONCLUSION

This study has demonstrated that pediatric GIs report widespread use of AI tools across clinical, administrative, and scholarly activities. AI applications were most used to support documentation, answering clinical questions, and writing letters of appeals/medical necessity. Many clinicians reported meaningful time savings due to AI use. Importantly, greater AI use was associated with increased frequency of self‐reported reduced burnout, suggesting potential benefits for clinical efficiency and wellbeing. As the specialty continues to face increasing clinical complexity and administrative demands, thoughtfully implemented AI tools may offer a practical means of supporting clinicians.
