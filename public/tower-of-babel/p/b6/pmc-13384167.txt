Patterns in withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment in trauma patients with and without moderate to severe traumatic brain injury: an ACS-TQIP retrospective cohort study
Authors: Nicole Russell, Alexander L Eastman, Jennifer L Grant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTrauma-specific palliative care guidelines are limited, and physicians rely heavily on experience to guide goals-of-care discussions, leading to substantial practice variation. Existing literature does not adequately account for the marked heterogeneity among trauma patients. How traumatic brain injury influences the timing and determinants of delayed withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment in patients with concomitant major extracranial injuries remains poorly understood.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients ≥16 years with withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment in the American College of Surgeons Trauma Quality Improvement Program database (2021–2023). Patients were stratified by the presence of moderate to severe traumatic brain injury. The primary outcome was time to withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment. Our secondary aim was to assess predictors of delayed withdrawal (>75th percentile for each group) using separate binary logistic regression models.ResultsAmong 41 990 patients, 23 101 (55%) had a moderate to severe traumatic brain injury. Withdrawal occurred earlier in patients with moderate to severe traumatic brain injury (median 4 days (IQR 2–9) vs. 6 days (3–12), p<0.0001). The probability of delayed withdrawal increased non-linearly with increasing complication burden, plateauing near 100% after seven complications, with the first four having the greatest marginal effect. In patients with moderate to severe traumatic brain injury, unique predictors of delayed withdrawal included diffuse axonal injury, decompressive surgery, and intracranial pressure monitoring. The only major extracranial injury with predictive value was major chest injury (OR 1.3, 95% CI 1.1 to 1.4, p<0.001).ConclusionsWithdrawal of life-sustaining treatment occurs earlier in patients with moderate to severe traumatic brain injury and appears to be driven by distinct factors. Complications may serve as practical triggers for goals-of-care discussions. In polytrauma patients with moderate to severe traumatic brain injury, withdrawal decisions appear to be predominantly influenced by factors related to neurological injury. These findings support pursuing a more nuanced approach to palliative care research in the trauma intensive care unit.Level of evidenceLevel III.

Background

In the USA, approximately 6% of adult trauma patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) undergo withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment (WLST) each year.1 Physicians and families are faced with decisions regarding both whether to pursue WLST and when to begin these discussions, for which timing is critical. Decisions to withdraw life-sustaining treatment are often delayed due to prognostic uncertainty.2 While additional time may improve prognostic clarity, neither increased time nor escalating therapy intensity guarantees an improved clinical trajectory or greater prognostic certainty. Delaying WLST decisions carries the risks of delivering aggressive and invasive interventions that may conflict with patient preferences, prolong patient and family suffering, and lead to inefficient allocation of hospital resources.3 4 However, this is balanced with the fear of premature WLST, which may forfeit recovery potential.3 4

The American College of Surgeons Trauma Quality Improvement Program (ACS-TQIP) Palliative Care Best Practice Guidelines endorse early goals-of-care (GOC) and life-sustaining treatment discussions in trauma patients with serious illness.5 However, these guidelines have several limitations that hinder practical application. According to the ACS-TQIP guidelines, all hospitalized trauma patients should receive a palliative care assessment within the first 24 hours to (1) identify the healthcare proxy or surrogate decision maker, (2) identify any pre-existing advance directives, living will, or do-not-resuscitate orders, (3) understand the family and social contexts, and (4) assess prognosis.5 This initial screening aims to stratify patients into a negative screen (eg, healthy patients with non-life-threatening, non-disabling injuries), category 1 positive screen (eg, uncertain prognosis, ≥1 serious illness, frailty, and/or old age) and category 2 positive screen (eg, anticipated high risk of hospital mortality, permanent disability, and chronic illness, frailty, and/or old age) to further guide palliative care needs.5 While these guidelines attempt to provide guidance, in reality, it can be difficult to distinguish between category 1 and category 2 patients. In category 2 patients, the recommendation is to consider comfort measures, do-not-resuscitate orders and withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy.5 However, in category 1 patients, the recommendation is to pursue GOC discussions, code status discussions, time-limited trials, and palliative care consultations.5 It is in these patients, those in whom the prognosis is uncertain, and GOC discussions are tenuous, that decisions around transitioning to WLST can be especially difficult. Additionally, there is a paucity of high-level evidence in WLST decision-making in the trauma setting to help inform GOC discussions in this patient population. While the guidelines provide some examples for categorizing patients, there is a lack of prognostic tools that account for the medical complexity of polytrauma patients and can accurately predict prognosis early after injury, when these patients are being categorized. Finally, studies are beginning to show that traditional tools to trigger GOC discussions and more extensive palliative care, such as the Surprise Question, which is recommended by the ACS-TQIP guidelines, may not be generalizable to trauma patients.6

These limitations add significant strain to GOC decision-making in the ICU setting.2 7 Additional challenges in the trauma ICU include a patient population that commonly includes young individuals with few pre-existing medical conditions, as well as low rates of prior GOC discussions and advance directive documentation.811 Physicians are often left to rely on their assessment and experience to help families navigate the uncertainty embedded in these decisions.2 Consequently, there is substantial variability in practice patterns across both physicians and institutions regarding GOC discussions and WLST decision-making.1218

There is a clear need for more evidence-based guidelines to identify practical triggers for timely GOC discussions in the trauma ICU, yet the existing literature does not adequately account for the marked heterogeneity among trauma patients. In particular, moderate to severe traumatic brain injury (msTBI) is associated with distinct physiology, management, and prognostic challenges that distinguish it from other types of severe injury.19 Moreover, many patients in the trauma ICU with msTBI have additional major extracranial injuries, which importantly distinguish these patients from those with isolated severe traumatic brain injury (sTBI).

To our knowledge, differences in the timing and predictors of WLST between severely injured polytrauma patients with and without msTBI have not been directly explored. Understanding these differences is a crucial first step toward improving GOC discussions in the trauma ICU and improving the quality of WLST decisions. We hypothesized that there would be a significant difference in time to WLST between critically injured trauma patients with and without msTBI, and consequently, there would be a different timeline for determining delayed WLST in these groups. Additionally, we hypothesized that the presence or absence of msTBI in trauma patients would influence the factors associated with delayed WLST decisions, further accentuating the importance of pursuing a more nuanced approach to GOC discussions and palliative care research in the trauma ICU.

Methods

This is a retrospective cohort study using data queried from the ACS-TQIP database from January 1, 2021 to December 31, 2023. The database was filtered to include those aged ≥16 and with documented WLST. Patients were considered to have a WLST order if treatment was withdrawn based on a decision to either remove or withhold further life-supporting intervention, including: ventilator support, dialysis or other forms of renal support, institution of medications to support blood pressure or cardiac function, or a specific surgical, interventional or radiological procedure.20 Patients were excluded if they died in the emergency department, were pregnant, or had missing data (figure 1). A STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) checklist was used to ensure proper reporting of methods, results, and discussion (online supplemental information).21

Flowchart of study design. ACS-TQIP, American College of Surgeons Trauma Quality Improvement Program; ED, emergency department; msTBI, moderate to severe traumatic brain injury; WLST, withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment.

Patients were stratified based on the presence of msTBI. To identify the presence of msTBI, we first identified patients with a head/neck Abbreviated Injury Scale (AIS) score of ≥3. We then filtered this group further to isolate patients with a traumatic or hypoxic brain injury, using key terms in the AIS code descriptions (online supplemental information). We manually ensured that no additional brain injuries were incorrectly grouped. Patients who did not have msTBI by these criteria were placed in the non-msTBI group for analysis. Although ACS-TQIP guidelines defined msTBI based on post-resuscitation Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), the National Trauma Data Standard (NTDS) dataset provides only limited GCS data, specifically the initial GCS on arrival and the highest GCS during hospitalization. We anticipated that reliance on initial GCS alone could underestimate injury severity due to potential early undertriage, while highest GCS could miss patients who experienced neurological decline. Therefore, we used AIS-based criteria to better capture patients with significant anatomic brain injury.

The primary outcome was median time to WLST in patients with and without msTBI. Our secondary aim was to identify factors associated with delayed WLST in each group. Delayed WLST was defined as time to WLST greater than the 75th percentile of the group. Using this cut-off, delayed WLST was defined as >9 days in the non-msTBI group and >12 days in the msTBI group. Variables included in regression models in both groups comprised patient characteristics (age, gender, race, ethnicity, and pre-existing conditions), insurance type (private/commercial, Medicare, Medicaid, self-pay, other), Injury Severity Score (ISS), presence of any shock (shock index (SI) >0.6), presence of moderate/severe shock (SI >1.0), AIS score ≥3 in each body region, hemorrhage control surgery, hospital type (non-profit, for-profit, government), teaching status (academic/university vs. community/non-teaching), ACS trauma verification level, bed size, interhospital transfer, and in-hospital complications, as defined by the NTDS Data Dictionary (online supplemental information). Unique variables assessed in the msTBI group included GCS motor score, type of TBI, intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring, midline shift, and decompressive surgery. We identified patients who underwent decompressive surgery using ICD-10 (International Classification of Diseases–Tenth Revision) procedures codes (online supplemental table 1). We also included estimations of unfavorable outcome at 6 months based on the International Mission for Prognosis and Analysis of Clinical Trials (IMPACT) prognostic calculator. The IMPACT calculator is a validated prognostic tool to estimate 6-month outcome in patients with msTBI.22 We used the History and Physical only (core model), which assigns weight and points to age, initial GCS motor score, and pupil reactivity.23

Separate univariate analyses for each group were conducted to determine preliminary variables associated with delayed WLST. All variables with a p value <0.25 were then included in a multivariate analysis. These groups were analyzed independently with two separate multivariate binary logistic regression models. Missing values were handled with listwise deletion. Results are presented as OR with 95% CI. Results were considered significant at a two-sided alpha level of p value <0.05. Regression analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.28.0.1.1. Stata (V.18, StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX) was used for marginal effects analysis and for marginal plot figure. All other figures were made with GraphPad Prism (V.10.1.1).

Results

Full cohort analysis

A total of 41 984 patients were included in our analysis, of whom 23 101 (55%) had an msTBI (figure 1). Baseline patient demographics and characteristics differed significantly between groups (table 1). When compared with patients without msTBI, patients with msTBI were younger (70 years (53–80) vs. 75 years (64–83), p<0.001) and had fewer comorbidities (0 (0–1) vs. 1 (0–2), p<0.001), yet significantly greater injury severity (ISS 26 (22–30) vs. 10 (9–22), p<0.001). Black patients (OR 1.7, 95% CI 1.6 to 1.8), patients of other races (OR 1.3, 95% CI 1.2 to 1.4), and patients of Hispanic/Latino ethnicity (OR 1.2, 95% CI 1.1 to 1.3) were more likely to undergo delayed WLST compared with white and non-Hispanic patients. WLST was performed significantly earlier in patients with msTBI (4 days (2–9) vs. 6 days (3–12), U=1.9×108, p<0.0001, figure 2). The in-hospital mortality rate for the entire WLST cohort was 84%, consistent with the expectation of limited life expectancy after WLST. A remaining 12.7% of patients were discharged to hospice care. When stratifying by the presence of msTBI, the in-hospital mortality rates for trauma patients with msTBI who underwent WLST were 86%, compared with 83% in patients without msTBI.

In-hospital events and complications associated with delayed WLST

In both groups, in-hospital complications were strong positive predictors of delayed WLST (figures3 4). In patients with msTBI, the complications most strongly associated with delayed WLST were major infection (OR 8.7, 95% CI 7.3 to 10.5, p<0.001), venous thromboembolism (VTE) (OR 5.9, 95% CI 4.7 to 7.4, p<0.001), delirium (OR 2.8, 95% CI 2.3 to 3.3, p<0.001), cerebrovascular accident (CVA) (OR 2.3, 95% CI 1.9 to 2.8, p<0.001), alcohol withdrawal syndrome (OR 2.1, 95% CI 1.5 to 3.0, p<0.001), and acute kidney injury (AKI) (OR 1.5, 95% CI 1.3 to 1.9, p<0.001). Events and interventions such as unplanned ICU admission (OR 4.2, 95% CI 3.6 to 4.8, p<0.001), ICP monitoring (OR 2.3, 95% CI 2.1 to 2.5, p<0.001), unplanned surgery (OR 2.10, 95% CI 1.7 to 2.3, p<0.001), and decompressive surgery (OR 1.8, 95% CI 1.5 to 2.1, p<0.001) were also strongly associated with later time to WLST. In-hospital cardiac arrest with return of spontaneous circulation (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.73 to 1.0, p=0.06) and myocardial infarction (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.52 to 1.3, p=0.44) did not have significant associations with time to WLST.

Similar patterns were observed in patients without msTBI. Major infection (OR 3.7, 95% CI 3.2 to 4.2, p<0.001), delirium (OR 2.7, 95% CI 2.4 to 2.8, p<0.001), unplanned ICU admission (OR 2.6, 95% CI 2.3 to 3.1, p<0.001), VTE (OR 2.9, 95% CI 2.4 to 3.5, p<0.001), alcohol withdrawal syndrome (OR 2.0, 95% CI 1.5 to 2.7, p<0.001), CVA (OR 1.8, 95% CI 1.5 to 2.2, p<0.001), and AKI (OR 1.4, 95% CI 1.2 to 1.6, p<0.001) were significant predictors of delayed WLST. Unplanned surgery was a significant predictor of delayed WLST (OR 2.2, 95% CI 2.3 to 2.8, p<0.001), yet the need for hemorrhage control surgery was associated with earlier time to WLST (OR 0.89, 95% CI 0.78 to 0.98, p=0.02).

Predictors of delayed WLST in patients with sTBI

Patient characteristics

After adjusting for injury and institutional characteristics, each 10-year increase in age beginning at 20 years was associated with greater odds of late WLST in patients with msTBI (figure 3). There was no association between a greater comorbidity burden (≥3) and WLST timing. However, we did observe associations between WLST timing and specific comorbidities. A history of dementia (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.76, p<0.001) and disseminated cancer (OR 0.64, 95% CI 0.50 to 0.82, p<0.001) were associated with lower odds of late WLST. Uninsured patients had significantly lower odds of delayed WLST when compared with patients with any insurance (figure 3).

Injury severity

When compared with ISS ≤15, a moderate ISS (16–24) was associated with a greater likelihood of delayed WLST (OR 1.2, 95% CI 1.0 to 1.3, p=0.02). However, this trend was reversed with an ISS ≥25 (OR 0.96, 95% CI 0.76 to 0.98, p=0.02). Moderate or severe shock on presentation was a strong negative predictor of late WLST (OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.73 to 0.91, p<0.001). When assessing the effect of major extracranial injury, moderate to severe chest injuries (AIS score ≥3) were the only body region associated with delayed WLST (OR 1.3, 95% CI 1.1 to 1.4, p<0.001).

Institutional characteristics

After adjusting for patient and injury characteristics, there were no associations between interfacility transfer, bed size, ACS trauma verification level, hospital type (for-profit, non-profit, government), or teaching status and time to WLST in patients with msTBI (table 2).

Unique factors associated with delayed WLST in patients with sTBI

Higher initial GCS motor scores trended toward delayed WLST, but this was not statistically significant (figure 3). GCS motor scores of 1 with paralysis and without paralysis were associated with a lower likelihood of delayed WLST (figure 3). In contrast, estimations of the probability of an unfavorable outcome at 6 months using the IMPACT calculator were strong negative predictors of delayed WLST (figure 3). When compared with patients with a <25% probability of unfavorable outcome, intermediate probability groups (eg, 25–49% and 50–74%) were associated with similar odds of undergoing delayed WLST and were both statistically significant negative predictors of delayed WLST. A probability of ≥75% was associated with a much lower odds of undergoing delayed WLST.

Predictors of delayed WLST in patients without msTBI

Patient characteristics

In contrast to patients with msTBI, age was not a significant predictor of late WLST in patients without msTBI (figure 4). Like patients with msTBI, a greater number of comorbidities (≥3) was not predictive of WLST timing. Dementia (OR 0.73, 95% CI 0.64 to 0.83, p<0.001) and disseminated cancer (OR 0.80, 95% CI 0.65 to 0.99, p=0.043) were negative predictors of late WLST, while chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (OR 1.2, 95% CI 1.1 to 1.3, p<0.001) and cirrhosis (OR 1.3, 95% CI 1.1 to 1.5, p=0.002) were positive predictors of late WLST. Like patients with msTBI, uninsured patients in the no-msTBI cohort had lower odds of late WLST when compared with patients with any insurance.

Injury severity

When compared with ISS ≤15, a moderate ISS (16–24) was associated with a higher likelihood of delayed WLST (OR 1.2, 95% CI 1.0 to 1.3, p=0.013), while a severe ISS (≥25) was associated with lower odds of delayed WLST (OR 0.92, 95% CI 0.82 to 1.0, p=0.167). Moderate or severe shock (SI >1.0) was a significant negative predictor of late WLST (OR 0.86, 95% CI 0.77 to 0.96, p=0.008). Major pelvic/extremity injury (AIS pelvis/extremity ≥3) was a significant negative predictor of late WLST (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.72 to 0.95, p=0.008). Major abdominal (OR 0.85, 95% CI 0.62 to 1.2, p=0.302) and chest (OR 1.1, 95% CI 0.96 to 1.2, p=0.17) injuries did not have any significant association with time to WLST in patients without msTBI.

Institutional characteristics

After adjusting for patient and injury characteristics, admission to an academic/university hospital was associated with higher odds of delayed WLST when compared with patients at community and non-teaching hospitals (OR 1.3, 95% CI 1.1 to 1.4, p<0.001). Like patients with msTBI, there were no significant associations between interfacility transfer, bed size, ACS trauma verification level, or hospital type (for-profit, non-profit, government) and time to WLST in patients without msTBI (table 2).

Discussion

Our findings support our hypothesis that time to WLST differs significantly between trauma patients with and without msTBI. This is consistent with prior work demonstrating earlier WLST among patients with acute neurological injuries (eg, TBI, stroke, seizure, meningitis-encephalitis), compared with other critically ill patients.24 In our cohort, baseline demographics and injury characteristics also differed substantially between groups. Patients with msTBI were younger, had fewer pre-existing medical conditions, and sustained more severe injuries, reflected by higher median ISS.

Together, these differences suggest that the shorter time to WLST in polytrauma patients with msTBI may be driven in part by systematic differences in patient profiles that complicate prognostication and confound clinical decision-making, thereby distinguishing this patient population from critically injured polytrauma patients with msTBI.

In patients with msTBI, the effect of major extracranial injury on outcomes and WLST decisions can be difficult to discern. Our findings demonstrated that in patients with msTBI, only severe injury to the chest was associated with delayed WLST (OR 1.3, 95% CI 1.1 to 1.4, p<0.001), while no other major extracranial injury had predictive value. This aligns with results from a nuanced meta-analysis by van Leeuwen et al which showed that major extracranial injury may be a more relevant prognostic factor in the emergency department and immediately after injury; and that the prognostic value of major extracranial injury is greatly reduced in patients who survive their immediate injuries and are able to survive to admission to the ICU.25

Therefore, while polytrauma patients with msTBI should be considered a distinct patient population (ie, separate from polytrauma without msTBI and isolated msTBI), outcomes and WLST decisions among these patients who survive to ICU admission may be predominantly influenced by the factors associated with neurological injury.

Unique predictors of delayed WLST in patients with msTBI

After adjustment for injury severity and institutional factors, ICP monitoring was associated with higher odds of delayed WLST. This finding may in part reflect differences in ICP management associated with the use of ICP monitoring and subsequent differences in duration of care. Prior work has demonstrated higher therapy intensity levels (TIL) in patients with ICP monitoring compared with those without (median TIL 9 (7–12) vs. 5 (3–8), p<0.0001).26 However, complications related to ICP monitoring itself, its indications, and the complexity of ICP management in polytrauma patients are important confounding variables that we were unable to account for in this study. More research is needed to better understand the threshold of futility for ICP monitoring in patients with msTBI and uncertain prognosis.

Among injury patterns, the presence of diffuse axonal injury (DAI) was the most strongly associated with delayed WLST. This may reflect the greater prognostic uncertainty in patients with DAI when compared with patients with isolated intracranial hemorrhage or penetrating head injuries.27 In contrast, penetrating head injuries, subarachnoid hemorrhage, and midline shift were associated with earlier WLST, consistent with unfavorable outcomes in many of these patients.26

The GCS motor score has demonstrated high prognostic value in isolated sTBI (total GCS score <8),28 but it was not a significant predictor of WLST timing in our cohort of polytrauma patients with msTBI. We therefore examined the IMPACT prognostic model, which incorporates age, initial GCS motor score, and pupillary response to estimate the probability of an unfavorable outcome at 6 months.22 This is a particularly relevant tool, as it has been shown to positively facilitate communication with families regarding prognosis and decisions to pursue or withhold aggressive care.29 In our cohort, higher predicted probabilities of unfavorable outcome were associated with earlier WLST, supporting prior research demonstrating the model’s role in guiding treatment.29

In-hospital complications

In-hospital complications were strong predictors of delayed WLST, with major infections demonstrating the greatest effect. We postulate that treatable complications may be perceived as reversible and temporary, prompting continued escalation of care and postponement of WLST decisions until after these conditions resolve. A previous study of trauma patients showed that the first GOC discussion was frequently triggered by the development of an infection, and that multiple infections increased the time to death or discharge.30 This led us to quantify the marginal effect of each additional complication on the overall probability of delayed WLST (figure 5). As the total number of in-hospital complications increased, the predicted probability increased non-linearly from 18% to nearly 100%, with a plateau at seven complications. Notably, the first four complications had the greatest effect on increasing the probability of delayed WLST.

Marginal effect of in-hospital complications on the predicted probability of delayed withdrawal of life-sustaining treatment (WLST) in critically injured trauma patients.

We acknowledge that aggregating in-hospital complications without weighing them by severity limits relevant granularity and obscures clinically meaningful distinctions. However, stratifying complications by severity or complexity can introduce subjective bias, as classification of ‘minor’ versus ‘major’ complications is often inconsistent and may inadvertently downplay the cumulative burden of minor events or overemphasize individual severe complications. By analyzing total complications, we aimed to assess the impact of overall complication burden to identify points at which revisiting GOC discussions may be beneficial.

While treating acute complications may allow time for improved prognostic clarity, it may also inadvertently prolong non-beneficial care in patients with poor or uncertain trajectories, increasing patient suffering, family distress, and ICU resource utilization. Using actionable complications or escalating therapy intensity as prompts for GOC reassessment may help mitigate delayed WLST, though this approach warrants prospective validation.

Disparities

After adjustment for patient, injury, and institutional factors, uninsured patients were more likely to undergo earlier WLST when compared with insured patients, consistent with prior reports of insurance-based disparities in WLST decisions.31 32

Black patients, patients of other races, and patients of Hispanic/Latino ethnicity were more likely to undergo delayed WLST compared with white and non-Hispanic patients. These findings align with a prior 2013–2016 TQIP study showing later WLST among black and Hispanic patients.33 Other studies have demonstrated lower overall rates of WLST in these populations.34 35 Taken together, black and Hispanic patients may be less likely to choose WLST, and in the cases when WLST is pursued, it is often delayed.

These results reiterate findings of persistent racial and ethnic differences in end-of-life decision-making and emphasize the need for institutional and qualitative research to better understand how cultural, social, and structural factors shape WLST decisions.

Limitations

Our study has several important limitations. First, the retrospective design precludes determination of causality, and this large database study was inherently limited by the accuracy and availability of the data provided. The initial univariate analysis may have found statistical significance in variables as an effect of the large sample size, and not necessarily due to clinical significance. This was mitigated by selecting covariates that have previously been shown to be associated with WLST decisions or were determined by the authors to have clinical plausibility in influencing WLST decision-making. However, one example of this may be our finding that major chest but not abdominal injuries were found to be predictive of delayed WLST. Additionally, our selection of covariates was necessarily restricted and biased toward the variables included in the TQIP database. Future institutional studies are needed for optimal variable selection to account for the many nuances in WLST decision-making that were not captured in this study. For example, we were unable to determine the proportion of patients who died before a GOC discussion could occur and to capture instances in which discussions were attempted but declined by families. As such, this study does not reflect efforts to initiate GOC discussions. Inclusion of additional WLST decision-making variables in the TQIP database may aid in future quality improvement benchmarking.

Notably, the ACS-NTDS Data Dictionary definition of WLST that determined who underwent WLST in this study combines both patients who underwent withdrawal of an intervention as well as patients in whom the decision was made to withhold an intervention. This broad definition captures scenarios such as non-escalation of care, refusal of an operative intervention in favor of a non-operative alternative, or a do-not-intubate directive. This is a subtle, yet likely consequential limitation as we were unable to capture important nuances in these qualitatively distinct clinical decisions. It has long been acknowledged that withdrawing and withholding life-sustaining treatment are qualitatively distinct aspects of end-of-life care and decision-making.36 We propose that these two options may be conceptualized as existing along a continuum of end-of-life decision-making, within which movement between decisions may occur. Anecdotally, we have observed that decisions to withhold escalation of care often precede subsequent decisions to withdraw life-sustaining treatment. Thus, we speculate that the timing to WLST reflected in this study may be shorter than it would be had our operational definition of WLST excluded patients in whom withholding care was pursued.

Lastly, the decision to withdraw life-sustaining treatment is usually final, which makes interpreting WLST outcome data challenging. This study is reflecting WLST decisions made under uncertainty, without capturing what may have happened, had a different decision been made. This study does not provide any insight into how much additional time contributes to improved prognostic certainty. Conversely, while certain factors may be associated with earlier WLST decisions, it is possible that had these decisions been delayed, the patient’s clinical course may have evolved more favorably. Therefore, this study was designed to describe current patterns in WLST decisions and to highlight areas for future research, and more nuanced research is needed to determine the optimal timing of WLST in this patient population.

Conclusion

WLST occurs earlier in polytrauma patients with msTBI compared with those without msTBI, reflecting both differing thresholds for considering WLST and systematic differences in demographic and clinical profiles that confound decision-making. Among patients with msTBI, only severe chest injury was associated with delayed WLST, suggesting that factors associated with neurological injury predominate in determining WLST decisions among polytrauma patients who survive to ICU admission. In this population, the IMPACT prognostic model may have utility in informing GOC discussions.

Complications and interventions were strong predictors of delayed WLST. Using actionable complications and changes in therapy intensity as triggers for GOC reassessment may help prevent prolonged delays in WLST. In some cases, continued treatment of acute complications may become increasingly futile as the burden of complications accumulates.

The TQIP database lacks key patient-centered, social, and cultural variables necessary for a complete understanding of WLST decision-making. Future institutional studies incorporating both quantitative and qualitative data are needed to better define evidence-based approaches to GOC discussions and WLST decision-making in the trauma ICU.
