A trypanosome-specific complex mediates late-stage processing of cytosolic LSU rRNA
Authors: Simona Amodeo, Mauro Fischli, Silke Oeljeklaus, Salvatore Calderaro, Bettina Warscheid, André Schneider
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnlike most eukaryotes, Trypanosoma brucei processes its cytosolic large subunit (LSU) RNA into six fragments. The factors responsible for these processing events were previously unknown. Here, we identify TbLrRP1 and TbLrRP2 as essential components of this trypanosome-specific LSU RNA processing pathway. Each contains a single transmembrane domain and localizes to the ER membrane and the nuclear envelope, forming a heterodimeric complex. Depletion of either protein disrupts LSU RNA processing, causing accumulation of unprocessed intermediates that are incorporated into translationally active cytosolic polysomes. This disruption impairs, but does not fully halt, growth of both procyclic and bloodstream form trypanosomes. The nuclease-related domain (NERD) of TbLrRP2 is essential for LSU RNA processing. Intriguingly, NERD-containing proteins were predicted to have DNase activity; however, our results suggest that the NERD of TbLrRP2 is a ribonuclease. In contrast, the DEDDh domain of TbLrRP1 is dispensable for LSU RNA processing, indicating it may have a scaffolding role. Both proteins require their transmembrane domains for full functionality. Our findings reveal a lineage-specific processing complex acting at a late stage of LSU RNA maturation, highlighting the unique adaptations of trypanosomal cytosolic ribosome biogenesis.

Introduction

Eukaryotic ribosomes consist of the 40S small subunit (SSU), containing 30 proteins and the 18S rRNA, and the 60S large subunit (LSU), containing >40 proteins, as well as the 5S, 5.8S, and the 25/28S rRNAs. Ribosome biogenesis is complex and energy-consuming; it starts in the nucleolus and ends in the cytoplasm. The process has best been studied in the yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae [1–4]. It requires the combined action of dozens of assembly proteins and rRNA processing factors including endonucleases, exonucleases, helicases, and small nucleolar RNAs (snoRNAs).

Excluding the transcription of 5S rRNA gene by RNA polymerase III, ribosome biogenesis starts with RNA polymerase I-mediated transcription of the rDNA locus in the nucleolus [5]. The resulting pre-rRNA precursor contains the 5′ and 3′ externally transcribed spacers (ETSs) and the two internal transcribed spacers (ITS1 and ITS2) that separate the 18S from the 5.8S and the 25/28S rRNA genes, respectively [6]. The pre-rRNA precursor must undergo several endonucleolytic cleavages and trimming events to form the mature rRNAs [7, 8]. The order and timing, as well as the enzymes catalyzing these events, differ between different species [6].

In yeast, processing of the pre-rRNA precursor starts by a co-transcriptional cleavage in the 3′ ETS by the endonuclease Rnt1 [9, 10]. The resulting 90S particle is further processed in the nucleolus by endonucleolytic cleavages in the 5′ ETS and the ITS1 by the endonuclease Rcl1. This leads to the release of the pre-40S subunit containing the 18S rRNA precursor and the pre-60S subunit containing the precursors of the 5.8S and 25S/28S rRNAs [11].

While the mature 5′ and 3′ ends of the 5.8S and the 25S/28S rRNAs are generated by trimming through exonucleases in the nucleus, the mature 18S rRNA in yeast is generated in the cytosol by Nob1 through endonucleolytic cleavage [3, 12–14]. Maturation of the LSU rRNA precursors is more conserved between species than the upstream processing events. In yeast, completion of 3′ ETS processing mediated by the 3′–5′ exonuclease Rex1 starts in the nucleolus and ends in the nucleoplasm [15, 16].

ITS1 of the LSU rRNA is endonucleolytically cleaved by RNase MRP and further processed in a U3 snoRNA-dependent manner by Rcl1 in the nucleolus [1, 6, 17, 18]. Endonucleolytic cleavage and subsequent trimming of the ITS2, which separates the 5.8S and 25S LSU rRNA precursors [19–21], is mediated by Las1 and Rat1 (alias Xrn2), as well as Rrp17, respectively. These reactions take place in the nucleus and form the mature 25S LSU rRNA.

The 3′-end of the 5.8S LSU rRNA precursor is gradually trimmed by the exosome in the nucleus [22], whereas the final processing step is mediated by cytoplasmic Ngl2 in yeast (ERI1 in mammals) [23, 24].

The present study focuses on the biogenesis of cytosolic ribosomes of the parasitic protozoan Trypanosoma brucei. While in general the rRNA processing events observed in other eukaryotes are conserved in T. brucei, it is unclear to what extent they are carried out by similar pathways. The fact that trypanosomal orthologues of many rRNA processing factors such as Rcl1, Rex1, Las1, and Rat1 have been found and shown to be localized in the nuclear compartment suggests that at least some steps are conserved. However, only for the trypanosomal orthologue of Nob1, which is involved in the maturation of the 18S SSU rRNA, has this directly been shown [25, 26]. Moreover, trypanosomal orthologues of other rRNA processing factors such as Rnt1, RNase MRP, Rrp17, and Ngl2 could not be identified.

Instead, two trypanosomatid-specific nucleases appear to be involved in rRNA processing. One is the exosome subunit TbRRP44 (also termed Dis3). It appears to be involved in 5.8S rRNA 3′ end maturation, degradation of the excised 5′ ETS, and removal of ITS1 from the 3′ end of the 18S SSU rRNA precursor [27–29]. In addition, depletion of TbRRP44 results in a slight accumulation of many LSU processing intermediates, possibly because the protein is required for maturation of snoRNAs [29]. The second one is a highly divergent XRN-type 5′→3′ exoribonuclease (XRNE), the depletion of which causes accumulation of aberrant processing intermediates of 18S SSU and 5.8S rRNAs retaining their 5′ ETS or ITS1, respectively [30].

Besides these nucleases, several other proteins were shown to be involved in the maturation of the trypanosomal pre-rRNA precursor. Depletion of subunits of the 5S ribonucleoparticle (RNP) causes, possibly indirectly, retention of ITS2 and to a lesser extent of the trypanosomatid-specific ITS3 [31–35].

Moreover, RNAi of the following trypanosomatid-specific proteins results in retention of ITS1 (Puf7), retention of ITS2 (Nop44 and Nop46), and a processing defect of ITS1 and ITS2 (Hel66) [36–38]. Finally, depletion of either TbNog1 or TbNop2, both of which are conserved within eukaryotes, results in retention of ITS2 [39] and a general processing defect of the pre-rRNA precursor [40], respectively.

A special feature of trypanosomes and their relatives is that their LSU rRNA is further fragmented into 6 distinct fragments. In T. brucei, this happens by excision of ITS3 to ITS7 [26]. The resulting LSU rRNA fragments present in the mature trypanosomal ribosome are termed LSUα (1863 nt) and LSUβ (1544 nt) for the two large fragments and small rRNA1 (SR1), SR2, SR6, and SR4 (215, 182, 78, and 136 nt) for the smaller fragments [41–44]. The physiological relevance of these trypanosomatid-specific rRNA processing events and the factors that mediate them are presently unknown.

In the present study, we discovered two trypanosomal proteins, TbLrRP1 and TbLrRP2, which are involved in LSU rRNA fragmentation. The two proteins have conserved but different (ribo)nuclease-like domains and likely form a heterodimer. Both have a single transmembrane domain (TMD) each and localize to the ER and the nuclear envelope. Intriguingly, depletion or deletion of the two proteins only slows down but does not stop growth or polysome formation in either insect stage or bloodstream forms of the parasite. This suggests that the partially unprocessed rRNA precursor can assemble into functional ribosomes.

Materials and methods

Cell lines

Procyclic form cell lines used in this study were cultured in SDM-79 supplemented with 5% (v/v) fetal calf serum (FCS) and derived from the previously generated single marker T. brucei 427 cell line [45, 46]. The bloodstream form cell lines derive from a 427 single marker cell line expressing T7 polymerase and the tetracycline repressor and were grown in HMI-9 supplemented with 10% FCS [47, 48].

The TbLrRP1 RNAi procyclic form as well as bloodstream form cell lines express a tetracycline-inducible hairpin loop targeting the ORF of TbLrRP1 (nucleotides 345–757 of Tb927.3.5130). The TbLrRP1 double knockout (dKO) and conditional dKO cell lines were produced by replacement of the two TbLrRP1 alleles by either a hygromycin or a blasticidin resistance gene, each containing the last 527 nucleotides of the TbLrRP1 5′ UTR and the first 523 nucleotides of the TbLrRP1 3′ UTR, respectively. For the bloodstream form conditional dKO cell line, we inserted the tubulin 3′ UTR between the blasticidin ORF and the TbLrRP1 3′ UTR. Tetracycline-inducible ectopic expression of C-terminally triple HA-tagged TbLrRP1 was achieved using the modified pLew100 expression vector [47]. For C-terminal triple-HA in situ tagging of TbLrRP1, we used the pMO tagging approach as described [49]. For ectopic expression of TbLrRP1-HA, HA-TbLrRP1, and its mutants (TbLrRP1-dead-HA and TbLrRP1-ΔTMD-HA) in procyclic forms, we used the same modified pLew100 expression vector as described above.

The procyclic form and bloodstream form TbLrRP2 RNAi lines express a tetracycline-inducible hairpin loop targeting the ORF of TbLrRP2 (nucleotides 94–610 of Tb927.10.9200). For C-terminal in situ tagging of TbLrRP2 with a triple-c-MYC tag, we used the pMO tagging approach as above [49]. For ectopic expression of C-terminally triple MYC-tagged wild-type TbLrRP2, as well as TbLrRP2 mutants (TbLrRP2-dead-MYC and ΔTMD-TbLrRP2-MYC), we used the same approach as described for TbLrRP1.

Total RNA extraction and northern blot analysis

Total RNA was isolated by acid guanidinium thiocyanate-phenol-chloroform extraction [50]. The isolated RNA was then separated on a 1.5% agarose gel in 20 mM MOPS buffer, pH 7.0 containing 0.5% formaldehyde. For northern blot analysis, the RNA was blotted onto a nylon membrane by capillary transfer. For probing of the TbLrRP1 and TbLrRP2 mRNAs, to verify the RNAi efficiency, the probes were prepared from gel-purified PCR products corresponding to the target regions of the two RNAi cell lines. The radioactive labelling of the probes with 32P-labeled α-dATP was done using the Klenow fragment (Promega; Prime-a-Gene Labeling System; #U1100), and the probing of the membranes was performed as described [51].

For analysis of the various LSU rRNA processing intermediates, we labelled the probes with 32P-labeled γ−ATP using T4 polynucleotide kinase (NEB; #M0201S). Hybridization of the nylon membranes was done as described [51]. Sequences of the probes were as follows:

Polysome profiling

1–3 × 109 of procyclic form cells were grown on a shaker (120 rpm) to a maximal density of 2 × 107 cells/ml, incubated with cycloheximide (100 µg/ml) for 15 min while shaking, and finally harvested. Subsequently, the cells were washed in PBS containing cycloheximide (100 µg/ml) and resuspended in 500 μl of ribosome buffer A (20 mM Tris/HCl, pH 7.6, 120 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)) supplemented with 2.5 µl of RNasin Plus RNase Inhibitor 40 U/µl (Promega, #N2611) and cycloheximide (100 µg/ml). Cell extract preparation for polysome profiling and polysome profiling were performed as described [55]. For RNA extraction, the selected fractions were pooled and precipitated with EtOH and GlycoBlue coprecipitant (Invitrogen, #AM9515). RNA was isolated from the resulting pellets as described above. The RNA fractions of the individual polysome peaks were separated on a 1.5% agarose gel.

Fluorescence in situ hybridization

The protocol for fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) [56] was adapted for T. brucei as follows: 106 of exponentially growing cells of procyclic form cells were harvested, washed in PBS, spread on a slide, and let to settle for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min in a humidity chamber. Cells were then permeabilized in 70% EtOH at 4°C overnight or longer. Next, the cells were rehydrated twice for 5 min each in 2× SSC containing 10% formamide. 1.25 µl of the corresponding probe (10 ng/µl) was mixed with 4.375 µl water, 2.5 µl formamide, 1.25 µl 2× SSC, and 1.25 µl tRNA (10 mg/ml, Sigma, #R-1753) and denatured for 5 min at 95°C. Then, 12.5 µl 20% (w/v) dextran sulfate in 4× SSC, 0.625 µl BSA (10 mg/ml), and 1.25 µl ribonucleoside vanadyl complex (200 mM, NEB, #S140S) were added, mixed, and the probe was pipetted on the slide, covered by a cover slip, and incubated at 37°C for 4–5 h. After probing, the coverslip was removed, cells were rinsed twice in 2× SSC containing 10% formamide at 37°C for 10 min, and rinsed twice in PBS. Finally, cells were mounted in ProLong Gold antifade mountant with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Invitrogen, #P36941) and covered with a coverslip. Digitonin and alkaline carbonate extraction

To analyze the subcellular localization of TbLrRP1 and TbLrRP2, we performed extraction with a final digitonin concentration of 0.015% (w/v). The cells were washed in PBS and then resuspended in SoTE buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 0.6 M sorbitol, 2 mM EDTA) to a concentration of 107 cells/50 µl. Then, an equal volume of SoTE containing 0.03% (w/v) digitonin was added, and the reaction was incubated at 4°C for 5–10 min. The soluble fraction, largely corresponding to the cytosol, and the pellet fraction, corresponding to the cytoskeleton and organelles, were separated by centrifugation at 8000 rpm for 5 min at 4°C. To determine whether TbLrRP1 and TbLrRP2 are integral membrane proteins, we performed alkaline carbonate extraction of the organellar fraction as previously described [57].

Conventional and confocal immunofluorescence microscopy

Cells were processed as described [45] except that the secondary fluorescent antibodies were diluted 1:1000 in PBS containing 2% (w/v) bovine serum albumin. The samples were mounted in ProLong Gold antifade mountant with DAPI (Invitrogen, #P36941). Conventional immunofluorescence Z-stack images were acquired as described before [45]. Images for confocal microscopy were acquired as described previously [58] with additional use of the optical channel SD 432. Confocal images were deconvoluted with the Huygens Professional software. Images were analyzed with ImageJ.

Proteomic analysis of TbLrRP1 co-immunoprecipitation

Procyclic TbLrRP1-HA-expressing TbLrRP1 dKO cells were induced for 24 h. Stable isotope labeling of amino acids in cell culture (SILAC) CoIP (co-immunoprecipitation) was performed as described [59]. Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) and data analysis were performed as follows: Eluates obtained in TbLrRP1-HA SILAC-CoIP experiments (n = 4) were processed as previously described [60, 61] and analyzed by LC–MS using an UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano HPLC system coupled to a Q Exactive Plus LC–MS mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Germany) as described before [62]. Protein identification and SILAC-based relative quantification were performed using MaxQuant/Andromeda (version 2.4.4.0; [63]) and a fasta file containing the protein sequences for T. brucei TREU927 as provided by the TriTrypDB (https://tritrypdb.org; version 55). Proteins were identified and quantified based on at least one unique peptide and at least one ratio count, respectively. For all other parameters, MaxQuant default settings were used, including carbamidomethylation of cysteine as a fixed modification and N-terminal acetylation and oxidation of methionine as variable modifications. Lys8 and Arg10 were selected as heavy labels, and the “requantify” option was enabled.

Significantly enriched proteins were determined using the rank sum method [64, 65] implemented in the R package “RankProd” [66]. The rank sum, calculated as the arithmetic mean of a protein’s ranks across all replicates, was converted into an FDR-controlled q-value. For details on protein identification and quantification, refer to Supplementary Table S1.

Miscellaneous

Protease protection assays of crude organellar fractions (5 × 107 cells each) to determine the topology of TbLrRP1 and TbLrRP2 were done as described [51]. The heterodimer protein structure of TbLrRP1 (Tb927.3.5130) and TbLrRP2 (Tb927.10.9200) was predicted using the AlphaFold 3 model [68]. Predictions were made with the settings as [69]. Results

Tb927.3.5130 is essential for trypanosome-specific LSU rRNA processing

Tb927.3.5130 is a 44-kDa protein with a predicted DEDDh 3′–5′ exonuclease domain and a C-terminal TMD (Fig. 1A). RNAi-mediated depletion of Tb927.3.5130, while not interfering with growth of procyclic T. brucei, caused an essentially complete loss of the LSU rRNA fragment LSUβ (Fig. 1B and C). Similarly, the levels of the SR2, SR4, and SR6 rRNA fragments were reduced. However, the levels of the LSUα rRNA fragment, SSU 18S rRNA, 5S rRNA, 5.8S rRNA (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. S1A), and the SR1 rRNA fragment (Supplementary Fig. S1A) were not affected. Because of these phenotypes, we called the protein large subunit rRNA processing 1 (TbLrRP1). The absence of growth retardation after such a severe LSU rRNA processing defect was surprising. Thus, to completely remove TbLrRP1 rather than just deplete it by RNAi, we produced a procyclic TbLrRP1 dKO cell line (Fig. 1D). In these cells the LSUβ fragment was not detectable anymore, and growth was slightly slower than in wild-type cells (Fig. 1E). Intriguingly, essentially the same slow growth phenotypes and depletion of LSUβ were observed in TbLrRP1 RNAi and TbLrRP1 dKO bloodstream form cell lines of trypanosomes (Fig. 1F and G), which have a shorter generation time. Only in this case moderate growth inhibition was already observed in the RNAi cell line.

TbLrRP1 loss causes retention of ITS5, 6, and 7 in the LSU rRNA

In T. brucei, the 9.6 kb pre-rRNA encodes the 18S SSU rRNA, the 5.8S rRNA, and the trypanosomatid-specific LSU rRNA fragments (LSUα, SR1, LSUβ, SR2, SR6, and SR4) (Fig. 2A). Previous research has shown that three processing intermediates (5.8, 5.1, and 3.9 kb) are readily detectable in wild-type cells (Fig. 2A and B). The 5.8 kb intermediate generated by ITS1 processing corresponds to the LSU pre-rRNA containing the 5.8S rRNA and all trypanosomatid-specific LSU rRNA fragments. Further processing of ITS2 removes the 5.8S rRNA, creating a 5.1 kb intermediate containing the trypanosomatid-specific LSU rRNA fragments only. The 3.9 kb processing intermediate, finally, encodes the LSUα, ITS3, SR1, ITS4, and the LSUβ fragments. Note that, unlike reported in previous publications [38], the 3.9 kb intermediate was not detected in our analysis (Figs 2B and 3B).

To more precisely characterize the LSU rRNA processing defect TbLrRP1 depletion causes, we performed an extensive northern analysis. Total RNA of the uninduced and induced (day five) procyclic TbLrRP1-RNAi cell line was hybridized with probes specific for rRNA fragments LSUα, SR1, LSUβ, SR2, SR6, and SR4, as well as for the internal transcribed spacers ITS5, ITS6, and ITS7 (Fig. 3).

The results in Fig. 3B confirmed the loss of LSUβ, SR2, SR6, and SR4 (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Fig. S1A). In addition, we observed the accumulation of processing intermediates that are not seen in wild-type cells (Figs 2C and 3). The most abundant ones are the 2.2a fragment (comprising ITS4, LSUβ, ITS5, SR2, and ITS6) and the 0.5b fragment (comprising SR6, ITS7, and SR4). Furthermore, less extensive accumulation of the 2.7, the 1.8, and the 0.5a fragments was also observed (Figs 2C and 3). The observed phenotypes are consistent with a severely reduced processing of one or several of the internal transcribed spacers, ITS5, ITS6, and ITS7, indicating that TbLrRP1 is implicated in these trypanosome-specific processing events (Fig. 2C, red arrows; and Fig. 3).

Cytosolic ribosomes in TbLrRP1-depleted cells contain rRNA processing intermediates

To get a global view of SSU and LSU ribosome biogenesis and to estimate the translation capacity in both wild-type and TbLrRP1 dKO cells, we determined their polysome profiles. Intriguingly, these profiles looked very similar (Fig. 4A), and quantification of the means of the absorption maxima of the various peaks in triplicate experiments were not significantly different (Fig. 4B). Next, we extracted RNA from all the peaks of the polysome profile. Gel analysis of the RNA fractions from the TbLrRP1 dKO cells revealed ethidium bromide-stained bands corresponding to the 2.7, 2.2a, 0.5a, and 0.5b processing intermediates (Fig. 3) in the 60S and 80S as well as the peaks of the polysome (dis. and tris.) (Fig. 4C). Moreover, the band corresponding to processed LSUβ was absent as expected. These results show that the LSU rRNA processing intermediates that accumulate in the absence of TbLrRP1 were integrated into what appear to be translationally active ribosomes and polysomes, even though they lack the mature LSUβ fragment.

These results were corroborated on the cellular level by FISH analyses using the probes specifically detecting ITS1, ITS5, ITS6, and ITS7 (Fig. 4D). ITS1, which is only present in the 9.6 kb pre-rRNA found in the nucleolus, served as a control. It showed the same nucleolar localization in wild-type as well as TbLrRP1 dKO cells (Fig. 4E, top row). However, the nucleolar localization of ITS5, ITS6, and ITS7 observed in wild-type cells shifted to the cytoplasm in TbLrRP1 dKO cells (Fig. 4E). As expected, all LSU rRNA fragments (LSUα, SR1, LSUβ, SR2, SR6, and SR4) were enriched in the cytosol in both cell lines (Supplementary Fig. S2A and B).

In summary, these results show that the LSU processing intermediates that accumulate in the absence of TbLrRP1 were integrated into what appear to be translationally active cytosolic ribosomes and polysomes, which lack the mature LSUβ fragment. These findings are in line with the only marginal growth retardation that is observed in TbLrRP1-depleted RNAi or TbLrRP1 dKO cells.

TbLrRP1 is an integral ER and nuclear envelope protein

Unexpectedly for a putative exonuclease, TbLrRP1 has a predicted single TMD at its C-terminus. We therefore determined the localization of TbLrRP1 using biochemical methods. Three different cell lines expressing HA-tagged TbLrRP1 versions were analyzed. In the first one, TbLrRP1 was C-terminally tagged and expressed in situ. In addition, N- and C-terminally tagged TbLrRP1 were also expressed ectopically. All three cell lines were extracted with low concentrations of digitonin to produce crude organellar pellets (containing ER and mitochondria) and supernatant fractions corresponding to the cytosol, respectively. Tagged TbLrRP1, as well as the integral mitochondrial membrane protein ATOM40, was exclusively recovered in the pellet in all three cell lines (Fig. 5A, top panels). In subsequent alkaline carbonate fractionations of the crude organellar fractions, TbLrRP1 continued to co-fractionate with the ATOM40, strongly suggesting that, in line with its predicted TMD, it is indeed an integral membrane protein (Fig. 5A, lower panels).

To determine the topology of TbLrRP1, we used protease protection assays. Crude organellar fractions from cell lines ectopically expressing N- and C-terminally HA-tagged TbLrRP1 were incubated with proteinase K (Fig. 5B). The results showed that, whereas C-terminally HA-tagged TbLrRP1 was largely resistant to proteolytic degradation, the HA-tag of the N-terminally tagged TbLrRP1 was efficiently degraded. Together with the controls, the cytosol-facing outer mitochondrial membrane ATOM69, which gets efficiently degraded, and the ER lumen marker BiP, which is protease-resistant, these results indicate that the N-terminus of TbLrRP1 faces the cytosol.

Finally, to determine to which membrane TbLrRP1 localizes, we used confocal immunofluorescence analyses (Fig. 5C). In these experiments BiP and NRG-1 served as ER and nucleolar markers, respectively. The staining of ectopically expressed C-terminally HA-tagged TbLrRP1 largely overlapped with BiP, although TbLrRP1 appeared to be more enriched in the nuclear envelope than BiP. Intriguingly, this localization differs from the one reported in the TrypTag database (https://tryptag.org/), where the protein localizes to the entire ER. Import of integral inner nuclear envelope proteins is not well understood in any system. It is therefore possible that the GFP (27 kDa) used in the Tryptag database, which is approximately eight-fold larger than the triple HA-tag (3.3 kDa), blocks this transport. No co-localization with the nuclear marker NRG-1 was observed. Moreover, N-terminally tagged TbLrRP1 showed an essentially identical localization.

We conclude that TbLrRP1 is an integral ER protein with a C-terminal TMD that is most enriched in the nuclear envelope. The large soluble domain of the protein is cytosolically exposed when present in the ER. When present in the nuclear envelope, it is either exposed to the cytosol or to the nucleoplasm or to both. This is a rather unexpected localization for an rRNA processing factor. It suggests that TbLrRP1 functions at a late stage of ribosome biogenesis after or during nuclear export of the pre-60S subunit.

The putative exonuclease motifs are dispensable for TbLrRP1 function

TbLrRP1 is a member of the large protein subfamily of DEDDh 3′–5′ exonucleases, which contain three conserved exonuclease motifs that are essential for catalytic activity (Fig. 6A). To test whether these motifs are essential for trypanosome-specific LSU rRNA processing, we produced a putative catalytic dead version of TbLrRP1 in which the conserved residues in all three motifs were replaced by alanines (Fig. 6B). Subsequently, both wild-type and the catalytic dead version of TbLrRP1 were expressed under tetracycline control in the background of the TbLrRP1 dKO cell line. Surprisingly, the addition of tetracycline resulted in full complementation of the observed rRNA processing defects irrespective of whether the wild-type or the catalytic dead version of TbLrRP1 was expressed (Fig. 6C and D).

The three exonuclease motifs are easily identified in TbLrRP1. Nevertheless, we have not directly shown that the wild-type version of the TbLrRP1 indeed has catalytic activity. Thus, the fact that the engineered catalytic dead version of the protein behaves like its wild-type counterpart indicates that the function of TbLrRP1 in LSU rRNA processing cannot be connected to a putative exonuclease activity of the protein.

TbLrRP1 likely forms a heterodimer with TbLrRP2

LSU rRNA processing is predicted to require endo- and exonucleases. Yet TbLrRP1 that lacks catalytic activity is still functional. This suggests that TbLrRP1 functions together with one or several partner proteins with which it may form a complex. To test this, we performed SILAC-based CoIPs using the TbLrRP1 dKO cell line, allowing tetracycline-inducible expression of HA-tagged wild-type TbLrRP1 (Fig. 6). Uninduced and induced cells were grown in the presence of isotopically labeled heavy or light lysine and arginine. Subsequently, identical cell numbers from both populations were mixed, and detergent-soluble supernatants of crude organellar extracts were prepared from quadruplicate experiments and subjected to IPs using anti-HA antibodies.

The resulting eluates were analyzed by quantitative MS, and the results (Fig. 7A and Supplementary Table S1) showed that TbLrRP1-HA was 5-fold enriched when compared to the negative control. Besides the bait, only four proteins were detected that were significantly enriched more than two-fold. They include: (i) the mitochondrial RNA-editing uridylyl transferase, (ii) Tb927.7.6220, a putative cytosolic kinase, (iii) Tb927.5.780, a conserved protein of unknown function dually localized in the nucleus and the cytoplasm, and (iv) Tb927.10.9200, a nuclease-related domain (NERD)-containing protein (Fig. 7A).

(A) Visualization of proteins detected in a quantitative mass spectrometry analysis of co-immunoprecipitation experiments (n = 4) using TbLrRP1-HA expressed in the TbLrRP1 dKO cell line as bait. The vertical dotted line indicates a two-fold enrichment. The horizontal dotted line indicates a corrected P-value of .05. (B) TbLrRP1-TbLrRP2 heterodimer prediction by AlphaFold 3.0 (ipTM = 0.62, pTM = 0.66).

We focused on the 48 kDa Tb927.10.9200, which we call TbLrRP2, for further analysis because it has a nuclease-related domain and, similar to TbLrRP1, a predicted TMD at its N-terminus. More importantly, AlphaFold3 predicts with high confidence (ipTM = 0.62, pTM = 0.66) that TbLrRP2 and TbLrRP1 form a heterodimer (Fig. 7B). The fact that the structure perfectly aligns the predicted TMDs of the two proteins adds further confidence to the prediction. Moreover, the same type of AlphaFold3 analysis with any of the other three putative TbLrRP1 interactors yields ipTM predictions well below 0.5, suggesting that the two proteins do not interact with each other.

TbLrRP2 is an integral ER membrane and nuclear envelope protein

To confirm that TbLrRP2, as predicted, is N-terminally anchored in the membrane (Supplementary Fig. S3A), we performed the same type of analyses that were done for TbLrRP1 (Fig. 5). Extractions at low digitonin concentrations followed by alkaline carbonate extractions indicated that C-terminally myc-tagged TbLrRP2 is an integral membrane protein (Supplementary Fig. S3B), whose localization in immunofluorescence analyses largely overlapped with the ER marker BiP that also stained the nuclear envelope (Supplementary Fig. S3C). Furthermore, in line with the AlphaFold 3.0 structure predictions of the TbLrRP1/TbLrRP2 heterodimer, a protease protection assay established that C-terminal myc-tag of TbLrRP2 is exposed to the cytosol (Supplementary Fig. S3D). These localizations were further corroborated by a recent spatial proteomics study in which the profiles of both TbLrRP1 and TbLrRP2 were identical to the ones produced by ER markers [70] (Supplementary Fig. S3E).

TbLrRP2 is essential for growth and LSU rRNA processing

To study the function of TbLrRP2, we produced tetracycline-inducible RNAi cell lines for both procyclic and bloodstream forms of T. brucei. The results in Fig. 8 showed that depletion of TbLrRP2 interferes with normal growth in both life cycle stages. The RNAi-mediated growth retardations caused by the depletion of TbLrRP2 were stronger than the ones observed for TbLrRP1 (Fig. 1), especially in the bloodstream forms. In line with this, the ethidium bromide stain of the TbLrRP2 RNAi cell line showed a strong depletion not only of the LSUβ, as is seen in the TbLrRP1 RNAi cell line (Fig. 1), but also of the LSUα rRNA fragment with a concomitant accumulation of a larger precursor of LSU rRNAs (Fig. 8C).

These LSU rRNA processing phenotypes were analyzed in detail by doing an analogous northern blot analysis (Fig. 9), as had been done for TbLrRP1 (Fig. 3). Qualitatively, all processing intermediates detected in TbLrRP1-depleted cells were also seen after TbLrRP2 depletion. However, the relative amounts of the detected intermediates differed in the two cell lines (Figs 9 and 3). Thus, the 5.1 LSU rRNA fragment (LSU pre-rRNA lacking ITS2 and the 5.8 rRNA) and the 2.7 fragment (extending from ITS4 to 3′ETS) accumulated to much higher levels than in TbLrRP1-depleted cells. Moreover, strong accumulation of an additional intermediate that was specific for TbLrRP2-depleted cells and termed 2.2b (comprising LSUα, ITS3, and SR1) was also detected. Conversely, the amounts of LSUα and LSUβ rRNA fragments, as well as of all four small rRNAs (SR1, SR2, SR6, and SR4), were greatly diminished (Fig. 9). In summary, individual depletion of either TbLrRP2 or TbLrRP1 inhibits processing of ITS5, ITS6, and ITS7 (Fig. 2), whereas processing of ITS3 and ITS4 appears to depend on TbLrRP2 only (Fig. 2D, red arrows).

Cytosolic ribosomes in TbLrRP2-depleted cells contain rRNA processing intermediates

To determine whether the LSU rRNA processing intermediates in the TbLrRP2-depleted cells are incorporated into translating cytosolic ribosomes, we performed the same type of polysome profiling and FISH analyses that were done for TbLrRP1-depleted cells (Fig. 4). The results showed that the polysome profile did not significantly change after TbLrRP2 depletion (Fig. 10A and B). This indicated that the detected LSU rRNA processing intermediates were integrated into what appear to be translationally active cytosolic ribosomes, even though they contain only spurious amounts of the mature LSUα and LSUβ rRNA fragments (Fig. 10C). These results were confirmed by FISH analysis, which demonstrated that LSU rRNA processing intermediates containing ITS3, ITS4, ITS5, ITS6, and ITS7 were detected in the cytosol only (Fig. 10E). ITS1, however, which is only present in the 9.6 kb pre-rRNA, showed a nucleolar staining as expected.

A putative catalytic dead TbLrRP2 is not functional

The NERD domain of TbLrRP2 contains five conserved charged residues that likely are required for its nuclease function. To test whether these residues are essential for LSU rRNA processing, we produced a putative catalytic dead version of TbLrRP2 where the five residues were replaced by alanines (Fig. 11A). Wild-type and catalytic dead versions of TbLrRP2 tagged at their C-terminus were expressed under tetracycline control in the background of the TbLrRP2 RNAi cell line. The result showed that while both versions were expressed in similar amounts (Fig. 11B), only the wild-type version was able to restore proper LSU rRNA processing and normal growth (Fig. 11C and D). This strongly suggests that TbLrRP2 has nuclease activity and this activity is required for trypanosome-specific LSU rRNA processing.

The TMD domains of TbLrRP1 and TbLrRP2 are essential for efficient rRNA processing

Both TbLrRP1 and TbLrRP2 are integral membrane proteins that, at least in part, localize to the nuclear envelope. To test whether their integral membrane localization is essential for LSU rRNA processing, we produced C-terminally tagged variants of the two proteins that lack the TMDs, termed TbLrRP1-ΔTMD-HA and ΔTMD-TbLrRP2-MYC, respectively. The cytosolic domains of the two proteins remained unchanged, and thus their biochemical activities should not be affected (Fig. 12A).

TbLrRP1-ΔTMD-HA was expressed under tetracycline control in the background of the TbLrRP1 dKO cell line and partially reversed the LSU rRNA processing defect when compared to the same cell line complemented with full-length TbLrRP1-HA (Fig. 12B, left panel). This suggests that efficient LSU rRNA processing requires a heterodimer TbLrRP1/TbLrRP2 dimer that is membrane-anchored. The partial complementation that is observed is likely due to a fraction of TbLrRP1-ΔTMD that forms a heterodimer with the membrane-anchored TbLrRP2.

ΔTMD-TbLrRP2-MYC was expressed in the TbLrRP2 RNAi cell line and compared to the same cell line expressing full-length TbLrRP2-MYC. Addition of tetracycline to this cell line results in the replacement of the endogenous TbLrRP2 by ΔTMD-TbLrRP2-MYC or TbLrRP2-MYC, respectively. However, ΔTMD-TbLrRP2-MYC only marginally complemented the LSU rRNA processing defect caused by the depletion of TbLrRP2 (Fig. 12B, right panel). Intriguingly, whereas TbLrRP1-ΔTMD-HA was mainly cytosolically localized, ΔTMD-TbLrRP2-MYC is efficiently imported into mitochondria (Fig. 12C). This can be explained by a cryptic mitochondrial targeting signal that gets exposed after deletion of the TMD of TbLrRP2. Thus, the essential absence of complementation observed for ΔTMD-TbLrRP2 is likely caused by both the lack of the TMD and by the sequestration of the protein into the mitochondrion.

Discussion

In contrast to most other eukaryotes, the cytosolic LSU rRNA of trypanosomes and its relatives is fragmented into six pieces, a process that involves the excision of 5 short ITSs. In the present study, we discovered a protein complex that includes TbLrRP1 and TbLrRP2 that is essential for these processing events.

TbLrRP1 contains DEDDh 3′–5′ exonuclease motifs. The T. brucei genome also encodes two additional ORFs, Tb927.3.5140 (762 aa) and Tb927.3.5150 (329 aa), which have DEDDh-motifs containing exonuclease domains highly similar to the one of TbLrRP1 (378 aa), although the molecular weight of the three proteins is different. TbLrRP1, Tb927.3.5140, and Tb927.3.5150 are conserved in essentially all kinetoplastids. Beyond kinetoplastids, a variety of eukaryotic and some bacterial proteins were detected that had DEDDh motif-containing exonuclease domains similar to TbLRP1. However, TbLrRP1 has a C-terminal TMD that is absent from non-kinetoplastid DEDDh motif-containing proteins. Intriguingly, the DEDDh motif-containing domain of TbLrRP1 is similar to the one of yeast and mammalian ERI1, a multifunctional protein proposed to be involved in siRNA processing, histone mRNA regulation, and 3′-end processing of the 5.8S rRNA. However, while ERI1 and TbLrRP1 are both involved in LSU rRNA processing, they work by different mechanisms, since intact catalytic DEDDh motifs were only required for 5.8S rRNA processing by ERI1 but appeared to be dispensable for the role TbLrRP1 plays in LSU rRNA processing in trypanosomes [71].

Unexpectedly, TbLrRP2 is a NERD-containing protein. Such proteins are normally only found in a wide range of bacteria, although a few isolated examples have been detected in plants and Archaea [72]. Using TbLrRP2 as a query for BLAST searches and HHPred analyses, only bacterial NERD-containing proteins were detected. Moreover, TbLrRP2 contains a C-terminal TMD, like many bacterial NERD-containing proteins. These results could indicate that TbLrRP2 was acquired by horizontal gene transfer in the last common ancestor of the metakinetoplastids (see below). While the precise function of the NERD remains unknown, it has been predicted to be involved in DNA processing events and may have a nuclease function.

Extensive fragmentation of the eukaryotic cytosolic LSU rRNA as observed in T. brucei is highly unusual. It is found in all metakinetoplastids, which covers most of the kinetoplastid diversity, but is absent from the much smaller sister group of the prokinetoplastids (e.g. Perkinsela sp.) which have an unfragmented LSU rRNA [44]. The second group showing even more extensive LSU rRNA fragmentation than the metakinetoplastids are the euglenoids; they process their LSU rRNA into 13 distinct fragments. However, the diplomenids, the sister group of the euglenoids, have an unfragmented LSU rRNA. Even though kinetoplastids and euglenoids belong to the same phylum of the Euglenozoa, and thus are closely related, the fragmentation of the LSU rRNA likely evolved independently of the two groups. Interestingly, in line with the occurrence of fragmented LSU rRNAs genes, orthologs of TbLrRP1 and TbLrRP2 were found in all metakinetoplastids but not in the only available prokinetoplastid genome of Perkinsela sp.

TbLrRP1 and TbLrRP2 form a complex, which is required for the excision of all trypanosome-specific ITSs (ITS3 to ITS7). Lack of TbLrRP1 mainly inhibits the excision of 3 ITSs (ITS5 to ITS7), whereas lack of TbLrRP2 has a stronger phenotype and inhibits the excision of all 5 ITSs (ITS3 to ITS7) (Fig. 2). An intact NERD in TbLrRP2 is required for LSU rRNA processing (Fig. 11). The simplest explanation for this would be that TbLrRP2 acts as an endonuclease. However, unlike previously proposed for NERD-containing proteins, the substrate of the NERD in the trypanosomal TbLrRP2 would be RNA instead of DNA. The function of TbLrRP1, on the other hand, does not appear to require intact 3′–5′ exonuclease DEDDh motifs (Fig. 6). While the exact function of TbTbLrRP1 remains unknown, we would like to suggest that it binds substrate RNAs to present them to the putative RNA endonuclease TbTbLrRP2. This TbTbLrRP1-dependent step might be more easily bypassed than the lack of the endonucleolytic activity, which might explain the milder phenotype observed in the absence of TbLrRP1 when compared to the lack of TbLrRP2. Moreover, while TbLrRP1 and TbLrRP2 are key factors required for the trypanosome-specific steps of LSU rRNA processing, they likely act in concert with other as yet unknown factors such as exonucleases and/or snoRNAs [73].

The ER and the nuclear envelope form a single compartment, which can be stained by the ER lumen marker BiP. TbLrRP1 and TbLrRP2 are integral ER membrane proteins that are enriched at the nuclear envelope but not in the nucleolus. The large soluble domains of the two ER-localized proteins are exposed to the cytosol (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S3). For the nuclear envelope-enriched fraction, the same is the case. However, it cannot be excluded that a subfraction of the proteins might be localized at the inner nuclear membrane, where it would face the nucleoplasm. Deletion of the TMD of either of the two proteins prevents efficient LSU rRNA processing, suggesting that integral membrane localization improves their processing activity (Fig. 12B). TbLrRP1 lacking its TMD is cytosolically localized, as expected. The situation is more complicated for bLrRP2, which in the absence of its TMD, is efficiently imported into mitochondria, indicating that an essential function of the N-terminal TMD is to mask a mitochondrial targeting sequence (Fig. 12C). In summary, these results suggest that the trypanosome-specific processing of the LSU rRNA is a late step in the ribosome biogenesis pathway that happens immediately after or immediately prior to nuclear export of the virtually fully assembled LSU of the ribosome. Should this be the case, the assembly of LSU would occur with the unprocessed precursor of the LSU rRNA, and ITS3 to ITS7 would be excised on the fully assembled ribosomal LSU only. Indeed, based on a recent high-resolution structure of the trypanosomal ribosome, the nucleotides immediately flanking ITS3 to ITS7 in the uncleaved precursor LSU rRNA are all exposed on the surface of the mature ribosomal LSU (Supplementary Fig. S4) [74], making post-assembly excision of ITS3 to ITS7 at least in principle possible.

That the processing of ITS3 to ITS7 happens on the assembled LSU of the ribosome is also supported by polysome profile analyses. These experiments showed that actively translating polysomes can be formed that either lack most of the completely processed LSUβ fragment (after TbLrRP1 depletion) or even most of the completely processed LSUα and LSUβ fragments (after TbLrRP2 depletion) (Figs 4A and 10A). This demonstrates that unprocessed or only partially processed precursor LSU rRNAs can assemble into the ribosomal LSU and form functional ribosomes. The most extreme case is found in polysomes of TbLrRP2-depleted cells, which incorporate large amounts of the unprocessed 5.1 kb precursor of the LSU rRNA (Fig. 10C).

Based on the structure of the T. brucei LSU ribosome, it has been suggested that processing of SR1 might be required to allow space for the unusually large expansion segment 19L of the trypanosomal LSU rRNA. Moreover, processing of SR2, SR6, and SR4 was proposed to be a structural necessity to prevent collision of the not-excised ITSs with the kinetoplastid-specific domain of the LSU rRNA [75]. Our results now suggest, at least in case of TbLsRP2-depletion, that actively translating ribosomes can be formed with the unprocessed LSU rRNA precursor (Fig. 10). Although, the translation activity of such ribosomes might well be lower.

This raises the question regarding the fitness costs that the impairment of the trypanosome-specific LSU rRNA processing has on the cells. Intriguingly, TbLrRP1-lacking procyclic or bloodstream form cells show only a marginally slower growth than the corresponding wild-type cells (Fig. 1). TbLrRP2 depletion, however, causes stronger phenotypes; it slows down growth of procyclic cells. And in the case of bloodstream forms, growth inhibition is more severe (Fig. 8). The stronger effect observed in bloodstream forms might be due to their shorter generation times when compared to procyclic cells. Importantly though, in both cases growth does not stop but continues at a reduced rate. Thus, even after inhibition of the trypanosome-specific LSU rRNA processing events, at least a fraction of translation-competent ribosomes can still form and support growth.

In summary, we have identified TbLrRP1 and TbLrRP2, two previously uncharacterized proteins, as essential components of the kinetoplastid-specific LSU rRNA processing pathway. Although depletion of either protein does not completely inhibit cell growth, it compromises cellular fitness, underscoring their functional importance. Defining the precise roles of TbLrRP1 and TbLrRP2, as well as the mechanism underlying cleavage site selection on the LSU rRNA, will require future in vitro studies using recombinantly expressed proteins, along with structural analyses of the TbLrRP1/TbLrRP2 complex, approaches that are beyond the scope of the present work.
