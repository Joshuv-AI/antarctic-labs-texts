Effectiveness of low-dose perampanel in focal epilepsy patients aged twelve or older in Southern China: an observational study
Authors: Xiangru Lu, Yuwen Deng, Xiaoli Shi, Yongfeng Feng, Jinou Zheng, Yuan Wu, Lu Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis real-world study from southern China retrospectively evaluated the effectiveness, tolerability, and retention of low-dose perampanel (PER 2–6 mg) in focal epilepsy and explored associated clinical factors. Based on the steady-state maintenance dose of PER (2, 4, or 6 mg/day), 190 patients were enrolled from July 2020 to August 2023. Outcomes included seizure reduction, responder rates (≥ 50% reduction), adverse events (AEs), and retention at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months. At 12 months, seizure frequency reductions were 100% (2 mg), 91% (4 mg), and 82.5% (6 mg). Efficacy rates at 12 months were 44.6% (2 mg), 50% (4 mg), and 66.8% (6 mg) (P = 0.168). Exploratory analyses suggested a higher responder rate with 6 mg versus 2 mg in patients with disease duration ≥ 5 years or focal seizures only (P = 0.023; P = 0.044). Multivariable analysis indicated that a lower number of concomitant ASMs was independently associated with better early response (6-month), while a shorter disease duration (< 5 years) was associated with better long-term (12-month) response, independent of the specific dose within the 2–6 mg range. AE incidence was lowest with 2 mg (14.3%) versus 4 mg (24.1%) and 6 mg (23.9%). Respective 12-month retention rates were 32.2, 46.8, and 58.6%. In conclusion, this study suggests that low-dose PER (2–6 mg) can achieve favorable short- and long-term seizure control in focal epilepsy patients aged ≥ 12 years, with a good tolerability profile. Introduction

Epilepsy is a chronic and serious neurological disorder affecting approximately 70 million people globally1,2. Standardized treatment and effective seizure control remain crucial for patients. Perampanel (PER), a third-generation antiseizure medication (ASM), acts as a selective, noncompetitive α-amino-3-hydroxy-5-methyl-4-isoxazolepropionic acid (AMPA) receptor antagonist3. The 2018 guideline from the American Academy of Neurology and the American Epilepsy Society indicate PER is effective in reducing seizure frequency in adults with drug-resistant focal epilepsy4. As monotherapy, Study 342 reported a 74% response rate in focal epilepsy5. The recommended dosage is 8–12 mg/day6, and PER is positioned as a first-line ASM7, approved in China in December 2019 for patients aged ≥ 4 years with focal epilepsy. However, PER’s psychiatric effects raise clinical concerns. A 2024 retrospective analysis in Japan found a 23.7% incidence of psychiatric adverse events (AEs)8. At 8 mg, PER showed lower sedation and euphoria than alprazolam and ketamine, but at 24 mg and 36 mg, its sedative effect exceeded that of ketamine, with euphoria comparable to ketamine and 3 mg alprazolam9. Supratherapeutic doses (> 24 mg/day) may induce euphoria, with potential for low-to-moderate physical or high psychological dependence10. In monotherapy, euphoria was the most common AE, reaching 5.9%11. The US prescribing information includes a boxed warning for serious psychiatric and behavioral reactions11, and PER is classified as a Schedule III-controlled substance in the US in 2013 and a Category II Psychotropic Substance in China since July 2023.

Recent real-world studies support low-dose PER for seizure control12–14, but these have limitations. A Hong Kong study focused on drug-resistant epilepsy with only 70 patients12. A Japanese study had a large sample but allowed ASM adjustments during follow-up, potentially biasing effectiveness14. Research from Huashan Hospital in Shanghai included both children and adults but did not stratify by age or dose13. Variations by country, ethnicity, and region, particularly within Chinese populations, highlight the need for further real-world evidence on optimal PER dosing to balance efficacy with minimized side effects and dependency risks. To address these gaps, this study employs subgroup and stratified analyses based on dose and clinical characteristics, using real-world data from southern China. It aims to evaluate the effectiveness and tolerability of low-dose PER in focal epilepsy and identify factors influencing dose- response, thereby supporting optimized PER treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Participants

Consecutive patients aged ≥ 12 years with focal epilepsy who visited the Epilepsy Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University between July 2020 and August 2023 were enrolled. The following inclusion criteria were adopted: (1) age ≥ 12 years and both sexes; (2) meeting the diagnostic criteria for focal epilepsy established by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) in 201715; and (3) demonstrating a seizure frequency of ≥ 1 per month within the 3 months prior to PER treatment and no adjustment of ASMs within the month before treatment initiation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pregnant patients; (2) patients with psychiatric disorders or severe mood disorders; (3) patients with cognitive impairments, wherein neither the patient nor their caregiver could accurately record their condition; (4) patients with severe hepatic, renal, or other organ diseases; (5) patients who had received traditional Chinese medicine or Chinese herbal compound formulations for epilepsy, epilepsy surgery, or ketogenic diet therapy within the 3 months prior to PER add-on treatment; and (6) patients with intracranial active lesions/diseases related to seizures, such as encephalitis in the acute phase or intracranial malignant tumors.

The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Guangxi Medical University (Approval No. 2025-E0389) in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki, and all of the enrolled patients provided written informed consent.

Prior to participation, written informed consent was obtained from all subjects (or from the parents/legal guardians of minors under 16 years of age).

Protocol

Patient grouping and data collection

Based on the steady-state maintenance dose of PER, patients were categorized into three groups: 2 mg/day, 4 mg/day, and 6 mg/day. The steady-state maintenance dose was defined as the daily dose that remained unchanged for at least 4 weeks prior to and throughout the efficacy evaluation period.

Data were collected from outpatient/inpatient records, telephone questionnaires, and clinic visits. Information on seizure types, symptoms, frequency per 28 days, PER dosage, concomitant ASMs, and adverse reactions was recorded at 3-, 6-, 9-, and 12-month follow-ups. Maintenance for 6 months was defined as short-term treatment, and 12 months as long-term treatment. Follow-up discontinuation was defined if patients could not tolerate AEs, voluntarily withdrew, or had adjustments to concomitant ASMs.

Further analyses included disease course, etiology, seizure types (classified per 2017 ILAE criteria15, number/type of concomitant ASMs, and neuroimaging findings. Drug-resistant epilepsy was defined according to Kwan P et al. (2010)16. Neuroimaging results from 3.0T brain MRI were assessed by neurologists and radiologists. Positive findings indicated abnormalities potentially causative of seizures; negative findings indicated no abnormalities or nonspecific changes unrelated to seizures. Concomitant ASMs were classified as enzyme-inducing (EI-ASMs), enzyme-suppressing (ES-ASMs), or other ASMs based on hepatic enzyme effects.

Clinical assessment endpoints

Primary endpoint: Efficacy of PER at different doses

Primary endpoint: Efficacy of PER at different doses

Baseline seizure frequency was defined as the mean monthly seizure frequency during the 3 months before PER initiation. Following Segal et al.17 and Hong et al.18, the following were assessed:① Median monthly seizure frequency during stable PER treatment (months 1–3, 4–6, 7–9, and 10–12) and the median percentage change from baseline for each dose group;② Short-term (6-month) and long-term (12-month) treatment response rates by dose group.

The seizure change rate was calculated as: [(posttreatment monthly frequency - baseline frequency) / baseline frequency] × 100%19. Negative values indicated reduction, positive values indicated increase, and zero indicated no change. Seizure control was categorized per Chung et al.20 and Leppik et al.21 as: ① Complete control: seizure-free throughout follow-up; ② Response: ≥50% reduction in seizure frequency (50% responder rate); ③ Non-response: <50% reduction; ④ Worsening: ≥25% increase in seizure frequency.

(2)Secondary endpoints: Adverse events and drug retention rate

Secondary endpoints: Adverse events and drug retention rate

The retention rate was defined as the proportion of patients remaining on their baseline PER dose at 6 and 12 months, relative to the total enrolled in each dose group (number of patients/total number of enrolled patients × 100%). Patients with dose adjustments during treatment were excluded from retention calculations.

Treatment methods

All patients received oral PER [Eisai (China) Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd.; 2 mg/tablet] as add-on therapy without altering baseline ASMs. Dosing followed the prescribing information and reference22, starting at 1–2 mg daily. Titration occurred every 7–14 days and could be individually adjusted. The specific starting dose, titration schedule, and maintenance dose were determined by the physician based on clinical assessment. Treatment continued for at least one month, with dose reduction or discontinuation in cases of intolerable AEs or seizure worsening.

Statistical methods

The data were analyzed by using SPSS 25.0 statistical software. Continuous data with a nonnormal distribution are described by using the median (M) and interquartile range (Q1, Q3). Categorical data are presented as frequencies and percentages. Intergroup comparisons were performed by using the chi-square test or the Mann-Whitney U test/Kruskal-Wallis test for nonnormally distributed data or ordinal categorical data. Logistic regression analysis with interaction terms was used to examine clinical factors potentially influencing PER efficacy. A P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Efficacy evaluation

Seizure frequency and changes in seizure frequency across dose groups

During the first 1–3 months of treatment, the seizure frequencies per 28 days [median (Q1, Q3)] for patients receiving 2 mg, 4 mg, and 6 mg doses were 0.3 (0, 1.925), 2 (0.7, 4), and 1.3 (0.3, 2.7) seizures/month, respectively; during months 4–6, the frequences were 0.2 (0, 1.15), 1.3 (0.5, 3.65), and 1.25 (0.35, 2.175) seizures/month, respectively; during months 7–9, the frequences were 0 (0, 0), 1.0 (0, 3.5), and 1 (0, 4) seizures/month, respectively; and during months 10–12, the frequences were 0 (0, 0), 0.75 (0, 2.125), and 1 (0, 4) seizures/month, respectively. Compared with the other doses, the seizure frequencies significantly differed at 1–3, 4–6, and 7–9 months of treatment with the 2 mg dose (Fig. 2A).

Seizure frequency per 28 days and median percentage change in seizure frequency across dose groups.

Compared with baseline, changes in seizure frequency across all of the PER dose groups during each treatment period were negative, thus indicating a reduction in seizures. At 3, 6, 9, and 12 months of treatment, the seizure reduction rates [M (Q1, Q3)] for patients in the 2 mg group were 84% (54.5%, 100%), 83% (50.0%, 99.75%), 100% (100%, 100%), and 100% (100%, 100%), respectively; for the 4 mg group, the rates were 47% (1%, 78%), 75% (50.5%, 95%), 80% (0%, 100%), and 91% (36.5%, 100%), respectively; and for the 6 mg group, the rates were 60% (14.25%, 99%), 62.5% (2.75%, 96.25%), 80.5% (52.25%, 100%), and 82.5% (50.00%, 100%), respectively. At 3 and 6 months of treatment, the reduction in seizure frequency was significantly greater in the 2 mg group compared to the 4 mg group (P < 0.001). At 9 and 12 months of treatment, the reduction in seizure frequency in the 2 mg group was also significantly greater than that in the other dose groups (Fig. 2B).

Comparison of effectiveness levels by clinical characteristics across dose groups

To clarify differences in response rates among different doses across various clinical characteristics, including disease duration, etiology, imaging findings, number and types of concomitant ASMs, and drug-resistant epilepsy (DRE), further subgroup analyses were performed. At 6 months of treatment, there were no statistically significant differences observed in response rates across clinical characteristics among patients who received different PER doses (P > 0.05). At 12 months of treatment, among patients with a disease duration ≥ 5 years and without FBTCS, the response rate was observed to be higher with 6 mg than with 2 mg (P = 0.023 and P = 0.044, respectively). Furthermore, at both 6 and 12 months, the response rate in patients with DRE tended to increase with increasing PER doses, although the difference did not achieve statistical significance (Table 2).

Drug retention rate

Among the 190 enrolled patients, 5 were lost to follow-up at the 6-month follow-up, thus resulting in 185 patients for statistical analysis. Fifty-one patients (54.3%), 39 patients (62.9%), and 22 patients (75.9%) remained on the 2 mg, 4 mg, and 6 mg doses, respectively. The drug retention rates for patients with drug-resistant epilepsy in the aforementioned dose groups were 35 (68.6%), 28 (71.8%), and 20 (90.9%), respectively (P > 0.05); moreover, a significant difference was observed between the 2 mg and 6 mg groups (P = 0.037). At the 12-month follow-up, 34 patients (36.2%), 29 patients (46.8%), and 17 patients (58.6%) remained on the 2 mg, 4 mg, and 6 mg doses, respectively. The retention rates for drug-resistant epilepsy patients in these aforementioned dose groups were 22 (64.7%), 21 (72.4%), and 15 (88.2%), respectively (P > 0.05) (Supplementary Tables 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Adverse drug events

After excluding one patient who was lost to follow-up during the second month, 189 patients were included in the AE analysis. Among these 189 patients, 223 patient-group observations were analyzed (in which enrollment in each dose group was considered to be one observation). AEs were reported in 44 observations (19.7%), with dizziness being the most frequently reported event, followed by psychiatric symptoms (including irritability, agitation, and depression, among others). AEs occurred in 14 observations (14.3%) in the 2 mg group, 19 observations (24.1%) in the 4 mg group, and 11 observations (23.9%) in the 6 mg group. Discontinuation due to side effects occurred in 5 patients (5.1%) in the 2 mg group, 5 patients (6.3%) in the 4 mg group, and 6 patients (13.0%) in the 6 mg group. Nine patients experienced two AEs, and two patients experienced three adverse symptoms, with the vast majority of these events occurring in the 4 mg and 6 mg groups (details in Supplementary Tables 2 and Supplementary Fig. 2).

Discussion

Chinese expert consensus recommends PER as initial monotherapy or first-line adjunctive therapy for focal-onset seizures (FOSs), particularly in patients with FBTCS23. This real-world study, conducted in a multiethnic region of Southern China, represents one of the few global investigations into the dose-response relationship of PER and its influencing factors. The study design minimized the impact of concomitant antiseizure medications on efficacy assessment. Our findings indicate that low-dose PER (2–6 mg), used as monotherapy or early add-on therapy, provides effective short-term seizure control in this regional epilepsy population. Patients with a disease duration of < 5 years also demonstrated favorable long-term efficacy, accompanied by low adverse event rates and high retention rates. Ethnic and racial differences may significantly influence the dose-efficacy relationship of PER.

The internationally recommended maintenance doses for PER are primarily derived from Studies 304 (NCT00699972), 305 (NCT00699582), 306 (NCT00700310), and 335 (NCT01618695)24–27, which enrolled patients aged ≥ 12 years with drug-resistant focal epilepsy. Studies 304 (conducted predominantly in the Americas) and 305 (multiregional, including Europe and North America) involved mainly Caucasian populations at doses of 8–12 mg/day. In contrast, Study 335, performed in the Asia-Pacific region, evaluated doses of 4–12 mg/day, while Study 306 (116 centers across 24 countries in Europe, Asia, and Australia) explored efficacy at lower doses of 2–4 mg/day.

This study demonstrated that at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months of PER maintenance therapy within the daily dosage range of 2–8 mg, the seizure frequency decreased from baseline in all of the dose groups, with the majority of patients achieving a > 50% reduction. At 12 months of maintenance therapy, the response rates (≥ 50% seizure reduction) within the 2–8 mg/day range varied from 44.6% to 68.8%. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled phase III study (Study 335) conducted in the Asia-Pacific region revealed that add-on PER treatment at 4 mg, 8 mg, and 12 mg in patients aged ≥ 12 years with drug-resistant FOS reduced the median baseline seizure frequency by 17.3%, 29.0%, and 38.0%, respectively, over 25 weeks27. In our study, the reduction in seizure frequency observed with 2–6 mg was greater than that reported in Study 335. Another prospective, real-world, observational single-center study from Nanjing conducted in eastern China28 reported seizure-free rates of 69.84% and 65.08% at 6 and 12 months, respectively, among patients with focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures (FBTCS) during PER titration and maintenance (mean maintenance dose of 4.64 ± 1.55 mg/day, with 4 mg/day being the most common dosage).

A multicenter open-label study in Chinese epilepsy patients29 reported a mean PER maintenance dose of 5.9 ± 1.5 mg/day as early add-on therapy, with a 50% response rate of 67.9% and a median seizure frequency reduction of 100% during both titration and maintenance periods. In a Japanese real-world study14, patients ≥ 65 years received 3.7 ± 1.9 mg/day with 89% efficacy, while those < 65 years received 3.8 ± 2.0 mg/day (60.1% efficacy). A Huashan Hospital study13 indicated 52.8% of patients used 2 mg, and the one-year dose was 4.3 ± 2.7 mg, with efficacy between 59.1% and 66.4%. The Hong Kong WRAPPER study12 reported a median PER dose of 2 mg and 50% response rates of 40.0%-48.7% over 52 weeks. Overall, our cohort’s dose and efficacy align with these real-world findings. A review30 noted 63% seizure freedom in newly diagnosed patients at 4 mg/day5. These results suggest low-dose PER achieves favorable effectiveness for focal-onset seizures in Asian/Chinese practice. The lower doses in Asian populations may relate to ethnic variations in height, weight, drug metabolism, or genetic background, warranting further pharmacometabolomic and pharmacogenomic investigation.

Previous studies have demonstrated that although the PER dose is linearly related to the plasma concentration, sex and age do not affect the plasma PER concentration31. This study did not demonstrate that sex or age influenced the dose effectiveness relationship of PER, which corroborates previous findings from another perspective. Furthermore, pharmacokinetic modeling analyses performed by some researchers have indicated that the clearance of PER increases when it is coadministered with carbamazepine32. However, in this cohort, the concomitant use of enzyme-inducing antiseizure medications (EI-ASMs) appeared to have no significant effect on the effectiveness of PER across different doses, which is consistent with the results of the Huashan Hospital cohort study conducted in China13 and a Korean study33. Currently, research on whether valproate affects PER effectiveness is limited. This cohort did not demonstrate an influence of concomitant valproate on the relationship between PER dose and efficacy; a Chinese study also reported no significant difference in effectiveness when PER was combined with sodium valproate, levetiracetam, or oxcarbazepine29. However, the Hong Kong WRAPPER study suggested a lower efficacy rate for PER when it was combined with sodium valproate12. These inconsistent findings warrant verification through rigorous clinical study designs. Additionally, this study revealed that at 6 months of treatment, patients who received low-dose PER treatment (2–4 mg) either as monotherapy or combined with one ASM exhibited higher efficacy rates than those who received other doses combined with ≥ 2 ASMs. These results validate the conclusions of the PEREAGAL study, which proposed that early adjunctive therapy yields greater effectiveness than late adjunctive therapy34.

Additionally, the results of this study suggest a higher response rate with 6 mg PER in patients with a disease duration ≥ 5 years. Concurrently, within the 2–6 mg/day range, higher PER doses were associated with higher drug retention rates. Notably, more than 70% of the patients in this cohort had drug-resistant epilepsy. Although regression analysis did not reveal a statistically significant association between drug-resistant epilepsy and the PER dose-response relationship, it did indicate an increasing trend in both efficacy rates and retention rates with higher PER doses within the 6 mg range among this subgroup. These findings suggest that higher doses of PER may be required to control seizures in patients with drug-resistant epilepsy and that a positive dose-response relationship may exist within a specific dose range.

A review of PER use in European countries revealed AE incidences ranging from 18.2% to 67.4% (median value: 37.1%), with AE-related discontinuation rates ranging from 6.2% to 56% (median value: 12.5%)35. In this cohort, low-dose PER treatment was largely associated with a single adverse reaction, whereas an increase in the dose led to a greater proportion of patients experiencing two or more concurrent adverse reactions. The incidence of adverse reactions (14.3–23.9%) and discontinuation rates due to side effects (5.1–13.0%) across dose groups in this study were lower than the median values reported in the European studies, although the incidence of AEs in patients receiving 4 mg was slightly higher than that in those receiving doses of 2 mg and 6 mg. These results may be related to the generally lower PER doses that were used in our cohort and the higher proportion of patients receiving 4 mg; however, they also suggest a dose dependency for adverse events.

In this observational retrospective study, univariate analysis suggested that a 2 mg dose of PER was associated with higher seizure control rates. However, since dosing was individually titrated based on clinical response rather than randomized, the significant imbalance in drug-resistant epilepsy status represents a potential limitation. Consequently, patients maintained on lower doses (e.g., 2 mg) may have had more favorable baseline characteristics (e.g., shorter disease duration, less severe seizure types), and the observed association might therefore be influenced by selection bias. To minimize confounding, we performed multivariate analysis adjusting for available baseline covariates. The results indicated that monotherapy or early adjunctive use of low dose PER was associated with favorable short-term efficacy, while a 6 mg dose was more frequently observed in patients with longer disease duration. We acknowledge several methodological limitations. Propensity score methods were not feasible due to the incomplete recording of key confounders, particularly physician prescribing preferences and treatment adherence, which are inherently difficult to ascertain retrospectively. Despite these efforts, residual confounding cannot be excluded. In conclusion, this study describes real world treatment patterns and associations. A treatment strategy aiming for the lowest effective dose within the 2–6 mg range appears clinically feasible. However, no causal conclusions regarding dose superiority or dose–response efficacy can be drawn from these observational data.

Conclusion

In conclusion, accumulating evidence recommends early and low-dose (4 mg/day) use of PER36. Our study differs from prior research by employing a dose-stratified analysis to explore associations with clinical outcomes. This exploratory, real-world design provides complementary evidence to randomized trials, reflecting treatment patterns under routine clinical conditions. This recommendation is further substantiated with practical evidence encompassing seizure control rates, AE incidence, and retention rates. Additionally, our findings, which are based on a multiethnic region in southern China, provide valuable ethnic and racial data for formulating more precise individualized PER treatment strategies, thereby offering guidance for clinical practice. However, the limitations of this study include its relatively small sample size, single-center retrospective design, and the potential for selection bias in dose assignment. Furthermore, we did not collect pharmacokinetic or pharmacogenetic data, thus precluding validation of the hypothesis regarding ethnic/racial influences on the PER dose-response relationship. Future research should aim to prioritize multicenter, large-sample, prospective studies with more comprehensive and scientifically rigorous clinical data collection and analysis.
