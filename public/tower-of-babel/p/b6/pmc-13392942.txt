Galangin ameliorates Salmonella Pullorum-induced enteritis in Danzhou chicks through gut microbiota–derived indole-3-lactic acid–mediated AHR activation
Authors: Chang Cheng, Wenjie Lu, Weiqin Fan, Han Wu, Xueqi Tian, Diqi Yang, Hui Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Antibiotic restrictions in poultry production necessitate natural alternatives against Salmonella Pullorum, a pathogen causing severe enteritis and high chick mortality. We show that the dietary flavonoid galangin alleviates S. Pullorum–induced intestinal injury not via direct antimicrobial action, but by modulating gut microbiota to enrich tryptophan-derived indole-3-lactic acid (ILA). Galangin restored growth, preserved barrier integrity, reduced liver bacterial translocation, and suppressed inflammation in infected chicks. Fecal microbiota transplantation from galangin-treated donors recapitulated these benefits, confirming microbiota dependence. ILA activated the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), concurrently inhibiting NF-κB and HIF-1α pathways—key drivers of Salmonella-exploited inflammation and metabolic reprogramming—thereby enhancing mucosal defense and limiting intracellular bacterial survival. Pharmacological AHR blockade or NF-κB/HIF-1α activation abolished galangin’s effects. Collectively, these findings establish that galangin acts as a prebiotic-like agent via the ILA–AHR axis, providing a mechanism-based strategy for antibiotic reduction in sustainable poultry production.

Introduction

Over the past decade, global production and consumption of poultry meat and its derived products have increased substantially (OECD/FAO, 2021). As the most extensively produced meat worldwide, poultry serves as an indispensable protein source across the globe (OECD/FAO, 2021). Global meat supply is projected to reach 374 million metric tons by 2030, with poultry accounting for a growing share of this total (Commission, 2026). As of 2021, China ranked as the world’s second-largest producer of poultry meat, contributing approximately 13% of global output, while simultaneously serving as the leading global producer of eggs, responsible for 34% of worldwide egg production (OECD/FAO, 2021, Commission, 2026). Salmonella enterica serovar Gallinarum biovar Pullorum (S. Pullorum) infection represents one of the most severe diseases jeopardizing the poultry industry, and mitigating its impact is crucial for enhancing poultry meat output. In young chicks, S. Pullorum infection can cause severe systemic disease with mortality rates reaching up to 100% within two to three weeks post-hatching. Affected birds typically display clinical signs such as anorexia, weakness, lethargy, and characteristic pasty white feces adhering to the vent area (Lu et al., 2025). The pathogen rapidly colonizes the intestinal tract and disseminates to internal organs—particularly the liver and spleen—triggering intense inflammation, tissue damage, and growth retardation (Liljebjelke et al., 2005, Foster et al., 2021, Thøfner and Christensen, 2021, Luigi et al., 2023). Historically, antimicrobial agents have been the primary intervention for controlling pullorum disease in commercial poultry operations, while vaccination has been employed only sporadically due to limited efficacy and inconsistent protection across different field conditions. Indeed, existing vaccines often fail to prevent intestinal colonization or shedding, offering incomplete defense against infection (Li et al., 2023). With the global phase-out of non-therapeutic antibiotics driven by antimicrobial resistance concerns, the lack of reliable alternatives has left flocks increasingly vulnerable to S. Pullorum outbreaks, resulting in substantial economic losses to the poultry industry (Luigi et al., 2023, Patangia et al., 2022). Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop novel, cost-effective, safe, and efficacious strategies to manage S. Pullorum-induced enteritis and systemic infection without relying on conventional antibiotics.

Accumulating evidence underscores the pivotal role of the gut microbiota in sustaining host systemic health and physiological homeostasis (Pangga et al., 2025, Lyte, 2026, Zhou et al., 2026). In combating Salmonella infections, interventions with specific probiotics have shown promise. For instance, Ligilactobacillus salivarius XP132 has been reported to inhibit horizontal and vertical transmission of S. Pullorum in chickens, while Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus P118 attenuated S. Pullorum infection in chick and embryo models (He et al., 2024, Jin et al., 2026). In contrast, combined supplementation of Lactobacillus reuteri with Candida rugosa was evaluated in broilers infected with S. Typhimurium, showing alleviation of intestinal barrier lesions via gut microbiota modulation (Yang et al., 2025). Furthermore, studies utilizing fecal microbiota transplantation have revealed that certain bioactive compounds exert protective effects through modulation of intestinal microbial communities. For example, phloretin was shown to alleviate S. Pullorum-induced intestinal injury in chicks via the gut microbiota-AHR axis, whereas Artemisia argyi essential oil was tested against S. Pullorum in chicks, and α-linolenic acid was evaluated in chickens challenged with S. Typhimurium (Ding et al., 2025, Ding et al., 2025, Ma et al., 2024). Collectively, these findings indicate that regulating gut microbiota metabolism through probiotic interventions or bioactive natural products represents a viable and promising alternative strategy to conventional antibiotics for controlling S. Pullorum infection in poultry.

Galangin is a naturally occurring flavonoid that has been demonstrated to possess various bioactive properties, including antioxidant, anticancer, and antimicrobial activities, thereby contributing positively to animal health (Singh et al., 2022). It is widely distributed across a range of plant and dietary sources, with notably high concentrations found in Alpinia galanga and honey propolis (Morales Ochoa et al., 2026, Wagh, 2013). Galangin has been shown to possess a range of pharmacological properties, ranging from anti-inflammatory and antioxidant to neuroprotective and anti-tumor effects (Herath et al., 2025, Moakmamern et al., 2025, Wu et al., 2025, Zhang et al., 2025). Galangin is often considered to exert anti-inflammatory effects in intestinal bacterial diseases and is also believed to possess certain antibacterial capabilities of its own (Pepeljnjak and Kosalec, 2004, Zheng et al., 2025, Wang et al., 2023). Recent studies have found that dietary supplementation with galangin can effectively prevent intestinal damage caused by colitis by improving the gut microbiota, while also alleviating conditions such as alcohol-induced liver injury and preventing hyperuricemia (Zhao et al., 2024, Xuan et al., 2020, Zhao et al., 2025). As a naturally occurring flavonoid, galangin shares structural features with other dietary flavonoids that have been investigated for their protective effects against Salmonella infection in poultry. For instance, one study reported that phloretin, a dihydrochalcone flavonoid, alleviated S. Pullorum-induced intestinal injury in chicks by restructuring gut microbiota and enriching the microbiota-derived metabolite 3-phenylpropionic acid, which subsequently activated the AHR/IL-22/STAT-3 axis to preserve barrier integrity and suppress inflammation (Ding et al., 2025). In another study focusing on gut microbiota modulation, a combination extract of fenugreek (Trigonella foenum-graecum) and turmeric (Curcuma longa), both of which are rich in polyphenolic flavonoids, was shown to modulate the cecal microbiota in chickens co-infected with Salmonella enterica and Escherichia coli, with medium-dose supplementation producing a more balanced microbial community (Kerek et al., 2026). Despite extensive research on the pharmacological properties of galangin, its application and mechanistic action against Salmonella infection, especially pullorum disease, have not been documented; consequently, how it mitigates Salmonella-induced intestinal injury remains unknown.

This study aimed to investigate whether galangin alleviates S. Pullorum-induced intestinal damage in chicks by modulating gut microbiota-derived indole-3-lactic acid(ILA). Having established the protective functions of galangin through in vivo experiments, we plan to employ fecal microbiota transplantation to verify its gut microbiota‑dependent mechanism and to further investigate the downstream signaling pathways through which ILA exerts its protective effects, including the activation of the aryl hydrocarbon receptor, which subsequently inhibits NF‑κB and HIF‑1α signaling, thereby reducing inflammatory responses and Salmonella invasion.

Materials and methods

Animals and models

Total of 40 one-day-old healthy Danzhou chickens with comparable growth performance were obtained from Danzhou Rekeyuan Ecological Breeding Co., Ltd. Each group contained 4 male and 4 female chicks (mixed sex, 1:1 ratio). The ambient temperature was maintained at approximately 34°C during days 1–3, and then kept at around 31°C for the subsequent six days (days 4–9). Similarly, artificial lighting was provided continuously from day 1 to day 3, after which it was progressively decreased by 2 hours per day until reaching a stable photoperiod of 16 hours of light per day. Chicks were housed in stainless steel cages (4 chicks per cage), with each treatment group comprising two cages (2 replicate pens per group). The chickens were randomly assigned to four experimental groups (n = 8 per group) using a random number table generated by Excel. Briefly, each chick was assigned a random number using the RAND function, sorted by the random numbers, and then allocated sequentially to the four groups: control (CON), S. Pullorum challenge (SP), galangin supplementation (GAL), and galangin supplementation plus Salmonella challenge (SG).Galangin (purity >98%, Refins, China) was first dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, Biosharp, China) to prepare a stock solution at 125 mg/mL. This stock solution was then diluted in 1 mL of sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) per chick per day to achieve a daily dose of 50 mg/kg body weight. The vehicle control groups (CON and SP) received the same volume of PBS containing DMSO without galangin. The galangin dosing solution was prepared fresh daily; the DMSO stock solution was stored at −20°C and diluted immediately before use. S. Pullorum standard strain C79-1 (CVCC 79201) was purchased from the China Veterinary Culture Collection Center. The strain was cultured in LB broth at 37°C with shaking at 220 rpm for 12 h, harvested by centrifugation (2,000 × g, 10 min, 4°C), and resuspended in PBS. Bacterial concentration was estimated by measuring OD₆₂₅ using a pre‑established standard curve. A dose of 1 × 10¹⁰ CFU per chick was selected for challenge. At 2 days post‑infection (9 days of age), all chicks were euthanized by intravenous injection of sodium pentobarbital (100 mg/kg), and tissues were collected. Mortality and clinical signs were monitored daily throughout the 14‑day experiment; no mortality occurred. The investigator responsible for sample collection, histological analysis, and qPCR assays was blinded to group allocation. However, due to the nature of oral gavage administration, the person delivering the treatments could not be blinded. No exclusion criteria were applied, and all 32 chicks survived the entire experimental period without any mortality. To avoid further ambiguity, we have revised the Materials and Methods section to explicitly describe the independent experimental repetitions and the statistical analysis based on the pooled data from the replicated experiments. Throughout the experimental period, animals had ad libitum access to feed and water. The basal diet was formulated in accordance with the nutritional guidelines provided in the Ross Broiler Manual. Feed composition (moisture AOAC 2001.12, crude protein AOAC 984.13, ether extract AOAC 920.39, crude fiber AOAC 978.10) was analyzed using standardized methods prescribed by the Association of Official Analytical Chemists (AOAC). This study was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Hainan University (Approval No. HNUAUCC-2026-00235).

H&E-staining

Jejunal and ileal segments (n = 8 per group) were surgically excised from all four experimental groups. Tissues were fixed in neutral-buffered 4% paraformaldehyde (pH 7.2) for 24 h before paraffin embedding. Consecutive sagittal sections were prepared using a rotary microtome (Leica RM2235), followed by routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Histological architecture was assessed under brightfield microscopy (Leica DM4500B, magnification 100–400 ×), and images were acquired with integrated digital imaging software (Leica LAS X). Quantitative morphometric analysis of small intestinal parameters was performed by selecting 10 intact, well-oriented villi and their associated crypts per section. Villus length (VL) was measured from the villus tip to the crypt opening, and crypt depth (CD) was defined as the linear distance from the crypt base to its luminal opening. The villus-to-crypt (V/C) ratio was then calculated. Measurements were taken on five sections per sample, and the mean values were derived for statistical analysis.

Bacterial loads in the liver

To assess bacterial load in the liver, tissues were collected from eight birds per group with comparable body weights (n = 8 per group). Samples were homogenized in PBS, serially diluted, and plated onto MacConkey agar plates. The plates were then incubated overnight at 37 °C. Following incubation, Salmonella colonies were enumerated, and the bacterial burden was expressed as colony-forming units (CFU) per gram of liver tissue.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from jejunal tissues and HD11 macrophages using TRIzol™ reagent (AG, China) through chloroform–isopropanol phase separation, followed by isopropanol precipitation and washing with 75% ethanol. The RNA quality was verified by measuring the absorbance ratio A260/A280, which ranged between 1.8 and 2.1 using a UV spectrophotometer. Reverse transcription was performed with 0.1 μg of total RNA in a 20 μL reaction system under the following thermal profile: 37 °C for 2 min, 55 °C for 15 min, and 85 °C for 5 min, followed by holding at 4 °C. Quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) was carried out in a 20 μL reaction volume containing 2 μL of cDNA, 10 μL of TransStart® Tip Green qPCR SuperMix (abclonal), 0.8 μM of target-specific primers (Table S1), and nuclease-free water using the FQD-96X Real-Time PCR Detection System (BIOER, China). β‑actin was used as the internal reference gene. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 5 s and combined annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s. A melting curve analysis was then performed by heating from 65 °C to 95 °C at a rate of 0.3 °C/s. Primer efficiency for each target gene (Table S1) was validated using a 5‑point, 4‑log dilution series of pooled cDNA; efficiencies ranged from 92% to 105%. Each qPCR run included a no‑template control (NTC; water instead of cDNA) and a no‑reverse‑transcription control (no‑RT; RNA sample processed without reverse transcriptase); neither produced detectable amplification. Relative gene expression was calculated using the 2^–ΔΔCt method, normalizing to β‑actin and calibrating to the control group (CON for uninfected samples; SP for infected samples). All primer pairs (Table S1) were designed using Primer-BLAST, yielding amplicons of 80–150 bp with uniform Tm values within ±1 °C.

Fecal microbiota transplantation protocol

One-day-old Danzhou chicks were selected as donors (n = 10) and orally administered galangin (50 mg/kg/day) for seven consecutive days. On day 8, fresh fecal samples were collected, pooled, and homogenized in PBS at 1:50 (w/v). The homogenate was filtered, centrifuged (5,000 × g, 20 min), and the supernatant was mixed with 25% sterile glycerol and stored at −80°C. On the day of FMT, the suspension was thawed, diluted with PBS to 1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL, and incubated at 25°C. Viability was confirmed by plating. For the FMT experiment (independent of the main experiment), recipient one-day-old chicks were assigned to four groups (n = 8 each): CON, SP, FMT alone, and SF (S. Pullorum + FMT). Recipients received 1 mL of fecal suspension by gavage daily for seven days; CON and SP groups received PBS alone as sham control. On day 7, SP and SF chicks were challenged with 1 × 10¹⁰ CFU S. Pullorum. The FMT experiment was likewise independently repeated using separate recipient cohorts under the same experimental conditions. Data from the independent experiments showed highly consistent trends and were pooled for statistical analysis.

Transcriptomics analysis of jejunal tissues

Jejunal tissues from the S. Pullorum-challenged (SP) and galangin-treated, S. Pullorum-challenged (SG) groups were subjected to transcriptomic profiling. Total RNA was extracted and its integrity was assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit on an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system. Sequencing libraries were prepared following poly(A)-based mRNA enrichment, fragmentation, and double-stranded cDNA synthesis using random hexamer priming and M-MuLV Reverse Transcriptase. After adapter ligation and size selection (370–420 bp), libraries were amplified with index primers and their quality was verified on the Agilent Bioanalyzer. Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform. Raw reads were quality-filtered using fastp to remove adapters, poly-N sequences, and low-quality bases. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.0.5), and transcript assembly was performed with StringTie (v1.3.3b). Raw read counts for each gene were obtained using featureCounts (v1.5.0‑p3). FPKM values were calculated only for data visualization (e.g., heatmaps). Differential expression analysis between SP and SG groups was carried out using DESeq2 (adjusted p < 0.05, |fold change| ≥ 2) with raw counts as input. Functional annotation and pathway enrichment of differentially expressed genes were performed through GO and KEGG analyses using the clusterProfiler R package, and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was applied to identify coordinated expression patterns in predefined biological pathways. The raw sequencing data have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) at the China National Center for Bioinformation (CNCB) under accession number CRA033512.

Metabolomic profiling

Jejunal tissues from the SP and SG groups were subjected to untargeted metabolomic profiling. Samples (100 mg) were ground under liquid nitrogen, homogenized in pre-chilled 80 % methanol, and incubated on ice for 5 min. After centrifugation (15,000 g, 4 °C, 20 min), the supernatant was diluted with LC-MS-grade water to a final concentration of 53 % methanol, recentrifuged under the same conditions, and the resulting supernatant was injected into the LC-MS/MS system. A pooled quality control (QC) sample, prepared by mixing equal volumes of all study samples, was injected every 10 runs to monitor instrument stability and signal drift. Solvent blanks were injected at the beginning and end of each batch. An internal standard mixture (L‑2‑chlorophenylalanine and lidocaine) was added to each sample prior to extraction to monitor extraction efficiency. Chromatographic separation was performed on a Vanquish UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher) equipped with a Hypersil Gold column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.9 μm) using a 12-min linear gradient of 0.1 % formic acid in water (eluent A) and methanol (eluent B) at a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. Mass spectrometry detection was carried out on an Orbitrap Q-Exactive HF instrument in both positive and negative ionisation modes with a spray voltage of 3.5 kV, capillary temperature of 320 °C, sheath gas flow of 35 psi, and auxiliary gas flow of 10 L/min. Raw data were processed with Compound Discoverer 3.3 for peak alignment, picking, and quantitation. Batch effects were corrected using QC‑based LOESS signal correction in metaX. Peak areas were normalized against total spectral intensity, and metabolites were annotated by matching against the mzCloud, mzVault, and MassList databases. Differential metabolites were identified based on variable importance in projection (VIP) > 1, false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 (Benjamini‑Hochberg correction), and fold change ≥ 2 or ≤ 0.5. Statistical and pathway analyses were performed using R and metaX software, with enrichment evaluated via the KEGG and HMDB databases.

Immunofluorescence of jejunal tissue

Multiplex immunohistochemical (mIHC) staining was performed on jejunal paraffin-embedded sections. After deparaffinization, rehydration, and antigen retrieval, endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol, and nonspecific binding sites were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Occludin and Claudin-1 (Affinity, DF7504, DF6919), followed by appropriate secondary antibodies. Sequential immunodetection was carried out with an antibody elution step between rounds to ensure signal specificity. In the final visualization, Occludin expression was displayed as a red signal, while Claudin-1 was shown as a yellow signal, under bright-field microscopy.

Cell culture and treatment

HD11 cells are a chicken macrophage-like cell line widely used in in vitro bacterial infection models and research on avian intestinal diseases (Bu et al., 2025, Li et al., 2024, Zhang et al., 2019). HD11 cells were purchased from Shanghai Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China, Catalog No. ZQ1193). HD11 cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM medium (Servicebio, China) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Kefusi, China) under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Subculture was performed when the monolayer reached 80–90% confluence. For the Salmonella infection model, HD11 cells were first grown to 80–90% confluence and then infected with Salmonella at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 50 for 1 h. After infection, the cells were washed three times with PBS to remove non-adherent bacteria, followed by incubation in DMEM containing 100 μg/mL gentamicin for 1 h to eliminate extracellular bacteria. Following three additional washes with PBS, the cells were maintained in DMEM with 10 μg/mL gentamicin for 4 h before proceeding to subsequent experiments.

Western blot

Protein extraction from jejunal tissues and cell samples was performed using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with 1% PMSF, followed by homogenization and centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined with a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Biosharp, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Equal amounts of protein lysates (20 µg per lane) were denatured at 95 °C for 10 min, separated on 10% SDS-PAGE gels (Bio-Rad Mini-PROTEAN Tetra), and transferred onto PVDF membranes via semi-dry blotting (80 V, 30 min). All Western blot experiments were performed with three independent biological replicates (n = 3 per group). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk in TBST (pH 7.6) for 2 h at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-NF-κB (Bioss, bs-0465R, 1:1000), anti-phospho-NF-κB (Bioss, bs-0982R, 1:1000), anti-HIF-1α (Wanlei, WL02635, 1:1000), and anti-β-actin (Abclonal, AC006, 1:10,000) as a loading control. After washing three times with TBST, membranes were probed with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Abclonal, AS038, 1:5000) for 60 min at 37 °C. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (Bio-Rad Clarity ECL) and imaged with a ChemiDoc MP system. Band intensities were quantified by densitometric analysis using ImageJ software (v1.53, NIH, USA).

Immunofluorescence assay for Salmonella detection in HD11 cells

For immunofluorescence analysis, HD11 cells were seeded on sterile coverslips in 24-well plates and cultured until reaching 70–80% confluence. Following respective treatments, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 h. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary Anti-Salmonella antibody (abcam, ab35156) diluted in blocking buffer. After three washes with PBS, samples were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with a FITC-conjugated secondary antibody (Servicerbio, GB22303) and counterstained with DAPI (Beyotime, C1006; 1 µg/mL) for 10 min to visualize nuclei. Following final washes with PBS, coverslips were mounted onto glass slides using an anti-fade mounting medium. Fluorescence images were acquired under consistent exposure settings using a confocal laser scanning microscope and analyzed with ImageJ software (v1.53, NIH, USA).

Statistics

Quantitative data are expressed as mean ± SEM. The experimental unit was the individual chick (in vivo) or the individual cell well (in vitro). Normality (Shapiro–Wilk) and homogeneity of variance (Levene) were tested before analysis. Two-group comparisons were performed using two-tailed independent Student’s t-test. Multi-group comparisons were performed by one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. For repeated-measures body weight data, a mixed-design repeated-measures ANOVA was used (between-subject factor: group; within-subject factor: time), followed by Bonferroni-adjusted post-hoc comparisons when the group × time interaction was significant. For analyses involving multiple endpoints (e.g., cytokines, gene expression, metabolites), false discovery rate (FDR; Benjamini–Hochberg) correction was applied to control the type I error rate. In all tests, two-sided p < 0.05 was considered significant, and p < 0.01 as highly significant.

Results

Notably, galangin administration alone (GAL group) had no adverse effect on body weight gain compared to the control (CON) group, indicating its safety under non-infected conditions. Upon S. Gross pathological examination revealed focal intestinal swelling, luminal gas accumulation, and wall thickening in the SP group (Fig. 1D, arrow), accompanied by a significant reduction in intestinal length (Fig. 1E, P < 0.01). Moreover, S. Pullorum infection caused marked hepatomegaly with visible congestion (Fig. 1F), along with significantly increased liver weight and liver index (Fig. 1G, P < 0.01 and P < 0.001, respectively). Splenomegaly was also observed, as evidenced by elevated spleen weight and spleen index in the SP group (Fig. 1H–I, P < 0.001). Importantly, galangin supplementation in the SG group significantly attenuated these pathological changes. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that galangin does not impair normal growth in healthy chicks and exerts protective effects against the systemic pathological manifestations of S. Pullorum infection.

Pullorum infection significantly reduced villus length and increased crypt depth, resulting in a markedly decreased villus-to-crypt ratio (Fig. 2A–D), indicative of severe mucosal damage. Immunofluorescence staining showed that S. Pullorum infection disrupted the expression and apical localization of the key tight junction proteins Occludin and Claudin-1, with fragmented or discontinuous signals at the enterocyte membrane (Fig. 2E). By comparison, the galangin-treated group exhibited strong and continuous membrane-associated staining for both proteins, suggesting enhanced barrier integrity. Quantitative analysis confirmed that the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of Occludin and Claudin-1 was significantly higher in the SG group than in the SP group (Fig. 2F).

Furthermore, bacterial translocation was assessed by culturing liver homogenates. S. Pullorum infection led to a substantial increase in bacterial load in the liver (Fig. 2G–H), whereas the SG group showed a significant reduction in colony-forming units (CFU), indicating restricted systemic dissemination.

Finally, qRT-PCR analysis demonstrated that galangin treatment downregulated pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, IFN-γ) and upregulated immunoregulatory factors (IL-22, TGF-β) in the jejunum (Fig. 2I), consistent with an anti-inflammatory and tissue-repairing response in the galangin-treated group.

Collectively, these findings indicate that galangin alleviates S. Pullorum-induced enteritis by preserving epithelial barrier integrity, limiting bacterial translocation, and modulating local immunity.

Galangin remodels jejunal metabolism during S. Pullorum infection with concomitant activation of tryptophan metabolism and enrichment of ILA

To investigate the metabolic alterations induced by galangin during S. Pullorum infection, untargeted metabolomic profiling was performed on jejunal tissues from the SP and SG groups. Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation between the SP and SG groups along both PC1 and PC2 axes (Fig. 3A), demonstrating that galangin treatment induces substantial metabolic reprogramming in the host intestinal environment. Volcano plot analysis identified differentially abundant metabolites under stringent criteria (|log₂FC| > 1, P < 0.05). In negative ion mode, a total of 976 metabolites were detected, of which 41 were significantly upregulated and 91 were downregulated in the SG group compared to SP. Compared with the SP group, chicks in the SF group exhibited significantly improved body weight gain throughout the observation period (Fig. 4B). Notably, the FMT-alone group showed a growth trajectory essentially identical to that of the control (CON) group, indicating that FMT itself has no adverse effects on the growth and development of healthy chicks. Gross pathological examination further revealed that intestinal lesions were markedly alleviated in the SF group, with better-preserved mucosal architecture, reduced congestion and edema, and normalized luminal contents (Fig. 4C). The intestinal appearance of the FMT group was also similar to that of the CON group, with no obvious abnormalities observed. Consistent with these findings, intestinal length—a key indicator of epithelial integrity—was partially restored in the SF group compared to the SP group (Fig. 4D). Intestinal length in the FMT group showed no significant difference from that of the CON group, further confirming the safety of FMT treatment. Systemic inflammatory responses were significantly attenuated in the SF group. Although SP-infected chicks exhibited hepatosplenomegaly and dark discoloration (Fig. 4E–G), the liver and spleen morphology in the SF group was close to normal. Histological analysis of jejunal tissues demonstrated that the SF group maintained well-organized villus structure, with greater villus height and shallower crypt depth (Fig. 4J–L), reflecting enhanced mucosal barrier integrity. This structural advantage was functionally manifested as reduced bacterial translocation: significantly fewer S. Pullorum colonies were recovered from liver homogenates in the SF group compared to the SP group (Fig. 4M–N). At the molecular level, immunofluorescence staining showed that the apical localization of tight junction proteins Occludin and Claudin-1 was largely preserved in the SF group (Fig. 4O), whereas it was markedly disrupted in the SP group. Pullorum infection by preserving intestinal architecture, reinforcing epithelial barrier function, alleviating systemic inflammation, and modulating mucosal immune homeostasis.

To explore potential associations between transcriptional changes and metabolic alterations, we integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic profiles. Given that tryptophan metabolism can generate multiple natural ligands for the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), we hypothesize that galangin may indirectly promote AHR signaling activation by enhancing tryptophan metabolism, thereby contributing to its intestinal protective effects.

To preliminarily assess this possibility, we examined the expression of AHR pathway-related genes in the jejunum by qRT-PCR. As shown in Fig. 5I, galangin treatment significantly upregulated AHR itself and its canonical downstream targets CYP1A2 and CYP1B1, suggesting that the AHR pathway may be activated. Discussion

In this study, galangin exerted significant protective effects against S. Pullorum infection in chicks, associated with remodeling of the gut microbiota, enrichment of tryptophan metabolism, and increased ILA. In vitro experiments confirmed that ILA activates the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR) pathway, suppressing NF‑κB‑driven inflammation and HIF‑1α‑mediated metabolic stress. Collectively, our findings outline a mechanistic framework underlying galangin’s “prebiotic-like” activity and support the development of microbiota–metabolite–host axis‑targeted, antibiotic‑free interventions in poultry health. This study demonstrates that galangin effectively maintains mucosal homeostasis in the jejunum of chicks during S. Pullorum infection. Histological and immunofluorescence analyses revealed that galangin significantly reversed infection-induced villus atrophy and crypt hyperplasia, and restored the continuous apical distribution of tight junction proteins Occludin and Claudin-1 in the intestinal epithelium, thereby reinforcing the physical barrier and reducing bacterial translocation to the liver. At the immunological level, galangin upregulated the expression of anti-inflammatory and reparative factors IL-22 and TGF-β, while suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and IL-6. Notably, IL-22 plays a pivotal role in this process: it not only promotes epithelial cell proliferation and regeneration but also directly inhibits pathogen colonization, serving as a central hub linking innate immunity and epithelial repair (Wang et al., 2025, Cineus et al., 2025). Our findings are consistent with previous reports showing that various flavonoids (e.g., quercetin, phloretin) protect the intestinal barrier by modulating tight junctions or immune mediators (Ding et al., 2025, Guan et al., 2025). However, this study is the first to explicitly link galangin’s barrier-protective effects to its modulation of a specific microbial metabolite (ILA) and the subsequent activation of the AHR/IL-22 axis, thereby deepening our understanding of its mechanism of action.

A key advance of this study is the identification of ILA as the central microbial metabolite mediating galangin’s protective effects. Metabolomic profiling revealed that galangin treatment specifically enriched the tryptophan metabolism pathway and elevated ILA levels. Notably, ILA is exclusively produced by gut bacteria from dietary tryptophan and cannot be synthesized by the host; thus, its abundance directly reflects microbial metabolic capacity. Critically, exogenous ILA supplementation in vitro recapitulated the protective effects of galangin, and these effects were completely abolished by the AHR antagonist CH-223191, establishing a model in which galangin promotes the production of microbiota-derived ILA, which in turn activates the AHR, ultimately leading to host protection. This provides evidence for galangin’s function as a “prebiotic-like” modulator. However, the specific bacterial taxa responsible for ILA production remain unidentified. Future studies employing metagenomics, culturomics, or gnotobiotic models will be needed to isolate and validate key ILA-producing strains (e.g., certain Lactobacillus or Clostridium species) (Yang et al., 2025, Yang et al., 2026), thereby bridging the gap from functional association to strain-level causality. This limitation also highlights that our current conclusions are based on shifts in overall microbial metabolic potential rather than defined single-species contributions.

Our findings reveal a unique anti-infective mechanism whereby the ILA – AHR axis simultaneously targets two critical host pathways. On one hand, AHR activation markedly suppresses NF-κB signaling, as evidenced by reduced phosphorylation of p65, thereby dampening the pro-inflammatory cytokine storm. On the other hand, AHR concurrently downregulates HIF-1α protein accumulation. This is pathophysiologically significant because S. Pullorum hijacks host HIF-1α signaling to induce a “pseudohypoxic” state even under normoxia, driving aerobic glycolysis (the Warburg effect) to fuel its intracellular replication (Zhang et al., 2019, Yu et al., 2023). By inhibiting HIF-1α in an AHR-dependent manner, ILA effectively disrupts this metabolic support system exploited by the pathogen. This explains why galangin significantly reduces intracellular S. Pullorum burden in HD11 cells. Such coordinated targeting of the inflammatory hub (NF-κB) and the metabolic hub (HIF-1α) represents a highly efficient host defense strategy. We acknowledge that the detailed mechanistic studies on AHR‑mediated suppression of NF‑κB and HIF‑1α were performed in HD11 cells. While these cells are a well‑established model for studying Salmonella‑host interactions, they do not fully recapitulate the complex cellular microenvironment of the intact intestinal epithelium, which includes enterocytes, goblet cells, Paneth cells, and various immune cells (El-Shall et al., 2026, Chappell et al., 2009). Therefore, caution should be exercised when extrapolating our in vitro findings to the in vivo intestinal tissue. Future studies using primary intestinal epithelial cells, intestinal organoids, or tissue‑specific in vivo approaches (e.g., conditional knockout of AHR in epithelial cells) are needed to confirm the regulatory interplay among AHR, NF‑κB, and HIF‑1α in the gut during S. Pullorum infection.

In the context of global efforts to reduce or eliminate antibiotic use in animal agriculture, galangin—a natural plant-derived flavonoid—holds considerable promise. Its advantages lie in its non-bactericidal mode of action (minimizing selection pressure for resistance) and its ability to enhance host resilience through barrier fortification and immune homeostasis, aligning well with the principles of sustainable and green poultry farming. Nevertheless, several challenges must be addressed before field application. First, the current study used oral gavage, which is impractical for large-scale operations; thus, stable feed-compatible formulations (e.g., microencapsulation or nano-delivery systems) and cost-effective dosing regimens need to be developed. Second, the efficacy of galangin across different poultry breeds, ages, and management systems requires further validation. Most importantly, although ILA’s central role is established, the specific ILA-producing bacterial strains have not yet been isolated, limiting the development of precision microbiome-based interventions such as next-generation probiotics. Therefore, future work should systematically evaluate the long-term safety, pharmacokinetics, and impact on production traits of galangin to enable its successful translation into practical, antibiotic-free poultry production strategies.

Conclusions

In summary, this study delineates a gut microbiota–metabolite–host signaling axis through which galangin effectively alleviates S. Pullorum–induced enteritis and systemic inflammation in chicks. Galangin modulates gut microbial metabolism, thereby elevating luminal and tissue levels of the microbial metabolite ILA. ILA activates the AHR in intestinal and immune cells, leading to suppression of NF-κB– and HIF-1α–mediated pro-inflammatory responses and glycolytic reprogramming. Consequently, Galangin preserves intestinal barrier integrity, attenuates mucosal inflammation, and restricts bacterial translocation of Salmonella within the host. The protective phenotype was fully recapitulated in recipient chicks following fecal microbiota transplantation from galangin-treated donors, providing causal evidence that galangin’s efficacy is microbiota-dependent. Thus, we conclude that galangin represents a promising antibiotic alternative that combats enteric bacterial infection in poultry by harnessing host–microbe crosstalk via the ILA–AHR axis.
