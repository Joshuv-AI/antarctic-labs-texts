Characterizing Combined Central and Peripheral Demyelination—Insights From a Multimodal Comparison With Chronic Inflammatory Demyelinating Polyneuropathy and Multiple Sclerosis
Authors: N Dubuisson, A Kassab, P Y K Van den Bergh, A F J E Vrancken, W L van der Pol, L H van den Berg, H S Goedee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsCombined central and peripheral demyelination (CCPD) is a rare dysimmune disorder sharing features with multiple sclerosis (MS) and chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP). Direct comparisons of central and peripheral diagnostic findings across these entities remain limited. We, therefore, performed a systematic study assessing nervous system involvement in CCPD, using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), nerve ultrasound (US), and nerve conduction study (NCS) and compared findings to MS and CIDP controls.MethodsWe conducted a descriptive case study including 4 CCPD patients, 13 CIDP patients, and 10 MS controls. All subjects underwent a standardized protocol of NCS and US. In addition, MS and CCPD patients also underwent brain and spinal cord MRI. Cerebrospinal fluid testing was performed in 20/27 patients.ResultsNCS revealed electrodiagnostic features suggestive of demyelination in all CCPD and CIDP patients, fulfilling the electrodiagnostic criteria for CIDP. We found motor conduction abnormalities in two MS patients, not fulfilling criteria for demyelination. US showed a similar pattern of multifocal nerve enlargement in CCPD and CIDP patients, while three MS patients also demonstrated mild proximal median nerve enlargement. Finally, CSF oligoclonal bands were only found in MS patients.DiscussionPeripheral diagnostic tools reveal strikingly similar electrophysiological and morphologic features in CCPD and CIDP, underscoring a potential overlap in their disease mechanisms and the challenges of distinguishing these entities based on peripheral nerve assessment alone. Our findings further suggest that US could serve as a potentially useful screening tool in patients with predominantly central nervous system demyelinating syndromes and suspected peripheral involvement, helping to guide subsequent electrophysiological testing.Keywords: chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP), combined central and peripheral demyelination (CCPD), demyelination, multiple sclerosis (MS), nerve sonography, plexus MRI

Introduction

Demyelinating lesions are usually limited to the central or the peripheral nervous system (CNS and PNS, respectively). Multiple sclerosis (MS) is the most common dysimmune CNS disorder, affecting the brain, spinal cord, and optic nerves heterogeneously [1]. Consequently, the clinical presentation of MS is diverse, and its diagnosis is mainly based on a combination of clinical and imaging features called the McDonald criteria [2, 3].

In contrast, chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy (CIDP) is an acquired dysimmune peripheral nerve disorder, mainly affecting the limbs [4]. CIDP diagnosis is mainly based on compatible clinical features combined with electrophysiological criteria [5]. CNS and PNS lesions may also occur simultaneously or successively, resulting in a combined central and peripheral demyelination disorder (CCPD). The clinical presentation of CCPD is heterogeneous as the degree of CNS and PNS involvement is variable. CCPD diagnosis is currently defined by acute, subacute, or chronic (weeks, respectively 1–2 or > 2 months) onset of symptoms and impairment of CNS and/or PNS, in combination with the presence of CNS and PNS lesions suggestive of demyelination shown by brain/spine magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and nerve conduction studies (NCS), respectively [6].

Imaging of peripheral nerves has been included as a supportive test in consensus criteria of CIDP [5, 7, 8]. The current studies in MS on diagnostic usefulness of ultrasound (US), however, have mainly focused on optic nerves [9]. US studies on peripheral involvement in MS and CCPD are lacking. Therefore, we aimed to systematically evaluate the characteristics of US, MRI and NCS in CCPD and compare this to MS and CIDP controls.

Methods

We performed a descriptive case study, using a multimodal approach in two tertiary neuromuscular reference centers (University Medical Center [UMCU] and Cliniques Universitaires Saint‐Luc [CUSL]); comparing clinical, electrodiagnostic, and imaging findings of consecutive patients with CCPD and random samples of CIDP and MS patients, all fulfilling the relevant diagnostic consensus criteria.

Patients

We recruited CCPD patients presenting at our outpatient clinic between March 2018 and 2020, with diagnosis based on CNS and/or PNS impairment, in combination with the presence of CNS and PNS lesions suggestive of demyelination as documented by brain/spine MRI and NCS, respectively [6]. We selected a sample of prevalent patients with MS and incident CIDP as disease controls. All MS and CIDP patients underwent the relevant routine diagnostic investigations, and fulfilled the 2017 revised McDonald criteria [2] and CIDP criteria as defined by European Academy of Neurology/Peripheral Nerve Society (EAN/PNS), respectively [5]. Patients with known other causes for peripheral neuropathy or CNS lesions were excluded. We reviewed the following clinical information in all patients: demographics and general medical history, known risk factors for neuropathy, symptoms at presentation, disease activity and treatment response.

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committees at UMCU and CUSL, and with the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its later amendments. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion in the study.

In addition to routine clinical evaluations, all enrolled patients underwent standardized clinical grading of muscle strength and sensory functions in all four limbs as reported previously [7, 8]. In summary, muscle strength was evaluated bilaterally, using MRC (Medical Research Council, UK) scale (0–5) in upper and lower limbs (shoulder abductors, elbow flexors, wrist extensors; hip flexors, knee extensors, and ankle dorsiflexors). All patients also underwent examination of sensory modalities including light‐touch, pin‐prick as well as vibration assessment; if one of these modalities was abnormal, the patient was considered as having sensory involvement. These sensory signs were also categorized as distal or proximal in the limbs. Routine laboratory investigations were performed in all CCPD patients in order to exclude other known causes of combined CNS and PNS involvement (e.g., fasting blood glucose, hemoglobin A1c [Hb1Ac], vitamin B12, folic acid, homocysteine, serum protein immunofixation, serology for human immunodeficiency virus [HIV], hepatitis C virus [HCV], Lyme disease, syphilis, ANA, ENA, ANCA, anti‐AQP‐4 [cell‐based assay], anti‐ganglioside, and anti‐sulfatide antibodies, and serum angiotensin converting enzyme [ACE]). Patients also underwent cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) testing if deemed appropriate as an add‐on diagnostic test (protein level, cell count and, in CCPD and MS, presence of oligoclonal bands). Testing for nodal antibodies was not part of our routine investigation.

Electrodiagnostic Protocol

We performed a standardized NCS protocol (Figure S1) in all enrolled patients, as published previously, on a Nicolet Viking IV EMG machine (CareFusion Japan, Tokyo, Japan) [6, 8]. In short, we evaluated motor function and sensory function in arm and leg nerves bilaterally as reported previously, after a standardized warming protocol [7]. This included evaluation of the following NCS parameters: compound muscle action potential (CMAP) amplitude, CMAP duration and area, distal motor latency, motor conduction velocity, F‐wave latency. We used previously published reference values to define NCS features compatible with demyelination [5, 7]. All NCS studies were performed by experienced neurophysiologists, HSG and ND respectively.

Nerve US Protocol

All patients underwent a standardized sonographic protocol (Figure S2) using a PureWave eL18‐4 US probe on a Philips Affiniti 70G and EPIQ Elite US machines (Philips Medical Instruments, Bothell, WA) at UMCU and CUSL, respectively. We evaluated nerve size on transverse images as cross‐sectional area (CSA), as reported previously at predetermined sites of the median nerve and brachial plexus [7, 8]. We did not use zoom magnification, while we measured nerve size within the hyperechoic rim and used previously published reference values to determine abnormal nerve size [7, 8]. All sonographic studies were performed by experienced neurophysiologists (H.S.G. and N.D.). Both raters were unaware of the MRI's results. Nerve enlargement was classified as reported previously [7, 8]:

–Prominent enlargement median nerve: CSA forearm > 10 mm2 and upper arm > 13 mm2.

Prominent enlargement median nerve: CSA forearm > 10 mm2 and upper arm > 13 mm2.

–Mild enlargement median nerve: CSA forearm > 9 to ≤ 10 mm2 and upper arm > 10 to ≤ 13 mm2.

Mild enlargement median nerve: CSA forearm > 9 to ≤ 10 mm2 and upper arm > 10 to ≤ 13 mm2.

MRI Protocol

All patients with CCPD and MS underwent a routine diagnostic MR imaging protocol including relevant axial and sagittal planes of brain (3D T1‐weighted, 3D T2‐FLAIR, 3D T2‐weighted, post‐single‐dose gadolinium‐enhanced T1‐weighted sequences) and spinal cord (T1‐weighted and proton attenuation, STIR or phase‐sensitive inversion recovery, axial T2‐ or T2*‐weighted imaging through suspicious lesions, and postcontrast gadolinium‐enhanced T1‐weighted imaging). Our local neuroradiologist systematically evaluated MRIs and rated them for presence of typical demyelinating lesions, including ovoid periventricular lesions, the central vein sign, callosal‐septal interface lesions, isolated U‐fiber lesions, juxtacortical lesions, T1 black holes, brain atrophy, and contrast‐enhancing lesions following gadolinium administration [10]. In addition, six CIDP patients also underwent plexus MRI protocol, as reported previously [11]. All MRI images were rated by experienced neuroradiologists, who were unaware of electrodiagnostic and US findings; for CIDP and CCPD cases, image interpretation was performed at the diagnostic stage based on a clinical vignette without a definitive diagnosis, whereas for MS cases, follow‐up MRI scans were reviewed with knowledge of the established diagnosis.

Statistics

We used GraphPad Prism v.8.1.2 (Graph‐Pad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) for statistical analysis. We evaluated and compared the distributions of demographic and clinical data. In addition, we deployed the chi‐square test to compare categorical variables and the one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's test to compare continuous variables. Results with p < 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

We enrolled 27 patients (4 CCPD, 13 CIDP, and 10 MS as disease controls). Their baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. A male predominance was only observed in patients with CIDP. Patients with CCPD were younger, and the disease duration was shorter than CIDP and MS controls. Clinical signs and symptoms were mostly symmetric in CCPD and CIDP, whereas asymmetry was more common in MS. More severe weakness was seen in CCPD, predominantly involving proximal upper limbs, in comparison with CIDP and MS. Sensory deficits were more prevalent in the upper limbs of MS patients and in lower limbs in CCPD. In addition, sensory symptoms were mainly symmetric in the lower limbs of CIDP and CCPD. We found oligoclonal bands in CSF samples in 80% of MS patients, but none in the CCPD or CIDP patients. CSF protein content was increased in 3/4 of both CCPD and CIDP patients. CCPD patients had higher CSF protein levels than CIDP and MS. We found no pleocytosis in any of the enrolled patients.

Nerve Conduction Studies

The electrophysiologic results are summarized in Figure 1. We found similar features compatible with demyelination in CIDP and CCPD. Furthermore, we found no NCS parameters or pattern of abnormalities that were able to reliably discriminate between CIDP and CCPD patients. In addition, two MS patients showed prolonged F‐wave latencies and mildly slowed motor conduction velocity, but did not meet the electrodiagnostic criteria for CIDP, nor the criteria for demyelination [5]. However, these F‐wave abnormalities corresponded with demyelinating abnormalities on spinal MRI.

Clinical Course and Treatment in CCPD Patients

Detailed characteristics from CCPD patients are displayed in Table 2. We found that treatment with IV immunoglobulins (IVIg) was effective in most CIDP patients. However, one CIDP patient ultimately required initiation of fituximab when IVIg and steroids were no longer effective and subsequently had a good clinical response. Two CCPD patients responded well to treatment with IVIg after initial failure on corticosteroids. The third patient required further treatment escalation with Rituximab after failure of immunoglobulins and steroids, with good clinical response and has remained clinically stable since then. The fourth patient did not respond to IVIg; steroids were not considered appropriate due to a history of psychiatric disease and no further escalation was deemed warranted as her condition has remained stable.

Discussion

Our study showed that MRI and US are important complementary imaging tools in CCPD, capable of identifying abnormalities in disorders affecting both PNS and CNS. Using NCS and US, we found multifocal nerve enlargement and electrophysiologic features of demyelination in CCPD that were strikingly similar to those observed in CIDP, underscoring both the diagnostic challenges of distinguishing these entities based on peripheral nerve assessment alone and a possible overlap in their underlying disease mechanisms. In contrast, MRI demonstrated comparable white matter abnormalities in CCPD and MS, supporting the concept that inflammatory processes in CCPD extend beyond the peripheral nerves and involve the CNS. Notably, peripheral nerve abnormalities on NCS and US were observed in 20% and 30% of MS controls, respectively. These abnormalities were mild, consisting of moderate nerve enlargement, modest prolongation of F‐wave latencies, and slight reductions in motor conduction velocity. Taken together, these findings suggest that subclinical PNS involvement may occur, possibly in a subset of MS patients. Larger cohort studies could help to further support this, as peripheral nerve assessment is not routinely performed in MS and subtle PNS abnormalities may therefore remain unrecognized.

Previous MRI studies in CCPD reported CNS lesions being unclassifiable and idiopathic inflammatory demyelinating, or corresponding to the more characteristic MS‐type lesions with a periventricular, subcortical and spinal predominance [6, 12]. We found similar distribution of white matter lesions in MS controls and CCDP, all considered to represent CNS demyelination. In contrast to a previous report, we did not find spinal contrast enhancement (myelin, cauda and/or nerve roots) in our patients [12]. Possible explanations for this discrepancy include different timing of the spinal imaging, dosing of gadolinium contrast, qualitative rating and lack of objective criteria for abnormal enhancement [12, 13, 14].

In line with previous studies, we found that CCPD patients exhibited electrodiagnostic features indistinguishable from CIDP [15], all fulfilling the CIDP electrodiagnostic criteria [5]. This further supports the notion that NCS alone cannot reliably differentiate CCPD from CIDP when peripheral involvement is present. In contrast, the infrequent occurrence of exclusively axonal polyneuropathy has been reported previously in up to 6% of MS patients [16]. In line with this, only a few of our MS controls demonstrated equivocal electrodiagnostic abnormalities, indicating that peripheral nerve function is relatively spared in MS. In contrast, up to 1/3 of MS controls in our study showed mild multifocal nerve enlargement of arm nerves and brachial plexus, suggesting that morphologic abnormalities beyond CNS may be more common but could remain subclinical.

These findings also have practical diagnostic implications; in patients with suspected MS presenting with predominantly CNS syndromes and clinical doubt regarding peripheral nerve involvement, nerve US could serve as a useful, non‐invasive first‐line screening tool to detect morphologic nerve abnormalities and guide the subsequent use of more extensive electrophysiological testing.

Our results also confirm that US is more sensitive than plexus MRI in demonstrating abnormalities in dysimmune neuropathies, as even state of the art plexus MRI was considered normal in CIDP patients with abnormal nerve root sizes on US [17]. The lack of objective cut‐off for abnormal MRI and need for neuroradiological expertise further limits the use of MRI in routine practice [18].

While our findings show overlapping imaging and electrophysiological features between CCPD and CIDP and occasional subclinical findings in MS, further studies are needed to determine whether these reflect true biological overlap. The causal mechanisms of CIDP and MS have been studied extensively. However, no clear pathophysiological process has yet been identified for CCPD. Although a study suggested a possible genetic contribution, CCPD is commonly considered to have an acquired autoimmune origin [19]. Interestingly, previous proteomic studies showed a similar myelin lipid composition in both the CNS and PNS [20] and the axo‐myelin interaction has been shown to be affected in MS as well as in CIDP [21]. Therefore, future studies that address the periaxonal region may further improve our understanding of these entities [22, 23]. We also found increased CSF protein in the majority of CCPD and CIDP patients, which could be compatible with proximal (nerve root) inflammation [24].

Taken together, our data indicate possible shared pathophysiological mechanisms, but further studies are warranted to determine whether CIDP, MS, and CCPD belong to a spectrum of demyelinating disorders affecting CNS and/or PNS.

Our study has several limitations, including a small number of CCPD patients. The absence of specific antibody testing prevents us from exploring potential immunological biomarkers or mechanistic differences between the disease groups. Our CCPD patients and most of the CIDP controls were treatment‐naive at initial investigation. In contrast, nearly all MS patients were on active treatment, with 30% on natalizumab. However, in a previous multicenter study we did not find a treatment effect on nerve size in CIDP [25], and therefore suspect that this is unlikely to be different in MS. Moreover, we found nerve enlargement in 1/3 MS patients, which is higher than previously reported in NCS studies in MS, indicating that our detection rates are unlikely to be affected by treatment. However, the small sample size warrants caution in interpretation, and these findings should be considered exploratory.

In addition, no standardized brain and spine MRI was performed in CIDP patients, so we cannot exclude possible subclinical CNS abnormalities in CIDP.

In summary, our study demonstrates that combined imaging of the CNS and PNS using MRI, US, and NCS provides complementary diagnostic information in patients with CCPD. While MRI images in CCPD are comparable with MS, and peripheral nerve abnormalities are similar to those observed in CIDP, our findings highlight the diagnostic complexity of CCPD and suggest overlapping features of both central and peripheral demyelinating disorders. Although the small sample size limits definitive interpretation, our results support the role of US as a possibly useful adjunctive tool in the diagnostic evaluation of CCPD. Further studies with larger cohorts are warranted to clarify the clinical relevance of peripheral nerve abnormalities in MS and to explore the potential utility of US in a broader spectrum of dysimmune demyelinating disorders.
