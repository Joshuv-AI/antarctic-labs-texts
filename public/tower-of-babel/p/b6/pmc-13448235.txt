Endovascular Management of Inferior Vena Cava–Right Atrial Luminal Narrowing Following Orthotopic Heart Transplantation: A Case Report
Authors: Dong Hee Lee, Chang Ho Jeon, Suk Min Seo, Joon Kyu Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a rare case of early postoperative inferior vena cava–right atrial (IVC–RA) narrowing following bicaval orthotopic heart transplantation in a 68-year-old woman. Six days after transplantation, the patient developed severe venous congestion and multi-organ dysfunction. Mechanical obstruction at the IVC–RA junction, rather than intrinsic stenosis, was diagnosed using hepatic Doppler ultrasonography and venography. Due to the high risk associated with surgical reintervention, urgent endovascular treatment was performed. A self-expanding stent was deployed using a bidirectional approach via the femoral and jugular veins to overcome anatomical distortion. The procedure immediately restored venous flow and reversed organ dysfunction. This case highlights that mechanical IVC–RA distortion can cause life-threatening obstruction, and that prompt endovascular correction can serve as a safe, minimally invasive alternative to surgery.

INTRODUCTION

In orthotopic heart transplantation, the bicaval technique typically yields better atrial geometry and valve competence than the classical biatrial approach, although rare complications can occur at the caval suture lines. Inferior vena cava–right atrial (IVC–RA) outflow obstruction is a rare complication of orthotopic heart transplantation, with most anastomotic lumens measuring approximately 2 cm in diameter, comparable to that of the native IVC (1234). Early postoperative narrowing generally arises from mechanical kinking or compression associated with graft position or local edema rather than intrinsic stenosis. However, re-sternotomy in the immediate post-transplantation period carries a prohibitive risk. In this context, percutaneous endovascular therapy represents a definitive and minimally invasive alternative.

CASE REPORT

A 68-year-old woman underwent deceased-donor bicaval orthotopic heart transplantation for end-stage dilated cardiomyopathy and demonstrated excellent early postoperative graft function. However, on postoperative day six, the patient developed progressive oliguria, increased creatinine and bilirubin levels, and marked fluid sensitivity to hypotension. Despite inotropes and vasopressin, the patient’s condition remained unstable. Systemic vasoplegia was suspected, and intensive care management was continued. Cross-sectional imaging was not feasible during this period because the patient’s severe hemodynamic instability precluded safe transportation to the CT suite. On postoperative day 10, hepatic Doppler ultrasonography showed preserved arterial inflow but no hepatic or portal venous flow (Fig. 1A). Venacavography revealed near-occlusion at the IVC–RA junction with reflux into the hepatic veins, consistent with mechanical distortion (Fig. 1B).

Urgent endovascular treatment was performed under combined fluoroscopic and ultrasonographic guidance using an Artis-Q Cardiac angiography system (Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Once right femoral and internal jugular venous access had been obtained, a 0.035-inch hydrophilic guidewire was advanced from the femoral sheath and repeatedly deflected leftward toward the right ventricle due to the acutely angled compressed segment. Subsequent superior access attempts were unsuccessful. Thus, the wire was advanced into the right atrium from below; however, it was unable to reach the superior vena cava. A Gooseneck 15-mm snare (EV3, Plymouth, MN, USA), introduced from the jugular sheath, then captured the wire tip within the right atrium and was withdrawn into the superior vena cava, establishing a through-and-through rail (Fig. 2A). After confirming intraluminal positioning, low-pressure balloon predilatation using a Mustang 5 × 8 mm balloon catheter (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA, USA) demonstrated elastic recoil, indicative of extrinsic compression.

To preserve hepatic venous drainage, a safety wire was inserted from the right atrium to the hepatic vein to allow placement of an additional stent if hepatic outflow worsened (Fig. 2B). A Venovo 16 × 60 mm self-expanding nitinol venous stent (BD, Tempe, AZ, USA) was placed across the distorted segment from the IVC into the right atrium (Fig. 2C). Post-dilatation with a Mustang 10 × 40 mm balloon restored luminal patency with brisk antegrade flow and no residual reflux (Fig. 2D). As a result, hemodynamics stabilized immediately, urine output increased within hours, and renal and hepatic parameters normalized within three days. Follow-up Doppler imaging demonstrated restoration of triphasic hepatic venous flow and hepatopetal portal flow (Fig. 3). At the six-month follow-up, CT and Doppler imaging confirmed durable patency and normal venous drainage.

The need for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective and anonymous nature of this case report.

DISCUSSION

This case illustrates an uncommon but clinically significant form of venous outflow obstruction following bicaval orthotopic heart transplantation. Although the IVC–RA anastomosis is typically constructed with a luminal diameter comparable to that of the native IVC (12), early postoperative narrowing seldom represents true fibrotic stenosis. Instead, it arises from mechanical kinking or compression of the caval conduit due to the graft position or surrounding perianastomotic edema (345). True anastomotic stenosis is rarely reported (6), whereas narrowing due to graft malposition or edema has been described as a potentially critical phenomenon (45). Recognizing this mechanism is crucial, as its presentation can mimic low cardiac output or vasoplegia, leading to delayed diagnosis. Even when standard surgical precautions, including pericardial fixation, are employed to maintain proper graft alignment, mechanical distortion at the IVC–RA junction may still occur due to perianastomotic edema or compression at the caval anastomosis.

Surgical reintervention is nearly impossible in the immediate post-transplantation period. Re-sternotomy carries prohibitive risks, including massive bleeding and graft injury. Furthermore, a second caval anastomosis cannot be created without another donor heart, which is anatomically and ethically infeasible. As a result, endovascular management represents the most viable method to restore venous inflow (78). However, this approach presents unique technical challenges. The IVC–RA segment is not a native straight conduit but a surgically created and often tortuous pathway distorted by edema and cardiac rotation. To overcome these difficulties, a dual-access strategy using the femoral and jugular veins was employed. This through-and-through configuration facilitated simultaneous control in both directions.

Moreover, simple balloon dilation provides only temporary relief, with a high rate of restenosis (9). To overcome this limitation, stenting is required to maintain long-term luminal patency (10). In this case, a self-expanding nitinol venous stent was selected because of its high radial strength and flexibility, which enabled effective remodeling of the kinked segment. Because the IVC–RA junction lies immediately inferior to the hepatic vein confluence, stent deployment could exacerbate hepatic congestion. To mitigate this risk, a safety wire was placed from the right atrium to the hepatic vein to ensure immediate bailout capability without additional access. When a relatively long portion of the stent extends into the right atrium, potential complications include in-stent thrombosis and tricuspid valve interference. In the present case, systemic anticoagulation was initiated immediately after stent deployment, and six-month follow-up confirmed durable patency without evidence of thrombosis or tricuspid valve dysfunction.

After the intervention, the patient’s hemodynamic status improved dramatically. Understanding that early IVC–RA narrowing commonly reflects mechanical kinking or external compression rather than fibrotic stenosis may prevent unnecessary surgical exploration and facilitate timely percutaneous management before irreversible organ injury. This case highlights that an accurate venographic diagnosis and endovascular stent reconstruction can safely restore venous drainage during early IVC–RA narrowing caused by postoperative kinking or perianastomotic compression after heart transplantation.
