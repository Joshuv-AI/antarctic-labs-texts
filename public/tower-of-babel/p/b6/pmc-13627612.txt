Association between Kaplan fiber injury and anterolateral tibial subluxation in patients with anterior cruciate ligament injury
Authors: Bailin Pan, Shangmin Chen, Yuanying Lin, Du Lin, Zhaohan Zhou, Bendan Lin, Laixi Zhou, Jinjin Ma, Xiaozhuan Wang, Chuangxin Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundKaplan fibers (KFs) are critical stabilizers of the knee, yet their role in anterior tibial subluxation of the lateral compartment (ATSLC) in anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury remains unclear. This study is to determine whether KF injury is independently associated with increased ATSLC on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in patients with an injured ACL, and to identify risk factors for high-grade ATSLC (≥ 4.5 mm).Patients and methodsThis retrospective study enrolled 132 patients with ACL injury who underwent MRI within 12 weeks of trauma. MRI was used to evaluate the integrity of the ACL, KFs, anterolateral ligament (ALL), bony morphology, and ATSLC. Two trained observers independently assessed all images using standardized criteria. Agreement between observers was calculated using standard reliability metrics. Multivariable linear regression was performed to evaluate the independent association between KF injury and ATSLC. Logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic analysis were used to identify predictors of high-grade anterior tibial subluxation (≥ 4.5 mm) in patients with combined ACL and anterolateral complex injuries.ResultsOf the 132 patients, 35.6% had KF injuries. These patients exhibited higher rates of ALL injury and distinct bony morphologic features. KF injury independently explained 15.1% of anterior tibial subluxation variability (P < 0.001). In patients with combined anterolateral complex injuries, increased lateral femoral condyle width ratio was identified as an independent predictor of high-grade subluxation.Data conclusionKF injury is independently associated with greater anterior tibial subluxation, suggesting a key role in post-traumatic knee instability. Specific femoral anatomical feature may further predict high-risk cases.Level of evidenceLevel IV.

Introduction

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury is one of the most prevalent sports-related knee injuries, with a rising global incidence [1, 2]. It significantly compromises joint stability and predisposes patients to cartilage degeneration and post-traumatic osteoarthritis [3, 4]. Although anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction (ACLR) is the standard treatment, many patients suffer from graft failure, particularly in cases with anterolateral rotatory instability (ALRI), where isolated ACLR may be insufficient [5–7]. This highlights the importance of the anterolateral stabilizers of the knee.

The anterolateral complex (ALC) of the knee consists of the superficial and deep layers of the iliotibial band, including the Kaplan fibers (KFs), as well as the iliopatellar band, anterolateral ligament (ALL), and joint capsule [8]. As secondary stabilizers of the ACL, these structures play a vital role in resisting internal tibial rotation during knee flexion. Moreover, biomechanical evidence suggests that KFs contribute more to rotational restraint than the ALL, especially at flexion angles beyond 30° [6, 9].

In recent years, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has enabled reliable visualization of KFs, with identification rates ranging from 93.4% to 100% using standard imaging sequences [10–12]. Despite evolving diagnostic criteria, KF injuries have been reported in 6–61% of ACL-injured knees, suggesting they may be more frequently affected than previously recognized [11, 13]. However, the clinical implications of KF injury, especially its potential independent contributions to knee instability, remain underexplored.

Anterior tibial subluxation of the lateral compartment (ATSLC), measured on MRI, has emerged as a promising static imaging marker of knee instability following ACL injury. An increased ATSLC is closely associated with higher degrees of rotatory instability and may persist even after ACLR, negatively affecting surgical outcomes [14–16]. While prior studies have identified ALL and lateral meniscus posterior root injuries as independent contributors to elevated ATSLC [17, 18], the specific role of KF injury in this context has yet to be clearly defined. Considering the biomechanical role of KFs in controlling internal rotation, it is reasonable to hypothesize that damage to these structures may contribute to abnormal tibiofemoral relationship.

Therefore, the present study was designed to investigate whether KF injury is independently associated with increased ATSLC in patients with an injured ACL, and to identify risk factors for high-grade ATSLC (≥ 4.5 mm) in those with combined ACL and ALC injuries.

Patients and methods

Patient selection

This retrospective study included patients with an injured ACL who underwent primary ACLR at a single medical center between January 2020 and December 2023. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age 14–55 years, (2) MRI performed within 12 weeks post-injury at our institution, (3) MRI-confirmed partial or complete ACL injury, (4) complete clinical data, and (5) arthroscopically confirmed ACL injury. Patients were excluded if they had any of the following: (1) prior fractures or ligament injuries (n = 48), (2) joint infection (n = 2), (3) previous surgery (n = 19), (4) MRI evidence of tears involving the popliteus tendon (PT), biceps femoris tendon (BFT), or popliteofibular ligament (PFL) (n = 28), or (5) inadequate MRI image quality (n = 121). After applying these criteria, a total of 132 patients were included in the final analysis. The study was approved by the institutional ethics board, and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Baseline data collection

Baseline clinical variables included age, sex, side of injury, and the time interval from injury to MRI evaluation.

MRI evaluation and measurements

MRI examinations were performed on 3.0-T (Verio, Siemens Healthineers) or 1.5-T (Aera, Siemens Healthineers) scanners following a standardized institutional protocol, which included sagittal T1-weighted sequences and proton-density-weighted sequences in coronal, sagittal, and axial planes. Imaging parameters were at or close to the following ranges: repetition time (TR), 500–3500 ms; echo time (TE), 19–39 ms; field of view, 15–18 cm; slice thickness, 3.0–4.0 mm with an interslice gap of 0.3–1.2 mm; and number of excitations, 1. Parallel imaging was applied when available (acceleration factor up to 2). Typical acquisitions consisted of 23–25 slices. Patients were positioned supine with the knee relaxed in a natural flexion of 10–15°, and secured in a dedicated knee coil.

A musculoskeletal radiologist and an orthopedic surgeon (Z.Z. and P.B., 6 years and 3 years of experience) independently evaluated all images, blinded to clinical information. Both had prior training to standardize assessment of structures such as KFs and ALL. In cases of disagreement, a senior radiologist (L.B., 32 years of experience) conducted an independent review after 2 weeks.

ACL status was categorized as partial or complete rupture. Meniscal injuries and collateral ligaments injuries were recorded by location, as were associated injuries of the posterior cruciate ligament (PCL). The status of the KFs was evaluated according to the diagnostic criteria proposed by Batty et al. [19], with the same the threshold applied for images obtained from both 1.5-T and 3.0-T scanners. As summarized in Table 1, KF injury was diagnosed if ≥ 1 direct sign or ≥ 2 indirect signs appeared on any plane. Representative MRI findings of KF injury are shown in Fig. 1. ALL injury was defined per Helito et al. [20] as fiber discontinuity or surrounding edema with irregular contour. ATSLC was measured according to the method described by McDonald et al. [22]. Iwaki et al. initially described this electronic measurement technique on cadaveric knee specimens and validated the accuracy of MRI as a tool for measuring knee kinematic parameters [23]. Later, Tanaka et al. began using this MRI technique to assess the static tibiofemoral relationship [24]. Results obtained using this method have demonstrated excellent inter- and intra-observer reliability in several studies [14, 16, 18]. To ensure consistency in the lateral compartment, measurements were taken using standard reference points for each compartment. On sagittal slices at the most medial aspect of the tibiofibular joint, the distance between the LTP and the LFC was recorded. Positive values indicated anterior subluxation, while negative values indicated posterior displacement. Measurement precision was set at 0.01 mm (Fig. 4b).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software (version 25.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent-samples t-tests; non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as counts (percentages) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Ordinal variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

A multivariable linear regression model was used to examine the association between KF injury and ATSLC. Prior studies have shown that meniscal injury and ALL injury are associated with anterolateral tibial subluxation [17, 18], suggesting that these structural injuries and anatomic variations may confound the association of our interest. To account for potential confounding factors, selected variables were entered into the regression model in a hierarchical fashion. The change in R2 and significance of variables across models were calculated to evaluate the independent contribution of KF injury to ATSLC.

Potential risk factors for high-grade ATSLC (≥ 4.5 mm) were identified through univariate analysis followed by multivariable logistic regression (P < 0.20 for entry). Odds ratios (OR), 95% confidence intervals (CI), and P-values are reported. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, including area under the curve (AUC), optimal cutoff (Youden index), and corresponding sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). AUC values were interpreted according to Mandrekar’s criteria [25]. Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with VIFs < 10 indicating no concern. A two-sided P-value ≤ 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Interobserver agreement for KFs and anterolateral ligament assessments was assessed using Cohen’s kappa according to established guidelines [26]. Measurement reliability for continuous variables was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) following recommended standards [27].

A total of 132 patients with ACL injury were enrolled, including 96 men and 36 women, with an average age of 31.1 ± 10.7 years. Left knee injuries were observed in 79 patients, and right knee injuries in 53 patients. The time interval from injury to MRI evaluation was 3.9 ± 3.9 weeks on average. Fifteen patients had partial ACL injuries, while 117 had complete ACL ruptures.

Association between KF status and ATSLC

Hierarchical linear regression analysis showed that model 1 (confounders only) explained 24.6% of ATSLC variance (R2 = 0.246, P < 0.001). Adding KF injury (model 2) increased the R2 to 0.397, reflecting an additional 15.1% variance explained. KF injury was associated with a mean ATSLC increase of 3.367 mm (95% CI 2.124–4.611, P < 0.001). The time interval from injury to MRI (P = 0.012) and a smaller LTP depth (P = 0.031) were also identified as significant predictors. After adjusting for KF injury, variables such as ALL injury and LFC width ratio were no longer significant. Risk factors for high-grade ATSLC in ACL + ALC injury patients

Given the close relationship between KF and ALL injuries, patients with either lesion were classified as ACL + ALC injury (n = 98). These patients were further stratified into a high-grade ATSLC group (ATSLC ≥ 4.5 mm, n = 44) and a low-grade ATSLC group (ATSLC < 4.5 mm, n = 54) for subsequent analyses.

Univariate analysis identified sex (P = 0.111), medial collateral ligament (MCL) injury (P = 0.111), LFC width ratio (P = 0.008), and LTS (P = 0.067) as candidate variables (Table 7). Multivariable regression analysis showed that LFC width ratio (OR = 1.281; 95% CI 1.035–1.585; P = 0.023) was the only independent risk factor. Discussion

MRI remains the primary noninvasive tool for evaluating anterolateral structures, and thus its diagnostic accuracy and reliability are critical. A study has shown that routine MRI has been reported to detect ALL injury with high sensitivity and specificity (88% and 100%, respectively) [28], and Berthold et al. confirmed the accurate detection of KF injuries on MRI in a cadaveric study [29]. However, other available evidence remains limited. In this study, KF and ALL injuries were evaluated using standardized diagnostic criteria, which have demonstrated excellent interobserver agreement in previous studies as well as in our cohort [19, 20], supporting the reliability of our evaluation of anterolateral structures injuries.

Prior study has demonstrated that KFs can be reliably identified on routine MRI, with no significant difference in injury detection rates between 1.5-T and 3.0-T images when using the same criteria [11]. And the interval from injury to MRI has been recognized as an important factor affecting diagnosis of anterolateral structure injuries [30, 31]. Previous studies have suggested that the detection rate of KF and ALL injuries may decrease as the time from injury increases, possibly owing to varying degrees of healing [12, 32]. To maintain adequate diagnostic sensitivity and reduce heterogeneity, only patients who underwent MRI within 12 weeks after injury were included in the present study, which is consistent with prior studies of anterolateral structure injuries [11, 33]. Moreover, baseline comparisons showed no significant difference in the injury to MRI interval between patients with and without KF injury, suggesting that the potential influence of this factor on the reliability of our findings may be limited.

We found no significant associations between KF injury and PCL, meniscal, or collateral ligament injuries. Previous studies have reported varying results, highlighting the complex injury mechanisms of the knee. Batty et al. and Devitt et al. respectively found a higher incidence of lateral meniscal injury in patients with combined KF injury [19, 34]. In our study, although patients with combined KF injury had higher rates of lateral meniscus and its anterior and posterior horn injuries, no statistical significance was observed, consistent with the findings of Lynch et al. and Watanabe et al. [11, 35] Balendra et al. reported a correlation between KF injury and MCL injury [36], while another study revealed that KF injury is associated with increased risk of both MCL and lateral collateral ligament (LCL) injuries [19]. Although our study also showed a similar trend, it did not reach statistical significance. However, our finding of a higher ALL injury rate in the ACL + KF group reinforces the close anatomical and functional relationship within the ALC, consistent with ALL’s recognized role in anterolateral stability [37–39].

Prior studies have linked similar morphological features to ACL injury risk and reconstruction outcomes, suggesting altered tibiofemoral kinematics and increased rotational laxity as potential mechanisms [40–42]. On the basis of MRI measurements, our study suggests that larger LFC depth, height, and width ratios may be associated with a higher risk of KF injury. However, given the limited research on the correlation between bone morphology and KF injury, this relationship lacks biomechanical evidence for support. While previous studies have proposed that femoral condyle depth may be linked to altered loading patterns of anterolateral structures such as the ALL [43], further high-quality cadaveric studies are needed to validate the potential mechanisms underlying KF injury patterns.

The key finding of this study is that KF injury is an independent risk factor for increased ATSLC in patients with an injured ACL, accounting for 15.1% of ATSLC variability. While cadaveric studies show that KFs provide stronger restraint against tibial rotation than the ALL [6, 44], their complete transection models may not reflect clinical KF injury patterns. By using ATSLC as a static MRI marker of rotational instability, our study provides in vivo imaging evidence of the association between KF injury and knee instability. Recent studies support this association: KF injury has been linked to greater tibial rotation and high-grade pivot shift in ACL-deficient patients [38, 45].

Importantly, after adjusting for KF injury, the association between ALL injury and ATSLC disappeared, suggesting that KFs may be the dominant contributor to ALC-related instability. Notably, prior studies linking ALL injury to ATSLC did not account for KF injury [17]. Accordingly, we analyzed KFs and ALL as a combined ALC injury to better capture their collective role.

Since posterior–lateral structures (PLS) injury is commonly observed in the ACL-injured population and is considered to play a significant role in knee instability [46–48], we aimed to minimize the potential confounding effect of these structures on our causal inference of ATSLC. After excluding PT, BFT, and PFL injuries, we included LCL injury, another component of PLS, as a confounder in the ATSLC regression model. The results indicated that, even after accounting for LCL injury, the correlation between KF injury and ATSLC remained significant. Moreover, in our model, longer time from injury to MRI was significantly associated with increased ATSLC, consistent with findings from previous studies [49]. Although our study suggests a potential relationship between LTP depth and ATSLC, the supporting evidence remains limited.

We found that greater LFC width ratio emerged as an independent predictor of high-grade ATSLC in ACL + ALC injuries, indicating potential anatomical correlates of knee instability. LFC morphology may alter tibiofemoral kinematics and contribute to increased pivot shift [50]. Zheng et al. found that increased LFC depth and decreased LFC height are associated with a higher risk of ATSLC [17]. However, research on the relationship between LFC width and knee stability remains limited. In addition, the cutoff value of the LFC width ratio (27.5%) was calculated using the Youden index. Although this threshold demonstrated promising sensitivity (0.932) and negative predictive value (0.885) in our cohort, the limited specificity and predictive performance indicate that this threshold may lead to a higher risk of overdiagnosis. Therefore, it should be interpreted with caution. More importantly, this threshold should be regarded as an exploratory result for high-grade ATSLC risk factors, as it has not been validated in any external cohorts and thus cannot provide reference value for clinical practice. Future studies still need to verify the reliability of this threshold in larger and more diverse cohorts. Moreover, while the relationship between LTS and knee injury has been widely reported [51–53], Song et al. also identified increased tibial slope as an independent risk factor for ATSLC > 6 mm [54]. In our multivariable model, the impact of LTS on high-grade ATSLC was not statistically significant.

This study has several limitations. (1) All MRI data were obtained at a single center using an institution scanning protocol. Although this protocol reflects routine clinical practice, differences in sequence settings and acquisition parameters among different institutions may affect structural visualization and quantitative measurements; therefore, our findings should be interpreted with caution when extrapolated to other imaging protocols. (2) Anterolateral structure injuries were assessed using MRI as a single detective tool, without confirmation from other imaging techniques or direct visualization, which may introduce measurement-related bias. Interpretation may be influenced by partial volume effects, particularly in individuals with relatively thin KFs or in the presence of surrounding edema. (3) Evaluation of KF injury using MRI inevitably involves subjectivity; however, independent assessment conducted by two observers and reporting of interobserver agreement were used to mitigate this bias. (4) Meniscal root integrity was not systematically evaluated owing to the absence of arthroscopic probing evidence. As lateral meniscus posterior root lesions have been reported as an independent contributor to increased ATSLC, the absence of this variable may introduce confounding factors into our research. This should be taken into account when interpreting our findings. (5) The findings of this study were based on an ACL injury cohort from a single center, which limits external validity. Moreover, limited clinical characteristics may reduce comparability with other studies. More comprehensive clinical information (e.g., activity level, sport involvement, and body mass index (BMI)), and multicenter ACL injury cohorts are warranted in further studies.

Conclusions

On the basis of single-center data, the incidence of KF injury within 12 weeks of ACL injury was 35.6%, and it was closely associated with ALL injury and LFC morphology. KF injury was independently associated with increased ATSLC, a marker of knee instability. Finally, in ACL + ALC injuries, greater LFC width ratio was identified as a potential risk factor for high-grade ATSLC.
