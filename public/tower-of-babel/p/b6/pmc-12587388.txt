Bile Acid-based Microcapsule-Like Cocrystals of Phytosterols with Enhanced Solubility, Bioavailability, and Bioactivity
Authors: Lei Tang, Yinan Feng, Yifei Bian, Zhixiong Sun, Qiaoce Ding, Dongying Chen, Jian-Rong Wang, Xuefeng Mei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Phytosterols are a class of natural steroids found in various plants. Commercially available phytosterols (PS) are primarily extracted from the deodorized distillate of soybean oil and consist predominantly of β-sitosterol with smaller amounts of stigmasterol and campesterol. Numerous studies have consistently demonstrated the significant lipid-lowering activity of PS. However, the application of PS is limited due to their low oral bioavailability. To enhance the water solubility and in turn the absorption of PS at the molecular level, microcapsule-like cocrystals were designed using bile acids via hydrogen bonding. As expected, four microcapsule-like cocrystals were successfully prepared: β-sitosterol with cholic acid (CA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), and PS with CA and CDCA. The cocrystal of PS with CA (PS–CA) was selected for further investigation, and the results demonstrated that PS–CA exhibited significantly enhanced solubility in vitro and markedly improved oral bioavailability in vivo. Specifically, the AUC1–24 h of PS–CA was 6 times that of PS, while the C max was 4 times. Moreover, as a result of the improved bioavailability of PS, PS–CA showed superior lipid-lowering activity.

Introduction

Phytosterols are a class of natural steroids found in a wide range of plants. As essential natural sterols, phytosterols exhibit various biological activities, including lowering blood cholesterol levels, inhibiting cancer cell proliferation, modulating immune responses, and alleviating symptoms of prostatic hyperplasia. Commercially available phytosterols (PS) are primarily extracted from the deodorized distillate of soybean oil and consist predominantly of β-sitosterol, with smaller amounts of stigmasterol and campesterol (Figure ).

Chemical structures of β-sitosterol, stigmasterol, campesterol, cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid.

In recent years, the cholesterol-lowering effect of PS has been extensively validated through both mechanistic studies and clinical applications. Regarding the mechanism, first, cholesterol absorption occurs in the intestinal lumen where it forms micelles with bile acids. PS, due to their hydrophobic properties, compete with cholesterol for incorporation into these micelles, displacing cholesterol and thereby reducing its absorption. Second, PS suppress the activity of 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase, which is the rate-limiting enzyme in cholesterol synthesis, thereby reducing endogenous cholesterol production. The efficacy has also been substantiated through a series of clinical trials. In a clinical study involving 38 patients diagnosed with nonalcoholic fatty liver disease, the daily administration of 1.6 g PS for 8 weeks resulted in a significant reduction of low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels by 17% (p < 0.001). In a comparative clinical trial including rosuvastatin, fish oil, cinnamon, allicin, curcumin, PS and red yeast rice, PS demonstrated the most significant efficacy in reducing total cholesterol levels among the natural active ingredients. Moreover, combining PS with ezetimibe (Zetia) showed enhanced efficacy in lowering lipids, while coadministration of PS and fish oil resulted in improved therapeutic outcomes for treating hepatic steatosis.

However, the bioavailability of PS is extremely low due to their poor solubility in both water and oil. Therefore, the absolute bioavailability of their principal component, β-sitosterol, was reported to be only 0.14%, which significantly hinders their broad clinical application. Various strategies, including microencapsulation, phytosterol nanoparticles, and esterification, have been employed to improve PS solubility and thereby enhance bioavailability. Among these, esterification is the most commonly employed technique. However, this method also introduces certain limitations, such as potential safety and efficacy concerns arising from modifications to the chemical structures of PS, and decreased stability due to a possible reduction in the melting point. Recently, cocrystallization technology has garnered significant attention as a promising avenue for modulating drug properties. A cocrystal is a crystalline material that encompasses two or more distinct molecules within the same crystal lattice, typically involving an active pharmaceutical ingredient (API) and a cocrystal former (CCF). The formation of cocrystals has been extensively investigated and demonstrated to significantly enhance the in vitro and in vivo properties of APIs, such as solubility, chemical stability, and bioavailability.

To enhance the water solubility and in turn the absorption of PS at the molecular level, microcapsule-like cocrystals were designed using bile acids via hydrogen bonding. As expected, four microcapsule-like cocrystals were successfully prepared: β-sitosterol with cholic acid (CA) and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA), and PS with CA and CDCA.

Materials and Methods

Materials

Phytosterols (PS, purity 98.4%) were purchased from Shaanxi Jiahe Phytochem Co., Ltd. β-Sitosterol (SIT, purity 95%) was obtained from Shaanxi Xupeptide Biological Technology Co., Ltd. Campesterol (CAM, purity 98%) was acquired from TargetMol Chemicals Inc., Shanghai, China. Stigmasterol (STI, purity 90%), cholic acid (CA, purity 95%), and chenodeoxycholic acid (CDCA, purity 95%) were obtained from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Fish oil was procured from Guangdong Byhealth Co., Ltd., and curcumin was obtained from Beijing InnoChem Science & Technology Co., Ltd. 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) tissue fixative was purchased from Beijing Lanjie Ke Technology Co., Ltd. The rats were purchased from Beijing Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. and the license number is SCXK 2024-0003.

Buffers with pH 2.0, 4.5, and 6.8 were prepared as follows: the pH 2.0 buffer was prepared by diluting concentrated hydrochloric acid; the pH 4.5 buffer was formulated using a mixture of citric acid monohydrate and sodium phosphate; and the pH 6.8 buffer was prepared using sodium dihydrogen phosphate and dibasic sodium phosphate. Materials for preparing the dissolution medium, including sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. All solvents utilized in the study were of analytical grade and were also sourced from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China, without further purification. Ultrapure water was generated using a Milli-Q Ultrapure purification system (Millipore, USA).

Preparation of Cocrystals

The vacuum rotary evaporation method was employed to prepare the cocrystal of β-sitosterol and cholic acid (SIT–CA), and the cocrystal of PS and cholic acid (PS–CA). Specifically, 1 mmol of SIT and an equivalent amount of CA (1 mmol) were accurately weighed and fully dissolved in 100 mL of acetone. The solution was then subjected to rotary evaporation at a speed of 90 rpm in a water bath maintained at 55 °C until complete solvent removal. The resulting material was thoroughly dried and subsequently characterized by PXRD. The preparation of the PS–CA cocrystal followed the same procedure.

The cocrystal of β-sitosterol and chenodeoxycholic acid (SIT–CDCA), and the cocrystal of PS and chenodeoxycholic acid (PS–CDCA) were synthesized via the liquid-assisted grinding (LAG) technique. Specifically, equimolar amounts (1 mmol each) of SIT and CDCA were ground with 80 μL of acetone for six cycles, each lasting 10 min, with a 1 min rest period between cycles. The grinding was conducted using a Jingxin Tissuelyser-II instrument at a frequency of 40 Hz with 6 mm stainless steel balls. The PS–CDCA cocrystal was prepared following the same procedure. After ball milling, the powder samples were dried under vacuum conditions and characterized by PXRD.

Preparation of Single Crystals

The single crystals were obtained through a cooling crystallization process. Taking SIT–CA single crystal as an example, excess SIT and CA were dissolved in 4 mL of acetone at room temperature, followed by centrifugation to collect approximately 2 mL of the resulting supernatant after a duration of 4 h. Subsequently, about 20 mg of SIT–CA cocrystal was added to the supernatant and heated to 80 °C to ensure complete dissolution. Following a gradual cooling procedure, a colorless SIT–CA single crystal was successfully obtained. The remaining single crystals were prepared using a similar methodology.

Powder X-ray Diffraction (PXRD)

A Bruker D2 Phaser X-ray diffractometer was utilized to obtain the PXRD patterns. The voltage and current of the generator were set to 30 kV and 10 mA, respectively. Samples were put on the sample holder and data collection was performed within the 2θ range of 3° to 40° at a speed of 0.1 s/step. All measurements were conducted under room temperature conditions. The acquired data were imaged and integrated using RINT Rapid, and peak analysis was carried out utilizing Jade 6.0 software from Rigaku.

Single Crystal X-ray Diffraction (SCXRD)

A Bruker Apex II CCD diffractometer using Cu Kα (λ = 1.54178 Å) was utilized to conduct the X-ray diffraction collection of single crystals at 170 K. The crystals were carefully selected and loaded on the loops, following which the SAINT program was employed to integrate and scale the intensity data. The refinement was conducted using SADABS to eliminate the influence of the absorption effect. SADABS was used to correct the data for absorption effects, and the full-matrix least-squares method was employed in OLEX2 for refining the crystal structure. Crystallographic data and refinement details are summarized in Table S1. The CCDC numbers for SIT–CA, SIT–CDCA, PS–CA, and PS–CDCA are 2431790, 2431791, 2440399, and 2440400, respectively.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)

All DSC experiments were performed using a DSC TA Q2000 differential scanning calorimeter from USA. The nitrogen flow was purged at 50 mL/min. Samples weighing accurately ranging from 1 to 5 mg were heated in sealed aluminum pans from 20 to 250 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min. Data analysis was conducted using TA Universal Analysis software.

Thermogravimetric Analysis (TGA)

A TGA 55 instrument (TA Instruments, New Castle, DE, USA) was used to conduct all thermogravimetric analyses. The nitrogen flow was purged at 60 mL/min. Samples weighing between 5 and 10 mg were heated in nonhermetic aluminum pans from 25 to 410 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min. Thermal data were collected and analyzed using the built-in TA TRIOS analysis software.

Fourier Transform-Infrared (FT-IR) Spectroscopy

FT-IR spectra were obtained using a Nicolet-Magna FT-IR 750 spectrometer, and the samples were scanned in the range of 4000–400 cm–1 at a resolution of 4 cm–1 with a scanning frequency of 16 scans.

Powder Dissolution Tests

Powder dissolution experiments were conducted using a mini-Bath dissolution apparatus equipped with a Julabo-5 heating system. The experimental conditions were set at a temperature of 37 °C and a rotational speed of 50 rpm. All samples were sieved through 100-mesh sieves to eliminate the potential impact of particle size on solubility and powder dissolution. The dissolution media comprised buffers at pH 2.0, 4.5, and 6.8, with 0.5 wt % SDS added to each buffer. Approximately 100 mg of samples (PS, PS–CA, and PS–CDCA) were added to 15 mL of buffer solution under nonsinking conditions. At the specified time points (5, 10, 15, 30, 45, 60, and 120 min), 0.6 mL aliquots of the suspension were collected and subjected to centrifugation for 5 min. Subsequently, 0.4 mL of the supernatant was diluted to a final volume of 0.8 mL by adding 0.4 mL of pure water, and the concentration was analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The remaining powder was filtered and dried for further analysis via PXRD.

An Agilent 1260 Series HPLC system (Agilent Technologies, USA), equipped with a quaternary pump (model G1311C) and a diode array detector (model G1315D), was utilized to determine the concentration of PS. A 4.6 × 150 mm, 5 μm Agilent ZORBAX SB-C8 column was selected for the analysis. The mobile phase comprised pure water (eluent A) and acetonitrile (eluent B) at a ratio of 12% A and 88% B. The injection volume was set to 10 μL, and the flow rate was maintained at 1 mL/min. Detection was performed at a wavelength of 210 nm, with the column temperature set to 35 °C. Powder dissolution experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3).

Micellar Solubility Experiments

A ZWY 200D constant-temperature incubator shaker (Shanghai Zhicheng Co., Ltd.) was used to conduct the micellar solubility experiments. Briefly, 1.54 g of cholesterol, 2.26 g of oleic acid, 15.42 g of sodium chloride, 11.40 g of sodium phosphate, and 3.34 g of a bile salt mixture (containing 50% sodium cholate and 50% sodium deoxycholate) were accurately weighed and dissolved in 200 mL of water. The solution was subjected to ultrasonic treatment for 30 min. Subsequently, it was transferred into the constant-temperature incubator shaker and agitated at 200 rpm for 24 h at 37 °C. Afterward, the resulting suspension was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min and filtered through a 0.22-μm nylon membrane to remove any undissolved particles. The filtrate obtained was designated as the cholesterol-loaded bile salt micelle solution.

Next, varying concentrations of PS, PS–CA, and PS–CDCA (0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 mM/100 mL, calculated based on PS content) were introduced into the cholesterol-loaded bile salt micelle solution. The mixture was placed in the constant-temperature incubator shaker and agitated at 200 rpm for 1 h at 37 °C. Following incubation, the suspension was centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 15 min and filtered through a 0.22-μm nylon membrane. The supernatant was collected for further analysis. The concentrations of cholesterol and PS in the supernatant were quantified using HPLC, with the PS detection method following the procedure outlined in Section .

An Agilent 1260 Series HPLC system (Agilent Technologies, USA), equipped with a quaternary pump (model G1311C) and a diode array detector (model G1315D), was utilized to determine the concentration of cholesterol. A 4.6 × 150 mm, 5 μm Agilent ZORBAX Eclipse Plus C18 column was selected for the analysis. The mobile phase comprised pure water (eluent A) and methanol (eluent B) at a ratio of 7% A and 93% B. The injection volume was set to 10 μL, and the flow rate was maintained at 1 mL/min. Detection was performed at a wavelength of 205 nm, with the column temperature set to 35 °C. Under this detection method, PS do not interfere with the determination of cholesterol (R > 1.5).

Pharmacokinetic Experiments in Rats

Fourteen male Sprague–Dawley rats (weighing 200–250 g) were adaptively fed a phytosterol-free diet for 1 week and then randomly divided into two groups. Prior to administration, rats were fasted for 12 h while maintaining free access to water. PS and PS–CA powders were sieved through 100-mesh sieves to eliminate the influence of particle size. The American Heart Association recommends a daily intake of 2 g of phytosterols for adults, which is equivalent to approximately 210 mg/kg per day for rats. To facilitate administration, PS and PS–CA were orally administered at a dose of 210 mg/kg (based on the PS content), and the samples were suspended in water with 0.5 wt % Tween 80. Blood samples of approximately 400 μL were collected from the infraorbital venous plexus of each rat at 0 h (predose) and at 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, and 24 h postadministration. The blood was collected into centrifuge tubes containing 20 μL of 1% sodium heparin. The samples were then centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min, after which 150 μL of the resulting supernatant was carefully extracted and stored at −80 °C. Following the completion of blood sampling, the rats were humanely euthanized using carbon dioxide. Then initially, 100 μL of thawed plasma was combined with 1000 μL of a 1:1 methanol and methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) solution. The mixture was vortexed for 30 min to ensure thorough extraction and protein precipitation, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 5 min. Subsequently, 800 μL of the supernatant was collected and dried under nitrogen until all liquid had evaporated. The residue was then redissolved in 400 μL of methanol, vortexed for approximately 20 min, and subjected to mass spectrometry analysis. A SCIEX Triple Quad 4500 LC–MS instrument was used to analyze the concentration of PS in plasma. A 2.1 × 50 mm, 2.7 μm Agilent Poroshell 120 EC-C8 column was selected for the analysis. We employed multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) modes to analyze SIT, STI, and CAM. The respective m/z transitions were as follows: SIT 397.5 → 147.1, STI 383.5 → 146.9, and CAM 395.5 → 83.1. An atmospheric pressure chemical ionization (APCI) source in positive ion mode was utilized for ionization. The column temperature was maintained at 40 °C with a flow rate of 0.5 mL/min. Gradient elution was employed, where mobile phase A consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water and mobile phase B comprised 0.1% formic acid in methanol. The detailed gradient elution protocol is provided in Table S2.

All pharmacokinetic experiments were conducted in strict accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Shanghai Institute of Materia Medica.

Evaluation of the Lipid-Lowering Efficacy in Rats

Eighty male Sprague–Dawley rats (180–220 g) were housed in a standardized barrier environment with free access to chow diet and water. The rats were randomly divided into two groups: a normal control group (n = 8) fed a standard diet and a hyperlipemia model group (n = 72) fed a high-fat diet (XTHF 60, Xietong Organism, China). All rats underwent a one-week adaptation period. After 4 weeks of dietary intervention, blood samples (500 μL) were collected from the infraorbital venous plexus of overnight-fasted rats into centrifuge tubes containing 20 μL of 1% sodium heparin. The samples were centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 10 min, and the plasma was isolated for the determination of total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) supported by the Chinese national compound library. Statistical analysis was performed on the lipid profiles of the control and model groups. The model would be deemed successfully established if there were significant increases in TC, TG, and LDL-C. Eight rats in the normal control group were fed a standard diet and orally gavaged with 0.5 wt % Tween 80 daily, referred to as the control diet. A total of 56 successfully modeled rats were randomly divided into seven groups, each consisting of eight rats: high-fat diet (HFD), fish oil supplementation (HFD + FO), curcumin supplementation (HFD + CC), low-dose PS supplementation (HFD + LPS), high-dose PS supplementation (HFD + HPS), low-dose PS–CA supplementation (HFD + LPS–CA), and high-dose PS–CA supplementation (HFD + HPS–CA). The initial indices of the rats did not show significant differences among the groups, ensuring comparable baseline conditions. The HFD group was fed a high-fat diet and orally gavaged with 0.5 wt % Tween 80 daily as the model control. The FO group was administered 210 mg/kg based on the recommended dose by Byhealth Co., Ltd., while the CC group received 200 mg/kg according to the literature, serving as positive controls. The low and high doses of PS and PS–CA were set at 210 mg/kg and 315 mg/kg, respectively (based on the PS content). This dosing regimen was determined in accordance with the National Cholesterol Education Program’s recommendation of a daily phytosterol intake of 2–3 g for humans, which is equivalent to approximately 210–315 mg/kg in rats. All rats underwent oral administration via intragastric gavage, with samples suspended in 0.5 wt % Tween 80, for a duration of 4 weeks.

Body weight was recorded weekly. After 4 weeks of administration, blood samples were collected and plasma was immediately isolated. The levels of TC, TG, LDL-C, and HDL-C in the plasma were subsequently determined using an automated blood analysis system at the Chinese National Compound Library. Drug administration was continued following blood collection until the final sampling time. The rats were then sacrificed without fasting, and their livers were excised. Connective tissue and fat were carefully removed from the liver, which was subsequently washed with normal saline, dried, and observed; representative samples were photographed. Portions of the livers were fixed in PFA tissue fixative at room temperature according to the sample number. Based on the blood lipid levels, 3–4 samples from each group were selected for pathological examinations including hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining and Oil red O staining. Perirenal and epididymal adipose tissues were isolated, washed with normal saline, dried, weighed, and observed. All of the experiments followed the Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Shanghai Institute of Materia Medica provided support.

Results and Discussion

The Design of Cocrystals

Bile acids, as vital constituents of bile, play a pivotal role in the metabolism of dietary fats. Cholic acid (CA) (Figure ), a primary constituent of bile acids, is an amphipathic molecule that readily assembles into a channel-like stacked cavity structure, demonstrating significant potential in drug transportation and molecular encapsulation. CA exhibits high surface activity, effectively emulsifying fat-soluble compounds into chylomicrons and enhancing their solubility. Moreover, CA enhances the permeability of the intestinal epithelial cell barrier by reducing the transepithelial electrical resistance of Caco-2 cells.

In the CA monohydrate (depicted on the left in Figure ), CA molecules form dimers via the carboxy R2 2(8) supramolecular synthon. These dimers are further interconnected by water molecules, resulting in a microcapsule-like structure with a width of approximately 7.7 Å. Given that the maximum width of the SIT molecule is 5.4 Å (CCDC No. 933712), which is smaller than the cavity width of 7.7 Å, it can effectively fit within the cavity. Additionally, the presence of alcohol hydroxyl groups within the SIT molecules enables them to displace water molecules and become embedded in the cavity, thereby forming a microcapsule-like cocrystal. The design is amenable to extension toward other analogous molecular structures.

Cocrystal design of SIT–CA.

PXRD Analysis and Confirmation of Cocrystal Formation

As a mixture, PS consist of SIT (55.7%), STI (14.0%), and CAM (28.7%), with SIT being the predominant component. Consequently, we systematically selected bile acid coformers for cocrystal screening with SIT and its mixture PS. Ultimately, four distinct cocrystals were successfully synthesized: SIT–CA, SIT–CDCA, PS–CA and PS–CDCA.

The disappearance of characteristic diffraction peaks for the API and CCFs, along with the emergence of new peaks, suggests the formation of novel solid forms. This is evidenced by the PXRD patterns presented in Figure . For instance, in Figure a, SIT–CA exhibits new characteristic peaks at 2θ angles of 6.3°, 7.8°, 10.7°, 13.5°, 14.1°, 17.1°, and 21.1°. In contrast, the characteristic peaks observed for SIT at 2θ angles of 4.9°, 7.2°, 12.8°, 18.5°, 19.4°, and 22.0°, as well as those for CA at 2θ angles of 7.4°, 10.4°, 11.8°, 12.9°, and 18.3°, are notably absent. This indicates the formation of a distinct new phase that differs from both API and the CCF. Moreover, the close agreement between experimental and simulated results confirms the high purity of the powder samples.

Crystal Structure Analysis

As expected, four microcapsule-like cocrystals are formed. Because both PS–CA and PS–CDCA are cocrystals formed from mixtures, the side chains of their PS molecular structures exhibit disorder. HPLC analysis was performed on PS, PS–CA, and PS–CDCA, with the corresponding compositional data presented in Table S3. Based on the composition ratios, it can be concluded that PS–CA and PS–CDCA each contain three distinct components, and the relative proportions of these components remain largely unchanged. However, from the perspective of single-crystal structure analysis, this structural disorder introduces certain inaccuracies in determining the molecular arrangements. Consequently, the PS molecular structures were resolved as β-sitosterol molecular structures, as shown in Figure S1 and Figure S2. The single crystal structures of SIT–CA and SIT–CDCA will be discussed.

Single Crystal Structure Analysis of SIT–CA

The colorless flake crystal SIT–CA was obtained through cooling crystallization in acetone. It is solved in the monoclinic P2 space group, with the asymmetric unit comprising one molecule of SIT and one molecule of CA (Figure a). In the crystal structure, two CA molecules are linked into a dimer through O5–H···O6 (2.659 Å) hydrogen bonds. This dimer is further extended by connecting to another dimer via O3–H···O2 (2.839 Å) and O4–H···O3 (2.770 Å) hydrogen bonds, ultimately forming an infinite one-dimensional chain along the c-axis. Each SIT molecule interacts with each CA molecule through an O2–H···O1 (2.671 Å) hydrogen bond, suspending alongside the one-dimensional chain (Figure b). These one-dimensional chains are stacked and extended along the a-axis through van der Waals forces between SIT molecules, thereby forming the two-dimensional layer of the cocrystal (Figure c). Adjacent two-dimensional layers interact through O1–H···O4 (2.660 Å) hydrogen bonds, leading to an infinite extension along the b-axis. As designed, CA molecules assemble to form a channel-like cavity structure, wherein SIT molecules are encapsulated through hydrogen bonding, leading to the formation of a microcapsule-like cocrystal (Figure d).

(a) Fundamental units and interaction of SIT–CA; (b) hydrogen bonding in one-dimensional chains; (c) two-dimensional packing arrangement; (d) microcapsule-like structure observed along the a-axis.

Single Crystal Structure Analysis of SIT–CDCA

Similar to SIT–CA, the colorless flake crystal SIT–CDCA was obtained via cooling crystallization in acetone. It is solved in the monoclinic P2 space group, with the asymmetric unit comprising one molecule of SIT and one molecule of CDCA (Figure a). In the crystal structure, two CDCA molecules are linked into a dimer through O5–H···O4 (2.659 Å) hydrogen bonds. Each CDCA molecule additionally forms an O1–H···O2 hydrogen bond (2.781 Å) with a SIT molecule. The SIT molecule subsequently establishes an O3–H···O1 hydrogen bond (2.899 Å) with another CDCA dimer, resulting in the formation of an infinite one-dimensional chain along the c-axis (Figure b). These one-dimensional chains are stacked and extended along the a-axis through van der Waals forces between SIT molecules, thereby forming the two-dimensional layer of the cocrystal (Figure c). Adjacent two-dimensional layers interact through O1–H···O2 (2.781 Å) hydrogen bonds, leading to an infinite extension along the b-axis. As designed, CDCA molecules assemble to form a channel-like cavity structure, wherein SIT molecules are encapsulated through hydrogen bonding, leading to the formation of a microcapsule-like cocrystal (Figure d).

Thermal Analysis

Thermal analysis techniques, such as DSC, can confirm the presence of new solid forms and evaluate the phase purity of the products. TGA can determine whether any solvents are present on the surface or within the lattice structure. The results are displayed in Figure and Figure S3.

Compared DSC curves of (a) SIT–CA, (b) SIT–CDCA, (c) PS–CA, (d) PS–CDCA.

Taking PS–CA as an example, PS have a melting point of 140 °C and CA exhibits a melting point of 200 °C. In comparison, the PS–CA cocrystal demonstrates a melting point of 168 °C, which is markedly different from the melting points of its individual components. The TGA analysis of the cocrystal reveals no significant weight loss prior to the melting event, confirming that it is a nonsolvated form. In summary, all four cocrystals were further validated as new solid forms with adequate phase purity.

FT-IR Spectroscopy

The formation of cocrystals modifies the intermolecular interaction forces, resulting in notable changes in both the position and intensity of the molecular infrared absorption peaks. The FT-IR analysis results are presented in Figure S4. All cocrystals exhibited distinct wavenumber shifts compared to the API and coformers, accompanied by changes in peak width and intensity. Taking PS–CA as an example, the O–H stretching vibration bands in PS and CA are observed at 3418 cm–1 and at 3521, 3316, and 3178 cm–1, respectively. Upon cocrystal formation, the electron cloud density becomes more evenly distributed due to molecular association, leading to a shift of the O–H stretching vibration frequency in PS–CA to 3351 cm–1, manifesting as a broad peak. Furthermore, the CO stretching vibration peak of the carboxylate groups in CA exhibits a red shift from 1712 cm–1 to 1705 cm–1. This red shift signifies the establishment of a robust hydrogen bond interaction between PS and CA.

Solubility and Dissolution Research

Given the negligible solubility of PS in water (the dissolved concentration falling below the detection limit of HPLC), it is essential to incorporate a small amount of emulsifier to facilitate dissolution testing. Therefore, the powder dissolution experiments for PS, PS–CA, and PS–CDCA were conducted in buffers at pH 2.0, 4.5, and 6.8, each containing 0.5 wt % SDS. The solubility of PS, which is a mixture comprising β-sitosterol, stigmasterol, and campesterol, was determined by summing the individual solubilities of these three components, as illustrated in Figure . The results demonstrated that the dissolution rates of PS–CA and PS–CDCA were significantly higher than those of PS alone. For instance, in a pH 6.8 + 0.5% SDS solution, the concentration in the PS group was only 19.8 μg/mL within 5 min. In contrast, both the PS–CA and PS–CDCA groups rapidly achieved high concentrations of 3230 μg/mL and 1940 μg/mL, respectively, representing approximately 163-fold and 98-fold increases compared to the PS group at the same time point. Notably, this solubility advantage persisted for up to 2 h. The precipitates were collected and characterized by PXRD, as shown in Figure S5. The PXRD patterns confirmed that the cocrystals precipitated in the form of themselves. This observation provides insight into the mechanism by which cocrystals can maintain high concentrations in buffer solutions over extended periods.

Micellar Solubility Experiments

According to the intestinal absorption mechanism of PS, PS must combine with bile acids, phospholipids, and free fatty acids in the intestinal lumen to form micelles, which subsequently enter enterocytes and are further emulsified into chylomicrons for transport through the lymphatic system. When PS coexist with cholesterol, due to their similar molecular structures, PS can compete with cholesterol for incorporation into mixed micelles, thereby reducing cholesterol absorption by enterocytes. Theoretically, the greater the exposure of PS, the stronger their affinity for micelles and the more pronounced their competitive inhibitory effect on cholesterol, leading to more effective reduction of cholesterol solubility. Relevant micellar experiments have validated this mechanism. Jia et al. evaluated the cholesterol-inhibiting effect of ornithine phytosterol ester hydrochloride with modified properties using model bile salt micelles and found that this compound effectively reduced cholesterol solubility in vitro. Hu et al. discovered that introducing hydrophilic β-sitosterol glycosyl derivatives into cholesterol-loaded bile salt micelles not only significantly increased β-sitosterol concentration in the micelles but also demonstrated a dose-dependent reduction in cholesterol solubility. These studies reveal the interaction between modified phytosterol derivatives and bile salts and their impact on cholesterol solubility.

Based on these findings, cholesterol-loaded bile salt micelles were designed and synthesized containing varying concentrations of PS, PS–CA, and PS–CDCA to investigate their effects on cholesterol micellar solubility. As shown in Figure , the results indicate that PS addition inhibits cholesterol micellar solubility, consistent with Hu et al.‘s findings. Following cocrystal formation, the emulsifying effects of CA and CDCA significantly enhanced PS micellar solubility, while cholesterol solubility exhibited a dose-dependent reduction with increasing PS concentration in the micelles. These results suggest that the emulsifying effects of cholic acid and chenodeoxycholic acid in cocrystals enhance the interaction between PS and bile salts, leading to physical changes in micellar structure (with more hydrophobic PS occupying larger areas in the micelles), thereby reducing cholesterol micellar solubility. This finding suggests that PS–CA and PS–CDCA may exhibit greater potential for lipid-lowering activity compared to PS.

Pharmacokinetic Research

Based on the correlation between the enhanced in vitro dissolution rate and in vivo pharmacokinetic behavior, as well as the safety profile of the coformers, PS–CA was conducted for further pharmacokinetic evaluation. The rats were administered 210 mg/kg of PS. Given that rats already contain a baseline concentration of PS prior to administration, the 0-h data must be subtracted as a control for accurate comparative analysis during data processing. As illustrated in Figure , the oral bioavailability of PS was significantly enhanced through cocrystallization. Specifically, the AUC1–24 h of PS–CA was 6-fold that of PS, and C max was 4-fold. The T max of PS–CA was significantly prolonged compared to that of PS, likely due to the fact that PS molecules released after cocrystal dissociation form micelles with CA molecules, which temporarily encapsulate PS and transiently reduce the concentration of free PS, thereby delaying their absorption and prolonging the duration of action. It is worth noting that while PS–CA extends the absorption duration, the increased exposure time leads to greater absorption, making the formulation overall advantageous. As discussed, the cocrystal exhibited a markedly enhanced bioavailability that was directly correlated with its superior in vitro solubility, thereby demonstrating a more promising application potential.

Conclusion

In this study, four microcapsule-like cocrystals of the API (SIT and PS) with coformers (CA and CDCA) were successfully designed and synthesized. The cocrystals were comprehensively characterized using PXRD, FT-IR, DSC, and TGA, and their crystal structures were determined by SCXRD. In vitro dissolution tests were conducted for PS, PS–CA, and PS–CDCA in various buffer solutions. The results demonstrated that both PS–CA and PS–CDCA exhibited significantly faster dissolution rates compared to pure PS. Subsequently, based on considerations of both safety and efficacy, PS–CA was selected for further investigation. The in vivo pharmacokinetic experiment results were consistent with the observed improvement in solubility. Specifically, the AUC1–24 h of PS–CA was 6 times that of PS, while the C max was 4 times. The evaluation of lipid-lowering efficacy indicated that PS–CA showed a more pronounced effect on reducing blood lipids and improving hepatic steatosis, which may be attributed to enhanced absorption of PS. In conclusion, PS–CA is expected to function as a superior medicinal solid form in comparison to PS. Furthermore, the designed microcapsule-like cocrystal model can be extended to other steroid drugs, offering a new way to address the bioavailability issues of these compounds.
