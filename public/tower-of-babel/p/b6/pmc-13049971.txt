Disordered colonic microbiota as a potential contributor to inflammation in diarrheal Jinhua weaned piglets
Authors: Jiaheng Li, Chengzeng Luo, Fangfang Lou, Xiaojun Zhang, Jiujun Duan, Zeou Wei, Xujin Hu, Pingguang Tu, Xizhong Du, Ruqing Zhong, Liang Chen, Hongfu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Diarrhea is a major cause of mortality in Jinhua piglets, accounting for up to 80% of total piglet deaths and severely restricting the sustainability of pig production systems. In this study, colonic morphology, organ indices, gut microbiota composition, and transcriptomic profiles were compared between diarrheal piglets (DG) and healthy piglets (HG) to identify diarrhea associated bacteria in Jinhua piglets and to explore potential microbial intervention strategies. The results revealed that diarrheal piglets had lower organ indices (p < 0.01) and worse intestinal conditions such as inflammatory factor infiltration, reduced number of goblet cells (p < 0.05), and increased colonic crypt depth (p < 0.05). Transcriptomics analysis of colonic mucosa revealed that differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between two groups (FC > 1.5, p < 0.05) were enriched mainly in Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation and Th17 cell differentiation. Microbiota analysis showed that bacteria such as Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, which were enriched in the colon of piglets in HG, may contribute to host metabolic and immune modulation via metabolic products such as short chain fatty acids and crosstalk with other microbial taxa. In contrast, potential harmful bacteria, particularly Fusobacterium, were significantly enriched in the colon of piglets in DG. Correlation analysis further identified IL-2Rβ, TBX21, and LCK as key immune related genes associated with differentially abundant microbes. Enriched Fusobacterium showed associations with inflammatory gene expression, whereas Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 exhibited opposite correlations. In conclusion, harmful bacterial colonization may promote intestinal inflammation and diarrhea in Jinhua piglets, while beneficial bacteria exert protective effects that support piglets’ growth and intestinal health. Background

The Jinhua pig is a Chinese native breed that is also described as a “panda pig” because of its limited population and the difficulty associated with its production. One of the major challenges in Jinhua pig breeding is the high death rate of weaned piglets [1]. In pig production, piglet mortality can reach 12–20%, with approximately 80% of these deaths attributed to diarrhea, representing a major source of economic loss [2]. Therefore, preventing and alleviating diarrhea in weaned piglets is essential for reducing rearing difficulty and increasing the population size of Jinhua pigs.

Regarding animal immunity, T-helper (Th) cell subsets, including Th1, Th2, and Th17 cells, play crucial roles in host defense against intracellular and extracellular pathogens [3, 4]. Th1 cells primarily produce interferon gamma (IFN-γ), which activates macrophages and other immune cells, whereas Th2 cells mainly promote antibody-mediated immune response through the production of cytokines such as IL-13 [5]. Th17 cells contribute to protection against extracellular pathogens by secreting cytokine IL-17, and dysregulation of Th17 responses has been implicated in inflammatory and autoimmune diseases [6]. The differentiation of Th cell subsets is regulated by multiple cytokines and transcriptional factors. For example, T-bet (TBX21) is a Th-cell-specific transcription factor that promotes the development of Th0 cells into effector Th cells [7], while ZAP70 initiates downstream of an activated T cell receptor [8]. Therefore, the changes in T-helper-cell differentiation driven by differentially expressed genes (DEGs) may contribute to the development of diarrhea.

Gut microbes can regulate host gene expression to maintain immunity and metabolism in response to pathogenic challenges [9]. Beneficial bacteria can exert their beneficial effects through multiple mechanisms, including competitive exclusion, nutrient competition, production of antimicrobial substances, enhancement of the epithelial barrier, modulation of the immune system, and the generation of bioactive metabolites [10]. Recently, more probiotic products have been used to promote animal growth and health [11, 12]. In contrast, colonization by harmful bacteria or disruption of gut microbial balance can impair host health and contribute to disease. For example, Fusobacterium, a well-recognized pathogenic bacterium, has been associated with microbial dysbiosis, abscess formation, and systemic infections [13, 14]. Moreover, studies have shown that an imbalanced intestinal microbiota can increase the abundance of pathogenic bacteria and contribute to gastrointestinal disorders such as irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) and diarrhea [15]. Therefore, gut microbiota represents a critical factor in the prevention and treatment of diarrhea.

Previous studies have mainly focused on the mechanisms underlying diarrhea in Duroc × Landrace × Yorkshire piglets, demonstrating that microbiota dysbiosis frequently occurs in diarrhea piglets [16, 17]. In these studies, harmful bacteria such as Fusobacterium were enriched in diarrhea piglets and were suggested to contribute to intestinal inflammation [16, 18], whereas probiotics or beneficial microbes were shown to alleviate or prevent diarrhea [19, 20]. Although these findings provide valuable insights for pig health research, evidence specifically addressing the causes of diarrhea in Jinhua piglets remains limited.

This study revealed differences in growth performance, organ indices, and colonic morphology and secretory function between diarrheal and healthy Jinhua piglets. Thereafter, we combined the colon transcriptome and microbiome to identify potential associations between dominant bacterial taxa and DEGs to explore the potential bacterial evidence resulting in diarrhea in Jinhua piglets. Based on these findings, we propose that modulation of intestinal bacterial colonization may represent a feasible strategy to reduce diarrhea and improve the production performance and health of Jinhua piglets.

Methods

Experimental animals

Three Jinhua pregnant sows with similar reproductive performance were selected for this study. Backfat thickness was measured 20 days before farrowing at the P2 site and ranged from 19 to 22 mm. All sows were of the same parity (second parity) and were inseminated using semen from the same boar line. The sows were moved to the farrowing room 5 days before the expected farrowing date and were housed under identical conditions. After farrowing, all sows were provided with feed and water ad libitum. All piglets were exclusively suckled by their respective sows prior to weaning. The diet composition of the sows is provided in Table S1. During the experimental period, environmental conditions, including temperature and humidity, were automatically regulated using evaporative cooling systems and ventilation fans (Henan Hengyin Automation Technology Co., Ltd, Henan, China).

Experimental design

Piglets were weaned at 35 days of age. The diarrheal situation of the piglets started from birth until weaning, with fecal assessments twice daily. Diarrhea was diagnosed using a fecal scoring system, in which piglets presenting watery stools (score ≥ 3) in both daily assessments for at least 7 consecutive days were classified as diarrheal piglets. Table 1 shows the fecal scoring system. Based on this diagnosis, six piglets with persistent diarrhea were chosen as the diarrheal group (DG), while six piglets with normal fecal consistency throughout the monitoring period were chosen as the healthy group (HG). Sample collocation

All the piglets were individually weighed on day 35. After weighing, piglets were euthanized through an injection of sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg BW) followed by exsanguination. After euthanasia, major organs were excised and weighed. Organ indices of the piglets were determined by calculating the ratio of organ weight to body weight (mg/g). Two intestinal segment samples (2 cm in length) were cut from the colon (10 cm to the ileum). One segment was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde fixative solution, while the other was fixed in Carnot’s solution. In addition, one part of the colon was cut open to collect contents, while another part of the colon was gently rinsed with sterile saline twice to remove residual contents. The mucosa was then carefully scraped using the back of a sterile surgical blade. Both mucosa samples and colonic contents were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and subsequently stored at −80 °C.

Histological evaluation

The colon samples were dehydrated, cleared, and embedded in paraffin wax. Two sections (5 um thickness) were subsequently cut from each sample. The sections were deparaffinized in xylene. After rehydration, two sections of each sample were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) and Alcian blue-periodic acid-Schif (AB-PAS). The tissue sections were not coded until data analysis. Thereafter, the slides were scanned under a microscope at 80× magnification using Leica Application Suite X (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany). Ten crypts were randomly selected from different regions of each slide. Specifically, for HE slides, we measured the crypt depth from the luminal surface to the base of each crypt. The mean crypt depth (µm) was calculated for each sample based on measurements from ten crypts. For AB-PAS slides, we manually counted the number of goblet cells in each crypt under the same magnification, and the average number of goblet cells per crypt was determined for each sample. Each sample was thus represented by one mean crypt depth value and one mean goblet cell count for subsequent statistical analysis.

Transcriptome analysis

RNA isolation and sequencing

The colonic mucosal RNA was extracted with TRIzol Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific). A NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, Inc.) was subsequently used to determine the RNA concentration, quality, and integrity. The mRNA of each sample was then purified with Poly T oligo-attached magnetic beads.

The purified mRNA was then fragmented and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. The cDNA was adenylated and ligated to Illumina PE adapter oligonucleotides. The cDNA was subsequently amplified via 15-cycle PCR. The purified products were analyzed and quantified via the Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Assay on the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA), ensuring precise measurement and quality assessment. Only RNA samples meeting the following criteria were used for library construction: OD260/280 = 1.8~2.2, OD260/230≥2.0, RIN≥6.5, 28S:18S≥1.0, and total RNA amount > 1 μg. The sequencing library was ultimately sequenced via the NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) using 1 μg of total RNA with a paired-end 2 × 150 bp strategy. The sequencing depth was approximately 20 million reads per sample.

Quality control, read mapping, and filtering

The raw paired-end reads were subjected to trimming and quality control procedures via fastp, and the default settings were used to ensure optimal data quality and accuracy. The clean reads were subsequently aligned to the reference genome via HISAT2 (version 2.0.5). The raw data were deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under accession number PRJNA1018298.

16S rRNA sequencing

DNA isolation and Illumina PE300 sequencing

Total DNA from the colonic digesta and mucosa samples was extracted using the E.Z.N.A.® Soil DNA Kit (Omega Biotek, Norcross, GA, USA). The hypervariable V3–V4 regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA genes were then amplified via a T100 Thermal Cycler PCR system (Bio-Rad, USA) with the primers 338F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’). The PCR products were separated on a 2% agarose gel, purified using the PCR Clean-Up Kit (YuHua, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s guidelines, and quantified via Qubit 4.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

The purified amplicons were combined at equal molar concentrations, and paired-end sequencing was performed on the Illumina PE300 platform (Illumina, San Diego, USA) following standard protocols provided by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The raw data were deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database under accession number PRJNA1017159.

Data processing

The raw FASTQ files were processed for demultiplexing through a custom Perl script developed in-house, followed by quality filtering using fastp (version 0.19.6) and merging using FLASH (version 1.2.7). The optimized sequences were classified into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) via UPARSE (version 7.1), applying a 97% sequence similarity cutoff for clustering. In the final step, the 16S rRNA gene sequences from each sample were normalized to the minimum sequencing depth observed across all samples (24,833 for colonic mucosa and 35,847 for colonic contents), ensuring that variations in sequencing depth would not bias the subsequent analysis.

Correlation analysis

For the transcriptome results, differentially expressed genes involved in immune related pathways were selected, resulting in a total of 11 immune associated DEGs. For the microbiome analysis, the top 50 most abundant microbes at the genus level were first identified, and significantly differentially abundant microbes shared between colonic mucosa and contents were further selected because these taxa are considered representative of the representative microbial taxa and reflect consistent microbial alterations [21, 22]. The relationships between selected DEGs and microbes were subsequently calculated using the Spearman test via the psych package (version 2.3.6).

Validation of DEGs via qRT-PCR and western blotting (WB)

Total RNA was isolated from the colonic mucosa via a Tissue/Cell Total RNA Mini Kit (Gene-Better, Beijing, China). The PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara, Kusatsu, Shiga, Japan) was subsequently used to reverse transcribe 2 µg of total RNA. The primers used were synthesized commercially (Sangon Biotech, Shanghai, China) (Table S3). For qPCR, 1 µL of cDNA was mixed with 5 µL of SYBR Premix Ex Taq II, 0.2 µL of ROX Reference Dye II, 0.2 µL of primers (0.2 µM each for forward and reverse primers), and 3.4 µL of double-distilled water. Amplification and detection were performed via a QuantStudio 7 Flex (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, USA) under the following conditions: predenaturation at 95 °C for 30 s; denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s and then annealing/extension at 60 °C for 30 s (40 cycles of PCR); and melting at 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and 95 °C for 15 s. GAPDH served as the reference gene for normalizing the Ct values of the target gene, and relative gene expression was determined via the 2^-ΔΔCt method.

RIPA lysis buffer was used to extract total protein from the colonic mucosa, and a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) was used to quantify the protein content. After the protein concentration was normalized, the same quantity of protein from each sample was subjected to sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk for 2 hours and incubated with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Thereafter, the membrane was incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Sangon Biotech, #D110058) at room temperature for 2 hours. An enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Bio-Rad, CA, USA) was used to identify protein signals via a Bio-Rad Chemi XRS imaging system (Bio-Rad). The main antibodies used were as follows: anti-ACTB (Sangon Biotech, #D110001), anti-ZAP70 (Bioss Antibodies, #bs-1942R), anti-IL2Rβ (ABclonal, #A6207), anti-TBX21 (ABclonal, #A23414), and anti-LCK (ABclonal, #A23988). Moreover, Image Lab (version 3.0) software was used to quantify target protein expression.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the average body weight, organ indices, goblet cell counts, and crypt depth was assessed via the Shapiro-Wilk test in GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA, http://www.graphpad.com). Depending on the normality results, statistical significance was analyzed using Student’s t test or Mann-Whitney test. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

HTSeq (version 0.9.1) was used to compare the read count values to the original expression values of the genes. DESeq2 (version 3.17) was used to analyze the significant differential expression of genes (read count was used) with the following conditions: genes with a |log2FoldChange| > 0.58 (fold change of 1.5 or greater) and a p value < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The package pheatmap (version 1.0.12) was used to plot the fold change of DEGs in R (version 4.3.1). Thereafter, transcripts per million reads (TPM) were used to standardize the read count. After standardization, the Factoextra package (version 1.0.7) was used for principal component analysis (PCA). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis of DEGs was then performed via KOBAS 2.0, with a focus on pathways with a p value < 0.05.

The 16S rRNA sequencing results were calculated on the Majorbio Cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com). Alpha diversity indices were calculated via Mothur (version 1.30.1), the statistical significance was calculated via Student’s t test, and the threshold for significance was p < 0.05. Beta diversity was analyzed via R (version 4.3.1) software, in which principal component analysis (PCA) and principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) were plotted via the vegan package (version 2.6–4). Group differences in beta diversity were statistically evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, in which the threshold for significance was p < 0.05. Finally, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was performed to identify the significantly abundant microbial taxa between the two groups (LDA score > 2, p < 0.05).

Correlations were considered statistically significant when the Spearman correlation coefficient exceeded 0.5 and the corresponding p value was less than 0.05. Correlation results were visualized using the pheatmap package (version 1.0.12). Statistical analyses and graphical presentation of qRT-PCR and western blot results were performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (GraphPad Software, Boston, MA, USA), with differences considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Relationships between the colonic microbiota and immune related host genes

To explore the relationships between host genes and the colonic microbiota, as well as their possible contributions to the immune system of diarrheal piglets, we examined the correlations between 11 immune system related DEGs and 14 differential microbes in the colonic contents (Fig. 7A) or 10 differential microbes in the colonic mucosa (Fig. 7B). The correlations between all microbes and IL-5, MAPK10, HSP90αA1, or IL-13 were weak (Fig. 7A and B). Among the DG piglets’ dominant bacteria, only Fusobacterium was highly significantly negatively correlated with most downregulated genes, especially IL-2Rβ, ZAP70, TBX21, and LCK. qRT-PCR validation confirmed the transcriptomic findings for these four genes, showing consistent expression changes (Fig. 7C). However, the western blot results revealed that the expression of only three proteins, IL-2Rβ, TBX21, and LCK, was significantly decreased in piglets in the DG, similar to their gene expression (Fig. 7D). In contrast, most of the dominant microbial taxa in HG piglets presented significantly positive correlations with DEGs. Specifically, Terrisporobacter, Eubacterium_siraeum_group, norank_f__norank_o__Clostridia_vadinBB60_group, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, UCG-005, and Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group were highly significantly positively correlated with IL-2Rβ, TBX21, and LCK, and there were highly significantly positive correlations between CD3D and Terrisporobacter, UCG-005, and Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group. Discussion

Compared with piglets in the healthy group (HG), piglets in the diarrheal group (DG) exhibited reduced body weights at 35 days of age (Table S2) and significantly decreased organ indices. Previous studies have consistently reported that diarrhea is associated with impaired growth performance in piglets [23]. In addition, significantly lower organ indices are commonly observed in animals with reduced body weight and compromised health status [24]. Together, these findings suggest that diarrhea is closely associated with poor growth performance and organ development in DG piglets, highlighting the need to first evaluate general health status when investigating growth impairment.

The mucus layer serves as the primary defense barrier which protects against the infiltration of pathogens or harmful bacteria [25]. Colonic crypt depth reflects the capacity for cell generation. Shallower crypts are generally associated with greater epithelial cell maturity and enhanced secretory function. [26]. Moreover, the mucus layer is established by mucins secreted by goblet cells, and more goblet cells also indicate better secretory function and barrier integrity [27]. Based on these considerations, we further evaluated intestinal health by examining differences in colonic crypt morphology and secretory cell function between the two groups piglets. Diarrheal piglets had significantly increased crypt depth and reduced goblet cell numbers, consistent with previous reports showing that diarrhea is associated with impaired intestinal secretory capacity and a weakened mucus barrier [23]. In addition, pronounced inflammatory cell infiltration was observed in the colon of DG piglets, further indicating compromised intestinal health. These alterations may result from intestinal injury and inflammation associated with diarrhea, which can impair mucus barrier function. Meanwhile, gut microbiota dysbiosis has also been reported to contribute to similar pathological changes, including inflammatory factors infiltrating, increased crypt depth and reduced goblet cells [28, 29].

To gain deeper insight into changes in intestinal immunity at the transcriptional level, we investigated the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the colonic mucosa of piglets from both groups. Substantial differences in gene expression were observed between the two groups with 623 upregulated genes and 217 downregulated genes in diarrheal piglets. These findings indicate that diarrhea is associated with pronounced transcriptional alterations in the intestinal mucosa. Based on the KEGG enrichment results, two immune system related pathways with significant differences were selected: the Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation pathways and the Th17 cell differentiation pathway. Most genes involved in Th1, Th2, and Th17 cell differentiation were downregulated in diarrheal piglets, suggesting worse T cell function. Th1 and Th2 cells mainly regulate humoral immunity and contribute to host defense against intracellular pathogens such as stimulating B cells to produce IgM, IgG1, or IL-4, while Th17 cells mainly defend against extracellular pathogens and various diseases [4, 30]. Accordingly, disorganized Th1, Th2, and Th17 cell responses may contribute to the poor health status observed in diarrheal piglets. Previous studies have also reported that T cell immunity function damaged occurred during diarrhea, including reduced Foxp3+ regulatory T cell responses and altered Th1 and Th17 responses [31], which was in line with our results. Among the 11 DEGs enriched in these two pathways, IL-5 and IL-13 were enriched only in Th1 and Th2 cell differentiation, while HSP90αA1 was enriched in Th17 cell differentiation. IL-5, a proinflammatory cytokine [32], was the only gene upregulated in diarrheal piglets, further indicating the inflammation of diarrheal piglets. The remaining downregulated DEGs can be broadly grouped into anti-inflammatory cytokine genes, proinflammatory cytokine receptor genes, and other immune regulatory genes. For example, IL-13, an anti-inflammatory cytokine, maintains IgE and regulates IgE-mediated allergic responses [33]. CD3D is highly related to immune checkpoints and T lymphocytes, especially CD8+ T cells [34]. Moreover, either ZAP70 or TBX21 play critical roles in T cell responses or the development of naive T lymphocytes [8, 35]. TBX21 is originally described as a regulator of Th1 cell development, and it is recognized to play a necessary role in both the innate and adaptive immune systems [36]. It supports the development of naive T lymphocytes by controlling the expression of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ), and it controls the differentiation of Th1 cells through IFN-γ-STAT1 and IL-12-STAT4 pathways [37]. In addition, as the essential kinase in T cell signaling, ZAP70 can combine with the T cell receptor (TCR) and be recruited from the cytoplasm to the plasma membrane, leading to T cell stimulation, expansion, and maturation [8]. LCK can also be involved in the activation loop of ZAP70. Mutual phosphorylation of tyrosine residues by LCK and ZAP70 affects ZAP70 activation. [8]. Furthermore, LCK also regulates the initiation of TCR signaling and T cell development or homeostasis by increasing the phosphorylation of immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motifs (ITAMs) [38]. Together, the reduced expression of these genes suggests impaired TCR signaling and T-cell homeostasis in diarrheal piglets. Moreover, for genes encoding proinflammatory cytokine receptors, the standard expression of IL-2Rα, IL-2Rβ, and IL-12Rβ1 indicates the normal immune ability of piglets. The lower expression of these genes always occurs in primary and secondary immunodeficiencies [39, 40], which also suggests that worse health even immunodeficiencies occurred in diarrheal piglets in our study. Therefore, identifying upstream factors, particularly microbial drivers, that influence the expression of these immune related genes may provide valuable insights into strategies for preventing or alleviating diarrhea in piglets.

The intestinal microbiota can be considered a vital organ for the host, with a stable and healthy microbiota playing a crucial role in supporting the host’s growth and overall well-being. Gut microbes regulate the host’s metabolism through their metabolites, and themselves act as the last barrier to protect the intestine [41]. In turn, the mucus layer strongly influences the intestinal microbiota. The healthy mucus layer is rich in metabolites such as O-glycans, which support gut microbiota growth by providing bacterial ligands and nutrients [23]. Recent studies have further confirmed the crucial role of gut microbiota in piglet health. Zhu et al. (2024) and Huang et al. (2025) reported significant differences in the gut microbiota structure between diarrheal and healthy piglets [42, 43] Additionally, Wang et al. (2025), Xie et al. (2025), and Park et al. (2024) demonstrated that probiotics supplementation significantly reduced diarrhea incidence in weaned piglets by enhancing gut barrier function and regulating immune responses [44–46]. Together, these findings highlight the central role of the gut microbiota in the development of diarrhea. In our experiment, based on the impaired intestinal function and immune status observed in DG piglets, we analyzed the colonic microbiota to further identify microbial signatures associated with poor intestinal health. Our results revealed significant differences in either the colonic contents or mucosa between HG and DG piglets except for one healthy piglet (H6), whose microbiota clustered with that of the DG group. Although this piglet did not exhibit clinical diarrhea, mild microbial dysbiosis may have contributed to its DG like microbial profile. Overall, compared with healthy piglets, diarrheal piglets had significantly lower microbial diversity indicating a disrupted and unstable intestinal microbiota. Consistent with our findings, Yang et al. (2019) reported reduced fecal bacterial α-diversity in diarrheal piglets at five weeks of age [47]. Moreover, multiple studies have shown that harmful bacteria tend to colonize piglets with various intestinal disorders [48, 49]. In line with this, some dominant bacteria of diarrheal piglets in our result were related to inflammation. For example, Olsenella enrichment occurs in osteoporosis patients and ulcerative colitis patients to lead inflammation [50, 51], and this bacteria reportedly infect the lungs to lead inflammation [52]. Similarly, Alloprevotella enrichment always occurs when inflammation appears such as early rheumatoid arthritis, oral dysbiotic infections, and COVID-19 [53–55]. It is also increased in IBD rats, suggesting an association with intestinal inflammation [56]. Notably, Fusobacterium, the only significantly enriched genus among TOP 50 taxa in the colonic mucosa of DG piglets, was also enriched in the colonic contents. Fusobacterium is a recognized harmful bacterium that can cause diverse infections, and it is involved in multiple human diseases especially intestinal inflammation [57, 58]. Across multiple diarrheal piglet models from commercial crossbreds to various farm settings, enriched Fusobacterium have been repeatedly observed. Neonatal case-control studies reported a doubling of the phylum Fusobacteria in diarrheic piglets [59, 60], and species-level associations such as F. mortiferum have been linked to neonatal diarrhea [18]. In virus-induced diarrheal models (PEDV), infected piglets also exhibited higher proportions of Fusobacterium compared with healthy controls [61]. Similar signatures have been summarized in longitudinal and review-type analyses [47]. These converging findings across breeds and etiologies suggest that Fusobacterium may represent a general microbial contributor to piglet diarrhea rather than a breed-specific marker. Overall, these dominant bacteria in diarrheal piglets indicate that inflammation occurred in DG piglets. In contrast, several bacterial taxa were enriched in the colonic mucosa and digesta of healthy piglets, most of which are considered beneficial microbes. For example, Subdoligranulum was significantly abundant in both the colonic contents and mucosa of healthy piglets. This genus is known to produce butyric acid which enhances epithelial cell proliferation, promotes mucin secretion, and reduces inflammation to benefit gut function [62]. Although the physiological role of Subdoligranulum remain incompletely understood, increased abundance of this genus has been consistently associated with improved metabolic health [63]. Similarly, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 was another dominant taxon in both colonic contents and mucosa of healthy piglets. Although the exact function of Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 remains unclear, this genus commonly colonizes the intestines of breastfed infants early in life [64], and it is significantly reduced in IBD model mice and necrotizing enterocolitis rats [65, 66], which in in line with our result. In addition, it has been reported to reduce the expression of IL-12, IL-6, and IFN-γ to improve the immunity of the host [67]. Moreover, it can produce volatile fatty acids (VFAs) by metabolizing mucus-derived sugars. VFAs can promote intestinal mucus barrier functions by promoting the growth and repair of intestinal epithelial cells, enhancing mucin secretion, and reducing inflammation [68]. Several studies have identified Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 as a prevalent and beneficial taxon in the intestines of healthy pigs [47, 49]. Furthermore, Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group was the dominant bacterium in the mucosa of healthy piglets. It is known to break down crude fiber and contribute to the regulation of metabolic processes in animals. [69]. Consistent with our results, Huang et al. (2019) reported a reduction of this taxon in diarrheal piglets [49]. In the colonic digesta, Terrisporobacter was enriched in healthy piglets，and it has been implicated in organic matter (OM) degradation and metabolic processes [70]. Collectively, these beneficial bacterial taxa may contribute to maintaining intestinal homeostasis and immune competence in healthy piglets and represent potential microbial targets for improving piglet health.

To better understand the bacterial factors behind the deteriorating health status of diarrheal piglets, we explored the relationships between shifts in microbial abundance and DEGs. Our results showed that most of the DEGs were significantly correlated with microbes in either the colonic contents or mucosa, with the exceptions of IL-5, MAPK10, HSP90αA1, and IL-13. These findings suggest that differentially abundant microbes are primarily associated with common immune related DEGs involved in Th1, Th2, and Th17 cell differentiation, thereby potentially influencing intestinal inflammation. Specifically, Fusobacterium showed the most prominent associations with host immune gene expression. Fusobacterium was the only bacterium that was highly significantly negatively correlated with multiple DEGs, we hypothesized that Fusobacterium may contribute to inflammatory responses in diarrheal piglets. Domingue et al. (2019) reported that Fusobacterium, through its’ surface proteins FadA and Fap2, can modulate Th1 and Th17 immune responses by activating NF-κB signaling [71] and increasing the expressions of IFN-γ and IL-6 [72]. These inflammatory mediators may further affect the expression of immune related genes such as IL-2 Rβ and TBX21, as well as T cell receptor signaling pathways such as LCK signaling pathway [67, 73]. In addition, Brennan et al. (2021) reported that Fusobacterium influences intestinal immunity by shaping Th17 responses through increased expression of IL-17A and IL-17F in a free fatty acid receptor 2 (FFAR2)-dependent manner [74]. Together, these findings are consistent with our finding that Fusobacterium enrichment is associated with diarrhea and is linked to alterations in T cell immune pathways involving IL-2Rβ, TBX21, and LCK. In contrast, several beneficial bacterial taxa enriched in healthy piglets showed significant positive correlations with immune related DEGs, suggesting a protective role in maintaining immune homeostasis. Specifically, Terrisporobacter, Eubacterium_siraeum_group, norank_f__norank_o__Clostridia_vadinBB60_group, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, UCG-005, and Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group presented exhibited correlation patterns opposite to those of Fusobacterium with respect to IL-2Rβ, TBX21, and LCK. Among these taxa, Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, the dominant bacterium in both the colonic mucosa and contents, may play a particularly important role in regulating Th1, Th2, and Th17 cell differentiation. Li et al. (2023) reported that Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 promotes propionate production to support immune function [75]. Propionate can regulate immune responses by binding to the GPR41 receptor on immune cells, suppressing the production of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-6 and TNF-α [76], and promoting regulatory T cell differentiation, which in turn supports balanced Th1 and Th2 immune responses [77]. Such balanced T-cell responses are essential for maintaining normal expression of immune genes including IL-2Rβ, TBX21, and LCK, thereby preventing excessive immune activation and inflammation. Consistent with our findings, Huang et al. (2019) reported that Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 exerted effects opposite to those of the pathogenic genus Escherichia-Shigella, which was positively associated with proinflammatory cytokines and negatively associated with anti-inflammatory cytokines [78]. In addition to their similar correlations with IL-2Rβ, TBX21 and LCK, norank_f__norank_o__Clostridia_vadinBB60_group and Eubacterium_siraeum_group in the colonic mucosa were also positively correlated with IL-2Rα, suggesting a role in maintaining immune competence. Furthermore, Terrisporobacter in the colonic contents and the UCG-005 or Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group in the colonic mucosa showed the positive correlation with CD3D, indicating potential involvement in T-cell signaling. Previous studies have revealed similar results that these taxa can modulate the immune system through their metabolic products. For example, Terrisporobacter is a SCFAs producer that can modulate the immunity of the host via SCFAs [79]. Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group can increase the effects of tryptophan metabolites on T cell differentiation by activating the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AhR) [80, 81]. Taken together, these results suggest that reducing the abundance of Fusobacterium while promoting beneficial taxa, particularly Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, may represent a potential strategy for preventing or alleviating diarrhea in piglets. These beneficial bacteria may help maintain Th1, Th2, and Th17 cell differentiation and counteract the inflammatory effects associated with harmful microbes such as Fusobacterium. However, the sample size in this experiment was limited small which may affect the robustness of the observed associations. Therefore, this study should be regarded as a pilot investigation into microbial-immune interactions in Jinhua piglets. Further research with larger cohorts and targeted intervention experiments, such as supplementation with Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1 as a probiotic, are required to validate these findings and elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Conclusions

In summary, diarrheal Jinhua piglets exhibit poor health status accompanied by elevated levels of intestinal inflammation. These piglets exhibit an increased abundance of harmful bacteria and lower immunity related genes expressions, which indicates the compromised health and heightened inflammation. Harmful bacteria such as Fusobacterium may be associated with Th1, Th2, and Th17 cell differentiation related genes, potentially affecting inflammation. In contrast, bacteria that preferentially colonize the colon of healthy piglets, such as Clostridium_sensu_stricto_1, may exert effects opposite to those of Fusobacterium and potentially support immune function in Jinhua piglets. However, these observations require further validation through targeted probiotic supplementation trials to clarify their exact role in immune regulation and gut health.
