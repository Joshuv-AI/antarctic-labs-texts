Wenyang Decoction Ameliorates DSS‐Induced Ulcerative Colitis by Regulating Macrophage Polarization and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR/HIF‐1α Signaling Pathway
Authors: Shengwei Li, Guanghui Yuan, Chan Chen, Jihong Lu, Xing Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimUlcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory bowel disease characterized by intestinal inflammation and epithelial damage. This study aims to investigate the therapeutic effects of Wenyang decoction (WYD) in a dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)‐induced UC model and its underlying mechanisms, with a focus on macrophage polarization and the regulation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR/HIF‐1α signaling pathway.MethodsA DSS‐induced UC model was established in C57BL/6 mice. Treatment groups received WYD at low (8.25 g/kg/day), medium (16.50 g/kg/day), or high (33.00 g/kg/day) doses, with 5‐aminosalicylic acid (5‐ASA, 200 mg/kg) as a positive control. Assessments included disease activity index (DAI), colon length, histopathology, and levels of inflammatory cytokines (IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α) and oxidative stress markers (SOD, CAT, and MDA). Mechanisms were probed using western blot, qPCR, immunofluorescence, and flow cytometry to analyze macrophage phenotypes (M1/M2) and key signaling pathway proteins.ResultsWYD administration dose‐dependently alleviated UC symptoms, significantly mitigating body weight loss, reducing DAI scores, and restoring colon length. Histological analysis revealed improved mucosal integrity and reduced inflammatory infiltration. Quantitatively, WYD significantly suppressed pro‐inflammatory cytokines (e.g, IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α) and attenuated oxidative stress by enhancing antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD and CAT) and decreasing MDA content. Mechanistically, WYD inhibited M1 macrophage polarization (evidenced by downregulation of inducible nitric oxide synthase [iNOS] and CD16/32) and promoted M2 polarization (upregulation of Arg‐1 and CD206). Furthermore, WYD treatment significantly inhibited the activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and its downstream target, HIF‐1α, both in vivo and in LPS‐stimulated RAW264.7 cells.ConclusionIn summary, our findings indicate that WYD exerts significant therapeutic effects in DSS‐induced UC. WYD alleviates intestinal inflammation and injury by reshaping macrophage polarization, attenuating oxidative stress, and modulating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR/HIF‐1α signaling pathway. This study provides a potential therapeutic strategy for UC in the future.

1. Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory disease resulting from the interaction between genetic predisposition and environmental factors. It is characterized by persistent inflammatory responses in the colorectal mucosa, with lesions appearing continuously, involving the rectum, and to varying degrees, the colon. Clinically, UC is marked by alternating periods of relapse and remission. Epidemiological studies indicate that UC has the highest incidence and prevalence rates in North America and Northern Europe, with an incidence of 9–20 cases per 100,000 individuals and a prevalence of 156–291 cases per 100,000 individuals [1]. In contrast, the incidence of UC is steadily increasing in Asian countries. The etiology of UC is believed to be associated with genetic susceptibility, environmental factors, gut microbiota dysbiosis, and immune system dysfunction. However, its exact biological mechanisms remain unclear. These unresolved mechanisms contribute to a significant decline in patients’ quality of life and pose long‐term challenges due to severe complications, making UC a pressing medical issue that requires effective solutions.

In the pathogenesis of UC, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway and hypoxia‐inducible factor 1 α (HIF‐1 α) play critical roles. One hallmark of UC is excessive inflammatory responses, and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is pivotal in regulating the activation of inflammatory cells and the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, exerting significant modulatory effects in UC‐associated immune inflammation [2, 3]. Studies have demonstrated that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway is activated in both UC patients and dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)‐induced colitis mouse models, leading to the upregulation of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐6 and IL‐1β, thereby exacerbating inflammation [4]. As a key downstream transcription factor of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, HIF‐1 α promotes the survival of intestinal epithelial cells and regulates immune cell functions within hypoxic and inflammatory microenvironments. Evidence suggests that HIF‐1 α is regulated by the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway [5, 6], and other studies indicate that LPS‐induced succinate acts as a signal to enhance IL‐1β expression via HIF‐1 α [7]. Another hallmark of UC is the excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in intestinal epithelial cells, making therapies targeting ROS clearance potentially beneficial for UC. Previous studies have shown that ROS increases the activation of HIF‐1 and NF‐κB, enhancing HIF‐1 stability [8]. Numerous studies have also revealed that the overexpression of HIF‐1 α is closely associated with the clinical manifestations of UC, suggesting that its regulation may serve as a potential therapeutic target for alleviating UC symptoms and improving intestinal function.

Current western medical treatments for UC primarily include aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, immunosuppressants, probiotics, and various biologics. Although these therapies have shown some efficacy, their remission rates remain suboptimal. A proportion of patients develop steroid dependence or resistance, or lose responsiveness to immunosuppressants and biologics, leading to poor prognosis [9]. The Wenyang decoction (WYD) is derived from clinical practice and is specifically designed for the treatment of UC. WYD consists of Codonopsis pilosula, Atractylodes macrocephala, Aucklandia costus, Paeonia lactiflora, Cimicifuga foetida, Dioscorea opposita, Myristica fragrans, Prunus mume, Terminalia chebula, Zingiber officinale, Glycyrrhiza uralensis, et cetera. However, the specific mechanisms by which WYD improves UC through the PI3K/AKT/mTOR/HIF‐1 α pathway remain insufficiently elucidated. Systematic studies on its underlying mechanisms are limited in the existing literature, highlighting the urgent need for further in‐depth investigation.

This study aims to investigate the mechanism by which WYD ameliorates DSS‐induced UC through the regulation of HIF‐1 α via the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway. By employing animal models and molecular biology techniques, we seek to elucidate the potential therapeutic effects of WYD on UC and provide a theoretical foundation for its clinical application.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Chemicals and Reagents

DSS (CAS 9011–18−1, Cat. No. D806297) was obtained from Macklin (Shanghai, China). The 5‐aminosalicylic acid (5‐ASA; CAS 89–57−6, Cat. No. A3537) was sourced from Sigma–Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). WYD was supplied by the Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing University of Chinese Medicine and prepared by its pharmacy department. ELISA kits for detecting IL‐1β (Catalog No. ZC‐37974), IL‐6 (Catalog No. ZC‐37988), and TNF‐α (Catalog No. ZC‐39024) were purchased from Zocai (Shanghai, China). Kits for measuring antioxidant‐related parameters—CAT (Catalog No. BC4785), MDA (Catalog No. BC0025), and SOD (Catalog No. BC0175)—were obtained from Solarbio (Beijing, China). Immunofluorescence antibodies for CD68 (Catalog No. 97778 s) were purchased from CST (Denver, USA), mannose receptor (Catalog No. ab64693) from Abcam (Cambridge, UK), and CD16/32 (Catalog No. 80366 s) from CST (Denver, USA). For western blotting, antibodies for CD206 (Catalog No. 24595, dilution 1:1000), inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS; Catalog No. 13120, dilution 1:1000), p‐PI3K (Catalog No. 17366, dilution 1:1000), and PI3K (Catalog No. ab182651, dilution 1:500) were purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Denver, USA) and Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Antibodies for p‐AKT (Catalog No. 4060, dilution 1:1000), AKT (Catalog No. 9272, dilution 1:1000), p‐mTOR (Catalog No. 5536, dilution 1:1000), mTOR (Catalog No. 2983, dilution 1:1000), HIF‐1 α (Catalog No. 36169, dilution 1:1000), GAPDH (Catalog No. 5174, dilution 1:5000), and anti‐rabbit IgG (H + L) (Catalog No. 14708, dilution 1:5000) were all obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Denver, USA). The source of antibodies and the dilution ratio are detailed in Figure S1.

2.2. High‐Performance Liquid Chromatography Conditions

The samples were separated using C18 and HPLC systems. HPLC separation was achieved using a YMC‐Pack Pro‐C18 column (250 mm × 4.6 mm; 5 μm) equipped with a gradient elution process at the speed of 0.8 mL/min. The mobile phase consisted of solvent A (water with 0.1% formic acid) and solvent B (acetonitrile). The running duration was 40 min with the following gradient: 0–15 min, 5%–20% B, 15–40 min, 20%–32% B, and 5% B; 10 μL was injected and used for the final washing and equilibration. The column temperature was kept at 30°C.

2.3. Animals

A total of 60 specific pathogen‐free (SPF) male C57BL/6 mice were obtained from Jiangsu Ailingfei Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (License No. SCXK(Su)2023‐0020). Animals were maintained under standardized environmental conditions (temperature: 21–23°C; relative humidity: 45%–65%) with a 12 h light/dark cycle and unrestricted access to standard chow and water. All procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. The animal study was designed and conducted in strict accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines to ensure the reliability and reproducibility of the results.

2.4. Induction and Treatment of Colitis

Following a 7‐day adaptation period, the mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 10): Control, DSS, low‐dose WYD (low; 8.25 g/kg/day), medium‐dose WYD (medium; 16.50 g/kg/day), high‐dose WYD (high; 33.00 g/kg/day), and 5‐ASA (200 mg/kg). All groups, except the control group, received 3% DSS in drinking water for 7 days, followed by normal drinking water for the subsequent 3 days to establish the UC model. From day 4 of modeling, 5‐ASA and herbal treatment groups were gavaged daily with the respective doses until day 11, while the control and DSS groups were gavaged with an equivalent volume of saline. During this period, the mice were monitored continuously, and body weight was recorded daily.

2.5. Sample Collection and Preparation

At the end of the experiment, mice were euthanized by injecting pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg; Sigma, P3761), colon tissues were collected from each group, rinsed with cold saline, and the colon length was measured. The middle section of the colon was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, while the remaining tissue was stored at −80°C.

2.6. Assessment of Colitis Severity

The disease activity index (DAI) was used to evaluate the severity of UC based on body weight changes, stool consistency, and the presence of rectal bleeding, according to the criteria outlined in Figure S2.

2.7. Histopathological Analysis

Colon tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. The sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) prior to microscopic examination.

2.8. ELISA Measurement of Cytokines

Colon tissue samples were weighed and homogenized in ice‐cold phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) containing a protease inhibitor (Solebo, A8260) at a working concentration of 1 μg/mL. The homogenates were centrifuged at 5000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and the resulting supernatants were collected for analysis. Levels of IL‐6, IL‐1β, and TNF‐α were measured using commercial ELISA kits in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

2.9. Quantification of CAT, MDA, and SOD

Colon tissue homogenates were prepared, and commercial kits were employed to measure CAT activity, SOD activity, and MDA content according to standard protocols.

2.10. Cell Culture and Treatment

The Raw264.7 macrophage cell line was obtained from the National Center for Cell Cultures (Shanghai, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. To induce the inflammatory model, RAW264.7 cells were treated with 100 ng/mL LPS for 24 h, followed by incubation with drug‐containing serum at 2.5%, 5%, or 7.5% concentrations for 24 h. The cells were then collected for analysis.

2.11. Immunofluorescence

The tissues were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X‐100 for 10 min, blocked with goat serum and used for subsequent experiments. Information, such as antibody dilution ratios, is provided in materials and methods. Paraffin sections were deparaffinized, antigen was repaired (Solebo, C1032), blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide at room temperature for 10 min, blocked with goat serum blocking solution (Sijel, EE0008) at room temperature for 30 min, and then incubated with primary and secondary antibodies, respectively. At the end of the incubation, the TSA working solution diluted in tyrosine buffer was added and incubated at room temperature for 10 min for TSA labeling. If multiple staining was performed, antibody stripping, antigen retrieval, blocking and antibody incubation were repeated. DAPI was used at 1 μg/mL for 5–10 min, and after washing with PBS, sections were sealed with anti‐quenching medium and imaged by fluorescence microscopy after coverslips. 2.12. Flow Cytometry

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using dihydroethidium (DHE) staining. Following a 24 h drug exposure, cells were incubated with 10 μM DHE at 37°C for 30 min. Excess dye was removed by washing twice with PBS. The cells were subsequently trypsinized, centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 5 min, and resuspended in 500 μL PBS. ROS levels were then assessed by flow cytometry using the PE channel, and the proportion of positive cells was determined based on fluorescence intensity.

2.13. Western Blotting

Total protein was extracted from tissues or cells using RIPA lysis buffer, followed by incubation on ice for 20 min. The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C to collect the supernatant. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA protein assay kit and adjusted to 20 mg/mL. A total of 100 μg of protein per lane was loaded and separated by 8% SDS‐PAGE, then transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk prepared in TBST, using 5 mL per blot, for 2 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies at the dilutions specified in the methods section, followed by incubation with corresponding secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Protein bands were visualized using the LI‐COR Odyssey imaging system, and band intensities were quantified with ImageJ software, with GAPDH serving as the loading control.

2.14. Quantitative RT‐PCR

Total RNA was extracted from colon tissues using TRIzol reagent. cDNA synthesis was carried out with a First‐Strand cDNA synthesis Kit, and quantitative PCR was performed with SYBR Green. Gene expression was normalized to GAPDH according to the 2−ΔΔCT method.

2.15. Quantification of Macrophage Polarization

Macrophage subsets were automatically quantified at each field of view (40× magnification) using CellProfiler software. Three images per group were used for statistical analysis. Macrophages were defined as CD68‐positive cells that co‐expressed with the nucleus. M1 and M2 cells were counted as macrophages co‐expressing CD16/32 or CD206, respectively. That is, the number of double‐positive cells was counted.

2.16. Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10 software and are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess statistical differences. A p‐value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

3.2. WYD Significantly Alleviates DSS‐Induced Colitis Symptoms in Mice

We successfully established a DSS‐induced colitis model in mice to evaluate the therapeutic effects of WYD. The results showed that, compared to the DSS group, the WYD groups (8.25, 16.50, and 33.00 g/kg/day) and the 5‐ASA group (200 mg/kg) significantly alleviated DSS‐induced weight loss, diarrhea, and rectal bleeding (Figure 2A–C). Colon length was significantly reduced following DSS treatment compared to the Control group; however, treatment with WYD and 5‐ASA restored colon length, with this restorative effect showing a dose‐dependent relationship for WYD (Figure 2D,E). Optical microscopy of colon tissue revealed that the Control group exhibited well‐organized and intact colonic mucosal structures with no significant pathological changes or inflammatory cell infiltration. In contrast, DSS treatment caused irregular and damaged mucosal structures, with substantial inflammatory cell infiltration and crypt loss. WYD and 5‐ASA treatments notably improved mucosal damage, inflammatory cell infiltration, and crypt loss (Figure 2F). These results suggest that WYD effectively ameliorates DSS‐induced colitis in mice, and this effect is dose‐dependent.

3.3. WYD Modulates Inflammatory Cytokines and Reduces Oxidative Stress in DSS‐Induced Colitis

Inflammatory responses play a critical role in UC pathogenesis through immune dysregulation and an imbalance of pro‐inflammatory factors, leading to disruption of the intestinal mucosal barrier, microenvironment imbalance, and chronic inflammation, which ultimately exacerbates tissue damage. In DSS‐treated mice, levels of pro‐inflammatory markers, such as TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6, were significantly higher than the Control group. However, treatment with WYD at different doses significantly reduced these markers (Figure 3A–C). Oxidative stress is a major pathogenic mechanism in UC, with excessive ROS production causing damage to the colonic mucosal barrier [10]. We found that, compared to the Control group, DSS‐treated mice exhibited significant oxidative stress, as indicated by reduced levels of CAT and SOD and increased MDA levels. As expected, WYD treatment reversed these effects (Figure 3D–F). ROS levels in the DSS group were significantly higher than the Control group, but WYD treatment reduced ROS levels, with the lowest ROS content observed in the high‐dose group (Figure 3G,H). Therefore, WYD suppresses the production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, increases CAT and SOD levels, and reduces ROS content, thereby alleviating oxidative stress and improving DSS‐induced colitis.

3.4. WYD Modulates M1/M2 Macrophage Polarization in Colon Tissue

Macrophages play a crucial role in the onset and progression of UC, and targeting macrophage polarization is a key strategy for UC treatment [11]. We assessed whether WYD regulates the M1/M2 macrophage balance in colon tissue. In this study, M1 macrophage markers CD68 and CD16/32 were labeled with fluorescence. The DSS group exhibited higher relative expression of CD68 and CD16/32, which was significantly reduced following treatment with WYD (16.50 and 33.00 g/kg/day) (Figure 4A,B), suggesting that WYD inhibits M1 macrophage polarization. Fluorescent labeling of M2 macrophage markers, CD206 and CD68, revealed that WYD treatment significantly increased the relative expression of CD206 and CD68 (Figure 4C,D), indicating that WYD promotes M2 macrophage polarization. Collectively, these data suggest that WYD alleviates colitis by selectively modulating the balance between M1 and M2 macrophages.

3.5. WYD Affects the PI3K/AKT/mTOR Pathway and HIF‐1α in DSS‐Induced Colitis

Western blotting analysis revealed that DSS treatment upregulated the protein levels of CD206 and iNOS, whereas WYD treatment significantly enhanced CD206 expression and suppressed iNOS levels in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 5A–C). To further explore the potential mechanisms underlying WYD’s therapeutic effect on UC, we hypothesized that the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway might be involved. Western blotting was used to detect the levels of proteins involved in the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and HIF‐1α in vivo and in vitro. In vivo, compared to the Control group, protein levels of PI3K (Figure 5D,G), AKT (Figure 5E,H), mTOR (Figure 5F,I), and HIF‐1α (Figure 5J,K) were significantly increased following DSS treatment, suggesting that DSS activates the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and promotes HIF‐1α expression. However, WYD treatment inhibited DSS‐induced activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and reduced HIF‐1α expression. Dose‐dependent reductions in these proteins following WYD treatment confirmed this effect (Figure 5D–K). To validate whether the protective effects of WYD on UC in vitro are mediated by the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, we also measured PI3K, AKT, mTOR, and HIF‐1 α levels in RAW264.7 cells. The results were consistent with the in vivo findings (Figure 6A–H).

3.6. WYD Inhibits Inflammatory Cytokines and Oxidative Stress in RAW264.7 Cells In Vitro

To elucidate the potential cellular mechanisms of WYD in anti‐inflammatory and antioxidant effects, we measured the levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6 in RAW264.7 cells. The results showed that LPS treatment significantly increased the levels of TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6 compared to the Control group. As expected, WYD treatment at various doses significantly reduced the levels of these inflammatory cytokines in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 7A–C). Additionally, we found that LPS treatment significantly decreased CAT and SOD levels in RAW264.7 cells while increasing MDA levels. WYD treatment reversed these changes (Figure 7D–F). To explore the effect of WYD on ROS levels at the cellular level, we measured ROS levels using flow cytometry. LPS treatment significantly increased ROS levels, whereas WYD treatment reduced ROS levels in a dose‐dependent manner (Figure 7G,H). These results suggest that WYD alleviates colitis by effectively inhibiting inflammatory responses and reducing oxidative stress in RAW264.7 cells.

3.7. WYD Reshapes M1/M2 Macrophage Polarization Balance In Vitro

Typical markers of M1 macrophages include iNOS, TNF‐α, and IL‐12p40. To further confirm the inhibitory effect of WYD on M1 macrophage polarization, we evaluated the expression of these markers in RAW264.7 cells using qPCR. The results showed that LPS treatment significantly increased the expression of iNOS, TNF‐α, and IL‐12p40, while medium and high doses of WYD inhibited their expression (Figure 8A–C). Furthermore, Arg‐1, Fizz‐1, and Ym‐1 are typical genes of M2 macrophages. As expected, WYD treatment enhanced the expression of Arg‐1, Fizz‐1, and Ym‐1 (Figure 8D–F). These findings indicate that WYD inhibits M1 macrophage polarization and promotes M2 macrophage polarization in vitro, reshaping the M1/M2 macrophage polarization balance.

3.8. WYD Exerts Anti‐Inflammatory Effects by Inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR Pathway and HIF‐1α Expression

To further investigate whether the protective effect of WYD on DSS‐induced colitis is mediated by the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway and HIF‐1 α, we used the cell‐permeable PI3K activator YP740 to activate the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway. The results showed that co‐treatment with WYD and YP740 reversed the inhibitory effect of WYD on HIF‐1 α expression (Figure 9A,C). Similarly, co‐treatment with WYD and YP740 reversed the inhibitory effect of WYD on AKT (Figure 9B,D). We then assessed the levels of TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and IL‐6 in RAW264.7 cells after YP740 intervention, and the results showed that YP740 intervention counteracted the inhibitory effect of WYD on these cytokines (Figure 9E–G). Interestingly, qPCR results showed that YP740 intervention reversed the inhibitory effects of WYD on iNOS and TNF‐α expression (Figure 9H,I) and counteracted the promoting effect of WYD on Arg‐1 and Ym‐1 expression (Figure 9J,K). These findings suggest that WYD inhibits the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, thereby reducing HIF‐1 α expression and decreasing IL‐1β production to improve DSS‐induced UC.

4. Discussion

This study comprehensively examined the therapeutic effects of WYD on UC and its underlying mechanisms in both in vivo and in vitro models. Initially, we established a DSS‐induced UC mouse model to assess WYD’s therapeutic efficacy. We found that WYD significantly reduced DAI and histopathological scores, suppressed pro‐inflammatory cytokines, alleviated oxidative stress, and notably improved body weight and colon length in UC mice. Mechanistically, we demonstrated that WYD reshapes the M1/M2 macrophage polarization balance, inhibits the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, and reduces the expression of HIF‐1α, thereby decreasing IL‐1β production and improving UC.

UC pathogenesis is primarily defined by intestinal mucosal inflammation, compromised barrier integrity, altered epithelial morphology, defective cell junctions, and disrupted intestinal homeostasis [12]. Intestinal inflammation perturbs the equilibrium between gut antigens and host immune responses [13], compromising the intestinal mechanical barrier and allowing bacterial or metabolic stimuli to activate the immune system, which in turn triggers the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, such as IL‐1β, IL‐6, and TNF‐α, further exacerbating inflammation. Additionally, an imbalance between pro‐ and anti‐inflammatory cytokines contributes to the persistence of intestinal inflammation [14]. Consequently, inhibiting pro‐inflammatory cytokines could slow disease progression and alleviate or even reverse UC symptoms. Previous studies have shown that Rhein significantly reduces LPS‐induced production of IL‐6, IL‐1β, and TNF‐α in a dose‐dependent manner by downregulating pro‐inflammatory cytokines in vitro, thereby effectively alleviating inflammation [15]. Our results confirm that WYD similarly inhibits the production of pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐6, IL‐1β, and TNF‐α), thus mitigating intestinal inflammation, which is consistent with previous findings.

The role of imbalanced macrophage polarization in UC pathogenesis has garnered increasing attention in recent years. Macrophages can be classified into pro‐inflammatory M1 and anti‐inflammatory M2 phenotypes based on their functional roles. Emerging research suggests that M1 macrophages may impair stem cell regeneration during intestinal epithelial repair, thereby aggravating the pathological progression of UC [16, 17]. Furthermore, recent findings indicate that the immune regulatory function of M2 macrophages is dependent on gut microbiota metabolites (such as short‐chain fatty acids and indole derivatives), which are significantly reduced in UC patients, limiting the functionality of M2 macrophages [18]. Reports have shown that astragaloside IV inhibits M1 macrophage polarization through the STAT signaling pathway and enhances M2 macrophage activity, thereby improving experimental colitis [19]. Similarly, Resatorvid treatment has been found to significantly reduce M1 macrophage polarization, inhibit Th1 and Th17 cell infiltration, increase Th2 cell infiltration, and promote M2 macrophage polarization [20]. In this study, we observed that WYD increased the expression of M2 macrophage markers (CD206 and CD68) in the colon and decreased M1 macrophage marker expression (CD68 and CD16/32). Additionally, using RAW264.7 cells to establish an inflammatory macrophage model, we found that WYD inhibited the expression of M1 macrophage markers (iNOS, TNF‐α, and IL‐12p40) while enhancing the gene expression of M2 macrophage markers (Arg‐1, Fizz‐1, and Ym‐1), indicating that WYD modulates the M1/M2 macrophage polarization balance to improve UC.

Recent studies have highlighted the critical role of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway in UC pathogenesis, primarily by regulating immune responses, intestinal epithelial cell growth and repair, and the persistence of inflammation. PI3K, as a key upstream molecule in the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, activates a cascade reaction by phosphorylating phosphatidylinositol‐4,5‐bisphosphate (PIP2) to form PIP3, which recruits PDK1 and AKT to the cell membrane, leading to PDK1‐dependent activation of AKT. AKT then phosphorylates and inhibits apoptosis, blocks cell cycle proteins, activates mTOR, and regulates the expression of downstream genes, such as HIF‐1α, contributing to tumor development [21]. Previous research has shown that Moringin treatment reduces p‐PI3K, p‐AKT, and p‐mTOR levels, suppressing UC development by inhibiting the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway [22]. Rhein treatment similarly inhibits the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway by decreasing the phosphorylation of PI3K, AKT, mTOR, and p70S6K1, exerting anti‐UC effects [15]. Notably, under normoxic conditions, HIF‐1α accumulates in all pro‐inflammatory immune cells (including macrophages) and regulates the transcription of inflammatory genes such as IL‐1β. Inhibition of HIF‐1α significantly reduces IL‐1β expression, thus alleviating inflammation [7, 23–26]. Our findings indicate that WYD inhibits the activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway, thereby reducing HIF‐1α expression and inhibiting IL‐1β production to improve UC.

Compared to current UCUC therapies, such as 5‐ASA, corticosteroids, and biologics, WYD represents a promising alternative, particularly for patients with steroid dependence or resistance. While 5‐ASA and corticosteroids are associated with limited long‐term efficacy and side effects including immunosuppression, biologics, such as TNF‐α inhibitors, entail high costs and potential adverse events. As a traditional Chinese medicine, WYD modulates immune responses and oxidative stress, suggesting a potentially safer and more accessible option for UC management. Its action on macrophage polarization and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR/HIF‐1α pathway provides a distinct mechanism that may complement existing therapies, potentially improving outcomes while reducing adverse effects. However, this study has several limitations. Although the effects of WYD on macrophage polarization and the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway were demonstrated, further causal validation using gene knockout models or specific inhibitors is required. Additionally, the toxicity of WYD remains unassessed; toxicological studies are essential to evaluate its safety profile for long‐term use. Furthermore, the chemical standardization of WYD has not been fully established, and variability in herbal composition may affect reproducibility. Future studies should focus on standardizing the formulation and identifying active constituents to clarify the basis of its therapeutic effects.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, our results confirm the therapeutic effects of WYD in DSS‐induced UC. Our data suggest that WYD mitigates UC by reshaping the M1/M2 macrophage polarization balance, reducing oxidative stress, and modulating the PI3K/AKT/mTOR/HIF‐1α signaling pathway, thereby reducing the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and intestinal damage. Overall, this study provides a potential therapeutic strategy for UC.
