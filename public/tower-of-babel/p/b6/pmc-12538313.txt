Destructive upper limb malignant ulcer: A case report of angiosarcoma

Introduction

Angiosarcomas are rare malignant neoplasms arising from vascular or lymphatic endothelial cells, characterised by aggressive local invasion and a high propensity for early metastasis [1]. Although they can occur in any region of the body, they more commonly affect the head and neck, particularly the scalp and face in elderly individuals [2]. Presentation in the distal upper extremity, including the hand and forearm, is uncommon and thus not frequently encountered in clinical practice [2].

The clinical features of angiosarcoma can be highly variable, often mimicking benign dermatological conditions or chronic non-healing wounds, which may contribute to diagnostic delays [3]. Early identification and intervention are essential, as prognosis is closely tied to tumor size, grade, and the ability to achieve clear surgical margins [4]. In many low-resource settings, however, the lack of routine access to advanced diagnostic modalities, histopathological services, and oncological care can hinder timely diagnosis and comprehensive management [5].

This report presents a challenging case of upper limb angiosarcoma in a middle-aged woman, illustrating the consequences of delayed recognition, fragmented care, and socioeconomic barriers to diagnosis and treatment. It serves to highlight the critical need for heightened clinical suspicion in chronic cutaneous lesions and emphasises the role of multidisciplinary collaboration, even within the constraints of limited resources.

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 checklist [6].

Case presentation

A 43-year-old female presented with a 4-year progressive history of an ulcer extending from the palm to approximately 10 cm below the elbow on her left upper limb. The condition began as a spontaneous blister on the left wrist, which gradually increased in size and eventually burst open to form an ulcer. Over time, the ulcer progressed into a fungating lesion on her hand.

She had visited a primary health center on two occasions, where the lesion was resected on both occasions, but it was never sent for histopathological analysis despite its recurrence. The condition was accompanied by a yellowish discharge that was foul-smelling and bled easily upon touch. Throughout the illness, she applied various herbal and over-the-counter medications, but found no relief.

The lesion worsened over time, leading to swelling of the left upper limb up to the shoulder joint, ultimately rendering the limb dysfunctional. The bleeding intensified two days prior to admission, necessitating her referral to our health facility for specialized care. She had no significant past medical history of chronic illnesses or long-term medication use.

On general examination, she was fully conscious and oriented, afebrile, severely pale per conjunctiva, not jaundiced, not cyanosed, and no lower limb oedema. There were no peripheral enlarged lymph nodes appreciated. Her resting blood pressure and pulse rate were 112/55 mmHg and 135 beats/min respectively. Her axillary temperature was 36.9 °C and was saturating at 99 % on room air. Locally, there was an extensive fungating mass on her left hand with necrotic tissues, foul-smelling purulent discharge, easily bleeding on touch with no function of the hand due to its gross distortion (Fig. 1). The rest of the left upper limb was edematous to the shoulder. Her baseline laboratory results revealed a hemoglobin level of 3.9 g/dl, leukocyte count of 29.3 × 109/l and thrombocytosis of 1240 × 109/l. Her serum creatinine was 238 μmol/l, blood urea nitrogen of 10.59 mmol/l and serum potassium of 7.03 mmol/l. X-ray of the left hand and forearm revealed a huge lobulated soft tissue mass associated with carpal bone destruction, highly suggestive of a malignant process (Fig. 2). Plain chest X-ray and abdominal ultrasound done for metastatic work up were essentially normal with no obvious nodules. She was admitted to the surgical department and initiated local hyperkalemia protocol, tranexamic acid and intravenous vitamin K to prevent bleeding from the fungating mass. Pus swab was taken for culture and sensitivity and was empirically started on intravenous Ceftriaxone and Metronidazole. She was transfused four units of whole blood over six days and an incisional biopsy was done. Histological examination of the incisional biopsy showed a skin tissue with superficial ulceration. The underlying layer had infiltration by large pleomorphic cells with large eosinophilic nucleoli accompanied by marketable number of mitotic figures. Morphology was suggestive of malignant melanoma. After tumor board discussion with the oncologists, surgeons and dermatologists, the plan was to amputate the left upper limb. She was counseled and informed consent was obtained for both below elbow and trans-humeral depending on the intra operative findings.

Below elbow amputation was scheduled and intraoperatively, fish-mouth below elbow incision was made, anterior compartment was opened and fungating tumor infiltrating anterior compartment muscles of forearm was encountered that was easily pliable. Another fish-mouth incision was made 10 cm above elbow joint in attempt to achieve free margin resection. Anterior and posterior compartments were dissected to the level of humerus. Trans-Humeral amputation was performed and myodesis was done. The fascia layers were closed, stump was then washed with normal saline and skin was closed. The resected part of the limb was sent to histopathology.

The amputated specimen sections demonstrated an invasive tumor ulcerating the skin. Individual cells were large, spindle or epithelial with high endothelial venules. Mitoses were frequent. Resection margins were negative. Morphological features were pointing to high grade malignant tumor, most likely angiosarcoma, with differential diagnosis of malignant melanoma (Fig. 3). Immunohistochemistry was positive for CD31 and CD34, and negative for HMB 45. Post operatively developed superficial surgical site infection. Pus swab was taken for culture that grew Citrobacter freundii ESBL positive that was sensitive to meropenem and piperacillin/tazobactam. She was treated with intravenous Meropenem for 10 days, then discharged home with a healed stump. She was reviewed two weeks later at our surgical outpatient clinic whereby the stump had healed with no edema and had adequate range of movement at the shoulder joint. She was then referred to the oncology, physiotherapy and occupational therapy units for on-going management and rehabilitation but was unfortunately lost to follow up.

Discussion

Angiosarcoma is an uncommon and highly aggressive malignancy of endothelial origin, accounting for less than 2 % of all soft tissue sarcomas [7,8]. Its presentation in the distal upper limb, particularly the hand and forearm, is extremely rare and typically associated with diagnostic delay due to its nonspecific initial features [3,8]. In this case, a 43-year-old woman presented with a chronic, neglected ulcerative lesion that progressed over several years into a fungating mass of the distal upper limb, later confirmed as a high-grade angiosarcoma.

The disease initially manifested as a spontaneous blister on the wrist, which was resected twice without histopathological evaluation. This lack of appropriate diagnostic investigation represents a significant missed opportunity for early detection and intervention. Such oversight is not uncommon in under-resourced settings, where primary care providers may lack access to diagnostic tools or protocols for handling chronic or recurrent lesions [9].

The patient's lesion progressed to involve the entire hand and distal half of the forearm with extensive soft tissue destruction and rendered the limb non-functional. By the time of presentation to our tertiary facility, the tumor had grown to such an extent that limb preservation was no longer viable. A trans-humeral amputation was performed to achieve oncologic clearance, which was later confirmed histologically by negative margins.

Histologically, angiosarcomas can pose a diagnostic challenge due to their overlapping morphology with other spindle cell malignancies such as malignant melanoma [10]. In this case, an initial suspicion of melanoma based on cellular pleomorphism was revised following immunohistochemical staining. Tumor cells were positive for endothelial markers CD31 and CD34 and negative for HMB-45, confirming a diagnosis of angiosarcoma. CD31, in particular, is regarded as the most specific marker for endothelial differentiation [11].

Optimal management of angiosarcoma includes wide surgical excision with negative margins, often followed by systemic chemotherapy or radiotherapy [12]. Unfortunately, staging was limited in this patient due to financial constraints, precluding a contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis. As a result, a plain chest X-ray and abdominal ultrasound were used instead, which are considerably less sensitive for detecting distant metastases [13]. This limitation underscores a persistent challenge in low-resource settings, where accurate staging and full assessment of disease burden may be compromised [5].

Furthermore, the patient did not return for post-operative follow-up, and thus discussions regarding adjuvant systemic therapy or enrolment in surveillance programmes could not be pursued. This highlights another critical issue—patient retention and continuity of care—particularly for individuals from economically disadvantaged backgrounds [5]. Without appropriate follow-up, opportunities for early detection of recurrence or metastasis are lost, likely contributing to poorer long-term outcomes [4].

Conclusion

This case illustrates the multifaceted challenges associated with diagnosing and managing rare malignancies like angiosarcoma in resource-limited settings. It underscores the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for malignant transformation in chronic ulcers, the necessity of histopathological evaluation for recurrent lesions, and the essential role of multidisciplinary care. It also serves as a reminder of how socioeconomic constraints can hinder staging, treatment, and follow-up, all of which are critical to improving survival in aggressive sarcomas.

Limitations

Failure to obtain funding to do staging CT scan obscures assessment for metastasis in this case, especially given the early metastasis tendency of angiosarcomas.
