Porous Ceramics from Iron Tailings Using Triethanolamine Dodecyl Sulfate as a Foaming Agent and Xanthan Gum as a Curing Agent
Authors: Jiadong Shao, Lijun Zheng, Qingdong Hou, Yimeng Gao, Jiayin Qu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Anshan iron tailings were used as the primary raw material to efficiently and comprehensively utilize secondary resources from iron tailings. Triethanolamine dodecyl sulfate (TLS) served as the foaming agent, and xanthan gum (XG) as the curing agent. The iron tailings porous ceramics were then prepared through ball milling, foaming, gel injection molding, and sintering. Experiments were conducted to explore the relationships and patterns among process factors, such as the amount of foaming agent, as well as the microscopic structure and physical properties of the iron tailings porous ceramics. The results showed that at a sintering temperature of 1150 °C with 2% foaming agent, the porous ceramics made from iron tailings achieved the most uniform pore-size distribution and the best overall performance. Under these conditions, the bulk density of the ceramic was 0.839 g/cm3, the apparent porosity was 62.42%, and the compressive strength was 8.11 MPa. Compared with previously reported iron-tailings-based ceramics, the obtained materials exhibit superior performance, indicating promising potential for lightweight structural applications.

Introduction

Iron tailings are a type of solid waste discharged after a series of beneficiation processes during iron ore processing. , A large amount of untreated iron tailings is typically concentrated and piled in tailing ponds, occupying large areas of land and causing significant environmental pollution and ecological damage. − In recent years, the preparation of porous ceramics using various tailings as raw materials has received increasing attention. , Porous ceramics, with their high porosity, low bulk density, large specific surface area, strong corrosion resistance, chemical stability, and excellent thermal insulation properties, are therefore widely used in various environmental, energy, construction, and chemical engineering fields. − Iron tailings are rich in SiO2, Al2O3, and other alkali metal oxides, , and the use of iron tailings as raw materials for ceramic production can significantly reduce manufacturing costs. Yin et al. prepared foamed glass using iron tailings and waste glass as the main raw materials, with silicon carbide as the foaming agent. At a sintering temperature of 830 °C, the obtained foamed glass possesses a bulk density of 0.224 g/cm3 and a flexural strength of 0.53 MPa. Meng et al. used iron tailings from the Hebei region of China as the main raw material, and introduced iron ore, aluminum oxide, potassium feldspar, and kaolin as auxiliary additives. Using a semidry pressing process followed by pressureless sintering, they fabricated a new type of heat-absorption functional ceramic for solar energy applications. Li et al. utilized iron tailings from Panzhihua of Sichuan Province, China, as the raw material and silicon carbide (SiC) as the foaming agent. By adding Clay and quartz to the mixture, porous ceramics with a uniform pore-size distribution were obtained at a sintering temperature of 1130 °C. The resulting ceramics had a bulk density of 0.77 g/cm3, a water absorption rate of 14.25%, and a compressive strength of 7.65 MPa.

The accumulation of iron tailings causes environmental problems, and, at the same time, realizing their high added value is desired. Therefore, in this study, Anshan iron ore tailings were used as the primary raw material, without the addition of large quantities of conventional ceramic auxiliary materials including clay, feldspar and quartz. This formulation design greatly simplifies the recipe system and substantially reduces the production cost. Additionally, TLS was selected as the foaming agent, XG as the curing agent, and the gel-casting process was used to systematically prepare porous ceramics from iron tailings. By varying the foaming agent content from 0% to 3% and the sintering temperature from 1050 to 1200 °C, the effects and underlying mechanisms of these parameters on the ceramic microstructure, including pore size and distribution, and macroscopic physical properties such as bulk density, apparent porosity, and compressive strength, were systematically studied. Based on these results, the material with optimal, comprehensive performance was identified, and its potential applications in the construction industry were further evaluated.

Experimental Section

Raw Materials

In this experiment, iron tailings with a particle size of 45 μm from Anshan of Liaoning Province, China, were used. The detailed chemical composition of iron tailings is shown in Table . The SiO2 content of these tailings was as high as 67.58% by mass, indicating that they can be classified as high-silicon iron tailings. Triethanolamine dodecyl sulfate (TLS, analytical grade) was used as the foaming agent and purchased from Asahi Kasei Chemical Company in Japan. TLS is an anionic surfactant that exhibits excellent foaming capacity and favorable thermal decomposition behavior. XG is a biopolymer thickener capable of enhancing slurry viscosity and stabilizing bubble structures throughout the gel-casting foaming process.Xanthan gum (XG, analytical grade) was used as the curing agent and purchased from Shanghai Shangpu Chemical Company.

Preparation Methods

The iron tailings were first dried, then ground at 1000 r/min for 1 h to obtain fine particles with a size of approximately 200 mesh. The experimental raw materials were prepared as shown in Table . An 0–3% amount of foaming agent (TLS) was subsequently added to the raw materials to produce bubbles during mechanical stirring at 1000 r/min for 10 min.Then, the stirring was temporarily stopped, and the curing agent (XG) was added to stabilize the bubbles. The stirring was then resumed, and once the process was complete, the slurry was poured into a mold with a diameter and height of 50 mm each. The slurry was compacted at 15 MPa to form a preform block, which was initially dried at room temperature for 24 h. The block was subsequently dried in an oven at 40 °C for 24 h, followed by further drying at 100 °C for an additional 24 h. After drying, the block was placed in a muffle furnace for sintering. To determine the optimal sintering conditions, dried blocks were sintered at 1050, 1100, 1150, and 1200 °C, respectively. The sintering time for each sample was set at 2 h, with a heating rate of 10 °C/min. After allowing the furnace to cool naturally to room temperature, the sintered block was removed for subsequent performance testing. Testing and Analysis

The phase composition of the sintered products was characterized using a Panalytical X’pert Powder X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Netherlands) with Cu Kα radiation. The microstructure of the samples was examined using a SUPRA 55 thermal field emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, Germany). A ball mill (Model XQM-2, Changsha Tianchuang Powder Technology Co., Ltd., China) and an electric stirrer (Model Z89–1, Tianjin Lihua Instrument Factory, China) were employed for the sample preparation. The apparent porosity and bulk density of the sintered samples were measured in accordance with GB/T 2997–2015, and the room-temperature compressive strength was determined in accordance with GB/T 5072.2–2004. The statistical data on the pore size distribution of the samples were obtained through the image analysis software (ImageJ).

Results and Discussion

Phase Analysis of Iron Tailings Porous Ceramic Materials

Figure shows the XRD patterns of the samples after 2 h of sintering at different temperatures. As shown in Figure , when the sintering temperature increases from 1050 to 1100 °C, the phase composition of the samples remains almost unchanged. Moreover, the crystal phases show no obvious temperature-dependent variation. The dominant crystalline phase in the samples is quartz, with secondary crystal phases including Fe2O3 and Fe3O4. When the sintering temperature is increased to 1150 °C, the diffraction peak intensities of cristobalite and other crystal phases increase significantly. In contrast, the quartz diffraction peaks gradually weaken, indicating a phase transformation from quartz to cristobalite. During sintering, quartz can react with silicate and other substances to form a low-melting eutectic mixture. Therefore, at this temperature, a larger proportion of SiO2 participates in the reaction, generating a liquid phase, decreasing the quartz phase, and leading to a rapid increase in the liquid phase content. The molten liquid phase fills the interparticle voids, promoting sintering shrinkage and densifying the ribbon-like skeleton. However, the glass phase is amorphous, making its presence difficult to detect in the XRD patterns (Figure ). When the sintering temperature is further raised to 1200 °C, the diffraction peaks of quartz disappear entirely. In addition, the intensities of the diffraction peaks of the other phases are further enhanced, confirming that quartz has completely transformed into cristobalite. The entire sintering process can be summarized as follows. During the low-temperature stage, the adsorbed water and bound water in the raw materials gradually evaporate. As a result, organic substances decompose, releasing CO2, H2O, and other gases. Moreover, as the temperature increases further, minerals decompose and undergo phase transformations, leading to the formation of new crystalline phases and a liquid glass phase.

XRD patterns of the samples sintered at different temperatures.

Figure shows the XRD patterns of the samples with foaming agent contents ranging from 0% to 3% after sintering at 1150 °C for 2 h, to examine the effect of the foaming agent on the phase composition of the sintered products. As the foaming agent content increases from 0% to 3%, no significant changes in the crystalline phases of the samples are observed. The primary crystal phases are quartz and orthoclase, and the secondary crystal phases are Fe2O3 and Fe3O4. The sample ingredients are organic components that are oxidized during the high-temperature pyrolysis process to produce volatile gases. This observation confirms that TLS underwent complete decomposition during the high-temperature sintering process, without introducing any impurity phases, thus preserving the intrinsic phase purity of the ceramic matrix.

Microstructure of Iron Tailings Porous Ceramics

Figure shows the SEM images of the sintered samples at different sintering temperatures. As shown, the pores inside the samples are composed of irregular, large spherical pores and micropores. The large spherical pores are generated by the addition of foaming and curing agents, as well as by mechanical stirring. Micro pores are produced when CaCO3 and dolomite decompose, and organic matter is removed at high temperatures. To further determine the distribution of hole sizes, the microholes in the sample were statistically analyzed using ImageJ and Origin software. The results are shown in the inset of the SEM image (Figures and ). The spherical pores have a size distribution between 100 and 300 μm. When the sintering temperature is between 1050 and 1100 °C, the pores are smaller and mostly elongated due to the high viscosity of the melt, which leads to uneven gas permeation on both sides. At a sintering temperature of 1150 °C, the bubbles in the melt grow homogeneously, forming spherical closed pores. When the sintering temperature is raised to 1200 °C, as shown in Figure (d), the number of internal bubbles in the sample skyrockets, leading to excessively thin bubble walls. At the same time, the viscosity and surface tension of the liquid phase also decrease excessively. The bubbles burst through the liquid film, forming through-holes or escaping from the sample body. The bubbles become irregular, and the sample collapses. Macroscopically, the porosity decreases, and the average pore diameter continues to increase. As a result, the bulk density increases, but the compressive strength continues to decrease. Therefore, the optimal sintering temperature is 1150 °C.

SEM images of the sintered samples with different contents of foaming agent: (a) 0%, (b) 1%, (c) 2%, and (d) 3%.

Figure shows SEM images of sintered samples with different foaming-agent contents. SEM images show that without a foaming agent, CaCO3 and dolomite decompose at high temperatures. However, the reduction of high-valent iron releases gas, generating many uniformly sized micropores. When the foaming agent content is 1% or 2%, the proportion of larger pores increases. At 3% foaming agent (Figure (d)), the amount of produced gas increases. The gas pressure within the clay body exceeds the surface tension of the liquid phase. As a result, the gas breaks through the liquid-phase encapsulation, forming pores that continue to merge into larger pores, and some pores coalesce, even rupturing pore walls. It significantly affects the properties of porous ceramic materials. Therefore, the optimal additional amount of the foaming agent is 2%.

Figure shows the macroscopic photos of the porous ceramic samples prepared from iron tailings. Because the raw iron tailings contain some hematite, the porous ceramic samples appear brown. As shown in Figure (b), increasing the content of foaming agent in the slurry raises the apparent porosity of the samples, and the pore size becomes larger. When the foaming agent is 3%, many three-dimensionally connected collapsed pores are observed on the fracture surface, distributed in a highly irregular manner. As shown in Figure (a), with increasing sintering temperature, the apparent porosity of the samples decreases, the pore structure becomes more uniform, and the pore size gradually decreases.

Physical Properties of Iron Tailings Porous Ceramics

Figure (a) shows that the bulk density of the sample increases with increasing sintering temperature. It increases from 0.71 g/cm3 at 1050 °C to 0.90 g/cm3 at 1200 °C. Because an increase in sintering temperature causes volume shrinkage, the sample then fills the pores with a liquid phase, making its structure more compact. As the foaming agent content increases from 0% to 3%, more pores form in the slurry. It then increases the porosity from 48.81% to 72.73% and lowers the density from 1.35 g/cm3 to 0.66 g/cm3 in the samples. As shown in Figure (a,b), the apparent porosity of the sample increases and the bulk density decreases with the increase of foaming agent content. As shown in Figure (b,c), the apparent porosity of the samples decreases with increasing sintering temperature. The apparent porosity of the samples decreased from 71.63% at 1050 °C to 58.97% at 1200 °C. However, the compressive strength of the samples increases from 3.53 to 8.62 MPa with the increase in sintering temperature from 1050 to 1200 °C It is because of the high temperatures that quartz can react with silicate to form a low eutectic compound. Therefore, at these temperatures, more SiO2 participates in the reaction, forming a liquid phase. As the liquid phase rapidly increases in volume, it fills the gaps between solid particles, connecting them more tightly. Eventually, the body becomes denser. In addition, the liquid phase slightly blocks the pores as well. Therefore, the apparent porosity gradually decreases. As a result, the strength and hardness increase continuously, leading to significant changes in the apparent porosity and compressive strength of the porous ceramics. Notably, the compressive strength of the sample decreases with increasing foaming agent content. When the amount of the foaming agent increases from 0% to 3%, the compressive strength of the sample decreases from 9.51 to 3.87 MPa. It occurs because the excessive foaming agent causes the pores in the sample to merge, significantly enlarging pore size, and leads to a loose framework.

Analysis of Pore Formation

Large spherical pores form upon the introduction of foaming agent TLS. Bubbles are generated in the mixture when 2% foaming agent (TLS) is added to the raw materials and mechanical stirring is performed. Then, by adding a curing agent (XG) to stabilize the bubbles and mechanically stirring again, a three-phase slurry of solid, liquid, and gas with sufficient fluidity can be obtained. The slurry-foamed body is solidified in a mold to obtain a preform body with high microscopic homogeneity and a high-quality inner reticular structure, which is then sintered.

The early stage of micropore formation is observed, representing CaCO3/MgCO3 decomposition and the oxidation of iron ions. At 700 °C, CaCO3 and dolomite decompose, and the oxidation of iron ions releases gas, forming gas-pore nuclei. At this stage, a large amount of viscous liquid phase forms, and the gas pores grow slowly as the temperature rises. At higher temperatures, CaCO3 fully decomposes, initially forming various macroscopic gas pores. If the liquid-phase viscosity is sufficiently high, the reaction gases become encapsulated by the liquid phase, and some through-holes form as the gas escapes.

The details of the later stage of micropore formation are now observed during the high Fe3+ reduction reaction. As the temperature rises, the reduction reaction of high-valent iron releases gas, which forms micropores along the pore diameter direction. After that, the temperature-holding operation can stabilize the growth of the gas pores. After natural cooling to room temperature, a finished product with uniform pore distribution and high porosity can enhance the filtration and heat insulation properties of ceramics. ,

As shown in Table , the bulk density (0.839 g/cm3) and compressive strength (8.11 MPa) of the sample in this work fully satisfy the standard requirements, with the compressive strength far exceeding the minimum threshold. Yang et al. and Zhao et al. prepared iron tailings-based porous ceramics with porosities of 79.22% and 78% via the direct foaming method and solid-state sintering method, respectively. However, the bulk densities (0.49 g/cm3, 0.45 g/cm3) and compressive strengths (3.53 MPa, 0.47 MPa) of their corresponding samples are both lower than those of the as-prepared samples in this work (0.839 g/cm3, 8.11 MPa).The reason lies in the hierarchical pore structure formed by the gel-casting foam method. This unique hierarchical pore structure, along with the characteristic of the presence of closed pores, results in a denser ceramic framework and a more stable structure.Furthermore, compared with the porous ceramics prepared using the same raw materials and the same preparation method as this work by Chen et al., the compressive strength of the samples reported (4.93 ± 0.80 MPa) was 39.2% lower than that of the samples in this work (8.11 MPa); the apparent porosity was approximately 17.2% lower; and the sintering temperature was 10 °C higher. This is because the iron tailings used by Chen et al. were dominated by biotite as the primary crystalline phase, and the foaming-curing system adopted in their study differs from that in this work.Even compared with the study on iron tailings-based porous ceramics that achieved high strength via modification with additives such as fly ash, this study still achieved a 9.5% relative increase in porosity without the addition of any auxiliary materials, while the compressive strength decreased by only 7.3%. Meanwhile, the bulk density of the sample increased from 0.62 g/cm3 to 0.839 g/cm3, and the sintering temperature was reduced by 27 °C.

Although high-quality data and well-established models were used, the ultimate goal of this research is to develop a high-performance, environmentally friendly porous ceramic from iron tailings that meets application requirements in the construction field.

Conclusions

The iron tailings from the Anshan area were used as the main raw material. Sodium dodecyl sulfate triethanolamine (TLS) was selected as the foaming agent and xanthan gum (XG) as the curing agent. Porous ceramics from iron tailings were prepared through gel injection molding and sintering. SEM images confirmed that the pores in the samples were composed of large spherical pores and micropores. When the sintering temperature increases, the bulk density and compressive strength of the sample increase, while the apparent porosity decreases. With an increase in the content of the foaming agent, the apparent porosity of the sample increases, while the bulk density and compressive strength decrease. When the sintering temperature is 1150 °C, and the foaming agent content is 2%, the homogeneity of the pore-size distribution and the comprehensive performance of the prepared iron tailings porous ceramics are optimal. Eventually, the apparent porosity of the porous iron tailings ceramics is 62.42%, the bulk density is 0.839 g/cm3, and the compressive strength reaches 8.11 MPa.
