Effects of resistance-band shoulder exercise on pain, range of motion, and functional disability in diabetic adults with adhesive capsulitis: a pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Afshin Moghadasi, Shaghayegh Jafari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdhesive capsulitis is a disabling musculoskeletal condition in individuals with diabetes mellitus, characterized by shoulder pain, limited range of motion (ROM), and functional impairment. Although exercise therapy is commonly recommended, evidence on resistance-band shoulder exercises in diabetic populations remains limited. This pilot randomized controlled trial evaluated the effects of a resistance-band shoulder exercise program in adults with diabetes and adhesive capsulitis.MethodsIn this stratified randomized controlled trial, 28 adults with diabetes and clinically confirmed adhesive capsulitis were assigned to a control group (n = 8), a type 1 diabetes exercise group (ExT1D, n = 9), or a type 2 diabetes exercise group (ExT2D, n = 11). The intervention consisted of supervised resistance-band stretching and strengthening exercises performed three times weekly for 8 weeks. Primary outcomes included shoulder ROM (flexion, abduction, internal and external rotation). Secondary outcomes were pain intensity (Visual Analog Scale) and functional disability (DASH and SPADI). Data were analyzed using ANCOVA with baseline adjustment.ResultsAfter baseline adjustment, significant between-group differences were observed in post-intervention shoulder ROM across all movements in both shoulders (p = 0.001), with large effect sizes. Background

Frozen shoulder, also known as adhesive capsulitis, is a painful and disabling musculoskeletal disorder characterized by progressive stiffness and limited active and passive shoulder range of motion, with loss of external rotation being a hallmark feature [1, 2]. The condition typically progresses through distinct clinical phases, including freezing, frozen, and thawing stages, and early diagnosis and management are considered important for improving clinical outcomes and reducing the duration of disability [3]. Frozen shoulder most commonly occurs in middle-aged adults, particularly between 40 and 60 years of age, with an estimated prevalence ranging from 1% to 5% in the general population [4–6]. This disorder significantly limits daily activities and impairs quality of life, highlighting the importance of timely diagnosis and management [7–9]. Recent literature also emphasizes a structured clinical decision-making approach that integrates patient history, symptom assessment, physical examination, diagnostic testing, and evaluation of comorbidities to guide accurate staging and personalized management of adhesive capsulitis [10].

Type 1 and type 2 diabetes are among the most prevalent risk factors for frozen shoulder, with diabetic individuals being 2–4 times more likely to develop the condition compared to the general population [1, 11]. The prevalence of frozen shoulder in diabetic populations ranges from 10% to 36%, substantially higher than the 0.75–5% observed in the general population [1, 11, 12]. Longitudinal evidence also indicates that even prediabetes is associated with an increased risk of developing frozen shoulder, emphasizing the role of hyperglycemia in capsular structural changes and fibrotic damage [11].

The underlying pathophysiological mechanisms linking diabetes and frozen shoulder include non-enzymatic glycation of collagen, low-grade chronic inflammation, microvascular alterations, and capsular fibrosis, ultimately leading to reduced range of motion, pain, and functional disability [1, 13]. Diabetes-related cross-linking of collagen and increased capsular thickness contribute to tissue adhesion and decreased joint flexibility, playing a central role in the development of adhesive capsulitis [11, 12].

Despite the high prevalence of frozen shoulder in diabetic patients, musculoskeletal complications of diabetes are less recognized and often undertreated, leading to prolonged disability [1, 2, 12]. Early detection and targeted rehabilitation interventions, such as resistance-band exercise programs, have the potential to improve shoulder range of motion, reduce pain, and enhance functional performance [14–16].

However, there is limited evidence regarding the effectiveness of targeted resistance exercise programs on shoulder range of motion, pain, and functional disability in diabetic patients with frozen shoulder. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of an 8-week resistance-band shoulder exercise program on shoulder range of motion, pain, and functional disability in adults with diabetes and adhesive capsulitis. Findings from this study may provide evidence to inform targeted rehabilitation strategies for this population.

Methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a stratified pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT) and is reported in accordance with the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2025 guidelines. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Payame Noor University (Ethics Committee Reference Number: IR.PNU.REC.1403.536). The trial was retrospectively registered with the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT; registration number: IRCT20180521039762N4) on 02/02/2026, and the full protocol is available through the registry website. No separate statistical analysis plan was prepared. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. No important changes were made to the trial design, eligibility criteria, interventions, or outcomes after the trial commenced. Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of this trial.

Participants and study groups

Out of 76 patients with type 1 and type 2 diabetes referred to Labbafinejad Hospital in Tehran, a total of 28 individuals (17 women and 11 men) who met the inclusion criteria were purposively selected. The study was conducted at Labbafinejad Hospital, a tertiary referral center in Tehran, Iran. The exercise interventions were delivered by a specialist in Sports Injury and Corrective Exercises with 7 years of clinical experience, in accordance with a predefined study protocol.

Recruitment and follow-up

Participant recruitment was planned for 20 April 2025 to 29 April 2025 and was completed between 20 April 2025 and 9 May 2025. Follow-up for outcomes of benefits and harms continued throughout the 8-week intervention period, and the trial was completed on 8 July 2025.

Randomization, allocation concealment, and implementation

To assign participants to the study groups, a stratified selection process was employed based on diabetes type. Out of the 28 enrolled participants (13 with type 1 diabetes and 15 with type 2 diabetes), a total of 8 participants (4 with type 1 diabetes and 4 with type 2 diabetes) were randomly allocated to the control group (n = 8). This random sequence was generated using Microsoft Excel by an independent researcher who was not involved in participant recruitment or assessment. The remaining 9 participants with type 1 diabetes and 11 participants with type 2 diabetes were assigned to the exercise with type 1 diabetes group (ExT1D, n = 9) and the exercise with type 2 diabetes group (ExT2D, n = 11), respectively (Fig. 1). Due to the nature of the allocation, concealment of group assignment was not feasible after the random selection of the control group.

Sample size and power analysis

The sample size was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). The calculation was based on an F-test (ANOVA: repeated measures, within–between interaction) with three groups and two measurement time points. Assuming a medium effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.25), an alpha level of 0.05, and a statistical power of 0.80, the estimated total sample size was 27 participants.

Considering potential dropouts and the pilot nature of this randomized controlled trial, 28 participants were recruited and allocated to the three study groups. Recruitment was constrained by strict eligibility criteria (adhesive capsulitis in patients with type 1 or type 2 diabetes) and the requirement to complete an 8-week supervised intervention program.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants included in the study were adults aged 40–60 years with a confirmed diagnosis of type 1 or type 2 diabetes based on laboratory markers and the American Diabetes Association (ADA) criteria. All participants had a diagnosis of adhesive capsulitis confirmed by an orthopedic specialist. The diagnosis was established primarily based on standardized clinical criteria, including a history of persistent shoulder pain and progressive stiffness for at least three months, together with a marked restriction in both active and passive shoulder range of motion (ROM). In particular, participants demonstrated substantial limitation in external rotation (approximately 40–50% reduction compared with expected normal values or the contralateral side), accompanied by restriction in at least two additional planes of motion such as flexion or abduction, along with functional limitations in daily activities. These findings were consistent with the frozen phase of adhesive capsulitis.

Clinical orthopedic examination and functional tests were performed to differentiate adhesive capsulitis from other shoulder disorders. When clinically indicated, imaging assessments such as plain radiography or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) were requested to exclude structural pathologies including rotator cuff tears, fractures, glenohumeral osteoarthritis, or calcific tendinopathy. Eligible participants had no medical contraindications to participate in a resistance-band shoulder exercise program and were able and willing to provide written informed consent.

Participants were excluded if they had irregular attendance or were unable to comply with the exercise program, experienced exacerbation of diabetes or other acute medical conditions requiring hospitalization, or reported severe shoulder pain or injury during the intervention. Additional exclusion criteria included a history of rotator cuff tears, recent upper limb trauma within the past six months, fractures, or surgeries involving the upper limb or chest; diagnosed cervical radiculopathy, thoracic outlet syndrome, carpal tunnel syndrome, or neurological deficits affecting upper limb function; a history of stroke with residual upper limb impairment or central nervous system disorders affecting motor function; severe dermatological conditions in the shoulder region, rheumatic diseases, connective tissue disorders, or use of mobility aids (e.g., cane or walker); cognitive impairments limiting comprehension of verbal instructions; cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, or other comorbidities contraindicating exercise; and previous participation in structured shoulder rehabilitation programs within the past 12 months [15–17].

Blinding

Participants and exercise instructors were aware of group allocation, and blinding of these individuals was not possible. However, outcome assessors during the pre- and post-tests, as well as the person who analyzed the data, were blinded to group assignments.

Exercise program

The resistance-band shoulder exercise program consisted of three stretching exercises and seven resistance exercises, performed over 8 weeks with three supervised sessions per week (Table 1). Session duration progressed from approximately 30 min in the initial weeks to 45–60 min in later weeks.

Stretching exercises included cross-chest stretch, neck stretch, and doorway pec stretch, targeting the posterior shoulder capsule, neck muscles, upper trapezius, and pectoralis muscles using proprioceptive neuromuscular facilitation (PNF) techniques. Each stretch was performed in three sets, with a 7-second isometric contraction opposing the stretch, followed by relaxation, and a 20–30 s static hold. Stretch duration was progressively increased throughout the 8-week program.

Resistance exercises included low row, Y-raise, T-raise, W-raise, external shoulder rotation, scaption, and serratus punch, all performed using Theraband resistance. Each exercise was completed in three sets of 7–15 repetitions, with 10–30 s rest between sets. To maintain progressive overload, Theraband resistance was adjusted every six sessions (Yellow → Red → Green → Blue) according to each participant’s ability. Participants were encouraged to achieve 15 repetitions per set before increasing band resistance, while maintaining proper form and muscle control [16].

Participants were instructed to follow American Diabetes Association guidelines for diabetes management, including blood glucose monitoring, daily foot care, and blood pressure control, and to continue their prescribed medical treatments, medications, and routine daily activities throughout the intervention [15].

Participants in the control group continued their usual diabetes care and routine daily activities and did not participate in any structured or supervised exercise program during the study period. Participants in the intervention group maintained their usual care and daily routines in addition to completing the supervised resistance-band exercise program during the 8-week intervention period. Adverse events and safety outcomes were monitored throughout the 8-week period at each training session through participant self-report and direct observation by the intervention provider. Predefined adverse events included unexpected pain, joint instability, or cardiovascular symptoms. Session attendance was recorded at each supervised session to monitor adherence to the exercise program.

Data collection instruments

Shoulder range of motion (ROM) assessment

Shoulder range of motion (ROM) was assessed by measuring the active ROM of the arm during flexion, abduction, internal rotation, and external rotation using a standard goniometer. Prior to the measurements, each participant completed a standardized warm-up lasting approximately five minutes, which included ten pendulum arm swings in both clockwise and counterclockwise directions, as well as ten repetitions of scapular squeezes performed while standing [18]. Following the warm-up, measurements were taken first on the dominant arm and then on the non-dominant arm.

To familiarize participants with the required movements, the examiner passively moved the participant’s arm to the end range in each plane of motion prior to each measurement. Participants then actively performed the four movements consecutively in the following order: flexion, abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation, with a one-minute rest between each measurement. During each active repetition, participants were instructed to move their arm to the maximum range and hold the position while the angle was recorded using the goniometer. After recording, the arm was returned to the neutral zero-degree position, and the measurement was repeated. Each movement was measured twice, and the average of the two measurements was used for analysis.

All goniometric assessments were conducted by an experienced examiner who was blinded to group allocation. Verbal feedback was provided throughout the assessment to ensure correct form and maximal ROM. The measurement protocols for active flexion, abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation followed the guidelines established by Clarkson, which have demonstrated high intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC ≥ 0.85) [19].

Assessment of shoulder pain, disability, and function

Shoulder pain, disability, and function were evaluated using two self-reported questionnaires: the Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder, and Hand (DASH), the Shoulder Pain and Disability Index (SPADI), and the Visual Analog Scale (VAS). These questionnaires are commonly used to assess shoulder pain and function in orthopedic research. The DASH is a 30-item questionnaire designed to measure physical function and symptoms in individuals with upper extremity musculoskeletal disorders. It assesses the prevalence of musculoskeletal impairments and evaluates the ability to perform activities and symptoms experienced over the previous week. Each item is scored on a scale from 1 to 5, assessing upper limb function. Twenty-one items assess difficulties in daily activities, five items measure pain severity during rest and activity, weakness, and joint stiffness, and four items evaluate the impact of upper limb problems on social and occupational activities. For the DASH results to be valid, participants must respond to at least 27 items. The DASH score is calculated out of 100; to compute the final score, the sum of the responses is averaged (sum of scores divided by the number of answered items), then one is subtracted from the average, and the result is multiplied by 25. Higher final scores, closer to 100, indicate greater disability [16, 18, 20].

The SPADI is also a self-reported questionnaire consisting of 13 items addressing pain intensity and the ability to perform activities of daily living (ADL). It consists of two subscales: one for pain and one for functional activities. The pain subscale contains five questions regarding the intensity of pain experienced by the individual. The functional subscale consists of eight questions designed to measure the degree of difficulty in performing various upper limb-dependent daily activities. Scoring for SPADI is based on a visual analog scale ranging from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst imaginable pain). The pain subscale includes five items assessing pain at its worst, when lying on the affected side, reaching overhead, touching the back or neck, and pushing with the affected hand. Participants rated their pain intensity for each item on the 0 to 10 scale. To calculate the total pain subscale score, the sum of these five items is divided by 50 and multiplied by 100. The functional subscale includes eight items related to washing hair, washing the back, putting on a shirt or blouse, buttoning a shirt, putting on pants, placing an object on a high shelf, carrying a heavy object (4.5 kg), and removing an object from the back pocket of pants. Participants rated the difficulty experienced during these activities on a scale from 0 (no difficulty) to 10 (so difficult that assistance is required). The total functional subscale score is calculated by dividing the sum of these eight items by 80 and multiplying by 100. The overall SPADI score ranges from 0 (best) to 100 (worst) and is calculated either as the average of the two subscales or by dividing the sum of both subscales by 130 and multiplying by 100 [18, 21].

The Visual Analog Scale (VAS) is a simple, valid, and widely used tool for measuring subjective and qualitative variables such as pain intensity, fatigue, stress, or perceived performance. The VAS consists of a 10-centimeter (or 100-millimeter) horizontal or vertical line with its two ends representing the extremes of the variable being measured. To assess pain intensity, each participant was asked to mark a point on the line that best described their perception of shoulder pain. The distance from the beginning of the line to the marked point was then measured in millimeters, ranging from 0 to 100 [22].

Statistical analysis

To compare the demographic characteristics of participants across the three groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. To test the research hypotheses, mixed-design analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed. This approach was chosen over a simple repeated measures ANOVA to provide a more robust estimation of treatment effects by adjusting for baseline differences between groups, thereby increasing statistical precision despite the pilot sample size. Prior to conducting ANCOVA, the homogeneity of regression slopes (p > 0.05) and linearity of scatterplots between groups for each variable were examined. In addition, Levene’s test was used to assess homogeneity of variances, and the Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to verify the normality of data distribution (p > 0.05). Bonferroni post hoc tests were used to identify significant differences between groups. Eta squared (η²) was reported as the measure of effect size, with values interpreted as small (0.01), medium (0.06), and large (0.14). All analyses were conducted on the full sample, as no participants were lost to follow-up and no missing outcome data occurred. No prespecified or post hoc subgroup or sensitivity analyses were performed. The significance level was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS software version 25.

Shoulder range of motion

Controlling for baseline values via ANCOVA, the three groups of adults with diabetes and adhesive capsulitis differed significantly in post-test ROM across all measured movements, including flexion, abduction, internal rotation, and external rotation, in both arms (p = 0.001), with large effect sizes (Table 3). Shoulder pain, disability, and function

ANCOVA adjusting for pre-test scores revealed significant differences among the groups in post-test measures of shoulder pain and disability (p < 0.05), with large effect sizes (Table 3). Subsequent Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons showed that both exercise groups (ExT1D and ExT2D) experienced markedly better outcomes in shoulder pain, functional disability, and overall shoulder function compared to the control group (p = 0.001 for both), while no significant differences were observed between the two exercise groups (p > 0.05).

Discussion

The findings of the present study demonstrate that an 8-week resistance-band shoulder exercise program significantly improved shoulder range of motion, reduced pain, and alleviated functional disability in diabetic adults with adhesive capsulitis. These improvements were consistently observed in both ExT1D and ExT2D groups, with no significant differences between the two, suggesting that structured resistance training may produce comparable benefits regardless of diabetes type. Notably, participants presented with moderate to severe limitations in shoulder mobility, consistent with the frozen phase of adhesive capsulitis, which highlights the clinical relevance of these improvements for restoring functional performance. In line with recent clinical perspectives, early recognition and timely initiation of tailored exercise interventions are crucial to prevent prolonged disability and optimize long-term clinical outcomes in this patient population [3, 10].

These results are consistent with previous studies [14–16]. Mueller et al. (2018), reported that eight weeks of daily shoulder mobility exercises significantly improved active range of motion, as well as pain and disability, in individuals with type 2 diabetes. In that study, SPADI scores improved by an average of 12.7 points in the intervention group compared to the control [15]. Moreover, studies including both diabetic and non-diabetic participants have shown that targeted strengthening and stretching programs significantly reduce SPADI and DASH scores, suggesting that resistance-band shoulder exercises can enhance shoulder function independently of diabetes type [15, 16]. Similarly, Kyhlbäck et al. (2014) demonstrated that a program combining low-load resistance exercises, shoulder coordination training, and muscle relaxation techniques reduces pain and joint stiffness while improving daily functional performance [14].

The underlying mechanisms for these benefits are multifactorial. Diabetic adults with adhesive capsulitis often exhibit increased capsular and connective tissue stiffness, pain, and elevated levels of advanced glycation end-products (AGEs), which reduce shoulder mobility and predispose the joint to further damage [1, 5, 13]. Resistance-band exercises may improve range of motion by increasing local blood flow, reducing collagen glycation, enhancing proprioceptive input, and promoting repeated activation of the rotator cuff and scapular stabilizing muscles, including serratus anterior, middle and lower trapezius, and rhomboids [8, 16, 23–24]. These adaptations improve scapulohumeral rhythm, dynamic stability, and joint compliance, thereby alleviating pain and restoring functional movement. Additionally, exercise-induced improvements in insulin sensitivity and glycemic control may indirectly contribute to reducing musculoskeletal and neuropathic pain [8, 15].

Importantly, the absence of significant differences between the ExT1D and ExT2D groups suggests that the physiological responses of muscles and pain pathways to resistance training are largely independent of diabetes type. Instead, the responses may depend more on the severity of adhesive capsulitis symptoms and adherence to the exercise protocol. This finding underscores the potential for structured exercise programs to benefit a broad range of diabetic patients with shoulder dysfunction, irrespective of diabetes subtype.

The clinical implications of these findings are substantial. The structured, low-cost resistance-band program used in this study specifically targeted scapular stabilizers and rotator cuff muscles through exercises such as T-, Y-, and W-raises, serratus punches, low rows, scaption, and external rotation. These exercises can be safely incorporated into self-management and rehabilitation programs for diabetic adults with adhesive capsulitis, promoting improved scapulohumeral rhythm, enhanced dynamic stability, and better shoulder mobility. While the primary focus was on resistance-band exercises, PNF stretching exercises included in the program likely contributed to the observed improvements in shoulder mobility by reducing capsular and muscular stiffness. Consequently, functional independence may be enhanced, disability reduced, and quality of life improved. Considering that routine daily activities such as reaching overhead, dressing, and grooming require at least 50 degrees of external rotation [16], the observed improvements in range of motion are functionally meaningful and likely to translate into practical benefits in everyday life.

Despite the promising findings of the present study, several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the results. First, the relatively small sample size and the distribution of participants across three groups may limit the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution and considered preliminary in nature. Larger randomized controlled trials are needed to confirm these findings and allow more robust subgroup analyses.

Second, the study evaluated only the short-term effects of the intervention following the eight-week resistance-band exercise program. The absence of longer follow-up assessments (e.g., 3- or 6-month follow-up) limits our ability to determine whether the observed improvements in shoulder function and disability are maintained over time.

Third, although the diagnosis of adhesive capsulitis was established using standardized clinical criteria and orthopedic examination, imaging assessments were not systematically performed for all participants and were used only when clinically indicated to exclude other structural shoulder pathologies. While clinical evaluation remains central to diagnosis, recent literature highlights the potential value of imaging modalities such as ultrasound or MRI for supporting diagnostic accuracy, characterizing disease stage, and informing treatment decision‑making [10]. Future studies may benefit from incorporating systematic imaging assessments to further strengthen diagnostic precision and monitor structural changes.

Fourth, participants were limited to adults with type 1 or type 2 diabetes, which may restrict the generalizability of the results to other age groups or populations with different comorbid conditions.

Finally, potential confounding factors such as dietary habits, glycemic control, and levels of physical activity outside the intervention were not strictly controlled and may have influenced the outcomes. In addition, metabolic indicators of glycemic control (e.g., HbA1c levels) were not systematically assessed as study variables, which may have limited the ability to evaluate their potential influence on treatment response. Future research with larger samples, longer follow-up periods, and more comprehensive clinical and metabolic monitoring is recommended to better understand the long-term effectiveness of resistance-band exercise programs in individuals with diabetes and adhesive capsulitis.

The findings of this pilot study also have several implications for clinical practice and future research. For clinicians, the results suggest that a structured resistance-band shoulder exercise program may represent a safe, low-cost, and accessible adjunct to conservative management for diabetic adults with adhesive capsulitis. Given the improvements observed in pain, shoulder mobility, and functional disability, such exercises may be considered in rehabilitation programs aimed at improving daily function and reducing disability in this population. The similar responses observed in participants with type 1 and type 2 diabetes also suggest that this approach may be applicable across diabetes subtypes. For future researchers, these findings support the need for larger randomized controlled trials with longer follow-up periods to confirm the present results and determine the durability of treatment effects. Future studies should also consider incorporating more comprehensive metabolic and clinical monitoring, including glycemic indicators and, where feasible, imaging assessments, to better clarify the mechanisms underlying exercise-related improvements in diabetic adults with adhesive capsulitis.

Conclusions

This investigation demonstrates that an 8-week, structured resistance-band shoulder exercise program yields significant improvements in shoulder range of motion, reductions in pain intensity, and enhanced functional capacity among diabetic adults diagnosed with adhesive capsulitis. The inherent attributes of resistance-band exercises—namely their cost-effectiveness, safety profile, and accessibility—position this intervention as a valuable component within comprehensive diabetes care and musculoskeletal rehabilitation strategies. While these findings are promising, the study’s limitations, including a modest sample size and the lack of longitudinal follow-up data, necessitate cautious generalizability. Future research employing larger cohorts and extended follow-up protocols is imperative to solidify the long-term efficacy and clinical utility of this therapeutic approach.
