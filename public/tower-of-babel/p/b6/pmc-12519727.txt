The relationship between diet quality, social participation, quality of life, and occupational balance in adolescents with Celiac disease
Authors: Nevra Koç, Özgü İnal Özün, Edibe Gözde Başaran, Hatice Aybüke Çalişkan, Büşra Ay, Coşkun Fırat Özkeçeci, Necati Balamtekin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAdolescents diagnosed with Celiac Disease are at risk of experiencing physical, psychological and social difficulties that affect their quality of life. Dietary restrictions also create stress related to social difficulties in daily life.This study aimed to examine the associations between diet quality, social participation, quality of life, and occupational balance among adolescents diagnosed with Celiac Disease, and to evaluate the interrelationships among these variables.MethodsThe study sample consisted of 52 adolescents diagnosed with Celiac Disease (aged 12–18 years) and 55 age-matched healthy controls. Data were collected on participants’ body composition, anthropometric measurements, adherence to the Mediterranean diet (assessed via the KIDMED index), social participation (Child and Adolescent Scale of Participation, CASP), Quality of Life (Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory, PedsQL), and occupational balance (Adolescent Occupational Balance Scale, A-OBS). Spearman’s rank-order correlation analysis was conducted to examine the associations among the study variables.ResultsThe mean KIDMED score was significantly higher in adolescents with Celiac Disease compared to healthy controls (24.09 ± 1.65 vs. 22.96 ± 2.45; p = 0.006). Additionally, the Celiac Disease group demonstrated significantly higher scores in total PedsQL, physical functioning, and school functioning domains (p < 0.05). Scores for the Adolescent Occupational Balance Scale (A-OBS), including total score, temporality, and rest and sleep subdomains, were also significantly elevated in the Celiac group (p < 0.05). The correlation analysis revealed no significant associations between the total KIDMED score and other measured parameters, except for a weak but statistically significant negative correlation with the CASP Home and Community Living Activities subscale (rho = − 0.292, p = 0.036). Furthermore, total PedsQL score was positively correlated with A-OBS total, temporality, and rest and sleep scores.ConclusionsThis study demonstrated that adolescents with Celiac Disease exhibit higher diet quality compared to their healthy counterparts; however, this improved dietary adherence may be negatively associated with social participation. These findings underscore the importance of a holistic approach in the clinical management of Celiac Disease, taking into account not only nutritional adequacy but also psychosocial well-being.Keywords: Celiac disease, Gluten free diet, Well-being, Adolescent, Health, Occupational, Life quality

Background

Celiac Disease (CD) is a chronic, immune-mediated systemic disorder triggered by the ingestion of gluten-containing grains—such as wheat, barley, and rye—in genetically predisposed individuals. To date, strict lifelong adherence to a gluten-free diet (GFD) remains the only effective treatment for CD [1]. However, adherence to the GFD is frequently suboptimal, particularly among adolescents [2, 3]. Adolescence is a period of complex physical, cognitive, social, and emotional development [4] and this is a time of significant developmental and occupational changes.

Compared to adults, adolescents with CD may encounter unique challenges in adhering to dietary restrictions and accessing appropriate gluten-free products [5]. Previous studies have emphasized multiple barriers faced by adolescents with CD, including the high cost and limited availability of gluten-free foods, as well as difficulties encountered during social events, travel, and dining out [6]. Additionally, during adolescence, increased peer pressure and greater awareness of being different due to a chronic condition such as CD can lead to feelings of stigma and isolation [7] and they often experience feelings of vulnerability in social contexts [8]. Compared to healthy peers, they tend to engage in fewer social and recreational activities [9]. Participation in leisure activities allows adolescents to explore their identities, develop self-esteem, and prepare for adulthood; therefore, reduced social participation may have detrimental effects on identity formation and social development [9]. The psychosocial impact of CD is frequently assessed through health-related quality of life (HR-QoL) measures, which encompass the physical, psychological, and social dimensions of functioning in the context of a health condition [10].

Living gluten-free is a situation in which children diagnosed with CD must have knowledge of the gluten-free diet and strictly adhere to their diet in order to have a good quality of health [11]. Dietary variables, such as adherence to a GFD, are key determinants of long-term HR-QoL in individuals with CD [12]. A recent meta-analysis of adolescents diagnosed with CD showed that quality of life (QoL) tended to decrease in subdomains related to adherence to a gluten-free diet, but improved significantly after adherence to the diet [13]. In a recent study examining the relationship between diet quality and health-related quality of life in children and adolescents with celiac disease, it was noted that although diet quality was not associated with HR-QoL, better dietary compliance improved HR-QoL in celiac patients. It was also noted that this situation could help develop solutions to the problems experienced by celiac patients and thus improve CD management [14]. A recent study of adolescents aged 13–18 years found low total health-related quality of life scores in almost half of participants. Furthermore, adherence to the GFD was shown to have a significant positive correlation with health-related quality of life [11].

The World Health Organization (WHO) defines QoL as a multifaceted concept encompassing various dimensions that reflect the overall well-being of an individual or a population [15]. Assessing HR-QoL is a valuable tool for identifying the psychological and physical burden associated with chronic conditions such as CD. The WHO emphasizes that QoL is not merely an individual concern but also a public health priority [16], and serves as a critical indicator of how effectively national health systems manage chronic diseases, particularly prevalent conditions like CD. Given the potential for CD to cause lasting psychological effects, evaluating HR-QoL both at the time of diagnosis and during follow-up has become increasingly important, especially in children and adolescents who may experience impaired QoL even years after diagnosis [17]. Existing literature indicates that most studies examining diet quality, dietary adherence, HR-QoL, and daily life challenges in individuals with CD have been conducted predominantly in adult populations [18–20]. However, in the last few years, the number of studies examining HR-QoL in adolescents diagnosed with CD has been increasing [12, 21].

CD is recognized as a global public health concern [22], and ranks among the most prevalent chronic conditions in childhood. Living with a chronic illness can present numerous challenges for individuals, particularly in maintaining engagement in meaningful occupations and achieving occupational balance [23]. Adolescence is a period when individuals experience intense phases of development and transition due to occupations [24]. Occupations are the familiar and ordinary things people do every day and are the “doing” of human activities within the physical, temporal, and sociocultural context in which individuals experience them. These activities are categorized into occupations, including activities of daily living (such as eating), instrumental activities of daily living, work, rest and sleep, leisure, education, play, and social participation, of which some are relevant to adolescents’ and children’s daily life with CD [25, 26]. The human experience of health and well-being is linked to the activities or occupations one engages in. Eating is a fundamental occupation essential for self-sufficiency and is widely regarded as the most social of human activities [27]. And the relationship between what a person does and their experience of health and well-being is influenced by the meaning, purpose, value and balance of occupations [28, 29]. Considering that adolescence is a transition period in terms of occupations and the relationship between health and occupational balance, it is thought that occupational balance may be affected in adolescents with a disease whose only treatment is GFD. In addition, adolescence is characterized by different developmental, psychological and social characteristics. Therefore, studies investigating health status and/or health-related behaviors across different patient groups should clearly differentiate adolescents from both young children and adults [6].

The aim of this study was to examine the associations between diet quality, social participation, HR-QoL, and occupational balance in adolescents diagnosed with CD, and to evaluate the interrelationships among these variables. It was hypothesized that diet quality, social participation, quality of life, and occupational balance would be significantly associated in this population.

Methods

This study was carried out in the Pediatric Gastroenterology Outpatient Clinic of the University of Health Sciences Gülhane Training and Research Hospital. This study was conducted between June 2023 and June 2024, during which time the study data were collected, and the patients/controls were seen in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from each participant and their parent. Ethics Committee approval of the study was obtained with the meeting decision of the Gülhane Scientific Research Ethics Committee, dated 16/05/2024, and project number 2023 − 172, numbered 2022/05. The study included adolescents with and without a diagnosis of CD who were over 12 years old, the age group in which the quality of life, diet quality, and occupational balance assessment tools could be applied together. Inclusion criteria for the patient group were (I) age 12–18 years and (II) having a CD diagnosis for at least 3 months. Exclusion criteria were (I) having a diagnosis of cancer, psychiatric disease, neurological disease, kidney or liver failure, type-1 diabetes, Hashimoto thyroiditis, or other autoimmune disease accompanying CD, (II) having a history of bariatric surgery, and (III) having more than 5% weight loss in the last 3 months. The control group of the study consisted of healthy volunteers of similar age and sex distribution in whom CD was excluded serologically.

Demographic characteristics, anthropometric measurements, and body composition ratios were recorded using a structured data collection form developed by the research team. All participants were assessed using the Mediterranean Diet Quality Index for Children and Adolescents (KIDMED) [30, 31], the Child and Adolescent Scale of Participation (CASP) [32, 33], the Pediatric QoL Inventory (PedsQL) [34, 35] and the Adolescent Occupational Balance Scale (A-OBS) [36].

Data collection

Information form

Participants were asked to provide demographic and lifestyle information, including sex, age, city of residence, parental education and employment status, household income level, number of siblings, level of physical activity, and medical history.

Dietary habits were assessed by recording the number and timing of daily meals, frequency of skipped meals, daily water intake, and regular use of dietary supplements.

For participants diagnosed with CD, additional questions included age at diagnosis, the healthcare provider responsible for their medical nutrition therapy, duration of adherence to a GFD, self-reported adherence to the GFD, frequency of gluten-free product consumption, food label reading behaviors, ease of access to and consumption of gluten-free foods, typical locations where the three main meals were consumed, and perceived challenges in adapting to the GFD.

Assessment tools

Body composition measurement: Body composition was assessed using the Tanita BC-418 ST device, which utilizes bioelectrical impedance analysis technology. The following parameters were measured: body weight (kg), height (cm), body mass index (BMI; kg/m²), body fat percentage (%), and lean muscle mass (kg).

Diet quality assessment: Mediterranean Diet Quality Index (KIDMED): The Mediterranean diet is widely recommended for both children and adults as a preventive approach against nutrition-related diseases and for promoting overall health. The KIDMED index is a validated 16-item questionnaire designed to assess adherence to the fundamental principles of the Mediterranean diet. It has been extensively utilized in pediatric and adolescent populations across various studies [30, 31].

Social participation assessment: The Child and Adolescent Scale of Participation (CASP) is a standardized instrument designed to assess the extent to which children and adolescents with special health care needs participate in daily activities at home, school, and in the community, relative to their healthy peers. The scale comprises 20 items across four domains: home (6 items), community (4 items), school (5 items), and home and community living activities (5 items). The total possible score ranges from 0 to 80, with higher scores reflecting a greater degree of age-appropriate participation [32, 33].

Quality of life assessment

Pediatric QoL Inventory™ 4.0 (PedsQL) is a widely used instrument designed to assess health-related QoL in children and adolescents. It comprises four subscales measuring physical, emotional, social, and school functioning. The physical functioning domain includes 8 items; the emotional and social functioning domains each contain 5 items; and the school functioning domain includes 3 items for children aged 2–4 years and 5 items for older age groups. The PedsQL uses a 5-point Likert-type response scale (0 = never, 1 = almost never, 2 = sometimes, 3 = often, 4 = almost always), which is reverse-scored and linearly transformed to a 0–100 scale for ease of interpretation. The scale is suitable for children aged 2 to 18 years and typically takes approximately 5–10 min to complete. The total score is calculated by summing the item scores and dividing by the number of items answered. Higher scores reflect better health-related QoL [34, 35].

Occupational balance assessment: Adolescent Occupational Balance Scale (A-OBS) is a self-report instrument designed to evaluate occupational balance in adolescents. It consists of 27 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale and encompasses four subscales: temporality, rest and sleep, occupational variations and adaptation, and occupational meaning and value. The scale requires approximately 15–20 min to complete. Total scores range from 27 to 108, with higher scores indicating greater occupational balance [36].

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were presented as means and standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables, and as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. The normality of distribution for continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. For between-group comparisons, the Student’s t-test was used for normally distributed variables, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed variables. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Correlations between variables were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. The strength of the correlations was interpreted according to the following thresholds: 0.00–0.19 = very weak, 0.20–0.39 = weak, 0.40–0.69 = moderate, 0.70–0.89 = strong, and 0.90–1.00 = very strong [37].

Clinical significance was evaluated using Cohen’s d statistic for Student’s t test. The magnitude of the effect was interpreted based on Cohen’s thresholds: 0.20 indicating a small effect, 0.50 a medium effect, and 0.80 a large effect [38]. For variables analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test, the Rank-Biserial Correlation coefficient was used to determine effect size. According to Cohen’s guidelines, the following thresholds were applied: 0.10 for a small effect, 0.30 for a medium effect, and 0.50 for a large effect [39].

IBM SPSS 23 package program was used to evaluate the data. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Sample size and power analysis

To detect a difference in means between two independent groups, an a priori sample size calculation was performed using Student’s t-test in the G*Power software version 3.1.9.4. Assuming a Type I error rate of 0.05, a statistical power of 0.80, and an estimated medium effect size (Cohen’s d = 0.55), the analysis indicated that a minimum of 106 participants—at least 53 per group—would be required.

Comparison of CASP scale scores between CD and control groups

The mean total CASP score of adolescents with CD (93.53 ± 11.06) was lower than that of the control group (95.52 ± 6.72); however, this difference was not statistically significant (Z = 0.663, p = 0.507). The clinical significance, evaluated using the rank biserial correlation coefficient, indicated a small effect size (0.072). In the home participation subscale, the mean scores of the CD and control groups were similar (95.11 ± 10.39 and 95.91 ± 8.31, respectively), with no statistically significant difference (Z = 0.415, p = 0.678). The corresponding rank-biserial correlation also indicated a small effect size (0.040). Regarding the neighbourhood and community participation, school participation, and overall community participation subscales, the CD group had lower mean scores (93.75 ± 11.67, 95.58 ± 11.05, and 89.42 ± 16.65, respectively) compared to the control group (95.11 ± 8.89, 97.18 ± 6.92, and 93.72 ± 8.40, respectively). Nevertheless, these differences were not statistically significant (p = 0.587, p = 0.784, and p = 0.233, respectively). Clinical significance analyses based on rank-biserial correlation coefficients revealed small effect sizes (0.049, 0.022, and 0.126, respectively).

Comparison of PedsQL scale scores between CD and control groups

On the PedsQL the mean total score, as well as the physical functioning and school functioning subscale scores, were significantly higher in the CD group compared to the control group. Specifically, the CD group scored 87.79 ± 8.27 on the total scale, 90.75 ± 10.61 in physical functioning, and 81.92 ± 12.61 in school functioning, whereas the control group scored 82.61 ± 10.83, 83.64 ± 13.66, and 76.00 ± 15.13, respectively (p = 0.004, p = 0.001, and p = 0.021). Clinical significance for the PedsQL total score, physical functioning, and school functioning subscales was evaluated using rank-biserial correlation, and moderate effect sizes were identified (r = 0.326, r = 0.384, and r = 0.256, respectively). No statistically significant differences were found between the CD and control groups in the emotional functioning and social functioning subscale scores (p = 0.139 and p = 0.522, respectively). Clinical significance analyses for these subscales also revealed a near-moderate effect size for emotional functioning (r = 0.165) and a small effect size for social functioning (r = 0.053).

Comparison of A-OBS scale scores between CD and control groups

The CD group also had significantly higher A-OBS total, temporality subscale, and rest and sleep subscale scores (A-OBS-total: 90.13 ± 9.85, A-OBS-temporality: 51.06 ± 5.44, and A-OBS-rest and sleep: 10.88 ± 1.53) compared to the control group (82.18 ± 15.17, 45.58 ± 7.83, and 9.93 ± 2.42, respectively) (p = 0.004, p < 0.001, and p = 0.030, respectively). Clinical significance was assessed using the rank-biserial correlation coefficient for the A-OBS total score and the rest and sleep subscale, revealing moderate (r = 0.320) and near-moderate (r = 0.232) effect sizes, respectively. For the temporality subscale, clinical significance was evaluated using Cohen’s d, which indicated a large effect size (d = 0.808).

No significant differences were observed between the CD and control groups in the A-OBS occupational variations and adaptation subscale or the occupational meaning and value subscale scores (p = 0.079 and p = 0.677, respectively). Clinical significance was assessed using the rank-biserial correlation coefficient, revealing a near-moderate effect size for the occupational variations and adaptation subscale (r = 0.196) and a small effect size for the occupational meaning and value subscale (r = 0.046).

Correlation analysis results

Correlation between CASP scale scores and KIDMED, PedsQL scale scores

Negative associations were observed between the total KIDMED score and the CASP total score as well as its subscales. A weak negative correlation was found between the KIDMED total score and the CASP total score; however, this correlation was not statistically significant (rho= -0.257, p = 0.066). In contrast, a weak but statistically significant negative correlation was found between the KIDMED total score and the CASP Home and Community Living Activities subscale score (rho= -0.292, p = 0.036).

Positive associations were found between the PedsQL Physical Functioning subscale and the CASP total score and its subscales. Weak positive correlations were found between the PedsQL Physical Functioning subscale and both the CASP Total score (rho = 0.266, p = 0.057) and the CASP Home and Community Living Activities subscale (rho = 0.253, p = 0.070); however, these correlations were not statistically significant. No statistically significant correlation was found between the PedsQL Physical Functioning subscale and the Home Participation (rho = 0.219, p = 0.118) or Neighborhood and Community Participation (rho = 0.202, p = 0.150) subscales.

Furthermore, weak but statistically significant positive correlations were found between the PedsQL Social Functioning subscale score and both the CASP Community subscale (rho = 0.341, p = 0.013) and the CASP School subscale (rho = 0.358, p = 0.009). A weak positive correlation was found between the PedsQL Social Functioning subscale score and the CASP Home Participation subscale (rho = 0.261, p = 0.062); however, this correlation was not statistically significant. In addition, no statistically significant correlation was observed between the PedsQL Social Functioning subscale and the CASP Total score (rho = 0.219, p = 0.119).

Positive associations were also observed between the PedsQL Total score and the CASP total score and its subscales. However, these correlations were weak and did not reach statistical significance: CASP Total (rho = 0.230, p = 0.101), Home Participation (rho = 0.243, p = 0.082), Neighborhood and Community Participation (rho = 0.206, p = 0.144), and School Participation (rho = 0.231, p = 0.100).

Correlation between A-OBS scale scores and PedsQL scale scores

Among adolescents with CD, weak positive correlations were found between the A-OBS total score and the PedsQL total (rho = 0.331, p = 0.016), physical functioning subscale (rho = 0.330, p = 0.017), and emotional functioning (rho = 0.290, p = 0.037) subscale scores. A weak positive correlation was found between the A-OBS total score and the PedsQL Social Functioning subscale (rho = 0.262, p = 0.060); however, this correlation was not statistically significant. Additionally, weak but statistically significant positive correlations were observed between the A-OBS Temporality subscale score and the PedsQL Total (rho = 0.351, p = 0.011), Physical Functioning (rho = 0.347, p = 0.012), Emotional Functioning (rho = 0.349, p = 0.011), and Social Functioning (rho = 0.301, p = 0.030) scores. Similarly, weak positive correlations were found between the A-OBS rest and sleep subscale score and the PedsQL Total (rho = 0.314, p = 0.023), physical functioning (rho = 0.278, p = 0.046), and emotional functioning (rho = 0.373, p = 0.006) scores. However, no statistically significant correlation was found between the A-OBS Rest and Sleep subscale and the PedsQL Social Functioning subscale (rho = 0.195, p = 0.165).

A weak positive correlation was observed between the A-OBS Occupational Meaning and Value subscale and the PedsQL Social Functioning subscale; however, this correlation was not statistically significant (rho = 0.232, p = 0.098).

Discussion

This study examined the relationship between diet quality and social participation, quality of life, and occupational balance in adolescents with CD. The study found that the quality of diet was better and the quality of life was higher in celiac patients than in healthy controls. The celiac disease group had better occupational balance compared to the control group. This study also found that the quality of diet in celiac patients was related to social participation, home and community life activities.

Our findings suggest a significantly better diet quality in adolescents with CD compared to their healthy peers. Compliance with the GF diet is one of the main challenges among children and adolescents diagnosed with CD [40]. Children and adolescents with CD frequently suffer from malnutrition; in fact, it has been found that 20–38% of celiac patients suffer from nutritional deficiencies [41]. Determining the diet quality of celiac patients and looking at how it affects their health related QoL are therefore crucial [14]. Many factors (such as psychosocial, social factors) may influence dietary compliance in individuals with CD.

In this study, diet quality was determined to be associated with social participation. Patients with celiac disease must adhere to a GFD that must be strictly monitored and maintained throughout their lives. However, there are significant obstacles to this diet, including social life issues and the availability of gluten-free goods [42]. Socialization may pose challenges for these individuals in a variety of contexts, including school settings, community events, recreational activities, and home life due to dietary restrictions. Social withdrawal has been identified as a prominent problem in adolescents diagnosed with CD in 2025 [43], and this finding suggests that adolescents with CD have lower levels of social participation compared to adolescents without celiac disease [44]. The majority of adolescents (86.7%) experience at least one difficulty in adhering to the GFD when eating outside the home (e.g. risk of gluten contamination) [45]. Peer interactions and social events that often occur during mealtimes can compound these difficulties, and adolescents with CD may face feelings of social isolation or discomfort when dietary restrictions are not adhered to [45]. In this context, it is an expected result that better diet quality negatively affects social participation in adolescents with CD in our study.

In the present study, adolescents with CD reported better QoL compared to their healthy peers, regardless of diet quality. Moreover, no significant correlation was found between diet quality and quality of life. Although adherence to a GFD is essential for disease management, it often requires substantial changes to long-standing eating habits, which may, in turn, negatively affect both QoL and dietary adherence [46, 47]. However, among those whose condition was well managed through strict adherence to a GFD, QoL improved to levels comparable to those of the general population [48]. Although associations between specific domains of quality of life and adherence to the GFD have been noted, overall evidence suggests that there is no consistent relationship between overall quality of life and adherence to the diet [46]. QoL in individuals with CD may also be influenced by factors such as younger age at diagnosis, recent diagnosis, delays in obtaining a diagnosis, and dissatisfaction with the information provided by healthcare professionals [49]. The inconsistency observed in the literature regarding the relationship between diet quality and QoL may, in part, be attributed to the variability in the parameters considered across different studies. Furthermore, the inclusion of adolescents in the current study, as well as the use of a generic rather than a CD-specific QoL instrument, may explain the lack of a significant association between diet quality and QoL observed in our findings. In a study involving children with CD, Meyer and Rosenblum defined QoL as “the person’s perception of the impact of the condition on objective functioning and subjective assessment of well-being,” emphasizing both functional limitations and individual experiences in QoL assessment [50]. In this context, it may be essential to consider personal factors in order to gain a more comprehensive understanding of QoL among adolescents with CD.

In the present study, adolescents with CD demonstrated higher levels of occupational balance compared to the control group. Occupational balance has been widely associated with overall health and well-being, underscoring its relevance in the management of chronic conditions such as CD [51]. Living with a chronic illness can pose significant challenges for individuals in engaging in meaningful occupations and maintaining a balanced daily life [23]. The capacity of the individual to regulate their own health is inextricably related to the effective management of a chronic illness. A study examining psychological flexibility and celiac disease in adolescents found that adolescents showed a greater capacity to effectively organize their daily routines while adhering to gluten-free diets [52]. In the current study, the higher levels of occupational balance observed among adolescents with CD may be attributed to the development of more advanced self-management skills as a result of living with the condition.

Conclusions

This study revealed that better diet quality was associated with lower levels of social participation among adolescents with CD. Adolescents with CD also showed significantly better occupational balance compared to their healthy peers. Increasing social participation among children and adolescents with CD is essential to improve quality of life and overall well-being. In this context, both individual and societal awareness about CD should be strengthened. Given the difficulties individuals face in accessing gluten-free products outside the home, policy-level interventions are needed to ensure the widespread availability and affordability of a variety of gluten-free food options. Public regulations should address the daily challenges of individuals with CD, such as access to gluten-free products [53]. Furthermore, it is crucial for health professionals and educators to provide targeted education in school settings to promote social communication and interpersonal empathy and to prevent stigmatization of adolescents living with CD. Additionally, psychosocial risk factors that may hinder effective disease management in adolescents with CD should be identified at the earliest stage, and in particular, individuals’ self-management skills should be developed. Such efforts may contribute to increasing social participation and reducing experiences of social stigma in this population. In addition, in order to evaluate the psychosocial effects, especially in adolescent CD individuals, longitudinal studies that include participants from various socioeconomic and cultural backgrounds and use quantitative and qualitative methods together are considered important.

Limitations and strengths

This study has several limitations. First, due to the lack of validated tools specific to adolescents with CD in our country, we were unable to use assessment instruments tailored to this population for measuring QoL, social participation, and occupational balance. Second, participants were not asked about the presence of CD-related symptoms, even though persistent symptoms are known to be associated with poorer physical functioning and diminished QoL [19]. Third, the self-assessment tools employed were not specific to individuals with CD, which may limit the sensitivity of the measurements. Additionally, the study did not examine adolescents’ perspectives on barriers to social participation, which may have provided a deeper understanding of psychosocial challenges. Self-reported dietary intake may have been influenced by social desirability bias, as noted in previous research [54, 55]. Finally, although it was not the main purpose of this study, the fact that parameters related to disease symptom severity and mental health were not examined may have created a limitation in the interpretation of the results, especially in terms of parameters such as social participation and quality of life. Despite these limitations, the study has important strengths. The main strength of this study is that it is the first to investigate the relationships between diet quality, social participation, quality of life, and occupational balance in adolescents with CD and contribute new information to literature. The inclusion of a healthy control group is another strength of the study.
