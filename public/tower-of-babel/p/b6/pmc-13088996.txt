Hepatic mucinous cystic neoplasm with hematoma: A case report
Authors: Nonoka Yamagata, Tomoko Hyodo, Masatomo Kimura, Takaaki Murase, Fumiaki Ono, Koji Sugimoto, Takuya Nakai, Takao Satou, Kazunari Ishii
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a case of mucinous cystic neoplasm that presented with intratumoral bleeding. The tumor showed unusual imaging morphology mimicking echinococcosis and other cystic liver lesions. A woman in her 60s with frequent contact with multiple dogs presented with an incidentally detected liver mass. Computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging revealed a 53-mm lobulated hepatic mass with a central hematoma and peripheral cysts. Differential diagnoses included hemorrhagic cyst, echinococcosis, hepatocellular adenoma, mucinous cystic neoplasm, and metastatic tumors. Serologic testing for echinococcus antibodies was performed based on the imaging findings and yielded negative results. The patient underwent surgical resection, and the lesion was diagnosed as mucinous cystic neoplasm. No signs of recurrence were observed during the 5-year follow-up period.

Introduction

Hepatic mucinous cystic neoplasm (MCN) is defined as a cyst-forming epithelial neoplasm composed of mucin-producing columnar or cuboidal epithelium and a distinctive ovarian-like stroma, typically without communication to the biliary tree [1,2]. In the 2010 World Health Organization (WHO) classification, some lesions previously categorized as biliary cystadenoma or biliary cystadenocarcinoma were reclassified as MCN [1]. For pathological diagnosis, ovarian-like stroma is considered an essential criterion, while the other features are regarded as desirable [1,2]. Reported frequencies of MCN among hepatic cystic lesions vary widely, from 0.6%–2.2% to approximately 11%, reflecting differences in study populations and diagnostic criteria [3]. On imaging, MCN typically presents as a multilocular cystic lesion, sometimes described as having a “cyst-in-cyst” appearance [4]. Although uncommon, hemorrhage within the cyst wall may occasionally occur. We report a case of MCN that presented with central bleeding within a liver tumor and showed an unusual imaging morphology, making the diagnosis challenging.

Case report

A woman in her 60s presented with an incidentally detected liver mass. She was asymptomatic, and a chest radiograph obtained during a routine check-up had shown an abnormal finding, the details of which were unavailable. Subsequent computed tomography (CT) performed for further evaluation incidentally identified the hepatic mass, and she was referred to our hospital for additional assessment. For occupational reasons, she had been involved in the care of multiple dogs. Her medical history included contact dermatitis, and her family history was unremarkable. On admission, her vital signs were stable. Laboratory tests showed no elevation of tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen, high-sensitivity protein induced by vitamin K absence or antagonist-II, alpha-fetoprotein, carbohydrate antigen 19-9, and soluble interleukin-2 receptor.

Abdominal ultrasonography performed at another hospital 2 years earlier had demonstrated a cluster of cysts in hepatic segment 5/8 (Fig. 1A). At presentation, ultrasonography revealed a newly developed hyperechoic area in the central portion of the lesion (Fig. 1B). CT examination showed a 53-mm low-density mass with irregular margins in the anterior liver segment, accompanied by multiple punctate calcifications (Fig. 2A), and no contrast enhancement. Magnetic resonance (MR) imaging identified a 34-mm round component in the center of the mass that appeared hyperintense on T1-weighted images (Fig. 2B) and markedly hypointense on T2-weighted images (Fig. 2C), findings consistent with a hematoma. Several small cysts, each a few millimeters in diameter, were present in the surrounding area (Fig. 2B and C). No dilation of the bile ducts proximal or distal to the tumor was observed. On hepatobiliary-phase images with gadoxetic acid (EOB Primovist; Bayer Yakuhin, Osaka, Japan), no uptake was seen (Fig. 2D). Diffusion restriction was not observed within the lesion (Fig. 2E and F). Differential diagnoses included hemorrhagic cyst, echinococcosis, hepatocellular adenoma, MCN, intraductal papillary neoplasm of the bile duct (IPNB), and metastatic tumors. Echinococcus antibodies were negative on both enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and Western blot testing.

Serial ultrasound examinations revealed enlargement of the lesion and changes in its internal structure (Fig. 1A and B), making it difficult to exclude malignancy. As echinococcosis represents an indication for surgical resection and neoplastic entities such as MCN and IPNB were also included in the differential diagnosis, surgical resection was selected for both diagnostic confirmation and curative treatment. Given the tumor location in segments 5/8, its proximity to the Glissonean pedicles of the anterior sector, abutment of the middle hepatic vein, as well as the need to avoid intraoperative spillage of potentially mucinous contents with a risk of postoperative dissemination, an extended right hepatic lobectomy was performed.

On gross examination, multiple cysts were observed at the periphery of the hematoma (Fig. 3). Histopathologically, multiple cysts lined by columnar epithelium were present around the hematoma. The cyst walls showed marked fibrosis and lacked a clear ovarian-like stromal appearance. A few spindle-shaped cells were scattered within the fibrotic wall and demonstrated weak estrogen receptor positivity on immunohistochemistry. These findings suggested an ovarian stromal origin and were considered compatible with a probable diagnosis of hepatic mucinous cystic neoplasm according to the WHO classification, despite attenuated stromal features due to marked fibrosis. Discussion

We encountered a rare case of hepatic MCN presenting with a central hematoma and atypical imaging findings. In MCN of the liver (MCN-L), multilocular cystic morphology is a typical imaging feature and is defined as a single cyst divided by internal septa or showing a cyst-in-cyst configuration while maintaining a smooth outer contour. In the study by Zen et al. [4] multilocularity was observed in 81% of cases, whereas Kozaka et al. [5] reported septation or multilocularity in 33 of 34 patients (97%) and a cyst-in-cyst appearance (defined as a small cyst less than one-fourth the diameter of the entire lesion attached to the wall or septum without external indentation) in 31 of 34 patients (91%). These findings suggest that cyst-in-cyst morphology represents a specific manifestation within the broader category of multilocularity and that both are highly characteristic imaging features of MCN-L [4,5].

In contrast to these typical multilocular or cyst-in-cyst morphologies, the present lesion showed a lobulated configuration with a central hematoma, which made diagnosis challenging. On ultrasonography performed 2 years before presentation, the lesion had a multicystic or lobulated morphology, defined as an aggregation of multiple cysts with an indented contour or a cluster of small cysts associated with a solid component [4,6]. In the comparative study by Zen et al. [4] examining radiologic and macroscopic features of MCN and IPNB, a multicystic configuration was observed in 2 of 26 MCN cases (8%) and in 8 of 10 IPNB cases (80%) [4]. In the radiologic analysis by Kozaka et al. [5] 8 of 34 MCN lesions (24%) demonstrated a lobulated contour. Kozaka et al. [5] also described biliary prolapse—protrusion of the cystic component into the upstream dilated bile duct—as one of the morphologic features of MCN; however, the present lesion exhibited circumferential bulging of the outer contour without biliary dilatation and was therefore considered distinct from biliary prolapse. Taken together, these reports suggest that lobulated morphology is less commonly observed in MCN-L and more frequently associated with IPNB.

Based on the patient’s lifestyle history and imaging findings, echinococcosis was considered among the differential diagnoses. Of the imaging features typical of echinococcosis [7], this case shared several with alveolar echinococcosis, including a hypovascular pattern, the presence of calcifications, and surrounding fibrosis and cysts. The lesion resembled type 3 in the MR-based classification of echinococcosis proposed by Kodama et al. [8], defined as a solid component surrounding large and/or irregular cysts with multiple small round cysts. However, several discrepancies were noted, particularly in cyst size and metabolic activity on FDG-PET: in echinococcosis, peripheral cysts are typically much smaller, and increased FDG uptake along the lesion margin is often observed. In addition, central cystic areas in echinococcosis usually represent necrosis or liquefactive degeneration rather than hemorrhage, although differentiation was difficult here because the chronic hematoma mimicked degenerative or necrotic change on CT and MR images. The serologic testing used to exclude echinococcosis in this case—combining Echinococcus multilocularis Em2 and recombinant Em18 ELISA with immunoblot—has nearly 100% sensitivity and approximately 95% specificity [7]. Although certain imaging features overlapped with those of alveolar echinococcosis, the overall radiologic and serologic assessment did not strongly favor this diagnosis. This case illustrates the complexity of differentiating atypical MCN-L from other cystic hepatic lesions.

Although bleeding into hepatic MCN is relatively uncommon, it is encountered in clinical practice. Large-scale studies have not reported the frequency of hemorrhage. In the series by Kozaka et al. [5] hemorrhage within the cyst wall was noted on T1-weighted MR images in 3 of 15 cases (20%) [5]. Zen et al. [4] reported hemosiderin deposition in 13 of 26 cases (50%) on pathological examination. Case reports have proposed several mechanisms of hemorrhage, including tumor-associated angiogenesis, cyst wall fragility, epithelial degeneration, stromal inflammation, and increased intracystic pressure due to mucin production [[9], [10], [11]]. The mechanism in the present case could not be determined. However, the central hematoma was considered chronic at presentation, supported by extensive surrounding fibrosis in the resected specimen. Histopathology revealed no distinct morphological features of ovarian-like stroma, possibly because of marked fibrosis. In the study by Quigley et al. [12] 12 of 34 MCN cases (35%) showed tumor-like or nodular degenerative changes such as fibrous inflammation or hemorrhage, and the authors noted that such changes may raise suspicion for malignancy on imaging. Atypical MCNs with hemorrhage or fibrosis require careful evaluation using both imaging and histopathological findings, as they can resemble infectious or neoplastic cystic lesions [13]. Although malignant transformation of MCN-L is relatively rare (reported in approximately 3%–6% of cases [4,12]), certain imaging features such as mural nodules, enhancing solid components, calcification, and hypervascularity have been associated with invasive carcinoma [13]. In the present case, no definite mural nodules or enhancing solid components were identified, and FDG-PET showed no abnormal uptake, suggesting a low likelihood of overt malignant transformation [14]. However, imaging findings alone cannot reliably exclude malignancy [13].

Conclusion

This case highlights an unusual presentation of hepatic mucinous cystic neoplasm with a central hematoma, which posed a diagnostic challenge. Recognition of such atypical imaging features is important for distinguishing MCN from other cystic liver lesions and guiding appropriate management.
