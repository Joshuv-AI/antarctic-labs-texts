Enhanced ovarian cancer diagnosis using deep learning on pelvic ultrasound with integrated clinical data: retrospective multicenter study
Authors: Sohyeon Jeong, Hyewon Cho, Jooyeon Kim, Sanghoon Lee, Jae Yun Song, Jin Hwa Hong, Jae Kwan Lee, Seon Kyung Kim, Hyun-Woong Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to develop a deep learning (DL) model to enhance the differential diagnosis of benign and malignant ovarian tumors by integrating pelvic ultrasound images with clinical data, such as age and cancer antigen (CA)-125 levels.MethodsWe analyzed pelvic ultrasound images, age, and CA-125 levels from 804 patients diagnosed with ovarian tumors (446 benign, 358 malignant) from 2015 to 2022. Images were segmented into cystic and solid components for feature extraction. Patients were divided into training (n=565), validation (n=76), and test datasets (n=163). ResNet50 and DenseNet121 models were trained on these data, with clinical information added to classifier architecture for improved prediction accuracy.ResultsUsing ultrasound images alone, ResNet50 and DenseNet121 achieved areas under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUCs) of 0.84 and 0.82, respectively. When clinical data and segmented solid images were included, AUCs improved to 0.95 for ResNet50 and 0.96 for DenseNet121. For the test set, ResNet50 and DenseNet121 achieved sensitivities of 90% and 81%, specificities of 93% and 97%, positive predictive values of 92% and 95%, and negative predictive values of 92% and 86%.ConclusionBinary classification model based on DL algorithms using ultrasound images can distinguish between benign and malignant ovarian tumors accurately. Segmentation of solid portion and clinical information of age and CA-125 at diagnosis combined with the pelvic ultrasound images increased the accuracy of the classification model.

INTRODUCTION

Ovarian cancer poses a significant threat to women worldwide, with its high mortality rates often stemming from late-stage diagnoses [12]. In localized stages, the 5-year relative survival rate for ovarian cancer is 93%; however, this rate decreases significantly to 31% when distant metastasis is confirmed [3]. Thus, early detection plays a pivotal role in improving patient outcomes, highlighting the need for effective screening methods. However, as of now, there are no screening methods that have been proven effective [45]. Nevertheless, ultrasonography is a key imaging modality for diagnoses of incidentally found adnexal masses, due to its accessibility and non-invasiveness, making it widely used in clinical practice [67].

The Simple Rules and ADNEX (Assessment of Different NEoplasias in the adneXa) are 2 structured approaches used to evaluate ovarian cancer using ultrasound imaging [8910]. The Simple Rules, developed by the International Ovarian Tumor Analysis (IOTA) group, are a set of ten criteria designed to classify ovarian masses as benign or malignant based on their morphological features, such as irregular solid areas, ascites, and the presence of papillary projections. ADNEX, also an IOTA tool, goes further by not only distinguishing between benign and malignant masses but also categorizing the type of malignancy. However, the application of these rules relies heavily on the skill and experience of the ultrasound operator, potentially leading to variability in results [11].

In recent years, deep learning (DL) algorithms have shown promise in aiding medical imaging diagnostics [121314]. Numerous studies have demonstrated the potential of DL models to assist clinicians in accurately identifying various medical conditions, including cancers, from imaging data [151617]. Despite these advancements, research specifically targeting ovarian cancer using pelvic ultrasound remains limited. Previous studies have primarily focused on developing Artificial Intelligence (AI) models solely based on ultrasound images [1819], overlooking the potential benefits of incorporating additional clinical information and distinguishing specific features, such as solid components of the ovarian mass within the images, which is one of the features of ovarian malignancy.

Addressing this gap in the literature, our study aims to leverage DL techniques to enhance the accuracy of ovarian cancer diagnosis using pelvic ultrasound. We propose a comprehensive approach that integrates both imaging data and relevant clinical information, such as patient age and cancer antigen (CA)-125 levels, to improve diagnostic precision. Moreover, we aim to differentiate between benign and malignant lesions by specifically identifying solid components within the ultrasound images, which may serve as crucial indicators of malignancy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study design and participants

This retrospective, multicenter study was conducted to evaluate DL models for ovarian cancer diagnosis using pelvic ultrasound images, with data collected from Korea University Guro Hospital and Korea University Anam Hospital from 2015 to 2022. The study included 804 patients with histologically confirmed ovarian tumors, with 446 classified as benign and 358 as malignant. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of participating institutions, including Korea University Guro Hospital (IRB No. 2022GR0505), and the requirement for informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Inclusion criteria required complete clinical data, including age, CA-125 levels, and high-quality pelvic ultrasound images. Exclusion criteria removed cases with incomplete information or poor-quality images to minimize bias and ensure dataset integrity.

2. Procedure

The study utilized a comprehensive DL approach to analyze pelvic ultrasound images for accurate ovarian cancer diagnosis. Although different ultrasound machines were used at the 2 participating institutions, all pelvic ultrasound examinations were conducted by experienced gynecologic ultrasound specialists following consistent imaging protocols. This approach minimized procedural variability. Patient referral patterns were also comparable across both centers, with most cases originating from incidental findings during routine check-ups or from secondary referrals. Because image preprocessing and model training were conducted in a standardized manner, potential variability due to institutional differences was unlikely to have introduced significant bias.

The raw ultrasound images were manually annotated by 2 highly experienced ultrasound specialists in gynecologic oncology. Both annotators were blinded to the final histopathological outcomes to eliminate potential bias. Segmentation involved clearly distinguishing solid components within ovarian masses from cystic regions, with tumor boundaries highlighted in green and solid components marked in red (Fig. 1A). Given the visually clear distinctions between solid and cystic areas commonly observed in pelvic ultrasound images of ovarian tumors, annotation consistency was high, resulting in strong inter-observer agreement. These labeled regions formed the basis for image processing and DL model training. Following manual annotation, the images were pre-processed for DL model input. Image dimensions were standardized, and pixel intensities were normalized to a common scale. This ensured uniformity across the dataset, facilitating more accurate feature extraction by the DL models.

The clinical data, including age and CA-125 levels, were incorporated directly into the DL algorithm alongside the ultrasound images, without applying any normalization or transformation to the CA-125 values. Three convolutional neural network (CNN) models were developed: (1) tumor only (Classifier T), which used only the ovarian tumor images, (2) tumor + solid component (Classifier TS), which included both the tumor and solid component images, and (3) tumor + solid component + clinical information (Classifier TSC), which combined tumor and solid component images with clinical data, specifically patient age and CA-125 levels (Fig. 1B).

The dataset was randomly divided into 3 subsets: a training set (n=565), a validation set (n=76), and a test set (n=163) (Fig. 2). The training set was used to optimize the model’s parameters, while the validation set was employed for tuning hyperparameters and preventing overfitting. Early stopping was applied based on the performance on the validation set to avoid overfitting and ensure model generalizability. The final evaluation of the models was conducted on the test set, which was kept entirely separate from the training and validation datasets. The test set provided an unbiased assessment of the models’ diagnostic performance in distinguishing benign from malignant ovarian tumors.

Patient selection flowchart. From 1,040 patients screened, 804 were included after excluding cases with poor image quality or missing CA-125. Patients were split into training, validation, and test sets.

CA-125, cancer antigen 125; US, ultrasound.

The DL framework was based on CNN architectures due to their proven efficacy in image classification tasks. Both Residual Network 50 (ResNet50) and Densely Connected Convolutional Network 121 (DenseNet121) were chosen for their ability to handle complex medical images. ResNet50, with its residual learning capabilities, and DenseNet121, known for its efficient feature propagation, were both trained on the labeled ultrasound images. Each model was trained using a binary cross-entropy loss function, and the Adam optimizer was employed to minimize the loss. The models were trained on a high-performance computing cluster to ensure efficient processing of the large dataset.

3. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Python (scikit-learn, SciPy; Python Software Foundation, Beaverton, OR, USA) and R statistical software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were summarized using means ± standard deviations or medians and ranges, depending on their distribution. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

For comparisons between benign and malignant ovarian tumors (Table 1), continuous variables (age, CA-125 level, body mass index [BMI]) were assessed using the Student’s t-test for normally distributed data or the Mann-Whitney U test for data not normally distributed. Categorical variables, including menopausal status, parity, histopathological subtypes, and International Federation of Gynaecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage, were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Comparisons of patient age and CA-125 levels across the training, validation, and test datasets (Table S1) were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test due to non-normal distributions of these variables. Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided p-value of less than 0.05.

The diagnostic performance of DL classifiers (Classifier T, TS, and TSC) was evaluated using multiple metrics: accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), Matthews correlation coefficient, and F1-score. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated based on predicted probabilities from the independent test dataset, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. Optimal cut-off thresholds for sensitivity and specificity were determined using Youden’s index.

A primary aim of this study was the exploratory evaluation of the incremental performance gain achieved by integrating features from T to TS to TSC. Therefore, formal statistical tests for multiple comparisons were not performed to compare the AUC performance across the T, TS, and TSC classifiers, nor between the ResNet50 and DenseNet121 architectures. This decision was made to prevent overly conservative corrections from potentially obscuring clinically meaningful improvements, although we acknowledge this as a methodological limitation.

RESULTS

1. Patient characteristics

A total of 804 patients with ovarian tumors were included, comprising 446 benign and 358 malignant cases. Patient demographics and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Significant differences were observed between benign and malignant groups in terms of age, menopausal status, parity, and CA-125 levels (all p<0.001). BMI did not differ significantly between groups (p=0.496). The distribution of age and CA-125 levels across training, validation, and test datasets showed no significant differences, confirming balanced datasets for model evaluation (Table S1).

2. Model performance across classifiers

The study developed and compared 3 distinct DL classifiers based on their input data. The first classifier (Classifier T) used only the ultrasound images of the ovarian tumors. The second classifier (Classifier TS) included both the ovarian tumor and the solid component of the tumors. The third classifier (Classifier TSC) integrated the ultrasound images and clinical data, age and CA-125 levels.

When applied to the test dataset, Classifier T achieved moderate diagnostic accuracy of 73.6% with ResNet50, reflecting the value of tumor images alone, but lacked precision in detecting malignant cases. In comparison, Classifier TS performed better, with an accuracy of 82.2% with ResNet50, showing that adding information about the solid component improved the model’s ability to differentiate between benign and malignant tumors. The highest accuracy was achieved by Classifier TSC, with an accuracy of 92% in ResNet50, demonstrating the value of incorporating clinical data along with the ultrasound images. The trends were consistent across both ResNet50 and DenseNet121 architectures, confirming the robustness of the models across different neural network designs (Table 2).

To assess the models' ability to distinguish between benign and malignant ovarian tumors, ROC curves were generated for each classifier, and the AUC was used to compare the diagnostic performance of the models (Fig. 3, Table S2). For Classifier T, the AUC was 0.82 (95% CI=0.75–0.87), reflecting the limited diagnostic capacity when using only tumor images. Adding the solid component improved the AUC to 0.92 (95% CI=0.89–0.97) for Classifier TS, indicating a notable increase in diagnostic accuracy. The most significant improvement was observed with the Classifier TSC model, which achieved an AUC of 0.96 (95% CI=0.93–0.98). This model, which integrated tumor and solid component images along with patient-specific clinical data, provided superior performance in identifying malignant tumors. DenseNet121 yielded slightly higher AUC values compared to ResNet50, though both architectures were effective.

3. Diagnostic metrics and model comparisons

The diagnostic performance of the classifiers was further evaluated using metrics such as sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and the F1-score. The F1 score is a metric that measures the balance between precision and recall in an AI model, providing a single score that reflects the model’s accuracy in identifying positive cases. The Classifier T model showed limited sensitivity (45.2%) but high specificity (96.7%) in detecting malignant tumors. This indicated that while the model was good at identifying benign cases, it missed a substantial number of malignant tumors, resulting in a relatively low diagnostic accuracy. Classifier TS improved the sensitivity to 63% and maintained a high specificity of 97.8%. The combination of these metrics resulted in a higher F1-score of 0.76, compared to 0.61 for Classifier T (Table S3).

The Classifier TSC model, which incorporated clinical data in addition to imaging, achieved the best diagnostic performance, with a sensitivity of 90.4% and a specificity of 93.3%. The F1-score for Classifier TSC ResNet50 model was 0.91, reflecting its balanced performance in identifying both benign and malignant cases. The model’s PPV and NPV were also high, at 91.7% and 92.3%, respectively (Fig. 4).

Both ResNet50 and DenseNet121 models exhibited high diagnostic performance, with ResNet50 slightly outperforming DenseNet121 in some areas. For the Classifier TSC, ResNet50 achieved a higher F1-score of 0.91 compared to DenseNet121’s 0.87, indicating that ResNet50 was more effective in balancing precision and recall. In terms of accuracy, ResNet50 achieved 92%, which was higher than DenseNet121’s 89.6%. ResNet50 also had a higher sensitivity at 90.4% compared to DenseNet121’s 80.8%, indicating that ResNet50 was better at identifying true positive malignant cases. However, DenseNet121 performed marginally better in terms of specificity, achieving 96.7%, compared to ResNet50’s 93.3%. DenseNet121 also had a higher PPV of 95.2% compared to ResNet50’s 91.7%.

DISCUSSION

This study developed and validated DL models that integrate pelvic ultrasound images with clinical data, demonstrating a high diagnostic performance in differentiating between benign and malignant ovarian tumors. Among the tested classifiers, the model combining tumor and solid component images with clinical data (age and CA-125 levels) provided the highest accuracy, achieving an AUC of 0.95 and 0.96 with ResNet50 and DenseNet121. Furthermore, the F1 scores for the classifier that combined ultrasound images with clinical data were high, reaching 0.91 for ResNet50 and 0.87 for DenseNet121.

The high diagnostic performance of our models underscores the potential of DL models to assist clinicians in accurately diagnosing ovarian cancer, especially in cases where conventional ultrasound evaluation may be limited. AI models may act as decision support tools [202122], especially in settings where clinicians may not have specialized expertise in interpreting complex ovarian ultrasound images. Given that ovarian cancer diagnosis often relies on subtle imaging findings that may be challenging to interpret, particularly for general practitioners or clinicians in resource-limited settings, AI models with high sensitivity and specificity could significantly reduce diagnostic errors and improve patient outcomes. Timmerman et al. [1023] developed the ADNEX model, which integrates clinical data and ultrasound features to differentiate between benign and malignant adnexal masses. This model demonstrated an AUC of ≥0.90 across different settings, underscoring the clinical utility of combining imaging with clinical information to assist non-specialist clinicians [24]. However, our AI models provide a more advanced solution by integrating CNNs with clinical data and tumor segmentation. This enables them to go beyond existing risk prediction models, such as ADNEX and Ovarian-Adnexal Reporting and Data System (O-RADS) [25], by automatically analyzing complex imaging data and reducing reliance on manual interpretation. By automating this process, our models offer clinicians an enhanced tool that may improve diagnostic accuracy in clinical settings. In practical terms, a high AUC translates into fewer false-negative and false-positive results, thereby allowing clinicians to more confidently distinguish malignant from benign ovarian tumors. Consequently, this may help in better triaging of patients, reducing unnecessary surgical interventions for benign lesions and expediting accurate diagnoses and appropriate treatment planning for malignancies. Although a direct comparison of our DL model’s performance to clinical expert diagnosis was beyond the scope of this retrospective study, the high diagnostic accuracy achieved by our DL model strongly suggests its potential clinical utility. Nonetheless, prospective validation studies directly comparing DL algorithms to clinicians' diagnostic performance are necessary to further substantiate these findings.

The high diagnostic accuracy achieved in this study is likely attributable to 2 key factors: segmentation of solid tumor components and the integration of clinical data. Previous AI models for ovarian cancer diagnosis have typically focused on image analysis alone [1926]. For instance, Gao et al. [27] explored the use of CNNs for classifying ovarian cancer based on medical imaging, but their models did not achieve the high level of accuracy seen in our study, likely due to the lack of integrated clinical data. The inclusion of clinical factors such as CA-125, which is elevated in many ovarian cancer cases [2829], significantly enhanced our model's performance. Moreover, segmentation of the solid components within tumors was a critical factor contributing to the improved performance of our models. Presence of solid component is one of the well-known important predictors of a malignant ovarian mass [92530]. Thus, segmentation of specific tumor features, such as solid components in our model, can lead to more accurate classification models for ovarian cancer. By incorporating this information, our model achieved a balance between sensitivity (90.41%) and specificity (93.33%) in identifying malignancies, a notable improvement over simpler image-only model. The F1 score, which is a balanced metric considering both precision and recall, was near 1.0 for our classifier that combined tumor, solid component, and clinical data, indicating its effectiveness in differentiating benign from malignant ovarian tumors.

In the current study, we employed both ResNet50 and DenseNet121 frameworks to classify ovarian tumors as benign, or malignant. These 2 architectures, although both widely used in DL for medical image analysis, differ significantly in their design principles and capabilities. ResNet50 introduces residual learning through skip connections, which allow the network to bypass certain layers and avoid the vanishing gradient problem [313233]. ResNet50, with its residual learning framework, was particularly effective at identifying complex image patterns within the ovarian tumors, achieving higher sensitivity (90.4%) and a balanced F1-score (0.91). This makes ResNet50 advantageous in clinical settings where it is crucial to capture true positive malignancy cases to avoid missed diagnoses. On the other hand, DenseNet121 takes a different approach by connecting each layer to every other layer in a feed-forward manner [34]. DenseNet121’s densely connected architecture facilitates better feature propagation and reuse throughout the network, allowing it to learn more diverse and robust features with fewer parameters compared to ResNet50. In this study, DenseNet121 demonstrated higher specificity (96.7%) and PPV (95.2%), suggesting it could reduce false positives more effectively, which is beneficial in minimizing unnecessary interventions for benign cases. However, DenseNet121’s F1-score of 0.87, slightly lower than ResNet50, reflects a slight trade-off in the model’s balance between precision and recall. Given the high diagnostic performance of both frameworks, a hybrid approach that leverages the strengths of both ResNet50’s deep feature extraction and DenseNet121’s feature reuse might further enhance the accuracy of ovarian tumor classification models. Future work could explore combining these architectures to create a more robust model that excels across all performance metrics.

Despite the strong performance of our models, certain limitations must be acknowledged. First, the relatively small dataset, while multicenter, limits the model’s generalizability. Studies have demonstrated that larger, multicenter datasets are crucial for validating AI models, as they help to better capture the diversity of patient populations and imaging protocols, which ultimately leads to more robust and widely applicable models [2735]. Without validation across multiple centers and broader populations, there is a risk that the current model may perform less effectively when applied to different demographic groups or clinical settings. Second, the retrospective study design may introduce biases inherent to the collected data, and prospective studies are needed to validate the model's performance in real-time clinical settings. Third, the study did not fully explore the impact of outliers or rare cases on model performance. Previous research has shown that AI models can struggle with rare or atypical cases, such as borderline ovarian tumors, which often present with ambiguous features that are harder to classify [36]. A more comprehensive analysis of misclassified or edge cases could help improve the model’s reliability and address its limitations in handling more challenging diagnoses. Addressing these issues would require further research, including the incorporation of rare cases into training datasets, as well as prospective validation to ensure the model’s applicability in diverse clinical environments. Furthermore, our study did not evaluate the diagnostic performance of DL models separately by ovarian cancer histological subtype or FIGO stage due to the limited number of cases in certain subgroups. Further studies with larger datasets are warranted to investigate model accuracy across specific histological subtypes and disease stages.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates the potential of DL models to enhance the accuracy of ovarian cancer diagnosis using pelvic ultrasound images. By incorporating clinically relevant features alongside imaging data, we developed a model that outperforms traditional ultrasound-based algorithms and previous AI models. While further validation with larger datasets is required, the results of this study highlight the potential for AI to assist clinicians in making accurate and timely diagnoses of ovarian malignancies, which may ultimately improve patient outcomes.
