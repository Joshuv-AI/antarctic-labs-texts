Prevalence and factors associated with psychosocial distress and trauma among Rohingya refugees living in Cox’s Bazar and Bhasan Char in Bangladesh
Authors: Nuruzzaman Khan, Md Badsha Alam, Gemma Tarpey-Brown, M Mofizul Islam, Karen Block
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•This study is the first to examine psychosocial distress and/or trauma among Rohingya refugees relocated to Bhasan Char, addressing a critical gap in refugee mental health research.•A significantly higher prevalence of distress was found in Bhasan Char (60.8 %) compared to Cox’s Bazar (38.4 %), highlighting the mental health risks in isolated relocation settings.•Multivariable analysis revealed that distress was not only location-dependent but also significantly associated with perceived safety, disability level, household size, and unmet healthcare needs.•Refugees feeling unsafe at night or living with moderate to severe disabilities had up to 72 % higher odds of experiencing distress, indicating the compounding effects of environmental and individual factors.•The findings underscore the need for targeted, context-specific mental health interventions addressing both structural and personal risk factors in refugee settings.

Introduction

The Rohingya refugee crisis has resulted in the prolonged displacement of over a million refugees in Bangladesh, primarily concentrated in the overcrowded camps of Cox’s Bazar (Abdelkader, 2013; UNHCR, 2024). Integrated transportation and communications connected to the main administrative system in Bangladesh, as well as infrastructure in the camps, have been developed over the years, enabling better access to international aid (USAID, 2020). However, recent efforts to alleviate the severe overcrowding in Cox’s Bazar, where approximately one million Rohingya refugees currently reside, entail the Bangladeshi government relocating Rohingya refugees to a newly developed settlement in Bhasan Char, located 40 km from the mainland (Islam et al., 2021). This island is highly susceptible to extreme weather events, including cyclones and flooding. Its geographic isolation complicates access to essential services and resources, making it challenging for humanitarian organizations to deliver aid and health care services effectively (Islam et al., 2021). Limited infrastructure further strains the capacity of international agencies to provide adequate support, creating a burden on relief efforts (M.M. Islam MS et al., 2022). As a result, human rights organizations, including Human Rights Watch and Amnesty International, have raised concerns about the island's isolation, labeling it an "island prison" and requesting that refugees not be sent there (Hossain MS, 2022). Despite these criticisms and international calls for action, Bangladesh has continued the relocation, arguing that it provides a solution to the challenges faced in Cox’s Bazar. This relocation has led to approximately 35,629 Rohingya refugees now living in Bhasan Char (Hossain MS, 2022; UNHCR, 2023b).

Both settings present unique challenges to refugees’ mental health. The Cox’s Bazar camps grapple with extreme overcrowding and limited resources, while Bhasan Char’s geographical isolation, environmental vulnerability, and restrictions on refugee mobility further compound mental health issues alongside conditions similar to those in Cox’s Bazar (Hossain MS, 2022; Islam et al., 2021; Neef et al., 2023). Existing data from the Cox’s Bazar camps indicate a high prevalence of psychosocial distress and/or trauma, often manifesting as anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), influenced by demographic characteristics, past trauma, and socio-economic factors (O’Connor and Seager, 2021; Riley et al., 2020; Ritsema and Armstrong-Hough, 2023; Trang et al., 2024; Ullah et al., 2023). Although these estimates are primarily based on small-scale survey data, they highlight the common mental health concerns of Rohingya refugees residing in Cox’s Bazar. In contrast, the mental health conditions of Bhasan Char residents remain largely unknown, as no previous research has been conducted in this area.

Given the ongoing relocation process and the growing population in Bhasan Char, there is an urgent need to understand the mental health implications of living in such a structurally and environmentally distinct setting. Bhasan Char is a remote island in the Bay of Bengal, while Cox’s Bazar lies along the southeastern coast of Bangladesh and hosts the world’s largest refugee camps. The Cox’s Bazar refugee camps were first established in 1977–1978 when around 200,000 Rohingya refugees fled persecution and mass arrests in Myanmar (Islam, 2020). The population surged again in 2017 after renewed military violence and now exceeds one million refugees living in the overcrowded camps (Selth, 2018). In response to extreme congestion, the government of Bangladesh developed infrastructure in Bhasan Char—a 13-square-kilometer island—to relocate part of the refugee population (Banerjee, 2020; Islam and Siddika, 2022). Despite criticism regarding the island’s isolation and natural hazard risks, relocations began in December 2020, and by September 2024, more than 35,000 Rohingya refugees resided there (UNHCR, 2023b).

It is also important to explore mental health conditions using large-scale, representative data, particularly in disparate locations such as Cox’s Bazar and Bhasan Char. Comparing mental health outcomes between these two settings may offer valuable insights into how place-based factors—such as isolation, overcrowding, and access to services—interact with individual vulnerabilities to shape psychosocial outcomes. To address this gap, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of psychosocial distress and/or trauma among Rohingya refugees in Bhasan Char and compare it with that in Cox’s Bazar, while also identifying key sociodemographic and environmental predictors influencing these outcomes.

Methods

Study setting

This study was conducted by UNHCR in two locations: the Rohingya refugee camps in Cox’s Bazar and the relocated refugee settlement on Bhasan Char Island in Bangladesh. Cox’s Bazar, located in southeastern Bangladesh, comprises a cluster of densely populated camps administered under two sub-districts—Ukhiya and Teknaf (Fig. 1). These camps have established humanitarian infrastructure and ongoing international aid interventions. In contrast, the Bhasan Char relocation is a relatively new settlement endeavor (UNHCR, 2023b). The government of Bangladesh developed the infrastructure to resettle a portion of the Rohingya population. This endeavor is currently supported by selected national and international agencies under restricted access and mobility conditions (Hossain MS, 2022). The two sites differ considerably in geographic accessibility, population density, and access to services—factors that may influence the mental health and wellbeing of residents. Both locations were included in this study to capture and compare the psychosocial impacts of these contrasting environments on Rohingya refugees.Fig. 1Geographical representation of Cox’s Bazar and Bhasan Char refugee camps in Bangladesh.Fig 1

Geographical representation of Cox’s Bazar and Bhasan Char refugee camps in Bangladesh.

Survey and sampling technique

This observational study was conducted by analyzing secondary cross-sectional UNHCR survey microdata, obtained from the UNHCR by submitting a research proposal specifically tailored to this project. The survey was conducted through a collaboration between Research, Evaluation, and Assessment for Communities in Humanitarian Need (REACH), Agency for Technical Cooperation and Development (ACTED), Needs and Population Monitoring - International Organization for Migration (NPM-IOM), UNHCR, and other non-government stakeholders, with results released as part of the Bhasan Char Needs Assessment for individuals residing in Bhasan Char, and the Joint Multi-Sectoral Needs Assessment (J-MSNA) for individuals residing in Cox’s Bazar (UNHCR, 2022, 2023a). In Cox’s Bazar, a multi-sectoral survey has been conducted since 2018, with face-to-face interviews since 2019. We analyzed data from the fourth round of the survey conducted in 2024 (UNHCR, 2022, 2023a). In Bhasan Char, the survey was first conducted in 2022, with the 2023 Bhasan Char Needs Assessment marking the second round, which was included in our analysis (UNHCR, 2022, 2023a).

Data in both locations were collected through random sampling. The 2024 J-MSNA survey in Cox’s Bazar that we analyzed was conducted across 33 camps, covering 3400 households selected using a two stage stratified random sampling approach, with a 95 % confidence interval and a 10 % margin of error at the camp level (UNHCR, 2022). At the first stage, strata were selected randomly, followed by the inclusion of eligible respondents at the second stage. This approach ensured that every unit in the population had a known and non-zero probability of selection, thereby minimizing selection bias and improving the precision of estimates within and across strata. Data were collected from the adult household representatives responding on behalf of the household and its members, resulting in a total of 18,117 individuals represented in the dataset. In Bhasan Char, the 2023 survey interviewed a total of 408 households, covering a total of 1777 individuals following a similar sampling strategy to the Cox’s Bazar survey (UNHCR, 2023a). In this study, we pooled and separately analyzed these two surveys.

Study sample

We analyzed a sub-sample of 9087 individuals’ data from the original survey, including 8274 from the Cox’s Bazar Rohingya refugee camps and 813 from the Bhasan Char refugee camps. The sub-sample selection criteria are presented in Fig. 2. Specifically, the analyzed samples were extracted based on the following inclusion criteria: (i) Individuals aged 18 and older, (ii) availability of distress and/or trauma-related data, and (iii) those whose responses provided information for other variables included in this study.Fig. 2Study sample selection process to investigate psychosocial distress and/or trauma among Rohingya Refugees, 2023–2024.Fig 2

Study sample selection process to investigate psychosocial distress and/or trauma among Rohingya Refugees, 2023–2024.

Outcome variable

The outcome variable considered was the presence of psychosocial distress and/or trauma, classified dichotomously as "yes" or "no." During the survey, data were collected from an adult household representative responding on behalf of the household and its members. For the considered outcome in our study, this adult household member was asked the following question regarding all household members aged 18 or more: “Has any member of your household suffered or shown frequent signs of psychosocial distress or trauma in the last two weeks?” This question is commonly used in surveys or assessments conducted by humanitarian organizations, particularly those focusing on mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) in crisis-affected populations, including UNHCR and WHO. The question also aligns with the Inter-Agency Standing Committee (IASC) Guidelines on Mental Health and Psychosocial Support in Emergency Settings. Following the question, examples were provided (e.g., nightmares, prolonged sadness, extreme fatigue without physical exertion, frequent tearfulness, hopelessness about the future, and avoidance behaviors) with detailed explanations to ensure clarity. Responses were recorded dichotomously as "yes" or "no," along with the names of every household member for whom the response was provided. This allows us to calculate the psychosocial distress and/or trauma variable, where the numerator is the number of individuals with an affirmative response, and the denominator is the total number of individuals in the sample.

Explanatory variables

We considered a range of explanatory variables, selected based on a review of existing literature and their availability in the surveys analyzed (Hossain et al., 2021; Jahan et al., 2024; Riley et al., 2020; Ullah et al., 2023). These included: resident location (Cox’s Bazar or Bhasan Char) and, within Cox’s Bazar, specific camps (Ukhiya, Teknaf); individuals’ age (18–59 years, 60 or over years); individuals' sex (male, female); perceived safety when walking alone after dark (very safe, fairly safe, a bit unsafe, very unsafe, never walk alone after dark); level of disabilities (no disabilities, moderate disabilities, severe disabilities); age of household head (18–59 years, 60 or over years); household size (≤4, 5–7, ≥8); and unmet healthcare need (yes, no). The unmet healthcare needs variable was generated based on individuals’ indications of needing and wanting healthcare in the past two weeks but not receiving it. These variables were generated based on the responses provided by the heads of households and the characteristics reported for the individuals (Supplementary Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize individuals’ demographic, geographic, and socioeconomic characteristics across the study sites. The prevalence of psychosocial distress and/or trauma was calculated overall and by location (Cox’s Bazar and Bhasan Char) while their differences were tested for statistical significance using the chi-square test.

To identify factors associated with psychosocial distress and/or trauma, we conducted multivariable logistic regression analyses. Separate regressions were conducted for the overall sample, as well as for individuals residing in Teknaf and Ukhiya camps within Cox’s Bazar, and for those in Bhasan Char. In addition, we conducted sex-specific models to explore whether the predictors of psychosocial distress and/or trauma differed between males and females. We used Hosmer-Lemeshow test to produce goodness of fit of the models.

All models were adjusted for key socio-demographic variables, including age, sex (except in sex-specific models), disability status, perceived safety when walking alone after dark, household size, age of household head, and unmet healthcare needs. For the overall sample, place of residence (Cox’s Bazar vs. Bhasan Char) was included as a key exposure variable, while the Cox’s Bazar-only models included sub-district (Teknaf vs. Ukhiya).

Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF) before final model estimation. Only adjusted models are presented in the results tables, reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with corresponding 95 % confidence intervals (95 % CI). Unadjusted models were used in preliminary analyses but are not included in the final tables. Geographic distribution of distress prevalence across camps was visualized using ArcGIS 10.4, while all other analyses were performed using Stata version 18.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

We analyzed secondary data obtained from UNHCR after submitting a research proposal for this project. The UNHCR secured ethical approval from the relevant organization and shared non-identifiable data with us. We analyzed secondary non-identifiable data and, therefore, no additional ethical approval was required.

Results

Background characteristics of the individuals

The background characteristics of the individuals in the overall sample and by area are presented in Table 1. The majority of the individuals were aged 18–59, comprising 90.0 % overall, with a slightly higher representation in Bhasan Char (93.8 %) than in Cox’s Bazar (89.7 %). Sex distribution showed a nearly equal split, with females representing 52.9 % overall. Most household heads were aged 18–59 (79.9 % overall), with Bhasan Char showing an even higher concentration (91.6 %) in this age group. Household sizes tended to be larger in Cox’s Bazar, where 21.0 % reported eight or more people, compared to only 8.6 % in Bhasan Char. Feelings of safety when walking alone after dark varied significantly between resident locations: in Bhasan Char, a notably higher proportion (49.0 %) felt very safe, contrasting with Cox’s Bazar (22.1 %). Prevalence of psychosocial distress and/or trauma among Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh

Fig. 3 presents the prevalence of psychosocial distress and/or trauma among Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh, overall and by area, and the statistical significance of differences. We found an overall prevalence of approximately 40 %, with higher levels among residents of Bhasan Char (60.5 %) compared to those in Cox’s Bazar (38.4 %). When we performed a proportion test to determine the statistical significance, we found that this difference was statistically significant. Distribution of psychosocial distress or trauma among Rohingya refugees in Cox’s Bazar and Bhasan Char across selected explanatory variables

Table 2 presents the distribution of psychosocial distress and/or trauma across selected explanatory variables. There were considerable variations in psychosocial distress and/or trauma among Rohingya refugees based on resident location, age, sex, disability status, safety perceptions, household size, and unmet healthcare needs. In Cox’s Bazar, psychosocial distress and/or trauma were relatively balanced between Teknaf (40.8 %) and Ukhiya (37.8 %). However, older individuals in Bhasan Char reported a significantly higher prevalence of distress (74.0 %) than their counterparts in Cox’s Bazar (42.3 %). Sex differences indicate that females in Bhasan Char experience higher psychosocial distress and/or trauma (61.7 %) than males (59.1 %), while psychosocial distress and/or trauma rates are more balanced by sex in Cox's Bazar. There was also a strong link between perceived safety and psychosocial distress and/or trauma; those feeling "very unsafe" reported the highest psychosocial distress or trauma, reaching 66.7 % in Bhasan Char versus 45.1 % in Cox’s Bazar. Disabilities further amplified psychosocial distress and/or trauma, with severe disabilities associated with 79.2 % psychosocial distress and/or trauma in Bhasan Char, compared to 50 % in Cox’s Bazar. Psychosocial distress and/or trauma were also more common in larger households in Bhasan Char (75.7 %) compared to Cox’s Bazar (38.9 %). Finally, unmet healthcare needs appeared to exacerbate psychosocial distress and/or trauma, with higher rates of distress among those requiring healthcare—63.3 % in Bhasan Char versus 41.0 % in Cox’s Bazar. Predictors of psychosocial distress and/or trauma among Rohingya refugees in Cox’s Bazar and Bhasan Char of Bangladesh

Factors associated with psychosocial distress and/or trauma among Rohingya refugees, both overall and separately for individuals in Cox’s Bazar and Bhasan Char, are presented in Table 3. Overall, residing in Bhasan Char was associated with a markedly higher likelihood of psychosocial distress and/or trauma, with an adjusted odds ratio (aOR) of 2.86 (95 % CI: 2.44–3.34, p < 0.01) compared to Cox’s Bazar. Within Cox’s Bazar, individuals in Teknaf had a slightly higher likelihood of psychosocial distress and/or trauma than those in Ukhiya, with an aOR of 1.13 (95 % CI: 1.01–1.26, p = 0.03). Safety perceptions had a substantial impact, particularly in Bhasan Char, where individuals feeling "a bit unsafe" had nearly five times the likelihood of psychosocial distress and/or trauma (aOR of 4.72, 95 % CI: 1.90–11.70, p < 0.01) compared to those feeling "very safe." In Cox’s Bazar, this effect was weaker, with those feeling "a bit unsafe" showing only a 15 % increased likelihood of psychosocial distress and/or trauma (aOR of 1.15, 95 % CI: 1.01–1.32, p = 0.04). Additionally, individuals who felt very unsafe or never walked alone after dark, compared to those feeling very safe, reported higher likelihoods of psychosocial distress and/or trauma in the overall Disability status significantly affected psychosocial distress and/or trauma in both locations. Moderate disabilities were associated with an aOR of 2.75 (95 % CI: 1.66–4.55, p < 0.01) in Bhasan Char and 1.24 (95 % CI, 1.10–1.40, p < 0.01) in Cox’s Bazar. Severe disabilities further elevated psychosocial distress and/or trauma in Cox’s Bazar (aOR of 1.68, 95 % CI: 1.39–2.05, p < 0.01). Household size also influenced psychosocial distress and/or trauma likelihood, with larger households (5–7 members and ≥8 members) showing significantly higher odds of psychosocial distress and/or trauma compared to smaller households, with the effect highest for individuals in Bhasan Char. We also found higher odds of psychosocial distress and/or trauma among individuals who reported healthcare needs in the overall sample (aOR: 1.24, 95 % CI: 1.13–1.36, p < 0.01) and in the Cox’s Bazar sample (aOR: 1.25, 95 % CI: 1.13–1.37, p < 0.01). Further segregation of individuals in Cox’s Bazar by residence in Teknaf and Ukhiya camps showed results consistent with those reported for the overall sample and Cox’s Bazar specifically (Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

The study aimed to explore the prevalence of psychosocial distress and/or trauma, and associated factors, among Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh, specifically comparing those residing in Cox’s Bazar and Bhasan Char. We found an overall prevalence of psychosocial distress and/or trauma of 40.4 %, with significantly higher rates among Rohingya refugees in Bhasan Char (60.8 %) compared to those in Cox’s Bazar (38.4 %), though prevalence varied across different camps. After adjusting for socio-demographic factors, we observed a nearly threefold higher likelihood of distress and/or trauma among Rohingya refugees in Bhasan Char compared to those in Cox’s Bazar. Significant predictors of psychosocial distress and/or trauma included increasing age, perceived lack of safety when walking alone after dark, higher level of disability, larger household size, and unmet healthcare needs, both overall and within each location. These findings highlight the significant rate of mental distress among Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh, particularly those in Bhasan Char. The higher rates of psycho-social distress and/or trauma in Bhasan Char may reflect concerns from international organizations, NGOs and human rights groups regarding the Bangladeshi government’s relocation of refugees to Bhasan Char, given the island’s susceptibility to natural disasters and its geographic location (Islam and Siddika, 2022; Mahjabin and Ahmed, 2024). These environmental factors underscore the urgent need for targeted mental health services for this population and consideration of the suitability of the site for housing refugees.

The prevalence of psychosocial distress and/or trauma we found among Bhasan Char residents is the highest among the rates reported globally for refugee populations, which typically range from 5 % to 44 % (Alemi et al., 2014; Bryant et al., 2023; Cohen and Yaeger, 2021; Schlechter et al., 2021; Seruwagi et al., 2022; Tomasi et al., 2022; Walther et al., 2020). Since this is the first study that included data from Bhasan Char, the results could not be directly validated. However, the reported prevalence of psychosocial distress and/or trauma among Rohingya refugees in Cox’s Bazar aligns with existing global estimates for refugees (Alemi et al., 2014; Bryant et al., 2023; Cohen and Yaeger, 2021; Schlechter et al., 2021; Seruwagi et al., 2022; Tomasi et al., 2022; Walther et al., 2020). By contrast, these rates are substantially elevated compared to the general population (estimated at around 8 % for psychosocial distress), highlighting the distinct risks related to mental ill health experienced by refugees (Srikanthan et al., 2019; Viertiö et al., 2021).

The heightened prevalence of psychosocial distress and/or trauma among Rohingya refugees reflects a combination of past and ongoing adversities that amplify their mental health challenges (Jahan et al., 2024). As a community that has endured decades of discrimination, violence, genocide, and forced displacement from Myanmar, Rohingya refugees carry significant psychological burdens from traumatic experiences prior to arriving in Bangladesh (Abdelkader, 2013; M.M. Islam et al., 2022). These adversities have created widespread mental health issues, including symptoms of anxiety, depression, and PTSD, which are common among forced migrant populations (Riley et al., 2020; Schlechter et al., 2021). In Cox’s Bazar, overcrowded living conditions, limited access to healthcare, economic hardship, and an uncertain future compound these psychological challenges, creating a persistent environment of stress and anxiety (Jahan et al., 2024; Trang et al., 2024; Ullah et al., 2023). For Bhasan Char residents, the situation is particularly acute due to additional stressors unique to the island, which can exacerbate individual factors. Bhasan Char's remote location and environmental vulnerability to cyclones and flooding create chronic insecurity (Banerjee, 2020; Hossain MS, 2022; Islam and Siddika, 2022). The island is geographically isolated, which restricts access to essential resources, including mental health services, making it difficult for residents to seek timely support (Banerjee, 2020; Islam and Siddika, 2022). The limited infrastructure and restricted freedom of movement may intensify feelings of confinement and hopelessness, as residents face uncertainty about their future and isolation from wider support networks. This may contribute to Bhasan Char residents reporting a higher prevalence of psychological distress and/or trauma, despite having a lower prevalence of several individual risk factors considered in this study.

Age, lack of perceived safety, higher level of disability, household size, and unmet healthcare needs are significant predictors of psychosocial distress and/or trauma among Rohingya refugees. These findings reveal refugee mental health challenges are intertwined with a range of individual, environmental and structural level factors. This supports findings from other studies conducted on refugee mental health at the global level (Alemi et al., 2014; Bryant et al., 2023; Cohen and Yaeger, 2021; Schlechter et al., 2021; Seruwagi et al., 2022; Tomasi et al., 2022; Walther et al., 2020). Older age often correlates with an accumulation of traumatic experiences and exposure to prolonged displacement, increasing vulnerability to distress (Pietrzak et al., 2012). As refugees age, they may face increased mental health challenges due to factors such as chronic health conditions and reduced physical strength, making it more difficult to cope with the stresses of forced displacement (Ekoh and Walsh, 2024). This is especially true in refugee contexts where resources are scarce, and older adults may find it harder to access needed social support and healthcare, thus amplifying feelings of isolation and helplessness (Ullah et al., 2023).

The perception of a lack of safety, particularly at night, significantly affects mental health. In environments where safety is compromised, refugees are likely to experience heightened anxiety and hypervigilance, particularly at night when dangers such as harassment, theft, sexual or physical assault are perceived as higher (Vukčević Marković et al., 2023). This constant state of alertness and fear can exacerbate symptoms of PTSD and anxiety (Vukčević Marković et al., 2023). This predictor is crucial in settings like Bhasan Char, where isolation, limited accessibility, and environmental risks such as cyclones and floods can contribute to a sense of vulnerability and danger, despite the presence of security personnel and constant monitoring. Additional factors, such as limited trust in authorities, weakened community networks, restricted autonomy, and heightened anxiety from relocation to an unfamiliar environment, may further exacerbate these perceptions of insecurity.

Disability further compounds distress due to dependence on others in order to maintain basic needs for refugees living with disabilities (Fedele et al., 2021). Refugees with disabilities may also face social stigma and reduced mobility, which isolates them from community activities and limits their access to healthcare services (Crock and Ernst, 2017; Reilly, 2010). The physical and social barriers resulting from disability can lead to increased frustration, hopelessness, and dependence, making it difficult for these individuals to manage their mental health effectively in refugee settings (Crock and Ernst, 2017). Household size appears to be another significant predictor of distress. Larger families often face more economic pressures, with limited resources stretched among many members (Tomioka et al., 2024). Overcrowding can lead to a lack of privacy and a heightened sense of tension within households (Tomioka et al., 2024). Conditions in refugee camps, where personal space is often limited and infrastructure is insufficient, can lead to conflicts and mental strain, particularly when parents and caregivers feel they cannot provide adequately for their families. Finally, unmet healthcare needs stand out as a significant factor affecting mental health among refugees. In the absence of timely medical and mental health services, minor health concerns can develop into chronic issues, and untreated mental health conditions can lead to severe distress (Seruwagi et al., 2022). Refugees often lack access to essential healthcare due to logistical barriers, limited healthcare infrastructure, or insufficient resources (Jahan et al., 2024). This lack of healthcare exacerbates feelings of vulnerability, especially among those with pre-existing mental health conditions or physical ailments, as they are left to manage distressing symptoms without professional support (Alemi et al., 2014; Quader et al., 2021).

Together, these predictors paint a picture of compounded stressors that amplify mental health challenges in refugee settings. For Rohingya refugees, these factors underscore a complex and interdependent relationship between social, environmental, and health-related elements that heighten psychosocial distress. Addressing these vulnerabilities through tailored, location-specific interventions, improved healthcare access, and safety measures can provide critical support to enhance the mental well-being of this population.

The study presents several strengths and a few limitations. A major strength is that it is the first report on psychosocial distress and/or trauma among Bhasan Char residents, utilizing large-scale survey data, thereby filling a critical gap in the existing literature. By comparing findings with those from Cox’s Bazar, the study provides valuable insights into the mental health challenges faced by refugees in different contexts. Moreover, the question asked to record psychosocial distress and/or trauma is commonly used in surveys or assessments conducted by humanitarian organizations, particularly those focusing on mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) in crisis-affected populations, including UNHCR and WHO. As such, it provides an opportunity for broader comparison. The identification of significant predictors enhances our understanding of the specific risks within this population, which is crucial for informing targeted interventions and support services. Additionally, the data were extracted from UNHCR, which conducts routine data collection and analysis with robust methodologies, lending credibility to the findings. This agency supports the development of evidence-based policies and programs. However, as the data were sourced from the first investigation focused on the mental health of Bhasan Char residents, the findings cannot be directly compared with earlier studies specific to this population, limiting validation of our results. Furthermore, the cross-sectional design of the study restricts the establishment of causal relationships between identified predictors and psychosocial distress and/or trauma.

The reliance on self-reported measures may make the study findings subject to individual interpretation, which could introduce bias, as individuals might underreport their distress due to stigma or fear of repercussions. Moreover, the responses analyzed in this study were drawn from surveys conducted with the head of the household, rather than with the individuals whose data were used in this paper. There was a possibility that the head of household’s response could differ based on their characteristics, such as gender and length of stay in the camps. However, we were unable to account for these factors in the analysis due to the lack of relevant data. In addition, data were collected using a questionnaire rather than well-recognized, validated tools for measuring mental health conditions, such as the DASS-21. This makes the study findings less comparable to those from other settings. Furthermore, the findings from regressions may have suffered from the endogeneity bias, mainly through omitted variables and measurement errors. Lastly, the study does not account for the broader socio-political context and its impact on mental health, which may also influence the findings.

Conclusion

This study found an alarmingly high prevalence of psychosocial distress and/or trauma among Rohingya refugees, particularly in Bhasan Char, where rates exceed those reported in other refugee populations globally. The findings underscore the complex interplay of factors contributing to mental health challenges, including age, perceived safety, disability, household size, and unmet healthcare needs. As the first study focused on Bhasan Char, it serves as a crucial step in understanding the mental health landscape of Rohingya refugees temporarily living on the island. These insights are vital for informing targeted mental health interventions and policies, emphasizing the urgent need for comprehensive support services tailored to the unique challenges faced by Rohingya refugees in both Bhasan Char and Cox’s Bazar. Addressing these mental health needs is essential not only for improving individual well-being but also for fostering resilience within the broader refugee community.
