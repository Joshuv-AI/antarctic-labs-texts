Comparative Effectiveness and Safety of Upadacitinib and Vedolizumab in Biologic‐Experienced Patients With Ulcerative Colitis: A Retrospective Study
Authors: Neta Sror, Haim Leibovitzh, Ayal Hirsch, Tamar Thurm, Yulia Ron, Nitsan Maharshak, Nathaniel Aviv Cohen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesUpadacitinib (UPA) and vedolizumab (VDZ) have been approved to treat moderate‐to‐severe ulcerative colitis (UC); however, direct comparative data between these two regimens are lacking. We aimed to compare the effectiveness and safety of UPA and VDZ among patients with moderate‐to‐severe UC who had previously been treated with advanced therapies.MethodsPatients with moderate‐to‐severe UC receiving VDZ or UPA for at least 14 weeks following advanced therapy failure, including at least one anti‐tumor necrosis factor (TNF) agent, between 2015 and 2024, were retrospectively recruited. Clinical response and remission were defined by the Partial Mayo Score and the Simple Clinical Colitis Activity Index. The safety profile during the treatment was also compared.ResultsAltogether 66 patients treated with VDZ and 22 patients with UPA were included. The median age of the VDZ and UPA groups was 38 and 37 years, respectively. Patients receiving UPA achieved significantly higher rates of corticosteroid‐free remission after 52‐week therapy (75.0% vs. 25.0%; adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 5.68, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.50–21.00, p = 0.011); however, these patients were more likely to experience adverse events (AEs) (40.9% vs. 9.1%; aOR 4.60, 95% CI 1.20–17.00, p = 0.02), although medication discontinuation due to AEs was only noted in two (3.0%) patients receiving VDZ. No patients were hospitalized during the treatment period.ConclusionUC patients receiving UPA achieved significantly higher rates of corticosteroid‐free clinical remission but with more AEs, suggesting that decision‐making based on disease severity and comorbidities can help guide the positioning of these therapies in UC.

Introduction

Management of moderately to severely active ulcerative colitis (UC) involves a range of therapeutic options, targeting different sites in its inflammatory pathway. Advanced therapies, including biologic therapies and small molecules, have significantly changed the therapeutic paradigm for UC over the past two decades. Despite these advances, many patients either do not respond or lose response to the therapy, often requiring multiple switches between therapies with different mechanisms of action. Currently, anti‐tumor necrosis factor (TNF) agents remain the most commonly used advanced therapeutic regimen with the most significant evidence [1]. In patients failing anti‐TNF therapy, though there are multiple treatment options, comparative data to help guide therapeutic choice is still limited [1].

Vedolizumab (VDZ) is a monoclonal antibody that blocks lymphocytes' homing to the inflamed gut through the inhibition of the α4β7 integrin–mucosal cell adhesion interaction [2]. While upadacitinib (UPA) is a small‐molecule, selective Janus kinase (JAK)‐1 inhibitor that attenuates proinflammatory pathways [3]. In general, however, direct comparative effectiveness data between UPA and VDZ among UC patients are lacking. Indeed, only one head‐to‐head randomized clinical trial (RCT) showing VDZ superiority to adalimumab in achieving clinical remission and endoscopic improvement has been published [4]. As such, current therapeutic decision‐making is based on extrapolations from landmark clinical trials and studies involving indirect comparisons.

Multiple systematic reviews and network meta‐analyses, based on the comparisons of treatment response and remission rates with placebo, have shown that UPA is the best performing advanced therapy from the perspective of clinical efficacy at a cost of significantly higher rates of adverse events (AEs) [5, 6, 7, 8]. Although the efficacy of VDZ is lower in these studies, it consistently shows a favorable safety and tolerability profile, indicating that in certain patient populations VDZ remains an important option [9]. These studies do provide certain insights for positioning; however, all these data are interpreted based on RCTs and are anchored on response rates to placebo. There have been few studies comparing these therapies in real‐world setting where the patient populations are often distinct from those included in clinical trials [10, 11, 12].

Given the need for better insight regarding therapeutic positioning in biologic‐experienced patients with UC, we aimed to directly compare the effectiveness and safety of VDZ and UPA among those who have previously been treated with biologic therapies and at least one anti‐TNF agent.

Patients and Methods

Study Design and Patient Enrollment

This was a retrospective single‐center cohort study conducted at the Tel Aviv Sourasky Medical Center (Tel Aviv, Israel) between January 2015 and July 2024. Consecutive adult (≥ 18 years) patients with moderately to severely active UC were recruited, defined as meeting at least one of the following criteria: (a) Mayo Clinic score of 6–12 points; (b) Mayo endoscopic score of 2–3 points; (c) dependent on or refractory to corticosteroids; and (d) had experienced at least one episode of acute severe UC according to the Truelove‐Witts criteria [6, 13]. In addition, all patients had received either VDZ or UPA following prior advanced therapies, including anti‐TNF agents and small molecules. Patients experienced previous treatment failure with at least one anti‐TNF agent were mandatory for inclusion. Those who had received multiple advanced treatment regimens or more than one anti‐TNF agent were also eligible. Among all patients, one patient met the inclusion criteria for both cohorts and was included in both of them. Patients with a previous history of malignancy, prior colectomy, or insufficient data for analysis were excluded from the study. The cohort was followed for 52 weeks, with a minimum follow‐up duration of 14 weeks.

For patients treated with UPA orally, the induction regimen consisted of 45 mg daily for the first 8 weeks, followed by maintenance therapy with either 15 or 30 mg daily, depending on the discretion of the treating physician. Some of the patients were given the induction regimen for a longer time period of up to 16 weeks, which was also decided by the physician. While VDZ was administered intravenously with an induction regimen of 300 mg at baseline, Week 2, and Week 6, followed by maintenance treatment of 300 mg every 8 weeks thereafter. Dose adjustment of both drugs was considered every 12–14 weeks at the treating physician's discretion. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Board of our medical center (approval no. #0376–17‐TLV). Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective study design.

Definitions

Clinical response and remission were assessed using the partial Mayo (pMayo) score and the Simple Clinical Colitis Activity Index (SCCAI). Due to the limited and incomplete clinical data available in the medical records of some patients, the utility of both scores enabled a clinical assessment for all patients with at least one of these measures. Clinical response was defined as either a decrease of 2 or more points in the pMayo score from baseline or an SCCAI score of less than 5 [14, 15]. While clinical remission was defined as a pMayo score of < 2 or an SCCAI score of 2 points or less [16]. For patients with baseline pMayo or SCCAI scores already meeting the criteria for clinical remission, maintaining these values throughout the 52 weeks was considered both clinical response and remission. Corticosteroid‐free remission was defined as clinical remission without the use of corticosteroids for at least 4 weeks at Week 52 of the treatment. Disease extent and endoscopic scores at baseline were determined based on the Montreal classification and the Mayo endoscopic score, respectively [17]. The safety analysis included all patients receiving at least one dose of VDZ or UPA. While the effectiveness analysis was conducted in patients with a follow‐up period of at least 26 weeks. Patients who discontinued treatment due to primary or secondary non‐response or AEs were considered nonresponders. In case of missing data, or patients with a follow‐up period shorter than 26 weeks, their response rates were excluded from the effectiveness analysis.

Statistical Analysis

Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and non‐normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as median with interquartile range (IQR), respectively. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and by visual inspection of histograms. Differences in these variables were compared by using an independent samples t‐test or Mann–Whitney U‐test, when appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and proportions or frequencies, and were compared using the Chi‐square test and Fisher's exact test for groups consisting of less than five patients. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to compare the rates of clinical response, clinical remission, and AEs between the two groups. Because of the small sample size, an adjustment was limited to variables that significantly differed between the two groups according to disease duration and cardiovascular comorbidity. Fecal calprotectin (FCP) levels at induction and termination of treatment were compared using a paired t‐test. Treatment persistence was compared between cohorts using the Kaplan–Meier method, and the proportional hazards assumption was visually assessed using Cox regression plots. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analysis was conducted using the SPSS software version 23.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Study Population

Of the 151 patients treated with UPA or VDZ during the study period in our institution, 88 patients were included for analysis, including 66 patients treated with VDZ and 22 with UPA. The patient enrollment and grouping are outlined in Figure 1. The patients excluded from analysis included those who had not previously received anti‐TNF therapy (n = 63), including 53 patients in the VDZ group and 10 in the UPA group. Four patients each in both groups were excluded from the effectiveness analysis because of a follow‐up duration of less than 26 weeks. Treatment discontinuation was reported in 17 patients in the VDZ group and 2 patients in the UPA group, respectively, who were classified as nonresponders.

Baseline Characteristics of the Patients

Baseline characteristics of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the VDZ and UPA groups was 38 (IQR 31–64 years) and 37 years (IQR 29.75–49.25 years), respectively (p = 0.25). There were 31 (47.0%) and 9 (40.9%) males in the VDZ and UPA cohorts. Patients treated with VDZ had significantly higher rates of cardiovascular comorbidities (30.3% vs. 0%, p < 0.001). Most patients in both groups were never smokers, with a trend toward a higher proportion of never smokers in the VDZ group (86.9% vs. 63.6%, p = 0.03). In addition, patients in the VDZ group had a shorter disease duration (median: 6 years [IQR 2–14 years] vs. 11 years [IQR 5.75–16.25 years], p = 0.036). Disease extent was similar between the two groups, with the majority of patients having left‐sided or extensive disease (VDZ: 79.7%; UPA: 94.5%).

The most commonly used anti‐TNF agent was infliximab (83.3% in the VDZ cohort vs. 95.5% in the UPA cohort, p = 0.15). In all, 27.3% of the patients in both groups had been previously treated with more than one anti‐TNF agent. A higher proportion of patients in the VDZ group were exclusively treated with anti‐TNF agents compared to the UPA group (57 [86.4%] vs. 3 [13.6%], p < 0.001). The most common reason for discontinuation of the previous anti‐TNF agent was loss of response (VDZ vs. UPA: 42 [63.6%] vs. 15 [68.2%], p = 0.69). Moreover, 19 (86.4%) patients in the UPA cohort had prior exposure to advanced treatment alongside anti‐TNF agents, including 17 (77.3%) who received VDZ, 8 (36.4%) treated with ustekinumab, 7 (31.8%) with tofacitinib, and one (4.5%) each with ozanimod and guselkumab, respectively. Among the seven patients previously treated with tofacitinib, six were included in the effectiveness analysis, with 3 patients achieving both clinical response and remission. Notably, 13 patients in the UPA cohort had experienced failure of at least three advanced‐line therapies.

Both baseline pMayo (VDZ vs. UPA: 6 vs. 6, p = 0.85) and SCCAI (VDZ vs. UPA: 6.00 ± 2.95 vs. 6.06 ± 2.98, p = 0.95) scores were comparable between the VDZ and UPA groups. Three (4.5%) patients treated with VDZ were in clinical remission at baseline when compared to none in the UPA cohort. However, the baseline Mayo endoscopic score also did not differ between the two cohorts either (VDZ vs. UPA: 2 [IQR 2–3] vs. 3 [IQR 2–3], p = 0.45).

Additionally, baseline biochemical markers, such as C‐reactive protein (CRP) (VDZ vs. UPA: 8.0 mg/L [IQR 4.7–13.7 mg/L] vs. 8.0 mg/L [IQR 2.2–17.4 mg/L], p = 0.74) and FCP (855 μg/g [IQR 484–1160 μg/g] vs. 472.5 μg/g [IQR 183–2790 μg/g]), were comparable between the two cohorts.

Clinical and Biochemical Outcomes of the Patients

Significantly more patients treated with UPA achieved corticosteroid‐free remission than those treated with VDZ (75.0% vs. 25.0%), which was further confirmed by the univariate analysis (odds ratio [OR] 9.00, 95% confidence interval [CI] 2.52–32.00, p = 0.001) and the multivariate analysis (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 5.68, 95% CI 1.50–21.00, p = 0.011). Furthermore, a higher proportion of patients treated with UPA achieved clinical response and remission at Week 14 and Week 52, though these findings did not reach statistical significance (Table 2). At Week 52, the clinical response rate was 72.2% in the UPA group compared with 50.0% in the VDZ group (aOR 2.50, 95% CI 0.75–8.60, p = 0.135), while the remission rate was 44.4% in the UPA group and 27.4% in the VDZ group, respectively (aOR 1.72, 95% CI 0.53–5.50, p = 0.360). Treatment persistence over the 52‐week follow‐up did not differ significantly between groups (VDZ vs. UPA: 74% vs. 91%, p = 0.19 for log‐rank test) (Figure S1).

Safety

The rate of any AE was significantly higher in the UPA group than in the VDZ group (40.9% vs. 9.1%; aOR 4.68, 95% CI 1.20–17.00, p = 0.020). Dermatological lesion was the most common AE in the UPA group, which was reported by 3 (13.6%) patients, including acne in 2 patients and one with an unspecified rash. The rate of infection was comparable between the two cohorts (UPA vs. VDZ: 4.5% vs. 3.0%), among which bacterial infection was reported in only one patient treated with VDZ who presented with pneumonia. One patient treated with UPA presented with a mild cutaneous herpes infection that required a temporary pause for therapy. No patient discontinued treatment due to AE in the UPA cohort, while VDZ was discontinued in two patients due to pulmonary embolism in one patient and per patient's request in the other. No patients experienced serious AEs resulting in hospitalization. Safety profiles of both groups are summarized in Table 3.

Discussion

In this study, we compared the effectiveness and safety of VDZ and UPA in treating patients with UC who had received advanced therapies, including at least one anti‐TNF agent. To our knowledge, this is the first clinical study to directly compare these therapies in this particular group of patients.

In the current study, we demonstrated a clinical benefit for patients treated with UPA in terms of achieving corticosteroids‐free remission over a 52‐week duration. In addition, a trend toward greater biochemical improvement in FCP level and higher rates of clinical response and remission in patients treated with UPA was observed, though these results did not reach statistical significance.

Most baseline characteristics of the patients were comparable between the two groups. However, no patient in the UPA cohort had cardiovascular comorbidity, highlighting the concerns about cardiovascular risks associated with JAK inhibitors. This stems from higher risks of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality in patients receiving tofacitinib [18]. While UPA itself has not been associated with a higher incidence of serious cardiovascular AEs, these concerns likely influence prescribing practices, leading to the avoidance of UPA in patients with preexisting cardiovascular conditions [19].

We noticed that patients in the UPA group had a longer disease duration and a lower proportion of never smokers compared to the VDZ group. Moreover, those in the UPA cohort were previously treated with other advanced treatment modalities including VDZ, while patients in the VDZ cohort had only been treated with anti‐TNF agents. This disparity between the cohorts suggests that the UPA group might have included patients with more refractory disease, which may explain the reason that clinical response and remission rates did not reach significance between groups. It is likely that UPA is prescribed to patients with more prolonged and advanced disease who had already failed other treatment options, as it had been approved to treat UC later than VDZ, leading to potential selection bias. The UPA cohort included 3 patients who had only been treated with anti‐TNF agents, making it impossible to balance the cohorts in terms of previous advanced treatment with anti‐TNF agents exclusively.

Our results are consistent with previous studies. A prospective study conducted by Straatmijer et al. demonstrated superior effectiveness of tofacitinib over VDZ in anti‐TNF‐experienced patients, with no differences in the rates of severe AEs [10]. Friedberg et al. assessed the safety and efficacy of UPA in patients with previous anti‐TNF treatment and demonstrated substantial clinical remission rates exceeding 80% during an 8‐week follow‐up [20]. Several indirect comparisons and meta‐analyses have shown greater clinical efficacy of UPA compared to VDZ, for both induction and maintenance in biologic‐naive and biologic‐exposed patients with UC [5, 6, 8, 21]. However, there are conflicting results regarding its safety profile. For example, Lasa et al. performed a network meta‐analysis and found that VDZ ranked the best performing agent for safety profiles, while UPA led to most frequent AEs for patients with moderately to severely active UC [8].

Although the mechanisms of action for UPA and tofacitinib are similar, the selective inhibition of JAK‐1 in UPA is thought to confer a more favorable safety profile [18, 19]. This assumption warrants further investigation and comparison with other available regimens. In our cohort, patients treated with UPA were significantly more likely to experience AEs, indicating a less favorable safety profile compared to VDZ. The most commonly reported AE in the UPA group was dermatological lesions, particularly acne, which aligns with previous findings [20]. That being said, no serious AEs were reported in the study cohort in both treatment groups, and only two patients treated with VDZ discontinued treatment due to AEs. The small sample size in the UPA cohort might have led to an overestimation of AEs. Furthermore, as UPA is a more novel therapy for UC, which was introduced into the market in 2022 [20], the higher reporting of AEs may be attributed to greater awareness of its AEs.

The strength of our study is its position as the first study so far to directly compare VDZ and UPA in biologic‐experienced patients with UC. Given the study design and the small sample size, further large‐scale studies are needed to better address this clinical question.

The primary limitations of our study are its retrospective design and small sample size, particularly in the UPA group, as all patients were recruited from a single medical center, which has limited the generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, the retrospective design potentially introduces measurement bias due to the reliance on medical records, selection bias due to the possible inclusion of patients with more refractory disease in the UPA cohort, and reporting bias from unreported AEs. Additional limitations include the lack of endoscopic response data and missing laboratory data, and the broader definitions of clinical response and remission using both pMayo and SCCAI scoring systems, which were chosen based on the available data from the patients' medical records.

Conclusions

Clinical effectiveness of UPA and VDZ was comparable in anti‐TNF agent‐experienced patients with UC, with a higher corticosteroids‐free remission rate in those receiving UPA. However, patients treated with UPA experienced a significantly higher incidence of AEs compared to those receiving VDZ. These findings suggest that clinical decision‐making, taking into account disease severity and comorbidities, should guide the choice between these therapeutic options.
