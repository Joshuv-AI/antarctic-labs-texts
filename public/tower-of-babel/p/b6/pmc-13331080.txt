Effectiveness and design specifications of home-based lower extremity rehabilitation systems for patients with stroke: a systematic review and meta-analysis protocol
Authors: Jimin Han, Joon Ho Shin, Myung-Joon Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionStroke, one of the leading causes of death worldwide, leads to disability in most patients, posing a burden on society and families. Intensive rehabilitation in the early stages is the most effective method of minimising these disabilities. Home-based rehabilitation strategies have emerged as a promising alternative to conventional approaches, as highlighted during the coronavirus disease pandemic. Currently, no systematic reviews are available on the effects of home-based lower extremity rehabilitation systems in patients with stroke, or on the design specifications for their further development. Therefore, we present a protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis to provide up-to-date evidence on this topic.Methods and analysisThe study search for this review will be conducted in October 2026 across five electronic databases—MEDLINE, CENTRAL, EMBASE, Web of Science and CHINAL—and will be supplemented by Google searches and manual screening of the reference lists of the finally included studies. The search strategy has been developed using relevant keywords such as stroke, lower extremity rehabilitation, robotics and virtual reality, and study selection, data collection and risk of bias assessment will be performed independently by two authors; if these yield different results, final decisions will be made after discussion with the third author. The risk of bias in the existing literature will be evaluated using appropriate tools and methods in accordance with the Cochrane group guidelines (eg, RoB 2, Risk of Bias in Nonrandomised Studies of Interventions). Moreover, the level of evidence presented by this study will be evaluated using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation approach. If feasible, we will conduct a meta-analysis using the RevMan Web version; otherwise, a narrative review will be presented.Ethics and disseminationSince this study will be conducted without recruiting participants or using personal information, ethical approval and informed consent are not required (PROSPERO registration number: CRD 42024551398). Our proposed study will provide important evidence for policymakers, rehabilitation providers and system developers, and the results will be published in a journal and presented at conferences.Keywords: Meta-Analysis, Stroke, Systematic Review

Introduction

Stroke remains the leading cause of death or disability worldwide.1 2 It can result in motor and cognitive impairment, including muscle weakness, contractures, joint laxity, spasticity, discoordination and gait abnormalities.3,6 Only 10% of patients recover completely after stroke, and more than 70% experience incomplete recovery.7 Therefore, most patients with stroke experience difficulty in participating in social and economic activities as well as deterioration in functional independence. As of 2017, the direct cost of stroke was estimated at US$891 (US$746–1077) billion globally, and indirect costs were estimated to be US$1369 (US$1182–1614) billion.8

Rehabilitation encompasses a series of processes designed to restore impaired functions and help patients return to their daily lives, rendering it a crucial approach for patients with stroke.9 10 Functional recovery in patients with stroke is most effective in the early phase after stroke onset, with recovery rates gradually decreasing from 6 months after stroke onset (chronic phase).11,15 Several previous studies have confirmed a positive relationship between the intensity of rehabilitation and extent of recovery.16,19 Therefore, early and intensive rehabilitation is an essential treatment factor in patients with stroke.3 20 21 However, stroke patients are often unable to receive frequent or long-term intensive rehabilitation due to a lack of resources, such as adequately trained rehabilitation professionals, accessible rehabilitation facilities and governmental financial support for continuous treatment.922,24 As one of the complementary approaches to conventional hospital-based rehabilitation, home-based rehabilitation can provide high-intensity stroke rehabilitation.25 26 Home-based rehabilitation effectively reduces transportation costs for hospital visits and reduces or prevents the risk of infectious disease.2527,29 In addition, most patients with stroke and their caregivers rate home-based rehabilitation very positively because of its convenience, continuity and environmental friendliness,30,32 suggesting the need for evidence on policies and technologies that can support home-based rehabilitation and combine it with conventional approaches.

Recently, devices such as robots, games, augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR) have been reported as effective in stroke rehabilitation programmes aimed at improving function in patients with stroke.3 33 As these tools can be used in home environments, they were at the forefront of practice during the coronavirus disease pandemic.34 Existing lower extremity rehabilitation systems (eg, robotic devices, games, AR and VR) have significant limitations for home-based rehabilitation in patients with stroke—including issues related to cost and accessibility, patient adherence, safety and technological constraints—thereby necessitating further development and refinement. Previously, Chi et al30 conducted a meta-analysis of 49 randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and confirmed that home-based rehabilitation was effective in improving physical function in patients with stroke (Hedges’ g=0.58; 95% CI 0.45 to 0.70). However, approximately 50% of the included studies did not clearly describe the episode and stage of stroke, thereby limiting the generalisability of the findings due to the inability to adequately consider differences according to stroke onset and stage. Moreover, as only one study satisfied the criterion of blinding of both participants and personnel, the potential influence of performance bias was noted, possibly leading to an overestimation of the effect on physical function. Qin et al35 investigated the effects of home-based rehabilitation on the improvement of activities of daily living (ADL) among patients with stroke. Their analysis found no significant differences in short-term outcomes when comparing home-based and institution-based rehabilitation (standardised mean difference (SMD)=0.24; 95% CI −0.15 to 0.62; I²=0%) and no significant differences in long-term outcomes when comparing home-based rehabilitation with usual care (SMD=0.02; 95% CI −0.17 to 0.22; I²=0%). However, patients receiving combined home-based rehabilitation and usual care demonstrated a significantly greater short-term improvement in ADL compared with those receiving usual care alone (SMD=0.55; 95% CI 0.22 to 0.87; p=0.001; I²=3%). Qin et al35 also noted that insufficient data from individual studies restricted the performance of the meta-analysis, and that inter-study heterogeneity in stroke severity, onset time and intervention type decreased the overall quality of evidence. In addition, the potential for publication bias due to the exclusion of grey literature was reported. Bok et al36 analysed ten studies to determine the effectiveness of home-based rehabilitation using games, robotics, and VR. Their findings indicated that home-based rehabilitation had a small-to-moderate effect on physical function, with effect sizes as follows: VR-based home rehabilitation (Hedges’ g=0.850; 95% CI 0.314 to 1.385), robot-assisted home rehabilitation (Hedges’ g=0.120; 95% CI 0.003 to 0.017) and game-based home rehabilitation (Hedges’ g=−0.162; 95% CI −0.036 to −0.534). However, the study reported several methodological limitations, including non-adherence to certain reporting guidelines (eg, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) in Exercise, Rehabilitation, Sports medicine and Sports science (PERSiST)), inaccuracies in intervention classification due to insufficient information in primary studies, and potential omission of relevant research caused by limited database coverage. Hestetun-Mandrup et al37 examined the effects of digital home rehabilitation and remote supervision on physical function after stroke. The meta-analysis revealed no statistically significant differences between the intervention (digital home rehabilitation) and control groups (home rehabilitation or clinic-based rehabilitation) across all reported outcomes. The authors acknowledged several limitations, such as small sample sizes in most RCTs, considerable heterogeneity across interventions and the potential omission of relevant studies. Furthermore, their analysis only included outcomes related to static balance within lower extremity function, and this was based on merely three studies. Chi et al30 and Qin et al35 evaluated the effectiveness of home-based stroke rehabilitation delivered by healthcare professionals rather than using technological instruments such as robots, games, AR or VR. While all four studies demonstrated the potential benefits of home-based rehabilitation, none addressed the design specifications necessary for technological advancement in home-based systems.38,41 Moreover, they consistently reported limitations related to inter-study heterogeneity, incomplete data and publication bias. These methodological shortcomings across individual studies collectively underscore the need for a more comprehensive and robust meta-analysis.

In home-based rehabilitation systems, design specifications are key determinants of both effectiveness and usability, as they must reflect not only the technical implementation but also the underlying principles of neurorehabilitation.42 Maier et al42 analysed the effects of specific VR (SVR) systems, which were developed for upper extremity rehabilitation, and non-specific VR (NSVR) systems, which were designed for general commercial use, on upper extremity function in patients with stroke. Compared with usual care, the SVR systems demonstrated a significant improvement in upper extremity function (SMD=0.23; 95% CI=0.10 to 0.36; p=0.0007), whereas the NSVR systems showed no significant effect (SMD=0.16; 95% CI=−0.14 to 0.47; p=0.30). Maier et al42 explained that this difference in effect may be attributed to the systematic integration of neurorehabilitation principles into the design specifications of SVR systems, which maximise the effects of training and learning. Furthermore, Chen et al,43 through a systematic review analysing both technical and human factors in home-based stroke rehabilitation, emphasised that the design specifications of home-based lower extremity rehabilitation systems should account for the unique characteristics of the home environment, where patients are required to perform exercises independently. Essential elements for this include external and internal motivation, the patient’s social environment, practical challenges and technical proficiency.43 Therefore, in home-based lower extremity rehabilitation systems for patients with stroke, design specifications represent crucial factors in ensuring both effectiveness and usability by integrating not only technical parameters but also neurorehabilitation principles, the unique characteristics of the home environment, and the needs of patients and their families.42 43

Meta-analyses use aggregated data, which can help improve the statistical power of analyses compared with that of individual primary studies. Given the recent increase in the volume of studies on poststroke rehabilitation using robots, games, AR and VR,44,76 a systematic review is required to summarise the current evidence. Therefore, we present the protocol for a systematic review and meta-analysis on this topic. The purpose of this planned systematic review and meta-analysis is to identify the effectiveness of home-based lower extremity rehabilitation systems in patients with stroke and to explore design specifications for developing improved systems. The results of this study will provide the latest comprehensive evidence and will help policymakers, rehabilitation providers and system developers make important decisions.

Methods and analysis

Review question

What is the effectiveness of a home-based lower extremity rehabilitation system for patients with stroke, and what are the design specifications necessary for further development?

Registration

This protocol has been registered and approved in the PROSPERO database (CRD 42024551398). The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) were used as guidelines for writing this protocol.77 In addition, the PRISMA 2020 will be used as a guideline for reporting the results of the systematic review and meta-analysis.78

Eligibility criteria

Types of participants

The intended study participants are patients with stroke aged >18 years who live at home. As this study primarily aims to examine the effects of home-based lower extremity rehabilitation systems on the improvement of lower extremity function in patients with stroke, neither the duration of stroke nor the level of physical function of the participants will be restricted.

Types of interventions

The study interventions are home-based lower extremity rehabilitation systems for patients with stroke. These interventions include tailored rehabilitation games, lower extremity exercises using AR mirrors or smart glasses, robotic training for lower extremity strength and range of motion, and VR-based gait and balance simulations. The effects of the interventions will be compared through subgroup analyses.

Types of comparisons

The type of comparison intervention is not specified in this study. Comparative interventions may be any interventions that constitute the current standard of care.

Primary outcomes will refer to clinical domains based on the International Classification of Functioning, Disability and Health (ICF) framework.

Information sources

A database search will be conducted in MEDLINE, CENTRAL, EMBASE, Web of Science and CHINAL. In addition, the reference lists of the selected studies will be further analysed to identify any additional eligible studies. To complement the search process, a supplementary search using Google will also be performed.

Search strategy

The strategy for database search was established based on keywords related to stroke, home-based rehabilitation, lower extremity and systems (online supplemental file 1).

Data management

We will use the ENDNOTE 2079 tool for study selection. One author will perform database searches, downloads and deduplication.

Selection process

Two authors will independently conduct the study selection. In the first stage, the two authors will screen the titles and abstracts, followed by a full-text review in the second stage. If a consensus cannot be reached through discussion between the two authors, a third author will be consulted to determine whether to include or exclude the studies in question.

Data collection process

Two authors will independently extract data based on a predefined data extraction list. If the data collected by the two authors are different, we will draw a consensus conclusion through review and discussion, and if the opinions of the two authors are consistently inconsistent, we will discuss with another author to derive the final conclusion.

Risk of bias in individual studies

Risk-of-bias assessment will also be independently performed by two authors. If the evaluation results are different, the two authors will attempt to reach a consensus through discussion; if a consensus cannot be reached, another author will be consulted to reach a final conclusion. The risk of bias in RCTs will be assessed using V.2 of the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool for RCTs (RoB 2).80 The RoB 2 tool consists of five bias domains, considering both empirical evidence and theoretical consensus. Evaluation will be performed for each domain.80 In addition, the final evaluation result for each domain will be divided into three categories as follows: ‘low’, ‘some concerns’ and ‘high’.80

Outcomes and prioritisation

The primary outcomes will focus on clinical domains based on the ICF framework, comprising body function and structure, activity and participation. Body function and structure will include measures assessing muscle tone, motor control and joint mobility, such as the Modified Ashworth Scale (for spasticity) and the Fugl-Meyer Assessment (for motor recovery and coordination). Activity will encompass tools evaluating the ability to perform functional tasks and daily activities, including the Barthel Index (ADL), Berg Balance Scale (postural stability), Functional Independence Measure (self-care and mobility independence), Chedoke-McMaster Stroke Assessment Scale (motor and functional recovery), Frenchay Activities Index (domestic and social activity), Modified Rankin Handicap Scale (global disability), Rivermead Mobility Index and Rivermead Motor Assessment (gait and motor performance) and the Timed Up and Go test (mobility and balance). Participation outcomes will assess quality of life and social reintegration, using instruments such as the Nottingham Health Profile (health-related quality of life), EuroQol-5D (general health status) and Stroke Impact Scale (perceived participation and functional outcomes). The secondary outcomes will address technical and design-related factors, including material and structure specifications (system components, sensors and interfaces), safety specifications (protective mechanisms and usability validation), application and challenges (implementation barriers and adherence), rehabilitation scenarios (training settings and exercise modes) and adverse events (any physical or technical complications).

Data synthesis

We will perform a meta-analysis when sufficient between-study homogeneity regarding participants, interventions and comparisons is observed.82 For continuous outcomes: (1) If the same outcomes are measured using different tools, we will use SMDs with 95% CIs82; (2) if all studies were assessed using the same tool for the same outcome, we will use mean differences with 95% CIs. In addition, for dichotomous outcomes, we will use ORs and 95% CIs.82 The meta-analysis will be performed using the RevMan Web version.83 The random- or fixed-effect model will be implemented based on the degree of heterogeneity among studies.84 Homogeneity will be interpreted according to the guidelines presented in the Cochrane Handbook,85 whereby an I² value of 0%–40% may indicate unimportant heterogeneity, 30%–60% may represent moderate heterogeneity, 50%–90% may indicate substantial heterogeneity and 75%–100% may represent considerable heterogeneity. A narrative review will be presented if data sources preclude a meta-analysis.86

Confidence in cumulative evidence

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation approach will be used as a standard to evaluate the level of evidence,84 85 which is the most commonly used tool for assessing summary evidence. Two authors will discuss the findings to help draw more reliable conclusions.85

Discussion

This study protocol outlines a systematic review and meta-analysis aimed at comprehensively analysing the clinical effectiveness of home-based lower extremity rehabilitation systems for stroke survivors, alongside their design specifications for future system development. To ensure methodological rigour and replicability, the review will be conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook and the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. This systematic approach will enhance the transparency and consistency of the study search, study selection, data extraction and evidence synthesis processes.

However, this study has several potential limitations. First, considerable heterogeneity may arise due to variations in the clinical characteristics of participants across the included primary studies, such as stroke severity, time since onset, intervention type and intervention duration. Furthermore, a meta-analysis may not be feasible if clinical outcome data are insufficiently reported; in such cases, a narrative synthesis will be conducted[86].

To mitigate these limitations, the authors plan to obtain supplementary data by directly contacting the original investigators for studies with missing information. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses will also be performed to systematically explore inter-study heterogeneity and evaluate the robustness of the findings[80,82].
