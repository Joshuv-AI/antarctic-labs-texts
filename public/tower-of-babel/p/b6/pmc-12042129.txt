Enhancing neurogenesis after traumatic brain injury: The role of adenosine kinase inhibition in promoting neuronal survival and differentiation
Authors: Andrea Campbell, Tho Lai, Amir E Wahba, Detlev Boison, Hoda M Gebril
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraumatic brain injury (TBI) presents a significant public health challenge, necessitating innovative interventions for effective treatment. Recent studies have challenged conventional perspectives on neurogenesis, unveiling endogenous repair mechanisms within the adult brain following injury. However, the intricate mechanisms governing post-TBI neurogenesis remain unclear. The microenvironment of an injured brain, characterized by astrogliosis, neuroinflammation, and excessive cell death, significantly influences the fate of newly generated neurons. Adenosine kinase (ADK), the key metabolic regulator of adenosine, emerges as a crucial factor in brain development and cell proliferation after TBI. This study investigates the hypothesis that targeting ADK could enhance brain repair, promote neuronal survival, and facilitate differentiation. In a TBI model induced by controlled cortical impact, C57BL/6 male mice received intraperitoneal injections of the small molecule ADK inhibitor 5-iodotubercidin (ITU) for three days following TBI. To trace the fate of TBI-associated proliferative cells, animals received intraperitoneal injections of BrdU for seven days, beginning immediately after TBI. Our results show that ADK inhibition by ITU improved brain repair 14 days after injury as evidenced by a diminished injury size. Additionally, the number of mature neurons generated after TBI was increased in ITU-treated mice. Remarkably, the TBI-associated pathological events including astrogliosis, neuroinflammation, and cell death were arrested in ITU-treated mice. Finally, ADK inhibition modulated cell death by regulating the PERK signaling pathway. Together, these findings demonstrate a novel therapeutic approach to target multiple pathological mechanisms involved in TBI. This research contributes valuable insights into the intricate molecular mechanisms underlying neurogenesis and gliosis after TBT.Keywords: Traumatic brain injury, hippocampus, Neurogenesis, Regeneration, Astroglia, Microglia, Adenosine kinase

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a pervasive public health concern, posing significant societal burdens due to its high incidence and potential for long-term disability. Despite its prevalence, effective therapeutic treatments for TBI remain elusive, emphasizing the urgent need for innovative approaches to mitigate the devastating consequences of brain trauma. Recent studies challenge the old view that neurogenesis occurs only during development and have shed light on the potential for endogenous repair mechanisms within the adult brain, particularly in the context of neurogenesis following injury (Cipriani et al., 2018; Dennis et al., 2016; H. Gebril et al., 2020; Moreno-Jimenez et al., 2019; Terstege et al., 2022). However, the intricate mechanisms governing post-injury neurogenesis are not fully understood, underscoring the importance of further exploration to unlock novel therapeutic interventions.

In mammals, adult neurogenesis occurs within the dynamic context of the central nervous system (CNS) to create functioning neurons from neural precursors. In adulthood, neurogenesis is maintained only in the subgranular zone of the hippocampus and subventricular zone (Toda et al., 2019). As a part of the brain’s inherent response to injury, TBI triggers endogenous neurogenesis. However, the survival and functionality of these newly generated neurons appear to be intricately linked to the brain’s microenvironment post-injury (H. Wu et al., 2018; Zheng et al., 2013). The beneficial role of TBI-induced neurogenesis remains a subject of debate. Studies have reported that inhibiting neurogenesis following CCI in mice (Blaiss et al., 2011) or lateral fluid percussion injury (LFPI) in rats (Sun et al., 2015) can impede cognitive recovery. Additionally, increased neurogenesis was associated with improved recovery in mice treated with growth differentiation factor 5 after CCI (H. Wu et al., 2018). Moreover, the injury-induced new neurons were found to integrate into the hippocampal circuitry (Emery et al., 2005; Sun et al., 2007) and were associated with cognitive recovery after brain injury (Sun et al., 2007). On the other side, reports have highlighted pathological features associated with TBI-induced neurogenesis. Notably, abnormal dendritic branching of newborn cells in mice following TBI has been documented (Villasana et al., 2015). In line with this notion, in mice after LFPI, new neurons with aberrant hilar-projecting basal dendrites which ectopically localized to the dentate gyrus (DG) hilus were reported (Robinson et al., 2016). Other studies reported the abnormal migration of the TBI-associated new neurons (Ibrahim et al., 2016; Ngwenya et al., 2018). The brain’s microenvironment modulates the growth and functionality of TBI-associated neurons. Targeting multiple aspects of TBI pathological cascades simultaneously may be a therapeutic approach to promote neurogenesis and modulate the injury microenvironment.

Adenosine, a purine nucleoside, is a crucial regulator in physiological and pathophysiological processes that exerts a multifaceted influence over the development and survival of neural stem cells (NSCs) in the adult brain. Adenosine influences a myriad of physiological processes, including learning and memory (Chen, 2014), development and patterning (H. Gebril et al., 2023), cell proliferation (H. Gebril et al., 2021; Jacobson et al., 2019), synapse fine-tuning, neuronal circuitry coordination, neuronal plasticity (Sebastiao and Ribeiro, 2015), astrocytic activity (Agostinho et al., 2020), and aging (Costenla et al., 2011). On the other hand, adenosine also plays a role in pathological disorders such as epilepsy (Boison and Jarvis, 2021; Tescarollo et al., 2020), amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (Ng et al., 2015; Sebastiao et al., 2018), ischemia, stroke (Melani et al., 2014; Pereira-Figueiredo et al., 2022; Pignataro et al., 2007; Williams-Karnesky and Stenzel-Poore, 2009), and TBI (M. J. Bell et al., 1998). In this dynamic landscape of the CNS, adult neurogenesis is driven by an intricate interplay involving the precursor cells, glial cells, and cellular intrinsic mechanisms including adenosine, inflammatory response, and cell death (Garcia-Gil et al., 2021; von Lubitz et al., 1995; N. Wu et al., 2023; Yuan and Wu, 2022). Glial cells, specially astrocytes, contribute significantly to the brain’s response following injury to limit the spread of damage throughout the brain. However, excessive, or prolonged activation of astrocytes, known as astrogliosis, leads to the formation of glial scars, impeding neuronal proliferation and branching (Bylicky et al., 2018; Jin et al., 2016; N. Wu et al., 2023). Hence, manipulating one or more elements of the injury microenvironment emerges as a therapeutic approach to promote TBI-induced neurogenesis.

Among the molecular players contributing to this intricate landscape is adenosine kinase (ADK), a key enzyme regulating intra- and extracellular adenosine levels in the brain. ADK exerts control over different cellular processes, such as regulating extracellular adenosine levels, receptor activation, and DNA methylation. Recent studies implicate ADK in the regulation of genes associated with cell proliferation, particularly after TBI, highlighting its potential significance in neuronal survival (Annes et al., 2012; H. Gebril et al., 2023; H. Gebril et al., 2020; Kiese et al., 2016).

This study investigates the role of ADK in the survival and differentiation of neurons following TBI, building upon previous research indicating that ADK inhibition promotes TBI-associated endogenous neurogenesis and cell proliferation (H. Gebril et al., 2020). Our study delves into the fate of newly generated neurons following TBI and ADK inhibition. Utilizing a mouse model of moderate-to-severe TBI and employing a pharmacological ADK inhibitor, we explore the impact of ADK on neurogenesis, astrogliosis, and gliosis-related secondary damage. This research expands our understanding of the intricate molecular mechanisms underlying neurogenesis and gliosis after TBI, with ADK inhibition emerging as a promising therapeutic strategy. The findings presented herein contribute to the growing body of knowledge aimed at harnessing endogenous neurogenesis for brain repair and regeneration in the aftermath of traumatic brain injuries.

Methods

Animals

In vivo studies were conducted at an AAALAC-accredited facility in compliance with approved Rutgers Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) protocols and following ARRIVE guidelines. Male mice aged 2 months (N = 20), C57BL/6 purchased from the Jacksons Laboratory and socially housed under standardized conditions, including regulated light, temperature, humidity, environmental enrichment, and ad libitum access to food and water. At 14 days post-TBI, all animals were anesthetized using 3% isoflurane and then euthanatized by either decapitation (for Western blot analysis) or through transcranial profusion using 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (for histology).

Controlled cortical impact

Mice were subjected to moderate-to-severe controlled cortical impact (CCI) open-skull injury following established procedures (H. Gebril et al., 2020). Briefly, mice were anesthetized with 3% isoflurane, fixed in a stereotaxic frame, and a 3 mm craniotomy was performed over the right motor cortex. CCI was induced using a 2 mm stainless steel piston at 4 m/s velocity, 1.2 mm, and 0.9 mm depth, with a 300 ms impact duration. Sham control mice underwent a midline skin incision without craniotomy.

ADK inhibitor and BrdU administration

After TBI, animals were randomly assigned into two groups (N = 9–10). Each group was intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected with either 5-iodotubericine (5-ITU, Sigma-Aldrich, cat# I100), at 1.6 mg/kg in 20% DMSO or 20% DMSO only. Animals were injected once every 12 h for three days (five total injections) starting immediately after TBI. All groups received 50 mg/kg BrdU for seven consecutive days after TBI.

Western blots

Brain (N = 4) samples were homogenized, and protein extracts were subjected to electrophoresis. The entire brain was extracted right away, hippocampi were isolated from the ipsilateral side of the injury, frozen in liquid nitrogen vapor, and stored at −80 ◦C. Protease inhibitor-containing radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA) was used to homogenize brain samples (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, cat# 20–188). The protein content was determined using a Thermo Fisher BCA Protein assay kit (cat# 23225). Using 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE, Thermo Fisher, cat# NP0301) gels, 20 μg of aqueous protein extracts were loaded, separated, and then transferred to PVDF (polyvinylidene fluoride) membranes (Bio-Rad, cat# 1620177). The blots were probed overnight at 4 ◦C in primary antibodies mixture containing, tris buffered saline (TBS) (20 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% Tween, pH 7.5) with 3% non-fat dry milk, mouse monoclonal mouse anti-phospho-AKT (SER473) antibody (1:1000, Sigma Aldrich, cat# SAB5600064), and Phospho-ERK1/ERK2 Polyclonal Antibody (1:1000, Invitrogen, cat# 44–680G). After the membranes were cleaned in TBS, they were incubated in TBS that contained 1% BSA, and 5% non-fat dry milk. A Bio-Rad Touch imager was used to scan the immunoreactivity, and Bio-Rad Image Lab software was used to digitize the results. The expression of pERK, PaKT was calculated as the ratio of the optical densities of the GAPDH bands through quantitative analysis of the western blot bands using Image J V. 1.52 software.

Immunohistochemistry

Mice (N = 4–5) were anesthetized and transcardially perfused using 30 ml of 1× cold phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.4) and 30 ml of ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA). Brains were post-fixed in 4% PFA for 24 h. Brains were stored in a solution containing 30% sucrose and 0.1% sodium azide in 1× PBS for two days at 4 ◦C, then kept at −80 ◦C. The brains were then sectioned into 30 μm sections coronally using a Leica CM3050S sliding stage cryostat. In cryoprotectant, all sections were kept until further processing. Immunohistochemistry of the mouse brain was performed on free-floating sections. Sections were washed in 1× PBS to remove cryoprotectant buffer and then permeabilized in 1× TBS (Tris-Buffered Saline) and 0.05% Triton X-100 (TBS-T) for 15 min. Sections were blocked in blocking buffer for 1 h then incubated overnight at 4 ◦C in Donkey blocking buffer containing primary antibodies, BrdU (1:250, abcam, cat# ab6326), monoclonal mouse anti-glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) (1:50, Invitrogen, cat# SC166458), NeuN (1:50, Invitrogen, cat# PA5–37407), IBA1 (1:50, Invitrogen, cat# PA5–18039). For the BrdU assay, DNA hydrolysis was conducted prior to primary antibody treatment. Briefly, sections were incubated in 1 M HCL for 15 min then neutralized in 0.1 M Sodium borate buffer for 15 min then washed three times in TBS-T buffer. Brain sections were incubated in a solution containing the matching secondary antibodies for an hour at room temperature. The secondary antibodies used were Donkey Alexa Fluor 488, 555, and 633 (1:500, Invitrogen, cat# ab150129, A31570, and A21082, respectively). After three five-minute washes in 1× PBS, sections were mounted on slides and left to dry in the dark. Sections were cover-slipped with DAPI mounting medium after they had dried and kept at 4 ◦C in the dark.

Nissl stain

Brain sections were affixed to slides and left to air-dry overnight. Subsequently, the sections underwent rinsing in xylene followed by hydration through a series of alcohol dilutions (97%, 95%, 90%, 85%, 75%). Following a 7-min incubation in a 0.1% cresyl violet solution (Abcam, cat# ab246817), the sections were differentiated in alcohol, cleared in xylene, and finally mounted using mounting medium (Fisher Scientific, cat# SP15–100 UN1294).

Cavity volume

The volume of the injury cavity was estimated using unbiased stereology using Stereo Investigator software (version 11.07, MBF Bioscience, Williston, VT USA). All measurements were performed using a Cavalieri volume estimator function (gride size 200 μm × 200 μm, CE < 0.01). Four to five Nissl-stained brain sections were analyzed per animal.

TUNEL assay

To measure cell death presented as double-stranded cleavage of DNA, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase biotin-dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assays was performed. Briefly, PFA fixed 30 μm brain sections were washed using 1× PBS then permeabilized using 1× TBS-T buffer for 15 mins. The sections were then incubated in the TUNEL re-action mixture (Sigma, cat# 11684809910) in a humidified chamber at 37 ◦C for 1 h. Brain sections were washed in 1× PBS then mounted on slides and left to dry in the dark. Sections were cover-slipped with DAPI mounting medium after they had dried and kept at 4 ◦C in the dark.

Image acquisition and cell counting

Fluorescence images were acquired using a Leica microscope fitted with a Stereo Investigator system (Micro brightfield) comprising colour and monochrome digital cameras. For image analysis, unbiased exclusion of poor-quality images was made before analysis. Cell counting was performed by an individual blinded to the age of the animals using ImageJ software (ImageJ, National Institutes of Health; http://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). Cell counts were quantified as described before (H. Gebril et al., 2020), in at least two sections per animal as cell number per μm2. For image analysis, we selected two 30 μm sections for each stain from each mouse, spaced every 90 μm and spanning an identical caudorostral location (spanning from 1.31 mm to 2.27 mm posterior to bregma). Images were acquired from the hippocampus on the ipsilateral side of each brain section. The borders of DG including the subgranular zone, granular cell layer, and molecular layer of the DG and hilus were highlighted as regions of interest (ROI). The area of ROI was determined using Image J area measuring tool. Binarization of NeuN, GFAP, and TUNEL-stained images was performed using the ImageJ software Auto Threshold command. Using thresholding technique, the foreground pixels were determined over the background pixels at a fixed threshold value. The optimal threshold values for each stain were achieved by manually adjusting the range of pixel intensity on a set of images (Crews et al., 2006). The remaining set of images were then subjected to the established pixel intensity range, which was maintained across all sections. The ROI’s total cell count was automatically calculated using the “particle analysis” command. A range of particle characteristics for automatic quantification was identified from binary image data for each stain to prevent unwanted noise and/or background pixels. For GFAP cell counting, the threshold was determined by adjusting the pixel range detect activated astrocytes in the ROI, then using particle count, the number of these activated astrocytes were counted. The double labeled cells of NeuN/BrdU and GFAP/BrdU were counted at different focal planes in Z stacked images using manual cell counter tool in n = 3–4 group.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism, version 10.1.2. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Two-tailed unpaired Student t-tests were used for experiments with two groups and one variable. * = p ≤ 0.05, ** = p ≤ 0.01, *** = p ≤ 0.001, **** = p ≤ 0.0001 for significance.

Results

Adenosine kinase inhibition promotes neuronal survival and differentiation after TBI

TBI triggers endogenous neurogenesis as a part of the brain’s inherent response to injury and repair attempts. Nevertheless, the proliferation and maturational fate of these new neurons is modulated by several physical and chemical cues (X. Wang et al., 2016; Y. Wang et al., 2011; H. Wu et al., 2018). Harnessing this endogenous neurogenesis and understating the molecular mechanism of TBI, is an attractive strategy to repair and regenerate the injured brain.

Our previous work demonstrated that ADK genetic and pharmacological inhibition acutely promote TBI-associated endogenous neurogenesis and cell proliferation (H. Gebril et al., 2020). However, the fate of those newly generated neurons remains unclear. In this study, we investigated the role of ADK in neuronal survival and differentiation at 14 days after TBI. C57BL/6 mice were subjected to moderate-to-severe TBI using CCI (Fig. 1A). Animals were randomly assigned to two groups, each group received 5 total i.p. injections of either 1.6 mg/kg ITU or vehicle every 12 h for three consecutive days after TBI. This dose has previously been shown to induce TBI-associated cell proliferation (H. Gebril et al., 2021) and to induce potent disease-modifying effects in an epilepsy model in mice (Sandau et al., 2016). Because TBI-associated endogenous neurogenesis was found to continue for around a week after experimental injury (Shapiro, 2017), we aimed to study neurogenesis after this time window. To track the fate of TBI-associated proliferative cells, animals were injected 50 mg/kg BrdU via i.p. once a day for 7 consecutive days starting immediately after TBI (Fig. 1A). Animals were sacrificed at 14 days post-TBI (DPI) and their brains were investigated. Using Nissl stain and stereological approaches, we first examined the injury cavity volume in experimental (ITU-treated) and control (vehicle-treated) groups. In ITU-treated mice, the injury cavity volume was significantly reduced (p < 0.0001) compared to the control mice Fig. 1B–D). TBI induces neurogenesis and cell proliferation in neurogenic areas in the brain including the subventricular zone (SVZ) and the hippocampus. Inhibiting adenosine kinase (ADK), whether through genetic modification or pharmacological treatment, has been reported to trigger neurogenesis and cell proliferation three days after TBI in the hippocampus (H. Gebril et al., 2020). Therefore, we hypothesized that this effect could promote neuronal survival and differentiation. Brain sections from each group were co-labeled with the mature neuronal marker (NeuN) and BrdU. Since BrdU was injected immediately after TBI, all BrdU positive cells are born after TBI (Fig. 2A). Strikingly, the number of NeuN and BrdU positive neurons were significantly higher (p = 0.008) in the hippocampus of ITU-treated mice compared to the control mice (Fig. 2B–C & S2A). However, there wasn’t a significant change (p = 0.06) in the number of total BrdU-positive cells in ITU- or vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 2D). Our results suggest that pharmacological nhibition of ADK through ITU can enhance tissue repair, neuronal survival, and differentiation after TBI.

TBI-induced gliosis as a target to promote brain repair

Glial activation (gliosis) after brain injury is one of the leading causes that facilitates neuronal damage, neuroinflammation, and contributes to cognitive decline (Broussard et al., 2018; Yang and Zhou, 2019). Additionally, gliosis has been reported to play detrimental roles in TBI-induced neurogenesis due to the secretion of neurotoxic proinflammatory cytokines (Boccazzi et al., 2014; Mukherjee et al., 2014).

Our results above, indicating that the brain repair and recovery in ITU-treated mice was promoted in ITU-treated mice, suggested that the brain micro-environment was improved to promote repair and arrest tissue damage after TBI in those mice. Therefore, we investigated the status of glial cells activation after TBI in ITU- and vehicle-treated mice. Brain sections from each group were co-labeled with the glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and BrdU (Fig. 3A–C&S2B–C). Since BrdU was injected immediately after TBI, all BrdU-positive cells are born after TBI. The number of GFAP-positive cells was significantly reduced (p = 0.007) in ITU-treated mice compared to control mice (Fig. 3B). Similarly, the number of cells double-labeled with GFAP and BrdU was significantly reduced (p < 0.0001) in ITU-treated mice compared to control mice (Fig. 3C). These results suggested that ITU treatment after TBI can target astrogliosis contributing to secondary brain damage. Importantly, these results support our findings in Fig. 2D that there is no significant change in the total number of BrdU-positive cells between ITU and vehicle-treated mice. Interestingly, further analysis of BrdU-positive cells in areas of the DG, including the subgranular layer (SGL), granular layer (GL), and hilus (Fig. 3D & E), showed a significant increase in the number of BrdU-positive cells in the hilus compared to the SGL and GL in vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 3D). Significant changes were also observed in BrdU-positive cells in the hilus compared to the SGL and GL in ITU-treated animals (Fig. 3E). Therefore, the interpretation outlined above, suggesting the presence of non-neuronal BrdU-positive cells, presumably glial cells, in vehicle-treated mice, remains valid. Given the inhibitory effect of ITU on astrogliosis, we expected similar effects on microglial activity after TBI. Therefore, we investigated the immunoreactivity of the microglial marker ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (IBA1) after TBI in ITU- and vehicle-treated mice. Brain sections from each group were stained with IBA1 (Fig. 4A–B). The number of IBA1-positive cells was significantly reduced (p = 0.004) in ITU-treated mice compared to control mice (Fig. 3B&S2D). Collectively, these results suggested that modulating adenosine metabolism through ADK inhibition is a therapeutic approach to manipulate active gliosis and promote tissue repair after TBI.

Adenosine kinase attenuates cell death and activates PERK Pathway

To date, the underlying mechanisms behind TBI remains unclear. One of the main pathological events occurred after brain injury is the cell death. Since we found remarkable recovery and tissue repair in ITU-treated animals, we investigated the cell death in those animals using terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP (TUNEL) assay. Brain sections from ITU-and vehicle-treated mice were stained with TUNEL (Fig. 5A), the fluorescence of TUNEL in the hippocampus of each group was evaluated. As expected, TUNEL stain was significantly (p = 0.004) reduced in ITU-treated mice, especially in the granular layer of the hippocampus, compared to vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 5B&S2E).

Next, we investigated the mechanism through which ITU may work to arrest the TBI-associated cell death. It is well established that TBI-induced cell death is correlated with the activation of PAKT and PKR-like ER-associated kinase (PERK) protein (Noshita et al., 2002; Szegezdi et al., 2006). Therefore, we investigated the activation status of PAKT and PERK proteins after TBI in ITU- and vehicle-treated mice. Fresh hippocampus from each group were extracted, and digested, and using Western blot, the protein bands of PAKT and PERK were blotted (Fig. 5C & S1). Interestingly, PAKT didn’t show any change in ITU- or vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 5D&&S2F). However, PERK was found to be significantly low in ITU-treated mice compared to control mice (Fig. 5E&S2G). These data suggested that ADK regulates TBI-associated cell death through the inhibition of the PERK signaling pathway.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the role of ADK in TBI-induced neurogenesis. Using open-skull CCI, TBI was modeled in mice and the fate of TBI-associated proliferative cells was tracked. Our results show that ADK inhibition through ITU can facilitate tissue repair, neuronal survival, and differentiation after TBI. Furthermore, ADK inhibition arrests TBI-associated gliosis and cell death. Finally, we show that ADK inhibition is associated with the inhibition of PERK activation.

Targeting adenosine metabolism is a therapeutic approach to promote tissue repair and neuronal survival

Our results demonstrated that ADK inhibition via ITU is sufficient to promote brain repair and neuronal survival after TBI in mice (Fig. 1–2). These results are consistent with the findings that ADK genetic and pharmacological inhibition enhanced cell proliferation and plasticity (H. Gebril et al., 2020). ADK has been shown to play a critical role in the brain development, cell proliferation, patterning, and neuronal differentiation (H. Gebril et al., 2023; H. Gebril et al., 2021). Following TBI in rats, a temporal upregulation (3–7 days) of ADK expression peaked at day 5 after TBI has been reported (Jin et al., 2016). Notably, it was also shown that this ADK upregulation is synchronously associated with neuronal death (Jin et al., 2016). This increase in ADK may presumably occurs because of the acute surge in adenosine few minutes after TBI. Following CCI in rats, acute surge in adenosine (61-fold), was observed, peaking at 20 min post-injury (M. J. Bell et al., 1998). This is likely a consequence of the rapid rise in ATP following an injury. (Faroqi et al., 2021; Moro et al., 2021). In agreement with this notion, acute ADK downregulation, which may prolong adenosine levels, has been associated with TBI and stroke (Pignataro et al., 2008). In line with these results, the administration of an adenosine agonist before TBI was observed to mitigate brain damage in a dose-dependent manner (Headrick et al., 1994). Therapies that promote neuronal growth, enhance survival, and alleviate cell damage have been linked to TBI-induced neurogenesis. For example, growth factors, including S100B and VEGF, have been shown to enhance neurogenesis after traumatic brain injury (TBI) in mice (Kleindienst et al., 2005; Thau-Zuchman et al., 2010). Additionally, various drugs such as statins, erythropoietin, progesterone, and imipramine have demonstrated positive impacts on neurogenesis and cognitive function post-TB (Barha et al., 2011; Han et al., 2011; Lu et al., 2005; Lu et al., 2007). Other therapeutic strategies such as hypothermia and environment enrichment were also reported to enhance TBI-induced neurogenesis (Bregy et al., 2012; Kovesdi et al., 2011). However, targeting only one mechanism of TBI pathology such as oxidative stress, cell death, or excitotoxicity may not be an efficient approach to promote neurogenesis and recovery after TBI. Indeed, most of these drugs failed in clinical trials (Galgano et al., 2017; Stein, 2015). Therefore, there is an urgent need for a drug that might impart multiple beneficial effects on the injured brain either simultaneously or sequentially. ADK is an endogenous mediator that falls into this category, with the potential to positively impact cell proliferation, brain development, apoptosis, gliosis, energy failure, seizures, acute inflammation, oxidative stress, and angiogenesis.

Targeting astrogliosis and neuroinflammation through ADK inhibition

The response of the central nervous system (CNS) to injury and disease involves a complex interplay among various neural and non-neural cell types. Over time, these cells collaborate to preserve homeostasis, protect viable cells, remove debris, and sustain overall function. (Burda and Sofroniew, 2014). Glial cells including astrocytes and microglia play a crucial role as responders to various CNS insults including TBI. After a brain insult, glial cells undergo activation, a phenomenon referred to as gliosis. This process leads to the formation of scars typically associated with different levels of cell death, neuro-degeneration, and inflammation. It is widely acknowledged that gliosis is a prevalent factor in the occurrence of the secondary brain injury and the subsequent neurological impairment (Cieri et al., 2023; Maas et al., 2008).

In this study, we demonstrated that acute inhibition of ADK after TBI has a significant impact on modulating TBI-induced glial activation (Fig. 3–4). We observed a significant reduction in the number of TBI-induced astrocytes and microglia cells in mice treated with ITU 14 days after TBI. Consistent with our results, heightened expression of ADK was reported to be spatially associated with active gliosis in rats within 1 to 14 days following TBI (Jin et al., 2016). Indeed, reactive glial cells demonstrate increased ADK expression in response to TBI in these animals. In the developing and adult brain, ADK is expressed in astrocytes (H. Gebril et al., 2023; H. Gebril et al., 2020; H. Gebril et al., 2021; Gouder et al., 2004), thus astrogliosis associated with brain insults would likely involve ADK overexpression and ambient adenosine reduction. In agreement with this notion, ADK overexpression and astrogliosis are concurrent pathological features in other brain pathologies, such as epilepsy and stroke, focal cortical dysplasia (Aronica et al., 2011; Fedele et al., 2005; Guo et al., 2023; Pignataro et al., 2007). Moreover, adenosine metabolites have been linked to the TBI-induced microglial activation (Davalos et al., 2005; Du et al., 2023; Nedeljkovic et al., 2008). The activation of glial cells results in the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, peaking 6 to 72 h after the injury. (Taib et al., 2017). Hence, attenuating the release of cytokines induced by TBI is crucial for mitigating neuronal death associated with TBI-induced neuroinflammation. Notably, the administration of the anti-inflammatory IL-10 in TBI animals has been demonstrated to enhance neurological outcomes (Knoblach and Faden, 1998). The augmentation of adenosine has been associated with the alleviation of TBI-induced neuroinflammation, improved neurological outcomes, reduced infarct size, and diminished gliosis (Bai et al., 2019; Ning et al., 2013). In LPS-stimulated primary astrocytes, ADK overexpression was observed (Jin et al., 2016). Interestingly, ADK knockdown was shown to inhibit the release of pro-inflammatory cytokine and neuronal death (Jin et al., 2016). Additionally, the level of extracellular ATP and the activity of adenosine receptors has been tightly linked to the rapid microglial response to astrogliosis and cellular cross talk after injury (Davalos et al., 2005b). Indeed, genetic manipulation of adenosine receptors has been shown to promote the microglial-induced transformation of astrocytes to a neuroprotective phenotype (Shinozaki et al., 2017). Our results support the hypothesis that adenosine augmentation through ADK inhibition is an effective approach to target TBI-associated glial activation and thus inhibit neuronal death and improve recovery after brain injury.

Targeting ADK arrest TBI-induce cell death through PERK pathway

The dramatic cell death that contributes to TBI-induced neurological dysfunction is triggered by a combination of various mechanisms, including glial activation, neuroinflammation, and the activation of intrinsic cell death signaling pathways (Morganti-Kossmann et al., 2001; Raghupathi, 2004). Our results showed that cell death, as indicated by TUNEL signals, was significantly reduced in TBI ITU-treated mice compared to control mice (Fig. 5A). It is well-established that cell death occurs as early as 10 min after TBI and proceeds spatially and temporally to impact distal parts in the brain (Hicks et al., 1996; Sato et al., 2001). In addition to the disruption of brain tissue homeostasis, activation of the intrinsic death signaling pathways is one of the leading causes of TBI-induced cell death (Gao and Chen, 2011; Shi et al., 2023). Endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress is one of the primary mechanisms that manage cellular stress which can either reestablish cellular homeostasis or activate cell death (Niu et al., 2017). ER works through three different signaling pathways including protein kinase R (PKR)-like endoplasmic reticulum kinase (PERK), activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6), and inositol requiring kinase 1 (IRE1) (Schröder and Kaufman, 2005). Our results revealed that ADK inhibition through small molecule treatment using ITU after TBI was sufficient to modulate PERK pathway without an effect on PAKT pathway (Fig. 5B–E). Following TBI, PERK was shown to be increased at 2 h after injury (Brady et al., 2021; Meng et al., 2018). In agreement with our results, treatment with PERK selective inhibitor GSK2606414 was found to inhibit neuronal death after TBI in vivo and in vitro (Meng et al., 2018). In contrary, treatment with GSK2606414 was not enough to alleviate neurological outcomes 4 weeks after FPI (Brady et al., 2021). These incongruent findings were attributed to methodological differences such as drug preparation, delivery, or injection time point (Brady et al., 2021). Consistent with our results, inhibiting the PERK pathway has been demonstrated to restore dendritic spines and mitigate memory deficits in the CCI animal model (Sen et al., 2017). Furthermore, PERK inhibition rescued the number of synapses and loss of dendritic outgrowth (Sen et al., 2017). The activation of the PERK pathway is implicated in several neurodegenerative disorders that exhibit pathological similarities to TBI, including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Alzheimer’s disease, and frontotemporal dementia (M. C. Bell et al., 2016). The activation of PERK pathway has been established as a major signaling cascade for the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines in glial cells (Ahmad et al., 2013; Ajizian et al., 1999). Therefore, it is plausible that ADK functions as an upstream regulator of both the PERK and pro-inflammatory signaling cascades. Notably, the modulation of ADK using ABT 702 was observed to mitigate LPS-activated microglia through PERK mediated pathway (Ahmad et al., 2014). Furthermore, manipulating adenosine signaling via A2AAR agonist demonstrated attenuation of the inflammatory response in activated microglia through the PERK pathway (Ahmad et al., 2013). Our findings substantiate the hypothesis that enhancing adenosine levels through ADK inhibition is an effective strategy to mitigate TBI-induced cell death while concurrently suppressing glial activation.

Conclusions

TBI triggers secondary events, including excessive cell death, neuroinflammation and astrogliosis, all of which influence endogenous neurogenesis and the expansion of the primary lesion. Harnessing endogenous neurogenesis while modulating the injury microenvironment represents a therapeutic strategy for brain injury. This study provides evidence that targeting ADK is a potential therapeutic approach to enhance neuronal survival post-TBI, simultaneously suppressing TBI-induced cell death, astrogliosis, and neuroinflammation.
