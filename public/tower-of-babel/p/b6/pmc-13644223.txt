Dynamic changes in axial length and refractive error following intraocular pressure normalization in acute primary angle-closure glaucoma
Authors: Yin Zhang, Qi Chen, Yue Wang, Qingmin Ma, Juan Li, Jianmin Wang, Fang Fan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo investigate the dynamic changes in axial length (AL) and refractive error following the normalization of intraocular pressure (IOP) in acute primary angle-closure glaucoma (APACG).MethodsThis study included thirty-one consecutive patients with APACG. AL and keratometric values were measured using IOLMaster before IOP normalization, after IOP normalization, and postoperatively. The analysis focused on IOP levels, AL, keratometric values, and the anticipated refractive discrepancy (the expected refractive error post-IOP normalization with intraocular lens power for emmetropia correction predetermined pre-IOP normalization).ResultsAfter IOP normalization, AL decreased significantly from 22.7 to 22.4 mm and IOP from 57.5 to 16.5 mmHg (P < 0.001). The change in IOP was positively correlated with the change in AL (r = 0.451, P = 0.011), with a decrease of 0.05 mm in AL per 10-mmHg reduction in IOP (P = 0.011). Moreover, the alteration in IOP was associated with the predicted refractive difference (r = 0.431, P = 0.015) and a predicted refractive difference of + 0.26 diopter (D) per 10-mmHg decrease in IOP (P = 0.015).ConclusionsDecreased IOP correlated with reduced AL and increased predicted refractive error (hyperopia). Therefore, lowering IOP before biometric assessment may improve refractive outcomes when feasible. In cases requiring immediate intervention, each 1-mmHg reduction in IOP decreases AL by 5 μm, yielding a hyperopic shift of 0.026 D.

Introduction

Acute primary angle-closure glaucoma (APACG) is an urgent ophthalmic condition marked by the sudden closure of the anterior chamber angle, resulting in a swift elevation of intraocular pressure (IOP), probably leading to optic nerve dysfunction [1]. Prompt reduction in IOP is essential in managing APACG to preserve visual acuity [2]. Following normalization of IOP via pharmaceutical, laser, or surgical interventions, the preservation and restoration of visual function become paramount in subsequent therapeutic approaches [3].

Sudden fluctuations in IOP pose a dual threat to the optic nerve and can affect the biomechanical properties of the eye [4]. Although existing studies have predominantly examined structural changes, such as scleral collagen reorganization and optic disc cupping [5], induced by prolonged elevated IOP, the dynamic alterations in axial length (AL) and refractive parameters during the rapid normalization process following acute IOP elevation are yet to be explored. Conventional perspectives attribute refractive changes in patients with APACG primarily to corneal edema [6]. However, emerging evidence has shown that the distinct ocular morphology of patients with APACG, characterized by a short AL, shallow anterior chamber, and lax lens zonules, can lead to intricate refractive anomalies [7].

Phacoemulsification, a technique for cataract removal along with posterior chamber intraocular lens (IOL) implantation, is not limited to cataract patients but is also employed for individuals with APACG and uncontrolled IOP [8, 9]. Phacoemulsification is beneficial in APACG because lens extraction removes the pupillary block, deepens the anterior chamber, and thereby facilitates better long-term IOP control and improved visual outcomes. Studies have reported that significant IOP fluctuations can impact AL and refractive error [10]. This effect is particularly notable in combined glaucoma and cataract surgeries, potentially resulting in errors in postoperative refractive predictions and thereby affecting visual acuity [11]. Hence, investigating the correlation between IOP and AL following IOP normalization in patients with APACG can assist in selecting IOLs with appropriate powers to achieve optimal refractive outcomes after cataract or combined surgeries. This study investigated the association between changes in ocular biometric parameters and IOP variation post-IOP normalization in patients with APACG. Additionally, we assessed the impact of elevated IOP on predicted refractive errors.

Methods

Study design and participants

This prospective study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Hebei General Hospital and adhered to the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki for research involving human subjects. Patients with APACG were consecutively enrolled in our Department of Ophthalmology from September 1, 2023, to December 31, 2024. Written informed consent was obtained from each patient prior to participation. The exclusion criteria were primary corneal disease, secondary angle-closure, contact lens use, ocular inflammation, a history of corneal or scleral surgery, and a history of any other ocular surgery within the previous 6 months in either eye.

Clinical assessments

Both eyes were examined for best-corrected visual acuity and subjected to slit-lamp biomicroscopy and dilated fundus examination. Partial coherence interferometry (IOL Master − 500, ZEISS, Germany) was used to obtain AL, anterior chamber depth (ACD), flat meridian (K1), and steep meridian (K2). IOP was measured using Goldmann applanation tonometry. Pupillary dilation for fundus evaluation and preoperative assessment was performed exclusively after IOP normalization, typically on the same day of surgery.

For participants who had a high IOP, all examinations were repeated after IOP normalization, 1 day before surgery, 1 week after surgery, and after 1 month. At each time point, we measured the IOP and ocular biological parameters of both eyes, focusing on analyzing changes in ocular biological measurement parameters before surgery due to changes in IOP, as well as the differences in the calculated refractive power of the intraocular lens caused by changes in ocular biological parameters. Notably, refractive shift is a theoretical prediction derived from the Barrett II formula, rather than from an observed clinical postoperative refractive outcome. All patients in this study underwent surgery after their IOP was reduced to normal.

To decrease IOP, we prescribed topical ocular hypotensive eye drops, myotics, and systemic therapy (a carbonic anhydrase inhibitor and/or hyperosmotic agent) depending on ocular conditions. The time from initiation of pharmacologic therapy to IOP normalization (defined as the first measurement meeting the criteria below) was documented, with IOP checked every 2 h. Following IOP normalization in the acute attack eye, phacoemulsification with IOL implantation combined with goniosynechialysis was performed by a single experienced surgeon using a standardized technique (3.0-mm clear corneal incision, continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis, and low-energy ultrasound settings). All surgeries were uneventful, with no intraoperative complications, such as posterior capsular rupture, zonular dialysis, or significant corneal edema. Postoperative complications, including but not limited to transient IOP elevation, hyphema, or persistent inflammation, were recorded at each follow-up visit (1 week and 1 month postoperatively). IOP normalization was defined as IOP ≤ 21 mmHg. None of the patients had undergone laser peripheral iridotomy before surgery.

All IOLMaster assessments were conducted using a single, highly experienced operator. The IOLMaster is currently recognized as the gold standard for IOL power calculation [12]. Its noncontact nature results in significantly smaller AL measurement errors than those associated with contact-type ultrasound biometry [13]. The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the device was recorded, and only eyes with (SNR) ≥ 2.1 were considered [14]. For eyes with clinically significant corneal edema, optical biometry was attempted; if the SNR remained below the acceptable threshold after three attempts, immersion A-scan ultrasonography was used as a complementary method. For abnormally elevated IOPs, the IOL power for emmetropia was calculated using the Barrett Ⅱ formula with an A-constant of 119.3. These values were obtained from IOLMaster printouts. Subsequently, we calculated the predicted refractive difference for each case. This value is defined as the anticipated refractive error after IOP normalization when an IOL of a power determined before IOP normalization is implanted (i.e., during an acute attack of angle-closure glaucoma).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 21.0. Fellow eyes lacking IOP elevation were used as controls. Ocular variables were compared within the same eye and between eyes (i.e., between participant eyes with elevated IOP and fellow [control] eyes) before and after IOP normalization. First, the normality of all data was tested. Parametric tests (e.g., t-tests or analysis of variance (ANOVA)) were used for normally distributed data, and non-parametric tests (e.g., Mann–Whitney U test) were used for non-normally distributed data. The correlation selection that conformed to the normal distribution was Pearson’s correlation. For variables not conforming to a normal distribution, Spearman’s correlation (non-parametric) was used.

Additionally, correlation and linear regression analyses were used to identify clinical variables associated with IOP changes, biometric values, and predicted refractive differences.

Results

Significant differences in mean IOP, AL, and the best-corrected visual acuity were observed between the affected and fellow eyes (57.5 vs. 20.4 mmHg, 22.7 vs. 22.5 mm, and 1.2 vs. 0.2 logMAR, respectively; all P values < 0.001). These differences decreased considerably after IOP normalization (16.5 vs. 16.5 mmHg, 22.4 vs. 22.4 mm, and 0.3 vs. 0.2 logMAR, respectively; P = 1.000, P = 0.101, and P = 0.138, respectively). The best-corrected visual acuity of the affected eyes was 1.2 (logMAR) before IOP normalization and 0.3 (logMAR) after normalization; this difference was statistically significant (P < 0.001). IOP in the affected eyes decreased significantly after IOP-lowering therapy from 57.5 to 16.5 mmHg (P < 0.001), and the AL became significantly shorter when the IOP was normalized (22.7 to 22.4 mm, P < 0.001). The ACD, K1, and K2 values did not change significantly (2.3 vs. 2.3 mm, 44.3 vs. 44.4 D, and 45.4 vs. 45.5 D, respectively; P = 0.769, P = 0.308, and P = 0.637, respectively). The IOP of fellow eyes was 20.4 mmHg before IOP normalization and 16.5 mmHg after normalization; this difference was statistically significant (P < 0.001). IOP normalization in the acute-attack eye did not affect the AL, ACD, K1, and K2 values in the fellow eye. The differences in all parameters, except ACD, K1, and K2, before and after IOP normalization of the affected eyes were significantly higher than those of the fellow eyes (all P < 0.001). The predicted refractive difference of the affected eyes was 0.8 D; therefore, they were significantly more hyperopic than the fellow eyes (0.0 D, P < 0.001). Specifically, 29.0% of eyes had a predicted refractive difference within ± 0.50 D, and 74.2% of eyes within ± 1.00 D. These findings indicate that almost two-thirds exceeded ± 0.50 D, highlighting the potential clinical impact of performing biometry during an acute attack. (Table 2).

Table 3 presents changes in ocular parameters in eyes with acute attacks before and after surgery. Significant differences were observed in best-corrected visual acuity and ACD between preoperative day 1 and postoperative week 1 (0.3 vs. 0.1 logMAR and 2.3 vs. 3.9 mm; P = 0.027 and P < 0.001, respectively), while other parameters, including IOP, AL, K1, and K2, exhibited no statistically significant changes. Similarly, the best-corrected visual acuity and ACD exhibited significant improvements when comparing preoperative day 1 with postoperative month 1 (0.1 logMAR and 3.9 mm; P = 0.029 and P < 0.001, respectively), with no other parameters reaching statistical significance. Reducing IOP via medication and surgery leads to shortening of AL as pressure levels normalize. The IOP measured at 1 day preoperatively and at 1 week and 1 month postoperatively was analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA, revealing no statistically significant differences (P = 0.465) (Fig. 1).

Table 5 presents the results of the linear regression analyses of clinical variables associated with the change in AL. Univariate linear regression showed that the AL after IOP normalization was negatively associated with the change in AL (B = -0.405, P = 0.024). The change in IOP and the predicted refractive difference were positively correlated with the change in AL (B = 0.005 and B = 0.074, P = 0.011 and P = 0.021, respectively) (Fig. 2). The decrease in AL per 10-mmHg IOP decrease was 0.05 mm. The change in IOP and predicted refractive difference exhibited a relatively strong bivariate correlation. Therefore, these two parameters could not be included in the multivariate analysis. Thus, we constructed an alternate multivariate model: the change in IOP and AL after IOP normalization was associated with the change in AL (B = 0.004 and B = -0.051, P = 0.019 and P = 0.012, respectively).

Discussion

A reduction in IOP and an increase in ACD following cataract surgery in individuals with APACG have been reported [3, 6, 8, 9]. Medical therapy temporarily reduces IOP. Previous studies have demonstrated that poor medication adherence and drug-induced side efects, e.g., ocular surface diseases and drug allergy, are related to poor visual prognosis in glaucoma [2]. Phacoemulsification with IOL implantation has been considered a first-line treatment in APACG, and the combination of goniosynechialysis and phacoemulsification may be more effective than phacoemulsification alone for lowering IOP in APACG [15]. In this study, after patients with APACG achieved medically controlled and stable IOP, they underwent phacoemulsification combined with goniosynechialysis surgery. IOP-lowering medications were discontinued after the operation. The IOP was monitored at 1 week and 1 month after operation and remained below 20 mmHg at both time points. No statistically significant differences were observed across the three time points, indicating that the patients achieved excellent IOP control through the surgery. The follow-up period was relatively short. Future studies with extended follow-up periods are required to evaluate the long-term effects of intraocular pressure control.

Various studies have highlighted that patients with glaucoma who have undergone filtration procedures resulting in decreased IOP exhibit a reduction in AL [10, 11]. These findings imply that the IOP-reducing impact of cataract surgery may affect AL, particularly in individuals with APACG.

Accurate prediction of refractive power is crucial for meeting postoperative visual outcome expectations following cataract extraction. Patients with APACG present challenges because of unpredictable refractive outcomes resulting from their unique anatomical features [16]. An implanted IOL may shift hyperopically if it moves toward a more posterior position because of deepening of the anterior chamber and a decrease in AL [17–20]. Conversely, some studies have reported significant myopic shifts [21, 22], postoperatively, possibly because of the instability of in-the-bag IOLs in APACG eyes. Increased capsular volume and weakened lens zonules in these eyes are believed to contribute to this IOL instability. Kim et al. [23] observed a positive correlation between increased lens thickness and greater refractive change (RC), irrespective of the direction of the shift (hyperopic or myopic). Importantly, all patients in the present study underwent preoperative ultrasound biomicroscopy (UBM) to address clinically significant lens subluxation or other abnormalities of the suspensory ligament. The involvement of lens vault (LV) in angle closure, a well-established phenomenon, may contribute to discrepancies in IOL prediction. Nongpiur et al. [24] proposed a potential association between an elevated LV and alterations in zonular laxity. Seo et al. [20] evaluated biometric parameters affecting the accuracy of IOL power calculations for cataract surgery in eyes with primary angle closure or APACG. Their findings reveal a postoperative hyperopic RC. A multivariate analysis of the aforementioned variables showed that only relative LV (rLV) emerged as a significant predictive factor for refractive errors following cataract surgery. This assessment underscores that although LV is primarily determined by lens thickness and position, rLV is also influenced by ACD, reflecting the composite impact of various factors.

A lack of consensus has been observed among emerging modern formulas regarding their predictive accuracy in shallow anterior chamber eyes, particularly in eyes with APACG [20, 21, 25]. Previous studies have assessed the predictive capabilities of various IOL calculation formulas in eyes with APACG, yielding inconsistent results. These studies typically utilized third-generation IOL calculation formulas that rely solely on AL and keratometry values to determine effective lens position [25]. By contrast, contemporary IOL power calculation methods are based on fourth-generation formulas that integrate additional parameters such as ACD to enhance the accuracy of predicting effective lens position [25, 26]. Our study employed the fourth-generation Barrett II formula for IOL power calculation.

Each 1-mm alteration in ACD may induce an RC of at least 0.32 diopters (D) [25], whereas a 1-mm change in AL results in a shift of 2.5 D [27]. Existing studies have shown that a reduction in AL can lead to a hyperopic shift in post-cataract extraction eyes with APACG [19, 21, 28] and open-angle glaucoma [29]. This study validates that lowering IOP via trabeculectomy causes AL reduction. The refractive outcome is influenced by the surgical sequence, with superior results observed when phacoemulsification follows trabeculectomy. Significant differences in refractive outcomes were observed between the two groups: phacoemulsification first (0.35 ± 0.75 D) and trabeculectomy first (− 0.05 ± 0.36 D), p = 0 0.018. Not all combined cataract and glaucoma surgeries result in a postoperative hyperopic shift, which typically arises from AL reduction-induced hyperopia and changes in effective lens position post-surgery [21, 22].

This study demonstrated that reducing IOP via local and systemic medications leads to shortening of AL as pressure levels normalize. Following stabilization of IOP, patients underwent cataract surgery along with goniosynechialysis. No statistically significant differences in IOP or AL were observed at Post-surgery at 1 week and 1 month compared with pre-surgery. These findings show that the reduction in AL after glaucoma surgery is primarily associated with preoperative IOP levels, which serve as primary determinants of AL alterations. Results showed that IOP decreased from 57.5 ± 9.4 mmHg during acute attacks to 16.5 ± 2.6 mmHg, resulting in a decrease in AL from 22.7 ± 0.8 mm to 22.4 ± 0.9 mm. Using the Barrett II formula, a 1-mmHg reduction corresponds to an AL shortening of approximately 0.005 mm, resulting in a refractive error of approximately + 0.0026 D. Previous studies [13] have shown that post-trabeculectomy, as IOP diminishes, AL decreases, with a 1-mmHg IOP reduction leading to an AL shortening of approximately 0.006 mm. Kim et al. [10] obtained similar results. They employed pharmacological interventions or laser iridotomy to lower IOPs and observed a significant correlation between IOP and AL reduction. Their results demonstrate a direct relationship between IOP reduction and AL shortening. They predicted that a 1-mmHg decrease would cause a + 0.015-D shift using the SRK/T formula. We applied the Barrett II formula and obtained a + 0.026D shift. This difference may be attributed to the use of different formulas. Notably, the participants in the two studies differed. Our study included only participants with APACG, whereas previous studies included those with various types of glaucoma. Notably, our study did not reveal significant discrepancies in corneal K-values (K1 and K2) or ACD during acute attacks, consistent with previous outcomes [10, 30].

We report three quantitative relationships: (1) AL decreases by approximately 5 μm for every 1-mmHg drop in IOP, (2) the predicted refractive difference increases by + 0.026 D per 1-mmHg decrease in IOP, and (3) a 1-mm decrease in AL corresponds to approximately an RC of + 2.323 D. Combining the first and third relationships predicts a refractive shift of approximately + 0.011–0.012 D per 1-mmHg reduction in IOP, which is considerably smaller than + 0.026 D per 1-mmHg, possibly because of the following analysis: (1) The sample size was relatively small, which might have led to failure to detect significant factors such as changes in ACD and corneal curvature related to the correlation. Although we observed no correlation, the combined effect of some factors may have caused subsequent statistical variations. (2) In this study, 0.5% pilocarpine eye drops were administered to patients upon admission to rapidly lower IOP. This drug induces lens accommodation and reduces ACD, with reported reductions averaging 57 ± 36 μm [31]. Consequently, this may lead to AL elongation, with reported increases of 0.04 ± 0.04 mm [32]. However, to maintain stable IOP before surgery, the drug was not discontinued, thus acting as a confounding factor influencing ocular biometric measurements, potentially contributing to the observed statistical discrepancies.

A contentious aspect of this study pertains to the measurement of ocular biometric parameters under high IOP conditions. Ocular biometric parameters were measured using the IOLMaster system. Not all readings obtained with the IOLMaster were of good quality, and considerable differences with conventional (contact) A-scan ultrasound (US) were observed. The error between US and IOLMaster decreased significantly with increasing SNR, exhibiting a minimum error at an SNR value exceeding 2.1 [14]. This study established an SNR > 2.1 as the inclusion criterion, and patients with unavailable data were excluded. Our observations revealed that although the majority of patients presented with high IOP and corneal epithelial edema, these conditions did not compromise the optical penetrability measured by IOLMaster. Provided that ocular biometric parameters were measurable with a minimum SNR of 8, the reliability of the data was ensured. The IOLMaster measures AL from the corneal endothelial apex to the pigment epithelium layer. Therefore, in cases of high IOPs, corneal epithelial edema does not affect AL. However, residual and unquantifiable errors from subclinical corneal edema during acute measurements may contribute to variability.

Many studies have established the relationship between changes in IOP and AL; however, the precise mechanism has not been identified. Hata et al. [33] and Wang et al. [34] observed that the change in choroidal thickness (CT) correlated with the changes in IOP and AL, indicating that choroidal thinning is, at least in part, a contributor to AL elongation caused by IOP increase. However, considering that a change in CT accounts for between 25% and 50% of the change in AL, other contributors, including scleral stretching caused by IOP elevation, to axial elongation are possible. [33] The relationships among elevated IOP, reduced scleral thickness, and AL elongation have been intensively investigated [35–37]. These studies have reported that elevated IOP contributes to AL elongation by inducing characteristic changes in the biomechanical properties of the sclera. Specifically, these changes include extracellular matrix remodeling and reduced mechanical elasticity of the sclera.

This study has some limitations. Primarily, the sample size was limited, and the study was conducted at a single center, necessitating larger sample sizes and multi-center studies for broader generalizability. Additionally, the follow-up duration was short. Although no statistically significant disparity in AL was observed between the 1-week and 1-month postoperative assessments, an extended follow-up period is essential to track the trajectory of AL alterations post IOP stabilization. Notably, 0.5% pilocarpine eye drops can induce ciliary muscle contraction, leading to increased lens thickness and anterior displacement of the lens–iris diaphragm, which could independently alter ACD, AL, and other biometric parameters. Future studies should consider alternative study designs (e.g., comparing biometric changes after IOP-lowering interventions, such as selective laser peripheral iridoplasty or certain systemic medications, that do not affect accommodation) to better isolate the mechanical effects of IOP changes.

In conclusion, we observed a reduction in AL and a corresponding increase in predicted refractive error (hyperopia) in correlation with a decrease in IOP. We recommend AL measurement and IOL power calculation following IOP normalization. Moreover, in a case where IOP normalization is not possible and immediate intervention is necessary, IOL power may be calculated based on the expected IOP change following surgery. Our findings suggest a decrease in AL of 5 μm for every 1-mm Hg reduction in IOP, resulting in a hyperopic shift of + 0.026 D.
