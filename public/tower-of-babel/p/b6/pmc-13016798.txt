In vivo self-assembled SOD1-siRNAs mitigate muscle atrophy and denervation in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis
Authors: Jingwei Guo, Qian Zou, Jiawei Xu, Jieqiong Lei, Xin Yin, Botao Li, Jinyu Fu, Junjie Mi, Yanbo Wang, Huan Huang, Chen-Yu Zhang, Xi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a neurodegenerative disorder characterized by the death of both upper and lower motor neurons. Approximately 20% of familial ALS cases are associated with mutations in the superoxide dismutase type 1 (SOD1) gene. Developing a specific strategy to characteristically silence the pathogenic SOD1 gene remains a crucial goal amidst significant challenges.In this study, we developed a synthetic biology strategy to reprogramme the liver as a tissue chassis for the in vivo self-assembly of small extracellular vesicles (sEVs)-encapsulated SOD1-siRNA, aiming to target spinal neurons and silence mutant SOD1 specifically in Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice. We designed a cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter-directed synthetic construct to encode a SOD1-siRNA along with a neuron-targeting rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) tagged on the sEV surface. Theoretically, upon liver uptake, this construct reprogrammes liver cells to generate and self-assemble SOD1-siRNAs into RVG-tagged sEVs. Subsequently, the sEV-encapsulated SOD1-siRNAs are transported via the endogenous sEV circulation and guided by the RVG tag to the spinal neurons.Experimental results illustrated that intravenous administration of this synthetic construct effectively facilitated in vivo self-assembly of SOD1-siRNAs into circulating sEVs. The functional delivery of SOD1-siRNAs to the spinal cord and cerebral cortex was confirmed through in vivo tracking of sEVs and sEV-encapsulated siRNAs. Treatment of Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice with this construct significantly reduced mutant SOD1 protein levels in the spinal cord and cerebral cortex. Consequently, the characteristic symptoms of ALS, including decreased body weight, shortened lifespan, compromised motor function, muscle atrophy, neuroinflammation, motor neuron loss and neuromuscular junction degeneration, were substantially ameliorated by the synthetic construct. Furthermore, an AAV-based strategy was devised for the enduring self-assembly of sEV-encapsulated SOD1-siRNA, whereby a single injection led to substantial and sustained inhibition of mutant SOD1 and significant symptom amelioration in transgenic mice.Overall, this study established an effective and convenient therapeutic approach for mitigating muscle atrophy and denervation in animal model, presenting a promising solution for future ALS treatment.

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal neurodegenerative disease characterized by the progressive loss of upper motor neurons in the motor cortex and brainstem, as well as lower motor neurons in the ventral horn of the spinal cord.1,2 The loss of respiratory muscle function severely shortens patients’ lifespans. Due to the complex cellular processes involved in ALS progression, identifying a definitive pathological cause remains challenging. In 1993, the first pathological factor of ALS, a mutation in the superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) gene, was identified.3 Mutations in SOD1 accelerate ALS progression through multiple mechanisms: (i) mutated SOD1 increases oxidative stress and causes neuronal damage4; (ii) mutated SOD1 results in the accumulation of excitatory neurotransmitters and neuronal death5,6; (iii) mutated SOD1 disrupts mitochondrial function and cell survival7; (iv) mutated SOD1 interferes with calcium ion homeostasis, leading to energy metabolic disorders and cell death8; and (v) mutated SOD1 affects non-neuronal cells such as glial and muscle cells.9 Overall, in ALS, SOD1 gene mutations impact both upper and lower motor neurons and affecting microglia, astrocytes and other cells.

In recent years, RNA interference (RNAi)-based therapies, utilizing antisense oligonucleotides (ASO), small interfering RNA (siRNA) or microRNA (miRNA) as triggers, have provided an innovative treatment approach to tackle a broad range of diseases caused by genetic mutations or abnormal amplification. Since the identification of SOD1 mutations as a root cause of ALS, RNAi therapies targeting this mutated gene have attracted significant attention.10-13 The primary techniques involve delivering ASO of SOD1 intrathecally or intraventricularly, as well as expressing SOD1-siRNA specifically in neuron cells through adeno-associated virus (AAV) subtypes. For example, Tofersen is an ASO that silences mutant SOD1 by interfering with its mRNA. Clinical trial results reveal that Tofersen can decrease the level of neurofilament light chain (NF-L), indicating its potential protective effect against neurodegeneration.14 However, RNAi therapies face several challenges in clinical applications. (i) In clinical trials, the use of Tofersen on ALS patients has resulted in several side effects. AAV vectors also elicit an immune response from patients, particularly to their shell protein.14 (ii) Their delivery methods require intrathecal administration or intrathecal or CSF injection. (iii) Intrathecal injection of ASO or siRNA often have restricted biodistribution only near the injection site. Thus, developing an efficient, safe and low-invasive delivery system that can promote widespread expression of siRNA or AAV throughout the pathological sites, rather than being limited to the injection site, is a crucial scientific issue that RNAi therapies must address at present.

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are a subtype of biological vesicles derived from the phospholipid bilayer of cellular membranes. They play a crucial role in regulating intercellular communication in both healthy states and disease conditions.15 EVs facilitate this communication by transporting bioactive molecules—such as proteins and miRNAs—encased within the vesicles from the source cell to the target cell, thereby influencing the functions of target cells.16 Given the similarities between siRNAs and miRNAs in molecular properties, packaging siRNAs in EVs is a viable strategy. Compared to viruses, liposome nanoparticles and polymer nanoparticles, EVs are derived from endogenous cells and exhibit low immunogenicity.17,18 Their nanoscale size helps them evade clearance by monocyte-scavenger cells and allows them to easily permeate blood vessels into the extracellular matrix.19 Additionally, modifying the surface membrane proteins of EVs can grant them the ability to cross the blood–brain barrier (BBB), a significant advantage in treating neurological disorders that other drug delivery methods cannot match.18 However, the process of packaging siRNAs into EVs involves large-scale in vitro cell culture and the subsequent separation and purification of EVs, which are complex and costly. These challenges hinder the ability to achieve stable production and clinical application of sEV-encapsulated siRNAs.

To address the challenges of in vitro loading of siRNAs into EVs, we have developed a synthetic biology strategy to reprogramme the liver as a tissue chassis for the delivery of in vivo self-assembled siRNAs (IVSA-siRNAs) to specific tissues and cells.20 This strategy leverages the liver's natural ability to express transgenes introduced through intravenous injection of synthetic constructs (in the form of naked DNA plasmids). When these plasmids are taken up by liver cells, the liver's miRNA processing machinery generates and self-assembles siRNAs into secretory EVs. These EVs are then transported by the body's endogenous EV circulation system to target tissues and cells. This approach reduces the costs and complexities associated with in vitro siRNA loading and EV purification. In this study, we designed a synthetic construct to reprogramme mouse livers to enable the self-assembly of small EVs (sEVs)-encapsulated SOD1-siRNA for treating ALS in transgenic mouse model. To achieve neuron-targeting functionality, we fused the rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG) peptide with the EV membrane protein lysosome-associated membrane protein 2b (Lamp2b). The RVG peptide specifically targets nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAchR) on neurons. By anchoring the RVG tag to the EV surface through Lamp2b, we achieved targeted delivery of SOD1-siRNA to the spinal cord and cerebral cortex, effectively silencing SOD1 and alleviating the disease at behavioural, pathological and molecular levels.

Results

Design of the synthetic construct for producing IVSA-siRNA targeting mutant SOD1

We have designed and assembled a synthetic construct consisting of three modules aimed at producing and self-assembling SOD1-siRNA into neuron-targeting sEVs (Supplementary Fig. 1A), offering a potential therapeutic approach for treating ALS. The synthetic construct comprises the following three modules. (i) Promoter: the cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter was chosen for its proven efficacy in driving both protein and siRNA transcription.21 (ii) siRNA scaffold: the miR-155 precursor was selected as the expression scaffold for SOD1-siRNA. This structure has been shown to optimize the expression of the siRNA guide strand while minimizing the expression of unwanted passenger strands.20,22 It also enhances the packaging of intracellular siRNAs into sEVs and their subsequent secretion into the extracellular space.23 Consequently, the siRNA sequence targeting mutant SOD1 was inserted at the 5′ end of the pre-miR-155 scaffold. (iii) Neuron-targeting module: the RVG peptide, known for its specific targeting of nAchR on neurons, was incorporated to confer neuron-targeting functionality to the engineered EVs.18,24 This was achieved by introducing the coding sequence for the RVG peptide at the N-terminus of Lamp2b. The synthetic construct, comprising these three components, can be administered intravenously to mice (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Then the synthetic construct is absorbed by mouse liver25 and facilitates the generation of pre-miRNAs in liver cells, which is further processed by endogenous miRNA machinery to produce mature form, miRNA-like SOD1-siRNAs, leading to spontaneous packaging of cytoplasmic SOD1-siRNAs into secretory sEVs with surface-anchored RVG peptides. These sEV-encapsulated SOD1-siRNAs can traverse the BBB and reach the CNS, particularly the spinal cord, where they specifically target and suppress mutant SOD1 expression.

In vitro validation of the synthetic construct for silencing mutant SOD1

To model the pathogenesis and progression of ALS in vitro, we developed a lentivirus vector to express the mutant SOD1 gene, which contains a G93A mutation associated with familial ALS, in fusion with the eGFP gene (LV-SOD1G93A-eGFP). This vector was used to establish a stable HEK293T cell line expressing LV-SOD1G93A-eGFP. We verified the successful construction of this transformed cell line using quantitative real-time (RT) PCR and western blot analyses. Quantitative RT-PCR results indicated that SOD1 mRNA levels were significantly higher in the transformed HEK293T cells compared to untransformed cells (Supplementary Fig. 2A). Western blot analysis confirmed that these cells expressed the SOD1G93A-eGFP fusion protein at high levels (Supplementary Fig. 2B). The molecular mass of the fusion protein was approximately 45 kDa, which was distinct from the 18 kDa wild-type SOD1 protein.

Next, we designed five specific siRNA sequences targeting SOD1. The first three siRNAs targeted exon 4 of the SOD1 mRNA, while the fourth and fifth targeted exon 2 (Supplementary Fig. 2C). The second SOD1-siRNA sequence was specifically designed to cover the G93A mutant region of the human SOD1G93A gene. As a result, this sequence displayed perfect complementary with the human SOD1G93A gene, with only a single base mismatch against the wild-type human SOD1 gene. However, it had a higher number of mismatches with the wild-type mouse SOD1 gene (Supplementary Fig. 2D). To evaluate the efficacy of these siRNAs in silencing mutant SOD1, we synthesized and transfected them into HEK293T cells stably expressing LV-SOD1G93A-eGFP (Supplementary Fig. 2E). Western blot and quantitative RT-PCR analyses revealed that the second SOD1-siRNA was the most effective in reducing both SOD1G93A protein and mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. 2F–H). Consequently, the second SOD1-siRNA sequence was selected for integration into the synthetic construct for further research.

To ensure that the synthetic construct effectively inhibits mutant SOD1, we integrated the SOD1-siRNA sequence into the SOD1-siRNA expression scaffold and further combined this with the RVG-Lamp2b fusion protein module, under the control of the CMV promoter (denoted as CMV-RVG-siRNA). As a negative control, we designed a synthetic construct encoding a scrambled RNA sequence and the RVG-Lamp2b fusion protein, also under CMV promoter control (denoted as CMV-RVG-scrR). Additionally, we created a synthetic construct that encodes only the SOD1-siRNA (denoted as CMV-siRNA). We transfected equivalent amounts of CMV-RVG-scrR, CMV-siRNA and CMV-RVG-siRNA into HEK293T cells stably expressing LV-SOD1G93A-eGFP and assessed the levels of SOD1 protein and mRNA (Supplementary Fig. 3A). Equal amounts of SOD1-siRNA were accumulated in HEK293T cells after transfecting with CMV-siRNA or CMV-RVG-siRNA (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Similarly, there was no significant difference in the inhibitory effects on SOD1 protein and mRNA between CMV-siRNA and CMV-RVG-siRNA, and both constructs significantly reduced the levels of mutant SOD1 protein and mRNA in the cells (Supplementary Fig. 3C–E). However, both CMV-siRNA and CMV-RVG-siRNA showed no interference on the wild-type human SOD1 protein, despite only having a single base mismatch (Supplementary Fig. 3F and G).

In vivo characterization of synthetic construct for functional IVSA-siRNA targeting mutant SOD1

To investigate the synthetic construct's capability to produce and secrete sEV-encapsulated SOD1-siRNA into circulation, we administered C57BL/6J mice intravenously with phosphate buffered saline (PBS), CMV-RVG-scrR, CMV-siRNA or CMV-RVG-siRNA every 2 days for a total of seven times, at a dosage of 5 mg/kg per injection. We then isolated sEVs from mouse plasma using ultracentrifugation and quantified SOD1-siRNA levels in the plasma sEVs (Fig. 1A). The isolated sEVs were characterized by nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA), transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and detection of canonical sEV markers. NTA results showed that the purified sEVs from mouse plasma exhibited typical sizes (∼100 nm) and morphologies consistent with sEVs (Supplementary Fig. 4A). TEM analysis confirmed that the sEVs had a double-layered membrane structure and a characteristic cup-shaped morphology (Supplementary Fig. 4B), aligning with expected standards. Moreover, numerous sEV markers, including Alix, TSG101 and CD9,26 were detected in the isolated plasma sEVs (Supplementary Fig. 4C). Notably, these markers were absence in the supernatant obtained during the sEV purification process, demonstrating the high purity of the isolated sEVs. By determining the yield of plasma sEVs as total protein content using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay and measuring the amount of SOD1-siRNA in sEVs using a quantitative RT-PCR assay, the absolute concentration of SOD1-siRNA in plasma sEVs was calculated. A significant amount of SOD1-siRNA was concentrated in plasma sEVs purified from mice injected with CMV-siRNA or CMV-RVG-siRNA (Fig. 1B).

There are two primary mechanisms by which miRNAs circulate in the blood despite the presence of ubiquitous RNases: encapsulation within sEV membrane structures or complexation with RNA-binding proteins such as Ago2.27 To exclude the possibility that SOD1-siRNA is complexed with Ago2 after injection of the CMV-RVG-siRNA construct, we performed immunoprecipitation of plasma from mice injected with CMV-RVG-siRNA using anti-Ago2 or anti-IgG antibodies (Fig. 1A). We then measured the levels of SOD1-siRNA and miR-16 (served as a control) in Ago2 and IgG immunoprecipitates. A specific Ago2 band (∼97 kDa) was detected in the Ago2 immunoprecipitates, while no Ago2 band was observed in the IgG immunoprecipitates, confirming the successful pull-down of Ago2 from mouse plasma (Fig. 1C). Anti-Ago2 treatment depleted miR-16 from the plasma, and this depleted miR-16 was significantly recovered from the immunoprecipitated beads, consistent with previous reports indicating that miR-16 is largely bound to Ago2 in plasma.28 In contrast, SOD1-siRNA could not be immunoprecipitated by anti-Ago2, and SOD1-siRNA was absent from Ago2 immunoprecipitates (Fig. 1D), suggesting that SOD1-siRNA does not associate with Ago2 protein in plasma. Given that miR-155 is primarily associated with the sEV-enriched fraction in plasma, it is likely that SOD1-siRNA, embedded within the pre-miR-155 backbone, circulates in the blood encapsulated within sEVs rather than being complexed with RNA-binding proteins.

To confirm that the SOD1-siRNA encapsulated within plasma sEVs is specifically produced and secreted by the liver, we measured the levels of SOD1-siRNA in liver-derived plasma sEVs. Following seven injections of the CMV-RVG-siRNA construct, liver-derived sEVs in the plasma were specifically isolated from total plasma sEVs using anti-asialoglycoprotein receptor 1 (ASGPR1)29 immunoprecipitation (Fig. 1A). The abundance of ASGPR1 did not differ significantly between the total sEV isolates (input) and the ASGPR1 immunoprecipitates, while ASGPR1 was absent in the non-captured IgG immunoprecipitates (Fig. 1E and F). Considering that liver-derived sEVs constitute 7.82%–13.70% of the total circulating sEVs,29 a proportionate level of sEV protein markers (Alix, TSG101 and CD9) was detected in the ASGPR1-positive sEVs compared to total sEVs (Fig. 1E and F). These results demonstrate that anti-ASGPR1 immunoprecipitation effectively isolates liver-specific sEVs from the circulation. Notably, the amount of SOD1-siRNA recovered from ASGPR1-positive sEVs was nearly equivalent to that in the total sEVs (Fig. 1G), indicating that the SOD1-siRNA encapsulated in sEVs is predominantly released into the bloodstream by the liver.

We further investigated the gene-silencing efficacy of the SOD1-siRNA encapsulated within plasma sEVs. C57BL/6J mice were administered PBS, CMV-RVG-scrR, CMV-siRNA or CMV-RVG-siRNA, and sEVs were subsequently isolated from the mouse plasma. These sEVs were labelled with the lipophilic membrane dye PKH26 and then incubated with SH-SY5Y cells infected with LV-SOD1G93A-eGFP. Intracellular fluorescence intensity was assessed using confocal microscopy. After 48 h of incubation, SH-SY5Y cells effectively took up the fluorescently labelled sEVs from all four groups (Supplementary Fig. 3H). However, compared to SH-SY5Y cells treated with sEVs from mice injected with PBS or CMV-RVG-scrR, efficient knockdown of SOD1 mRNA and protein levels was observed in cells treated with sEVs from mice injected with CMV-siRNA or CMV-RVG-siRNA (Fig. 1H–J). Notably, sEVs derived from CMV-RVG-siRNA-injected mice exhibited a more rapid silencing effect on the mutant SOD1 protein compared to those from CMV-siRNA-injected mice (Fig. 1I and J). This result suggests that the RVG peptide on the sEV surface enhances the sEVs’ affinity for neuronal cells.

Tracking the in vivo delivery of IVSA-siRNA to the spinal cord and cerebral cortex

To monitor the in vivo delivery of sEV-encapsulated SOD1-siRNA to the spinal cord and cerebral cortex, we administered C57BL/6J mice intravenously with synthetic constructs every 2 days for a total of seven injections, at a dosage of 5 mg/kg per injection. Subsequently, we measured the levels of synthetic constructs and SOD1-siRNA in plasma, spinal cord and cerebral cortex. After administration of the CMV-RVG-siRNA construct, the liver displayed rapid accumulation of the construct within the first hour, with levels returning to baseline after 9 h (Fig. 2A). In contrast, there seems to be no accumulation of CMV-RVG-siRNA construct in the heart, spleen, lung, kidney, cerebral cortex or spinal cord (Fig. 2A). Consequently, administration of the CMV-RVG-siRNA construct led to notable levels of SOD1-siRNA in mouse plasma, which peaked approximately 9 h post-injection and returned to background levels by 48 h (Fig. 2B). As a control, there was no difference in the efficiency for the CMV-RVG-scrR to assemble scrambled RNA compared with that of the CMV-siRNA or CMV-RVG-siRNA to assemble SOD1-siRNA (Fig. 2C). However, compared to CMV-RVG-scrR and CMV-siRNA, only the CMV-RVG-siRNA construct led to significantly higher concentrations of SOD1-siRNA in the spinal cord and cerebral cortex (Fig. 2D). This result highlights the crucial role of the RVG guiding tag in enhancing siRNA delivery to the CNS.

We also evaluated the delivery and accumulation of RVG-tagged sEVs to the spinal cord and cerebral cortex. C57BL/6J mice were intravenously administered PBS, CMV-siRNA or CMV-RVG-siRNA every 2 days for a total of seven injections at a dose of 5 mg/kg per injection. Twelve hours after the final injection, sEVs were purified from the mouse plasma and labelled with the PKH26 dye.30 These fluorescently labelled sEVs were then injected into new C57BL/6J mice to evaluate their in vivo distribution. Fluorescent signals (red) were detected in the livers of mice across all three groups (Supplementary Fig. 5A), but only those receiving CMV-RVG-siRNA exhibited significant fluorescence in the spinal cord and cerebral cortex (Fig. 2E and Supplementary Fig. 5B and C). This finding indicates that the RVG-Lamp2b fusion protein module effectively confers neuron-targeting capabilities to the engineered sEVs.

Additionally, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was employed to visualize the in vivo distribution of SOD1-siRNA in the spinal cord. Digoxigenin (DIG)-labelled locked nucleic acid (LNA) probes were used to detect SOD1-siRNA.31 In mice injected with either CMV-RVG-scrR or CMV-siRNA, no hybridization signals for SOD1-siRNA were detected in the spinal cord, whereas significant signals were observed in the spinal cord of mice injected with CMV-RVG-siRNA, and the DIG signal does not seem to accumulate in the cell nucleus (Fig. 2F and Supplementary Fig. 5D).

Meanwhile, we utilized a transgenic mouse model that ubiquitously expresses eGFP to evaluate the functional transfer of IVSA-siRNA to the spinal cord and cerebral cortex. We developed a CMV-directed synthetic construct expressing an eGFP-silencing siRNA, with or without an RVG-Lamp2b fusion protein (denoted as CMV-RVG-siRNAeGFP or CMV-siRNAeGFP). eGFP transgenic mice were intravenously administered PBS, CMV-RVG-scrR, CMV-siRNAeGFP or CMV-RVG-siRNAeGFP every 2 days for a total of seven injections, at a dose of 5 mg/kg per injection. We then assessed eGFP fluorescence reduction in the liver, spinal cord and cerebral cortex. Compared to eGFP transgenic mice injected with PBS or CMV-RVG-scrR, a significant reduction in eGFP fluorescence was observed in the livers of mice injected with CMV-siRNAeGFP or CMV-RVG-siRNAeGFP (Supplementary Fig. 6A). Notably, only CMV-RVG-siRNAeGFP injection resulted in a substantial reduction of eGFP fluorescence in the spinal cord and cerebral cortex (Fig. 2G and Supplementary Fig. 6B and C). Quantitative RT-PCR analysis of eGFP mRNA and western blot analysis of eGFP protein further confirmed a significant reduction in eGFP levels in the spinal cord and cerebral cortex with CMV-RVG-siRNAeGFP (Supplementary Fig. 7). These results demonstrate that neuron-targeted IVSA-siRNA is efficiently delivered to the spinal cord and cerebral cortex through intravenous administration of the synthetic construct.

Evaluation of the effects of IVSA-siRNA on survival and motor function of Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice

We investigated the therapeutic effects of in vivo self-assembled SOD1-siRNA in Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice, which express the mutant human SOD1G93A gene and exhibit paralysis in one or more limbs due to the loss of spinal motor neurons, accompanied by severe neuroinflammation and denervation of skeletal muscle.31,32 We randomly assigned Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice into three groups and administered CMV-RVG-scrR, CMV-siRNA or CMV-RVG-siRNA via intravenous injection twice a week for 10 weeks, at a dosage of 5 mg/kg (Fig. 3A). Wild-type mice received intravenous injections of PBS as controls. Throughout and after treatment, we monitored the mice’ body weight and survival rates until their demise. The body weight of wild-type mice naturally increased along with age, whereas Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice receiving CMV-RVG-scrR or CMV-siRNA injections experienced significant weight loss (Fig. 3B). In contrast, CMV-RVG-siRNA injection significantly delayed the weight loss in Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice during the disease progression (Fig. 3B). Moreover, in Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice treated with CMV-RVG-scrR or CMV-siRNA, survival rates progressively declined, with all mice succumbing within 160–170 days (Fig. 3C). In contrast, treatment with CMV-RVG-siRNA significantly improved survival outcomes: all mice were still alive at 170 days, the median survival time increased from 146 and 148 days in the CMV-RVG-scrR and CMV-siRNA control groups, respectively, to 185 days in the CMV-RVG-siRNA-treated group, and the maximum survival was extended to 212 days (Fig. 3C). These results indicate that IVSA-siRNA targeting mutant SOD1 significantly improves survival status for Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice.

Furthermore, we evaluated the motor performance of Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice using a series of behavioural tests, including the limbs grip strength test, rotarod test and open field test. As expected, wild-type mice performed better in all three tests, while Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice exhibited a gradual decline in grip strength and a tendency to fall during the rotarod test following CMV-RVG-scrR or CMV-siRNA injections (Fig. 3D and E). However, the injection of CMV-RVG-siRNA into the tail vein of Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice significantly delayed these defects, as evidenced by an improvement in grip strength and an extension of the duration on the rolling rod (Fig. 3D and E). Specifically, the representative schematic diagram of the open field test revealed a dramatic improvement in locomotor activity in Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice treated with CMV-RVG-siRNA (Fig. 3F and G). Taken together, these data provide compelling evidence for the therapeutic potential of in vivo self-assembled SOD1-siRNA in enhancing motor function in Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice.

Evaluation of the effects of IVSA-siRNA on muscle atrophy, SOD1 aggregation and neuroinflammation

Skeletal muscle atrophy is a typical feature of ALS.23,33 As the disease progresses, the hindlimb muscles of Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice usually exhibit significant atrophy at or near the end stage of the disease (about 4–6 months), when wild-type mice do not show any pathological changes. We examined the extent of muscle atrophy in the hindlimbs of Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice after treatment with synthetic constructs. Specifically, we assessed the hindlimb appearance of each mouse at approximately 140 days. The representative images of the hindlimb in Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice treated with CMV-RVG-scrR or CMV-siRNA revealed significant muscle atrophy during the later stages of disease progression as compared to wild-type mice (Fig. 4A). In contrast, treatment with CMV-RVG-siRNA resulted in a substantial attenuation of muscle atrophy in the hindlimbs of transgenic mice (Fig. 4A). To further assess the effects of the synthetic constructs on muscle fibre cross-sectional area (CSA) of the gastrocnemius muscle, immunofluorescence staining for laminin was performed. As expected, Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice treated with CMV-RVG-scrR or CMV-siRNA displayed a significantly smaller CSA of gastrocnemius compared to wild-type mice (Fig. 4A and B). However, treatment with CMV-RVG-siRNA led to a significant restoration of muscle fibre CSA in the gastrocnemius of transgenic mice (Fig. 4A and B).

The ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) is a major mechanism for muscle protein degradation, playing a central role in controlling muscle size. Two muscle-specific E3 ubiquitin ligases, Atrogin-1 and muscle RING-finger protein-1 (MuRF-1), are crucial for ubiquitin-mediated protein degradation in skeletal muscle.34-36 Overexpression of these genes is commonly observed in muscle atrophy due to various diseases or aging, making them useful markers of muscle atrophy. Western blot analysis demonstrated a higher expression of Atrogin-1 and MuRF-1 protein in the gastrocnemius of Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice treated with CMV-RVG-scrR or CMV-siRNA compared to wild-type mice (Supplementary Fig. 8A and B). However, administration of CMV-RVG-siRNA significantly reduced the expression of these two ubiquitin ligases in the gastrocnemius of the transgenic mice (Supplementary Fig. 8A and B). These findings suggest that in vivo self-assembled SOD1-siRNA may block the activity of the ubiquitin-mediated muscle protein degradation pathway, potentially protecting skeletal muscle from atrophy.

Moreover, we compared the inhibitory impact of in vivo self-assembled SOD1-siRNA on the pathogenic SOD1 protein in Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice. After 10 weeks of treatment, we collected tissue samples from the L3–L5 segments of lumbar spinal cord for western blot and quantitative RT-PCR analyses. In contrast to the transgenic mice injected with CMV-RVG-scrR or CMV-siRNA, which expressed high levels of mutant SOD1 protein in the L3–L5 segments of lumbar spinal cord, the transgenic mice injected with CMV-RVG-siRNA displayed a significantly reduced level of SOD1 protein expression in these regions (Fig. 4C and D). Similarly, there was a significant reduction in the mRNA levels of SOD1 in the L3–L5 segments of lumbar spinal cord of transgenic mice injected with CMV-RVG-siRNA (Fig. 4E). Additionally, we evaluated SOD1 aggregation in Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice using immunofluorescence staining. In ALS, protein aggregation resulting from mutation-induced conformational changes and misfolding of mutated proteins is toxic to motor neurons.36,37 Compared to the transgenic mice treated with CMV-RVG-scrR or CMV-siRNA, the SOD1 aggregation was significantly decreased in the cerebral cortex and L3–L5 segments of the lumbar spinal cord of transgenic mice injected with CMV-RVG-siRNA (Supplementary Fig. 8C and D). These data demonstrate that in vivo self-assembled SOD1-siRNA effectively suppresses the expression of mutant SOD1 protein in the cerebral cortex and spinal cord, thereby alleviating muscle atrophy and denervation phenotypes in Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice.

Neuroinflammation is a hallmark pathological feature and a critical factor driving disease progression in ALS. In ALS, neuroinflammation primarily involves the activation of microglia38-40 and astrocytes,41,42 the release of inflammatory mediators41,43 and subsequent neuron damage. In the ALS mouse model, immune cell infiltration into the CNS and overactivation of astrocytes and microglia are typically observed. To investigate whether neuroinflammation can be alleviated by in vivo self-assembled SOD1-siRNA, we used classical immunofluorescence markers, IBA1 and GFAP, to label microglia and astrocytes, respectively. While wild-type mice have a normal number of IBA+ and GFAP+ glial cells to maintain normal physiological functions and tissue homeostasis, Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice treated with CMV-RVG-scrR or CMV-siRNA showed highly activated abnormal form of microglia and astrocytes in the cerebral cortex (Supplementary Fig. 9B and C) and L3–L5 lumbar spinal cord (Fig. 4F and G and Supplementary Fig. 9A). In contrast, the activation of microglia and astrocytes was significantly reduced in transgenic mice treated with CMV-RVG-siRNA (Fig. 4F and G and Supplementary Fig. 9A–C). Subsequent ELISA analysis of inflammatory cytokines in the cortical and lumbar spinal cord tissues revealed a significant reduction in the levels of these cytokines (Supplementary Fig. 9D). These results indicate that in vivo self-assembled SOD1-siRNA effectively mitigates the overactivation of microglia and astrocytes in the CNS, resulting in the absence of obvious neuroinflammation.

Evaluation of the effects of IVSA-siRNA on spinal motor neurons and neuromuscular junction

Motor neurons are specialized nerve cells responsible for transmitting signals from the CNS to muscles, leading to movement. The neuromuscular junction (NMJ) is a critical structure facilitating the transmission of signals from motor neurons to muscles, prompting muscle contractions.44 Muscle atrophy caused by loss of NMJs and motor neurons is a characteristic feature of ALS.45 Given the age-dependent degeneration of motor neurons observed in Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice, we examined spinal motor neurons in the ventral horn of these mice following treatment with synthetic constructs. Choline acetyltransferase (ChAT) acts as a key enzyme in motor neurons, catalysing the production of acetylcholine in presynaptic neurons crucial for nerve signalling at the NMJs. By immunofluorescence staining of ChAT as a specific biomarker, motor neurons can be accurately distinguished from other types of neurons. In the ventral horn of lumbar spinal cords from wild-type mice, a notable presence of ChAT-positive motor neurons was observed, contrasting with the scarcity or absence of such cells in Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice treated with CMV-RVG-scrR or CMV-siRNA. Conversely, following a 10-week intravenous injection of CMV-RVG-siRNA, the count of ChAT-positive cells in the ventral horn of transgenic mice significantly increased, reaching levels comparable to those in wild-type mice (Fig. 5A and B and Supplementary Fig. 10A). Phenotypic evaluation of motor neurons via immunohistochemistry mirrored these findings. While immunohistochemical staining of ChAT did not reveal notable positive signals in the lumbar spinal cord sections of transgenic mice treated with CMV-RVG-scrR or CMV-siRNA, the count of ChAT-positive motor neurons was significantly increased in the transgenic mice treated with CMV-RVG-siRNA (Supplementary Fig. 10B).

Muscle denervation at the NMJ represents a characteristic hallmark of ALS.46,47 With disease progression, the NMJ undergoes degeneration and dysfunction, hindering the effective communication between motor neurons and muscles.44 This degeneration typically begins with synaptic transmission abnormalities at the NMJ, leading to subsequent degradation of motor neuron axons and the progressive weakening and atrophy of muscles. During analysis of NMJ number and axon terminal innervation at NMJ, the postsynaptic acetylcholine receptors (AChRs) are labelled with α-bungarotoxin (α-BTX), while the axons and axon terminals are labelled with antibodies to neurofilament light chain (NF-L) and Synapsin-1 (Syn), respectively. The colocalization of α-BTX, Syn and NF-L not only indicates the integrity of presynaptic and postsynaptic structures but also reflects the functional synergy of NMJ in signal transmission and muscle contraction. Muscle denervation in Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice was assessed through immunofluorescence staining of frozen sections of the gastrocnemius muscle with α-BTX, anti-NF-L and anti-Syn. In wild-type mice, a significant overlap was observed between α-BTX signals in the postsynaptic region and NF-L and Syn signals in the axonal part (Fig. 5C and D), indicating functional NMJ integrity. Conversely, transgenic mice treated with CMV-RVG-scrR or CMV-siRNA exhibited markedly reduced colocalization of α-BTX, Syn and NF-L signals (Fig. 5C and D), suggesting NMJ defects. However, the administration of CMV-RVG-siRNA substantially increased the NMJ count and enhanced axon terminal innervation at NMJ in transgenic mice (Fig. 5C and D). Overall, in vivo self-assembled SOD1-siRNA effectively ameliorated NMJ degeneration and axon terminal denervation in Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice.

Evaluation of the safety profiles of in vivo self-assembled SOD1-siRNA

To ensure the safety profile of in vivo self-assembled SOD1-siRNA and preclude the induction of side effects, immune responses or tissue damage, wild-type mice were intravenously injected with PBS, CMV-RVG-scrR, CMV-siRNA and CMV-RVG-siRNA at a dosage of 5 mg/kg every 2 days for a total of seven injections. After treatment, comprehensive evaluations were conducted on complete blood counts encompassing white blood cell (WBC), red blood cell (RBC) and platelet (PLT) levels, in addition to serum biochemical markers such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (CREA), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), total bilirubin (TBIL), albumin (ALB), alkaline phosphatase (ALP) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH). No significant changes in these metrics were observed after the administration of the synthetic constructs (Supplementary Fig. 11A and B). Furthermore, histological examination of liver, spleen and kidney sections post-treatment revealed no discernible tissue damage attributed to the administration of the synthetic constructs (Supplementary Fig. 11C).

Development of an AAV-based strategy for long-term treatment of ALS with IVSA-siRNA

Leveraging the strong neuronal tropism of AAV serotype 9 (AAV9), AAV9-based CRISPR-Cas9 gene therapy and AAV9-based shRNA have been shown to effectively target the pathogenic SOD1 protein. However, AAV9-based therapies often require invasive surgical procedures. We aimed to optimize the delivery vehicle of AAV-based gene therapy to achieve a less invasive therapeutic approach for ALS treatment. Given the strong liver tropism of AAV8, we incorporated the entire expression cassette of CMV-RVG-siRNA into an AAV8 vector fused with eGFP (referred to as AAV8-CMV-RVG-siRNA) and administered it via minimally invasive intravenous injection. We then evaluated whether AAV8-mediated expression of the CMV-RVG-siRNA construct would enable the sustained production of SOD1-siRNA-loaded sEVs in the liver and facilitate the delivery of sEV-encapsulated SOD1-siRNA to the spinal cord and cerebral cortex, thereby ensuring continuous SOD1 silencing and effective ALS treatment. In Week 4, Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice were intravenously administered AAV8-CMV-RVG-siRNA at a viral concentration of 5 × 1011 vg/ml with an injection volume of 100 μl. As a control, the CMV-siRNA expression cassette fused with eGFP was incorporated into an AAV9 vector (referred to as AAV9-CMV-siRNA) to facilitate neuronal expression of SOD1-siRNA. In Week 4, transgenic mice received intrathecal injections of AAV9-CMV-siRNA at the lumbar region (L3–L5) at a viral titre of 5 × 1012 vg/ml with an injection volume of 10 μl (Supplementary Fig. 12A). Another control group received intrathecal administration of Tofersen at 30, 60, 90 and 120 days at the lumbar region (L3–L5) at a concentration of 25 μg/μl and an injection volume of 10 μl (Supplementary Fig. 12A). Following treatment, improvements in symptoms and changes in SOD1 protein levels in Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice after intravenous administration of AAV8-CMV-RVG-siRNA were compared with those observed after intrathecal injections of AAV9-CMV-siRNA or Tofersen.

Using eGFP as a biomarker for tracking tissue expression of the AAV transgene, we observed that intrathecal injection of AAV9-CMV-siRNA resulted in robust eGFP expression primarily concentrated at the injection site in the L3–L5 lumbar region, gradually diminishing in the thoracic and cervical regions (Supplementary Fig. 12B). Specifically, within the lumbar region where eGFP expression was most prominent, AAV9 exhibited limited penetration into the anterior horn, displaying higher eGFP levels in the dorsal grey matter (Supplementary Fig. 12B). The distribution pattern of SOD1-siRNA mirrored the expression profile of AAV9, with SOD1-siRNA content decreasing progressively from the lumbar to thoracic and cervical regions before reaching the cerebral cortex, and no detectable SOD1-siRNA in the plasma (Supplementary Fig. 12C). Conversely, while eGFP fluorescence in the liver of transgenic mice increased continuously following AAV8 injection (Supplementary Fig. 12D), the levels of SOD1-siRNA markedly increased throughout the lumbar, thoracic, cervical and cortical regions and plasma post-AAV8 administration (Supplementary Fig. 12E). Furthermore, the SOD1-siRNA content in the brain regions and plasma after intravenous AAV8 injection was several orders of magnitude higher than that observed following AAV9 intrathecal injection (Supplementary Fig. 12E).

After monitoring changes in body weight and survival rate, Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice receiving intrathecal injection of AAV9-CMV-siRNA showed similar weight loss and reduced lifespan as the control group of PBS-treated transgenic mice (Fig. 6A and B). In comparison, intrathecal injection of Tofersen partially delayed this weight loss and slightly extended the lifespan of the transgenic mice (Fig. 6A and B). Remarkably, intravenous injection of AAV8-CMV-RVG-siRNA significantly delayed weight loss and markedly extended the lifespan of transgenic mice, with a median survival time of 200 days and the longest survival time reaching 236 days (Fig. 6A and B). Correspondingly, while motor balance ability and grip strength significantly declined in transgenic mice after intrathecal injection of AAV9-CMV-siRNA, intrathecal injection of Tofersen modestly improved behavioural performance in transgenic mice, whereas intravenous injection of AAV8-CMV-RVG-siRNA substantially slowed down the deterioration of motor balance ability and grip strength during disease progression (Fig. 6C and D). At themolecular level, while intrathecal injection of AAV9-CMV-siRNA or Tofersen had no significant effect on the protein and mRNA levels of SOD1 in the spinal cords, intravenous injection of AAV8-CMV-RVG-siRNA significantly reduced SOD1 expression at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 6E–G). In terms of neuroinflammation, there was a pronounced overactivation of astrocytes and microglia in the L3–L5 lumbar spinal cord of transgenic mice after intrathecal injection of AAV9-CMV-siRNA or Tofersen, whereas intravenous administration of AAV8-CMV-RVG-siRNA notably attenuated the activation of immune cells in these regions (Supplementary Fig. 13A and B).

Finally, we evaluated the enduring therapeutic impact of AAV-based self-assembled SOD1-siRNA on muscle atrophy, motor neuron loss and NMJ degeneration. While Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice treated with AAV9-CMV-siRNA or Tofersen exhibited noticeable muscle atrophy and a significant reduction in the CSA of the gastrocnemius muscle, administration of AAV8-CMV-RVG-siRNA substantially mitigated muscle atrophy and increased the CSA of muscle fibres in the gastrocnemius (Fig. 7A and B). Discussion

The persistent challenge in treating SOD1-associated ALS lies in the efficient delivery of therapeutic nucleic acids (e.g. siRNA or ASOs) across the BBB to achieve robust and cell-type-specific silencing of pathogenic SOD1 expression in motor neurons and glial cells within the relevant CNS regions. Current clinical strategies primarily rely on intrathecal administration of ASOs (e.g. Tofersen), which directly target mutant SOD1 mRNA. Although this approach has demonstrated clinical benefit, it necessitates repeated invasive lumbar punctures, carries risks of procedural complications and neurotoxicity at high doses and is limited by uneven and often suboptimal biodistribution throughout the spinal cord and brain parenchyma.14,48,49 Viral vector-based approaches, particularly AAVs, offer the potential for sustained expression but face considerable challenges, including pre-existing immunity in patients, limited cargo capacity that restricts complex genetic payloads, potential immunogenicity and neurotoxicity, re-dosing difficulties and broad tropism that can lead to off-target effects or insufficient transduction of key cell types.50-52 Our study addresses these fundamental challenges by leveraging the advantages of sEVs for nucleic acid delivery. sEVs are endogenous nanocarriers characterized by inherent biocompatibility, low immunogenicity and a natural ability to traverse biological barriers, including the BBB. We developed a synthetic biology-based strategy to generate and deliver in vivo self-assembled, sEV-encapsulated SOD1-siRNA to the spinal cord and cerebral cortex for ALS therapy. This approach circumvents the complexities and potential toxicity associated with in vitro sEV loading and purification. By engineering host cells in situ to produce and release sEVs containing the target siRNA, we present a potentially less invasive alternative to intrathecal injection and achieve more physiologically targeted delivery through the intrinsic homing capabilities of sEVs. Indeed, by exploiting the natural trafficking properties of sEVs, this strategy enables effective delivery of SOD1-siRNA specifically to the spinal cord and cerebral cortex, thereby silencing mutant SOD1 in Tg(SOD1G93A) transgenic mice. Compared to untreated controls, these mice showed significant improvement in key ALS phenotypes, including mitigation of weight loss, extended survival, improved motor function, reduced muscle atrophy and neuroinflammation and preservation of motor neuron integrity. Particularly, treatment with in vivo self-assembled SOD1-siRNA preserved motor neuron morphology and maintained the structural integrity of NMJs, highlighting its potential for early therapeutic intervention. Overall, this approach directly addresses the delivery inefficiencies that have long hindered gene therapy for CNS disorders such as ALS. Moreover, our in vivo sEV-based delivery platform represents a promising and versatile next-generation strategy for targeted gene silencing in the CNS, with potential applications extending beyond SOD1-ALS to other neurodegenerative diseases requiring precise nucleic acid delivery.

While the current findings are promising, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, therapeutic efficacy was rigorously evaluated only in the context of the SOD1G93A mutation. Although the modular design of our delivery platform allows for rapid adaptation to other SOD1 variants (e.g. A4V, G85R) via simple reconfiguration based on base-pairing principles, this broader applicability requires experimental validation. Future studies should systematically assess the delivery efficiency, silencing potency, therapeutic benefit and off-target effects of the reconfigured system in models harbouring other SOD1 mutations. Second, all in vivo results were obtained using the Tg(SOD1G93A) mouse model, which represents only one familial form of ALS and may not capture the full heterogeneity of sporadic ALS or other genetic variants. Third, although significant therapeutic benefits were observed, a more detailed quantification of siRNA delivery efficiency and SOD1 protein knockdown across distinct neuronal and glial populations in targeted CNS regions would yield deeper mechanistic insight. Addressing these limitations—particularly validation across multiple SOD1 variants and testing in more complex or humanized models—will be essential to advance this delivery platform toward clinical application.

In summary, this strategy effectively overcomes critical barriers of conventional gene therapies for SOD1-driven ALS and offers a compelling therapeutic avenue for future ALS interventions.
