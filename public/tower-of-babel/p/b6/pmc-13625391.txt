Acceptability and feasibility of digital gamified cognitive training intervention for preschool children with cancer-related cognitive late effects: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Tao Liu, Mingli Liao, Feisal Hussein Hasen, Chensong Zhang, Wong Cho Lee, Lin Mo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCognitive late effects are a common and serious complication of childhood cancer. This risk is particularly high in preschool-aged children, who are in a critical period of cognitive development and thus more susceptible to cognitive late effects. However, interventions targeting cognitive late effects in preschool children with cancer remain scarce and lack a strong evidence base.ObjectiveThis study aimed to explore the acceptability and feasibility of Cognifit, an innovative digital gamified cognitive training method, in improving cognitive late effects among preschool children aged 3–6 years with cancer, and to describe the determinants of its implementation.MethodA single-group, pre–post embedded mixed-methods design was employed to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, adherence, and preliminary effectiveness of CogniFit among preschool children with cancer. Feasibility, acceptability, and adherence were assessed using quantitative indicators and qualitative caregiver interviews. Preliminary effectiveness was evaluated using the Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function–Preschool Version (BRIEF-P), Sustained Attention Test, Working Memory Test, and Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory Version 4.0 (PedsQL™ 4.0). Quantitative and qualitative findings were integrated for analysis.ResultEighteen children and their caregivers were enrolled in the study; two participants withdrew, and 16 dyads were included in the final analysis. Quantitative findings demonstrated good feasibility and acceptability, with an intervention completion rate of 88.9%, session attendance rate of 81.7%, and duration adherence rate of 72.9%. Qualitative interviews showed that caregivers generally perceived the CogniFit program positively despite challenges related to regular participation and cultural adaptation. Preliminary effectiveness outcomes warrant further evaluation in larger controlled trials.ConclusionOur study confirmed the feasibility and acceptability of the Cognifit digital gamified intervention for preschool-aged children with cancer and their caregivers. The study underscores the importance of adapting digital gamified interventions to better suit vulnerable populations such as preschool-aged children with cancer and proposes corresponding improvement recommendations. Introduction

Cognitive late effects refer to progressive cognitive impairment resulting from the cancer itself and its treatments [1]. This represents a common and serious complication among pediatric cancer patients, with approximately 35–60% of childhood cancer survivors experiencing such symptoms [2]. Preschool-aged children are particularly vulnerable, as this developmental stage is characterized by rapid brain maturation and cognitive growth; exposure to cancer and its treatments during this critical period substantially increases the risk of cognitive late effects [3]. These impairments can affect multiple domains of functioning, including intellectual abilities, learning capacity, quality of life, and social relationships. Ultimately, cognitive late effects may compromise independent living in adulthood and lead to a sustained decline in overall quality of life [4].

Current interventions targeting cognitive late effects in preschool-aged pediatric cancer patients primarily rely on non-pharmacological approaches, including physical training, cognitive training, and psychotherapy [5–7]. These interventions have demonstrated modest improvements in children’s cognitive function. However, factors such as preschoolers’ age, personality, interests, and medication side effects constrain their treatment adherence and effectiveness. Given prerschool-aged childern’s high acceptance of digital technology and preference for games, gamified cognitive training is considered an ideal strategy for this group. Cognifit [8], a novel digital gamified training program, is a cognitive training software developed by Israeli psychologist Professor Breznitz. It offers a variety of adaptive-difficulty cognitive training mini-games and demonstrates greater stability in long-term transfer effects. It has been confirmed to improve cognitive function in children with obesity, congenital heart disease, and epilepsy [9, 10]. Although Cognifit shows potential for improving children’s cognitive function, its application is limited to school-age and adolescent patients. However, evidence regarding the feasibility and acceptability of digital gamified cognitive training in preschool-aged children with cancer remain limited, particularly in the real-world clinical setting. In addition, little is known about the implementation barriers and facilitators influencing sustained engagement among children and caregivers in this vulnerable population.

Meanwhile, single quantitative or qualitative studies struggle to deeply explore the experiences of pediatric patients and caregivers during training or objectively and comprehensively quantify training outcomes [11]. Mixed-methods research integrates multiple data sources, thereby enhancing understanding of implementation compared to relying solely on quantitative or qualitative data [11]. Concurrently, further research indicates that pragmatic clinical studies in the early stages of digital technology implementation have become essential to elucidate implementation outcomes and identify barriers and facilitators, thereby guiding future large-scale implementation efforts [12, 13].

Therefore, we conducted an embedded mixed-methods study to explore the feasibility and acceptability of Cognifit-based digital cognitive training among preschool-aged children with cancer. The study also aimed to the barriers and facilitating factors during its implementation, with the goal of guiding future protocol refinement.

Methods

This study employed a single-group pre–post embedded mixed-methods design without a separate control group. The trial was retrospectively registered in November 2024 after participant recruitment had begun in April 2024. This study was conducted at the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University in China, the largest pediatric cancer center in western China. Previous research indicates that young children, due to their limited language and cognitive expression abilities, struggle to accurately and comprehensively describe complex medical experiences, often relying on caregivers as primary information providers [14]. Therefore, this study employed semi-structured interviews with caregivers to gain deeper insights into the experiences, needs, and suggestions of pediatric patients during the training process, as well as any barriers and facilitators in implementing this digital gamified cognitive training. The GRAMMS (Good Reporting of A Mixed Methods Study) checklist was used to guide the mixed methods approach and reporting [15], thereby supporting the methodological quality and transparency of the study in line with established standards of mixed methods studies in health services research(Checklist 1).

Participants

The study participants consisted of preschool-aged children diagnosed with cancer and their caregivers. Recruitment began in April 2024. Children were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (a) Aged between 3 and 6 years; (b) Diagnosed with malignant tumors in accordance with the Chinese Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Pediatric Malignant Tumors; (c) Currently in the maintenance phase of treatment, receiving regular chemotherapy; (d) A Wechsler Preschool and Primary Scale of Intelligence - Fourth Edition (WPPSI-IV) score greater than 70, This threshold ensured that children, despite exhibiting cognitive late effects, retained sufficient cognitive capacity to engage with and complete the training program [16]. Caregivers were eligible for inclusion criteria if they expressed willingness to participate, provided written informed consent in the study, completed the intervention feasibility survey, and confirmed the children’s agreement to participate in the study.

Materials

Intervention

CogniFit is an online intervention developed by neuroscience experts to stimulate cognitive skills and provide personalized daily training programs [17]. This intervention trains multiple cognitive abilities, including memory, attention, executive function, reasoning, planning, mental agility, and coordination—through activities such as brain control exercises, memory games, puzzles, and brain teasers. The research team conducted a standardized face-to-face training session for caregivers before implementation. Training content included: (1) demonstration of game operation rules for each module using a study-provided tablet (iPad Air, 4th generation), and (2) troubleshooting of common technical issues, including account login, game selection, interface navigation, and basic problem resolution.

Children were enrolled during active cancer treatment. The intervention was initiated during hospitalization using devices provided by the department and was subsequently continued at home after discharge under caregiver supervision via caregivers’ smartphones. To monitor adherence and address technical difficulties throughout the intervention period, the research team established a WeChat communication group for ongoing support and troubleshooting.

Training was conducted five times per week, with each session lasting 30–45 min. Researchers set the difficulty level of training modules, introducing new game types every four weeks. Children may advance to the next difficulty level once they achieve 80% task accuracy. The training cycle spans 8 weeks. The intervention content is detailed in Table 1 below.

To enhance participation, we established a WeChat group for communication with caregivers. The researchers sent check-in messages in the group daily. After caregivers supervised their children to complete the daily training each day, they would check in within the WeChat group. Check-ins were based on caregiver self-report of training completion, as the CogniFit platform did not provide real-time monitoring of session completion. To enhance the reliability of these self-reports, caregivers were asked to upload a screenshot of the completed training session from the Cognifit app, along with the session duration, during their daily check-in. When screenshots could not be uploaded on the same day, caregivers were allowed to submit them within 24–48 h; if screenshots remained unavailable after this period, self-reported completion data were retained and recorded, and missing screenshots were documented. As the cumulative number of check-ins increases, children will be eligible to redeem a higher quantity of check-ins for small gifts of escalating value at the conclusion of the study.

Caregivers may freely choose their preferred interview method (via telephone, video call, or in-person at the hospital or at home). This approach may increase response rates and provide an interview format that caregivers find comfortable [18]. The interview plan lasted approximately half an hour. Interviews averaged 36 min in duration ranging from 25 to 42 min. Two interviews were conducted by telephone, and 14 were conducted face-to-face including 6 at caregivers’ homes and 8 at hospitals.

Interview data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step phenomenological method [19]: (1) reading all transcribed interviews repeatedly to gain a sense of the whole; (2) extracting significant statements relevant to the phenomenon under study; (3) formulating meanings for each significant statement; (4) clustering formulated meanings into themes; (5) integrating themes into an exhaustive description of the phenomenon; (6) reducing the exhaustive description to its essential structure; and (7) returning to caregivers to validate findings and ensure congruence with their lived experiences. To enhance rigor, two researchers independently coded a subset of transcripts and discussed discrepancies until consensus was reached. The final thematic structure was then reviewed and refined through peer debriefing.

The primary interviewer was a doctoral candidate at a university in China. He held a master’s degree in clinical pediatric nursing and had previously received training in qualitative research methods. He was supported by a clinical psychologist with extensive experience in qualitative research and intervention studies focued at alleviating psychological, social adaptation, and cognitive difficulties among children with cancer. To minimize interviewer bias, the interviewer maintained a non-directive stance, used open-ended questions and completed reflective journaling after each interview. These reflections were discussed with the research team to identify potential personal biases or blind spots and to improve the quality of subsequent interviews. Two researchers independently coded the transcripts and resolved discrepancies through consensus to ensure interpretive consistency. The interviewer was not involved in participant recruitment or intervention delivery.

Outcome indicator

Feasibility was assessed using four indicators. The recruitment rate was defined as the proportion of eligible children who enrolled in the study. The completion rate was defined as the proportion of enrolled children who completed the full 8-week intervention and all post-intervention assessments. Dropout rate was defined as the proportion of enrolled children who discontinued the intervention before completion, with reasons documented. Full attendance rate was defined as the proportion of children who completed all 40 prescribed training sessions.

Acceptability was assessed using two indicators: the refusal rate and the System Usability Scale (SUS). The refusal rate was defined as the proportion of eligible children who declined participation, with reasons for refusal recorded when available. In addition, SUS was used to measure caregivers’ subjective acceptability of the CogniFit platform. This standardized questionnaire comprises 10 items rated on a 5-point Likert scale. The Chinese version of SUS was translated and validated by Wang et al. [20], demonstrating good reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.84) and sensitivity to differences in usage frequency. After analyzing SUS scores, a total score ranging from 0 to 100 is calculated. According to the Curved Grading Scale (CGS) [21], SUS scores are classified into ten grades: A (100.0-89.9), B(80.0-74.9), C (71.0-64.9), D (52.0–61.9), and F (0.0–51.9).

Adherence was assessed using multiple indicators. Session attendance adherence was defined as the proportion of prescribed training sessions completed relative to the total number of sessions (40 sessions over 8 weeks). Session duration adherence was defined as the proportion of actual training minutes completed relative to the expected total minutes (30–45 min per session). Training duration categories were established to describe individual-level adherence: high adherence, defined as more than 45 min per session; good adherence, defined as less than 30–45 min per session; and insufficient duration, defined as less than 30 min per session. Composite adherence was calculated as the mean of session attendance adherence and session duration adherence [22].

Children with cancer participated in the Working Memory Test and the Sustained Attention Test. Additionally, the results from parent-reported measures (executive function, quality of life) were assessed through parent-reported surveys. The specific measurements are shown in Table 2.

Procedure

This study included 18 children, which was considered an appropriate sample size for identifying key themes and achieving data saturation [27]. Children and their caregivers were recruited through purposive sampling from the Chongqing Medical University Children’s Hospital and Chongqing based patient organizations. Caregivers recruited through the hospital were informed about the study by trained nurses and provided written informed consent. For recruitment through patient organizations, caregivers were informed caregivers about the study via social media, email, and informational brochures. Interested caregivers received further details and consent forms after contacting the research team. After signed informed consent forms were received, the research team administered standardized assessment tools to evaluate children’s attention and working memory, while caregivers completed questionnaires. After completion of the intervention, children underwent reassessment within two weeks, and an interview was scheduled. After the interview, caregivers received a $10 gift card.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using descriptive statistics. Due to the small sample size, no inferential significance tests were conducted. The interview recordings were imported into NVivo 14.0 software for qualitative data analysis. Reflexive thematic analysis was employed to analyze the data. The first author (Liu) analyzed and coded the interview content by following the steps including familiarizing with the data, generating initial codes, searching for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and writing the report. To support coding adequacy and data richness, two researchers Liao and Mo, independently coded the first interview. Following this, they discussed this interview to determine relevant codes. The first author then coded all the remaining interviews and generated themes. Some excerpts in this article have been translated from Chinese into English and edited to enhance readability.

Ethical considerations

The research protocol received approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (Approval No. 2022-288-1) prior to the commencement of the study. The legal guardians of all children provided written informed consent before the baseline assessment, and the children also gave their assent to participate.

Qualitative data

Qualitative analysis identified three primary themes: “Solid structure and engagement”, “It fits my child”, “Barriers and facilitators to training completion”. All caregivers mentioned each of these themes. Details of the three themes and their subthemes are provided below.

Theme 1: solid structure and engagement

This theme reflects caregivers’ experience with Cognifit’s structure, including training methods and logical organization.

Subtheme 1: a logical structure in cognifit

Overall, caregivers highly valued Cognifit’s logical structure of “tutorial guidance → formal training → adaptive adjustment.” The initial tutorial familiarizes children with the interface and rules in a low-pressure manner; formal training enhances cognitive functions through mini games; and the adaptive difficulty mechanism was repeatedly cited as being key to sustaining engagement.

“This software (Cognifit) is quite intelligent—it automatically adjusts the difficulty based on the child’s performance, preventing them from getting bored too quickly.” (P16).

“(Cognifit) is quite logical, gradually increasing difficulty as the child becomes familiar with it… I see the child really respond well to this approach.” (P10).

Subtheme 2: interesting training methods, with room for improvement

Most caregivers believed Cognifit was suitable for preschool-aged children with cancer. However, some caregivers of younger children feel Cognifit was better suited for older children, as certain elements or rules were too complex for preschoolers.

“Cognifit is actually quite appealing… the colors, the approach—both my child and I like how it looks… but some of the more complex games aren’t suitable for him.” (P7).

When asked about potential improvements to Cognifit, one caregiver suggested adding more game modules suitable for preschoolers: “I feel there aren’t enough games tailored for children in their age group… Some games are simply too complex for them to grasp, like Sudoku… Personally, I recommend incorporating more age-appropriate mini-games.” (P9).

Subtheme 3. you can train anytime and anywhere you like

Caregivers discussed the advantages of digital cognitive training compared with traditional cognitive training. Overall, most of them preferred Cognifit, citing benefits such as privacy and convenience.

“You can do Cognifit at home, which is much simpler than having to travel far to another location and make appointments… Here, you just need to log in.” (P14).

“Before, we had to go to the hospital for training sessions, which took up almost an entire day including the round trip—it was hard to stick with. This software makes it so much more convenient.” (P11).

Theme 2: it fits my child

This theme reflects caregivers’ experiences with Cognifit’s therapeutic content. They found the content engaging, aligned with children’s interests, and suitable for use by children, resulting in a positive impact.

Subtheme 1: they seem to love it

Overall, Cognifit was reported to be acceptable to preschool children with cancer and their caregivers, who felt that the children enjoyed the gamified training content within the program. Most respondents (12/16) indicated that they would continue using the program.

“The child really enjoys this app. Sometimes when we don’t have time to guide him, he can play it on his own for over half an hour.” (P13).

“Sometimes when we forget to give her the phone to play this (Cognifit), she’ll remind us. She gets really immersed during the training sessions.” (P6).

Subtheme 2: cognifit enhanced my child’s performance.

Most caregivers reported observing improvements in cognitive functions, such as attention following completion of the Cognifit program.

“I feel like my child has shown some improvement. For instance, their speech is no longer like before, it is no longer unclear or difficult to understand. It’s much better now, a definite positive change.” (P2).

“I feel like their attention span has improved. Previously, teachers mentioned that they would zone out during class.” (P13).

Theme 3: barriers and facilitators to training completion

While most preschoolers were highly motivated to complete Cognifit because they found the program engaging, some encountered difficulties. This theme reflects factors that limited training completion among children in this group, as well as factors that increased motivation.

Subtheme 1: too busy to remember the training

Caregivers identified several barriers to completing Cognifit. As caregivers juggled multiple responsibilities, including their child’s cancer treatment, their own studies, and family obligations, some reported occasionally forgetting to complete training because of competing demands. However, even when children attended the scheduled sessions, caregivers noted that actual training duration often fell short of the expected 30–45 min.

“She had friends visiting sometimes, so she stopped playing halfway through.” (P2).

“Sometimes she would start the game but then get distracted by something else in the room and not finish the full session.” (P8).

“Most of the training can still be completed, but it requires an hour of practice every day. Sometimes unexpected matters come up, and then there’s no training.” (P13).

“Kids playing these kinds of games always lack patience. They might get bored after just a day or two, and they won’t listen to what adults say.” (P6).

Subtheme 2: usability challenges during training

Caregivers reported that preschoolers, due to limited cognitive development, experienced significant difficulties when tackling high-difficulty CCT tasks or comprehending abstract game rules. This difficulty level serves as a primary reason for children’s refusal to participate. The resulting frustration often triggered training fatigue and behavioral resistance, thereby undermining intervention compliance.

“Some of the games in Cognifit are too difficult for him. When he can’t figure them out, he sometimes gets frustrated and stops playing.” (P10).

“Sometimes he would keep playing the same game and refuse to try new ones because he didn’t fully understand the rules of some of those games. Maybe it’s because he is young, and his comprehension ability is not yet well developed.” (P12).

Although most of Cognifit’s training modules have been localized into Chinese, translation discrepancies and the retention of English names for certain games reduced children’s willingness to use the platform. Caregivers explicitly stated that: “fully localized training content would better align with the needs of Chinese children.”

“Some parts of Cognifit are still in English, and we can’t understand them, so we usually don’t choose that game… Next time, we can use our own Chinese app for training, right?” (P3).

Subtheme 3: it works better if i get guidance

Caregivers indicated that more detailed guidance from the research team might enhance the effectiveness of the intervention.

“I really wish the researchers would give me more guidance… We’re not very familiar with this technology… So sometimes it does get pushed to the bottom of the list.” (P1).

“I really need guidance from professionals—for instance, what the latest research findings are and how to achieve the best results.” (P11).

Subtheme 4. contact with research groups can increase motivation

Caregivers also believed that contact and reminders from research team could significantly boost engagement.

“Setting up a WeChat group for check-ins is helpful. Sometimes I forget about the training, but once reminded, I hurry to complete it.” (P19).

“Seeing everyone else check in and complete their tasks in the group chat made me feel a sense of urgency, and that’s how I kept going.” (P6).

Discussion

This study investigated the acceptability and feasibility of the Cognifit program among preschool children with cancer in China. Importantly, this study was designed as a feasibility and acceptability investigation rather than an efficacy trial, and the findings were interpreted accordingly.

Regarding acceptability, SUS scores indicated that caregivers rated the Cognifit system as Grade B, suggesting high acceptability. The qualitative findings also confirmed that digital gamified cognitive training was well received by both children and caregivers due to its structural design, engaging interactive features, and flexible mode of use. The logical structure of the Cognifit program, characterized by the tutorial guidance, formal training, and adaptive difficulty adjustment, was repeatedly identified as a key facilitator of engagement. This structure aligns with the scaffolding design principles proposed by Hardy et al. [28], which emphasize gradual task progression to enhance participation among children. By incorporating short, targeted and progressively challenging game-based tasks, Cognifit appears to leverage preschool children’s natural curiosity about games, thereby supporting sustained engagement during training.

On the other hand, the digital training mode overcomes the spatial and temporal limitations of traditional interventions. Its on-demand and flexible accessibility make this training mode not only suitable for both home and hospital settings, while also enabling access for population in remote areas, with limited medical resources, demonstrating good cost-effective and practical feasibility. However, it is important to acknowledge that a considerable number of invited children refused to participate (42.31%), and two children (11.11%) withdrew after the first week, indicating that the intervention still faces barriers in engaging the entire target population. This pattern is consistent with the findings of Heynsbergh et al. (2018), who reported that poor recruitment and attrition were frequently attributed to carers being too busy to participate, substantial caregiving commitments, and preference for face-to-face over web-based delivery modes and concerns about children’s screen time and gaming exposure [29].

Regarding feasibility, 16 children (88.89%) completed the 8-week program, and 7 children (38.89%) attended all 40 sessions without missing any sessions. In terms of adherence, the session attendance rate was 81.67%, and the composite adherence rate was 77.28%, suggesting that most children who started the program were able to complete the required training. Qualitative findings further supported and helped explain these quantitative results, as caregivers reported that the program’s logical structure, engaging gamified content, and adaptive difficulty mechanism were key facilitators of sustained engagement. This finding aligns with prior research demonstrating the feasibility of digital gamified cognitive training for preschool children [30]. Contrary to the high attrition rates reported elsewhere in the literature [31], the present study observed a low dropout rate. This phenomenon may be attributed to multiple factors. First, structural support within the implementation setting represented a critical distinction. Although part of the intervention was still delivered in a home-based setting, it differed from previous studies that have predominantly relied on completely unsupervised, home-based autonomous use models [32], as caregivers received guidance and supervision prior to the intervention, including fixed schedules, caregiver supervision, and technical support [33]. Similarly, Fowles et al. [34] observed among young children, digitally delivered interventions in supervised settings demonstrate superior retention rates compared with unsupervised, home-based implementations. Additionally, the check-in and reward system employed in this study may have promoted sustained participation through behavioral reinforcement mechanisms. Lewis et al. confirmed in their review that tangible and monetary rewards are particularly effective in pediatric populations [35].

Although no inferential statistical analyses were conducted, descriptive data suggested favorable trends in executive function, attention, and quality of life measures following the intervention. Specifically, children’s test results showed improvements in inhibitory control, cognitive flexibility, planning and organization, as well as social functioning and school functioning, These outcomes are consistent with the findings of Conklin et al. [36]. Qualitative reports from caregivers further supported these results, describing enhancements in their children’s attention, memory, and task engagement in daily life. Although these parent-reported outcomes may be subject to expectancy bias, as caregivers were aware of their child’s participation in the intervention, they offer complementary information that standardized scales may struggle to capture, particularly in reflecting fluctuations in children’s cognitive abilities and subjective daily experiences [37]. Furthermore, most caregivers indicated that they needed more technical guidance from researchers. This pattern aligns with the findings of Boström et al. [38]. Future research should explicitly address this key implementation requirement, including embedding structured caregiver training and supervision into intervention protocols and establishing support channels, thereby enhancing feasibility and acceptability across the broader target population. Specifically, such cognitive training should be incorporated into routine adjuvant treatment procedures. A medical team could develop personalized training plans based on the chemotherapy cycles and the cognitive baseline of the children, and conduct initial operational training and adherence supervision during the children’s hospital stays, with the aim of improving their cognitive function and long-term quality of life [39].

The mixed-method findings reveals the feasibility characteristics and optimization directions of digital gamified cognitive training in real-world settings. Firstly, although 93.75% of caregivers acknowledged the convenience of Cognifit, “forgetfulness” and “difficulty” factors were identified as barriers that negatively affected training adherence. A meta-analysis by Lustria et al. [40]. also showed that digital interventions lacking proactive reminders experienced a 58% decrease in adherence over six weeks. The underlying cause is not merely a time management issue but rather a depletion of attentional resources among caregivers as they cope with the multiple pressures of cancer treatment, family, and work [41]. More fundamentally, it is a matter of insufficient caregiver empowerment.” As pointed out by Halluin et al. [42]., parents are the decisive factor in implementing digital interventions for children, and without their participation, the effectiveness of digital interventions cannot be fully realized.

Lastly, this study also reveals cultural differences as another obstacle in the use of digital gamified cognitive training. As a Western software, Cognifit was not fully adapted to the cognitive characteristics of Chinese children during its localization process. Some English game names and stimuli inconsistent with local culture weakened the willingness to use it. This is not merely a language translation issue but rather a problem of cultural usability discontinuity, as emphasized by Resnicow et al. [43]. Health interventions need to achieve dual adaptation in terms of surface-level language and underlying values. Giordani et al.‘s digital intervention [44] for children in Uganda similarly corroborates this viewpoint.

Based on the current evidence, digital gamified cognitive training appears to be a promising approach for improving cognitive late effects in children with cancer; however, its effectiveness still needs to be confirmed through large-scale randomized controlled trials [45]. Therefore, future studies are recommended to enhance caregiver support through structured empowerment programs. In addition to health education and regular online consultations, brief onboarding sessions, instructional videos, and troubleshooting resources may improve caregivers’ confidence in supervising training activities [46]. Automated reminder systems, calendar synchronization, and personalized notifications can help address forgetfulness, which is a key barrier to adherence [47]. Furthermore, integrating progress-tracking dashboards and milestone-based reward mechanisms may further enhance children’s motivation and sustained engagement [48]. Second, culturally adapted design should be prioritized in accordance with established cultural adaptation frameworks, such as the process proposed by Naderbagi et al. [49]. Beyond language translation, game elements, including characters, visual design, reward systems, and training scenarios, should be tailored to the sociocultural context of China. During the development process, cognitive interviews and usability testing involving children, caregivers, and healthcare professionals should be conducted to ensure the ecological validity and cultural relevance of the intervention [50]. Third, the intervention should be integrated into routine pediatric oncology care pathways [51]. Healthcare professionals can play a key role in assessing children’s baseline cognitive function, developing individualized training plans according to treatment stages, and providing ongoing monitoring and technical support [52]. Data-driven approaches, such as machine learning algorithms, may further help identify children at risk of poor adherence and enable timely delivery of personalized motivational messages or targeted support [53, 54]. Finally, future research should extend beyond efficacy evaluation to include broader implementation outcomes, such as long-term adherence, caregiver burden, cost-effectiveness, and scalability across diverse healthcare settings [55, 56].

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include the use of a mixed-methods approach, which allowed for the collection of data from both children with cancer and their caregivers, thereby providing a deeper and more comprehensive understanding of the application of digital game-based cognitive training in pediatric oncology. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted at a single center and was limited in sample size and scope. At this early stage, a narrower focus was necessary to assess feasibility and to obtain in-depth feedback from caregivers to inform subsequent refinement of the application. Second, the first author was involved in participant recruitment, data collection, and the conduct of interviews, which may have introduced social desirability bias [57]. According to two component models of socially desirable responding, such bias may reflect impression management, whereby caregivers consciously present favorable responses, as well as self-deceptive enhancement. Therefore, caregivers may have been more inclined to provide positive responses during interviews. However, caregivers also shared a substantial amount of critical feedback, suggesting that the extent of this bias was limited. In addition, the first author received training in communication skills, which may have facilitated more open and detailed disclosure by caregivers. Finally, as this pilot study focused on the acceptability and feasibility of Cognifit, no control group was included. Future randomized controlled trials are needed to evaluate its efficacy.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrated the acceptability and feasibility of Cognifit, However, as a feasibility study, it does not support firm conclusions about the intervention’s effectiveness. The findings also highlight the need to tailor this intervention to better accommodate the needs of vulnerable populations. These results provide valuable guidance for future feasibility and pragmatic trials and contribute to advancing knowledge on the application of digital game-based cognitive training for improve outcomes in children with special healthcare needs. Future research should rigorously evaluate the Cognifit application in prospective clinical trials.
