Hyperspectral imaging of grains uncovers the genetic architecture of nitrogen response of development in bread wheat
Authors: Qiang Liu, Yinyin Zhang, Jiawei Shi, Wanneng Yang, Hui Feng, Weijuan Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractUnraveling the genetic architecture of nitrogen response of development is critical for improving wheat productivity while reducing nitrogen inputs. In this study, hyperspectral imaging (HSI) was applied to wheat grains obtained from nitrogen-deficient and normal conditions, combined with genome-wide association studies (GWAS), to investigate the nitrogen response of development in a diverse wheat panel. The 1,792 i-traits were acquired via hyperspectral imaging system, which reflect detailed phenotypic assessments of wheat development, capturing subtle variations in nitrogen response. A total of 3,556 significant loci and 3,648 candidate genes were identified. Key candidate genes involved in nitrogen uptake and utilization were identified by integrating agronomic traits with i-traits, including TaARE1-7A, TaPTR9-7B, TaNAR2.1, and Rht-B1. This approach underscores the potential of combining HSI on grains with GWAS to dissect complex traits like nitrogen response, offering valuable genetic insights for breeding nitrogen-efficient wheat varieties and enhancing sustainability in crop production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11032-025-01609-6.Keywords: Bread wheat, Hyperspectral imaging, Nitrogen use efficiency, Genome-wide association studies

Introduction

Common wheat (Triticum aestivum) is one of the most widespread staple crops worldwide, serving as a primary source of calories and protein for human populations (Grote et al. 2021). As demographic growth and climate change continue to impose pressure on agricultural systems, wheat increasingly plays a central role in global food security due to its adaptability to diverse environments (Shiferaw et al. 2013). Wheat breeding programs have become crucial for improving yields, quality, and stress tolerance (Senapati et al. 2022). Wheat is a genetically allohexaploid species with three distinct sub genomes (A, B, and D), exhibiting greater genomic plasticity, which contributes to its ability to adapt to environmental stresses ((IWGSC) et al. 2017; Walkowiak et al. 2020; Jia et al. 2023). The rich genetic diversity within the wheat population provides a foundation for exploring beneficial alleles related to agronomic performance (Pang et al. 2024), including nutrient uptake and stress responses, which are vital for wheat improvement in a changing world (Li et al. 2022). Nitrogen (N), an essential macronutrient involved in fundamental plant processes such as photosynthesis, protein synthesis, and overall growth. Despite its importance, nitrogen use efficiency (NUE) in wheat and other crops remains low, leading to substantial nitrogen losses through leaching and volatilization, which contribute to environmental pollution (Hirel et al. 2011; Silva et al. 2020). Therefore, improving NUE in wheat is a critical breeding goal for enhancing productivity while reducing environmental impacts.

In wheat, nitrogen is primarily absorbed as nitrate (NO₃⁻) and ammonium (NH₄⁺) through nitrate transporters (NRTs) and ammonium transporters (AMTs), respectively (Crawford 1995; Orsel et al. 2006; Ludewig et al. 2007). Nitrate is reduced to nitrite by nitrate reductase, followed by its conversion to ammonium by nitrite reductase (Campbell 1999). The Glutamine Synthetase – Glutamine Oxoglutarate Aminotransferase (GS-GOGAT) pathway then incorporates ammonium into amino acids, forming the basis for protein biosynthesis and other nitrogen-containing compounds crucial for yield quality (Lea and Miflin 2011). NIN-like proteins (NLPs) are master regulators of nitrogen-responsive genes, coordinating nitrogen metabolism with plant growth (Liu et al. 2017).

Nitrogen uptake, assimilation, and response are regulated by hormonal networks and signaling pathways. Cytokinins communicate nitrogen availability and regulate long-distance nitrogen signaling (Takei et al. 2002; Ruffel et al. 2011), while auxins modulate root system architecture (RSA) in response to nitrogen availability, enhancing nitrogen absorption (Ötvös et al. 2021). Abscisic acid (ABA), associated with stress responses, regulates nitrogen metabolism during deficiency by modulating nitrogen-responsive genes (Signora et al. 2001). Additionally, gibberellins (GAs) interact with nitrogen levels, reducing shoot growth under low-nitrogen conditions to conserve resources (Camut et al. 2021).

Several studies have demonstrated that the genetic architecture of NUE is influenced by multiple small-effect quantitative trait loci (QTLs), which are often context-specific (He et al. 2020; Rakotoson et al. 2021; Shi et al. 2022). A recent research has highlighted the importance of integrating high-throughput phenotyping (HTP) technologies with genomic studies to accelerate the discovery of genes related to nitrogen responses or NUE in crops (Ding et al. 2023).

One of the most promising HTP technologies is hyperspectral imaging (HSI). It captures detailed spectral information spanning the visible to near-infrared range by scanning plant surfaces. In contrast to conventional phenotypic measurement techniques, HSI provides a noninvasive, rapid, high-throughput, and remote method for plant phenotyping (Sarić et al. 2022). It also provides high spatial resolution, enabling the capture of detailed phenotypic information and significantly improving the efficiency and accuracy of phenomics research. Since HSI can record both spatial and spectral data, it is capable of extracting structural and developmental information from plants (Bergsträsser et al. 2015). Currently, HSI technology is primarily utilized for plant species identification, metabolite content detection, and assessing plant responses to environmental stress (Mishra et al. 2017; Caporaso et al. 2018a; Liu et al. 2021). The near-infrared spectral regions (970–980 nm, 1200–1300 nm) are characterized associated with protein content as with high vibrational-frequency absorption for chemical bonds such as C–H, O–H, and N–H in protein (Weyer and Workman 2007). The range of 1050–2500 nm have good performance on moisture content prediction (Xu et al. 2019).

However, HSI data presents several challenges in practical applications, particularly in data analysis. The data generated by HSI imaging is typically high-dimensional and redundant, making it difficult to extract meaningful information (Burger and Gowen 2011). Traditional analyzing methods are often inadequate for handling such complex data structures, which necessitates the use of more advanced computational techniques and narrow-down approaches, such as machine learning methods (Alhayani and Ilhan 2017; Audebert et al. 2019; Su 2022). These methods can identify patterns within the data and perform dimensionality reduction, allowing for the extraction of valuable biological information and its correlation with crop genetic background, environmental factors, and other relevant variables (Feng et al. 2025). For instance, dimensionality reduction techniques such as principal component analysis (PCA) and independent component analysis (ICA) can effectively reduce data dimensionality while preserving key information (Jiang et al. 2018). Additionally, machine learning algorithms, including support vector machines (SVM), random forests (RF), and convolutional neural networks (CNN), are widely applied in the classification and regression analysis of HSI data (Sheykhmousa et al. 2020; Sothe et al. 2020; Kaul and Raina 2022; Khan et al. 2022).

When combined with GWAS, it enables the identification of single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) associated with nitrogen-responsive i-traits. However, the predominance of small-effect QTLs within these traits adds further complexity, complicating the extraction of meaningful biological insights. The integration and exploration of such vast, intricate datasets remain a critical bottleneck, requiring innovative strategies to effectively uncover the genetic and phenotypic underpinnings of nitrogen responses in wheat.

In this study, HSI was applied to harvested grains from a diverse panel of wheat accessions grown under nitrogen-deficient (low nitrogen, LN) and nitrogen-sufficient (high nitrogen, HN) conditions. Reflectance data are captured, and rich spectral feature data sets are generated. Dimensionality of the data was reduced using PCA and successive projection algorithm (SPA) to select key hyperspectral traits (i-traits) informative for nitrogen responses. These traits were then integrated with GWAS to identify candidate genes associated with nitrogen response. The candidate genes were filtered with differential expression information in response to nitrogen levels which enabled narrowing down of functionally relevant genes involved in NUE. Networks of candidate genes and networks of key traits were constructed to transform the extensive identification data into accessible information. Our focus on grain-level HSI, rather than canopy-level traits, provides unique insights into the genetic architecture of nitrogen response of development during grain filling—a critical stage for yield and quality. This study pioneers the direct integration of HSI-derived traits with GWAS to investigate nitrogen responses, offering a novel methodology for precision breeding and enhancing NUE in wheat.

Materials and methods

Materials

The wheat germplasms are derived from a wheat diversity sequencing panel (Niu et al. 2023) and were evaluated under LN and HN conditions. For the LN treatment, the soil nitrogen content was set at 20 mg/kg, with a nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (N:P:K) ratio of 2:10:15 (g/100 kg of soil). This treatment involved the application of 4.4 g of urea, 19 g of mono-potassium phosphate (KH₂PO₄), and 13 g of potassium oxide (K₂O) per 100 kg of soil. In contrast, the HN treatment followed standard agricultural practices, with soil nitrogen content increased to 100 mg/kg and an N:P:K ratio of 10:10:15 (g/100 kg of soil). This HN treatment applied 22 g of urea, 19 g of mono-potassium phosphate (KH₂PO₄), and 13 g of potassium oxide (K₂O) per 100 kg of soil. The experimental design ensured that the two nitrogen treatments differed only in nitrogen levels, while other essential nutrients remained the same, allowing for the evaluation of the effects of nitrogen on plant growth and development. The seeds were sown on October 20, 2021, at the Crop Phenotyping Platform of Huazhong Agricultural University in Wuhan, China. The HSI data of the wheat grains were collected in October 2021.

Acquisition of agronomic traits

The number of tillers, panicles, and root traits were manually measured (Fig. 1a, b). The primary panicle was defined as the largest panicle on each plant. After oven-drying, the weight of the shoots was recorded. Root weight was measured after washing off the attached soil and oven-drying the roots. Grain phenotypes were assessed using a seed analyzer to ensure precision and consistency. A total of 18 agronomic traits were collected. The collected traits included tiller number (TN), aboveground dry weight (SW), number of panicles per plant (PNPP), primary panicle weight (PW), non-primary panicle weight (NPW), total panicle weight (TPW), grain number (GN), filled grain number (FGN), grain set rate (GST), grain length (GL), grain length standard deviation (GL-sd), grain width (GW), grain width standard deviation (GW-sd), grain length–width ratio (LWR), length–width ratio standard deviation (LWR-sd), thousand kernel weight (TKW), total kernel weight (KW), and root dry weight (RW).

Acquisition of hyperspectral image

The hyperspectral imaging system (Fig. 1c) used in this study (Headwall Photonics, USA) consisted of two components: a visible hyperspectral imaging system and a near-infrared hyperspectral imaging system. The system comprised hardware and system software.

The hardware included a hyperspectral camera, halogen lamps, a motorized translation stage, a standard whiteboard, and a computer. The visible hyperspectral camera had an imaging range of 400–1000 nm, covering 314 spectral bands with a spectral resolution of 1.91 nm. The near-infrared hyperspectral camera operated within 900–1700 nm, encompassing 172 spectral bands with a spectral resolution of 4.65 nm. The halogen lamps, each with a power of 150 W, were positioned near the hyperspectral cameras to provide a stable line-array light source. Both the divergence angle and intensity of the light source were adjustable. The motorized translation stage, with a speed range of 2 mm/s, facilitated sample movement. A standard whiteboard was used for calibration, while a computer controlled the translation stage, managed the hyperspectral cameras, and handled data acquisition and storage. To ensure spectral data stability, the entire system was placed in a sealed darkroom, with the halogen lamp as the sole light source during the experiment.

The system was equipped with proprietary image acquisition software, which included modules for attribute settings and image visualization. The attribute settings module allowed adjustments to the imaging field of view, translation stage speed, whiteboard calibration, camera exposure time, and image acquisition. The image visualization module enabled real-time observation during the image acquisition process.

Methods

Spectral data preprocess

The process of extracting spectral reflectance from binary data is shown in Fig. 1d. Initially, binary data streams were reorganized to generate full-spectrum images for each sample. Image segmentation, an essential step in spectral image processing, was performed using the OTSU to binarize the wheat grain images. The binary wheat grain images were then masked with spectral images across all bands, resulting in masked images that contained hyperspectral data for wheat grains. The average reflectance for each band was subsequently calculated from these images.

Multiple preprocessing operations were applied to mitigate the influences of sample variations, light scattering, and baseline drift. The preprocessing algorithms included first derivative (D1st), second derivative (D2nd), and logarithmic transformation algorithms (Fig. 1e).

Dimensionality reduction analysis

To further explore the potential of i-traits in wheat nitrogen responses, PCA and SPA were employed for dimensionality reduction. PCA is a dimensionality reduction technique that generates new, unrelated features from an existing set of features. These new features are called principal components and are linear combinations of the original features. Arranged in descending order by the amount of variance they capture, principal components allow for dimensionality reduction by selecting only the first few components that retain most of the variance present in the original data. By discarding components with less variance, the dimensionality can be reduced while retaining as much of the original data information as possible.

SPA is a deterministic search technique known for its repeatable results and excellent performance in selecting verification sets. The algorithm identifies the wavelength with the highest projection as the characteristic wavelength, reducing the collinearity effect in the original input data. The final characteristic wavelength is then selected according to the modified model, ensuring minimal redundancy and reduced collinearity in the selected variable set.

Genome-wide association studies

GWAS were conducted via GCTA version 1.94.1 (Yang et al. 2011). Suggestive cut-off of P-value = 1.0 × 10–5 was applied to define significant associations. SNPs above the suggestive cut-off within 10 Mb were merged and represented by the most significant SNP (the representative SNP). The candidate genes were selected as 4 genes that closet to the representative SNP for each significant peak extracted via bedtools version 2.27.1 (Quinlan and Hall 2010). Peaks with overlapping candidate genes were identified as shared peaks across the GWAS results of multiple traits. This approach allows for the detection of common genetic loci contributing to the regulation of different traits, highlighting potential pleiotropic effects or shared biological pathways.

RNA-seq data collection

Thirteen RNA-seq experiments were collected from NCBI (Li et al. 2019, 2024; Wang et al. 2019; Meng et al. 2021; Yan et al. 2021; Kaur et al. 2022; Deng et al. 2023; Liang et al. 2023; Sigalas et al. 2023), including PRJNA526019, PRJNA640981, PRJNA903505, PRJNA528563, PRJNA656889, PRJNA707202, PRJNA749278, PRJNA773211, PRJNA780342, PRJNA925925, PRJNA974975, PRJNA1082516, PRJNA985048. All these experiments designed with treatment of multiple concentrations of nitrogen fertilizers (291 samples in total).

Differential expression analysis

The publicly available RNA-seq data were processed using Salmon version 1.7.0 (Patro et al. 2017) to quantify gene expression levels. Salmon performs quasi-alignment of RNA-seq reads to reference transcripts (wheat IWGSC reference genome with gene annotation version 2.1).

For all the comparisons, genes were filtered first with more than 5 reads on average. For experimental designs with only nitrogen level as the variable, and exit multiple replicates, test was conducted with default setting of DESeq2 version 1.40.2 (Anders and Huber 2010) applying Wald tests. For designs with multiple factors and multiple replicates, likelihood ratio test was conducted to compare nested models via DESeq2. For experiments without replicates, the edgeR package version 4.0.16 (Robinson et al. 2010) was used in R to perform the analysis. Normalization of raw counts was carried out using the TMMwzp method (trimmed mean of M-values with zero preservation) to correct for library size differences and compositional biases. The deviance method was applied to estimate the common dispersion, which models the variability across genes given the absence of replicates. A quantile-adjusted conditional maximum likelihood (qCML) test was applied to detect significant differences in gene expression between nitrogen conditions.

Multiple testing corrections were conducted for all tests via Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedures to control false discovery rate (FDR) (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). Genes with an adjusted p-value (FDR) < 0.05 are considered significantly differentially expressed.

However, relying solely on FDR < 0.05 may result in the inclusion of genes with small but statistically significant changes that lack biological relevance. To address this, 10 different threshold combinations were evaluated by varying the FDR thresholds (0.05, 0.01, 0.001) and log₂ fold-change (log₂FC) thresholds (none, 1, 2).

Co-expression network construction

An expression matrix was constructed using the RNA-seq data from all the collected samples. Transcripts per million (TPM) were calculated for 267,230 annotated genes. Genes with a total TPM across all samples greater than 1 and non-zero variance were retained, resulting in 173,245 genes (65% of the annotated genes). To stabilize variance, all TPM values were log₂-transformed after adding 0.1 to avoid undefined values. To correct for batch effects, PEER (Probabilistic Estimation of Expression Residuals) version 1.0 was applied (Stegle et al. 2012), defining 10 hidden factors. A co-expression network was constructed using the WGCNA package (version 1.72–1) (Langfelder and Horvath 2008) in R, focusing on 96,412 high-confidence genes from the filtered 173,245 genes, while retaining 289 samples after removing two outliers (identified via hierarchical clustering with a height cut-off of 400). The soft-thresholding power (β) was determined to be 5, based on scale-free topology analysis and mean connectivity, to define co-expression modules. The network visualization was generated via Gephi 0.10.1(Csardi and Nepusz 2006).

The module eigengene (ME) of each co-expression module was utilized to assess its correlation with nitrogen levels. The eigengene, representing the first principal component (PC1) of the expression profiles within a module, serves as a summary of the overall expression pattern for the genes in that module. The nitrogen levels were coded as 0 for LN and 1 for HN. Pearson’s correlation was used to evaluate the association between eigengene and nitrogen levels across all samples.

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler package (version 4.8.3) (Wu et al. 2021) in R to identify overrepresented biological processes within gene modules. The minimum and maximum gene set sizes were set to 5 and 5,000, respectively. FDR < 0.05 was used as the significance threshold to identify enriched GO terms.

The GO annotation terms for Triticum aestivum were obtained from Ensembl Plants Genes 68 (IWGSC). The corresponding GO annotation database was built using the AnnotationForge package (version 1.42.2) (Carlson et al. 2017) in R, ensuring accurate and species-specific annotations.

Prioritization of traits

Narrow-sense heritability (h2) was estimated using the sommer package (version 4.3.5) (Covarrubias-Pazaran 2016) in R. A mixed linear model (MLM) was constructed, with the first three principal components of the genotype matrix included as fixed effects to account for population structure, and the kinship matrix modeled as a random effect to capture relatedness among individuals. The model was run for 50 iterations to ensure convergence. A chi-square test with one degree of freedom was performed to assess the significance of the additive genetic variance, testing whether it was significantly different from zero.

Bayesian casual network

The bnlearn R package (version 5.0.1) (Scutari 2009) was employed to construct Bayesian causal networks among traits, incorporating genetic structure correction by adding the first three principal components (PCs) of the genotype matrix as covariates. The network was initialized using the ic-dag method to generate a random graph with 10,000 iterations, ensuring a uniform probability distribution across potential connections. A Hill-Climbing search algorithm was applied to learn the optimal graph structure, using an imaginary sample size (iss) of 10 to account for uncertainty in small sample sizes. The final network structure was derived by averaging the results across all inferred connections. Only edges with a connection strength greater than 0.85 were retained to ensure robustness. Although the first three PCs were included to adjust for population structure, they were not displayed in the final network, as they did not form meaningful connections with any traits. Similarly, traits that were not connected to others were excluded from the final network visualization.

Results and discussion

Spectral data acquisition under different nitrogen conditions

By using the HSI system, the i-traits of wheat grain samples were captured in a noninvasive way (Table S1). The binary wheat grain images were masked with spectral images across all bands, resulting in masked images that contained HSI data for wheat grains across 486 bands. The average reflectance for each band was subsequently calculated from these images. The average spectral curve (Fig. 1e) reveals that wheat grain reflectance is generally lower than leaves in the visible spectrum (400–700 nm), as chlorophyll and other pigments absorb most visible light, resulting in a relatively flat curve. In the near-infrared range (700–1000 nm), reflectance increases significantly due to stronger light scattering and reflection by cellular structures within the grains. Reflectance reaches a local maximum around 800 nm, forming a distinct peak before gradually declining. In the range of 1000–1700 nm, reflectance exhibits an overall decrease with increasing wavelength, interspersed with several absorption peak (Sanaeifar et al. 2023). These absorption features likely correspond to the chemical properties of water, proteins, and starch in the wheat grain (Caporaso et al. 2018b). 304 bands were selected in the range of 400–980 nm and 144 bands in the range of 980–1665 nm (Table S2) due to substantial noise at the extremes of the spectral curve (bands beyond 980 nm (10 bands) from the visible hyperspectral camera, and bands out of the range 980–1665 nm (29 bands) from the near-infrared hyperspectral camera were removed) (Table S2). Ultimately, 448 i-traits were retained for analysis. After preprocessing, which included first derivative (D1st), second derivative (D2nd), and log transformation methods (Fig. 1e), the initial dataset containing 448 i-traits was expanded into a comprehensive one with 1,792 i-traits.

Dimensionality reduction analysis of spectral data

The 1,792 i-traits for each wheat sample provided extensive information but highly increased data complexity. To further investigate the potential of i-traits in nitrogen responses, PCA and SPA were employed for dimensionality reduction. PCA reduced the number of spectral bands while retaining maximum variance and capturing global patterns, the first and second PCs accounted for 40.6% and 17.9% of the variance, respectively. While these components failed to significantly distinguish wheat samples under LN and HN conditions, they revealed overall differences, suggesting complex interactions between genotypes and environmental factors (Fig. 2a). The condensed i-trait set of the first 20 PCs selected from the original dataset successfully explained more than 90% of the cumulative variance and was chosen for subsequent genetic studies (Fig. 2b). These PCs act as linear combinations of the original bands, forming new, unrelated variables. To select an optimal subset of original wavelengths while retaining relevant spectral bands with minimal redundancy, SPA was applied to minimize collinearity among the selected i-traits (Fig. 2c, Table S3; selected i-traits via SPA indicated by red points), resulting in 183 i-traits. In summary, the i-traits dataset consisted of 20 PCs and 183 parameters obtained from PCA and SPA.

Genome-wide association studies to understand nitrogen responses

The PCA-extracted 20 PCs served as part of the i-traits dataset. Additionally, SPA identified 183 parameters, forming another component of the i-traits. Alongside these, 18 agronomic traits were measured (methods). In total, 221 traits were used for the analysis. Given that the samples were grown under both LN and HN conditions, the environmental response was quantified as the HN-to-LN ratio for each trait. As a result, three sets of traits were generated: LN-specific traits, HN-specific traits, Nitrogen response (HN-to-LN ratio, hereafter referred to Ratio). This approach yielded a comprehensive dataset of 663 traits (221*3), encompassing both genotype-specific traits and environmental responses, all of which were utilized for GWAS to identify genetic elements associated with nitrogen responses.

For all traits obtained, -log₁₀ (P-value) = 5 was considered as the suggestive cut-off to identify significant associations. 599 out of 663 traits show significant associations (Fig S1). The highest SNP among the significant SNPs with 10 Mb were considered as the representative SNPs for the association peak (Fig. 3a, b; the representative SNPs were shown as red points; Table S4). For all the 599 traits, varied number of representative SNPs were observed (Fig. 3c) showing most traits have few significant peaks while a few traits exhibit extremely high number of peaks. A large number of peaks for a single trait is likely influenced by data structure artifacts rather than true genetic associations, which can compromise the biological interpretability. Additionally, traits with disproportionately high peak counts can introduce biases in downstream analyses, affecting the balance and comparability among traits. To mitigate these issues, a filter based on the third quartile (Q3) of the peak count distribution, which was 24 peaks, was applied (Fig. 3c, d; dashed vertical line). For traits with more than 24 significant peaks, only the top 24 peaks with the strongest association signals were retained (i.e., the lowest P-values) (Fig. 3d). This filtering ensures that the analysis remains balanced and focused on the most relevant associations, facilitating more reliable and interpretable downstream analyses.

In summary, most i-traits and agronomic traits exhibited significant genetic associations across all three conditions: LN, HN, and Ratio (Fig. 3e). However, the number of shared peaks, defined by the presence of common candidate genes across the three conditions (LN, HN, and Ratio), was limited (Fig. 3f). This suggests that distinct genetic loci may predominantly govern nitrogen responses under specific conditions, with relatively few loci contributing to consistent effects across all environments.

In our GWAS analysis for the PCA-based i-trait PC5 Ratio, which reflects genotype-specific responses to nitrogen availability, TaARE1-7A was identified as a promising candidate gene (Figs. 3a, d). This gene is well-known for its role in nitrogen response and utilization in wheat. A prior study investigated three TaARE1 homoeologs and used CRISPR/Cas9 to generate transgene-free mutant lines (Zhang et al. 2021). These mutant lines demonstrated enhanced nitrogen starvation tolerance, delayed senescence, and increased grain yield under field conditions, highlighting the potential of ARE1 orthologs in improving NUE through gene editing. Another study further validated the agronomic importance of TaARE1-A allelic variants by analyzing their distribution and effects across 261 wheat cultivars (Wang et al. 2023). The TaARE1-A-a genotype, more frequent in both landraces and modern cultivars, exhibited significantly higher thousand-kernel weight, kernel length, and kernel circumference compared to the TaARE1-A-b genotype. These findings align with the role of TaARE1-7A identified in our study and underscore the importance of i-traits in study of nitrogen utilization.

TaEIN4 was identified in multiple i-traits within visible wavelengths in response to nitrogen changes (Ratio) (Fig. 3g; Table S5) as an ethylene receptor ortholog of EIN4 in Arabidopsis, providing evidence for the involvement of ethylene signaling in nitrogen response and NUE in wheat. Ethylene, a key stress hormone, plays a central role in plant adaptation to environmental challenges by modulating various morphological and physiological responses. Previous studies have demonstrated that changes in nitrogen availability or forms can influence both ethylene biosynthesis and sensitivity, establishing a regulatory link between nitrogen status and ethylene signaling (Ma et al. 2023). Moreover, the similar group of i-traits identified TaPTS1 as a putative pterocarpan synthase (Fig. 3g; Table S5). The role of pterocarpan synthase in stress response and structural biosynthesis suggests it could indirectly influence plant metabolism, including processes related to growth and development under stress conditions including nitrogen deficiency (Meng et al. 2020). TaLEA3 was identified from the GWAS results of several visible and NIR bands under the LN condition and Ratio condition (Fig. 3g; Table S5). Its ortholog, LEA3, in rice is an ABA-inducible protein involved in responses to abiotic stresses (Hu et al. 2014). Given that nitrogen metabolism is influenced by ABA signaling and water deficit conditions (Planchet et al. 2011; Zakari et al. 2020), TaLEA3 is likely involved in nitrogen regulation, contributing to the adaptive response under varying nitrogen levels. TaPTR9-7B was identified through GWAS analysis associated with the i-trait dV167 under the HN condition and seed length under the Ratio condition. TaPTR9-7B is the wheat ortholog of OsPTR9, a member of the peptide transporter/nitrate transporter (PTR/NRT1) family. The functional characterization of OsPTR9 in rice revealed its significant role in nitrogen uptake and NUE (Fang et al. 2013). Additionally, OsNPF3.1, another member of the NRT1/PTR family, has been identified as a key player in nitrogen use efficiency (Yang et al. 2023). Our identification of TaPTR9-7B in wheat aligns with these findings, reinforcing the significance of the NRT1/PTR family in regulating nitrogen responses and NUE. In addition, TaNAR2.1 was identified from the GWAS results of multiple i-traits. This gene is the wheat ortholog of OsNAR2.1, which functions as a partner protein of nitrate transporters (NRT2) in rice. OsNAR2.1 is mainly expressed in roots and plays a crucial role in facilitating both high- and low-affinity nitrate uptake by interacting with OsNRT2.1, OsNRT2.2, and OsNRT2.3a, enabling rice to adapt to fluctuating nitrate supplies (Yan et al. 2011).

Our analysis identified two candidate genes involved in the GA pathway: TaGA20ox4, a key player in GA biosynthesis (Hirose et al. 2012), and Rht-B1, a DELLA protein that functions in GA signaling (Pearce et al. 2011) (Fig. 3g; Table S5). Rht-B1, encoding a DELLA protein, plays a key role in GA signaling by inhibiting GA responses through repression of growth-related processes. The semi-dwarfing allele Rht-B1b, a key contributor to the Green Revolution, reduces plant height, increases tiller number, and improves grain number per spike by modulating plant architecture (Hedden 2003; Xu et al. 2023). However, Rht-B1b lines require higher nitrogen inputs to achieve their yield potential, as reduced GA sensitivity affects nitrogen uptake and NUE (Loddo and Gooding 2012). Both genes highlight the intricate interplay between GA regulation and nitrogen metabolism, crucial for optimizing NUE in wheat (Sun et al. 2023).

The candidate gene Rht-B1 was identified in four i-traits (dN34LN, dN34Ratio, dN47LN, and dN47Ratio) and one agronomic trait, LWR, under the Ratio condition (Fig. 3g; Table S5). The significance level for LWR; Ratio was 6 × 10⁻⁶, whereas for the i-trait dN47LN, the significance level reached 4 × 10⁻⁸. Notably, all four i-trait associations exhibited stronger significance than the agronomic trait, underscoring the greater sensitivity of i-traits in detecting genetic associations (Fig S2).

This study highlights the utility of integrating agronomic traits and HSI-based i-traits to identify candidate genes involved in nitrogen regulation. These insights lay the groundwork for breeding strategies targeting enhanced NUE and yield in wheat.

Candidate genes optimization using published nitrogen-responsive RNA-Seq data

In most cases, a single causal gene is present under each significant peak. To identify the most likely candidate gene from the four candidate genes associated with each peak, expression variations of candidate genes related to nitrogen levels were examined by compiling 13 nitrogen-responsive projects from NCBI (Li et al. 2019, 2024; Wang et al. 2019; Meng et al. 2021; Yan et al. 2021; Kaur et al. 2022; Deng et al. 2023; Liang et al. 2023; Sigalas et al. 2023), totaling 291 RNA-seq samples, including 115 LN (low nitrogen), 7 MN (moderate nitrogen), and 169 HN (high nitrogen) samples. Differential expression analysis across these nitrogen conditions identified 65,210 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) at FDR < 0.05, overlapping with 12,114 GWAS candidate genes from 3,556 peaks.

The DEG results were biologically consistent with both the nitrogen response pathways and our GWAS findings. For instance, two key candidate genes, Rht-B1 and TaNAR2.1, showed significant differential expression in response to nitrogen (FDR = 2 × 10⁻⁷ and 4 × 10⁻1⁶⁸, respectively) (Fig. 4a). Stringent DEG cut-offs ensured reliability, but determining the optimal cut-off required balancing between sensitivity and specificity. Ten different cut-off combinations were evaluated, varying FDR thresholds (0.05, 0.01, 0.001) and log₂ fold-change (log₂FC) thresholds (none, 1, 2) (methods).

Optimization of candidate genes. (a) Differential expression of Rht-b1 (upper panel) and NAR2.1 (lower panel) from project PRJNA1082516, in the root tissue. P values indicated. (b) Number of peaks/representative SNPs remained (y-axis) with application of different cut-offs to define nitrogen-DEGs (i-traits PC5 and ddNIR145 under three conditions: LN, HN, Ratio). (c) Number of retained candidate genes at 10 different cu-off combinations. The horizontal dashed line represents total number of peaks/representative SNPs

The goal was to have only one defined nitrogen-responsive DEG among the four candidate genes for each peak. Ideally, applying stricter filters would not reduce the number of peaks, preserving the association signals. We found that the number of retained peaks declined slightly at the FDR < 0.001 & log₂FC > 1 threshold in both PC5 and ddN145 results (Fig. 4b), and in most traits. Using this combination narrowed the GWAS candidate genes to 3,648 genes (1,253 candidate genes under LN, 1,393 under HN, 1,718 under the Ratio condition), closely aligning with the number of peaks/representative SNPs, indicating this threshold as the optimal cut-off (Fig. 4c).

These findings suggest that the FDR < 0.001 & log₂FC > 1 threshold offers the best balance for refining candidate genes. This threshold was adopted to gain deeper insights into the overall patterns and functional roles of the GWAS candidate genes.

Classification of candidate genes through nitrogen-responsive network

In wheat genome, a large proportion of genes remain uncharacterized or have unknown functions. To uncover the overall patterns and potential functions of the identified candidate genes related to nitrogen responses, gene-to-gene relationships within a co-expression network were leveraged. Using the same publicly available nitrogen-responsive RNA-seq data, a nitrogen-responsive co-expression network was constructed to classify the optimized 3,648 candidate genes (Fig. 5a). In total, 75,252 expressed genes were grouped into 145 modules, each assigned a unique name and indicated by distinct colors (Table S6). Genes with similar expression patterns across nitrogen conditions were clustered into the same module, reflecting their likely coordinated roles in nitrogen metabolism and plant responses.

Classification of candidate genes. (a) co-expression network built. Colors represent unique modules assigned to genes. Each point is a gene that successfully built into the network existing highly-correlated genes (Pearson’s correlation > 0.1). (b) Distribution of p-values testing Pearson’s correlation between expression pattern of each module with nitrogen level of each sample. (c) GO enrichment result of genes in orangered4 module. (d) GO enrichment result of genes in lightsteelblue1 module

Given the nitrogen-centered focus of the study, it was expected that the co-expression network would tightly reflect nitrogen-responsive patterns. To assess this, the expression pattern of each module was analyzed by correlating its expression profile with nitrogen availability—coded as 0 for LN and 1 for HN. The results revealed a significant over-representation of p-values < 0.05 among the modules (Fig. 5b), demonstrating that the network captures nitrogen-responsive information rather than random patterns.

The functional roles of candidate genes were further inferred through GO enrichment analysis for each module. Most of the candidate genes were concentrated within 36 modules (Table S7), highlighting specific biological processes relevant to nitrogen responses. For instance, the orangered4 module appears to regulate nitrogen metabolism under abiotic stress conditions (Fig. 5c). In contrast, the lightsteelblue1 module likely focuses on ammonium transport through ethylene signaling pathways (Fig. 5d). The diversity of GO functions across the modules underscores the unique roles of different candidate gene groups, all contributing to nitrogen responses.

Filtering and prioritization of i-Traits

Given the large number of traits and candidate genes, the analysis was refined by focusing on the most relevant i-traits that contribute to agronomic traits and nitrogen responses. A series of filters were applied to the studied traits to ensure meaningful insights. Out of the 633 traits, 114 traits exhibited significantly non-zero heritability (Table S8), indicating reliable genetic contributions (Fig. 6a). These 114 traits included 89 traits measured across all three conditions (LN, HN, Ratio), and were prioritized for further analysis.

Filtering and prioritization of key i-Traits. (a) narrow-sense heritability of traits. (b) Spearman’s correlation among traits. (c) Bayesian causal network for prioritized i-traits and agronomic traits. (d) Bayesian causal network for prioritized PCA-based i-traits and agronomic traits

Next, to reduce redundancy, closely related wavelengths with high Spearman correlations were manually filtered out (Fig. 6b, Table S9), retaining a final set of 53 trait-condition combinations, including 42 traits spanning all three nitrogen conditions. These filtered traits were used to construct Bayesian causal networks to explore trait-trait relationships. In these networks, a connection between two traits was established only if their correlation remained significant after accounting for the relationships with other traits, ensuring the network captures meaningful dependencies.

We further divided the analysis between two types of i-traits: SPA-based i-traits, which represent individual wavelengths per se (Fig. 6c; Table S10), and PCA-based i-traits derived from linear combinations of i-traits (Fig. 6d; Table S11). Among the PC-based i-traits, PC5, focused on the traits ddV94-260, demonstrated a direct connection to TKW. Notably, PC5 under the Ratio condition showed significantly non-zero heritability, suggesting that PC5 plays a key role in modulating grain yield in response to nitrogen levels. This finding highlights the importance of PC5 in bridging NUE and agronomic performance, emphasizing its relevance for improving crop productivity under variable nitrogen conditions. This approach supports the integration of HSI as a powerful, non-invasive tool for HTP, offering new opportunities for linking genotype with phenotype in crop breeding programs.

Conclusions

In this study, HSI was applied to wheat grains in combination with GWAS to explore the genetic architecture underlying nitrogen responses of development in wheat. By integrating high-throughput HSI-derived traits with agronomic traits, several key candidate genes involved in nitrogen uptake and utilization were identified, such as TaARE1-7A, TaPTR9-7B, TaNAR2.1, and Rht-B1. These findings offer new insights into the genetic basis of nitrogen responses and highlight the power of HSI for precise phenotyping, particularly in detecting subtle phenotypic variations in response to nitrogen availability.

A total of 3,556 significant peaks were identified across all traits. To pinpoint the most likely candidate gene under each peak, expression variation related to nitrogen was leveraged, narrowing down the list of potential candidates to 3,648 genes. Expression variation has been widely used in studies as a powerful tool to identify causal genes underlying GWAS peaks (Schaefer et al. 2018; Li and Ritchie 2021; Mountjoy et al. 2021; Liu et al. 2024). By integrating expression data with genetic association studies, researchers can more precisely narrow down candidate genes. This integration of genomic and transcriptomic data enhances our ability to interpret genetic variation, ultimately improving breeding strategies by focusing on genes with clear functional roles in nitrogen response. Transcriptome-wide association studies (TWAS) provide a specialized approach by using gene expression levels as phenotypes to refine GWAS results and identify likely causal genes. Integrating TWAS to our study could provide further valuable insights into the genetic mechanisms governing the nitrogen responses of development in wheat.

The integration of HSI and GWAS offers a novel approach to understanding nitrogen responses. While most previous studies have utilized HSI primarily for analyzing canopy-level traits, our focus on grains enabled us to capture fine-scale spectral data related to grain composition, which is directly influenced by nitrogen availability during grain filling. This post-harvest hyperspectral imaging is both efficient and non-invasive, making it a valuable tool in breeding programs. For each candidate genes, the beneficial alleles or haplotypes should be identified to apply to molecular breeding program. By allowing for the rapid assessment of grain i-traits responding to nitrogen responses and other properties without destroying the samples, this method significantly accelerates the screening process. The spectral data from 448 wavelengths, expanded to 1792 spectral features (i-traits) through mathematical transformations, provided a rich dataset for genetic analysis. By reducing the dimensionality of the data using PCA and the SPA, key i-traits that were highly informative for nitrogen response were selected.

The i-trait PC5 in our study was identified as an informative principal component that tightly connected to specific nitrogen-related i-traits and grain biochemical properties (Fig. 6d). PC5 primarily emphasized bands from V95 to V250, corresponding to the red-edge region (680–760 mn) which has been shown to be associated with nitrogen content (Zheng et al. 2016; Silva-Perez et al. 2018; Vergara‐Diaz et al. 2020). In leaves, chlorophyll acts as a major nitrogen sink, making the green region (550 nm) indirectly indicative of nitrogen status (Yendrek et al. 2016). This relationship does not apply to grains highlighting both similarities and differences in the interpretation of HSI-derived traits between different plant tissues.

The integration of HSI and GWAS in this study not only allowed us to identify genetic loci related to nitrogen responses but also provided a framework for exploring the interplay between environmental conditions and genetic variation. The low overlap of significant genetic peaks between nitrogen-deficient and normal conditions suggests the presence of different loci regulating nitrogen responses under specific environmental conditions. This finding is consistent with previous studies indicating that the genetic basis of nitrogen responses is context-dependent, with genotype-by-environment interactions playing a crucial role (Hirel et al. 2007; Bouchet et al. 2016). Future studies could further investigate these interactions by applying HSI to plants grown under varying field conditions to capture more comprehensive nitrogen response data.

Overall, our findings demonstrate the potential of integrating HSI with GWAS to advance our understanding of nitrogen responses of development in wheat. This novel approach offers several advantages for breeding programs, including the ability to non-destructively capture phenotypic data, identify subtle genetic associations, and optimize nitrogen management strategies. As global demands for sustainable agriculture increase, improving NUE in staple crops like wheat will be crucial for enhancing food security while minimizing environmental impacts. Our study lays the groundwork for future research aimed at using HSI in combination with other high-throughput technologies to accelerate the development of nitrogen-efficient crop varieties.
