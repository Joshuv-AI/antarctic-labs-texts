Quality and safety for Sámi users of health and care services: a cross-sectional study from Norway
Authors: Rose Mari Olsen, Ivana Bojanic, Karl Ove Hufthammer, Siri Andreassen Devik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChallenges persist in delivering good-quality and safe health care to the Norwegian Sámi population. This cross-sectional study explored system-level factors affecting healthcare services for Sámi people. Specifically, it assessed (1) the extent to which Norwegian municipalities provided good and safe health and care services to Sámi users, as reported by managers, and (2) whether good and safe service provision was associated with selected municipal characteristics. An online survey was conducted in 2019 across 135 municipalities in the four northernmost counties, and 77 responded (57% response rate). Service quality was measured using a single five-point ordinal-scale question, and proportional odds regression models were used to analyse associations with municipal characteristics. Overall, 40% of municipalities reported offering good and safe services to a ‘large’ or ‘very large’ extent to Sámi users. More favourable assessments were observed among municipalities located within the Sámi language area and among those with higher net operating surplus. The results suggest that while certain contextual factors may support service provision, there remains considerable potential for improvement across municipalities. Continued policy attention and resource allocation, combined with initiatives to strengthen cultural and linguistic competence, are needed to enhance equitable access to health care for Sámi communities.

Introduction

Good and safe healthcare services are essential for promoting individual well-being and improving public health. According to the World Health Organization, quality healthcare must be safe, people-centred, integrated and timely, ensuring both efficient resource use and equitable access [1]. However, substantial health disparities, particularly in health outcomes and access to care, persist for many minority ethnic groups, including Indigenous populations, when compared to the majority population in countries such as Australia, New Zealand, the United States, Canada and Circumpolar regions [2,3]. Importantly, Indigenous peoples are highly diverse, and research findings from one context cannot easily be generalised to another. In circumpolar regions, many Indigenous groups experience poorer health outcomes than their non-Indigenous counterparts; however, the Sámi represent an exception, as their overall health profiles are comparable to those of the majority populations in the Nordic countries [3]. This demonstrates the importance of interpreting Indigenous health research within its specific cultural, historical and geographic setting.

In Norway, the Sámi are recognised as the country's only Indigenous people. This status is based on the International Labour Organization's Convention No. 169 on Indigenous and Tribal Peoples [4], which Norway ratified in 1990 as the first country in the world, and on the official policy of the Norwegian government [5]. As an Indigenous people, the Sámi hold specific rights related to language, culture, community life and self-determination. They have distinct languages that belong to the Uralic language family, as well as distinct and various cultures. The traditional homelands of the Sámi people, Sápmi, include the northern areas of Sweden, Norway, Finland and Northwestern Russia. Approximately 50,000–80,000 Sámi people reside across these four countries, with the largest populations in Norway and Sweden [6].

Indigenous notions of health, socioeconomic disadvantages, language and cultural barriers, historical trauma, inadequate healthcare access and systemic discrimination contribute to health risks and create barriers to accessing and receiving healthcare services [7]. All these factors – alone or combined – may underlie the observed health disparities among Indigenous populations worldwide [8]. While these mechanisms are not identical across all Indigenous peoples, similar dynamics have been identified in circumpolar regions, including among the Sámi [3,9].

The Norwegian health system prioritises the provision of good-quality, safe and accessible services, aiming to ensure equal and timely access for everyone regardless of social or ethnic background or place of residence. In the Sámi and Indigenous context, the concepts of good quality and safe healthcare extend beyond clinical effectiveness to include dimensions such as language accessibility, cultural relevance, and respectful, secure healthcare encounters. While there is no statutory or legal definition of these terms specific to the Sámi population, several national frameworks and policies nevertheless outline key principles for Sámi health services. The Equality and Anti-Discrimination Act [10] and the Sámi Act [11] stipulate that healthcare delivered to Sámi people must be culturally and linguistically adapted, and must recognise and accommodate Sámi language, culture and traditions. In this sense, quality care for Sámi people cannot be limited to clinical effectiveness and patient safety but must also include cultural safety and competence. Safe healthcare must therefore be understood not only as the prevention of clinical harm, but also as the reduction of risks stemming from linguistic or cultural misunderstandings, discrimination or systemic disregard for Sámi health beliefs and experiences.

Research underscores the ongoing challenges in translating these principles into practice. Lavoie et al. [9] highlight that while Norway has advanced compared to other Circumpolar countries through measures such as recognising Sámi language rights in legislation and developing Sámi-specific health education programs, culturally safe healthcare remains insufficiently implemented. The article argues that despite policies recognising Sámi rights, there is a notable lack of practical attention to culturally safe approaches in healthcare services. It calls for the integration of cultural safety as a guiding principle in health program development, stressing that the absence of such an approach represents a significant gap and diverges from international commitments to Indigenous health.

Certain areas of the Norwegian healthcare system still face challenges, particularly in delivering good-quality and safe services to the Sámi population [12], and these challenges resemble those reported among other Indigenous populations in circumpolar areas, such as Inuit and Alaska Natives [3,13]. Lavoie et al. [9] highlighted that the concepts of ‘cultural safety’ and ‘cultural competence’ are less discussed in the literature on how the healthcare system addresses Indigenous peoples in the Nordic countries compared to other circumpolar regions such as Canada and Alaska.

Similar to other Indigenous groups in circumpolar and Arctic regions, the Sámi have faced ethnic discrimination and assimilation pressures over the last century, resulting in social exclusion and the loss of Sámi language, traditions and subsistence rights [14]. All these experiences have been linked to various health risks, including unhealthy cardiovascular and metabolic risk profiles [15], cardiovascular diseases [15,16], diabetes [15] and mental health symptoms in Sámi populations [17,18].

Additionally, Sámi perceptions of health and treatment differ from those of non-Sámi individuals. This pattern is also observed among other Indigenous peoples [8]. Although the utilisation of healthcare services is similar among Sámi and non-Sámi people, Sámi healthcare receivers report lower satisfaction levels [19] and feelings of marginalisation and misunderstanding [20] during their interactions with healthcare staff and services. In particular, the Sámi experience communication challenges because many encounter healthcare professionals who lack language and cultural understanding [21]. Health professionals have confirmed that cultural characteristics can affect patient safety for Sámi people and lead to the risk of delayed or absent health care, as well as poorer treatment compliance, collaboration and follow-up [22]. Health services in Northern Norway are primarily offered in Norwegian and reflect Norwegian culture, with considerable underutilisation of interpreting services [23,24]. Importantly, language-appropriate care relies on clinicians' language assessments and the provision of language choice for Sámi patients [25].

To accommodate the Sámi's legal right to use their Sámi language in public services [12], 13 municipalities in Norway are defined as being within the administrative area for Sámi language (hereafter referred to as the Sámi language area) [26]. Municipalities are included in the Sámi language area either through historical ties or by municipal application, with approval from national authorities in consultation with the Sámi Parliament, as set by the Sámi Act (1987). Once included, they must ensure residents can use Sámi when accessing public services like health care. This means patients may communicate in Sámi, both orally and in writing, and receive official replies in the same language. Essential documents must also be available in both Sámi and Norwegian. Municipalities receive state grants to support these requirements, but allocations are based on population and scope of services, not the number of Sámi residents. Evaluations indicate that funding is often insufficient, partly due to missing local Sámi population data [26].

These insights show the need for studying system-level factors that are necessary for the effective and safe delivery of healthcare services to the Sámi population. Additionally, investigating potential differences between municipalities located within and outside the Sámi language area is relevant. This study aimed to (1) investigate the extent, as assessed by managers, to which Norwegian municipalities provided good and safe health and care services to Sámi people, both within and outside the Sámi language area, and (2) explore whether the provision of good and safe health and care services was associated with various municipal characteristics.

Materials and methods

This study used a cross-sectional design. Data were collected from October to December 2019 using an online survey.

Study setting and population

The study covered the four northernmost counties of Norway: Trøndelag, Nordland, Troms and Finnmark. At the time the study was conducted, the population included 135 municipalities, of which 12 were situated within the administrative district for Sámi language (see Figure 1).

Municipalities in the administrative district for Sámi language in 2019. The lines delineate the traditional Sami territory, known as Saepmie, which aligns with the boundaries of reindeer herding districts within the national borders of Norway, Sweden and Finland (based on [27]).

The municipalities varied according to size and geographical dispersion. In total, 948,482 inhabitants lived in the geographical area in 2019, and the population density was 7 inhabitants/km² [28]. Thirty-five per cent (327,944) of the inhabitants in the area were situated in rural municipalities classified at the two lowest levels of centrality according to the six-level centrality index [5]. The index is calculated based on the number of workplaces and different service functions that can be reached by car within 90 min [29].

Data collection

An email with an invitation to participate in the online survey was sent to the official municipal email addresses of all municipalities (N = 135) in the four northernmost counties. The email included information about the survey and a request to forward the email to a manager who could respond to the questionnaire. Only one person should respond on behalf of the municipality, and the municipalities themselves had to decide who was best suited to respond. A direct link to a web-based form was included in the email. The form was created and managed at https://nettskjema.no/, a tool for online surveys developed and operated by the University of Oslo. For municipalities that did not respond to the survey, up to four reminders were sent.

The survey was developed based on previous research and legal requirements for equitable services. It included mostly closed-ended questions on human resources, cooperation with other municipalities, language on written information given to Sámi inhabitants, use of interpreters, training in the use of interpreters, and quality and safety of the services. The extent to which the municipalities facilitated good and safe health and care services to Sámi users was measured by the question: In your opinion, to what extent does the municipality provide good and safe health and care services to Sámi users? Answers were given on a five-point ordinal scale (‘to a very small extent’, ‘to a small extent’, ‘to some extent’, ‘to a large extent’, and ‘to a very large extent’). All questions were mandatory.

Four variables measuring municipality characteristics were included from the KOSTRA (municipality-state-reporting) database [30]: inhabitants, net operating surplus11Net operating surplus (as a percentage of total gross operating revenue) measures the financial performance of an organisation by indicating the money left after covering operating expenses. (as a percentage of total gross operating revenue), person-labour years in health and care services (per 1,000 inhabitants) and immigrant population (prevalence in %). From Statistics Norway's population statistics, we included the variable municipality centrality. Additionally, we included a variable measuring if the municipality was within the Sámi language area.

Net operating surplus (as a percentage of total gross operating revenue) measures the financial performance of an organisation by indicating the money left after covering operating expenses.

These variables were selected because they capture structural and demographic characteristics that are likely to influence the capacity of municipalities to provide equitable and culturally appropriate health and care services. Population size and centrality reflect differences in settlement patterns, availability of specialised services and potential economies of scale in service provision. Financial capacity, as indicated by net operating surplus, is a well-established determinant of the ability of municipalities to invest in service quality and innovation. Staffing levels, here measured as person-labour years in health and care services per 1,000 inhabitants, directly affect service accessibility and continuity of care. Finally, the proportion of immigrants was included as an indicator of diversity in service populations, which may be associated with greater attention to language and with cultural competence and inclusive practices in municipal service delivery.

Analysis

We report quantitative data using frequencies and percentages (%) or means with standard deviations (SDs). To compare municipal characteristics for responders and non-responders, we used Welch's t-test (continuous data) and Pearson's chi-squared test (categorical data). We also report the characteristics stratified by response status.

To investigate whether municipal characteristics were associated with the perceived quality of the healthcare services provided, we used unadjusted and adjusted proportional odds (cumulative logit) regression models, with the municipal characteristics as explanatory variables. The outcome was the response to the question ‘In your opinion, to which extent does the municipality provide good and safe health and care services to Sámi users?’, with five response levels, ranging from ‘to a very small extent’ to ‘to a very large extent’. Mathematically, we model the (logarithm of the) probability of a response being at or above a given (arbitrary) response level relative to the probability of it being below this level. The results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Informally, OR values > 1 indicate that the participants were more likely to respond (more) positively to the question, implying that they perceived the municipality as providing better and safer healthcare services. The choice of model was based on the ordinal nature of the outcome and the fact that ordinal regression models provide higher precision and power than binary logistic models [31]. Due to the small sample size, few explanatory variables could be included in the models. The ones included were pre-specified (not based on the result of the fitted models). To avoid bias in the estimates, we did not perform bivariate screening of the variables included in the adjusted model [32]. No data were missing.

Sensitivity analysis: One municipality had an unusually large value for the ‘person-labour years in health and care services' characteristic. Due to concern that this might unduly influence the regression models, we reran the adjusted regression model with this municipality excluded, and we report these results separately.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28) or R version 4.3.2 [33]. The ordinal model was fitted using the R package ‘ordinal’ version 2023.12-4 [34]. p-values ≤ 0.05 are characterised as statistically significant.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Norwegian Social Science Data Services (Ref. No. 321816). The survey contained information about the purpose of the study and indicated that by answering the survey, the respondent consented to participate. No personal data were collected.

Results

We received responses from 77/135 municipalities, giving a response rate of 57%. The municipalities represented 48% (453,958 inhabitants) of the population in the geographical area.

Characteristics of the municipalities

The responding municipalities had between 461 and 52,357 inhabitants, and seven were within the Sámi language area. Most of the 77 municipalities (81%) were classified as having centrality 5 (second-least central) or 6 (least central), whereas none were classified as having centrality 1 (most central). Mean person-labour years in health and care services were 44 per 1,000 inhabitants (range 21–116), and the proportion of the population who were immigrants was on average 11% (range 3%–30%). The municipal net operating surplus as a percentage of gross operating revenues ranged from −11.3% to +5.6% (mean −0.2%, SD 3.2%).

Sensitivity analysis

The results from the sensitivity analysis were generally in agreement with Table 3. The estimated adjusted ORs for the two first variables hardly changed (OR 33.2, p < 0.001; OR 1.23, p = 0.008), while the p-value for the classification of centrality increased from 0.08 to 0.31. Otherwise, only minor changes existed in the coefficient estimates, and no p-values decreased (details not shown).

Discussion

This study investigated the extent to which Norwegian municipalities, as assessed by managers, provided good and safe health and care services for Sámi people and whether the provision of such services was associated with various municipal characteristics. The results show that 40% of the municipalities, both within and outside the Sámi language area, reported providing good and safe health and care services to Sámi users. Moreover, we found that being better off financially and being a municipality within the Sámi language area were positively associated with managers' assessments of good and safe provision of health and care services to Sámi users.

Although municipalities within the Sámi language administrative area rated their services more positively than those outside the area, the overall response pattern indicates there is still room to improve the quality of services for Sámi residents. This is further supported by the questions addressing municipal characteristics related to language and cultural adaptations (Table 1), which add nuance and help clarify the interpretation of our main findings. While the overall item captured how municipalities perceive their service provision, the follow-up questions offered more specific indicators of quality and revealed a more complex picture. For example, even within the Sámi language area, around half of the municipalities reported not having health information available in Sámi, and interpreter training for health personnel was limited. These findings suggest that municipalities may consider their services ‘good and safe’ even when important structural or cultural adaptations are lacking. With regard to interpreter use, none of the municipalities within the Sámi language area reported that they were likely to use family members – a practice often regarded as problematic in Sámi contexts [21] – whereas 16% of the other municipalities answered ‘yes’ to the same question.

Although these are self-reported and subjective responses, we argue that the strength of our results lies in the nuances they reveal, which may help generate discussion about what the concept of ‘good and safe’ health care services for Sámi residents actually entails.

Building on this, it is important to consider how such nuances relate to broader structural and contextual factors that shape the provision of culturally safe health care, including the role of language rights, cultural competence and the availability of financial resources.

Our findings indicate that efforts to provide culturally adapted and safe health care for Sámi patients likely exist in the municipalities that are obligated to facilitate the use of the Sámi language. A review of studies on cultural competence and safety in health care across circumpolar countries supports our findings, indicating that Norway has made progress in implementing initiatives to provide high-quality, safe care for Sámi patients compared to other Nordic countries [9]. However, the financial resources of healthcare institutions may influence the success of these initiatives, either enhancing or limiting their ability to provide high-quality and culturally safe health care for Sámi patients. Literature reviews [35,36] have shown that economic status is a primary determinant of healthcare quality and safety across global healthcare settings. Insufficient funding, along with cultural and language factors, has been identified as a significant barrier to healthcare service delivery and access for populations in the circumpolar north [37]. While these findings point to the importance of financial capacity, the picture appears to be more complex in the Norwegian context.

Despite the willingness and efforts of healthcare professionals, financial constraints may be a hindrance to the implementation of effective and safe healthcare delivery initiatives in municipalities both within and outside the Sámi language area. Wold et al. [26] showed in their report that the additional expenses for municipalities in the Sámi language area are primarily related to costs in the education and school sector, not to increased costs for the health and care sector. The municipalities in the Sámi language area have also reported [26] that grants do not fully cover their needs and that proper allocation based on the Sámi population is uncertain because statistics on residents with a Sámi background are based on unreliable figures. In light of this, the higher likelihood of reporting good-quality and safe health care for Sámi patients in municipalities within the Sámi language area should be interpreted with caution. Although financial resources are often associated with better service delivery, this does not necessarily imply that more money is being spent on health and care services in these municipalities. An alternative explanation may be that greater awareness of the special responsibility toward the Sámi population contributes to more positive assessments.

Concerning written information in Sámi about the municipality's health and care services, only three of the seven municipalities within the area reported that this was available, whereas all seven municipalities indicated that they had access to interpreting services. Notably, when choosing between a professional interpreter and Sámi-speaking healthcare personnel, the municipalities in the Sámi language area stated that they would most often use the latter. Sámi-speaking nurses are recognised as important translators, bridging both language and cultural nuances [24].

However, research shows that these professionals may experience the responsibility of being repeatedly called upon when patients have a Sámi background as burdensome [38]. Health services cannot rely solely on Sámi personnel to ensure cultural safety; rather, they require a systemic approach in which all health professionals have adequate cultural competence. Therefore, attention to cultural safety is needed in all municipalities, regardless of whether the municipality is located within or outside the Sámi language area. In the present study, municipalities outside the Sámi language area most often reported using professional interpreters, although 16% also indicated that family members were likely to be used. However, using family members as interpreters can be problematic because family taboos may lead patients to withhold certain information, and the dual role of interpreter and supportive family member can put relatives in a vulnerable position [21].

The need for a systemic approach to cultural safety has been recognised at the national level. The 2019 national guidelines for health and social care education in Norway include mandatory learning outcomes addressing Sámi culture, language and health perspectives for all students (Ministry of Education and Research [39]), and in 2021, a dedicated Sámi-language nursing education program was established in Kautokeino to increase the number of Sámi-speaking nurses [40,41]. Such initiatives represent important steps toward addressing health disparities but require sustained implementation and evaluation to ensure their impact.

In addition to integrating Sámi-related content into professional education, there is also a need for competence development at the local level. Such competence must be continuously strengthened and maintained within municipalities through workplace-based initiatives such as in-service training, courses, supervision and collaboration with Sámi stakeholders. Previous research has highlighted that cultural competence and cultural safety are sustained not only at the level of individual professionals but also through organisational structures and continuous professional development [42,43].

Our findings align with the evidence emphasising the need to integrate language and cultural traditions into healthcare services to ensure the delivery of good and safe care to the Sámi population [24,44]. Although this study does not provide direct evidence that healthcare services for the Sámi population are of better quality in municipalities within the Sámi language area than elsewhere, responses from these municipalities suggested somewhat greater availability of culturally-adapted information, interpreter services and Sámi-speaking healthcare personnel. Nevertheless, the results indicate that there remains considerable potential for improvement in municipalities both within and outside the language area, particularly regarding the provision of information in the Sámi language, staff training in the appropriate use of interpreters and inter-municipal collaboration to enhance services for Sámi users. Today, many Sámi live outside the language administrative area, and younger generations are increasingly urbanised [45]. This highlights the importance of ensuring that all municipalities – not only those in the Sámi core areas – are able to provide culturally and linguistically appropriate services. Future research should explore these issues through qualitative approaches at the administrative level, which could provide deeper insight into how the provision of good and safe health services for the Sámi population in Norway is understood and operationalised in municipal planning and management practice.

Strengths and limitations

This is the first study to evaluate the quality and safety of health care for Sámi patients across municipalities in Norway, identifying key characteristics that may contribute to the effectiveness of such health care provision. Although the response rate was only moderate, our comparison between responders and non-responders indicates that we had a representative sample of municipalities.

Some limitations need to be considered. Our data is based on responses from one manager within the health and care services in each municipality. Therefore, the results reflect their knowledge and perceptions of the provision of health and care services for Sámi users and not objective measurements. Importantly, these perspectives may vary from the experiences and perceptions of Sámi patients because the manager's viewpoint may not fully encompass the diverse range of experiences within the Sámi community. Managers from municipalities within the Sámi language area were more likely to report positively on the quality and safety of health and care services for Sámi users compared to the other municipalities. Given their obligation to provide specialised services for the Sámi community, these findings may reflect their motivation to present favourable findings while potentially underreporting any negative outcomes related to health and care services for Sámi users.

Another limitation relates to the study design. By asking each municipality to forward the survey to a manager they deemed most qualified, we aimed to gather informed perspectives on the provision of health and care services to Sámi users. However, we lack information about the respondents' roles, qualifications and managerial levels. Without such background, it is difficult to assess the authority and consistency of the responses. Professional context would have added valuable depth to the findings. Moreover, relying on internal forwarding routines may have introduced selection bias: municipalities more engaged in Sámi health issues may have delegated the survey to knowledgeable managers, while others may not have done so. Future studies should target specific roles and collect background information on the respondents to enhance validity and comparability.

The regression models are based on an assumption of proportional odds. Given the limited sample size, it is not possible to reliably test this assumption. However, the association between the outcome variable and the two main (statistically significant) variables appears approximately monotone (Figures 2 and 3), so it is plausible that the models provide reasonable effect estimates even if the assumption were not to hold exactly.

Finally, the small sample of municipalities within the Sámi language area presents an additional limitation. Our comparisons between municipalities inside and outside this area should be interpreted with caution, as only half of the relevant municipalities were represented in the sample. This restricts the generalisability of findings related to regional differences in service provision.

Conclusion

The results suggest that there remains room for improvement in the quality and safety of health and care services for Sámi residents in Norway. Municipalities within the Sámi language area tended to report somewhat more favourable conditions on several of the indicators, which may reflect their specific obligations under the Sámi Act and access to targeted financial support. At the same time, reliance on Sámi-speaking personnel to provide interpreting services represents a vulnerable resource. Moreover, Sámi residents live throughout the country and are entitled to culturally adapted services regardless of municipal boundaries. Ensuring such provision requires continued attention and initiatives, including culturally adapted training for both future healthcare professionals and current employees in municipal care services nationwide.
