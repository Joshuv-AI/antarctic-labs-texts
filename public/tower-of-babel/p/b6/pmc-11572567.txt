Optimizing Outcomes in Distal Tibial Deformity Correction: The Role of Supramalleolar Osteotomy with Computer‐assisted Hexapod External Fixator
Authors: Xiaosong Yang, Hui Du, Xinghua Yin, Xiaofeng Gong, Yan Wang, Ying Li, Ning Sun, Liangpeng Lai, Wenjing Li, Heng Li, Xing Li, Xiaotian He, Yong Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDistal tibial deformities can significantly impact patients if left uncorrected, often leading to pain, alterations in gait, and the eventual development of post‐traumatic arthritis. The criteria for surgical correction in these patients continues to be a subject of debate, while supramalleolar osteotomy (SMO) is an effective method for correcting distal tibial deformities. The purpose of this study was to evaluate and compare the clinical results of SMO using internal fixation or using computer‐assisted hexapod external fixator in the treatment of distal tibial deformity.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted on 290 patients who underwent SMO between June 2015 and January 2023. Forty‐four patients met the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Among the participants, 19 underwent SMO combined with a computer‐assisted hexapod external fixator, while 25 received SMO with plate and screw internal fixation. The tibial anterior surface (TAS) angle, tibial lateral surface (TLS) angle, the tibiotalar (TT) angle and the talocrural (TC) angle were assessed on weight‐bearing X‐ray films. Functional assessments were performed according to the American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) ankle‐hindfoot score.ResultsThe study followed patients for an average duration of 31.7 ± 15.3 months, with a range from 12 to 67 months. Successful bone union was achieved in all cases. For patients treated with the computer‐assisted hexapod external fixator, significant improvements were observed: the mean deviation in sagittal plane deformity parameters decreased from 14.3 ± 10.4 degrees preoperatively to 2.8 ± 3.8 degrees postoperatively (p < 0.05). Similarly, coronal plane deformity parameters showed a reduction from 25.9 ± 22.5 degrees preoperatively to 5.9 ± 11.0 degrees postoperatively (p < 0.05). The AOFAS ankle‐hindfoot score improved markedly from 66.0 ± 14.9 to 86.1 ± 11.7 points (p < 0.05). For patients undergoing internal fixation, the absolute difference in coronal plane parameters improved from 15.4 ± 12.6 degrees preoperatively to 3.7 ± 3.4 degrees postoperatively (p < 0.05). A significant enhancement in AOFAS ankle‐hindfoot score was also noted, increasing from 68.3 ± 14.3 points to 79.4 ± 13.5 points (p < 0.05). There were no significant differences in gender, side, follow‐up time, postoperative deviation of deformity, pre‐ or postoperative AOFAS between the two groups.ConclusionIn conclusion, comprehensive preoperative planning of SMO combined with either internal fixation or a hexapod external fixator for treating distal tibial deformities can achieve satisfactory outcomes. The utilization of a computer‐assisted hexapod external fixator facilitates a gradual and precise correction process, which proved to be an effective and relatively safe method.Keywords: External fixation, Supramalleolar osteotomy, Tibial deformity

Introduction

Distal tibial deformities, which include shortening, abnormal rotation, or angulation, can significantly impact patients if left uncorrected. These deformities often lead to pain, alterations in gait, and the eventual development of post‐traumatic arthritis. The primary causes of these deformities are malunions following distal tibial fractures or physeal injuries incurred during childhood. 1 , 2 The radiographic characteristics of tibial deformities are widely recognized, the criteria for surgical correction in these patients continue to be a subject of debate. Current literature suggests that surgical corrections are justified for addressing painful functional deficits, correcting cosmetically unacceptable deformities, or reducing the risk of degenerative osteoarthritis. 3 , 4

Supramalleolar osteotomy (SMO) is an effective method for correcting varus deformities of the ankle joint, supported by extensive clinical research focused on distal tibial deformities. 5 , 6 This technique successfully realigns the deformities, thereby adjusting the mechanical axis of the lower limb, redistributing forces across the ankle joint, and alleviating areas of stress load. As a result, SMO can play a critical role in slowing the progression of ankle osteoarthritis. 7 However, the variability in patient deformities requires customized surgical approaches. For mild distal tibial deformities, SMO combined with plate and screw fixation is often suitable. In contrast, complex multiplanar angulations and/or shortening may present challenges for internal fixation methods due to the increased surgical complexity, unreliable fixation, bone defects, and elevated risks of vascular and nerve damage, soft tissue imbalance, and compromised bone healing. 2 In such scenarios, external fixation represents a practical alternative for addressing deformities. The Ilizarov method, employing a hinged device for incremental correction, is one such technique, albeit demanding considerable surgical expertise. 8 , 9 While the traditional Ilizarov approach facilitates early weight‐bearing and offers controlled, hinge‐assisted correction at the angulation's center for gradual and stable osteosynthesis, it encounters specific challenges in the supramalleolar region due to the complexities associated with hinge device placement. Furthermore, during the correction phase, complex deformities may necessitate hinge adjustments or the construction of a new frame, potentially extending the treatment duration and impacting patient adherence.

The Taylor spatial frame (TSF), however, emerges as a robust solution for intricate lower limb deformities, building upon the Ilizarov principle of distraction osteogenesis. 10 , 11 , 12 This technique involves meticulous preoperative planning for the strategic installation of rings at the osteotomy sites, interconnected by six adjustable struts. Postoperative adjustments to these struts, guided by computer‐generated protocols, enable the precise realignment of bone segments to their desired positions, culminating in the removal of the external fixator once sufficient callus formation is achieved. The TSF method adeptly manages complex multiplanar deformities and facilitates the gradual adjustment of blood vessels, nerves, tendons, and other soft tissues to the new tension, thus minimizing the risk of acute ischemia, sensorimotor deficits, and the recurrence of soft tissue deformities. 4 , 13 The simplicity of strut adjustments and the clear protocol significantly improve patient compliance. The choice of TSF is justified by its user‐friendly nature and adjustability, with the literature affirming its superior performance in addressing complex lower extremity deformities. 13

Despite the proven effectiveness of SMO in addressing distal tibial deformities, research on its application remains limited. Thus, the present study aimed to retrospectively analyze the data from patients treated for distal tibial deformities with SMO to assess and compare the clinical outcomes of SMO using either internal fixation or the computer‐assisted hexapod external fixator for distal tibial deformity correction.

Methods and Patients

This retrospective study received approval (K202105‐01) from the institutional review board of the relevant hospital, ensuring adherence to ethical standards. All participants provided written informed consent before their inclusion in the study.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (i) diagnosed with distal tibial deformities adversely affecting daily activities, without significant improvement through non‐surgical treatment; (ii) aged 14 years or older, confirming that participants had reached lower limb skeletal maturity with closed physis; (iii) underwent treatment involving SMO combined with internal fixation or a computer‐assisted hexapod external fixator; (iv) clinical and radiological evidence meeting one or more of the following conditions, (a) anteroposterior (AP) radiographs indicating a tibial lateral surface (TLS) angle exceeding 82° or below 78°, (b) coronal radiographs revealing a tibial ankle surface (TAS) angle greater than 94° or less than 87°, and (c) evidence of osseous fusion in the hindfoot and ankle with remaining deformity. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (i) presence of an active infection within the ankle joint; (ii) patients who were lost to follow‐up; and (iii) follow‐up duration less than one year.

General Information

This retrospective study analyzed 290 patients who underwent SMO between June 2015 and January 2023. Adhering to the predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 44 patients were selected for the final analysis, comprising 20 males and 24 females. The age of patients at the time of surgery ranged from 15 to 62 years, with an average age of 35.0 ± 15.7 years. Each patient underwent the procedure on one foot, with the contralateral side being unaffected or presenting minor pathology. Among the participants, 19 (10 males and nine females) underwent SMO combined with a computer‐assisted hexapod external fixator, while 25 (10 males and 15 females) received SMO combined with plate and screw internal fixation. The etiologies of the deformities varied, including malunion post‐fracture (20 cases), physeal injuries (nine cases), and other conditions (15 cases) such as ankylosing spondylitis, rheumatoid arthritis, complications post‐ankle arthrodesis, previous infections, neurofibromatosis, multiple hereditary exostoses, acromegaly, poliomyelitis, congenital spinal anomalies, and various neuromuscular disorders, with one case unspecified. The majority of the deformities were non‐fused at the ankle joint (39 cases), whereas fusion was observed in five cases.

Operative Technique

The surgical procedure was conducted under either general or spinal anesthesia, with the patient positioned supine. All participants received SMO. The center of rotational angulation (CORA) of the deformity was identified by locating the intersection of the proximal and distal tibial mechanical axes. The CORA was considered the apex of the deformity. An osteotomy site was selected near or at the apex of the deformity.

For patients receiving SMO with internal fixation, the surgical procedures were as follows: for cases with mild to moderate varus deformity and a CORA located more than 1 cm above the ankle joint, we used a medial opening wedge osteotomy due to its simplicity and convenience. However, if the medial soft tissue might be compromised after the osteotomy or if the deformity was severe and the CORA was above the ankle joint, we opted for a lateral closing wedge osteotomy. Finally, for severe deformities with a CORA located less than 1 cm above the ankle joint, we utilized a dome osteotomy. This method offers greater power for deformity correction and the ability to correct the deformity precisely at the CORA. The osteotomy was performed using multiple drill‐hole and an osteotome. A fibular osteotomy at the same level was performed in every patient. The result of the correction was confirmed by fluoroscopy before completing the tibial fixation.

For patients who received SMO with a computer‐assisted hexapod external fixator, the fibula osteotomy is performed first. A longitudinal incision of about 2 cm was made horizontally at the middle and lower third of the fibula. The fibula was exposed through a standard lateral approach, and the peroneal tendons were retracted posteriorly. The osteotomy was performed using a thin osteotome, and the skin was rinsed and sutured. Next, a 2.0 mm Kirschner wire (K‐wire) was placed from the lateral to the medial aspect through the distal tibia, ensuring parallel alignment with the ankle joint. The K‐wire's placement and angle relative to the ankle joint surface were confirmed under fluoroscopic guidance. A distal reference ring of appropriate size was then mounted onto this K‐wire. An additional K‐wire was inserted from the lateral to the medial side through both the fibula and tibia, crossing them to stabilize the distal end of the reference ring against the tibia. Depending on the elevation needed for the SMO, one or two half‐pins were used to secure the distal reference ring. For the proximal osteotomy site, a full ring was chosen based on the required size and attached using three half‐pins. The configuration of proximal and distal rings was completed with six Fast Fx struts, arranged to prevent soft tissue irritation by maintaining adequate space between the ring and the skin during the adjustment period. After securing the distal ring to the distal segment of the tibia, orthogonal fluoroscopic views (AP for the coronal plane and lateral for the sagittal plane) were obtained in line with the reference ring's plane. These images were essential for determining the horizontal and axial distances from the ring's center to the CORA in the coronal and sagittal planes, respectively. The tibial osteotomy was performed using a percutaneous, multiple drill‐hole and osteotome technique.

Postoperative Treatment

Postoperative management for patients treated with a computer‐assisted hexapod external fixator begins one‐week post‐surgery, with precise adjustments made to the six struts as per the prescribed schedule. Following the initial adjustment, patients undergo regular follow‐up evaluations and X‐ray imaging to determine the necessity for further refinement or adjustments. Once optimal alignment is confirmed, the fixator is left in place to allow for callus mineralization. Patients are encouraged to begin ambulatory activities on the day following surgery, aiming for 6–8 short walks daily, each lasting 10–15 min, with the support of crutches. Weight‐bearing starts two weeks post‐surgery, beginning with a load of 5 kg and increasing by 5 kg each week. To compensate for uneven foot placement during this period, a foam pad is employed under the affected limb. The external fixator is removed upon satisfactory callus formation. This process may involve either complete removal of the device or partial removal, wherein the frame is detached while leaving 2–3 half‐pins in place, stabilized with a carbon rod until callus mineralization is deemed adequate for their removal. The duration for which the circular external fixator or the combination of carbon rod and half‐pins is utilized is included in the total external fixation period.

After the external fixation device, either the frame or carbon rods, is removed, a posterior below‐knee splint is applied for about one week. This precaution ensures that pin sites and surgical wounds remain dry and free from bleeding. Subsequently, patients are transitioned to an inflatable boot. This boot, which allows for air pressure adjustments, facilitates a gradual return to full weight‐bearing and helps maintain proper limb alignment. The boot is generally used for six weeks, after which an X‐ray evaluation is conducted. If callus formation is deemed satisfactory, patients begin a phased transition to conventional footwear. This process starts with four daily sessions of wearing shoes for approximately five minutes each, extending every three days by one session, until reaching eight daily sessions of 30 min each within roughly two weeks. If the patient experiences no discomfort, they may fully transition to regular footwear. Postoperatively, exercises to improve ankle and knee flexion and extension are recommended three times daily.

For patients receiving internal fixation, a cast or walking boot is applied in a neutral position for two weeks postoperatively to protect the soft tissues, with sutures removed after 2–3 weeks. Patients are encouraged to begin ankle range of motion exercises following suture removal, with gradual weight‐bearing starting 4–6 weeks post‐surgery and progression to full weight‐bearing by 8–12 weeks.

Outcome Assessment

For those treated with a computer‐assisted hexapod external fixator, radiological assessments are initiated after the initial postoperative adjustment to the external fixator, determining the necessity for subsequent adjustments. X‐ray imaging follows each adjustment as dictated by the treatment plan. Once the deformity correction aligns with the predefined objectives and no further adjustments are necessary, patients enter the bone mineralization phase, awaiting the removal of the external fixator. During this period, follow‐up appointments are scheduled every 1 to 2 months, with X‐ray imaging employed to monitor osteogenesis at the osteotomy sites. Patients receiving internal fixation are scheduled for follow‐up visits at 3, 6, and 12 months postoperatively, with subsequent annual assessments.

Radiographic analysis involves weight‐bearing AP images captured preoperatively and at the final follow‐up. For patients with non‐fused ankle joints, this includes measuring the TAS angle for coronal plane deformities and the TLS angle for sagittal plane deformities. The tibial axis is identified as the line connecting the midpoints of the tibial shaft 8 cm and 13 cm above the medial malleolus tip. The TAS angle is measured as the angle between the tibial axis and the distal tibial articular surface in the AP weight‐bearing view, 5 whereas the TLS angle is determined by the angle between the tibial axis and its distal articular surface in the sagittal plane. 8 For those with fused ankle joints, the Tibiotalar (TT) angle and the talocrural (TC) angle are measured to evaluate pre‐ and postoperative deformities in both planes. 14 The normal reference values are as follows: TLS = 80°, TT = 68°, TAS = 89°, and TC = 82°. EFDS refers to the absolute value of the difference in angle between the sagittal plane measurements and the normal reference values in the cohort receiving external fixation. EFDC refers to the absolute value of the difference in angle between the coronal plane measurements and the normal reference values in the cohort receiving external fixation. IFDC refers to the absolute value of the difference in angle between the coronal plane measurements and the normal reference values in the cohort receiving internal fixation. Clinical effectiveness is gauged using the American Orthopedic Foot and Ankle Society (AOFAS) ankle‐hindfoot score. 7

Statistical Methods

Data analysis was performed utilizing SPSS software (version 27.0, SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test assessed the normality of continuous variables. Variables adhering to a normal distribution were compared using the t‐test. Conversely, for variables deviating from a normal distribution, the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test was applied. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was as set statistically significant.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that SMO, whether utilizing internal fixation or a computer‐assisted hexapod spatial frame, effectively corrects distal tibial deformities and significantly enhances ankle functionality. There was a notable improvement in postoperative AOFAS ankle‐hindfoot score postoperatively, underscoring the efficacy of the procedure. The absolute difference in sagittal and coronal plane deformity parameters from the norm at the final follow‐up also improved significantly compared with the preoperative values. There were no significant differences in gender, side, follow‐up time, postoperative deviation of deformity, pre‐ or postoperative AOFAS between the external group and the internal group.

SMO

SMO aims to rectify deformities, optimize intra‐articular load distribution through axis overcorrection, decelerate or halt the degeneration of the tibiotalar joint, and enhance functional outcomes. The importance of meticulous preoperative planning cannot be overstated. Varus deformities are typically managed with medial opening‐wedge osteotomy or lateral closing‐wedge osteotomy, while medial closing‐wedge SMO of the tibia is the preferred approach for addressing valgus deformities. It is crucial to acknowledge that sagittal plane deformities have a more pronounced impact on the tibiotalar contact area compared to coronal plane deformities, necessitating careful consideration during correction strategies.

Fixation Strategies

The evolution of fixation strategies has progressed from the use of casts to plates and screws, external fixators, and ultimately to the circulator external fixator. 13 , 15 , 16 These advanced systems are now capable of being evaluated by computer and even programmed to precisely correct deformities.

In this study, patients treated with internal fixation typically presented with less severe deformities compared to those managed with external fixation, yet both groups achieved satisfactory postoperative outcomes and functional improvements. Nonetheless, internal fixation necessitates an immediate correction approach, which may not be suitable for addressing complex deformities. The ankle's soft tissue envelope, characterized by minimal muscle mass and a limited vascular supply, predisposes the area to delayed healing, and poses risks for tendinous and neurovascular injuries, infection, and wound management challenges. The acute correction process with plates, necessitating open surgery and soft tissue dissection, may further complicate the healing trajectory. Moreover, the placement of internal hardware carries inherent risks, especially in regions with compromised skin quality.

Using Hexapod External Fixator

Indications

Our study identified the primary causes of distal tibial deformities in patients undergoing external fixation as malunion of fractures and physeal injuries. Notably, the cohort receiving external fixation of this study was younger, with an average age of 28.6 ± 12.0 years, significantly below the 44‐year average reported by Horn et al. 4 Unlike prior studies, our patients showed less severe degenerative changes in the ankle joint but faced more pronounced deformities. The predominant aim for most seeking treatment was to alleviate the life‐impacting effects of their deformities. Those subjected to SMO combined with computer‐assisted hexapod external fixator were primarily dealing with significant sagittal and/or coronal plane deformities of the distal tibia, frequently alongside tibial torsion, usually without considerable or merely mild discrepancies in limb length. In cases where distal tibial deformity is accompanied by significant shortening or a deformity of the proximal tibia, a dual‐level osteotomy employing two sets of hexapod external fixators in series may be considered for comprehensive correction.

Assessment and Correction Goals for Complex Distal Tibial Deformities

Complex distal tibial deformities often entail multiplanar corrections. Beyond physical examination, detailed measurements of distal tibial deformities are obtained using localized and full‐length lower limb X‐rays. In instances where the CORA is clearly above the ankle joint, osteotomy is performed at this CORA to facilitate gradual correction, aligning with the primary osteotomy principle. For most patients whose CORA coincides with the level of the ankle joint line, the osteotomy line is strategically placed as close to this level as possible. This placement ensures adequate bone stock for robust fixation at the osteotomy's distal segment, adhering to the secondary principle of osteotomy.

The assessment of supramalleolar deformities primarily relies on two indices: the TAS angle and the TLS angle. In this study, 17 patients in the external fixation group had coronal plane deformities, the absolute difference in coronal plane parameters improved significantly from a preoperative value of 25.9 ± 22.5 to a postoperative value of 5.9 ± 11.0. Sagittal distal tibia deformities produce a greater change in tibiotalar joint contact areas than with valgus or varus deformities. 17 The commonly measured angle is the TLS angle, whose normal value is 80°. The most recommended osteotomy for anterior ankle arthritis is an anterior opening wedge osteotomy for angular corrections under 10° a dome osteotomy for corrections greater than 10°. In this study, the external fixator group comprised 16 patients with sagittal plane deformities. The absolute difference in sagittal plane parameters improved significantly from a preoperative value of 14.3 ± 10.4 to a postoperative value of 2.8 ± 3.8.

When formulating treatment plans for supramalleolar deformities, the overall condition of the foot plays a critical role. In cases without additional foot deformity, the therapeutic goal is to normalize the TLS and TAS angles. However, the presence of concurrent foot deformities necessitates a thorough evaluation to determine the need for surgical correction. For minor foot deformities that do not require surgery, the aim is to achieve a plantigrade stance by adjusting the TLS and TAS angles targets to compensate for the deformity. Conversely, significant foot deformities that require surgical intervention prompt a strategy to realign both the distal tibia and foot towards normal anatomical configurations, ensuring a level hindfoot stance.

A common scenario involves patients with chronic distal tibial valgus deformity, which often leads to compensatory inversion at the ankle and subtalar joints to maintain plantigrade foot. Over time, this compensatory mechanism may result in medial soft tissue contracture. Addressing the tibial deformity without considering the consequent hindfoot inversion can yield less than optimal results. Thus, preoperative examinations must evaluate the hindfoot's inversion and eversion mobility. In some instances, maintaining a slight valgus at the distal tibia may aid in achieving a neutral hindfoot position. The approach is inverted for patients with distal tibial inversion.

Application of Foot Rings and Carbon Rods

In managing patients with high soft tissue tension, particularly those with increased Achilles tendon tension, special consideration is required when correcting towards dorsiflexion. The use of a single ring may not adequately counteract the plantarflexion of the foot relative to the distal tibial segment. A foot ring, therefore, is employed to secure the foot and allow for a controlled, gradual correction of dorsiflexion, effectively preventing foot drop. This ring is typically removed two weeks after the initial adjustment once the primary deformity has been significantly corrected. However, in patients with low Achilles tendon tension, good dorsiflexion mobility, and minimal bone elongation, the use of a foot ring may be deemed unnecessary.

In this study, five patients retained 2–3 half‐pins when both proximal and distal rings were detached, employing a carbon rod to bridge and stabilize the half‐pins until the callus the callus matured sufficiently. Subsequently, both the carbon rod and half‐pins were removed. The carbon rod serves as an integral component of the external fixation system, effectively minimizing the pin count. In cases where osteogenesis is adequate for external fixator removal, but callus development is slow, employing a carbon rod for 1–3 months is advisable, pending removal based on mineralization progress. 18 The rationale behind using a carbon rod includes diminishing the external fixator's rigidity, fostering protective mineralization of the callus, and reducing the external fixator's intrusion into daily activities.

Limitations of the Study

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective manner, while providing a solid foundation for evaluation through comprehensive preoperative deformity parameters, postoperative correction plans, and established goals, inherently lacks a comparative analysis with a matched control group. Additional multicenter prospective studies are needed to remove the effect of confounding factors such as age. Second, five cases in this study had undergone ankle fusion before their participation, distinguishing them from others by reducing the number of articulating spaces in the hindfoot. This discrepancy might notably affect hindfoot mobility and stability in postoperative evaluations. Third, the duration of postoperative follow‐up was limited, restricting the ability to gauge the long‐term success and sustainability of the corrective procedures. It is imperative that future research extend the follow‐up period to determine the enduring outcomes of these interventions more accurately.

Conclusion

In conclusion, comprehensive preoperative planning of SMO combined with either internal fixation or a hexapod external fixator for treating distal tibial deformities can achieve satisfactory outcomes. The utilization of a computer‐assisted hexapod external fixator facilitates a gradual and precise correction process, which proved to be an effective and relatively safe method.
