Correlation Analysis of Nocturnal Noise with Melatonin Secretion and Bone Repair Factors in Patients with Osteoporotic Vertebral Compression Fractures
Authors: Tong Chen, NaiLong Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aim:This study investigated the association between nighttime noise control and melatonin secretion alongside bone repair-related factors in patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures.Methods:A retrospective study was conducted at LiuZhou Municipal Liutie Central Hospital between January 2022 and December 2023. Patients with acute osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures were divided into the following two groups: standard care group (n = 65) and noise control group (n = 72). The noise control group received enhanced acoustic management in specially designed quiet zones, whereas the standard care group was treated in regular hospital wards. Melatonin levels, osteoprotegerin (OPG), cortisol concentrations, receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL), RANKL/OPG ratio, Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for pain intensity and Single-Item Sleep Quality Scale (SQS) for sleep quality were measured at baseline and Day 7 of hospitalisation.Results:The noise control group demonstrated significantly elevated melatonin levels compared with the standard care group (P < 0.001). SQS scores were significantly higher in the noise control group than in the standard care group (P < 0.001). Cortisol concentrations were significantly reduced in the noise control group (P < 0.001). Bone metabolism regulation markers showed favourable changes in the noise control group, with significantly decreased RANKL/OPG ratio (P < 0.001), elevated OPG levels (P = 0.005) and reduced soluble RANKL concentrations (P < 0.001). VAS scores demonstrated a significant reduction in the noise control group (P < 0.001).Conclusion:Acoustic environmental modifications were associated with altered melatonin secretion patterns and changes in bone metabolism markers. These findings suggest potential associations between noise control measures and physiological parameters in orthopaedic patient populations.

INTRODUCTION

Osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures represent one of the most common skeletal complications associated with aging and decline in bone mineral density, affecting millions of individuals worldwide.[1] These fractures typically result from minimal trauma in patients with weakened bone integrity due to osteoporosis, metabolic bone diseases or age-related bone loss.[2] Conservative treatment remains the primary therapeutic approach for most patients, involving bed rest, analgesic management, anti-osteoporotic medications and supportive care during the acute healing phase.[3] Although medical interventions have shown efficacy in promoting bone repair, emerging evidence suggests that environmental factors within healthcare settings may influence patient recovery outcomes.[45] Among these factors, the acoustic environment has received increasing attention as a potentially modifiable determinant of healing success.[6]

Hospital settings are characterised by persistent noise pollution that frequently exceeds recommended guidelines established by international health organisations.[7] Elevated noise levels, particularly during nighttime hours, can disrupt sleep architecture, trigger stress responses and alter hormonal secretion patterns essential for tissue repair.[8] The physiological consequences of noise exposure include suppression of melatonin production, elevation of cortisol levels and activation of inflammatory pathways, all of which may compromise the bone healing process.[910] Despite growing awareness of noise-related health impacts, limited research has specifically examined the relationship between acoustic interventions and bone repair processes in patients with fractures. This knowledge gap represents a significant opportunity to enhance patient care through evidence-based environmental modifications.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of nighttime noise control management on melatonin secretion and bone repair-related factors in patients with vertebral compression fractures. By comparing outcomes between standard care and noise-controlled environments, this study seeks to elucidate the potential therapeutic benefits of acoustic management in orthopaedic patient populations and provide evidence for implementing noise reduction protocols in clinical practice.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Design and Participants

This retrospective study was conducted at LiuZhou Municipal Liutie Central Hospital from January 2022 to December 2023. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of LiuZhou Municipal Liutie Central Hospital (Approval No: KY-2023-12), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.[11] All patients have provided written informed consent. Patients were divided into two groups based on their ward assignment and the noise control measures implemented during their hospitalisation. The standard care group (n = 65) consisted of patients treated in standard orthopaedic wards with routine noise management practices. The noise control group (n = 72) included patients admitted to specially designated quiet zones with enhanced acoustic management protocols.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged 50–85 years with acute osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures confirmed by radiological examination and bone mineral density testing (T-score ≤−2.5)[12]; (2) fractures occurring within 72 hours prior to hospital admission; (3) patients admitted for conservative treatment including bed rest, analgesics and anti-osteoporotic therapy; (4) complete medical records; (5) hospital stay duration for at least 7 days and (6) absence of other concurrent fractures or major trauma.

The exclusion criteria included the following: (1) pathological fractures secondary to malignancy; (2) patients requiring surgery; (3) patients with pre-existing sleep disorders or psychiatric conditions requiring medication; (4) use of medications known to affect melatonin production or bone metabolism (excluding anti-osteoporotic agents); (5) history of chronic kidney or liver disease and (6) incomplete medical records or loss to follow-up.

A total of 187 patients with acute osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures were initially assessed for eligibility. Among them, 15 patients were excluded because of pathological fractures secondary to malignancy, 12 patients were excluded due to requiring surgical therapy, eight patients were excluded due to pre-existing sleep disorders or psychiatric conditions requiring medication, seven patients were excluded due to use of medications known to affect melatonin production or bone metabolism (excluding anti-osteoporotic agents), five patients were excluded due to history of chronic kidney or liver disease and three patients were excluded due to incomplete medical records or loss to follow-up during the 7-day observation period. Ultimately, 137 patients were enrolled in the final analysis.

Noise Detection and Control Methods

Environmental noise monitoring was conducted using calibrated sound level meters (Type 1, IEC 61672-1 standard) positioned at standardised locations within patient rooms. Noise measurements were recorded continuously over 24-hour periods, with particular attention to nighttime hours (22:00–06:00). Sound levels were measured in A-weighted decibels (dBA) and recorded at 1-minute intervals throughout the monitoring period.

In the standard care group, standard hospital protocols were followed, which included routine staff activities, medical equipment operation and normal visitor policies. No specific noise reduction measures were implemented beyond standard hospital practices.

The noise control group was managed in specially prepared ward areas with comprehensive acoustic management. These measures included the following: (1) installation of sound-absorbing materials on walls and ceilings; (2) implementation of quiet hour policy from 22:00 to 06:00 with restricted staff movement and reduced equipment alarms; (3) use of soft-closing doors and drawers; (4) placement of carpeting in corridors adjacent to patient rooms and (5) staff education regarding noise awareness and quiet communication practices.

The standard care group was housed in the east wing, whereas the noise control group occupied the west wing of the orthopaedic department, with these areas separated by approximately 50 m of corridor space and distinct nursing stations to minimise cross-contamination of acoustic management protocols. Each wing maintained independent staff rotations and equipment allocation schedules to ensure consistent implementation of the respective noise management strategies without interference between study environments.

Light Exposure Control

To minimise the confounding effects of light exposure on melatonin secretion, standardised lighting protocols were implemented across both groups. All patient rooms were equipped with identical light emitting diode (LED) lighting systems (3000 K colour temperature) with programmable dimming functions. During daytime hours (06:00–20:00), room illumination was maintained at 300–500 lux at bed level. Evening light reduction protocols were initiated at 20:00, with gradual dimming to <50 lux by 22:00. Nighttime lighting (22:00–06:00) was restricted to <10 lux using amber-filtered night lights (wavelength >580 nm) to minimise circadian disruption. Window coverings with light-blocking properties (>95% light reduction) were standardised across all rooms, and patients were instructed to keep curtains closed during nighttime hours. Healthcare staff were provided with red-filtered headlamps (<5 lux, 650 nm wavelength) for essential nighttime activities to minimise light exposure disruption. Light levels were monitored using calibrated photometers (LI-210R, LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA) positioned at patient eye level, with measurements recorded every 30 minutes during the study period.

Data Collection

Patient demographics, medical history, fracture characteristics and treatment details were extracted from electronic medical records via a standardised data collection form. All laboratory and clinical assessment data were collected retrospectively from existing medical records and archived samples. Data collection was conducted at two time points: baseline (within 24 hours of admission) and Day 7 (on the seventh day of hospitalisation).

(1) Serum melatonin levels were analysed using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits (Melatonin ELISA Kit, IBL International, Hamburg, Germany) with a detection limit of 1.0 pg/mL. All samples had been analysed in duplicate using a microplate reader (ELx800, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). Blood samples for melatonin measurement were collected at 22:00 (bedtime). Serum cortisol concentrations were quantified using a chemiluminescent immunoassay (Cortisol Immunoassay, Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics, Erlangen, Germany) with a detection range of 27.6–1656 nmol/L. Blood samples for cortisol measurement were collected between 07:00 and 08:00 to capture peak circadian levels, following standardised protocols for early morning cortisol assessment. All cortisol measurements were performed in duplicate to ensure analytical precision, with inter-assay and intra-assay coefficients of variation below 5%.

(2) Bone formation markers: Serum osteoprotegerin (OPG) levels were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Human OPG ELISA Kit, R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA) with a detection limit of 0.02 pmol/L. Soluble receptor activator of nuclear factor κB ligand (sRANKL) concentrations were determined using the Human sRANKL ELISA Kit (Biomedica, Vienna, Austria) with a detection limit of 0.08 pmol/L. The receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL)/OPG ratio was calculated to assess the balance between bone resorption and formation signals. All assays were performed according to manufacturer protocols by using a microplate reader (ELx800, BioTek Instruments, Winooski, VT, USA). Blood samples for bone metabolism markers were collected between 07:00 and 08:00 following overnight fasting.

(3) Pain intensity was assessed using the Visual Analog Scale (VAS), a unidimensional pain assessment tool consisting of a 100-mm horizontal line with anchors of ‘no pain’ (0) and ‘worst pain imaginable’ (10).[1314] The VAS demonstrates excellent reliability (test–retest reliability r = 0.94) and validity for acute pain assessment in hospitalised patients. Pain scores were recorded at admission and Day 7 from nursing documentation.

(4) Sleep quality was evaluated using the Single-Item Sleep Quality Scale (SQS), a validated self-report measure that assesses overall sleep quality using a single item rated on a 0–10 visual analogue scale (0 = terrible, 10 = excellent).[1516] Participants were asked to rate their overall sleep quality over the past 7 days. The SQS demonstrates excellent test–retest reliability over a 7–14 day interval [intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) = 0.82, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.66–0.91) and good convergent validity with the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (r = −0.73, P < 0.001]. As a single-item measure, internal consistency coefficients (Cronbach α) are not applicable or meaningful for this scale. Higher scores indicate better sleep quality, with the SQS providing a practical and efficient assessment tool for clinical populations.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. Normality of distribution was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Between-group comparisons were performed using independent t-tests, and within-group comparisons were performed using paired t-tests for continuous variables with normal distribution. Categorical variables were compared using χ2 tests. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Melatonin and Sleep Parameters

As shown in Table 3, baseline melatonin levels showed no significant differences between groups (P > 0.05). Within-group paired analysis revealed that melatonin levels significantly decreased in the standard care group from baseline to Day 7 (P < 0.05), but they significantly increased in the noise control group (P < 0.05). By Day 7, the noise control group demonstrated significantly higher melatonin concentrations compared with the standard care group (P < 0.001). Sleep quality, as measured by SQS scores, showed similar baseline values between groups (P > 0.05), but both groups demonstrated significant improvements from baseline to Day 7 (P < 0.05), with the noise control group showing greater improvement at Day 7 than the standard care group (P < 0.001). Cortisol levels showed no statistically significant differences at baseline (P > 0.05). The standard care group showed a modest decrease in cortisol levels at Day 7 compared with baseline (P < 0.05). By contrast, the noise control group demonstrated a significant reduction from baseline to Day 7 (P < 0.05). Between-group comparison at Day 7 showed significantly lower cortisol in the noise control group than in the standard care group (P < 0.001).

DISCUSSION

This study examined the association between acoustic environmental modifications and physiological parameters relevant to bone healing in patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures. The findings of this study demonstrated that nighttime noise control protocols were associated with enhanced melatonin secretion, improved sleep quality, reduced cortisol concentrations and elevated bone formation markers. These physiological changes suggest that acoustic environmental factors may represent a modifiable determinant of recovery trajectories in orthopaedic populations.

The observed biomarker modifications have significant implications for understanding bone repair physiology. The preservation of melatonin circadian rhythmicity is a critical finding, as melatonin exerts direct osteogenic effects through MT2 receptor-mediated stimulation of osteoblast differentiation and concurrent suppression of RANKL-induced osteoclastogenesis.[1718] This favourable shift in the bone remodelling balance towards net formation is further supported by the observed suppression of cortisol, as chronic hypercortisolaemia is known to inhibit osteoblast proliferation and collagen synthesis but promote osteoclast survival via the downregulation of OPG expression.[19] The combined changes in these biomarkers indicate a shift towards an anabolic bone environment favourable for fracture healing.[19]

The mechanistic pathways linking nocturnal acoustic exposure to altered bone metabolism likely involve complex neuroendocrine cascades. Noise-induced sleep fragmentation activates the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, resulting in sustained cortisol secretion and simultaneously disrupting pineal melatonin synthesis due to sympathetic nervous system overstimulation and altered suprachiasmatic nucleus function.[2021] Conversely, acoustic optimisation facilitates the preservation of normal sleep architecture, enabling physiological nocturnal melatonin peaks that activate the protein kinase A and mitogen-activated protein kinase/extracellular signal-regulated kinase (MAPK/ERK) signalling pathways crucial for osteoblast proliferation and differentiation.[22] The reduction in environmental noise likely attenuates stress-induced sympathetic activation, thereby preserving the delicate balance between bone formation and resorption.[2324] Furthermore, improved sleep continuity may enhance growth hormone pulsatility during slow-wave sleep, offering additional anabolic stimuli for bone repair.[25] These pathways suggest that environmental acoustics are a modifiable factor influencing bone healing outcomes, with noise reduction management potentially serving as a cost-effective adjunctive therapy in comprehensive fracture management protocols.

On the basis of these findings, several practical recommendations can be proposed for orthopaedic care settings. Healthcare facilities should consider implementing quiet hour policies during nighttime periods in fracture recovery wards, restricting staff movement and minimising equipment alarms. The installation of sound-absorbing materials and staff education programmes regarding noise awareness represent cost-effective modifications that do not require extensive infrastructure changes. Regular acoustic monitoring using calibrated sound level meters can ensure compliance with recommended guidelines and identify areas requiring intervention. These acoustic management strategies may serve as supplementary interventions to improve recovery outcomes in patients with osteoporotic vertebral compression fractures.

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting the findings of this study. The retrospective design inherently limits the ability to determine definitive causal relationships between acoustic management and physiological outcomes, and the relatively small sample size and single-centre setting may limit the generalisability of results to other healthcare environments with different architectural configurations and patient populations. The non-randomised allocation of participants based on ward availability rather than systematic randomisation introduces potential selection bias that may influence outcome measurements beyond statistical adjustments. Additionally, the implementation of enhanced acoustic management protocols may have inadvertently generated a Hawthorne effect, leading healthcare personnel to alter their behavioural patterns beyond the intended noise reduction measures, potentially confounding the attribution of observed improvements. Moreover, noise monitoring at standardised ward locations rather than individual-level exposure assessment may not fully capture variations in actual acoustic exposure among patients due to factors such as bed location, personal noise sensitivity or potential use of hearing protection. Melatonin samples were collected at 22:00 (bedtime) rather than during the peak secretion window (02:00–04:00) to avoid disrupting patient rest and potentially compromising recovery outcomes. Although this single-time-point assessment may not fully capture the circadian amplitude of melatonin secretion, it represents a practical compromise between methodological rigour and patient welfare in a clinical setting. The absence of pre-hospitalisation baseline assessments for melatonin and bone metabolism markers further limits the analytical ability to conduct within-group comparisons rather than individual longitudinal trajectories. Future prospective multicentre randomised controlled trials incorporating comprehensive baseline profiling, extended follow-up periods and rigorous control of confounding variables, including socioeconomic factors and individual noise sensitivity thresholds, would strengthen the evidence base. Stratified analyses examining varying effects across patient subgroups defined by age, gender, fracture severity and comorbidity profiles may clarify which populations gain the greatest benefit from acoustic interventions. Future research should also incorporate detailed acoustic characterisation, including noise source categorisation and frequency spectrum analysis, to elucidate the distinct effects of various noise sources on physiological recovery processes. An investigation of dose–response relationships between specific acoustic parameters and physiological outcomes could establish therapeutic thresholds for the clinical implementation of environmental noise management in orthopaedic care settings.

CONCLUSIONS

This study demonstrated associations between nighttime noise control and enhanced melatonin secretion alongside favourable bone metabolism parameters in patients with vertebral fractures. Although these findings suggest the potential therapeutic value of acoustic environmental modifications in orthopaedic settings, further controlled investigations are necessary to establish definitive causal relationships and clinical recommendations.
