Association of Body Mass Index with SARS‐CoV‐2 Infection in Inflammatory Arthritis Patients Receiving Biologic Therapy: Singapore National Biologics Register Findings
Authors: Preeti Dhanasekaran, Siaw Ing Yeo, Yi Xin Ng, Rosa Lim, Stephanie Wong, Stanley Angkodjojo, Andrew Yu Keat Khor, Li Wearn Koh, Manjari Lahiri, Margaret Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo determine the association of body mass index (BMI) with incident COVID‐19 infection in patients with inflammatory arthritis (IA) using biologic disease‐modifying anti‐rheumatic drugs (bDMARDs) and targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs) and to describe characteristics of patients with severe COVID‐19 infection.MethodsThis prospective inception cohort study included IA patients ≥ 21 years old initiating a bDMARD after July 2016 across five public hospitals in Singapore. Data were collected via questionnaires and electronic medical records. Baseline characteristics were compared between patients who developed COVID‐19 and those who did not. The association between BMI and time to incident COVID‐19 infection was assessed using Cox proportional hazards regression, adjusting for demographic and clinical factors.ResultsFive hundred sixty‐one patients (60.1% female, 69.0% Chinese; median age 49.0 years) were included. Diagnoses included rheumatoid arthritis (39.6%), psoriatic arthritis (26.6%), and spondyloarthritides (33.9%). Most (63.8%) were on tumor necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors. SARS‐CoV‐2 infection occurred in 140 (25.0%) patients. Those infected had higher BMI (27.0 vs. 25.0 kg/m2, p = 0.005). BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2 was independently associated with increased risk of infection (HR 1.63 [95% CI 1.05, 2.53]; p = 0.029). Severe COVID‐19 infection occurred in five patients. All were male, and four had underlying comorbidities. The underlying rheumatic diagnoses included rheumatoid arthritis, axial spondyloarthritis, and psoriatic arthritis. These patients were receiving b/tsDMARDs including golimumab, tofacitinib, adalimumab biosimilar (Amgevita), and infliximab biosimilar (Remsima). Importantly, no deaths were reported.ConclusionBeing overweight or obese (BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2) was independently associated with higher COVID‐19 infection risk. Severe infections were rare.Keywords: arthritis, biologic therapy, body mass index, COVID‐19, inflammatory arthritis, registry, SARS‐CoV‐2

Introduction

Obesity, a prevalent comorbidity among patients with inflammatory arthritis (IA) [1], has independently been associated with increased risk of both incident severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2) infection and severe COVID‐19 infection outcomes in the general population [2, 3, 4]. Mechanistically, obesity contributes to impaired innate and adaptive immune responses and promotes chronic low‐grade inflammation [5, 6], which may increase susceptibility to infections in general [7, 8]. In obese individuals, adipose tissue undergoes cellular senescence, during which senescent adipocytes secrete pro‐inflammatory mediators that drive immunosenescence and impair the clearance of dysfunctional immune cells such as T‐cells. This contributes to sustained inflammation and may compromise both viral clearance and vaccine responsiveness, an issue of particular concern in IA patients who already experience underlying immune dysregulation [9, 10]. Additionally, adipose tissue is known to express angiotensin‐converting enzyme 2 (ACE2). In obesity, the increased adipose mass expands the pool of ACE2‐expressing cells, potentially offering a larger reservoir for viral entry and replication [7, 11, 12]. In IA patients receiving immunosuppressive therapies such as anti–tumour necrosis factor (anti‐TNF) agents, obesity‐associated mechanisms may further compromise immune responses, as obesity itself has been linked to poorer therapeutic outcomes [13]. The convergence of elevated ACE2 expression, chronic inflammation, immunosenescence, and therapeutic immunosuppression may thus create a permissive environment for viral persistence, increasing the risk of severe outcomes and diminished vaccine efficacy in obese IA patients [14]. Despite these biological links, few studies have specifically examined the impact of body mass index (BMI) on COVID‐19 infection susceptibility among patients with IA who are treated with immunomodulatory therapies.

The COVID‐19 pandemic, due to SARS‐CoV‐2, has disproportionately affected vulnerable populations globally, including individuals with autoimmune and inflammatory diseases [15, 16]. IA encompassing rheumatoid arthritis (RA), psoriatic arthritis (PsA), and axial and peripheral spondyloarthritides (SpA) often requires long‐term treatment with biologic disease‐modifying anti‐rheumatic drugs (bDMARDs) and targeted synthetic DMARDs (tsDMARDs) [17, 18, 19, 20]. While these therapies are critical for controlling disease activity and preventing joint damage, their immunosuppressive effects have raised concerns about increased susceptibility to infections, including COVID‐19 [21, 22]. Patients with autoimmune inflammatory rheumatic diseases (AIIRD) have poorer outcomes when infected with SARS‐CoV‐2, including higher rates of hospitalisation, mechanical ventilation, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, and mortality [23, 24, 25].

While global attention on COVID‐19 has waned, understanding infection risk in immunosuppressed populations remains clinically relevant. Patients with IA continue to represent a vulnerable cohort due to their underlying immune dysregulation and treatment with b/tsDMARDs. As health systems shift toward endemic COVID‐19 management, identifying modifiable risk factors such as BMI in these patients is essential to inform long‐term care strategies, prioritise high‐risk subgroups, and strengthen preparedness for future infectious threats.

The Singapore National Biologics Register (SNBR) was established in July 2016, involving five major public‐sector tertiary healthcare institutions across Singapore [26]. The SNBR is a prospective inception cohort study created to investigate the utilisation, outcomes, and safety of bDMARDs and tsDMARDs in patients with IA in an Asian setting. By using data collected from the SNBR, we aimed to determine the association between BMI, classified using World Health Organization (WHO) Asian‐specific criteria [27], and incident COVID‐19 infection in IA patients treated with b/tsDMARDs, and to describe the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients who developed severe infection.

Methods

Study Setting and Population

SNBR cohort: Patients aged ≥ 21 years with a rheumatologist‐confirmed diagnosis of RA, PsA, or SpA were eligible for inclusion if they were newly initiated on a bDMARD or tsDMARD after July 13, 2016. Participants were systematically assessed at 6‐monthly intervals for the first 2 years following study entry, and subsequently at 3 and 5 years, with data collection synchronised with routine clinical visits whenever possible.

Baseline and follow‐up data were obtained through questionnaires administered by study team members either in person or via telephone, along with data extraction from electronic medical records (EMR). The questionnaires gathered information on demographic characteristics (age, sex, race), health and lifestyle behaviours (such as smoking and alcohol consumption), and occupational status. Physician‐diagnosed or confirmed comorbidities including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, chronic kidney disease (CKD), cardiovascular disease (CVD), cancer, osteoporosis, chronic lung disease, and chronic liver disease were identified through patient self‐report and verified against the EMR.

Clinical data, such as height and weight at the time of bDMARD initiation, laboratory test results, imaging findings, medication usage, and disease activity scores, were extracted from the EMR. BMI was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared (kg/m2) and classified according to Asian‐specific criteria, with BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2 indicating overweight or obesity [28]. Disease activity assessments, including the Disease Activity Score in 28 joints (DAS28), Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI), and Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Score with C‐reactive protein (ASDAS‐CRP), were recorded when available. Health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) was evaluated using the modified Health Assessment Questionnaire (mHAQ) and the EuroQol 5‐Dimension (EQ‐5D) questionnaire. All data were securely managed using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) system [29].

COVID‐19 Infection Status

COVID‐19 infection status was extracted from the EMR, which documented laboratory‐confirmed SARS‐CoV‐2 infections verified by reverse transcriptase‐polymerase chain reaction (RT‐PCR) testing. Additionally, patient‐reported infections based on positive results from home antigen rapid test kits were captured. Severe COVID‐19 infection was defined as infection leading to hospitalisation and/or death.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were compared between patients who developed incident COVID‐19 infection and those who did not. Categorical variables were analysed using the chi‐square test, while continuous variables were compared using the independent t‐test for normally distributed variables and the Mann–Whitney U test for non‐normally distributed variables.

Multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to evaluate the association between baseline BMI and the risk of incident COVID‐19 infection, adjusting for potential confounders such as clinical diagnosis, comorbidities (yes/no), and type of b/tsDMARDs. The analysis timeframe was set from January 1, 2020 (when COVID‐19 began to be actively monitored), until December 31, 2022, when COVID‐19 restrictions were gradually eased in Singapore. The time to COVID‐19 infection or the censoring date (whichever came first) was used as the event time. BMI was evaluated both as a continuous variable and dichotomised at ≥ 23 kg/m2 to reflect conventional Asian overweight thresholds [27]. Age was included as a continuous variable, while categorical variables such as sex, race, clinical diagnosis, and comorbidities (e.g., diabetes mellitus) were analysed as binary or grouped categories as appropriate. Variables were included in the multivariable model if they had a p‐value ≤ 0.2 in univariable analysis or demonstrated significant correlation with BMI.

Statistical significance was set at a two‐tailed p‐value of < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

The SNBR is funded by the Chapter of Rheumatologists, Academy of Medicine, Singapore.

Results

A total of 561 patients with IA receiving b/tsDMARDs were included, of whom 140 (25.0%) had documented COVID‐19 infection. Notably, COVID‐19 incidence was very low in Singapore during 2020 to 2021 due to stringent public health measures, with most cases occurring in 2022, coinciding with the emergence of more transmissible variants such as Delta and Omicron [30].

The median (IQR) age of the cohort was 49.0 years (38.0, 58.0), with no significant difference between the COVID‐19 and non‐COVID‐19 infection groups (p = 0.593). The majority were female (60.1%) and of Chinese ethnicity (69.0%), with comparable demographic distributions across subgroups. RA was the most common clinical diagnosis (39.6%), followed by SpA (33.9%) and PsA (26.6%). Among RA patients, the mean (SD) baseline DAS28 at biologic initiation was 3.7 (1.1), while among SpA patients, the mean (SD) BASDAI was 5.1 (2.1). The median (IQR) disease duration across the cohort was 8.5 (4.8, 15.3) years (Table 1). The distribution of clinical diagnoses, disease duration, disease activity, smoking status, and marital status did not differ significantly between groups (Table 1).

Patients who developed COVID‐19 had significantly higher BMI (median 27.0 vs. 25.0 kg/m2, p = 0.005), and a greater proportion were overweight or obese (77.0% vs. 65.7%, p = 0.013). Use of b/tsDMARDs was similar across groups, with TNF inhibitors (TNFi) being the most commonly prescribed (63.8%) (Table 1).

In the Cox proportional hazards model, overweight or obese IA patients (BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2) had a significantly higher risk of earlier COVID‐19 infection compared to those with normal or underweight BMI. In univariable analysis, the hazard ratio (HR) was 1.65 (95% CI 1.10, 2.46; p = 0.015), and this association remained statistically significant in the multivariable model (HR 1.63 [95% CI 1.05, 2.53]; p = 0.029), indicating that elevated BMI (≥ 23 kg/m2) independently predicted time to COVID‐19 infection in this time‐to‐event analysis. Other variables, including age and smoking status, were not associated with COVID‐19 infection (Table 2).

Severe COVID‐19 Infection

Severe COVID‐19 was defined as infections resulting in hospitalisation and/or death. Among the 140 patients with COVID‐19 infections, five developed severe COVID‐19 infections requiring hospitalisation. Most were of Chinese ethnicity, and their underlying rheumatic diagnoses included rheumatoid arthritis, axial spondyloarthritis, and psoriatic arthritis. Common comorbidities were hypertension and hyperlipidemia, with three patients also having diabetes mellitus. Patients were on a range of b/tsDMARDs including tofacitinib, golimumab, adalimumab biosimilar (Amgevita), and infliximab biosimilar (Remsima). Three patients had a BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2 (Table 3). No deaths due to COVID‐19 infection were recorded in this cohort during the study period.

Discussion

In this cohort of patients with IA receiving b/tsDMARDs, a higher body mass index (BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2) was independently associated with a 63% increased risk of contracting COVID‐19 compared to those with normal or underweight BMI (HR 1.63 [95% CI 1.05, 2.53]). This association remained significant after adjusting for gender, race, comorbidities, clinical diagnosis, and class of b/tsDMARD used. In contrast, other demographic and clinical variables, including immunomodulatory therapy type and common comorbidities such as hypertension and diabetes, were not significantly associated with infection risk. Despite initial concerns regarding COVID‐19 infection severity in immunosuppressed populations, most infections in this cohort were mild, with few hospitalisations and no reported deaths. Notably, all patients who experienced severe disease were male, older, had cardiometabolic comorbidities, and were receiving TNFi or JAKi.

While no deaths were recorded in our cohort, the small sample size limits the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the risk of severe outcomes among patients receiving immunosuppressive therapies. Although our study did not capture vaccination status or examine vaccine‐related outcomes, both cohorts share comparable demographic and disease profiles. Thus, our findings contribute additional granularity to the growing understanding of COVID‐19 infection patterns and risk factors, such as elevated BMI, in immunosuppressed populations. These observations underscore the need for larger, adequately powered studies to more precisely define the risk profiles and clinical outcomes in these vulnerable groups.

Interestingly, none of the 18 patients receiving anti‐CD20 therapy (rituximab; MabThera and Truxima) developed severe COVID‐19 or were overrepresented among those with SARS‐CoV‐2 infection in our cohort. This contrasts with existing literature, which has consistently reported increased risk of both infection and severe outcomes among rituximab‐treated patients, likely due to impaired humoral immunity and attenuated vaccine responses [31, 32, 33, 34]. The absence of such cases in our study may be explained by the relatively small number of rituximab users, selection bias, or differences in exposure risk and preventive behaviours. Rituximab‐treated patients are typically subject to closer monitoring and may have received stricter guidance on infection prevention, including self‐isolation or infusion delays during periods of high community transmission. They may also have engaged in more cautious social practices, had lower occupational exposure, or benefited from early vaccine access or booster prioritisation as well as the use of pre‐exposure prophylaxis. Nonetheless, these findings should be interpreted with caution and warrant validation in larger, adequately powered studies.

Although disease activity indices (e.g., DAS28, BASDAI) were collected at every follow‐up visit, infection events, including COVID‐19, were recorded retrospectively at subsequent visits rather than in real time. As such, disease activity measured at the time of infection reporting may not accurately reflect immune status at the point of SARS‐CoV‐2 exposure or infection onset. To ensure consistency and avoid temporal bias, we reported disease activity at baseline, defined as the time of biologic initiation. However, this approach does not capture fluctuations in disease control during follow‐up. Baseline disease activity data were also incomplete, as these measures are physician‐reported and not consistently recorded, and retrospective enrolment of some patients after treatment initiation may further limit accuracy. This may contribute to the apparent proportion of patients classified as being in remission or low disease activity at baseline and should be interpreted with caution. Prospective studies with improved temporal alignment of clinical and infection data may further clarify these relationships.

Baseline DAS28 values appeared lower among patients who subsequently developed COVID‐19. However, disease activity indices (DAS28 and BASDAI) were not included in multivariable analyses due to collinearity and their disease‐specific applicability (RA vs. SpA), as they are not directly comparable across diagnostic groups. Instead, disease activity was retained in the model in a categorised form (remission/low vs. moderate/high), allowing for adjustment while maintaining comparability across the cohort. Clinical diagnosis was also retained as their proxy for disease subgroup and their associated disease activity, which may partly explain this observation.

The association between elevated BMI and adverse COVID‐19 outcomes has been well documented in the general population, yet remains underexplored in patients with IA, particularly those receiving immunosuppressive therapies such as b/tsDMARDs. Our study contributes to the limited evidence by demonstrating the observed association between BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2 and COVID‐19 infection in an IA population within a multi‐ethnic Asian setting. This aligns with prior research in the general population, where higher BMI, particularly in the overweight and obese ranges, has been consistently associated with increased risk of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection and worse clinical outcomes [35, 36]. Several biological mechanisms may explain this relationship. Adiposity is associated with chronic low‐grade inflammation, impaired interferon responses, and increased expression of ACE2, the cellular receptor for SARS‐CoV‐2 entry [37]. In the context of IA, these factors may be compounded by immunosenescence and the effects of immunosuppressive therapy, potentially creating a permissive environment for viral replication and prolonged shedding [14, 38]. While our data did not show significant associations with specific drug classes, patients with higher BMI may be more immunocompromised functionally, even when receiving similar therapies. Moreover, obesity may influence vaccine responses, leading to suboptimal immunity in some patients [39].

Our findings underscore the clinical importance of considering BMI as a modifiable risk factor in the management of IA patients, particularly during periods of heightened infectious risk. Routine assessment of weight and incorporation of individualised weight management strategies through dietary counseling, exercise interventions, and, when appropriate, pharmacologic support could reduce infection susceptibility and improve overall health outcomes. Additionally, clinicians may consider enhanced monitoring for infections in overweight and obese IA patients, especially those receiving TNF or JAK inhibitors, and prioritise vaccination strategies in this subgroup.

An important consideration is the potential role of concomitant medications, particularly angiotensin‐converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), in modifying COVID‐19 infection risk among patients with IA. While ACEIs target ACE1 rather than ACE2, the known receptor for SARS‐CoV‐2, some studies have hypothesised indirect effects on ACE2 expression that may influence susceptibility to infection [40, 41]. In our study, we were unable to evaluate the influence of ACEI use, despite collecting data on comorbidities and some concomitant therapies. This limitation stems from the fact that many patients with cardiovascular or hypertensive comorbidities were primarily managed in primary care or polyclinic settings, and medication data were not consistently available unless prescriptions were issued through the specialist rheumatology outpatient clinics. As such, the possible modifying effect of ACEI use on the relationship between BMI and COVID‐19 infection in this cohort could not be assessed. Future studies with integrated medication data across primary and specialty care would be valuable in disentangling this potential confounder.

This study has several strengths. It leveraged a real‐world clinical dataset with access to verified EMRs, allowing for accurate identification of comorbidities, prescribed immunosuppressive therapies, and documented COVID‐19 infections. The use of EMR improved diagnostic verification, though some reliance on patient self‐report remained. Additionally, the inclusion of patients across multiple IA subtypes and a multiethnic population enhances the generalisability of our findings to broader clinical settings.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. Firstly, while COVID‐19 cases were identified through both EMR extraction and patient self‐report, asymptomatic or unreported infections may have been missed. Secondly, data on vaccine status and timing relative to infection were not captured, which may have confounded infection risk. Third, while BMI was significantly associated with infection, central adiposity and metabolic syndrome components were not directly measured. BMI was also measured at treatment initiation and may not reflect body habitus at the time of SARS‐CoV‐2 exposure or infection. Next, concomitant csDMARD use and corticosteroid exposure were not included in the analysis despite being clinically relevant to infection risk. Although these data were collected within the registry, they were recorded at discrete study visits and may not accurately reflect treatment status at the time of SARS‐CoV‐2 infection, particularly given potential treatment adjustments or interruptions. This limitation may contribute to residual confounding and should be considered when interpreting the findings. Lastly, the small number of severe COVID‐19 cases limited the power to analyse predictors of poor outcomes.

Future research should explore the mechanistic interplay between obesity‐related immune dysregulation such as chronic inflammation, altered adipokine signaling, and immunosenescence and immunosuppressive therapies in shaping viral susceptibility among IA patients. Incorporating immunological parameters, including neutralising antibody titres and T‐cell responses, may further clarify how excess adiposity modulates immune function. Prospective cohort studies incorporating detailed metabolic profiling and central obesity metrics could offer more granular insight into obesity‐related risk. Interventional studies examining the impact of weight reduction, as well as analyses of immunosuppressive therapy exposure and vaccine immunogenicity, would further inform risk stratification and management.

Conclusion

In this real‐world cohort of patients with IA treated with b/tsDMARDs, a higher BMI (≥ 23 kg/m2) was independently associated with a significantly increased risk of COVID‐19 infection. Importantly, severe outcomes were infrequent, with no COVID‐19‐related deaths reported, underscoring the relative safety of continuing immunosuppressive therapy in this population when appropriately monitored. These findings highlight the need for holistic care approaches in IA, beyond immunomodulation, to include optimisation of modifiable risk factors such as body weight. Integrating weight management strategies into routine rheumatology care may enhance infection resilience and overall outcomes, especially in the context of ongoing or future infectious disease threats.
