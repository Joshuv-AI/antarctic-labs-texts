Preprocedural frailty status and short- and long-term mortality risk after endovascular therapy in elderly acute ischemic stroke patients
Authors: Weiwei Gao, Xingyu Chen, Arslan Annadurdyyev, Lijuan Cai, Lingfeng Yu, Ziqu Xu, Renjing Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the Laboratory-based Frailty Index (FI-LAB) for predicting mortality following endovascular therapy (EVT) in elderly patients with large vessel occlusion acute ischemic stroke (LVO-AIS).MethodsThis retrospective study enrolled 382 patients aged ≥60 years undergoing EVT. FI-LAB was constructed using 44 preprocedural laboratory parameters. Primary endpoint was 12-month all-cause mortality; secondary endpoint was 14-day mortality. Cox proportional hazards models assessed FI-LAB-mortality associations.ResultsAmong 382 patients, 68.8% exhibited varying degrees of frailty. In fully adjusted models, compared with robust patients, frail patients demonstrated a 1.82-fold increase in 14-day mortality risk (P = 0.012) and a 2.61-fold increase in 12-month mortality risk (P < 0.001). Each 0.1-unit increase in FI-LAB was associated with a 51% increase in 14-day mortality risk (P = 0.001) and a 63% increase in 12-month mortality risk (P < 0.001).ConclusionsFI-LAB independently predicts short-term and long-term mortality following EVT in elderly stroke patients, offering objective risk stratification for clinical decision-making.Keywords: Laboratory frailty index, Risk stratification, Geriatric assessment, Mechanical thrombectomy, Prognostic biomarker, Large vessel occlusion

Introduction

Large vessel occlusion acute ischemic stroke (LVO-AIS) represents the most severe subtype of acute ischemic stroke (AIS), accounting for 28%–46% of all ischemic strokes and characterized by exceptionally high rates of disability and mortality [1,2]. The widespread adoption of endovascular therapy (EVT) has fundamentally transformed the therapeutic landscape for LVO-AIS. However, despite achieving recanalization rates exceeding 80%, clinical outcomes remain suboptimal, with 3-month mortality rates ranging from 15.91% to 18.6% [3,4]. This discrepancy suggests the presence of other critical prognostic determinants that remain inadequately recognized. Current prognostic models for EVT predominantly focus on neurological deficit severity and neuroimaging characteristics while overlooking baseline physiological reserve capacity—namely, frailty—which may possess equivalent predictive value for stroke outcomes [[5], [6], [7]].

Frailty is a geriatric syndrome characterized by multisystem physiological reserve decline and increased vulnerability to stressors [8,9], affecting 12%–18% of community-dwelling older adults [10,11] and up to 23%–24.6% of AIS patients [8,9]. Substantial evidence demonstrates that frailty is closely associated with increased mortality, prolonged hospitalization, poor functional outcomes, and elevated healthcare resource utilization [12]. Studies have shown that frailty assessment provides more comprehensive functional information than prestroke modified Rankin Scale (mRS) scores and may even serve as an effective alternative to the mRS [7,13]. Despite growing recognition of its clinical importance, current stroke management guidelines and clinical practice remain insufficient in their emphasis on frailty evaluation.

Currently available frailty assessment tools, including the Frailty Index (FI), Clinical Frailty Scale (CFS), and Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS), have demonstrated prognostic value in AIS populations [6,7]. However, these instruments primarily rely on medical history collection and functional assessments, limiting their applicability in LVO-AIS patients with impaired consciousness or cognitive dysfunction. The Laboratory-based Frailty Index (FI-LAB), first proposed by Howlett et al., objectively reflects the cumulative burden of physiological deficits by quantifying the proportion of abnormal laboratory parameters and has been validated for predicting mortality risk in older adults within the Canadian Health and Aging Study [14]. Subsequent investigations have further expanded the utility of FI-LAB across diverse clinical scenarios [15]. Nevertheless, the prognostic value of FI-LAB in LVO-AIS patients undergoing EVT remains largely unexplored. Given the reversibility of frailty demonstrated in multiple studies [16], developing an objective frailty assessment framework tailored to LVO-AIS patients holds significant clinical translational value for implementing precision intervention strategies and improving patient outcomes [17].

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the association between preprocedural frailty assessed by FI-LAB and short-term and long-term mortality risk following EVT in elderly LVO-AIS patients, providing objective evidence for clinical risk stratification and individualized treatment decision-making.

Material and methods

Study design and population

This single-center retrospective cohort study enrolled elderly LVO-AIS patients who underwent EVT at a comprehensive stroke center in China between January 1, 2019, and April 30, 2024. Inclusion criteria were: age ≥60 years and digital subtraction angiography-confirmed intracranial large vessel occlusion involving the anterior circulation (internal carotid artery, middle cerebral artery M1 or M2 segments) or posterior circulation (vertebral artery V4 segment, basilar artery, posterior cerebral artery P1 segment). We excluded patients with >30% missing preprocedural laboratory parameters required for FI-LAB construction (i.e., available data for <31 of 44 parameters) and those lost to follow-up [14,18,19]. After screening, 382 patients were included in the final analysis (Supplementary Figure S1).

Data collection

Baseline characteristics included demographic variables (age, sex) and cerebrovascular risk factors (current smoking status, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, atrial fibrillation, previous stroke or transient ischemic attack, and coronary artery disease). Neurological deficit severity at admission was assessed by certified neurologists using the National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS). Stroke etiology was classified according to the Trial of Org 10172 in Acute Stroke Treatment criteria as large-artery atherosclerosis, cardioembolism, or other subtypes.

EVT-related parameters were comprehensively documented, including occlusion site (internal carotid artery, middle cerebral artery M1 segment, middle cerebral artery M2 segment, anterior circulation tandem lesions [involving both internal carotid and middle cerebral arteries], or posterior circulation), temporal metrics (onset-to-puncture time [OPT], puncture-to-reperfusion time [PRT]), number of mechanical thrombectomy attempts, thrombectomy technique (stent retriever, aspiration, or combined approach), adjunctive procedures (balloon angioplasty or stent placement), and bridging intravenous thrombolysis status.

Frailty assessment

Preprocedural frailty was assessed using the FI-LAB according to standardized protocols. The FI-LAB comprises 44 laboratory parameters spanning hematological, biochemical, coagulation, and organ function domains, with specific parameters and normal reference ranges detailed in Supplementary Table S1 [20,21]. All 44 variables demonstrated missing data rates <8% in our study cohort. Each variable satisfied FI-LAB construction validity criteria: no variable showed abnormalities in >80% of patients, nor did any variable have abnormality rates <1%.

Laboratory abnormalities were quantified using binary coding: values within normal reference ranges were scored as 0, while values exceeding normal limits (above upper limit or below lower limit) were scored as 1. The FI-LAB was calculated as: FI-LAB = Σ (number of abnormal parameters) / Σ (total number of measured parameters). This index ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater physiological deficit accumulation and increased frailty severity. For clinical interpretation, FI-LAB was standardized in 0.1-unit increments, allowing hazard ratio (HR) to reflect risk changes per 0.1-unit increase.

Based on established cutoff values from previous studies [15,22], patients were categorized into three groups: robust/non-frail (FI-LAB <0.20), pre-frail (FI-LAB 0.20–0.35), and frail (FI-LAB ≥0.35). Quartile-based grouping was additionally employed for sensitivity analysis to validate the robustness of observed associations.

Clinical outcomes and follow-up assessment

The primary endpoint was 12-month all-cause mortality following EVT, with the secondary endpoint being 14-day all-cause mortality. All patients underwent one-year post-procedural follow-up. Survival status and death dates were determined through telephone contact with patients or next-of-kin, with supplementary verification through electronic health records, death certificates, and municipal health information systems. Overall survival was defined as the time interval from EVT procedure date to death from any cause.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.2.2. Continuous variable distributions were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, with normally distributed variables presented as mean ± standard deviation and non-normally distributed variables as median (interquartile range). Categorical variables were described as frequencies (percentages). Between-group comparisons employed appropriate statistical tests: one-way analysis of variance for normally distributed continuous variables, Kruskal–Wallis test for non-normally distributed variables, and Pearson χ² test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables.

Survival analysis was conducted using Kaplan–Meier methods to estimate cumulative survival probabilities, with log-rank tests for between-group survival curve comparisons. Prior to constructing Cox proportional hazards regression models, univariate Cox regression was used to screen potential predictors (P < 0.05), and multicollinearity was assessed through variance inflation factors and tolerance calculations (Supplementary Tables S2-S4). Three progressively adjusted multivariable Cox models were developed to evaluate the independent association between FI-LAB and mortality risk: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 adjusted for coronary artery disease for the 14-day mortality endpoint, and for age, smoking, diabetes mellitus, atrial fibrillation, and coronary artery disease for the 12-month mortality endpoint; Model 3 further adjusted for baseline NIHSS score, occlusion site, and treatment-related factors (PRT, thrombectomy strategy), with stroke etiology additionally included for the 12-month mortality endpoint. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residual tests.

Random survival forest analysis was employed to assess the association strength between FI-LAB and mortality risk, utilizing ensemble learning with 1000 decision trees, logrank splitting rules, and minimum node size of 15. Variable importance was quantified through variable importance measures (VIMP). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was used to explore nonlinear associations between FI-LAB and mortality risk, with 3–6 knots and optimal knot placement determined by the Akaike information criterion. Subgroup analyses were conducted according to sex, age, and cardiovascular risk factors based on previous research evidence and clinical biological plausibility, with effect heterogeneity across subgroups assessed through multiplicative interaction terms in Cox regression models. All statistical tests were two-sided, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study included 382 elderly LVO-AIS patients who underwent EVT. The study cohort had a median age of 69 years, with 257 (67.3%) male patients. Based on FI-LAB assessment, 263 (68.8%) patients demonstrated varying degrees of frailty, including 196 (51.3%) pre-frail and 67 (17.5%) frail patients. Baseline characteristics differed significantly across frailty groups (Supplementary Table S5). Compared with robust patients, frail patients had a higher proportion of females (P = 0.043), lower smoking rates (P = 0.010), higher prevalence of coronary artery disease (P = 0.015), and more severe baseline neurological deficits (P = 0.013).

Sensitivity analysis using quartile-based stratification was performed: Q1 (n = 89, FI-LAB 0.02–0.16), Q2 (n = 70, 0.18–0.21), Q3 (n = 126, 0.22–0.30), and Q4 (n = 97, 0.31–0.59). Quartile analysis confirmed these trends (Supplementary Table S6), with the proportion of females increasing progressively from Q1 to Q4 (22.5%–43.3%, P = 0.010), smoking rates decreasing (49.4%–30.9%, P = 0.021), diabetes mellitus prevalence increasing (21.4%–40.2%, P = 0.045), and median baseline NIHSS scores rising from 13 to 16 points (P = 0.004).

Association between frailty status and mortality risk

The overall 14-day and 12-month all-cause mortality rates were 16.2% and 29.6%, respectively. Mortality rates increased significantly with frailty severity (Supplementary Table S7): 14-day mortality rates were 8.4%, 18.4%, and 23.9% for robust, pre-frail, and frail groups, respectively (P = 0.012); corresponding 12-month mortality rates were 15.1%, 31.6%, and 49.3% (P < 0.001). Quartile analysis confirmed a similar dose-response relationship, with 14-day mortality increasing from 6.7% in Q1 to 23.7% in Q4 (P = 0.017), and 12-month mortality rising from 11.2% in Q1 to 48.5% in Q4 (P < 0.001).

Survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed significant differences in survival curves across frailty groups (log-rank test, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1A). Twelve-month cumulative survival rates were inversely correlated with frailty severity: 84.9% for robust, 68.4% for pre-frail, and 50.7% for frail patients. Quartile analysis further validated this gradient effect (log-rank test, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1B), with cumulative survival rates decreasing from 88.8% in Q1 to 51.5% in Q4 (P < 0.001).

(A) Survival curves for robust (FI-LAB <0.20, green), pre-frail (FI-LAB 0.20-0.34, orange), and frail (FI-LAB ≥0.35, purple) groups showed significant separation (log-rank test, P < 0.001), with 12-month survival probabilities of 84.9%, 68.4%, and 50.7%, respectively. (B) Quartile-based analysis (Q1 [0.0227,0.182), blue; Q2 [0.182,0.227), orange; Q3 [0.227,0.318), red; Q4 [0.318,0.591], green) demonstrated a stepwise decrease in survival probability from Q1 (88.8%) to Q4 (51.5%). Shaded areas represent 95% confidence intervals. The risk tables below each graph show the number of patients at risk at different time points.

Temporal analysis demonstrated that the adverse impact of frailty on outcomes intensified progressively throughout the follow-up period. The absolute difference in cumulative mortality between frail and robust groups expanded from 15.5 percentage points at 14 days post-procedure (23.9% vs 8.4%) to 34.2 percentage points at 12 months (49.3% vs 15.1%).

Cox proportional hazards regression analysis

Schoenfeld residual tests confirmed that all predictor variables and the overall models satisfied the proportional hazards assumption (P > 0.05), validating the appropriateness of Cox regression analysis (Supplementary Figure S2-S7).

When FI-LAB was included as a continuous variable in multivariable analysis, frailty severity demonstrated a significant association with mortality risk (Supplementary Table S8). In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), each 0.1-unit increase in FI-LAB was associated with a 51% increase in 14-day mortality risk (HR = 1.51, 95% CI: 1.17–1.95, P = 0.001) and a 63% increase in 12-month mortality risk (HR = 1.63, 95% CI: 1.36–1.97, P < 0.001).

Categorical analysis revealed that compared with robust patients, pre-frail patients had a 1.04-fold increased risk of 14-day mortality (HR = 2.04, 95% CI: 1.00–4.16, P = 0.049), while frail patients demonstrated a 1.82-fold increase (HR = 2.82, 95% CI: 1.26–6.32, P = 0.012). For the 12-month mortality endpoint, pre-frail and frail patients showed 1.05-fold (HR = 2.05, 95% CI: 1.19–3.52, P = 0.009) and 2.61-fold (HR = 3.61, 95% CI: 1.98–6.61, P < 0.001) increased mortality risks, respectively, with a highly significant trend test (P for trend <0.001).

Quartile analysis further confirmed the robustness of the dose-response relationship. In the fully adjusted model, compared with Q1, Q4 patients demonstrated a 2.51-fold increase in 14-day mortality risk (HR = 3.51, 95% CI: 1.41–8.78, P = 0.007) and a 3.65-fold increase in 12-month mortality risk (HR = 4.65, 95% CI: 2.30–9.41, P < 0.001).

Random forest variable importance assessment

Random survival forest analysis using ensemble learning with 1000 decision trees objectively quantified the association strength between FI-LAB and mortality risk (Supplementary Figure S8). VIMP revealed that for 14-day mortality prediction, occlusion site had the highest predictive value (VIMP = 0.112), with FI-LAB ranking second (VIMP = 0.091). For 12-month mortality prediction, FI-LAB emerged as the primary predictor (VIMP = 0.141), substantially exceeding all other variables in predictive importance.

Nonlinear associations between FI-LAB index and mortality risk

RCS analysis explored potential nonlinear associations between FI-LAB and mortality risk (Supplementary Figure S9). Tests for nonlinearity in 14-day mortality models were not statistically significant (P-nonlinear values of 0.187, 0.155, and 0.472, all P > 0.05). Twelve-month mortality analysis showed similar results: although overall association tests were highly significant (all P-overall <0.001), nonlinearity tests remained non-significant (P-nonlinear values of 0.059, 0.087, and 0.122).

Subgroup analysis

Subgroup analyses demonstrated that FI-LAB was significantly and positively associated with mortality risk across most subgroups, with no significant effect heterogeneity observed between subgroups (all P for interaction >0.05) (Supplementary Figure S10). However, stratification by coronary artery disease revealed significant interaction effects (14-day mortality P for interaction = 0.018; 12-month mortality P for interaction = 0.003). In patients with coronary artery disease, the predictive effect of FI-LAB was substantially attenuated or eliminated (12-month mortality HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 0.71–1.43, P = 0.637), while maintaining strong predictive value in patients without coronary artery disease (HR = 1.87, 95% CI: 1.53–2.28, P < 0.001).

Discussion

This study represents the first systematic evaluation of FI-LAB's prognostic value in elderly LVO-AIS patients undergoing EVT. Our key findings include several important observations. First, nearly seven in ten elderly LVO-AIS patients demonstrated varying degrees of frailty, with 51.3% classified as pre-frail and 17.5% as frail. Second, machine learning-based variable importance assessment revealed that FI-LAB ranked as the second most important predictor in short-term mortality models and emerged as the primary predictor for long-term outcomes. Third, multivariable Cox regression analysis confirmed an independent association between frailty status and mortality risk. After adjusting for confounding factors, frail patients exhibited 1.82-fold and 2.61-fold increased risks of 14-day and 12-month mortality, respectively, compared with robust patients. Furthermore, the predictive value of frailty intensified progressively over time. The absolute mortality difference between frail and robust groups expanded from 15.5 percentage points at 14 days post-procedure (23.9% vs 8.4%) to 34.2 percentage points at 12 months (49.3% vs 15.1%). These findings provide compelling evidence for the clinical utility of preprocedural FI-LAB assessment in identifying high-risk patients and informing individualized treatment decisions.

Previous research has established the prognostic role of frailty in AIS outcomes. Substantial evidence demonstrates close associations between frailty status and mortality, length of stay, functional outcomes, and readmission risk in AIS patients [7]. Several studies have similarly confirmed that frailty serves as an independent predictor of poor long-term outcomes following EVT in LVO-AIS patients. Evans et al. [23] demonstrated in 433 elderly AIS patients that CFS-assessed frailty was independently associated with 28-day mortality, with each 1-point increase conferring a 3% higher death risk. Tan et al. [24] further validated CFS scores as predictive of poor 3-month functional outcomes following EVT in AIS patients. Additionally, Pinho et al. [25] showed that HFRS scores could predict poor functional outcomes in EVT patients, with high frailty risk reducing the probability of achieving good functional recovery by 52%. Joyce et al. [26] developed a 33-item cumulative deficit frailty index that proved to be a strong predictor of 90-day mortality and functional dependence in AIS patients receiving mechanical thrombectomy.

However, existing studies have important methodological limitations. Traditional frailty assessment tools primarily rely on medical history collection, functional evaluations, and cognitive testing, which limits their applicability in LVO-AIS patients with impaired consciousness or cognitive dysfunction. Some elderly patients lack accompanying family members who can reliably report prestroke functional status. In contrast, FI-LAB assessment relies entirely on objective biological parameters, independent of patient cooperation or cognitive function, making it particularly suitable for patients with severe acute neurological impairment [15]. Moreover, the laboratory parameters required for FI-LAB are derived from routine preprocedural assessments, requiring no additional healthcare resource consumption or economic burden.

The FI-LAB concept was first introduced by Howlett et al. [14] in the Canadian Health and Aging Study, which demonstrated that FI-LAB correlated highly with traditional frailty indices based on cumulative deficit models and effectively identified community-dwelling older adults at increased mortality risk. Subsequent research has expanded FI-LAB's utility across diverse clinical settings: in both community and hospitalized populations, FI-LAB has been validated for predicting mortality, hospitalization requirements, increased medication use, healthcare utilization frequency, and health status deterioration [15]. Recent years have witnessed further validation of FI-LAB's predictive efficacy in specific disease populations. Rust et al. [27] developed a frailty index using 9 hematological parameters, demonstrating that each standard deviation increase was associated with 29% and 26% increases in in-hospital and 3-month mortality risks, respectively. Wang et al. [20] confirmed in elderly patients with primary lung cancer that a 44-parameter FI-LAB significantly predicted all-cause mortality risk in chemotherapy recipients. Additionally, in community-acquired pneumonia patients, frailty status has been closely associated with significantly increased risks of respiratory failure, septic shock, and in-hospital mortality [21].

The association between frailty status and adverse outcomes following EVT in LVO-AIS patients can be explained through multiple biological mechanisms. First, frailty reflects the accumulation of functional deficits at subcellular, tissue, and organ levels, with these microscopic structural and functional impairments ultimately leading to irreversible physiological decline [28,29]. Elevated preprocedural FI-LAB scores essentially represent the composite manifestation of multisystem dysfunction, including hepatic and renal impairment, compromised nutritional status, dyslipidemia, and electrolyte disturbances. This systemic physiological reserve deficit significantly reduces the body's adaptability and tolerance to acute ischemic injury. Second, frailty exacerbates outcomes by increasing the risk of post-stroke secondary complications. Clinical studies demonstrate that frail patients have significantly increased risks of nosocomial infections and delirium [29]. Finally, frailty diminishes patient adherence to and tolerance of rehabilitation therapy, compromises neuroplasticity remodeling potential, and ultimately impairs long-term functional recovery and quality of life improvement [17]. Additionally, widespread abnormalities in preprocedural laboratory parameters may reflect excessive stress responses to the acute pathological event of LVO-AIS, indicating impaired homeostatic regulatory mechanisms and insufficient capacity to cope with acute disease insults.

Our findings have important clinical translational implications. First, this study validates FI-LAB's predictive efficacy for short-term and long-term mortality risk in LVO-AIS patients following EVT. Healthcare institutions can establish appropriate frailty assessment thresholds based on local patient characteristics and laboratory testing standards. Second, previous research has demonstrated that frailty can be significantly improved through multimodal interventions, including physical exercise, nutritional interventions, and comprehensive geriatric management [16]. Therefore, implementing prehabilitation for patients identified as high frailty risk and developing individualized comprehensive intervention programs for frail stroke survivors may effectively mitigate the adverse impact of frailty on stroke outcomes. Finally, integrating FI-LAB into existing prediction models may substantially enhance high-risk patient identification capabilities, enabling more precise individualized risk stratification and optimized treatment decisions.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, we did not directly compare FI-LAB with other validated tools such as CFS and HFRS, which limits our ability to assess FI-LAB's relative predictive performance and incremental value. Second, the effect heterogeneity observed in the coronary artery disease subgroup requires cautious interpretation: the significantly attenuated predictive effect of FI-LAB in patients with coronary artery disease may be related to long-term secondary prevention therapies (antiplatelet agents, statins, ACE inhibitors/ARBs) partially offsetting the adverse effects of frailty through mechanisms such as improved endothelial function and reduced inflammatory responses. However, due to the limitations of our retrospective design, we could not obtain complete medication history and adherence data; therefore, this hypothesis requires validation through prospective studies. Finally, this study focused solely on all-cause mortality as an endpoint and lacked functional outcome assessment. For elderly patients, quality of life and functional independence may be more important than survival alone. Future research should incorporate patient-centered multidimensional outcome measures, including mRS scores, activities of daily living, discharge disposition, and other indicators, to comprehensively evaluate FI-LAB's clinical value in predicting outcomes for elderly LVO-AIS patients.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that FI-LAB independently predicts both short-term and long-term mortality risk following EVT in elderly LVO-AIS patients. As an objective and practical assessment tool, FI-LAB can be readily implemented in clinical practice to provide crucial support for risk stratification and individualized treatment decision-making in elderly LVO-AIS patients, potentially improving clinical outcomes in this high-risk population.
