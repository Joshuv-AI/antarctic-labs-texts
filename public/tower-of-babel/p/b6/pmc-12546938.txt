Linear and weakly nonlinear stability analyses of Darcy Bénard convection with feedback control
Authors: P. G. Siddheshwar, Vinit Vinod Revankar, M. S. Jagadeesh Kumar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this paper, the effect of feedback control on the criterion for the onset of Darcy–Bénard convection \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( \textrm{DBC}\right)$$\end{document} in a horizontal Boussinesq Newtonian fluid is studied theoretically. The bounding isothermal lower and upper surfaces are considered to be rigid. The single term Galerkin method \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( \textrm{STGM}\right)$$\end{document} and the Maclaurin series expansion \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( \textrm{MSE}\right)$$\end{document} are combined with the Newton-Raphson method \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( \textrm{NRM}\right)$$\end{document} of three variables to perform a linear stability analysis \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( \textrm{LSA}\right)$$\end{document} in order to determine eigen value. To make a weakly nonlinear stability analysis \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( \textrm{WNLSA}\right)$$\end{document} of the system, a Vadasz Lorenz model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( \textrm{VLM}\right)$$\end{document} is constructed. The model’s various properties are discovered to be identical to those of the standard Lorenz model. The \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{VLM}$$\end{document} exhibits both dissipative and conservative characteristics and the bounded nature of its solution is demonstrated by the trapping region, which takes the form of an ellipsoid. The Hopf-Rayleigh number determined from the autonomous dynamical system predicts the onset of chaos. The influence of the controller gain parameter and the Biot number on the onset of convection has been analyzed. Results from the study reveal that the controller gain parameter stabilizes the system and further delays the onset of chaos. Overall, the study establishes that an increase in the Biot number promotes long-term periodic motion over chaotic behavior, while an increase in the controller gain parameter enlarges the trapping region, thereby contributing to improved system stability.

Introduction

The mathematical modelling of the classical Rayleigh-Bénard-convection1,2 problem in a medium with low porosity is framed as the Darcy–Bénard-convection \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( \textrm{DBC}\right)$$\end{document} problem. This problem was first theoretically studied by Horton and Rogers3 and Lapwood4 and hence is also known in the literature as the “ Horton-Rogers-Lapwood ” problem. Drawing inspiration from the pioneering works carried out by Horton and Rogers and Lapwood, many researchers dedicated considerable effort in advancing the field. Many investigators (Katto and Masuoka5, Banu and Rees6, Barletta and Rees7, Siddheshwar and Lakshmi8, Suthar et al.9, Lagziri and Bezzazi10) have further explored and analyzed the problem in detail. In a porous media, Katto and Masuoka5 examined the stability of a fluid layer subjected to a vertical temperature gradient. Banu and Rees6 performed a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{LSA}$$\end{document} by including the local thermal non-equilibrium \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( \textrm{LTNE}\right)$$\end{document} effects on the onset of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{DBC}$$\end{document}. A similar study carried out by Barletta and Rees7 considered constant heat flux boundary condition. Siddheshwar and Lakshmi8 performed both linear and nonlinear stability analyses of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{DBC}$$\end{document} of Newtonian and Newtonian nanoliquids in cylindrical enclosures and cylindrical annuli. They demonstrated that taking into account the heat transport in a tall cylindrical annulus can greatly increase it in a water-saturated porous material. Suthar et al.9 in their study made use of matrix differential operator theory to perform a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{LSA}$$\end{document} on the onset of thermally modulated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{DBC}$$\end{document}. The study reported by Lagziri and Bezzazi10 included the effect of Robin boundary on both upper and lower surfaces. Siddheshwar11 showed the introduction of rough boundary condition has the potential to hinder convective instabilities in RBC. Siddheshwar et al.12 investigated the onset of DBC by incorporating phase lag effects in a thermal non-equilibrium model, leading to an extended VLM with three phase lag parameters. Their analysis reveal that the phase lag influences both the onset of regular convection and the nature of chaos in the system. Siddheshwar et al.13 introduced a Frank-Kamenetskii-VLM model that incorporates the effects of combustion. They concluded that the combustion advances the onset of both regular convection and chaotic convection. Bansal and Suthar14 investigated the effect of temperature modulation on chaos and heat transfer in DBC using a LTNE model. They made use of largest Lyapunov exponents and the bifurcation diagrams to quantify chaos in the system. They concluded that a small modulation frequency delays the onset of chaos compared to the unmodulated frequency modulation case. Siddheshwar et al.15 considered the problem of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{DBC}$$\end{document} with Dirichlet boundary condition on the lower surface and Robin boundary condition on the upper surface and studied the onset of chaos on it. Kanchana et al.16 reported that in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{DBC}$$\end{document} problem with a uniform vertical cross-flow, it is possible to successfully control the commencement of both regular convection and the chaotic motion. Suppression of the onset of convection can be achieved by considering an external magnetic field \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( \textrm{magnetohydrodynamics}\right)$$\end{document}17,18, rotation19, heat source/sink20 and the use of feedback control strategy. The idea of using the concept of feedback control strategy consists of a combination of finite number of sensors and actuators in the experimental setup of fluid confined between two infinitely horizontal plates with heating from below and relative cooling from below. This was first theoretically and experimentally studied by Tang and Bau21,22. They succeeded in demonstrating that it is possible to maintain the no-motion conductive state for the Rayleigh number beyond the critical Rayleigh number \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( R_{c}\right)$$\end{document}, which in absence of controller would have developed instabilities (onset of convection). Expanding on the innovative findings of feedback control strategy in RBC, similar findings were achieved by Tang and Bau23 in a fluid contained inside a heated horizontal porous layer. Or and Kelly24 performed a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{WNLSA}$$\end{document} of Rayleigh–Bénard–Marangoni \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( \textrm{RBM}\right)$$\end{document} convection in the presence of feedback control. Bachok and Arifin25 performed a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{LSA}$$\end{document} to show that the onset of convection can be delayed with the use of feedback control strategy in Marangoni–Bénard convection in the presence of internal heat generation. In the modelling of Marangoni–Bénard convection, Samoilava and Nepomnyashehy26 investigated the stability of the no-motion state using a linear control strategy. The study primarily utilized two forms of linear proportional controllers: The first controller aimed to identify instability by detecting free surface deflections, while the second controller focused on detecting the transition of fluid from the conductive state to the convective state. Vadasz27 investigated the control of chaos in porous medium convection, whereas Kanchana et al.28 investigated the control of regular convection, chaos, and periodic motion in the RBC using a feedback control system. The study on the feedback control mechanism in porous media convection is instrumental in optimizing processes across geothermal energy, enhanced oil recovery, and chemical reactors that utilize porous catalysts for improved reaction efficiency. In addition to it, a few examples of feedback control applications found in the literature. In order to monitor the arbitrary bounded smooth reference signal, Chen et al.29 proposed a nonlinear robust feedback control. Chen30 expanded the analysis for the 5D Lorenz model. Tannu31 designed a quotient controller targeted at stabilizing the chaotic flow found in a single-loop thermosyphon system with a high heat index. To lessen system complexity, Tao et al.32 suggested of using a three distinct feedback control mechanisms to the Lorenz model. The current work is practically significant because it incorporates a feedback control strategy and use of the Robin boundary condition on temperature to study the onset of both regular and chaotic convection, which are important in industrial and technological processes like heat exchangers and geothermal systems. Furthermore, determining the cause for the delay of chaotic convection is essential in order to maintain the stability and effectiveness of processes utilizing porous media, including chemical reactors and oil recovery filtering. The Biot number and the control gain parameter aid in the design of systems with improved heat transmission and accurate control. This undoubtedly helps to improve thermal system energy efficiency, making the process more sustainable and cost-effective.

From the literature survey covered above, it is evident that the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{WNLSA}$$\end{document} of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{DBC}$$\end{document} problem in the presence of a feedback control strategy and the Robin boundary condition on the temperature at the upper surface has not been performed in the literature. The current paper studies the dynamical behavior of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{DBC}$$\end{document} system in the presence of feedback control and the Robin boundary condition on temperature at the upper surface.

Results and discussion

The primary objective of the paper was to study the influence of feedback control and the effect of Robin boundary condition on temperature on the stability and chaos in DBC. A feedback-control setup typically includes sensors that measure system variables and actuators that apply control actions; each actuator is usually paired with one or more sensors (not necessarily physically “underneath” the actuator). Sensors measure the system state and provide signals (measurements or errors relative to a reference basic state) to the controller; the controller then computes and sends commands to the actuator to reduce the deviation. Upon receiving the controller’s command (computed from sensor feedback), the actuator implements a control action designed to damp perturbations and restore the system toward the reference state. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {K}$$\end{document} is the feedback gain: increasing \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {K}$$\end{document} amplifies the control action (within stability limits) and typically increases the rate at which perturbations are suppressed. In a quiescent layer heating from below, heat is initially transported by conduction; if the imposed temperature difference (or Rayleigh number) exceeds a critical value, buoyancy overcomes viscous and conductive damping and convection sets in52. The Biot number Bi is the ratio of the surface (convective) heat-transfer resistance to the internal conductive resistance of the layer; it quantifies how strongly the boundary enforces a temperature than the interior conduction does. For small Bi (small \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{h_{eff}~d}{\kappa _{eff}}$$\end{document}), surface heat exchange is weak compared with internal conduction; the boundary approximates an insulating condition and heat transport is dominated by conduction within the fluid. An insulating (small-Bi) boundary allows larger internal temperature gradients to develop for a given heat flux; if the Rayleigh number exceeds \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_c$$\end{document}, these gradients will drive convective instability. For large Bi (large \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{h_{eff}~d}{\kappa _{eff}}$$\end{document}), the boundary efficiently exchanges heat with the surroundings and behaves nearly isothermal; internal temperature differences are readily removed by the boundary, stabilizing the layer. Because a large-Bi boundary sinks or supplies heat efficiently, it prevents large internal temperature gradients from forming; the resulting weaker buoyancy lowers instability growth rates and increases the critical Rayleigh number.

The size of the Bénard cells formed is determined by the wave number, a. It is well known that a higher value of a indicates smaller cell and vice versa. Physically, a larger \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathscr {K}$$\end{document} implies increased damping of perturbations; therefore the system requires a greater thermal forcing (higher scaled Rayleigh number) to destabilize the conductive base state and transition first to sustained convection (limit cycles) and, at higher forcing, to chaotic behavior.

After the division of the maxima and minima branches, the bifurcation diagram prominently reveals two key features: periodicity windows representing stable, repeating dynamics, and chaotic regimes marked by irregular, unpredictable behavior. Chaotic regimes display aperiodic trajectories with strong sensitivity to initial conditions. In bifurcation diagrams they appear as densely filled vertical bands or scattered points, reflecting a continuous distribution of extrema rather than discrete branches; this indicates the system does not settle onto fixed points or simple limit cycles in those parameter ranges. Within chaotic parameter intervals narrow periodicity windows can appear—these correspond to stable periodic orbits embedded in chaos and appear as distinct continuous branches (solid lines) in the bifurcation diagram. The emergence of periodicity windows signifies that the system must revert to a periodic orbit or a limit cycle to save energy efficiently. We now shift our focus towards the Lyapunov exponent. The Lyapunov exponent is yet another important tool for investigating the dynamical behavior of the system. In an autonomous dynamical system describing a fluid flow, a minimum of a fourth-dimension autonomous dynamical system is required for the occurrence of hyperchaos: more than one positive Lyapunov exponent. Since the obtained VLM is three-dimensional, it cannot exhibit hyperchaotic behavior. Therefore, it is sufficient to plot only the largest Lyapunov exponent (LLE) in order to investigate chaotic motion in the system. To do it, we employed the Benettin’s algorithm53, which is a suitable and efficient way to find LLE for a three-dimensional system, rather than obtaining a full Lyapunov spectrum approach proposed by Wolf et al.54, which might be useful for a higher-dimensional system where there may be a chance of occurrence of hyperchaos. The Benettin algorithm considers a reference trajectory along with a set of infinitesimally close perturbation vectors to observe the behavior of the reference trajectory with the set of perturbation vectors over time. This algorithm makes use of the Gram-Schmidt reorthogonalization procedure to track their average growth or decay over time. LLE quantifies the average exponential rate at which nearby trajectories diverge in a dynamical system.
