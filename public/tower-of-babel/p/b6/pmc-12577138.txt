Association between atherogenic index and risk of falls: a decade-long prospective cohort study
Authors: Jingwen Liu, Wenfeng Lu, Yuan He, Yaqi Wang, Xueying Xu, Qingyun Lv, Hairong Chang, Li Fu, Xia Chen, Yue Zhao, Xiaoying Zang, Xiaonan Zhang, Na Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo investigate the association between the Atherogenic Index (AI) and several other untraditional lipid parameters with the risk of falls. Background

Falls among middle-aged and older individuals represent a significant public health challenge [1]. It is the predominant cause of injuries and injury-related fatalities in older adults [2]. Notably, approximately one-third of individuals aged 65 and older experience falls annually, a rate that escalates with advancing age [3, 4]. The ramifications of falls are grave, encompassing fractures, prolonged pain, diminished quality of life, and even death [5, 6]. A growing body of evidence suggests that various cardiovascular diseases are associated with an increased risk of falls [7, 8], emphasizing the importance of timely cardiovascular assessment in fall patients [9]. These findings underscore the need to prioritize fall prevention and intervention strategies in clinical practice.

Although falls have been extensively studied over the years, the predominant methodologies for assessing risk of falls still rely primarily on questionnaires and physical tests, including evaluations of balance, gait, and muscular strength [10, 11]. While these approaches can effectively identify high-risk individuals to a degree, they present several limitations. Questionnaires depend on self-reported data, which can be compromised by memory biases or subjective perceptions, potentially leading to inaccuracies [12]. Physical tests are typically conducted in controlled environments, which may not accurately represent an individual’s daily conditions or the impact of environmental factors [13]. Moreover, traditional assessment techniques tend to focus predominantly on external performance metrics such as balance and gait, while often neglecting the significant influence of internal biochemical changes on falls. In recent years, research has particularly emphasized the relevance of biochemical markers, including hormone levels and inflammatory indicators, in determining risk of fallss [14, 15]. Biochemical indicators, which can be obtained conveniently and efficiently through blood tests, may provide a more objective and accessible means of risk of falls assessment. When combined with traditional assessment methods, they offer a more comprehensive evaluation framework.

The atherogenic index (AI) is calculated as the ratio of total cholesterol (TC) minus high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) to HDL-C [16]. An elevated AI, which represents a higher ratio of total cholesterol to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, may reflect abnormalities in lipid metabolism within the body. High AI are commonly linked with atherosclerosis and cardiovascular diseases [17, 18], which in turn could heighten the risk of falls through several mechanisms. Firstly, atherosclerosis can lead to insufficient cerebral perfusion [19], impairing balance control and muscle responsiveness [20, 21], thereby increasing the likelihood of falls. Secondly, cardiovascular diseases may accompany reduced physical strength and unstable gait [22], escalating the risk of falling. Additionally, these conditions may be associated with impaired neurological function [23], further affecting the coordination and safety of movements. These associations suggest a potential link between AI and risk of falls. However, research directly investigating this relationship remains relatively limited.

Hence, this study aimed to investigate the association between AI and several other untraditional lipid parameters with the risk of falls. The findings may offer novel insights for the prevention and management of falls among middle-aged and older adults.

Methods

Data source and study population

Data were obtained from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), an initiative designed to collect high-quality and nationally representative data from households and individuals aged ≥ 45 years, to analyse the ageing trends in China. The CHARLS uses a four-stage stratified cluster sampling design, including 150 county units and 450 village units. Comprehensive details regarding the CHARLS survey design can be found in prior research publications [24]. The CHARLS datasets can be downloaded from the CHARLS homepage at http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en. The Ethics Committee of the Peking University Health Science Center approved the CHARLS. The ethical approval number is IRB00001052-11015. All participants provided written informed consent for participation.

The study involved five waves, starting with a baseline survey in 2011 comprising 17,708 community residents (Wave 1), and subsequent surveys in 2013 (Wave 2), 2015 (Wave 3), 2018 (Wave 4), and 2020 (Wave 5). The venous blood samples were collected by trained staff after participants had fasted for at least 12 h overnight in 2011 and 2015. On site, complete blood count was conducted immediately. Blood specimens were held at 4 degrees Celsius for further analysis, and all other samples were sent to a central laboratory located in Beijing. TC, HDL-C, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and triglycerides (TG) were assessed using enzymatic colorimetric tests.

We initially included 11,847 participants who provided blood samples during the first wave of collection. Participants who did not provide blood samples during the third wave of collection (n = 2,441), as well as those who were lost to follow-up or deceased from wave1 to wave 5 (n = 3,165), were excluded from the analysis. Consequently, a total of 6,241 eligible participants were incorporated into our study (Fig. 1).

The selection process of study participants in the CHARLS

Assessment of untraditional lipid parameters

TC, HDL-C, LDL-C and TG in fasting blood plasma were measured as previously described. Based on prior research findings, the untraditional lipid parameters analyzed in this study reflect the averages derived from data collected in 2011 and 2015 [25, 26]. The specific calculation methods for each untraditional lipid parameters were as follows:

AI = (TC − HDL-C)/HDL-C;

AI = (TC − HDL-C)/HDL-C;

Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP) = log (TG/HDL-C);

Atherogenic Index of Plasma (AIP) = log (TG/HDL-C);

Non-HDL Cholesterol (non-HDL-C) = TC − HDL-C;

Non-HDL Cholesterol (non-HDL-C) = TC − HDL-C;

Remnant Cholesterol (RC) = TC − LDL-C − HDL-C.

Remnant Cholesterol (RC) = TC − LDL-C − HDL-C.

Assessment of falls

Falls were the primary outcome of interest in this study. During each survey of the CHARLS, participants were queried about their fall history through the self-reported question, ‘Have you experienced any falls in the past two years?’ Those responding affirmatively were categorized as having a history of falls. Throughout the decade-long follow-up period, participants were categorized as having a history of falls if they reported any fall event at any survey wave.

Assessment of covariates

The covariates included in this study were age, gender, educational level, marital status, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, drinking status, and the presence of chronic conditions, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes or high blood sugar, chronic lung diseases, cardiovascular disease, and stroke. All covariate data were obtained from the baseline survey (Wave 1) of the CHARLS.

Handling of missing variables

Missing data were as follows: age (14 instances); gender (3 instances); educational level (8 instances); marital status (4 instances); BMI (764 instances); smoking status (20 instances); drinking status (22 instances); TC for 2011 (112 instances); HDL-C for 2011 (219 instances); TC for 2015 (35 instances); HDL-C for 2015 (200 instances); and falls (93 instances). For chronic conditions, missing values were observed for hypertension (54 instances), dyslipidemia (152 instances), diabetes or high blood sugar (78 instances), chronic lung disease (43 instances), cardiovascular disease (56 instances), and stroke (42 instances). Assuming that these missing values were missing at random, multiple imputation was employed to address this issue and preserve as large a sample size as possible, with the variable imputation conducted five times.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables following a normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas those with a skewed distribution were presented as median and interquartile range (25th–75th percentile). Between-group differences were assessed using the t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. To evaluate the association between AI and the risk of falls, logistic regression models were employed to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex. Model 3 included additional adjustments for educational level, marital status, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, and alcohol consumption. Model 4 further adjusted for the presence of chronic conditions, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes or hyperglycemia, chronic lung disease, cardiovascular disease, and stroke. Untraditional lipid parameters were categorized into quartiles to serve as the basis for the final model, thereby ensuring the robustness of the results. The model assessed the relationship between lipid quartiles and the risk of falls, using the lowest quartile as the reference category. In addition, a multivariable-adjusted restricted cubic spline (RCS) logistic regression with four knots was conducted to explore potential linear and dose-response relationships between AI and the risk of falls. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were also performed to identify possible effect modifiers. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were subsequently constructed, and the area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated. Additionally, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by excluding participants who had experienced falls at baseline. This was done to assess the robustness of the study’s findings.

Statistical analysis was carried out using SPSS version 26.0 and R version 4.3.1, with the ‘mice’ package employed for imputation of missing data, and the ‘rms’, ‘ggplot2’, and ‘plotRCS’ packages used for restricted cubic spline analysis. Forest plots were generated using the ‘forestplot’ package. Statistical significance in the analysis was indicated by a two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of participants

A total of 6,241 participants were included in this study, among whom 3,188 reported no history of falls, while 3,053 reported experiencing at least one fall. The median age of participants was 71 years, and 43.6% were male. 41.4% had completed primary school or lower educational level and 91.0% were married. Regarding BMI, 54.2% of participants had values within the 18.5–23.9 kg/m² range. Additionally, 62.9% were non-smokers, and 68.0% reported no alcohol consumption. With respect to chronic conditions, 24.6% had hypertension, 9.9% had dyslipidemia, 5.5% had diabetes or high blood sugar, 9.5% had chronic lung diseases, 11.4% had cardiovascular disease, and 1.9% had experienced a stroke. Relationship between untraditional lipid parameters and risk of falls

The relationship between various untraditional lipid parameters and risk of falls is summarized in Fig. 2, which presents the results of logistic regression analyses examining the relationship between different categories of untraditional lipid parameters and the risk of falls. Four covariate models were constructed to elucidate this association, each with varying levels of adjustment. In the fully adjusted model, AI showed a statistically significant association with falls. Compared to the lowest quartile (Q1), higher AI levels (Q2–Q4) were associated with increased odds of falls, with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) of 1.27 (1.10–1.47) for Q2, 1.22 (1.06–1.42) for Q3, and 1.29 (1.11–1.50) for Q4. Furthermore, when AI was treated as a continuous variable, each one-unit increase in AI was significantly associated with higher odds of falls, with an OR of 1.05 (95% CI: 1.00–1.10).

Associations between various untraditional lipid parameters and risk of falls across different regression models

Figure 3 displays the results of RCS analyses assessing the associations between various untraditional lipid parameters and the risk of falls. A statistically significant overall association was observed for both AI and AIP (P-overall = 0.023 for AI; P-overall = 0.027 for AIP), indicating dose–response relationships. However, the nonlinearity tests were not statistically significant (P-nonlinear = 0.080 for AI; P-nonlinear = 0.060 for AIP). No significant associations were found for non-HDL-C (P-overall = 0.861) or RC (P-overall = 0.450).

RCS analyses of the associations between various untraditional lipid parameters and the risk of falls

The ability of untraditional lipid parameters to predict the risk of falls

To compare the predictive performance of various untraditional lipid parameters, ROC curves were generated for each logistic regression model. The results indicated that models incorporating AI consistently outperformed those based on other untraditional lipid parameters. In the fully adjusted model (Model 4), the AI-based model achieved an AUC of 0.621, representing the highest predictive accuracy among all untraditional lipid parameters. Detailed results are presented in Fig. 4.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the applicability of AI across different population strata. Participants were stratified by age, gender, educational level, marital status, BMI, smoking status, alcohol consumption, and the presence of chronic conditions, including hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes or high blood sugar, chronic lung diseases, cardiovascular disease, and stroke. The results revealed a significant interaction between age and AI (p for interaction = 0.021). Specifically, among individuals aged below 65 years, ORs for falls in the Q2 and Q3 AI categories compared to Q1 were 1.53 (95% CI: 1.15–2.02) and 1.66 (95% CI: 1.24–2.22), respectively. In contrast, among individuals aged 65 years and older, the ORs for Q2 and Q4 compared to Q1 were 1.19 (95% CI: 1.00–1.41) and 1.30 (95% CI: 1.09–1.55), respectively. These findings suggest that the impact of AI on risk of falls is more pronounced in individuals under 65 years of age, highlighting age as a potential modifier of this association. No statistically significant interactions were observed in other subgroups, as all corresponding interaction P-values were greater than 0.05. Detailed associations between AI and risk of falls across each subgroup are presented in Figs. 5 and 6.

Sensitivity analyses

In the sensitivity analyses, after excluding participants who had experienced falls at baseline, the results indicated that higher AI levels remained significantly associated with an increased risk of falls among the 5,067 participants who had not experienced falls. These findings were consistent with those observed in the entire study cohort, thereby confirming the robustness of the study. Detailed results are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Discussion

This study investigated the association between AI and several other untraditional lipid parameters with the risk of falls. The results showed that higher AI levels were significantly associated with an increased risk of falls, with AI demonstrating the highest predictive accuracy among all untraditional lipid parameters examined. Subgroup analysis further revealed that the impact of AI on risk of falls was more pronounced in individuals under 65 years of age.

This study found that higher levels of AI are associated with an increased risk of falls. Previous research has demonstrated that untraditional lipid parameters such as AI and AIP reflect various health conditions. For instance, Zhang et al. found a positive association between AI and hearing loss in the general population. Specifically, each doubling increase in AI was significantly associated with a 62% increase in the risk of hearing loss (OR = 1.62, 95% CI: 1.21–2.17) [16]. Mardi et al. identified a strong link between AI with an increased risk of metabolic syndrome, suggesting these indicators could serve as potential predictors of metabolic syndrome in clinical practice [27]. Furthermore, Zhang et al. reported a robust association between AIP and the likelihood of vertebral fractures (adjusted hazard ratio (aHR) = 0.27, 95%CI = 0.10–0.71 for continuous AIP), indicating that low AIP may be an associated factor [28]. Hu et al. reported that the risk of developing cardiovascular diseases increased significantly with higher AIP levels. Specifically, the risk ratio was 1.31 (95% CI, 1.11–1.55) for each unit increase in AIP [29]. These findings help explain the association between AI, AIP, and risk of falls identified in the current study. For example, hearing loss can impair an individual’s ability to perceive their environment, thus affecting balance and safe movement [30]. Metabolic syndrome’s features, such as insulin resistance, hypertension, and obesity, can impair neurological function, leading to sensory deficits and slower lower limb responses [31]. Fetal malnutrition can have long-lasting effects on the skeletal and muscular systems, which may contribute to an increased risk of falls [32]. Given the strong associations between AI, AIP, and these risk factors, they may serve as potential indicators of risk of falls. This study also found that AI provided the highest predictive accuracy among all untraditional lipid parameters, suggesting that AI could be considered as a primary lipid parameter in future risk of falls assessments.

This study also found that the impact of AI on the risk of falls was more pronounced in individuals under 65 years of age. One possible explanation is that this age group tends to maintain a higher level of physical activity [33], making them more susceptible to factors that impair balance or physical function, thereby significantly increasing risk of falls [34]. In contrast, although individuals aged 65 and older also exhibited an elevated risk of falls with increasing AI, the magnitude of this increase was less prominent—possibly due to generally lower levels of physical activity among older adults [35]. Similar findings have been reported in previous studies. For example, Li et al. observed a significant association between AIP and the incidence of hypertension, particularly among individuals younger than 65 years. Specifically, for participants aged 1–39 years, the HR was 2.53 (95% CI: 1.46–4.38), while for those aged 40–64 years, the HR was 1.47 (95% CI: 1.09–1.98) [36]. Likewise, Mo et al. reported that the correlation between AIP and hypertension risk was especially evident in individuals aged 45 to 60 with normotensive status (OR = 2.55, 95% CI: 1.99–3.28) [37]. This may be attributed to the fact that middle-aged individuals are more likely to exhibit metabolic syndrome [38], insulin resistance [39], and heightened chronic inflammation [40], all of which can amplify the relationship between untraditional lipid parameters and disease risk. In contrast, the elderly often present with multiple comorbidities [41], introducing more potential confounding factors that may attenuate the strength of such associations. These mechanisms may help explain the findings of the present study—namely, that the effect of AI on risk of falls is more significant in individuals under the age of 65. This suggests that AI could be considered a primary lipid parameter for risk of falls assessment in middle-aged populations. However, in older adults, more research is needed to identify more suitable or sensitive indicators.

Nevertheless, it is crucial to recognise the specific limitations of this study. First, relying on older adults to recall fall incidents over a two-year period introduces potential recall bias. Future studies should consider shorter follow-up intervals to improve the accuracy of fall-related data. Second, this study utilizes the average of the AI from 2011 to 2015, as participants in the CHARLS study underwent blood tests only in these two years. These biochemical markers in the human body typically remain relatively stable in the short term without significant medical interventions or lifestyle changes, using the average of the AI to predict falls is meaningful. However, this approach may not capture subtle changes in biochemical markers over time, potentially resulting in less precise predictions. Consequently, future studies should increase the frequency and duration of data collection to more thoroughly investigate the correlations between changes in these markers and risk of falls, thereby enhancing the prediction and prevention of falls. Third, although this study employed the AI as a combined biochemical indicator, its calculation is based on TC and HDL-C, which may predominantly reflect cardiovascular health issues. Therefore, future research should incorporate a broader range of biomarkers, such as allostatic load, to provide a more comprehensive understanding of an individual’s overall health status and more accurately assess the risk of falling.

Conclusions

This study revealed a critical relationship between elevated AI and an increased risk of falls. These findings underscore the potential of incorporating AI measurements alongside traditional assessment methods to offer a more comprehensive evaluation framework for reducing risk of falls. Future research should delve deeper into the mechanisms connecting various intrinsic physiological and biochemical indicators with risk of falls by expanding the range of biochemical markers and incorporating longitudinal data collection. This approach can provide a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between these indicators and risk of falls, guiding more effective fall prevention strategies.
