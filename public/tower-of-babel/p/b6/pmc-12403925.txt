Comparative mitogenomics reveals evolutionary drivers of Strongyloidea nematodes dwelling in gastrointestinal tract
Authors: Junyan Li, Xiaochao Zhao, Pengwei Xu, Juan Lai, Gaowa Gong, Penglong Wang, Yanni Zhang, Xuerou Chen, Fang Guo, Xiaomin Wang, Yongfeng Liu, Xiaoping Luo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe nematode superfamily Strongyloidea represents a prevalent group of gastrointestinal parasites in ruminants and livestock, posing substantial veterinary and economic burdens worldwide. Here we present the first mitochondrial genomic investigation for reconstructing Strongyloidea phylogeny and investigating niche-specific selection dynamics across gastrointestinal habitats (abomasum, small intestine, and large intestine). This study presents a preliminary exploration of the evolutionary adaptations of these agriculturally relevant parasites through comprehensive mitogenomic analysis.ResultsPhylogenomic reconstruction revealed strong anatomical clustering with high nodal support. Comparative synteny analyses revealed conserved mitogenome architectures across Strongyloidea species, characterized by pronounced AT-richness. Codon usage patterns showed high consistency in Strongyloide (94% A/U-ending preferred codons), with ENC-plot and neutrality analyses confirming natural selection as the dominant driver. Pervasive purifying selection was observed, but abomasal species showed higher ω values than intestinal counterparts, particularly in ND4. In addition, positive selection was detected exclusively in CYTB within the small intestinal lineage. RELAX analysis identified differential selection signals among the three branches, with significant relaxation observed in ND5 and ND6 genes in the abomasal species. Amino acid polymorphism analyses revealed higher sequence variation in ND genes compared to the highly conserved COX genes.ConclusionsOur findings demonstrated niche-associated evolutionary trajectories between abomasal and intestinal Strongyloidea branches, reflected in both phylogenetic patterns and molecular evolution rates. The mitogenomic framework established here provided a foundation for future studies integrating expanded datasets, genomic data, and microenvironmental parameters to elucidate precise niche adaptation mechanisms in these economically important parasites.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-11980-5.Keywords: Strongyloidea, Mitochondrial genome, Abomasum, Large intestine, Small intestine, Selection pressure, Phylogenetic relationships

Background

The superfamily Strongyloidea comprises several well-known genera of nematodes, including Ostertagia, Teladorsagia, Trichostrongylus, Haemonchus, Cooperia, Nematodirus, and Dictyocaulus. These parasites primarily infect the gastrointestinal tracts of ruminants and other domestic animals, posing significant veterinary challenges and causing substantial economic losses to livestock industries [1, 2]. Consequently, considerable scientific interest has been garnered in exploring their biological characteristics, phylogenetic relationships and adaptive evolution to inform the development of effective prophylaxis strategies. Recently, genomic information has advanced the study of nematodes in the areas of population genetics, comparative genomics, and evolutionary biology [3–5].

Over the past two decades, the small subunit ribosomal DNA (SSU rDNA), particularly the 18 S rRNA gene, has served as the most widely used nuclear marker in nematode phylogenetic studies. This is attributed to its remarkable degree of conservation and extensive taxonomic coverage [6–8]. For instance, Chilton et al. elucidated the phylogenetic relationships of the different nematode groups within the order Strongylida using 18 S and 28 S rRNAs [9]. Similarly, phylogenetic reconstruction of Trichostrongylidae was mainly based on ecological and morphological characteristics, along with the SSU rDNA [10, 11].

Despite the advantages of SSU rDNA in phylogenetic research at higher taxonomy levels, it lacks sufficient resolving power to discriminate between closely related taxa due to their high evolutionary conservation [12, 13]. In the phylogenetic analysis of the superfamily Strongyloidea, Hosseinnezhad et al. demonstrated that the internal transcribed spacer 2 region within the nuclear rDNA was not a discriminative marker for the families Haemonchidae and Cooperiidae [14]. In addition, the use of nuclear rDNA markers for nematode phylogenies is often challenged by the sensitivity of sequence alignment to length heterogeneity across taxa [15]. Given these limitations, mitochondrial genomes (mitogenomes) are emerging as a powerful alternative or complement to traditionally used molecular markers, providing superior phylogenetic resolution and more precise divergence date estimates [16–19]. Mitogenomes are considered ideal tools for population genetics and systematics studies due to their maternal inheritance, high mutation rate, small genome size, low sequence recombination, and fast evolutionary rates [18–21]. While mitogenomes also showed notable limitations in phylogenetic research. For instance, the mitogenome essentially functions as a single genetic locus, and the lack of independence among its constituent genes may reduce the statistical power of phylogenetic inferences [22, 23]. Additionally, the mitogenome represents only mitochondrial evolutionary history, which may not correspond to species-level evolutionary patterns [24]. At present, studies investigating the mitogenomes of the Strongyloidea superfamily for phylogenetic relationship, species identification and adaptive evolution are limited [1, 25–27].

Mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) is a fundamental metabolic process that depends on the tightly coordinated expression of both mitochondrial (mtDNA) and nuclear DNA (nDNA)-encoded genes. The evolutionary dynamics of mito-nuclear interactions have emerged as a critical area of research in evolutionary and cellular biology [28, 29]. The high mutation rate and absence of recombination predispose mtDNA to the accumulation of deleterious mutations [30]. This genetic instability creates persistent selective pressure on the nuclear genome to evolve compensatory mechanisms that sustain mitochondrial function [31, 32]. A compelling demonstration of such mito-nuclear interplay is observed in the regulation of the pathogenic 3.1-kb mtDNA deletion variant uaDf5, whose levels can be reduced by inhibiting the activity of pol γ, the nuclear-encoded mtDNA polymerase [33, 34]. Therefore, eukaryotic adaptive evolution is fundamentally influenced by the dynamic, homeostatic interactions between mitochondrial and nuclear genomic systems [32].

In the study of comparative mitochondrial genomics, codon usage bias (CUB) is crucial for understanding genetic evolution and phylogenetic inference. Codon usage characterizes the differences in codon usage for amino acid (AA) translation among species and genes, serving as an indicator of molecular evolution, particularly for species with limited molecular information [35]. Several evolutionary processes, including natural selection, mutation pressure, and genetic drift, have been proposed as the predominant determinants of CUB [35, 36]. Genome GC content, probably the product of directional mutation pressure or nucleotide level selective pressure [37], also influences codon usage and AA composition [38]. Previous studies on codon usage in Caenorhabditis elegans have highlighted the role of optimal codons in translational efficiency, preferentially decoded by iso-accepting tRNAs with the highest gene copy numbers [39, 40]. Despite extensive research on CUB in nuclear genomes, investigations into mitochondrial CUB remain relatively limited. To the best of our knowledge, mitochondrial CUB within the Strongyloidea superfamily has not yet been explored.

Mitochondria play pivotal roles in respiration and energy production, supporting diverse cellular activities. These activities also drive the evolution of the mitogenomes. The protein-coding genes (PCGs) within mitogenomes are crucial for adaptive evolution, as they directly influence metabolic performance and are thus highly sensitive to energy-related selective pressures [41]. Studies have found that the selective pressures can act directly on mitochondrial energy metabolism, driving the adaptation of organisms to meet the diverse energy demands dictated by their unique ecological niches [42–44]. The adaptive evolution of mitochondrial genes is closely associated with several environmental factors, including temperature, energy metabolism demands, altitude, and oxygen availability [42, 45]. In ruminants, distinct anatomical regions of the digestive tract, such as the abomasum, small and large intestines, exhibit varying physicochemical factors (pH, O₂, temperature, etc.), shaping unique ecological niches. The abomasum displayed the lowest pH of the ruminant digestive tract, ranging from 2 to 4, while the pH of most gut sections ranges from 5 to 7 [46]. These differences may exert different selective pressures on the mitochondrial genomes of the corresponding parasitic nematodes [47]. These selective pressures drive the adaptive evolution of mitochondrial genes to help the parasites optimizing their metabolic performance and energy metabolism to thrive in their specific environments.

Focusing on the mitogenomes, our study aims to explore the phylogenetic relationships and genetic divergence within the Strongyloidea superfamily. Additionally, by examining the mitochondrial CUB and selective pressures within this superfamily, we seek to elucidate the adaptive evolution of mitochondrial genes in response to distinct ecological niches. These findings will inform the development of more effective strategies for parasitic control and management.

Method

Data collection

Eleven nematodes were specifically selected from the superfamily Strongyloidea for comparative analysis. These nematodes are characterized by their parasitism in different parts of the host’s digestive system. The abomasum parasitic species included Haemonchus contortus, Haemonchus placei, Marshallagia marshalli, Mecistocirrus digitatus, Ostertagia ostertagi, Teladorsagia circumcincta, and Trichostrongylus axei. For those parasitizing the small intestine, Nematodirus oiratianus and Nematodirus spathiger were chosen. As for the large intestine, the selection comprised Oesophagostomum asperum and Oesophagostomum columbianum. The free-living nematode C. elegans was included as an outgroup for comparative purposes. The detailed information of the 12 mitogenomes is listed in Table S1.

Phylogenetics and synteny analysis

The nucleotide and amino acid sequences of 12 PCGs (ATP8 was missing in nematode mitogenomes) and two ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes from each species were retrieved using PhyloSuite v1.2.2 [48]. The amino acid sequences of the 12 PCGs were individually aligned using MAFFT v7.525 [49] with the “G-INS-I” strategy, and the corresponding codon alignments were performed using PAL2NAL v14 [50]. Two rRNA genes were individually aligned using MAFFT with the “X-INS-I” strategy [49]. Poorly aligned regions in the amino acid, codon, and rRNA alignments were removed using Gblocks v0.9b [51] with parameters (-b1 = 7 -b2 = 10 -p = n -a = y), in protein (-t = p), codon (-t = c), or nucleotide (-t = d) mode as appropriate.

Codon position-specific datasets (first, third, and first–second codon positions) were extracted from the codon alignments using PhyloSuite v1.2.2 [48]. FASconCAT-G v1.06 [52] was used to concatenate gene alignments and generate eight datasets: (1) PCG1 (the first position of 12 PCGs) containing 3,319 sites; (2) PCG1R (the first position of 12 PCGs and two rRNAs) containing 4,656 sites; (3) PCG3 (the third position of 12 PCGs) containing 3,319 sites; (4) PCG3R (the third position of 12 PCGs and two rRNAs) containing 4,656 sites; (5) PCG12 (the first and second positions of 12 PCGs containing 6,638 sites; (6) PCG123 (all codon positions of 12 PCGs) containing 9,957 sites; (7) PCG123R (all codon positions of 12 PCGs and two rRNAs) containing 11,294 sites; (8) AA (amino acid sequences of 12 PCGs) containing 3,251 sites. All alignment was available on Figshare 10.6084/m9.figshare.29364185.v2 [53].

Sequence saturation of each dataset was assessed using the substitution saturation test developed by Xia, et al. [54] as implemented in DAMBE v7.3.11 [55]. Sequence divergence heterogeneity among the nucleotide and amino acid datasets was assessed using the AliGROOVE v1.08 [56], which implements a modified version of the ALISCORE masking algorithm. Analyses were conducted using a sliding window size of 6 with Monte Carlo resampling to generate pairwise similarity profiles. For amino acid datasets, similarity was scored using the BLOSUM62 substitution matrix; while for nucleotide datasets, a simple match/mismatch scoring scheme was applied. The resulting similarity scores were normalized across the full alignment length and summarized into a similarity matrix, enabling visualization of taxonomic heterogeneity in alignment ambiguity and phylogenetic signal across datasets.

The best-fit partitioning schemes and substitution models were identified using ModelFinder [57] as implemented in the IQ-TREE v2.4.0 [58], based on the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using both Bayesian inference (BI) and maximum likelihood (ML) approaches. For ML analyses, twenty independent tree searches were executed (-runs 20) in IQ-TREE v2.4.0 [58] under model-specific substitution schemes, with search termination triggered after 500 unsuccessful iterations (-nstop 500) and a perturbation intensity of 0.2 (-pers 0.2). Branch support was evaluated using 1,000 SH-like approximate likelihood ratio tests (SH-aLRT) [59] and 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot) replicates [60]. Additionally, site concordance factors (sCFs), representing the proportion of decisive alignment sites supporting each branch in the reference tree, were calculated using IQ-TREE with the updated likelihood optimization flag (-scfl) [61]. The BI analysis was performed using MrBayes v3.2.7a [62], with the best-fit nucleotide substitution models and partitioning schemes (Table S4). Two independent runs with four Markov chains each (three heated and one cold) were run for 100,000 generations, with trees sampled every 100 generations, and the first 25% of samples discarded as burn-in. Convergence was assessed by confirming that the average standard deviation of split frequencies between runs was below 0.01.

To further evaluate the phylogenetic signal and substitution saturation of each dataset, Treeness and Saturation metrics were calculated using PhyKIT v2.0.1 [63] based on the inferred phylogenetic trees and their corresponding alignments. Treeness measures the proportion of total branch length represented by internal branches and reflects the signal-to-noise ratio of a phylogeny [64]. Higher Treeness values are thought to be desirable because they represent a higher signal-to-noise ratio. Saturation quantifies the extent of multiple substitutions at the same site, with values ranging from 0 (complete saturation) to 1 (no saturation), where lower values indicate higher saturation.

To investigate the genomic organization and evolutionary relationships, whole genome alignments were performed using minimap2 [65]. Comparative genomic visualization of the 12 mitochondrial genomes was achieved through the gggenomes package in R [66], enabling detailed examination of syntenic relationships and structural variations.

Relative synonymous codon usages (RSCU) and optimal codons

Investigating codon usage patterns across species provides critical insights into genetic structure and evolutionary trends. The codon usage patterns of PCGs and nucleotide compositions across the 12 mitogenomes were determined by PhyloSuite v1.2.2 [48]. CodonW v1.4.4 [67] was employed to compute the effective number of codon (ENC) and RSCU for each PCG. To identify the optimal codons [68, 69], the 12 mitochondrial genes of each species were ranked by ENC values in descending order. The top and bottom 10% of genes were designated as high-expression and low-expression gene sets, respectively [68, 69]. ΔRSCU between these groups was calculated to assess codon preference. Codon usage patterns were categorized based on the following criteria: (1) Highly expressed codons: those exhibiting ΔRSCU > 0.08; (2) High-frequency codons: those with RSCU > 1; (3) Optimal codons: those satisfying both ΔRSCU > 0.08 and RSCU > 1.

RELAX analysis of habitat-specific selection

Selective constraint shifts were investigated across three gastrointestinal parasitization sites (abomasum, small intestine, and large intestine) in nematodes of the superfamily Strongyloidea using the RELAX algorithm [70] with the invertebrate-mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) codon model. Branches corresponding to each parasitization site were sequentially designated as test branches (abomasum-parasitizing [red], small intestine-parasitizing [blue], or large intestine-parasitizing [green]), while all other branches were treated as reference branches. We focused on terminal branches in this study because they best capture ongoing selection pressure in extant lineages.

The RELAX method was employed to compare the distribution of dN/dS ratios across sites between test and reference branches. Statistical significance of the k parameter was assessed against a null model assuming a single dN/dS distribution across all branches [70]. This approach enabled examination of whether relaxed selection drives evolutionary innovation while increasing functional degradation risk in site-specialized parasitic lineages.

Two datasets were analyzed: (1) a concatenated supergene comprising 12 PCGs, and (2) individual mitochondrial genes evaluated separately for each parasitization site-specific branch set. The HyPhy Vision platform (http://vision.hyphy.org/) was employed for visualization and interpretation of selection analysis results [71]. Significant results with K > 1 (p-value < 0.05) were interpreted as evidence of intensified selection pressure in test branches, while K < 1 (p-value < 0.05) indicated relaxed selection pressure [70].

Selection analysis

Selection pressure on concatenated and individual PCGs was evaluated by estimating the ω (dN/dS) ratios using the CODEML in PAML v4.9 [72]. The free-ratio model (model = 1) was employed to estimate the ω values for each PCG and the concatenated dataset across all species. Extreme ω (dN/dS) values (ω < 0.0001 or ω > 10) were excluded from subsequent analyses [72].

To assess whether selective constraints varied among nematode lineages parasitizing different gastrointestinal regions, statistical comparisons of the ω values for each PCG were performed among the three groups: abomasum-parasitizing, small intestine-parasitizing, and large intestine-parasitizing. Pairwise comparisons were performed using appropriate statistical tests based on the data characteristics: when any group included fewer than three species, a permutation test was used; for normally distributed data with equal variances, a Student’s t-test was applied; if variances were unequal, a Welch’s t-test was conducted; and for non-normally distributed data, a Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used.

To further investigate lineage-specific evolutionary patterns, a branch model was employed using CODEML in the PAML v4.9 [72]. In this analysis, branches corresponding to each parasitization group: abomasum-parasitizing (red), small intestine-parasitizing (blue), and large intestine-parasitizing (green), were sequentially designated as foreground (test) branches in separate analyses, while all remaining branches were treated as background (reference) branches. The evolutionary rates across these branches were assessed by comparing a one-ratio model (null hypothesis: model = 0, NSsites = 0), which assumed the same ω ratio across all branches, with a two-ratio branch model (alternative hypothesis: model = 2, NSsites = 0), which allowed different ω values for the foreground and background branches. A likelihood ratio test (LRT) was performed to evaluate whether the test branches exhibited significantly different selective constraints compared to the reference branches.

Additionally, branch-site model was performed to detect positive selection along the test branches. Each parasitization branch was tested separately by comparing Model A (model = 2, NSsites = 2, fix_omega = 0, omega = 0) with its corresponding null model (Model A null: model = 2, NSsites = 2, fix_omega = 1, omega = 1), using the same foreground (test) branches as defined above. LRTs were conducted to evaluate significance, and genes with LRT p-values < 0.05 (based on χ² distribution) were considered under significant positive selection.

Amino acid substitution pattern

AA polymorphic sites were obtained from the aligned AA sequences using a Perl script [53]. Due to variations in species numbers among the different parasitic groups (abomasum: seven species; small intestine: two species; large intestine: two species), the proportion of AA polymorphisms was calculated as: number of polymorphic sites/(AA length × number of species in group). This normalized metric was used to compare sequence variation across the three nematode groups occupying distinct parasitic niches for all 12 mitochondrial PCGs. Statistical comparisons of AA substitution ratios between groups (abomasum vs. small intestine; abomasum vs. large intestine; small intestine vs. large intestine) were conducted using the same approach as described in the selection analysis. Ultimately, a Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied due to the non-normal distribution of the data.

Results

Heterogeneity within Strongyloidea mitogenomes reveals evolutionary divergence patterns

Sequence heterogeneity analysis provided critical insights into the evolutionary divergence among 12 Strongyloidea mitogenomes. The heterogeneous divergence analysis was performed using eight distinct datasets: PCG1, PCG12, PCG3, PCG123, PCG1R (combined rRNA genes), PCG3R, PCG123R, and AA sequences (Figs. 1 and S1, Table S2). The PCG123 (mean similarity score = 0.537), PCG12 (0.635), and 123R (0.544) datasets exhibited relatively low heterogeneity in most pairwise comparisons, with the AA (0.884) dataset showing the lowest divergence (Figs. 1 and S2). Intriguingly, the PCG123R, PCG1R, PCG3R datasets (mean similarity score: 0.544, 0.56, 0.114) displayed marginally elevated heterogeneity compared to PCG123, PCG1, PCG3 (mean similarity score: 0.537, 0.54, 0.017), particularly in Oesophagostomum species, suggesting unique evolutionary dynamics in rRNA genes. The significantly reduced heterogeneity in PCG12 versus PCG123 (mean similarity score: 0.635 versus 0.537) indicated an accelerated evolutionary rate at the third codon position of PCGs. Conservation of amino acid sequences (lowest heterogeneity) implies that most third-position mutations are synonymous substitutions preserving protein function. As predicted, PCG3 demonstrated the highest heterogeneity (mean similarity score = 0.017), consistent with its high substitution rate and rapid evolutionary turnover. Interestingly, two large intestine-dwelling Oesophagostomum species exhibited relatively higher similarity scores (AA: 0.902; PCG123: 0.580; PCG12: 0.674) than small intestine (AA: 0.868; PCG123: 0.513; PCG12: 0.599), and comparable levels with abomasal counterparts (AA: 0.909; PCG123: 0.587; PCG12: 0.694). Obviously, congeneric species (e.g., Haemonchus, Nematodirus, and Oesophagostomum) displayed lower genetic heterogeneity compared to more distantly related taxa. This pattern suggested a relatively recent divergence time, limiting the accumulation of genetic variation, and reflected shared evolutionary trajectories among closely related species.

Phylogenomic reconstruction and mitogenomic architecture of Strongyloidea

Generally, strong heterogeneity among sequences significantly compromises the reliability of nodal support and topological accuracy in phylogenetic analyses [73]. Substitution saturation tests revealed no significant saturation across all seven datasets (PCG1, PCG1R, PCG12, PCG3, PCG3R, PCG123, PCG123R), as indicated by Iss values significantly lower than the critical thresholds (Iss < Iss.cSym or Iss.cAsym, p = 0; Table S3). Additionally, PhyKIT-based evaluation (Treeness and Saturation metrics) further supported the unsaturation of these datasets (Tables S4, S5). These results proposed that these datasets were appropriate for phylogenetic reconstruction using both ML and BI. Phylogenetic reconstruction of AA, PCG123, PCG12 datasets under both ML and BI analyses, along with BI-based PCG1 analysis, produced fully congruent tree topologies with significant statistical support (Fig. 2A). The resulting phylogeny demonstrated strong clustering of species occupying distinct gastrointestinal niches—abomasum, small intestine, and large intestine—indicating the potential of niche-driven adaptive divergence. Notably, small intestinal species exhibited closer phylogenetic affinity to abomasal branches than to large intestinal congeners. Synteny analyses (Fig. 2B) revealed conserved mitochondrial architectures across Strongyloidea, with uniform gene orientation comprising 12 PCGs, 22 tRNAs, two rRNAs, and a control region (CR). The CR, a major noncoding regulatory element governing replication/transcription, is also referred to as the AT rich region [74] and exhibited substantial length variation (155–1,203 bp, Table S6). M. digitatus harbored the largest mitogenome (14,672 bp) due to an extended CR (1,203 bp), whereas H. placei displayed the most compact mitogenome (13,604 bp) with a minimal CR (155 bp). All mitogenomes exhibited pronounced AT-rich composition (75.1–79.7%; mean 77.12%) (Table S6), consistent with nucleotide skewness patterns: negative AT-skew (−0.278 to −0.146) and positive GC-skew (0.253–0.444). GC content varied significantly across different genomic features, peaking in COX1 (27.8–32.6%) and reaching minima in CRs (10.0–19.8%).

ML- and BI-based phylogenies reconstructed from distinct codon positions (PCG1, PCG12, PCG3, PCG123) and combined rRNA datasets (PCG1R, PCG3R, PCG123R) revealed topology-dependent discordance (Figures S3 and S4). When using ML analysis, phylogenetic trees derived from the third codon position (PCG3/PCG3R) showed divergent topologies compared to AA-based reconstructions, specifically with Oesophagostomum (large intestine species) forming a sister clade to abomasal species rather than to Nematodirus (small intestine species). This incongruence highlighted that the third codon position may be unsuitable for phylogenetic tree reconstruction due to its high heterogeneity. Conversely, trees from PCG1, PCG12, and PCG123 datasets aligned with AA-based topology, except BI analysis of PCG1. Inclusion of rRNA genes in PCG1R and PCG123R induced slight topological shifts, particularly in the placement of O. ostertagi: clustering with M. marshalli in trees excluding rRNA versus grouping with T. circumcincta in trees including rRNA. These discrepancies highlighted the confounding effects of heterogeneous rRNA gene evolution. Crucially, all analyses based on the current species set consistently supported clustering by anatomical niche.

Codon usage bias analysis reveals A/U-ending preference in Strongyloidea mitogenomes

The RSCU metric, defined as the ratio of a codon’s observed frequency to its expected frequency under neutral usage assumptions, serves as a critical indicator of CUB in molecular evolution studies [75]. RSCU value > 1 denotes preferred codons, < 1 indicates the codons are less frequently used, and = 1 reflects neutral usage without bias. We systematically analyzed RSUC patterns across 12 Strongyloidea mitogenomes (Table S7), visualizing CUB variations through hierarchical clustering of RSCU values for 62 synonymous codons spanning 22 codon families (Fig. 3A). Despite subtle interspecific differences, codon usage exhibited remarkable consistency, with 18 codons consistently showing RSCU > 1 across all species: GCU (Ala), UGU (Cys), GAU (Asp), UUU (Phe), GGU (Gly), CAU (His), AUU (Ile), UUA (Leu), UUG (Leu), AUA (Met), AAU (Asn), CCU (Pro), CGU (Arg), UCU (Ser), AGU (Ser), ACU (Thr), GUU (Val), and UAU (Tyr). Strikingly, N. spathiger uniquely displayed maximal RSCU for AGU (Ser; 3.52), contrasting with other species where UUA (Leu) showed peak preference. Notably, 94% of preferred codons (17/18) terminated with A/U nucleotides, consistent with the mitogenomes’ AT-rich composition. This pervasive A/U-ending preference likely reflects both mutational bias and selection for efficient mitochondrial translation machinery.

Evolutionary optimal codons in Strongyloidea mitogenomes

The characterization of optimal codons, defined as synonymous codons that match with highly abundant tRNAs to maximize translation efficiency, offers a biochemical basis for enhancing heterologous gene expression in organisms. The selection of optimal codons is co-regulated by species-specific tRNA abundance profiles and anticodon-codon interaction rules, particularly the modified nucleotides at the anticodon wobble positions [76]. Employing the differential RSCU (ΔRSCU) metric (thresholds: ΔRSCU > 0.08 and RSCU > 1), we identified 40 optimal codons encoding 19 AAs (Fig. 3B). Strikingly, 70% (28/40) of these optimal codons ended in A/U nucleotides, directly aligning with the pronounced AT-rich genomic architecture of Strongyloidea mitogenomes (Table S6). Interspecific variation in optimal codon counts ranged from 10 in O. ostertagi, N. oiratianus, N. spathiger, and O. columbianum to 17 in M. marshalli. At a high-resolution level, the codons GGG (Gly), UUA (Leu), and AUA (Met) emerged as the most frequently used optimal codons, dominating 63.6% (7/11) of analyzed species (excluding outgroups). Conversely, CCC (Pro), ACC (Thr), GCG (Ala), CGG (Arg), GAG (Glu), UGG (Trp), and AAU (Asn) represented rare preferences, each representing only 9.1% (1/11) of species. The optimal codon GGG (Gly) was conserved across all abomasal nematodes, while AAG (Lys) and AUA (Met) were universally shared by small intestinal nematodes. Notably, large intestinal nematodes exhibited a broader spectrum of conserved optimal codons, including ACU (Thr), CAU (His), and CAA (Gln). Evidently, distinct differences were observed even within the same genus (H. contortus and H. placei; N. oiratianus and N. spathiger; O. asperum and O. columbianum).

ENC-GC3 and neutrality plot analysis indicate the predominant role of natural selection in shaping CUB

To further determine the effect of mutational pressure and natural selection on the codon usage preferences, we performed ENC-plot (ENC-GC3s) and neutrality plot analyses across 12 Strongyloidea species. In the ENC-plot analysis (Fig. 4), the majority of PCGs distributed below the expected neutrality curve in all species, suggesting that natural selection predominantly drives codon usage preferences. Neutrality plot analysis (Figure S4) further supported these findings. Except for T. circumcincta, the remaining 11 species exhibited no significant correlation between GC12s and GC3s (p > 0.05). The slopes of the regression lines ranged from 0.01 to 0.72, with most species displayed shallow regression slopes, indicating weak correlation between GC12 and GC3. Since a strong GC12-GC3 correlation suggested mutational pressure dominates codon usage bias, while a weak correlation implied predominant influence of natural selection [77, 78], our results indicated the prevailing leverage of natural selection in shaping codon usage patterns across Strongyloidea species.

Branch-specific selection pressures shape mitochondrial gene evolution in Strongyloidea

Gene-specific selection analyses uncovered distinct evolutionary trajectories across gastrointestinal niches. Designating abomasal species as foreground branches revealed relaxed selection in ND5 (K = 0.61, p = 0.012) and ND6 (K = 0.41, p = 0.000), while COX1 (K = 1.29, p = 0.012) exhibited intensified purifying selection. Conversely, intestinal branches showed no relaxation signals but displayed intensified selection: two genes (ND2 and ND5) in large intestinal species and five genes (COX1, COX2, ND2, ND4, and ND5) in small intestinal species (Table 1). Concatenated PCG analyses reflected similar trends, with significant selection intensification in intestinal branches (large intestine: K = 1.43, p = 0.001; small intestine: K = 1.12, p = 0.001), and nonsignificant relaxation in abomasal branches (K = 0.94, p = 0.139). This concordance between gene-specific and concatenated PCG analyses strengthened the evidence for divergent selection pressures across habitats.

The significant results of the relaxation and intensification

The selective pressures acting on PCGs are commonly evaluated using the ω ratio (dN/dS), where values below 1 indicate purifying selection, while values around 1 or above suggest neutral evolution or positive selection, respectively. Analysis under the free-ratio model revealed that all PCGs across the 11 examined species exhibited ω ratios significantly below 1 (Table S8), demonstrating the predominance of purifying selection in mitochondrial genome evolution. Comparative analysis revealed distinct evolutionary rates among species occupying different ecological niches. Specifically, abomasal species exhibited significantly higher mean ω values (ω = 0.0228) compared to both small intestinal (ω = 0.0113; p = 0.00167) and large intestinal congeners (ω = 0.0125; p = 0.0137) (Fig. 5A). Gene-level comparisons further highlighted these differences, with abomasal species showing elevated ω ratios in key mitochondrial genes. Notably, ND1 and ND2 displayed higher evolutionary rates in abomasal species (ND1: ω = 0.0415; ND2: ω = 0.0362) compared to small intestinal (ND1: ω = 0.0143; ND2: ω = 0.0280) and large intestinal groups (ND1: ω = 0.0192; ND2: ω = 0.00651), though these differences were not statistically significant (p > 0.05). ND4 displayed significantly elevated evolutionary rates in abomasal species (ω = 0.0243) compared to large intestinal branches (ω = 0.00893; p = 0.028), while showing a non-significant increase relative to small intestinal groups (ω = 0.0243 vs. 0.0118; p > 0.05) (Fig. 5B). Concatenated analysis of all 12 PCGs further supported these findings, with abomasal species showing consistently higher mean ω values (0.0143) compared to both small (0.00777) and large intestinal groups (0.00879), despite these differences not reaching statistical significance (p > 0.05).

Branch model analysis revealed intensified purifying selection across all PCGs in nematodes parasitizing three gastrointestinal niches compared to background branches (Table 2). The abomasum branch showed significantly stronger constraint in three mitochondrial genes: COX1 (background ω = 0.009 > branch ω = 0.004, p = 0.000), CYTB (0.013 > 0.006, p = 0.008), and ND4L (0.013 > 0.002, p = 0.025). RELAX analysis corroborated these findings, with COX1 showing significant selection intensification (K > 1, p < 0.05). Similarly, the small intestine branch exhibited enhanced purifying selection in four genes: COX1 (0.007 > 0.001, p = 0.000), COX2 (0.011 > 0.003, p = 0.010), COX3 (0.006 > 0.001, p = 0.002), and ND1 (0.021 > 0.008, p = 0.004), with both COX1 and COX2 showing congruent signals in RELAX tests (K > 1, p < 0.05). The large intestine parasites demonstrated only one gene (ND4) under significantly stronger purifying selection (0.016 > 0.006, p = 0.023). These consistent results collectively demonstrated niche-specific intensification of purifying selection, particularly affecting electron transport chain components across all three parasitic groups. The branch-site model analysis demonstrated CYTB as undergoing positive selection specifically in the small intestine branch (Table S9).

Discussion

Phylogenetic insights within Strongyloidea

The phylogenetic reconstruction based on concatenated AA, PCG123, and PCG12 datasets (Fig. 2A) consistently supported the monophyly of the examined Strongyloidea’s subfamilies (Trichostrongylinae, Molineinae, and Strongylinae) and genera (Haemonchus, Nematodirus, and Oesophagostomum), corroborating their current taxonomic classification [79]. Notably, we observed a sister-group relationship between Trichostrongylinae (abomasal species) and Molineinae (small intestinal species, Nematodirus), which aligned with preceding studie which proposed close evolutionary relationships between Molineidae (Nematodirus), Dictyocaulidae, Trichostrongylidae (T. axei and T. circumcincta), and Haemonchidae (M. digitatus and H. contortus) [25, 80, 81]. Consistent with Zhao et al.,s study, Oesophagostomum occupied a phylogenetic outgroup position, reflecting its distant evolutionary relationship and distinct ecological niche in the large intestinal environment [81].

Accurate phylogenetic reconstruction relies on the application of best-fitting substitution models, as our analyses demonstrated that different datasets and gene partitions often require distinct optimal models (Table S4, S5). The use of model selection is therefore critical to avoid biases in tree inference. Additionally, ML and BI analyses occasionally yield conflicting topologies (e.g., PCG1/PCG3 in this study), highlighting the necessity of integrating multiple phylogenetic approaches for robust evolutionary inferences. Notably, due to the insufficient sampling of large and small intestine, the observed monophyly of lineages associated with different anatomical locations should be interpreted with caution. Future studies incorporating more comprehensive taxon sampling, especially from under-represented intestinal habitats, will be crucial for resolving the evolutionary relationships within Strongyloidea nematode groups with greater confidence.

Evolutionary constraints and functional conservation in Strongyloidea mitogenomes

Mitogenomes serve as powerful markers in evolutionary biology due to their compact structure, maternal inheritance, and critical roles in cellular energetics [82]. Selective pressures on mitochondrial PCGs often arise from metabolic adaptations, potentially co-evolving with nuclear genomes to meet organism-specific physiological demands [83, 84]. To investigate niche-specific evolutionary dynamics, we analyzed mtDNA selective pressures across three gastrointestinal regions: abomasum, small intestine, and large intestine, which exhibit substantial differences in physicochemical conditions, including pH and oxygen availability [85, 86]. In contrast to intestinal regions, the abomasum is exposed to lower pH and higher oxygen concentrations, which may result in relaxed selection on PCGs of abomasum-dwelling species. This effect may be amplified by the inherently low dispersal capacity of gastrointestinal nematodes, which may lead to subpopulation isolation and consequently reduces effective population sizes (Nₑ) [87–89]. Additionally, the biphasic life cycle of Strongyloidea nematodes, marked by a critical environment-to-host transition [90], may trigger genetic bottlenecks, leading to drastic population size reduction during adaptation. Emerging evidence supports that in small Ne populations, genetic drift tends to dominate over natural selection [91]. Therefore, the relaxation signatures (K < 1, p < 0.05) detected by RELAX may reflect drift-driven evolution in functionally less constrained regions, such as the abomasum. This demographic constraint likely increases mtDNA mutation rates, as purifying selection becomes less efficient in small populations.

Based on free-ratio model analyses, abomasum-dwelling species exhibited significantly higher dN/dS (ω) ratios compared to intestinal species (Fig. 5A), indicating that extreme physicochemical conditions (low pH and high oxygen availability) may drive distinct evolutionary patterns in mtDNA-encoded proteins. While all 12 mitochondrial PCGs showed ω values < 1 across branches (Table S8), consistent with strong purifying selection maintaining core mitochondrial functions in energy metabolism [92]. Notably, ND4 showed significantly elevated ω values in abomasum species (Fig. 5B), suggesting these electron transport chain components may tolerate more AA substitutions. This relative relaxation of selective constraints could facilitate adaptive evolution, potentially helping species adapt to the diverse ecological niches [93].

Purifying selection serves as a key determinant of mitochondrial DNA evolution by efficiently eliminating deleterious mutations in PCGs [94]. Evolutionary rate acceleration is often associated with relaxation of purifying selection pressures [95]. Our analyses identified significant selection relaxation in two NADH dehydrogenase genes (ND5 and ND6) among abomasum-dwelling species compared to their intestinal counterparts (Table 1). Intriguingly, while free-ratio models detected elevated evolutionary rates (ω) in ND1, ND2, and ND4 for abomasum species, RELAX analyses did not support significant selection relaxation (K < 1, p > 0.05) in these genes in abomasal lineages. This pattern parallels findings in rapid-flow cyprinids, where adaptation to oxygen availability has been associated with relaxed purifying selection in nine mitochondrial genes [96]. Conversely, we detected intensified purifying selection acting on five genes (COX1, COX2, ND2, ND4, and ND5) in small intestinal species (Table 1). The strong selective constraints on COX1 and COX2 were further supported by branch-model analyses (Table 2), implying these cytochrome c oxidase subunits may perform highly specialized functions. Alternatively, their mutations may exert disproportionately severe consequences, driving more stringent purifying selection to maintain their essential functions [97]. Additionally, branch-site model analysis identified one gene, CYTB, as undergoing positive selection in intestinal-dwelling species (Table S9). As a key component of the electron transport chain in OXPHOS, CYTB is generally highly conserved and has been extensively used as a neutral evolutionary marker in phylogenetics [41]. However, despite strong functional constraints, evidence suggests that CYTB may undergo weak positive selection in response to energy-intensive lifestyles or oxygen-limited environments [98, 99]. Given the relatively hypoxic conditions in the small intestine compared to other parasitic niches, CYTB may have undergone positive directional selection in small intestinal species to enhance metabolic efficiency through amino acid substitutions. This finding aligned with comparative studies in flight-capable versus flightless insects, where mitochondrial PCGs had undergone positive selection associated with flight capability [100, 101]. These findings collectively highlighted the critical role of mitochondrial energy metabolism genes in facilitating organismal adaptation to distinct ecological niches. Future studies integrating nuclear-genome data and expanded taxonomic sampling are needed to disentangle niche-specific selection pressures shaping mitogenome evolution within Strongyloidea.

Evolutionary insights from codon usage dynamics and nucleotide composition

While adaptive selection fundamentally shapes mitochondrial evolution [83], CUB provides a sensitive proxy for detecting selection signatures [102]. Our cross-species CUB analysis revealed conserved codon usage patterns across gastrointestinal niches (Fig. 3), but revealed intra-genus variations that mirrored the rapid CUB divergence characteristic of nematode evolution [103, 104]. Consistent with other nematode mitogenomes [105–107], Strongyloidea species exhibited pronounced AT-rich genomic signature (75.0–79.7%) with characteristic nucleotide skews: negative AT-skew (−0.278 to −0.146) and positive GC-skew (0.253 to 0.444) (Table S6).

In addition, our analysis revealed that 94% of preferred codons (RSCU > 1) and 70% of evolutionarily optimized codons terminated in A/U nucleotides (Fig. 3 and Table S7), mirroring the AT-biased codon usage patterns observed in nematodes despite branch-specific refinements [6, 108, 109]. Skew values were relatively consistent across Strongyloidea species, but comparison analysis with C. elegans highlighted distinct genomic features, including elevated AT-skew and reduced GC-skew in the free-living lineage. These divergent skew patterns may reflect niche-specific evolutionary pressures shaped by parasitic adaptation versus free-living ecologies [21, 110, 111].

Evolutionary constraints and interpretive challenges in large intestinal branches

The low genetic heterogeneity observed in large intestine-dwelling Oesophagostomum species (Figs. 1 and S1) suggested possibly slowed evolutionary divergence through stabilizing selection or niche conservatism within this gut compartment. However, limited physiological data and potential sampling biases (only two large intestinal species) constrain definitive interpretation, necessitating expanded taxonomic sampling and more comprehensive phylogenetic and physiological analyses to distinguish biological signals.

Limitations and future directions

While our findings established baseline evolutionary patterns in Strongyloidea mitogenomes, several limitations should be noted. First, limited taxonomy sampling particularly for intestinal species (n = 4) may affect the phylogenetic tree topology and, consequently, the reliability of selection pressure estimates, which are sensitive to both species’ composition and tree structure. Second, our analyses relied exclusively on mitochondrial genes, which may not fully capture the true relationships. Incorporating nuclear genome and transcriptome data will enhance phylogenetic resolution and improve selection inference. Third, the absence of host microenvironmental data, such as physicochemical conditions (pH, oxygen levels, temperature) and external environmental factors (e.g., diet, co-infection dynamics)—hinders direct linkage between observed molecular adaptations and potential selective pressures. Despite these constraints, this study provided the first comparative evidence of niche-driven mitogenomic evolution in Strongyloidea. The framework presented here lays a foundation for future research integrating broader taxon sampling, multi-omic data, and environmental parameters to clarify the mechanisms underlying host-niche adaptation.

Conclusions

Our mitogenome-based analyses revealed strong phylogenetic clustering of Strongyloidea species according to their anatomical niches, alongside conserved mitochondrial architectures and codon usage patterns. Selection pressure analyses demonstrated predominant purifying selection, with notable variation among niche groups. Abomasal-dwelling species exhibited elevated ω values, especially in ND4, along with significant relaxation signals in ND5 and ND6. In contrast, small intestinal species showed intensified selection across multiple genes and a positive selection signal in CYTB. Amino acid polymorphism analysis revealed higher sequence variability in ND genes relative to the highly conserved COX genes. These results provided empirical evidence for mitochondrial genetic adaptation to host anatomical niches in Strongyloidea parasites, advancing our understanding of their evolutionary constraints and ecological specialization.
