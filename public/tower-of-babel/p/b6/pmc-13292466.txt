Nutritional and techno-functional enhancement of noodles through cricket powder incorporation: Implications for starch digestibility and flavor
Authors: Yu Liu, Yang-Ran Lu, Rui-Xin Liu, Hua Li, Sirithon Siriamornpun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCricket powder (CP) offers a high-protein and sustainable alternative to more traditional animal-based protein sources. In this study, CP was incorporated into noodles at 3%, 6%, and 9% replacement levels to evaluate its effects on dough rheology, nutritional quality, structure, starch digestibility, antioxidant activity, and flavor properties. CP addition enhanced dough viscoelasticity. It also increased protein, fat, and total amino acids, while reducing carbohydrate content. These changes were accompanied by lower rapidly digestible starch and estimated glycemic index, and higher resistant starch. Structural analyses showed strengthened hydrogen bonding, reduced relative crystallinity, and partial disruption of the gluten-starch network at higher substitution levels. In addition, CP improved antioxidant capacity and promoted umami-related flavor compounds. Although cooking loss increased, it remained within acceptable limits. Different substitution levels exhibited distinct advantages in nutritional, technological, and sensory attributes, allowing product selection according to consumer preferences for taste, texture, and health value. Overall, CP can serve as a sustainable functional ingredient to improve the nutritional and techno-functional properties of noodles.Keywords: Cricket powder, Noodle, Rheology, In vitro digestion, Flavor profile, Sensory evaluation

Introduction

Noodles are one of the traditional staple foods in Asia with a profound history, renowned for their straightforward production process, convenient cooking method, and long shelf life (Anushree et al., 2025). However, traditional noodles made from refined wheat flour exhibit high glycemic index (GI) due to the predominance of rapidly digestible starch (Tian et al., 2025). High-GI foods have been associated with increased risks of metabolic disorders such as Type 2 diabetes and obesity. Given their high consumption frequency as staple foods, reducing starch digestibility in noodles is therefore of particular importance for improving dietary health. In addition, noodles may provide limited protein content and an imbalanced amino acid profile, particularly due to the deficiency of lysine, which restricts their overall nutritional quality (Duda et al., 2019). Consequently, there is growing interest in improving both protein quality and starch digestibility in noodles through the incorporation of functional ingredients (Jia et al., 2025).

Edible insects have emerged as promising high-value ingredients for food fortification because of their high protein content, favorable amino acid composition, and sustainability advantages over conventional animal protein sources (Bruttomesso et al., 2024). Among them, cricket powder (CP) has attracted considerable attention as a functional ingredient that can be incorporated into cereal-based foods in powder form, thereby overcoming consumer acceptance issues associated with whole insects (Bruttomesso et al., 2024). Previous studies have reported that CP supplementation in products such as bread and muffins increased protein content and improved essential amino acid composition (Mihaly Cozmuta et al., 2022; Zielińska et al., 2025). Duda et al. (2019) reported that the incorporation of 5% CP into durum wheat pasta enhanced protein and mineral contents, improved cooking quality, and yielded highly acceptable sensory characteristics. Pasini et al. (2022) reported that the incorporation of 14% cricket protein into pasta increased cooking loss and hardness. These findings indicate that CP can effectively enhance the nutritional quality of wheat-based products, although its effects on processing and textural properties may vary depending on the incorporation level.

Despite these advances, research on CP-enriched noodles remains limited. Existing studies have primarily focused on single substitution levels or selected quality parameters, providing insufficient information on how CP incorporation influences noodle quality in a comprehensive manner. More importantly, the effects of CP incorporation on starch digestibility and its underlying structural mechanisms in noodle systems remain largely unexplored. In particular, integrated evaluations covering nutritional quality, processing performance, structural characteristics, and flavor-related properties of CP-enriched noodles are still scarce, restricting practical formulation and product optimization.

Therefore, this study incorporated CP into noodles at 0, 3, 6, and 9% levels to systematically evaluate its effects on nutritional, processing, structural, and flavor properties, as well as the correlations between sensory assessment and taste/aroma attributes. To the best of our knowledge, this study was among the first to systematically investigate the combined effects of CP incorporation on nutritional quality, starch digestibility, structural characteristics, and flavor properties in noodle systems. These findings are expected to provide practical guidance for the development of nutritionally enhanced, lower-GI noodle products and promote the high-value utilization of CP in sustainable food applications.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Cricket powder (CP) of Acheta domesticus (Linnaeus) was obtained from the Newgensinnofoods Co., Ltd. (Maha Sarakham, Thailand), containing 60.40% protein, 20.68% fat, and 7.99% carbohydrate according to our previous study (Boonarsa et al., 2025). All-purpose wheat flour was procured from the local supermarket and contained 11.0% protein, 1.6% fat, and 73.5% carbohydrate as reported on the label. DPPH, ABTS, gallic acid, and sodium bicarbonate were bought from Yuanye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Amyloglucosidase and α-amylase were supplied by Aladdin Biological Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Amino acids mixture standard solution was purchased from Wako Pure Chemical Co., Ltd. (Osaka, Japan). All other chemicals and reagents were of analytical grade and sourced from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Dough and noodles preparation

Noodles were prepared by replacing wheat flour with CP at substitution levels of 0%, 3%, 6%, and 9% (w/w, wheat flour basis). The total solid weight was kept constant at 100 g. Then, distilled water was added at 44% (v/w). All ingredients were mixed and kneaded for 15 min to make a smooth dough. After resting for 15 min at room temperature under sealed conditions, the dough was sheeted using a noodle-making machine (DM-YMJ00, Junxifu Company, Zhejiang, China) with 10 consecutive passes, during which the roller gap was gradually reduced to obtain sheets with a final thickness of 1.0 mm. The sheets were then cut into noodles (Fan et al., 2020). Noodles were cooked in boiling water (1:25, w/v) at 100 °C. The optimal cooking time was determined based on the disappearance of the white core, and a standardized cooking time of 3 min was applied to all samples under identical conditions.

Rheological properties of dough

The rheological properties of the dough were analyzed using a rheometer (MARSIII, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) equipped with P35/Ti with reference to the method described by Tian et al. (2025). The samples were coated with silicone oil to reduce moisture loss. The parameters were set as follows: parallel plate geometry with a 35 mm diameter and 1 mm gap, a strain value of 0.1%, and a scanning frequency ranging from 0.1 to 10 Hz at 25 °C. The values of the storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G") were recorded and computed using RheoWin Data Manager.

Proximate composition and water activity

Nutritional components (moisture, protein, fat, and ash) in the raw noodles were measured in accordance with AOAC methods, and carbohydrate content was estimated by the difference method. The water activity (aw) of the raw noodles was measured using a water activity meter (HD-3 A, Huake Instrument Co., Ltd., Wuxi, China).

Amino acids analysis

A 300 mg of raw noodle sample was hydrolyzed with 5 mL HCl (6 mol/L) at 110 °C for 22 h. The hydrolyzate was diluted to 10 mL, and a 50 μL aliquot was collected, dried under nitrogen, and then made up to 2 mL with HCl (0.02 mol/L). After filtration through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane, free amino acids were determined using an automatic amino acid analyzer (LA8080, Hitachi, Japan) (Cavalheiro et al., 2023).

Color

The color of the raw noodles was quantified by measuring L*, a*, and b* values with a colorimeter (CR-400, Konica Minolta, Tokyo, Japan), following calibration with white and black standards prior to each measurement. For raw noodles, five strands were aligned in a straight line, and six measurements were taken at different positions across the aligned strands for each sample.

Texture properties

Texture properties of the cooked noodles were carried out using Li et al. (2025) protocol on a TA.XT plus texture analyzer (Stable Micro Systems, Godalming, UK) equipped with a P/36R probe. Six strips of the noodles were placed parallel on a test platform. Testing conditions included pre-test (2.0 mm/s), test (0.8 mm/s), and post-test (0.8 mm/s) speeds, 5.0 g trigger force, and 70% deformation. Each test was repeated six times.

Cooking characteristics

The cooking characteristics, including water absorption and cooking loss, were evaluated as depicted in the method reported by Duda et al. (2019), with minor modifications. A 10 g of the noodle samples (m1) was boiled in 250 mL of water for 3 min, then the cooked noodles were weighed (m2). The cooking water was transferred to a beaker (m3) and evaporated in a drying oven (DHG-9055 A, Yiheng Technology, Shanghai, China) at 105 °C until a constant weight (m4) was achieved. Analysis of total phenolic content and antioxidant capacity

The raw noodle samples (0.5 g) were mixed with 25 mL of 70% methanol and extracted with orbital shaking (40 °C, 160 rpm, 2 h). Following centrifugation (3000 rpm, 20 min), the resulting supernatant was collected and used for TPC, DPPH, and ABTS determination, as described by Liu et al. (2025).

Starch digestibility analysis

Following the method described by Anushree et al. (2025), the estimated glycemic index (eGI) and starch composition of the cooked noodles were investigated. A 0.2 g of the sample was dispersed in 15 mL of 0.2 mol/L sodium acetate buffer (pH 5.2) and then the resulting mixture was digested with amyloglucosidase (15 U/mL) and α-amylase (290 U/mL) at 37 °C and 160 rpm for 3 h in a water bath shaker (SHZ-82 A, Shengwei Instruments, Changzhou, China). At 0, 20, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 min, 1 mL aliquot of digesta was mixed with 2.5 mL of 66% ethanol to deactivate the enzymes and the glucose content was quantified via the 3,5-dinitrosalicylic acid method. FTIR

The FTIR spectra of the raw noodles were obtained using the method of Li et al. (2024), with slight adjustments. After the noodle samples were ground with KBr at a 1:100 (w/w) ratio, their spectra were recorded from 4000 to 400 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1 and subsequently analyzed using Omnic 9.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The 1600–1700 cm−1 amide I region of the spectrum was analyzed using Peakfit 4.12 (Systat Software Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) with self-deconvolution and second derivative analysis.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) measurement

The crystalline structure of the raw noodles was investigated using an X-ray diffractometer (D8 Advance, Bruker AXS, Karlsruhe, Baden-Württemberg, Germany) with an operating voltage of 40 kV and a current of 40 mA following the method of Zheng et al. (2021). All samples were scanned from 5° to 40° with a step size of 0.35°. The diffraction patterns were analyzed using MDI Jade 6.0 software (Material Date, Inc., Livermore, CA, USA), and the relative crystallinity was calculated.

Scanning electron microscope (SEM)

Upon slightly modifying the previous method reported by Fan et al. (2020), the cross-sections of the lyophilized noodle strands were visualized by a scanning electron microscope (S-4800II, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV and magnifications of 500× and 1000 × .

E-nose and GC–MS analysis

The PEN3 E-nose (Airsense Analytics, Schwerin, Germany) with 10 sensors was used to differentiate the odor profile of the cooked noodles. The sample (7.5 g) was placed into a sealed 40 mL headspace vial, equilibrated at 60 °C for 30 min, and then data were collected for 60 s per sample. The profile of volatile organic compounds (VOCs) was characterized by headspace solid phase micro-extraction technique (HS-SPME) coupled with GC–MS, according to our previous procedure (Liu et al., 2025). Volatiles were semi-quantified by peak area normalization, and results are expressed as %TIC (individual peak area / total peak area of all identified compounds × 100%).

E-tongue analysis

The taste characteristics of the cooked noodles were carried out using an SA402B E-tongue (Intelligent Sensor Technology Co., Ltd., Atsugi, Japan) based on a modified protocol (Zhao et al., 2024). Briefly, the samples (15 g) were homogenized with deionized water (150 mL) and then centrifuged (10,000 rpm, 30 min, 4 °C) to remove insoluble material. The supernatant was collected and detected. Each sample was subjected to four measurement cycles, the data from the final three repetitions were used for analysis.

Sensory evaluation

The sensory evaluation was carried out according to the method of Duda et al. (2019). Thirty untrained participants aged between 21 and 54 years old, evaluated the appearance, aroma, taste, texture, and overall acceptability of the cooked noodles using a 9-point hedonic scale (1 = dislike extremely, 5 = neither like nor dislike, and 9 = like extremely), with drinking water provided for palate cleansing between samples. Ethic approval number is 004–901/2026.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted at least in triplicate and the results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's multiple range tests, with a p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Graphs were constructed using Origin 2021 (OriginLab, MA, USA). Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed on the relative abundance (%TIC) of volatile compound classes (aldehydes, alcohols, esters, ketones, pyrazines, and others) using Origin 2021 and a biplot was generated to illustrate sample clustering and class contributions. The correlations between each pair of indicators were compared, and the correlation heat map was plotted using ChiPlot (https://www.chiplot.online/) (assessed on 14 February 2026).

Results and discussion

Rheological properties of dough

Rheological properties of dough are related to the texture and cooking characteristics of noodles (Yang et al., 2020). The storage modulus (G') and loss modulus (G") were used to represent the elasticity and viscosity of dough (Li et al., 2022). As shown in Fig. 1A and B, the G' of dough was higher than G", indicating that the dough exhibited solid-like elastic behavior (He et al., 2026). Moreover, both G' and G" of CP-enriched dough were much higher than those of control, indicating that the incorporation of CP enhanced viscoelasticity of dough. This enhancement was likely associated with high protein content of CP, which may promote interactions between non-gluten proteins and gluten network, thus enhancing the degree of protein aggregation. In addition, the presence of hydrophilic groups in CP could enhance the hydration and crosslinking of gluten protein, facilitating the formation of a more robust gluten network (Li et al., 2026).

Proximate composition and water activity of noodles

Compared to the control, the protein content of CP3, CP6, and CP9 increased by 29.47%, 48.68%, and 70.31%, respectively (Fig. 1C). This expected increase in protein content of the final product is a direct consequence of raising the level of CP, which is the most protein-rich ingredient in the formulation. The contents of fat and ash also significantly increased with higher levels of CP, rising from 0.95% to 2.36% and from 0.58% to 2.01%, respectively, as CP increased from 0% to 9%. Similar results were reported by Cavalheiro et al. (2023), showing that the addition of CP increased the protein and ash contents of frankfurters. The increased ash content mainly reflected the mineral contribution of cricket powder and may enhance nutritional value. However, future commercialization should consider compliance with region-specific regulatory requirements for noodle products. Conversely, the carbohydrate content, moisture content, and water activity (aw) of the noodles decreased with increasing CP concentration. While the nutritional composition of the products is of great importance, the moisture content and aw are key quality factors affecting preservation and packaging. The moisture content and aw of CP-enriched noodles were within the range of 34.04–37.15% and 0.965–0.980, respectively (Fig. 1D). Most foods with aw higher than 0.95 are susceptible to mold growth (Karnjanapratum et al., 2022). Therefore, the proper storage of CP-enriched noodles should be considered.

Amino acid profile of noodles

The protein quality of flour products depends on both the content and composition of amino acids. Moreover, amino acids play an important role in the taste and flavor of products (Cavalheiro et al., 2023). Fig. 2 presents the amino acid profile of CP-enriched noodles. Glutamic acid was the most abundant amino acid in the noodles, the incorporation of CP resulted in elevated contents of all amino acids in the noodles. Notably, lysine increased by 63.93% compared with the control. Because lysine is the first limiting amino acid in wheat flour, its enrichment may markedly improve the protein nutritional quality of the noodles. Both essential amino acids (EAA) and total amino acids (TAA) contents increased with higher CP levels. Specifically, the EAA and TAA in CP9 reached 3.62 and 10.59 g/100 g, respectively, both significantly higher than the control values (1.88 and 6.08 g/100 g). As shown in Table 1, the EAA/TAA and EAA/NEAA ratios were comparable to the reference values of nearly 40% and 60%, respectively, as recommended by the FAO/WHO (Dong et al., 2020), which indicates that CP incorporation effectively enhanced the nutritional quality of noodle proteins by improving the amino acid balance.

Color and texture properties

Color is a main sensory attribute that exerts a profound influence on consumers' perceptions and evaluations of food quality (Cavalheiro et al., 2023). As shown in Fig. 3A, L* value decreased with increasing CP concentration, whereas a* and b* values progressively increased, consistent with the results of Li et al. (2025) for rice noodles. All CP-enriched noodles exhibited a total color difference value greater than 3, suggesting that the color changes in CP-enriched noodles could be visually detectable (Cavalheiro et al., 2023). These changes likely resulted from the dark brown color of CP, the presence of polyphenols and melanin, and enzymatic browning reactions (Bruttomesso et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025). Incorporating CP progressively darkened the noodles, with the color approaching that of whole wheat noodles as the CP level increased (Fig. 4). Since whole wheat noodles are widely recognized by consumers for their health-promoting value, this color similarity may help mitigate potential aversions to insect-derived ingredients by linking the CP-enriched noodles to a familiar, health-positive sensory profile (Duda et al., 2019).

The texture profile of cooked noodles are shown in Fig. 3B and C. The incorporation of CP significantly enhanced the hardness, chewiness, and springiness of noodles. This may be attributed to the hydrophilic nature of CP components (e.g., proteins and fibers), which could facilitate the hydration capacity of gluten proteins and the formation of a denser gluten network (Guo et al., 2020). In addition, chitin in CP may cross-link with gluten proteins via hydrogen bonds or hydrophobic interactions, further strengthening the network structure and contributing to the observed textural changes. Pongpichaiudom and Songsermpong (2018) found a positive correlation between noodle hardness and protein content. Similarly, Li et al. (2025) reported that CP addition increased the hardness and chewiness of rice noodles, aligning well with the findings of the present study. The addition of CP induced a higher springiness of the noodles, consistent with the increased G' as shown in the rheological curves. However, cohesiveness remained unchanged (p > 0.05), implying that the internal structural integrity of the noodles was not notably affected by CP addition (Anushree et al., 2025).

Cooking characteristics

Water absorption and cooking loss are key indicators for assessing the cooking performance of noodles (Bianchi et al., 2022). As detailed in Table 1, increasing CP addition from 0% to 9% induced a gradual decrease in water absorption (102.76% to 60.33%) and an increase in cooking loss (3.01% to 5.40%). The decrease in water absorption may be due to the interaction between starch and protein through hydrogen bonds, which weakens the starch-water interaction by competing for available water or coating the starch granules (Zhao et al., 2024). Rosa-Sibakov et al. (2016) found that water absorption of pasta was negatively correlated with protein content, but positively associated with starch content. Consistently, Zhou et al. (2024) also found that water absorption of extruded buckwheat noodles decreased with increasing levels of mung bean, and attributed this phenomenon to the reduction in total starch content. The reduction in water absorption resulted in increased noodle hardness, in agreement with the findings of Kang et al. (2018).

The incorporation of CP may disrupt the continuity of the gluten network, particularly at the noodle surface. This less compact surface structure likely facilitated the leaching of starch granules and other soluble substances (e.g., protein, dietary fiber, phenolics) into cooking water (Bianchi et al., 2022), leading to increased cooking loss. Previous study also demonstrated that protein could facilitate starch leaching through competitive water absorption (Sun et al., 2019). Notably, the maximum cooking loss in CP9 (5.40%) was below the acceptable threshold of 8 g/100 g for high-quality noodles (Pal et al., 2017). In general, with the increase of CP addition, the cooking quality of noodles gradually decreased, but the values were within the acceptable range.

Antioxidant capacity

As shown in Table 1, the TPC of the control noodles was only 5.20 mg GAE/100 g, whereas that of the noodles with 9% CP rose to 9.07 mg GAE/ 100 g, approximately 1.74-fold higher than the control. With the increase in CP incorporation from 0% to 9%, the DPPH and ABTS values increased from 55.39% to 66.23% and from 8.54% to 79.06%, respectively. These results indicate that CP supplementation could increase the antioxidant activities of noodles, consistent with previous studies showing that insect powder-enriched products had stronger antioxidant capacity (Li et al., 2025; Zielińska et al., 2025). This phenomenon may be attributed to the presence of phenolic and flavonoid compounds in CP (Boonarsa et al., 2025). Moreover, GC–MS analysis revealed increased formation of heterocyclic volatile compounds, particularly pyrazines (Table 2). Given that pyrazines have been reported to exhibit antioxidant activity (Zhang et al., 2024), their elevated levels may partially contribute to the enhanced antioxidant properties. In addition, cricket proteins contain aromatic and hydrophobic amino acids that can participate in radical quenching; furthermore, thermal processing may expose additional active residues or generate antioxidant peptides.

In vitro starch digestibility and eGI

As the key component of noodles, starch has a digestion rate that significantly affects energy release and, consequently, the postprandial blood glucose response. Table 1 shows the starch composition and eGI of the cooked noodles. After CP substitution, RDS was significantly reduced from 54.81% to 47.45%, while SDS increased from 20.79% to 23.02% and RS increased from 24.40% to 29.53% (p < 0.05). This was probably due to the lower concentration of carbohydrate and higher contents of protein and fat in CP than in the wheat flour (Li et al., 2022). Proteins, lipids, and phenolic compounds of CP can inhibit amylase via physical barriers (limiting enzyme diffusion and substrate access) and by altering granular swelling during gelatinization (Li et al., 2025). Additionally, the increased firmness of CP-enriched noodles as compared to the control may have contributed to the decreased in vitro starch digestibility, given the well-recognized influence of food texture on starch digestion (Bianchi et al., 2022). Moreover, the control, with an eGI of 76.90, is considered a high-GI food, while the 9% CP-enriched noodles had an eGI of 68.82, categorizing it as a medium-GI food. In conclusion, CP substitution significantly reduced the starch digestion rate and eGI of noodles while increasing the RS content, thereby attenuating the postprandial blood glucose responses. It may provide a theoretical basis for the development of health-promoting noodles.

FTIR

As shown in Fig. 3D, the strong and broad absorption peak at 3000–3600 cm−1 represents the O—H stretching vibration. Compared with the control, the O—H characteristic peak of the noodles containing CP shifted to a lower wavenumber, indicating enhanced hydrogen bonding interactions in the noodle system upon CP addition (Zheng et al., 2021). Noodles enriched with various ingredients, such as dietary fiber, protein, starch, and phytochemicals, constitute a complex system. Previous studies have indicated that these ingredients might affect the secondary structure (Kłosok et al., 2021). Fig. 3E illustrates that the β-sheet content in noodles decreased as CP addition increased, concomitant with a rise in α-helix and β-turn contents. The decrease in β-sheet content suggests a reduction in the compactness of the noodle protein network upon CP addition (Xie et al., 2025), which was accompanied by increased cooking loss.

X-ray diffraction analysis

The X-ray diffraction patterns and relative crystallinity (RC) of noodles with varying CP additions are shown in Fig. 3F. No significant shift in the position of diffraction peaks was observed with increasing CP addition. All noodles exhibited strong diffraction peaks near 2θ angles of 15°, 17°, 18°, and 23°, displaying a typical A-type diffraction pattern (Zheng et al., 2021). In addition, a weak diffraction peak was observed near the 2θ angle of 20°, indicating the existence of V-type crystalline structure in the noodles (Zhou et al., 2024). However, no new diffraction peaks were observed upon CP addition, suggesting that the crystalline pattern remained unchanged. Nevertheless, the RC of noodles decreased progressively as the CP concentration increased. It could be attributed to the formation of hydrogen bonds between CP and starch, which interfered with the inter-molecular hydrogen bonds among starch chains and hindered the recrystallization of starch molecules (Xu et al., 2022).

SEM

Fig. 4 presents the cross-sections of noodles with different levels of CP. In the control noodles, starch granules were spherical or elliptical and uniformly embedded in a complete gluten network matrix, characterized by few and small pores. When the CP addition was increased to 3% and 6%, more pores were observed, although the distribution of starch and protein remained relatively uniform. In contrast, further increasing the CP concentration to 9% led to uneven starch distribution and agglomeration. These results indicated that excessive CP addition disrupted the gluten network. Since CP is gluten-free, its incorporation diluted the gluten protein concentration, thereby weakening the gluten network's ability to effectively encapsulate and uniformly disperse starch granules. Moreover, phenolics in CP can form complexes with starch, reducing its swelling capacity and water solubility. This hinders complete starch hydration under limited moisture conditions, leading to exposed or insufficiently swollen starch granules that contribute to the observed increase in cooking loss with rising CP concentration (Fan et al., 2020).

E-nose and E-tongue analysis

Fig. 5A illustrates the E-nose sensor response profiles for noodles with varying CP additions. As CP concentration increased, the signal intensities of five sensors (W1C, W5S, W2S, W1W, and W2W) increased progressively. In contrast, the responses of the other sensors remained relatively stable and almost overlapped among the samples. This suggests elevated levels of nitrogen oxides, sulfur compounds, and aromatic components (including pyrazines) in CP-enriched noodles (Liu et al., 2025), likely due to the higher protein and lipid contents of CP, which promote the formation of volatile compounds during dough processing and thermal treatment.

E-tongue refers to a sensor-driven device imitating gustatory systems of human being (Xu et al., 2019). The response values of eight sensors are presented in Table 1. The intensities of sourness, astringency, aftertaste-B, and richness in noodles at all CP substitution levels were below the detection threshold (Fu et al., 2025), indicating that CP incorporation did not introduce any perceivable off-flavors. The unchanged richness further indicates that the mouthfeel was not compromised. In contrast, the umami intensity of the noodles increased progressively with CP addition. This enhancement in umami might be attributed to the increased release of umami-related amino acids (glutamic acid and aspartic acid) and the higher content of minerals (sodium and potassium), which are known to potentiate umami perception (Liu et al., 2025). Conversely, Zhang et al. (2022) reported a decrease in umami with the incorporation of pre-dried mealworm larvae flour in frankfurters, highlighting that the impact of such ingredients on taste can vary significantly across different food systems. Importantly, the enhancement of umami without concurrent changes in other tastes highlights the potential of CP as a functional ingredient for improving flavor quality.

Aroma components and contents

When CP was added, cooked noodles exhibited altered multi-scale structures and changed contents of fatty acids and amino acids, which collectively resulted in different aroma profile of noodles (Shen et al., 2021). As shown in Table 2, a total of 52 volatile flavor compounds were identified, including aldehydes, alcohols, esters, ketones, and pyrazines. Aldehydes exhibited the greatest diversity and the highest abundance among all volatile compounds in the noodle samples. (Fig. 5C and D). However, the relative proportion of aldehydes decreased with increasing CP addition, whereas that of ketones and pyrazines increased (Fig. 5D).

Aldehydes were the dominant class of volatile compounds in the noodles, with nonanal being the most abundant among them. Compared to the control, CP-enriched noodles exhibited lower relative abundance of hexanal (grass) and nonanal (citrus and rose) (Liu et al., 2025). In contrast, the relative abundance of benzeneacetaldehyde (hawthorn, honey, and sweet) increased progressively with CP addition, likely derived from the Strecker degradation of amino acids (He et al., 2026). Alcohols constituted another major class of volatile compounds, contributing floral and fermented fruit aromas (Jia et al., 2022). Among these, 2-ethyl-1-hexanol (rose and green) (Jia et al., 2022) was predominant, and its relative abundance increased progressively with CP addition. Ketones typically arise from Maillard reaction and lipid oxidation (Fu et al., 2025). CP-enriched noodles showed increased relative abundance of 2-decanone, 2-nonanone, and 3-octen-2-one, which are associated with green, nut, and crushed bug aromas. Higher relative abundance of pyrazines were observed in CP-enriched noodles. These compounds, which are typical Maillard reaction products, are associated with roasted potato aroma (Fu et al., 2025; Jia et al., 2022). This increase, likely attributable to lipid oxidation and amino acid degradation during thermal processing (Fu et al., 2025), contributed to the redistribution of volatile flavor substances, thereby imparting a unique flavor profile to the CP-enriched noodles.

Principal component analysis of aroma components

To compare the overall volatile profiles of noodle samples with different CP inclusion levels, PCA was performed at the compound class level. As shown in Fig. S1, the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2) explained 97.9% of the total variance (PC1: 76.4%, PC2: 21.5%), indicating that the PCA model adequately represented the volatile profile of the noodle samples. According to Table S1, aldehydes (−0.47) and others (−0.44) exhibited a moderate negative correlation with PC1, whereas pyrazines (0.45), ketones (0.45), and alcohols (0.43) demonstrated a moderate positive correlation with PC1. In contrast, Esters exhibited a negligible loading on PC1 (−0.0038) but a high positive loading on PC2 (0.88), indicating that they were the primary drivers of sample separation along PC2 while contributing little to PC1. A clear separation of the four samples on the PCA biplot revealed distinct volatile profiles driven by different addition levels. The Control profile was largely influenced by aldehydes and esters, which contributed fatty, fruity, and sweet notes. In contrast, CP9 was distinguished by a pyrazine-driven roasted character. CP6, associated with ketones and alcohols, displayed a more complex aroma pattern, whereas CP3 occupied a unique region in the third quadrant, suggesting a distinct volatile composition. These results confirm that the addition level is a critical determinant of the final volatile characteristics of the CP-enriched noodles.

Sensory evaluation

As presented in Fig. 5B, the control noodles received the highest appearance values. The lower appearance scores of CP-enriched noodles were mainly attributed to their darker color and reduced lightness, consistent with the color results, as well as a slightly decreased surface uniformity associated with CP particles. CP-enriched noodles had lower smell scores than the control, but no significant difference was observed among samples with different CP concentrations, possibly because the volatile changes induced by CP were not strong enough to exceed panelists' perception thresholds, while the inherent wheat flavor partially masked the new odors. Moreover, the scores for taste, texture, and overall acceptability did not differ significantly among all noodle samples (p > 0.05), indicating that CP incorporation did not impair palatability and maintained acceptable eating quality.

Correlation between sensory evaluation and electronic sensing technologies

The correlations between volatile compounds and E-nose sensor responses are shown in Fig. 6A. W1C, W5S, W3C, W1W, W2S, and W2W exhibited significant positive correlations with 2-decanone, 2-butyl-2-octenal, benzeneacetaldehyde, and 3-ethyl-2,5-dimethyl-pyrazine, which mainly belong to ketones, aldehydes, and Maillard-derived heterocyclic compounds associated with roasted, fatty, and nutty notes. These correlations indicate that CP substitution promoted lipid oxidation and Maillard reactions by providing proteins and lipids, thereby increasing aroma complexity.

Correlation analysis. (A) Correlations between volatile compounds and E-nose sensor responses. (B) Correlations between amino acids and E-tongue sensor responses. (C) Correlation network between flavor-related parameters (volatile compounds, E-nose responses, E-tongue responses, amino acids) and sensory evaluation metrics.

As shown in Fig. 6B, significant correlations were observed between amino acid composition and taste attributes. Phe, Leu, Cys, Tyr, Ser, Glu, Ala, Pro, His, and Arg had significant positive correlations with umami, suggesting that CP enrichment may enhance the release of amino acids during processing, thus improving savory perception. However, richness was negatively correlated with Phe, Leu, Ser, Glu, and Pro, and sourness exhibited a negative correlation with Pro. No significant correlations were found for bitterness, astringency, aftertaste-B, aftertaste-A, or saltiness, suggesting that CP did not introduce obvious off-flavors.

The correlation network (Fig. 6C) identified appearance and overall acceptability as central nodes, indicating that consumer preference depended on the combined effects of visual quality and flavor perception. Several lipid-oxidation-related aldehydes and esters (e.g., hexadecanoic acid methyl ester and (E,E)-2,4-nonadienal) were positively associated with overall acceptability, suggesting that pleasant fatty and cooked aroma notes enhanced product appeal. In contrast, compounds such as 2-decanone, benzeneacetaldehyde, pyrazine derivatives, and most amino acids were negatively correlated with appearance, suggesting that although CP improved nutritional value, it may have compromised visual quality. Notably, E-nose sensor W1S was positively correlated with overall acceptability, whereas multiple sensors were negatively associated with appearance, highlighting the high sensitivity of E-nose to appearance-related quality changes. Moreover, E-tongue richness showed positive correlations with both appearance and overall acceptability, while umami showed the opposite trend. This indicates that overall taste balance, rather than umami intensity alone, governed consumer preference.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effects of CP incorporation levels on the physicochemical, structural, antioxidant, and flavor characteristics of noodles. CP incorporation significantly increased protein, fat, amino acids, and total phenolic contents while reducing carbohydrate content (p < 0.05), resulting in enhanced antioxidant capacity. CP addition also strengthened intermolecular interactions and reduced starch crystallinity, indicating disruption of the ordered starch matrix. These structural changes were associated with lower RDS and eGI, together with increased RS content, highlighting the potential of CP to improve the nutritional quality and metabolic functionality of starch-based foods. In addition, CP incorporation increased noodle hardness, modified volatile flavor profiles, and enhanced umami intensity. Although darker color and lower appearance scores were observed compared to the control noodles, cooking quality remained acceptable, and no significant differences were found in taste, texture, or overall acceptability across all noodle samples (control and CP-enriched alike) (p > 0.05), indicating that palatability was largely maintained. Different substitution levels showed distinct advantages in nutritional enhancement, processing performance, and sensory quality, suggesting that formulation strategies may be tailored according to specific product targets and consumer preferences. Future studies should focus on flavor optimization, storage stability, shelf-life evaluation, consumer acceptance across markets, and in vivo validation. Overall, CP shows strong promise as a sustainable functional ingredient for improving the nutritional value, structural characteristics, and flavor attributes of starch-based foods.
