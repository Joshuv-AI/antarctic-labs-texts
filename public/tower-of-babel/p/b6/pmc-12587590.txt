Polymorphisms in DNA repair related genes as risk factors for lung cancer in Cuban population: a case control study
Authors: Elizabeth Reyes-Reyes, Elizabeth Cuétara-Lugo, José Alfredo Herrera-Isidrón, Mario Pérez-Benítez, Idania González-Pérez, Marlen Izquierdo-González, Yoanna Ivette Flores-Vega, Diana Laura Páramo-González, Claudina Angela Pérez-Novo, Miguel David Fernández-Pérez, Wim Vanden-Berghe, Idania Rodeiro-Guerra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSingle nucleotide variants are important factors involved in lung cancer onset and prognosis. In Cuba, there are scarce studies evaluating the possible association between genetic variants and lung cancer. Here, we described the frequency distribution of genetic variants in DNA repair related genes and assessed their impact on lung cancer susceptibility and survival prognosis in a cohort of Cuban populationMethodsCase-Control study included 300 lung cancer patients and 300 control subjects. Variants: rs1042522, rs11016879, rs13181, rs25487 and rs861539 were genotyped. The association analysis was evaluated by logistic regression and the impact on overall survival was estimated by Kaplan-Meier and Cox regression analyses. ResultsGenotype frequencies were in Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium except for rs1042522 in patients. The heterozygote of this variant was associated with low risk to lung cancer (Overdominant, OR: 0.53, p = 0.01). Conversely, rs25487 showed trend to additive genotype risk (Additive model, OR:1.61, p = 0.01). Differences in genotype frequencies according to skin color of controls were detected for rs1042522 and rs861539 (p = 0.01). The rs11016879 and rs1042522 showed joint protective effects with skin color. Interaction between rs25487 and cigarette smoking analysis revealed higher lung cancer risk (OR = 3.72, p = 0.03, p-interaction: 0.01). Interaction between alcohol consumption and rs13181 reached borderline significance for decreased risk of lung cancer (OR = 0.25, p = 0.03, p-interaction: 0.06). In addition, rs11016879 alternative allele was an independent factor for higher mean 5-years overall survival (log-rank test p = 0.03). ConclusionsThe study provides the first data evaluating the relevance of genetic variants in DNA damage repair related genes for lung cancer management in Cuban population. Our findings indicate a significant contribution of the genetic variants to lung cancer susceptibility and highlights the importance of considering skin color within analyses for disease susceptibility in Cuban population.

Introduction

Lung cancer (LC) is the leading cause of cancer deaths globally and was the most frequently diagnosed cancer in 2022 [1]. Malignant tumors are the second cause of death in Cuba, being LC, the first cause of cancer deaths and third cancer most commonly diagnosed [2]. Susceptibility to this disease involves a complex interplay between genetic and environmental factors, such as lifestyle, occupation, and the presence of high/moderate penetrance genetic variants.

The presence of single nucleotide variants (SNVs) in DNA repair genes have been linked to impaired DNA repair efficacy favoring genotoxicity and/or mutagenicity [3]. A less efficient reparative capacity may increase the risk of LC. In turn, it results in decreased removal of cytostatic induced adducts in DNA at the tumor cell, which correlates with better clinical response and prognosis [4]. It is known that genotype frequencies and the results of LC risk association studies exhibit marked differences between populations [5]. However, most studies have been conducted in Caucasian or Asian ancestry populations [6]. Therefore, the impact common SNVs previously evaluated in other populations, needs to be investigated to determine its role in LC across Latin American admixed populations.

Cuban population is highly admixed and it is informed that there is a strong relationship between admixture proportions, melanin levels and census categories in the Cuban population [7, 8]. In addition, several studies have reported on SNVs frequencies in different association studies. For instance, on embryonic implantation rates in patients receiving in vitro fertilization [9], schizophrenia [10], dementia [11], COVID-19 susceptibility [12], hypertension [13], Wilson´s disease [14] and dengue hemorrhagic fever risk [15]. Then, in present study we aimed to evaluate the impact of five low penetrance loci: rs1042522 (TP53), rs11016879 (MGMT), rs13181 (ERCC2), rs25487 (XRCC1) and rs861539 (XRCC3) on LC risk and on survival (SV) prognosis in a Cuban cohort.

Materials and methods

Description of study subjects

The present case–control study was conducted from 2017 to 2019. In total, 300 Cuban LC patients were included, and 300 controls were selected considering the proportion by age and sex reported for the Cuban population [16]. The controls were older than 35 years of age and considering that the prevalence of men is 49.7% we enrolled a similar distribution of control individuals in the study.

All participants answered a questionnaire, comprising smoking habit, alcohol consumption, occupational risk exposures, history of other respiratory diseases. The ethnic classification of individuals was conducted by self-perception, following the criteria used in the last Cuban census, which states that Cuban population can be clustered into three categories: White, Admixed and Black [17]. Occupational risk exposure was defined as a work history of at least three years with the occupational agents/activities described by The International Agency for Research on Cancer with increased risk for LC [18]. Clinical data was collected from the patient´s clinical records.

Sample handling and sequencing

Blood samples were obtained by venipuncture and stored in tubes with K2EDTA. Genomic DNA extraction was performed using Qiagen DNeasy® Blood & Tissue Kit following manufacturer recommendations. DNA quantification and purity was determined using Nanodrop (Thermo Scientific). The genotypes were determined by targeted sequencing (Amplicon Sequencing Method) on a Hiseq X Ten sequencer (Illumina platform) according to Illumina protocols. The reference genome used was Feb. 2009 (GRCh37/hg19) (UCSC Genome Browser Home). The selected SNPs are shown in Table 1.

Statistical analysis

The Fisher’s exact test was used to compare demographic characteristics between groups or T-test for continuous variables. For each SNV, genotype and allele frequencies were established in controls and patients by gene counting. Hardy-Weinberg Equilibrium (HWE) was tested using Pearson’s Chi-square test.

LC risk model was analyzed as: I. Risk Model Construction. LC risk was evaluated using Odds Ratio (OR). Forward selection procedure was used to choose the significant variables (confounding factors) to include in the multivariable logistic regression models. II Genetic Variants inheritance models. Each SNV was tested considering five penetrance models (codominant, dominant, recessive, additive and overdominant) and adjusting by the confounding factors. III. Risk Model Discrimination. The performance of the models was evaluated by Akaike Information Criteria (AIC) to determine the best fit model [19]. The 95% confidence intervals (CI) were considered as a measure estimation´s precision.

Gene-Environment interaction was evaluated through logistic regression including the interaction term. Stratified analyses were conducted among subgroups within smoking status, alcohol consumption, occupational risk exposure and history of respiratory diseases.

Kaplan-Meier analysis and log-rank test were used to compare the five-year overall survival (OS) time across subgroups defined by clinical stage and genotype. The OS was calculated as the number of months between the date of diagnosis and the date of death or last follow-up. Patients known to be alive on the date of last follow-up were censored. An event was considered as deaths related to lung cancer. Deaths caused by other reasons were considered as censored data. The information about patient´s status in the last follow-up was requested to the Cuban National Cancer Registry. Multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to estimate the adjusted hazard ratio (HR) and their 95% CIs.

Data was analyzed using R Studio Programming Environment for Data Analysis Version 4.3.2 and ‘SNPassoc’ package [20]. A statistically significant association was considered if the p-value < 0.05 in the two-sided test.

There were statistically significant differences given the risk factors analyzed (p < 0.01). Age and sex distributions were different between both groups. Smokers, prevalence of alcohol consumption, occupational risk exposure and presence of respiratory diseases were higher in the patient group (Table 2). The histological type of the patients´ tumor was Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer (NSCLC). The most frequent histological subtype was adenocarcinoma (ADC) (62%), followed by squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) (29%), while 9% of patients had other subtypes. At diagnosis, 85.4% of the patients were in advanced stages (stages III and IV). Following first line chemotherapy treatment, disease progression (37.6%) was the most common clinical evaluation, as well as occurrence of adverse events related to chemotherapy (83.6%). Additionally, the distribution of histological subtypes varied according to other variables. In individuals without occupational exposure, SCC was the most frequent diagnosis (Fisher exact test, p = 0.03).

SNVs and lung cancer risk

The observed genotype frequencies for genetic variants in both Controls and Patients did not deviate significantly from those expected from HWE, except for the TP53 rs1042522 in the patient group (p = 0.01). The distributions of genotypes and allele frequencies of each SNV did not show statistical differences between both groups (data not shown). Two SNVs were significantly associated with LC risk. Heterozygote of TP53 rs1042522 (genotype GC) compared with homozygote individuals (CC or GG) had a decreased risk to LC under two genetic models, Overdominant (OR: 0.53, 95% CI: 0.32–0.86) and Codominant (OR: 0.53, 95% CI: 0.31–0.90). Conversely, XRCC1 rs25487 was associated with increased LC risk under three genetic models. The risk of having LC was 69% higher in people having at least one alternative allele T (genotypes CT-TT) with respect to individuals having none (genotype CC) (dominant model, OR: 1.69, 95% CI: 1.05–2.71). Codominant model showed that the heterozygote risk estimate (genotype CT) was intermediate between the two homozygote risk estimates. Consistently, the additive model confirmed a trend to additive genotype risks (OR:1.61, 95% CI: 1.10–2.38). When the SNVs in the genes MGMT, ERCC2 and XRCC3 were analyzed, the OR estimations did not reach statistical signification in any of the considered genetic models (Table 3).

Gene–environment interaction analysis

In order to determine whether there is a significant synergic relation between the individual SNVs and non-genetic factors to LC risk, we assessed gene–environment interactions. Then, we determined the stratified ORs for the risk genotypes, the environmental factor and combined gene–environment interaction. In this analysis, the effect of cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, occupational risk exposure and history of respiratory diseases was evaluated, which would explain how the individual SNV affect the environmental-associated LC risk. Considering previous results (Table 3), we applied a dominant penetrance model for MGMT rs11016879, codominant for XRCC1 rs25487, a recessive model for ERCC2 and for TP53 rs1042522 and XRCC3 rs861539 an overdominant model was used (Fig. 1).

The risk of LC was increased by joint effects of XRCC1 rs25487 and cigarette smoking, with the highest effect attributed to the homozygous genotype TT (OR = 3.72, 95% CI: 1.26–11.01, p = 0.03). The interaction test for this SNV revealed a significant synergic relation between cigarette smoking and the genetic variant (p-interaction: 0.01). The opposite effect was found considering the variant TP53 rs1042522. A protector effect was estimated for smokers carrying the heterozygous genotype with borderline significance (GC) (OR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.43–1.02, p = 0.06), but the resulting interaction term was not statistically significant (p-interaction: 0.46). An increased LC risk estimation was found for the combined genotypes (GA-AA) of MGMT rs11016879 in smokers (OR = 1.22, 95% CI: 0.79–1.89, p = 0.37) compared to those who never smoked (OR = 0.63, 95% CI: 0.26–1.49, p = 0.29), but the interaction term was not significant (p-interaction: 0.18).

The LC risk also significantly increased when alcohol consumption was analyzed together with the heterozygote of XRCC3 rs861539 (GA) (OR = 1.77, 95% CI: 1.09–2.88, p = 0.02) and with homozygous genotype (TT) of XRCC1 rs25487 with borderline significance (OR = 2.78, 95% CI: 1.06–7.30, p = 0.07), but the interaction terms were not statistically significant for any of these two genetic variants (p-interaction: 0.30 and 0.73 respectively). On the contrary, the interaction between alcohol consumption and ERCC2 rs13181 suggests joint effects for decreased risk of LC, as the interaction term was in the limit of significance (p-interaction: 0.06). It was observed that in alcohol consumers the risk slightly increased but was not significant (OR = 1.10, 95% CI: 0.51–2.35, p = 0.81), while among non-consumers the risk was significantly lowered (OR = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.05–1.10, p = 0.03). Similarly, the interaction between TP53 rs1042522 and alcohol consumption showed a decreased LC risk (OR = 0.57, 95% CI: 0.36–0.90, p = 0.02), interaction term was not statistically significant (p-interaction: 0.14) (Fig. 1).

The analysis of joint effects between genetic factors, the history of respiratory diseases and occupational risk exposures was not statistically significant. However, a tendence between XRCC1 rs25487 and XRCC3 rs861539 with these two variables was observed (Fig. 1). The risk of LC increased when the T- genotypes (CT and TT) of XRCC1 rs25487 were combined with the presence of occupational risk exposures and when the variable history of respiratory diseases was assessed together with the homozygote (TT). Likewise, LC risk increased when the heterozygote of XRCC3 (GA) was analyzed in combination with the presence of respiratory diseases (OR = 1.58, 95% CI: 0.62–4.01, p = 0.33) and occupational risk exposures (OR = 1.67, 95% CI: 0.92–3.04, p = 0.09). Moreover, data suggested a potential protective effect of the homozygote of ERCC2 rs13181 (GG) in presence of respiratory diseases (OR = 0.49, 95% CI: 0.12–2.00, p = 0.34).

The genotype distribution of the variants TP53 rs1042522 and XRCC3 rs861539 was significantly different in the Controls’ group according to skin color (p = 0.01) (Table 4). The prevalence of the alternative homozygous genotype of TP53 rs1042522 (CC) increased from White (10%), to Admixed (18%) and to Black individuals (30%). In the case of XRCC3 rs861539, the alternative homozygous genotype (AA) was predominant in Admixed (16%), followed by White (11%) and Black (3%) individuals. Meanwhile, XRCC1 rs25487 reached a borderline significance (p = 0.05) among Controls. In the subgroups of White and Admixed individuals, the frequency of the alternative homozygous genotype (TT) was similar (7% and 8% respectively) and higher than in Black individuals (3%).

In general, no significant differences were detected in the genotype frequency between groups by skin color of individuals. Only in Admixed individuals, significant differences for MGMT rs11016879 (p = 0.03) were found. The low frequency of MGMT rs11016879 homozygous genotype in Admixed Patients (1%) compared to Controls (12%) resulted in a significant protective estimation for LC under the recessive model (OR = 0.11, CI: 0.01–0.84, p < 0.01). In addition, the calculated interaction term for joint effects of the combined factors was significant (p-interaction: 0.01), indicating that there is a synergic relation between the individual SNV and skin color of individuals. Then for TP53 rs1042522, borderline differences (p = 0.06) were found in genotype’s distribution of the polymorphism in Black skin color individuals (Table 4). For this group, the heterozygote was a protector factor for LC (OR = 0.32, CI: 0.12–0.84, p = 0.02). A significant interaction was detected between skin color and TP53 under the Overdominant model (p-interaction: 0.03). Overall, the characterization of the frequency distribution of SNVs by skin color and the risk association analysis highlights the importance of considering the ethnic origin of individuals to evaluate susceptibility to the disease.

Survival analysis

In the study we evaluated the clinical relevance of the selected genetic variants. The primary endpoint was OS. The mean follow-up time was 19 months (1–171 months) and at the moment of analysis 34.6% of patients were censored. The SNVs were assessed in relation to 5 year- SV of Cuban LC patients. After univariate analysis of SV (Kaplan-Meier) for each one of the genetic variants, only MGMT rs11016879 was an independent factor significantly associated with 5-year OS (log-rank test p = 0.03) (Fig. 2).

Under the dominant model, it was estimated a mean SV time of 23.3 months (95% CI: 19.9–26.8) for individuals carrying at least one alternative allele A of MGMT rs11016879 (genotypes GA or AA) compared to the reference genotype GG (mean SV = 18.3 months, 95% CI: 15.5–21.2). This variant remained a significant factor among the rest of genetic variants in a multivariate Cox regression analysis adjusting by clinical stage. A significant protective HR estimation was obtained for individuals carrying at least one alternative allele A (genotypes GA or AA) (HR = 0.61, 95% CI:0,44 − 0,83, p < 0.01). On average approximately a 39% lower risk of death on the A genotypes than the reference one (GG). The analysis also indicated a possible protective effect of the alternative genotype of ERCC2 rs13181, although the estimation did not reach the significance threshold (HR = 0.66, 95% CI: 0.39–1.12, p = 0.12).

Discussion

The study of interindividual differences due to SNVs is important to understand the mechanisms of complex diseases such as cancer, among individuals and populations. Here, it is reported the first case-control study evaluating the impact of five SNVs in DNA repair genes on LC risk and SV prognosis in Cuban population.

A protector effect for LC risk was found for the heterozygote of TP53 rs1042522 (overdominant model) among the Cuban sample evaluated. It is worth mentioning that a deviation from HWE was observed in the patients group. Deviations from HW proportions may indicate population inbreeding, admixture process or may be evidence of association between a genetic variant and the disease of interest [21]. This last remark supports the LC association obtained, as the protein p53 is critical for maintenance of genomic integrity. The rs1042522 at codon 72 leads to Arg72 (G allele) or Pro72 (C allele) protein variants with markedly altered primary structures and different biochemical functions. The p53–Arg72 protein is a more effective apoptosis inducer than the p53–Pro72 protein. Conversely, the p53–Pro72 protein preferentially translocate to the nucleus, where it promotes cell cycle arrest in G1 [22].

Meta-analysis conducted among Caucasian, African American and Asian populations have indicated that Pro72 allele is a risk factor for the development of LC [23]. Interestingly, markers of oxidative stress, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease severity and pulmonary emphysema, have been primarily observed in patients carrying the p53–Arg72 (GG genotype) [24]. Another study found the G allele to be associated with accelerated smoking-related decline in lung function. The authors discussed that those with p53-Pro72 alleles appear to be more resistant to smoking-induced lung damage, suggesting that cell cycle arrest and DNA repair are more important than apoptosis in protecting lung tissue from smoke-induced DNA damage. Although ultimately less efficient than apoptosis at removing DNA damaged cells, activation of repair mechanisms by p53-Pro72 would afford considerable protection from DNA damage and, at the same time, minimize tissue injury after exposure to cigarette smoke [25]. The protective effects observed in present study support this evidence. Overall, it has been proposed that in humans, p53 protects from DNA damage and provides a potential explanation for differences in the susceptibility of individuals to the adverse effects of smoking.

In the case of XRCC1 rs25487, the variant was associated with higher odds of developing LC under an additive genetic model. The rs25487 leads to the substitution of the aminoacid Arg for Gln and it is suggested to produce a conformational change in the BRCT1 interaction domain of the protein [26]. This change may affect the interaction with other BER proteins leading to reduced repair efficiency [27]. However, a protective effect of the alternative allele T (Gln) is reported against the occurrence of chromosomal aberrations in individuals with occupational risk exposure [28].

These contradictory results can be due to the existence of different DNA repair systems. Deficiencies in one system can be taken over by other repair mechanisms. Caldecott (2019) proposes a compensatory role mediated by HR: from unrepaired single-strand breaks, double-strand breaks may arise upon collapse of the replication fork, being then repaired by HR [29]. On the other hand, the association of XRCC1 rs25487 with LC in different ethnic groups have given inconsistent results [30]. The present results are similar to a study (113 cases) conducted among Hispanics with NSCLC, that found an increased risk for the T allele [31]. In addition, a small study (30 cases) in Brazilian population showed the highest cancer susceptibility among individuals with the heterozygous genotype [32]. In general, Latin American populations are underrepresented and have rendered inconclusive results.

SNVs and lung cancer risk in stratified analysis

Toxic habits and occupational exposure may lead to greater predisposition to LC. Our results suggest that the susceptibility to develop LC among Cubans varies by skin color of individuals, and that the effects of SNVs are enhanced in the presence of certain exogenous agents, indicating gene-exposure interactions.

Joint effects indicating synergic relation between individual SNVs and skin color of individuals was found. TP53 rs1042522 (GC genotype) showed protector effects among black individuals and MGMT rs11016879 (AA genotype) in admixed subjects. These polymorphisms have significant differences in their prevalence among world populations [33]. It is known that SNVs frequencies and the association studies to LC risk exhibit differences between populations of different genetic ancestry [34]. Since it is questionable whether skin color categories or SNVs alone have sufficient biological impact and predictive statistical value in clinical cancer diagnostics, further integration of ethnicity with epigenetic diet, lifestyle and environmental cancer risk factors may reveal synergies and strengthen statistical power of biomarker panels in LC precision medicine.

Interaction results support a synergic relation between smoking and XRCC1 rs25487 to LC risk. The scaffold protein XRCC1 plays a central role in the repair of abasic (AP) sites and DNA single-strand breaks. Several carcinogens in tobacco smoke produce single-strand breaks. The variant T allele (Gln) has been associated with higher levels of DNA adducts and decreased AP site and single-strand break reparation, that may lead to higher levels of DNA damage [35].

The effect of ERCC2 rs13181 was modified in no alcohol consumption group, as the alternative genotype (GG) decreased LC risk by 0.25-fold. The higher risk among alcohol consumers was not statistically significant. The repair of the DNA damage induced by genotoxic reactive oxygen species and the carcinogenic effects of alcohol-derived acetaldehyde, involves coordinated activities of different DNA repair pathways, such as NER and BER [36]. The protein ERCC2 is an important coordinator of the NER pathway. The Lys751Gln (rs13181) in ERCC2 gene is associated to faulty NER signal and reduced DNA repair capacity, and may thereby modulate cancer susceptibility [37].

Regarding OS evaluation, only MGMT rs11016879 was an independent prognostic factor of 5 years SV. A higher mean SV time was found in individuals carrying at least one alternative A allele (genotypes GA or AA) compared to the reference genotype (GG). Elevated MGMT expression level correlates with low SV time in patients treated with cisplatin [38]. This variant, located in an intronic region of MGMT gene, has been identified as a predictor of recurrence after surgery in patients with NSCLC. An intronic variant could alter the mRNA levels, splicing process and enzymatic activity, thus impacting negatively therapeutic response [27].

The study has some limitations, such as memory bias and sample size. Nevertheless, significant evidence was found, which could be attributed to a strong biological relation between the SNVs and the disease. The results could support the evaluation of genetic biomarkers for early disease detection and LC monitoring. Further research with higher sample size is needed to confirm the impact of the studied SNVs in the Cuban population.

Conclusions

The study provides the first data evaluating the relevance of genetic variants in DNA damage repair genes for lung cancer management in Cuban population. The results showed that TP53 rs1042522 and XRCC1 rs25487 may be relevant factors for LC risk assessment and MGMT rs11016879 for LC prognosis in the Cuban population. The susceptibility also varies by skin color and types of carcinogenic exposures among Cuban individuals. The study highlights the usefulness of SNVs in DNA repair related genes as susceptibility and clinical biomarkers for improving LC management, which would support the development of precision medicine in Cuba.
