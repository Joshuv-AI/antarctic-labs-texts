Dietary Intervention Trial Design in Patients With an Ileoanal Pouch: Lessons From a Randomized, Double‐Blind, Placebo‐Controlled Feeding Study
Authors: Zaid S. Ardalan, Miles P. Sparrow, Faran Rohani, Peter R. Gibson, Chu K. Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeThe aims of this study were to assess the effects of a whole diet strategy, a Monash Pouch Diet (MPD), on pouch‐related symptoms, inflammation, quality of life, and stool characteristics in a cohort of patients with symptomatic pouches and a history of pouchitis compared with a typical Australian pouch diet.MethodsIn this randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled dietary feeding trial, patients with ileoanal pouches received either a Monash Pouch diet or a typical Australian diet for 7 weeks. Clinical scores (Pouch Disease Activity Index, PDAI), pouch symptoms, tolerability, fecal calprotectin, and quality of life were measured pre‐ and post‐intervention. The primary outcome consisted of the proportion of patients achieving symptomatic remission (clinical PDAI ≤ 2).ResultsAll patients on the MPD experienced worsening symptoms, and the trial was terminated early. The majority of the participants reported partial adherence (50%–80%) and poor tolerability (median: 40 mm). Quality of life outcomes were highly variable across dietary arms, and fecal indices showed no consistent trends related to diet.ConclusionThis double‐blinded, placebo‐controlled, dietary feeding trial failed to determine the effect of the Monash Pouch Diet on pouchitis but suggests that the design of dietary trials for pouch patients requires careful consideration.

Introduction

Dietary factors significantly impact the development of ileoanal pouch‐related symptoms and pouchitis [1]. However, there is to date a paucity of evidence from dietary intervention trials to guide the effective dietary management of pouch‐related symptoms or pouchitis.

A Monash Pouch Diet (MPD) was proposed as a whole‐food diet strategy that hypothetically targets pathogenic mechanisms involved in pouchitis (reduction in hydrogen sulfide production and promoting carbohydrate fermentation) whilst optimizing pouch function (by reducing ileal free water content) [1]. A pilot study of 12 patients showed high patient tolerability, favorable changes in fecal metabolite profile, and signals of clinical benefit suggesting potential improvements in pouch function [2].

We conducted a randomized, double‐blind, placebo‐controlled dietary feeding study in a cohort of patients with symptomatic pouches and a history of pouchitis to assess the impact of the MPD on pouch‐related symptoms, inflammation, and quality of life (QOL), correlating these with changes in stool characteristics compared with a typical Australian pouch diet.

Methods

This 8‐week single‐center trial included patients ≥ 18 years of age who had an ileoanal pouch for ulcerative colitis, were ≥ 12 months post ileostomy closure, had a history of pouchitis, and were experiencing pouchitis symptoms (PDAI ≥ 3). Patients were eligible if they were receiving stable pouch‐directed therapies for ≥ 4 weeks. Exclusions were coeliac disease, stricturing disease, recent small bowel obstruction, probiotic use, plant‐based diets, or pregnancy.

During a 1‐week run‐in period, participants underwent pouchoscopy, collected a 24‐h fecal sample, and completed a 7‐day diary to capture habitual dietary intake, bowel habits, and symptoms. Participants were then randomized 1:1 in blocks of six to receive the MPD or a control diet (Table S1). Foods were prepared by chefs experienced in feeding studies and matched in macronutrients (except for protein), digestible starch, lactose, and dietary fiber content, modeled on healthy eating guidelines, and looked similar in appearance and packaging to achieve blinding of both patients and the research team (except for the study dietitian). Adherence was monitored by the study dietitian using food checklists and direct questioning. The success of blinding was recorded halfway through the intervention by asking participants to identify whether they were on the intervention diet to ensure that symptoms did not influence the patient's response.

Daily symptom and weekly tolerability scores were recorded throughout, with a final pouchoscopy, fecal calprotectin, and assessments including PDAI scores, obstructed defecation syndrome scores, and health‐related QOL measured via the Cleveland Global‐QOL at week 7. A 24‐h fecal collection was also completed to assess changes in calprotectin concentration, weight, water content, and consistency according to the King's Stool Chart as previously described [2].

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients achieving symptomatic remission defined as a clinical PDAI subscore ≤ 2. Secondary outcomes included symptomatic improvement (reduction of clinical PDAI subscore by ≥ 2), endoscopic improvement (reduction of endoscopic PDAI by ≥ 2), clinical response (a reduction of total PDAI by ≥ 3), clinical remission (clinical PDAI score ≤ 2, endoscopic activity ≤ 1, and histologic score of ≤ 1; a total PDAI ≤ 4), changes in fecal calprotectin, health‐related quality of life, fecal indices, pouch fermentation indices, tolerability and acceptability of the diet, and adverse events.

A total sample size of 64 was planned based on a 61% efficacy of the MPD in symptomatic pouch patients with a clinical PDAI of ≥ 3, as shown in the pilot study, and a 25% placebo response from dietary trials in pouch patients [3].

No Artificial Intelligence Generated tool was utilized in the content development of any portion of this manuscript.

Results

The study was prematurely terminated due to deteriorating outcomes across both arms. Six UC patients with symptomatic ileoanal pouches and a history of chronic pouchitis were recruited (Table S2). Four of six patients (67%) had current dietary restrictions, and all reported perceived food intolerances. Randomization placed three patients in each arm, with two from the control group showing active pouchitis at baseline, but none in the MPD arm. Two withdrew due to worsening pouch function and poor tolerability of interventional diets, one from each arm.

All patients on the MPD experienced worsening symptoms (Figure 1), with one patient's stool frequency increasing from nine to 16 daily, accompanied by escalating urgency and abdominal discomfort. Fecal calprotectin in this patient increased from 57 to 510 μg/g. Among those completing the study, all except one patient on the control diet achieved a clinical PDAI ≤ 2. In the control group, one with severe pouchitis (endoscopic PDAI of 6/6, fecal calprotectin 1203 μg/g) withdrew, a second showed endoscopic improvement (< 1 endoscopic PDAI) without changes in symptom frequency or severity, and a third achieved symptomatic remission with a clinical PDAI < 3 but had worsening nocturnal symptoms and worsening endoscopic inflammation. QOL outcomes varied, with no clear pattern across dietary groups (Figure 1). Fecal indices showed no consistent trends related to diet (Figure 1, Table S3).

The broken red line indicates threshold values for clinical symptoms (Clinical PDAI ≥ 2), endoscopic activity (Endoscopic PDAI ≥ 2), histologic activity (Histologic PDAI ≥ 2), pouchitis (total PDAI ≥ 7), and good quality of life (CGQOL ≥ 0.7). (B) Adherence and tolerability scores for both diets over the course of the study. Adherence to the diet was defined as ‘excellent’ when patient consumed of ≥ 80% of provided study foods and consumed ≤ 1 non‐study meals per week, ‘partial’ if they consumed 50%–80% of the provided studies and/or 2–3 non‐study meals per week and ‘poor’ for < 50% consumption and > 3 non‐study meals per week. Tolerability was considered ‘excellent’ if the VAS score was > 75 mm, ‘moderate’ if 50–75 mm, and ‘poor’ if < 50 mm.

Daily intake of key components of the habitual and interventional diets is shown (Figure S1). Adherence varied, with the majority reporting 50%–80% adherence. One patient on the MPD diet reported poor adherence (< 50%). Additionally, adherence dropped over the course of the study (Figure 1). Tolerability was generally low (median: 40 mm), except for one patient on the control diet where it was high (95 mm) (Figure 1). Weight loss over 5% and fatigue were noted in one patient in each arm. Bloating was reduced in two in the control arm. No serious adverse events were reported. Success of blinding was achieved in 6/6, with 5/6 patients unable to accurately identify their diet, while one patient on control was unsure.

Discussion

To evaluate the efficacy of the MPD in pouch patients, we adopted a strategy of following a dietitian‐taught feasibility study with a controlled feeding study. This approach, previously effective in other cohorts [4], encountered challenges in the latter stage with poor diet tolerance, suboptimal adherence, and worsening symptoms in both the investigational and control groups. Consequently, the intended assessment of the MPD's effects on pouch behavior was compromised.

The issues faced were multifaceted. Controlled feeding trials, typically the standard for dietary studies that aim to prove the concept, can falter due to significant deviations from participants' usual diets, inducing anxiety and a negative response, well‐reported in patients with functional gut disorders, known as the nocebo effect [5]. This is particularly relevant in symptomatic pouch patients, in whom irritable pouch syndrome is reported in 18%–43% [6], and is associated with high levels of anxiety [7]. Additionally, the 1‐week rotational menu, an inherent downside of a prolonged feeding study, contributed to diet fatigue and declining adherence. This was compounded by the volume and unpalatable nature of the diet despite all food being cooked by chefs experienced in such studies and followed variations of well‐tested recipes. The negative effect of such challenges on adherence and tolerability likely masked the efficacy (or lack of) for the MPD.

Pouch patients often navigate complex dietary challenges. Many have strict dietary restrictions stemming from current or past pouchitis [8], influencing their food choices and tolerance. In this study, all participants had perceived food intolerances, with two‐thirds applying dietary restrictions at study entry. Despite no active pouchitis, those on the MPD reported exacerbated symptoms, a pattern also seen in those on the control diet. Given the limited pool of participants with an ileoanal pouch, exclusion of participants with existing dietary restrictions from the study was impractical. The structure of our study design may not have fully catered to these nuances, as evidenced by better outcomes in our preceding dietary counseling study where participants enjoyed meal flexibility.

Moreover, the dietary goals for the MPD, particularly the increased oligosaccharide and resistant starch intake, presented additional challenges. It is unclear whether these specific components directly triggered intolerance or symptom worsening; however, they likely contributed to the dietary difficulties observed.

Patients with symptomatic ileal pouches are a heterogeneous group, varying widely in the etiology of their symptoms (inflammatory vs. visceral hypersensitivity), pathogenic mechanisms (microbial vs. immune‐mediated), and severity, along with self‐imposed dietary restrictions. These factors complicate the selection of endpoints for determining dietary intervention efficacy. Notably, in a pilot study of the MPD, two patients with high baseline calprotectin experienced further increases, suggesting potential diet‐related exacerbations of pouchitis.

In conclusion, this double‐blinded, placebo‐controlled dietary intervention trial failed to determine the true effect of the MPD on symptoms of pouch patients with a history of pouchitis. The number of patients studied and the limited adherence did not enable recognition of signals for benefit. Instead, it offers essential lessons for future dietary research concerning pouch patients. First, the dietary intervention should offer flexibility. Providing diverse meal options and catering to individual intolerances could increase adherence and reduce the nocebo effect. This could utilize a hybrid of dietary counseling and feeding intervention [9]. Secondly, it is crucial to categorize patients accurately based on their pouchitis status, such as patients with symptomatic pouches with or without associated pouchitis. The other consideration is to exclude patients who might mechanistically not benefit from MPD, such as those with severe pouchitis. Lastly, the study highlights the need for further modifications to dietary targets in the MPD. Making specific dietary recommendations, however, remains challenging due to the variability in individual tolerance and poor understanding of the diet‐pouch effects. If increasing fruits and vegetables are considered, as per recent recommendations [10], it should be done under the supervision of an experienced IBD dietitian. A wise strategy would be to induce remission in patients with severe pouchitis before trialing new dietary regimens, as these patients are less likely to tolerate changes and could experience worsening symptoms, as seen in the current study.
