Feasibility of using Simple Diet Self-Assessment Tool (SDSAT) to explore patterns of dietary intake for patients undergoing chemotherapy
Authors: Wen-Jie Zhu, Min Yang, Bao-Hua Zou, Hui-Min Qu, Hong-Fan Yu, Qian-Tong Dong, Xi Zhang, Jia-Xin Huang, Xiao-Dong Chen, Zhen-Guang Du, Ning Li, Cong Zhao, Le Tian, Jin-Feng Liu, Meng Tang, Sheng-Ling Qin, Rong Qin, Rui Sun, Lei Yu, Jiang Li, Xian Shen, Qiuling Shi, Ming-Hua Cong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChemotherapy is often accompanied by impaired dietary intake, which is linked to poor clinical outcomes if not effectively addressed. Predicting dietary changes can facilitate early nutritional support, but the dynamic patterns and interindividual variability in dietary intake during chemotherapy remain unclear. Our aim was thus to evaluate the feasibility of using the Simple Diet Self-Assessment Tool (SDSAT) to establish dietary intake patterns in patients undergoing chemotherapy.MethodsThis is an ongoing, prospective cohort study. Patients receiving chemotherapy from the Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences were enrolled. Diet was assessed using the SDSAT once before the treatment and at least three times during one chemotherapy cycle. We applied a group-based trajectory model to identify trajectories of dietary intake. The 24-hour dietary recall (24HR) was used to verify the validity of energy and protein intake estimated by SDSAT; compliance with SDSAT was also assessed.ResultsFive trajectory groups were identified for food intake based on 217 chemotherapy cycles by 56 patients. The compliance rate accounted for 81.11% for 4 cumulative responses per cycle. The agreement between the SDSAT score and the dietary energy and protein intake estimated from 24HR was high (κ: 0.888–1.0, P < 0.001). Groups 1 and 2 had significantly lower dietary energy/protein intake than Groups 4 and 5 (P < 0.001) but with distinct dietary patterns. Group 4 was the most common dietary trajectory (35.10%). Furthermore, Groups 1, 2, and 3 had significantly weaker handgrip strength (OR: 1.10, 95% CI: 1.01–1.19, P = 0.031) compared with Groups 4 and 5.ConclusionsSDSAT can be used to establish dietary trajectories during one chemotherapy cycle. Longitudinal analysis highlights the interindividual differences in dietary intake patterns among patients receiving chemotherapy and may help to inform proactive and individually tailored nutritional interventions.Trail registrationChictr.org.cn Identifier: ChiCTR2100047535 (Date: 2021-06-20) https://www.chictr.org.cn/indexEN.html.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12937-025-01223-4.

Background

Chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of cancer treatment modality in patients with metastatic cancer [1]. In addition, as adjuvant and neoadjuvant therapy and when combined with radiotherapy, chemotherapy plays a crucial role in enhancing curability and decreasing the recurrence rate [2, 3]. Malnutrition, however, frequently develops during chemotherapy, with a prevalence of up to 80% [4], which is attributable to tumor-based or chemotherapy-induced metabolic derangements [5] and nutrition-impact symptoms, such as nausea, anorexia, early satiety, and constipation [6–9]. These signs and symptoms interfere with patients’ oral intake to a variable extent [10, 11] and boost the risk of malnutrition and consequences, including increased treatment toxicity [12], decreased treatment tolerance [4, 13], impaired quality of life [14, 15], and greater risk of mortality [13, 16]. Therefore, nutrition therapy is increasingly regarded as an integral part of multidisciplinary cancer care, offering an opportunity to increase anticancer treatment tolerance, promote quality of life, and improve clinical outcomes [17–19].

However, a multicenter study conducted in China demonstrated that 55% of malnourished patients with cancer did not receive any nutritional intervention or nutrition counseling [20]. In contrast, 31.9% of well-nourished patients received at least one type of medical nutrition therapy, including parenteral nutrition (PN) [20]. Similar results have been reported in European countries [21]. The causes of insufficient and inappropriate nutrition intervention are multifactorial and highly associated with the underdiagnosis and misdiagnosis of malnutrition in the oncology setting [22, 23], which may be more prevalent in patients undergoing chemotherapy, who typically have gradual and volatile dietary changes during the course of treatment [10, 24], unlike patients treated with surgery or radiotherapy whose dietary intake alters rapidly and noticeably [25, 26]. Moreover, patients often fail to report their dietary changes proactively because they assume the deterioration in food intake is expected and normal over the time of therapy, combined with physicians’ lack of awareness of patient’s nutritional status and the shortage of oncology dietitians, making it easy for dietary inadequacy to be overlooked [27–29]. Taken together, understanding the trajectories of variation in patients’ dietary intake during the chemotherapeutic cycle provides a window for predicting their nutritional needs and initiating interventions promptly and effectively. However, the time-consuming and labor-intensive nature of traditional dietary assessment tools limits their ability to characterize food intake longitudinally [30, 31] and to capture the interindividual variability in patients’ food intake [11, 24, 32]. Therefore, we used a validated and time-saving tool, the Simple Diet Self-Assessment Tool (SDSAT), to generate dietary trajectories in patients undergoing chemotherapy. SDSAT has been well validated in patients with cancer [33, 34], has been embraced by nutrition and dietetics professionals in China, and is recommended in several Chinese nutritional oncology guidelines [35, 36].

Our study aimed to evaluate the feasibility of using SDSAT to establish longitudinal dietary intake trajectories across chemotherapy cycles and to explore the diversity of dietary patterns among patients undergoing chemotherapy. These objectives may ultimately enhance the efficiency of nutrition management and help alleviate insufficient or delayed nutritional support in this patient group.

Materials and methods

Study participants

Patients were recruited between July 2, 2021, and May 11, 2022, from the Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences in Beijing, China. Our study enrolled patients (1) aged ≥ 18 years with cytologically or histologically proven malignant tumors; (2) an estimated life expectancy of at least three months upon admission; (3) who understand and are willing to provide informed consent; (4) who are planning to receive or undergoing chemotherapy, (5) and who are able to complete electronic questionnaires and adhere to follow-up requirements. Exclusion criteria included: (1) significant cardiovascular, renal, or hepatic comorbidities; (2) pregnancy or lactation; (3) severe mental disorders with poor compliance; (4) abnormal glucose metabolism (glycated hemoglobin ≥ 7.0%); (5) allergic to the contents of oral nutritional supplements; (6) inability to feed by mouth due to dysphagia or recurrent laryngeal nerve injury; (7) deemed unsuitable for inclusion by investigators.

The study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the Cancer Hospital, Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences (21/297–2968), and that of other eight Chinese clinical centers and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Register, Chictr.org.cn (ChiCTR2100047535). All participants provided informed consent. The results were reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) Statement guidelines [37, 38].

Study procedures

After enrollment, participants were instructed to register and use an electronic Patient-Reported Outcome Tool Especially for Cancer Patients with Treatment (SHCD-PROTECT), which involves nutrition assessment tools, including SDSAT and 24-hour dietary recall (24HR) and an electronic patient-reported outcome (PRO) system. SDSAT was performed by trained dietitians once before the chemotherapy and at least three times during each chemotherapy cycle. 24HR was assessed by a different trained dietitians before and during the treatment. Participants were required to fill out the electronic PRO rehabilitation journal at least once a week to report their weight, handgrip strength, and fat-free mass, using a personal SHCD-PROTECT account during each chemotherapy cycle.

Clinical and pathologic characteristics

Data on age, sex, height, tumor site, tumor stage, comorbidities, laboratory measurements, and treatment regimens were collected and confirmed from the records maintained at the clinical center. Routine blood tests were performed according to standard clinical practice, typically before each chemotherapy cycle. Additional tests could be conducted during or at the end of a cycle if patients exhibited relevant symptoms. Relevant laboratory data were collected each time a patient’s blood test results were updated in their medical record. The Nutritional Risk Screening 2002 (NRS-2002) and Patient-Generated Subjective Global Assessment (PG-SGA) were assessed during each hospital admission. Weight, fat-free mass, and handgrip strength were measured during the in-hospital phase and were self-reported after hospital discharge.

Dietary assessment

Simple Diet Self-Assessment Tool (SDSAT)

Dietary intake was assessed by trained dietitians using the SDSAT once before the start of treatment and at least three times during each chemotherapy cycle. SDSAT is a cancer-specific dietary assessment tool to evaluate food intake during the past 24 hours with scores ranging from 0 (nil per os (NPO) diet) to 5 (regular diet), and each score corresponds to a range of energy and protein intake [39]. 24-hour dietary recall (24HR )

A trained dietitian (different from the one who conducted SDSAT) performed the 24HR. Food and beverages consumed during the previous 24 hours were recalled under a guided interview, and dietary data were entered into SHCD-PROTECT, which undertook dietary analysis software to quantify energy and protein intakes.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the trajectory of dietary patterns described using SDSAT. The secondary outcomes were daily and cumulative compliance with SDSAT completion, validity of the SDSAT compared with the 24HR, treatment completion rate, weight loss, and administration of medical nutrition therapy (MNT)). Cumulative compliance was defined as the proportion of chemotherapy cycles in which a specific number of valid SDSAT scores was obtained, relative to the total number of cycles. Daily compliance was defined as the proportion of chemotherapy cycles with a valid SDSAT score on a given day of the cycle (Day 0-Day 21), relative to the total number of cycles. One chemotherapy cycle was considered completed if any observed break was no longer than 3 days or breaks were longer than 3 days, but not associated with treatment intolerance. Treatment intolerance was defined as a white blood cell (WBC) count < 2 × 109/L, platelet (PLT) count < 75 × 109/L, hemoglobin (Hb) concentration < 80 g/L, or any other chemotherapy-induced adverse events that interrupted the treatment plan. The percentage of weight change was calculated by dividing the weight change during one chemotherapy cycle by the weight at the beginning of the cycle, expressed as a percentage. A decrease in weight of > 3% was defined as weight loss. The administration of MNT was determined by the frequency of use and the average energy and protein intake from oral nutritional supplements (ONS), enteral nutrition (EN), and PN during one cycle of chemotherapy.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were reported as the mean ± SD or median (interquartile range [IQR])). Categorical data were presented as frequencies and percentages. To assess the pattern of patients’ dietary intake, a group-based trajectory model (GBTM) was used to determine the trajectory of the SDSAT score from the 1 st to the 21 st day of each treatment cycle. Overall SDSAT scores at each timepoint were assumed to be normally distributed with a maximum of five, and the daily timepoint was considered as an independent variable. We compared models with one to seven trajectories and determined whether a trajectory shape of intercept, linear, quadratic, or cubic order provided the best fit. The final most fitted trajectories were retained based on a comprehensive assessment of the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) values, mean posterior probability of group membership, the smallest proportion of groups, and good clinical authenticity.

Following the selection of the best-fitting model, analysis of variance model (ANOVA), Mann-Whitney U-test, Chi-squared test, and Fisher’s exact test were used to test the statistical significance of differences between the different trajectory groups. The agreement between the SDSAT and 24HR was evaluated using the kappa statistic. Pearson correlation analysis was used to test the correlation of changes in dietary intake between groups. Treatment cycle-related characteristics (chemotherapy regimens, routine blood test results; NRS-2002; PG-SGA; handgrip strength; dietary and MNT total energy/protein intake) with P values < 0.01 were retained in the multivariable logistic regression models to identify impact factors for the dietary pattern trajectory. Longitudinal generalized estimating equations (GEE) models were employed to assess the associations between baseline characteristics and compliance. The analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and 2-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient selection and baseline patient characteristics

Among the 1397 patients assessed for eligibility, 56 were included in the analysis (Additional file 1). The patient characteristics are shown in Table 1. The average age was 59.79 ± 11.62 years and body mass index (BMI) was 23.03 ± 3.2 kg/m2. The patients were predominantly men (73.21%), with 58.93% with stage IV cancer, and 42.86% with cancer of the respiratory system. Of the patients, 67.86% scored < 3 points using the NRS-2002 and the PG-SGA scores were distributed as follows: 0–1 (8.93%), 2–3 (32.14%), 4–8 (30.36%), and ≥ 9 (28.57%). Most patients were treated with chemotherapy alone, received cisplatin/carboplatin-based for respiratory tumors, received first-line treatment, and cycle 1 was the most frequent cycle.

Dietary pattern trajectories

The BIC values dropped significantly from the one-class to seven-class models, with a significant turning point for the five-class trajectory model (Additional file 2 and Additional file 3). Results of the GBTM, presented in Fig. 1, showed that the five-class model tested with a different order term (Additional file 4). Patients in Group 1 (Cycles = 27; 12.3%; 1.78 ± 0.76) had the worst dietary intake with scores ranging between 1 and 2 points. The dietary trajectory of patients in Group 2 was ladle-shaped (Cycles = 16; 9.5%; 3.39 ± 0.53), and their score quickly descended to 1–2 points after the treatment, then slowly increased to 4–5 points until the end of the cycle. As the treatment progressed, the SDSAT score of patients in Group 3 (Cycles = 54; 24.9%; 3.72 ± 0.39) declined to 2–3 points from 4 points and then gradually returned to 4 points. Patients in Group 4 (Cycles = 80; 35.1%; 4.5 ± 0.25) and Group 5 (Cycles = 40; 18.2%; 4.96 ± 0.07) experienced relatively adequate dietary intake; and the trajectory of Group 5 was more stable than that of Group 4.

Compliance rate

We calculated the cumulative compliance (Table 2) and daily compliance during each treatment cycle (Additional file 5). The daily compliance ranged from 27.65% to 68.2% of total cycles. Among all cycles, 81.11% had ≥ 4, 52.53% had ≥ 7, and 23.50% had ≥ 14 cumulative response times. Group 2 had higher compliance than the other groups: 100% for ≥ 3, 75% for ≥ 7, and 43.75% for ≥ 14 cumulative response times. Group 5 had the poorest compliance.

Validity of SDSAT

As shown in Fig. 2 and Additional file 6, dietary energy and protein intakes were assessed using 24-hour dietary recalls. All groups were positively correlated in terms of their total energy (r = 0.637, P < 0.001) and protein intake (r = 0.486, P < 0.001), and dietary energy intake (r = 0.861, P < 0.001) and protein intake (r = 0.773, P < 0.001). The agreement between the SDSAT score and dietary energy and protein intake estimated from 24HR was perfect from one day before to 21 days after treatment initiation (κ: 0.888–1.0, P < 0.001).

Factors associated with the dietary pattern trajectory and compliance

Pearson correlation analysis revealed a correlation between NRS-2022 and PG-SGA (r = 0.50), and between dietary energy intake and protein intake (r = 0.87). We retained PG-SGA and dietary energy intake in the models and dropped NRS-2002 and protein intake to avoid collinearity. We combined the trajectories of Groups 4 and 5 as a reference group, since their longitudinal SDSAT scores were generally ≥ 4 points and represented relatively stable dietary intake, and compared this combined group with Groups 1–3. In both models, Groups 1–3 had significantly weaker handgrip strength (odds ratio [OR]: 1.10, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.01–1.19, P = 0.031 in Model 1) and dietary energy intake (OR: 1.02, 95% CI: 1.01–1.02, P < 0.001 in Model 1) compared with Groups 4 and 5 (Additional file 7). In terms of compliance (Additional file 8), the results showed that an Irinotecan-based chemotherapy regimen for gastrointestinal tumors was associated with higher compliance compared with a single-agent regimen (OR: 3.62, 95% CI: 1.2–10.97, P = 0.023), whereas clinical Stage I was associated with lower compliance compared with Stage IV (OR: 0.35, 95% CI: 0.17–0.75, P = 0.007).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to illustrate the longitudinal trajectories of dietary intake within chemotherapy cycles and capture the interindividual variability of dietary patterns of patients receiving chemotherapy. Our study found that using SDSAT to establish dietary trajectories was feasible, with sufficient validity and compliance. Five food intake trajectory groups were identified, consisting of the low-fluctuant trajectory (Group 1), slow-recovery trajectory (Group 2), fast-recovery trajectory (Group 3), high-fluctuant trajectory (Group 4), and high-stable trajectory (Group 5), which were highly concordant with the clinical observations.

Despite a wealth of literature on alterations in the oral intake of patients treated with chemotherapy, the majority of the studies share an inherent limitation in that they merely examined food intake at several timepoints before, during, and after chemotherapy but did not dynamically and continuously measure dietary intake throughout the whole cycle, and did not take into account the heterogeneity of food intake patterns, making the dietary profiles difficult to interpret [11, 24, 32]. This limitation may be attributed to the lack of simple and efficient dietary assessment tools. Therefore, a simple, evidence-based, and clinically meaningful food intake assessment approach is urgently needed both for research and practice to propel timely screening and intervention [40]. The SDSAT was invented specifically for patients with cancer in this context [39].

In our study, SDSAT showed excellent validity in terms of estimating energy and protein intake using 24HR as the reference method. The implementation of SDSAT was distributed evenly throughout each chemotherapy cycle, with approximately a 30–60% compliance rate for each day. According to the protocol, SDSAT should be conducted at least four times per cycle. In our study, 81.11% of the cycles met this requirement, and 52.53% exceeded the requirement, with seven valid SDSAT scores per cycle. Furthermore, during about 23.5% of cycles, dietitians performed SDSAT even for 14 days. This is more frequent than in previous studies, in which dietary assessment has generally been conducted for a maximum of 2–4 days per cycle [10, 11, 24]. Compliance with dietary evaluation was not only contingent on the dietitians who undertook SDSAT but also dependent on the patient’s cooperation. Therefore, our results demonstrated that more frequent assessments of SDSAT were acceptable for both patients and dietitians, making the establishment of dietary trajectories possible. Evidence from previous studies and applications has also shown that this tool is user-friendly and time-saving, and that medical staff and patients alike without any dietetic knowledge can use it properly after brief instruction, thereby advancing the multidisciplinary and patient-centered nutrition support [33, 41].

Most of the previous studies have concluded that during the chemotherapy cycle, patients have the worst food intake on the day of chemotherapy and that dietary intake subsequently gradually increases [10, 24]. These studies have not considered interindividual variability of dietary intake. According to our findings, there were five distinct dietary trajectories. Patients clustered in the low-fluctuant group experienced persistently and significantly inadequate dietary energy and protein intake during the chemotherapeutic cycle (21 days), with an average energy intake of 7.28 ± 2.89 kcal/kg/day (490.04 ± 195.28 kcal) and average protein intake of 0.33 ± 0.16 g/kg/day (22.09 ± 10.80 g), which was considerably lower than the requirement recommended in the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism (ESPEN) guideline for patients with cancer, which specifies an energy requirement of 25–30 kcal/kg/day and a protein requirement of 1.0–1.5 g/kg/day, evidencing the need for additional nutrition support. As expected, more than 90% of patients in the low-fluctuant group had a PG-SGA score ≥ 4 (indicating the need for nutritional intervention), and they had a higher energy intake from ONS than the other groups (p = 0.005). Using NRS-2002, however, merely 56% of patients were deemed at nutritional risk (NRS-2002 ≥ 3), suggesting that without further assessment, nearly half of the patients would not initiate nutrition therapy in a timely manner. Similarly, a multicenter study showed that only 15.6% of patients with cancer who suffered from impaired intake had positive screening results of NRS-2002 [27]. Another study conducted among older patients with cancer demonstrated that the NRS-2002 did not agree with the caloric deficits (k = − 0.1059) [42]. A potential explanation is that the NRS-2002 is generally recommended to be applied weekly [43], and may overlook day-to-day variation in dietary intake, rendering the tools less sensitive for detecting the risk of malnutrition in patients with cancer with changeable dietary intake [27, 44]. Hence, as a screening tool, the NRS-2002 alone may not be sufficient to identify the nutritional risk for this subgroup. Combining the NRS-2002 with a quick and validated dietary assessment tool, such as the SDSAT, may improve screening accuracy.

Patients in the slow-recovery group also had poor food intake on average (energy intake: 11.48 ± 3.81 kcal/kg/day; protein intake: 0.63 ± 0.30 g/kg/day), but they experienced a different dietary pattern to those in the low-fluctuant group. Overall, their dietary intake almost met the requirement before and on the first day of treatment, decreased rapidly on the second day of treatment, remained low (SDSAT score between 1 and 2) for approximately one week, and then gradually increased to return to normal by the end of the cycle (approximately 18 days after the chemotherapy). Membership in this group tended to have the worst handgrip strength and the lowest treatment completion rate. Intuitively, patients in the low-fluctuant group could be assumed to have the worst outcomes because they experienced consistent dietary deficits. However, also because of that, their additional need for nutritional intervention was easier to identify than those in the slow-recovery group whose intake was normal during the nutritional screening and assessment periods (before and at the beginning of the treatment), sustained the lowest intake even after discharge, and their intake remained insufficient for an extended period at home until the end of the cycle. Patients in the slow-recovery group had the lowest energy and protein intake from ONS, supporting the assumption that their need for nutritional therapy was unmet. This highlights the essentiality of nutritional reassessment and management throughout the treatment journey, particularly during the post-hospital phase in this subgroup.

Patients clustered in the fast-recovery and the high-fluctuant groups had a moderate level of dietary intake. Both groups experienced a slight decline in food intake and a quick rise to the original level, which was the most common dietary pattern among patients receiving chemotherapy (accounting for 60% of the cycles), consistent with findings of previous studies [10, 24]. In the high-stable group, patients’ dietary intake was not affected by the side effects of chemotherapy and remained stable at the target level. However, patients in this group had the lowest cumulative compliance rate, suggesting that patients with a stable dietary intake were less willing to cooperate with dietary surveys or that dietitians intentionally conducted fewer assessments for this group.

Although this study was conducted within a specific dietary culture, the underlying logic of the SDSAT is adaptable, and with appropriate integration of culturally relevant food items, it may be extended to other populations in future applications. Despite the strengths of this study, it has several limitations. First, it was a single-center study with a small sample size and patients were unevenly distributed in terms of cancer type and stage. Second, no risk factors or predictive factors were identified for each trajectory. Furthermore, owing to ethical considerations, all participants received routine nutrition support, including nutritional counseling and/or MNT, according to their nutritional assessment, which may have had an impact on the representativeness of the dietary trajectories. Similarly, the treatment completion rate and weight change of patients clustered in different trajectories did not reach statistical significance between groups, as the nutrition intervention was a major confounding factor. Moreover, missing data were common in patients with severe complications or who were approaching the end stage of life, and also in patients without distressing symptoms during treatment-free intermissions at home. Therefore, multicenter studies with a larger sample size, are warranted to validate these results.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study found interindividual variability in the dietary patterns of patients undergoing chemotherapy. We determined five distinct trajectories of food intake during treatment using SDSAT, with acceptable validity and compliance. Future studies should explore the predictive factors for each trajectory and examine the appropriate timing and approaches of nutrition management for each trajectory, thereby allowing proactive and high-quality nutritional intervention for patients receiving chemotherapy.
