Prospective bidirectional associations between social media use, body dissatisfaction, and disordered eating: predicting exacerbation and maintenance among young adults
Authors: Georgia M. King, Matilda O’Brien, Siân A. McLean
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundYoung adults are increasingly affected by the experiences of disordered eating, body dissatisfaction, and problematic use of appearance-focused social media. However, little evidence exists, particularly for men, regarding the direction of these relationships or if they reciprocally predict change over time The present study aimed to address this gap and examine if appearance-focused social media use predicts exacerbation (increases) or maintenance (stability) of body dissatisfaction and disordered eating over 6-weeks, and whether the reverse occurs.MethodsA sample of 488 men (N = 247) and women (N = 241) aged 18–30 (M = 26.50, SD = 2.96) were recruited through Prolific and completed an online survey at baseline and 6-weeks (T2).ResultsIn univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, reciprocal predictors of exacerbation were observed; higher social media use predicted exacerbation (versus asymptomatic-stability) of body dissatisfaction and exacerbation of disordered eating, and higher disordered eating predicted exacerbation of use of social media. Body dissatisfaction was a significant predictor of social media use exacerbation in univariate analyses only. Reciprocal relationships were not observed for prediction of maintenance (versus cessation). Only gender predicted social media use maintenance in univariate analysis (women had higher odds of maintenance than men) and higher body dissatisfaction predicted maintenance of disordered eating in univariate and multivariate analyses.ConclusionFindings of small bidirectional prospective associations between appearance-focused social media use, body dissatisfaction, and disordered eating enhance understanding of the direction of relationships. This underscores the importance of addressing bidirectionality in prevention efforts and theories. Outcomes also suggest different factors may maintain, rather than predict these issues, warranting further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40337-026-01560-7.

Introduction

Social media is a popular past time, with young adults (18–30-year-olds) frequent users of visual platforms such as Instagram, Snapchat, and TikTok [1]. Social media offers benefits like social support, self-expression, belonging, and exploring interests [2–4]. Nevertheless, its use is also related to psychosocial harms [5, 6]. In particular, engagement with appearance-focused platforms which facilitate observing, commenting, sharing, and liking content on appearance and bodies [7–9] may be particularly problematic for body dissatisfaction and disordered eating [10]. However, few studies have prospectively explored the impact of social media use in mixed-gender young adult samples and empirical examination of disordered eating is limited. Consequently, the direction of influence between social media and both body dissatisfaction and disordered eating remains unknown. This study seeks to fill these gaps by prospectively exploring the direction of the relationship between appearance-focused social media activity and body dissatisfaction and disordered eating in young adults.

Young adults commonly experience body dissatisfaction and disordered eating. Approximately 59% of women and 39% of men experience body dissatisfaction [11], and 20% of undergraduate students (men and women) experience disordered eating [12]. This is concerning, given body dissatisfaction and disordered eating can contribute to detrimental health outcomes, including depression, self-harm, and eating disorders [13, 14]. Young adults are prolific social media users [15] and social media use has consistently been found to be related to body dissatisfaction and risk for disordered eating [e.g., 10]. Consequently, exploring prospective relationships among this cohort is vital for informing prevention and treatment targets.

Several theories support plausible bidirectional pathways from social media to body dissatisfaction and disordered eating. In accordance with the tripartite influence model [16], exposure to social media may transmit appearance pressures, leading to body dissatisfaction and disordered eating through appearance-ideal internalisation and comparisons. Experimental research supports this pathway to body dissatisfaction. Exposure to appearance-ideal social media images results in increased body dissatisfaction among men and women [17, 18]. There is, however, a dearth of experimental research examining disordered eating outcomes. In the limited available research, one study observed that disordered eating urges were reinforced after posting a self-photo (selfie) to Instagram but decreased significantly among participants who did not post their photo [19]. An experimental ecological momentary assessment (EMA) study, whereby participants complete multiple repeated assessments of state-level, present-moment indicators of disordered eating in real time, found that viewing fitspiration images (relative to control images) did not impact young women’s state disordered eating urges [20].

Cross-sectional modelling supports associations between social media use and both body dissatisfaction and disordered eating among males and females [7, 21, 22], but prospective modelling is limited. Facebook use for the purpose of negative self-evaluations and comparisons was associated with young women’s greater self-reported body dissatisfaction, bulimic symptoms, and over-eating episodes four weeks later [23]. In contrast, using EMA, naturalistic exposure to thinspiration or fitspiration images did not predict changes in body dissatisfaction or disordered eating among young women over seven days [24]. It is unclear whether effects hold prospectively for men, emphasising the need for further research.

Consistent with uses and gratifications theory [25], it is also possible that body dissatisfaction and disordered eating predict greater social media activity. Applied to this context, and in line with the central tenets of the theory, individuals may actively select and consume appearance-focused social media to attempt to fulfil body dissatisfaction and disordered eating-related psychological needs, interests, and motives [25]. These may include comparing one’s appearance, seeking appearance-related feedback, and sourcing weight-control methods [26]. Several explicit gratifications have been explored and the gratifications of self-presentation and body image control were found to mediate cross-sectional associations between body shame and problematic social media use [27, 28]. Among people with eating disorders, lower levels of motivation to change eating disorder behaviours or cognitions were cross-sectionally associated with social media use for the purposes of impression management [29], suggesting that the presence of eating disorder symptoms is related to seeking specific gratifications through social media. Prospective unidirectional pathways from body dissatisfaction to social media use have also been explored. Higher appearance consciousness (awareness of attractiveness to an online audience) but not body dissatisfaction predicted use of visually focused social media via self-validation over 2-months among adolescents and emerging adults [30].

Several studies have explored bidirectional prospective pathways between body dissatisfaction and social media, with most in adolescent samples. Although reciprocal associations between disordered eating and social media use are also plausible, little research has explored these. Among adults, Marques et al. [31] found small bidirectional relationships between general social media use and body dissatisfaction over five waves of annual data collection. Focusing specifically on appearance-related engagement with social media, Linardon [32] found bidirectional relationships between appearance comparisons with fitspiration images and both body dissatisfaction and dietary restraint over 8-months among adult women. However, relationships were not consistently observed between the social media-related variable and each of body dissatisfaction and dietary restraint across each of the three data points.

Mixed findings have been observed among adolescents. Two studies found bidirectional associations; social media use and body dissatisfaction were bidirectionally related through appearance comparison over 12-months [33] and selfie-related activities and self-objectification were bidirectionally related over 6-months [34]. In the latter study [34], selfie-editing predicted body dissatisfaction but the reverse direction was not supported. This is consistent with other research among adolescents whereby social media activity predicted increased body dissatisfaction over 18-months among girls and boys [35] and over 2-years among girls but not boys [36]. In these studies, the reverse directions were not observed. In contrast, one study found that desire for a smaller body size predicted Instagram use 5-months later among girls but the reverse association was not supported [37]. These limited and inconsistent findings suggest the need for further research to clarify the direction of effects, the relationship over time in young adults, and determine prediction by and outcomes for disordered eating.

In addition to exploring directionality, prospective research can examine factors that may predict the exacerbation and maintenance of body dissatisfaction, disordered eating, and (problematic levels of) appearance-focused social media use. Isolating predictors of exacerbation may aid in uncovering targets for prevention and isolating predictors of maintenance may be advantageous for specifying treatment targets and developing new interventions. Currently, no research on social media, body dissatisfaction, and disordered eating has separately explored exacerbation and maintenance. This paradigm will be adopted here.

The present study aimed to establish the direction of prospective relationships between appearance-focused social media use, body dissatisfaction and disordered eating in a mixed-gender sample of young adults (18–30-years-old). Guided by previous research [38, 39], further aims were to examine reciprocal prediction of exacerbation and of maintenance of these concerns. To investigate these aims, a prospective examination over two time-points, baseline and 6-weeks (T2), was conducted.

Method

Participants

A convenience sample of (self-identifying) men and women who met inclusion criteria (aged 18–30 years, UK resident, English language proficiency, social media user) were recruited through the Prolific platform. Participants were invited to take part if they had completed at least 50 Prolific studies as we were seeking participants who were more engaged in the platform and more likely to contribute data at both timepoints. Prolific participants have shown excellent attention, comprehension, and honesty, providing high-quality data [40].

Anticipating approximately 15% attrition based on prior prospective research with Prolific recruitment [41], 619 participants were recruited and completed the baseline survey. After screening for failed attention checks, unrealistic completion times, and incomplete responses, data from eight participants were removed. Baseline responses from four participants were erroneously coded as ineligible for retention, and these four participants were not invited to complete T2. Thus, 607 participants were invited to complete the second survey; 520 participants did so, a completion rate of 85.67%.

At T2, data from 17 participants were removed upon screening for failed attention checks, unrealistic completion times, and incomplete responses. Data from 16 participants identifying as genders other than men or women were removed as there was an insufficient sample of gender-diverse participants for inclusion in analyses for which gender was used as a covariate. The final analyses included 488 participants. As shown in Table 1, gender was relatively evenly balanced, participants were on average aged in their mid-20s, and most were white and regular social media users.

Participant characteristics

Measures

To confirm eligibility, further questions assessed current country of residence and social media use. Demographic questions sourced participants’ age, gender, ethnicity, and height and weight from which body mass index (BMI) was calculated.

Appearance social media activity

The Appearance-Related Activity subscale of the Social Media Appearance Preoccupation Scale [9] measured self-reported appearance-focused social media use. Participants responded to 5-items on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree), such as “When on social media, I post, comment on, share or like content about getting or staying fit and/or muscular”. Scores were averaged to form a scale score (range 1–7). Higher scores indicated greater appearance-focused social media use. Developed and validated in mixed-gender university samples, scores have demonstrated good internal consistency reliability and evidence for validity [9]. Internal consistency reliability in the present study was acceptable at baseline (ω = 0.77) and good at T2 (ω = 0.82).

Body dissatisfaction

The Body Dissatisfaction subscale of the Eating Pathology Symptoms Inventory [42] measured body dissatisfaction. Participants responded to 7-items on a 5-point Likert-type scale (0 = never to 4 = very often) regarding how frequently each statement (e.g., “I did not like the size of my thighs”) applied to them during the past four weeks. Scores were summed (range 0–28), with higher scores indicating greater body dissatisfaction. Developed and validated in mixed-gender young adult samples, scores have shown good internal consistency and test-retest reliability and excellent validity [42]. Internal consistency reliability in the present study was excellent at baseline (ω = 0.91) and T2 (ω = 0.91).

Disordered eating

Disordered eating was measured with the 6-item InsideOut Institute-Screener [43]. Participants responded to the first item “How is your relationship with food” on a 5-point scale (1 = worry and stress free to 5 = full of worry and stress) and responded to remaining items on a 5-point Likert-type scale (1 = never to 5 = always). Scores were summed (range 6–30). Higher scores indicated greater risk of disordered eating. Scale scores have demonstrated excellent internal consistency, test–retest reliability, and validity among a mixed gender adult sample [43]. Internal consistency reliability in the present study was good at baseline (ω = 0.89) and T2 (ω = 0.89).

Procedure

The study was approved by La Trobe University’s Human Research Ethics Committee (HEC23124). Participants were invited to complete self-report questionnaires through an online survey hosted on QuestionPro and provided written (electronic) informed consent prior to beginning the study. Questionnaire order was counterbalanced to address sequence bias. To ensure attentiveness, two attention checks were included. Following Prolific’s policy, one question was an instructional set question (e.g., The colour test you are about to take part in is very simple, when asked what colour to select, you must enter purple. This is an attention check.) and one question was a nonsensical question (e.g., Please indicate your agreement with this statement: this is a nonsensical item. I pay a leprechaun to do my taxes each year.). Different questions were used at baseline and at T2.

Participants who completed the baseline survey and who had valid responses were prompted via Prolific to complete the T2 survey 6-weeks post baseline. Two timepoints were selected to enable exploration of the direction of temporal relationships, a key aim of the study. The six-week lag between timepoints was expected to be an adequate duration to observe change in outcome variables but also sufficiently short to minimise attrition. Participants provided their Prolific identification for data matching and were reimbursed ₤1.00 for baseline and ₤1.30 for T2 survey completion.

Data analysis

Using participants’ baseline and T2 scores, four participant groups were created to represent exacerbation, asymptomatic-stable, maintenance and cessation for each of the appearance-focused social media use, body dissatisfaction, and disordered eating variables. Participants’ baseline and T2 scores on these variables were separately classified as either being below or exceeding moderate levels using predefined cut-off scores determined from past literature. Change or stability in these levels from baseline to T2 determined participant grouping. Scores ≥ 2.5 for the appearance-related activity subscale indicated moderate (potentially problematic) appearance-focused social media use [9]. Using score distributions for the body dissatisfaction subscale [42, 44, 45], scores > 13.0 for men and > 18.0 for women indicated moderate levels of body dissatisfaction. Different thresholds were used for men and women to reflect gender norms for this subscale [e.g., 42]. Using established cut-off scores for eating disorder risk, scores of ≥ 13.50 on the InsideOut Screener [43] indicated moderate levels of disordered eating.

Participants who were asymptomatic at baseline (scores below identified cut-offs) were categorised into the asymptomatic-stable group if their scores remained below cut-offs at T2, or into the exacerbation group if their scores exceeded cut-offs at T2. Participants who were symptomatic at baseline (scores exceeded cut-off) were categorised into the maintenance group if their scores also exceeded cut-offs at T2, or into the cessation group if their scores no longer exceeded cut-offs at T2.

Data were analysed using JAMOVI software. Alpha was set at 0.05 for statistical significance and Cohen’s d (Cohen, 1988) and odds ratios were interpreted for effect size where OR of 1.68, 3.47, and 6.71 indicate small, moderate, and large effects, respectively [46].

Data were checked to ensure assumptions for analyses were met. Outliers were detected (n ≤ 5 across analyses) but had no influence on analyses and were retained as genuine values. Attrition analyses using chi-square and independent samples t-tests were conducted to determine if there were differences between participants who did and did not complete T2 on gender, age, baseline social media use, body dissatisfaction, or disordered eating. Paired-samples t-tests were conducted to confirm if expected differences in baseline and T2 social media, body dissatisfaction, and disordered eating scores were apparent within exacerbation, asymptomatic-stable, maintenance, and cessation groups. Analyses generally supported expectations such that T2 mean scores were significantly higher than baseline means for the exacerbation groups and significantly lower for cessation groups, with large effect sizes (d ranged from 1.45 to 2.06). There were no significant differences between baseline and T2 scores in asymptomatic-stable and maintenance groups, except for the asymptomatic-stable appearance-focused social media group where the mean T2 score was significantly lower than at baseline, with small effect size (d = 0.31; see Supporting Information 1).

To examine predictors of exacerbation and maintenance of concerns, first, univariate logistic regressions examined the association of each baseline predictor with T2 exacerbation (coded 1; versus asymptomatic-stable coded 0) and T2 maintenance (coded 1; versus cessation coded 0) for each of body dissatisfaction, disordered eating, and social media. Univariate models were also conducted with age and gender (men coded 0; women coded 1) as potential predictors in light of past research showing associations with outcome variables [31, 44]. Statistically significant predictors from univariate models were then included in multivariate logistic regression models to determine unique predictors of exacerbation and maintenance.

Results

Attrition analysis

Attrition analyses among participants who were screened as eligible for inclusion in data analyses compared baseline variables between those who provided baseline data only (n = 87) and those who provided both baseline and T2 data (n = 488). No differences were observed, as shown in Supporting Information 2.

Predicting exacerbation and maintenance of moderate body dissatisfaction

Univariate logistic regression analyses revealed that gender and baseline appearance-focused social media were significant predictors of exacerbation of body dissatisfaction. As shown in Table 4, being female and having higher use of appearance-focused social media at baseline were associated with higher odds of body dissatisfaction exacerbation (versus asymptomatic stability) at T2. Effect sizes were small. None of the independent variables significantly predicted maintenance (versus cessation) of body dissatisfaction at T2.

Discussion

The present study first aimed to establish the direction of prospective relationships between appearance-focused social media use, body dissatisfaction, and disordered eating in a mixed-gender sample of young adults. We also aimed to identify predictors of exacerbation and maintenance of moderate levels of these problems over 6-weeks. Overall, prospective bidirectional relationships were observed. In univariate analyses, higher baseline appearance-focused social media use was associated with exacerbation of both body dissatisfaction and disordered eating but not maintenance of either. In the reverse direction, higher baseline body dissatisfaction and disordered eating were associated with exacerbation of appearance-focused social media use. Only gender was associated with maintenance of social media use. Gender was also associated with exacerbation of body dissatisfaction where greater odds of exacerbation were associated with being female. Higher baseline body dissatisfaction as well as an increase in body dissatisfaction over 6-weeks were associated with exacerbation and maintenance of disordered eating. Multivariate analyses also supported bidirectional relationships over time for exacerbation but not maintenance of these problems. Greater use of appearance-focused social media at baseline was a unique predictor of exacerbation of body dissatisfaction and disordered eating, whereas only disordered eating emerged as a unique predictor of appearance-focused social media use exacerbation. Additionally, baseline and change in body dissatisfaction both uniquely predicted exacerbation and maintenance of disordered eating.

Findings of bidirectional relationships between appearance-focused social media use and body dissatisfaction support previous observations of reciprocal relationships between body dissatisfaction and general social media use among adults [31] and appearance-focused social media use among adolescents [33]. Consistent with these past research findings, effect sizes in the present study were small. Notably, our findings extend past research and to our knowledge are the first to find bidirectional prospective relationships between frequency of appearance-focused social media use and disordered eating. Indeed, few studies have explored prospective relationships among these variables [see 10]. The present finding that higher social media use at baseline predicted exacerbation (versus asymptomatic stability) of moderate disordered eating over 6-weeks supports previous observations of associations between Facebook use and greater disordered eating symptoms over a 4-week period [23]. Prediction of exacerbation of moderate levels of appearance-focused social media use by higher baseline disordered eating was a novel outcome of the present research and we are unaware of other studies that have examined this direction. In multivariate models, only disordered eating predicted social media use exacerbation, despite body dissatisfaction also being a significant univariate predictor. It is possible that shared variance between body dissatisfaction and disordered eating accounts for this.

Interestingly, baseline levels, not change in putative predictors tended to be associated with prediction of exacerbation of any outcome variables in univariate or multivariate analyses. This suggests that reaching a certain threshold of social media use, body dissatisfaction, and disordered eating predetermines a trajectory for exacerbation of the relevant outcome, and that once this threshold is reached, further increases over a short period do not contribute additional exacerbation. Also notable was the finding that maintenance of concerns (versus cessation) was not predicted in univariate or multivariate analyses by the expected variables. It is possible that the factors that maintain these problems may differ from those that contribute to their exacerbation, as seen in prior research on fasting and eating disorder pathology [38, 39]. Alternatively, given that social media algorithms determine the degree of exposure to content based on cues that may be outside of conscious control, such as time spent on content rather than active responses to the content [47], increases in appearance-focused social media use may not be perceived or reported by participants. Finally, the maintenance versus cessation models may have been underpowered, with a lower proportion of participants falling into these categories than exacerbation and asymptomatic-stable groups. However, inspection of effect sizes would suggest that, for the most part, associations between predictors and maintenance of concerns were simply not present. Future research utilising a third time point, or multiple time points such as through EMA may elucidate patterns of maintenance of concerns and the influence of trajectory of change in predictors over time.

The observation of bidirectional relationships suggests that a possible reinforcement loop, or cycle, operates between appearance-focused social media use and body dissatisfaction/disordered eating. In this manner, the presence of body dissatisfaction and disordered eating may predispose attention to and engagement with appearance- and eating-focused social media content, which in turn reinforces body dissatisfaction/disordered eating, potentially through mechanisms such as social comparison [10, 33]. Consequently, study findings appear to support sociocultural models of the development of body dissatisfaction and disordered eating [16], as social media derived appearance pressures influence development of these problems. Findings also support uses and gratifications theory [25] whereby body dissatisfaction and disordered eating drive engagement with particular social media content as a means of gratifying needs such as through appearance checking or monitoring of eating [29, 48, 49].

Although not primarily interested in age and gender, these were explored as potential predictors. Few relationships were observed. Only gender was found in univariate analyses to be associated with exacerbation of body dissatisfaction and maintenance of moderate levels of appearance-focused social media use with women more likely to experience exacerbation and maintenance than men. These findings are typical of gender patterns in prevalence of body dissatisfaction [e.g., 11] and align with findings that adolescent girls engage more in appearance-focused social media [36], which may persist into young adulthood.

The 6-week time-lag appeared to be sufficient for exploration of change, as a considerable proportion of participants shifted from symptomatic to non-symptomatic (and vice-versa). However, the magnitude of change was small. Therefore, whether the observed changes reflect fluctuation or lasting change remains unknown. In meta-analyses, length of time between data collection points did not moderate effects of social media use on body dissatisfaction [10], suggesting this timeframe was suitable for present purposes.

Study results need to be considered in light of several limitations. First, prospective research can suggest likely causal pathways but cannot confirm causality. Examination of only two-time points limited the ability to understand and distinguish change over time. The cut-off scores did not provide dichotomous categories, (i.e., absence versus presence of concerns), and the cut-off scores for social media use and body dissatisfaction were not based on empirically tested thresholds. Missing data prevented the inclusion of BMI as a covariate and skewed distribution prevented the inclusion ethnicity as a covariate, which future studies should consider. Similarly, the predominance of participants with white ethnicity limits the generalisability of findings. Also, due to a small proportion of participants identifying as genders other than women or men, not all genders were represented in analyses. Future research requires a larger and more diverse sample, including non-binary and transgender individuals, to understand their experiences. Finally, using self-report measures was also a limitation, thus incorporating objective measures like smartphone usage statistics [8] could enhance sensitivity in future research.

The present study was the first to observe prospective predictors of the exacerbation, but not maintenance, of body dissatisfaction, disordered eating, and appearance-focused social media use among mixed-gender young adults. Bidirectional associations indicate possible reciprocal effects. These findings provide new insights and suggest that targeting problematic appearance-focused social media use as well as body dissatisfaction and disordered eating would be potential prevention foci for each other. Media literacy programs exploring complexities of social media and incorporating motivations for appearance-focused social media use, including those related to needs that arise from body dissatisfaction and disordered eating could be useful. Overall, this study advanced understanding of the social media and body dissatisfaction/disordered eating relationship and findings highlight the importance of exploring bidirectionality for theory refinement and prevention efforts.
