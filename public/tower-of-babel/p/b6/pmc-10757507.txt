A Novel Window Into Human Vascular Remodeling and Diagnosing Carotid Flow Impairment: The Petro‐Occipital Venous Plexus
Authors: Wenjie Yang, Kevin Sam, Ye Qiao, Zhongqing Huang, David A. Steinman, Bruce A. Wasserman
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdaptive arterial remodeling caused by flow reduction from downstream stenosis has been demonstrated in animal studies. The authors sought to determine whether inward remodeling from downstream stenosis also occurs in humans and is detectable by ex vacuo expansion of the Rektorzik venous plexus (RVP) surrounding the petrous internal carotid artery.Methods and ResultsThe authors analyzed 214 intracranial magnetic resonance imaging examinations that included contrast‐enhanced vessel wall imaging. RVP symmetry was qualitatively assessed on vessel wall imaging. RVP thickness (RVPT) was measured on the thicker side if asymmetric or randomly assigned side if symmetric. Maximum stenosis (M1 or intracranial internal carotid artery) was measured. Posterior communicating artery and A1 diameters (>1.0 mm and 1.5 mm, respectively) defined adequate collateral outflow when proximal to the stenosis. Seventy‐two patients had stenosis downstream from RVPT measurements. For those without adequate outflow (38 of 72), 95.0% with RVPT ≥1.0 mm had ≥50% stenosis compared with only 5.6% with RVPT <1.0 mm. For these 72 patients, higher RVPT (RVPT ≥1.0 mm versus <1.0 mm) and absent adequate outflow were associated with greater downstream stenosis (P<0.001) using multivariate regression. For patients with downstream stenosis without adequate outflow, asymmetric RVP thickening was associated with greater ipsilateral stenosis (P<0.001, all had ≥46% stenosis) when stenosis was unilateral and greater differences in stenosis between sides (P=0.005) when stenosis was bilateral.ConclusionsInward internal carotid artery remodeling measured by RVPT or RVP asymmetry occurs as downstream stenosis approaches 50%, unless flow is preserved through a sufficiently sized posterior communicating artery or A1, and may serve as a functional measure of substantial flow reduction from downstream stenosis.

Animal studies have demonstrated structural changes in the arterial wall resulting in a caliber reduction following long‐term flow impairment by downstream stenosis. 1 , 2 , 3 Langille and O'Donnell demonstrated a 21% diameter reduction of the common carotid artery in response to a 70% reduction in flow in a rabbit model. 4 This adaptive remodeling is driven by reduced production of the endogenous vasodilator nitric oxide by endothelial cells in response to lower shear stress exposure. 5 , 6 The effect of this complex adaptive response to reduced flow is to attempt to maintain a constant wall shear stress (WSS) by a caliber reduction of the vessel. Local remodeling in response to atherosclerotic plaque formation in humans is well characterized, 7 but structural changes from downstream stenosis in animal models occur in the absence of wall thickening and affect the entire vessel through which flow is reduced.

Although the mechanisms have been well characterized in animal models, this adaptive inward remodeling due to downstream stenosis has not been demonstrated in humans. Rather, a caliber reduction of the internal carotid artery (ICA) has only been demonstrated in humans when stenosis of the extracranial carotid bulb exceeds 60%, explained by the authors as the effect of the pressure drop across the stenosis. 8 Whether the ICA is capable of these structural changes in response to downstream stenosis (ie, there is no pressure drop in the upstream ICA), whether a threshold of flow impairment needs to be exceeded for this response to be detected, and whether the inward remodeling is graded depending on degree of flow impairment remain unknown.

To detect a caliber reduction of the ICA, we focused on its petrous segment and hypothesized that we would observe ex vacuo expansion of the petro‐occipital venous plexus surrounding this segment, also known as the Rektorzik venous plexus (RVP), as this segment decreases in caliber. This effect is similar to the expansion of the venous compartment accompanying reductions in cerebral spinal fluid volume described by the Monro‐Kellie doctrine. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 We believed the degree of ex vacuo RVP expansion might relate to the degree of downstream stenosis that underlies these changes.

High‐resolution magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) vessel wall imaging (VWI) is an established technique for identifying neurovascular pathologies such as atherosclerosis, dissection, and vasculitis, and gadolinium contrast administration is often used. 13 VWI examinations acquired to assess intracranial ICA stenosis generally include the petrous ICA, enabling characterization of the RVP. We retrospectively reviewed our clinical database of intracranial VWI examinations to investigate the presence and degree of ex vacuo RVP expansion for varying degrees of downstream stenosis. This remodeling should occur regardless of whether the flow impairment is caused by atherosclerosis or other vasculopathies. Our goal was to determine whether inward ICA remodeling does occur in response to downstream stenosis, whether ex vacuo RVP thickening can be used as a marker of this stenosis, and whether a threshold of stenosis must be exceeded before remodeling can be detected, potentially identifying patients who require attention for significant ICA flow reduction.

Methods

The deidentified data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Patient Inclusion

We reviewed 284 consecutive intracranial VWI examinations acquired in patients referred to our center between September 2010 and December 2019 to evaluate intracranial or extracranial stenosis detected on conventional angiography or for suspicion of a neurovascular cause of a recent stroke. If multiple examinations were acquired on a single patient, the first examination was assigned for review. Patients were included in our study if imaging included intracranial time‐of‐flight magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) and contrast‐enhanced MRA (CE‐MRA) that covered the circle of Willis and intracranial ICAs and a contrast‐enhanced VWI scan that covered the petrous ICA segments. Patients with the following were excluded: (1) poor VWI quality; (2) age younger than 18 years; (3) occluded petrous segment of the ICA (eg, from thrombosis or dissection) because raters could not reliably assess RVP thickness (RVPT); (4) absence or hypoplasia of the carotid canal; and (5) extracranial carotid stenosis. Clinical characteristics including age, sex, race, and vascular risk factors (hypertension, diabetes, lipid profile, and smoking) were collected at the time of the MRI examination. Children were not studied because RVPT was categorized based on absolute measurements that assume that the skull is fully developed. An exemption was provided for this study by the institutional review board of Johns Hopkins University School of Medicine to allow inclusion of deidentified patient data with consent waived. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines. 14

MRI Acquisition

MRI examinations were performed on either of two 3T scanners (Achieva [Philips] or Magnetom Skyra [Siemens]) by trained technologists. A standardized intracranial VWI protocol was performed including 3‐dimensional time‐of‐flight MRA, 3‐dimensional high isotropic‐resolution VWI, and CE‐MRA sequences as previously described. 15 , 16 Protocol details can be found in Data S1.

Imaging Analysis

Two readers (W.Y. and K.S., both trained and certified for intracranial VWI interpretation 17 and with 4 years of experience in VWI interpretation and measurements) independently analyzed the petrous ICA segments for the degree of RVPT. Readers were blinded to the appearance of the ICA and its branches proximal and distal to the petrous segment. For each patient, only 1 ICA was selected for analysis based on visual assessment of RVPT symmetry between the right and left sides. When thicknesses were asymmetric, the thicker side was analyzed, and when thicknesses were considered symmetric, the side selected for analysis was randomly chosen.

RVP expansion was identified by homogeneous enhancement diffusely lining the wall of the petrous canal. In contrast, atherosclerotic plaques are characteristically heterogeneous and demonstrate irregular or focal wall thickening. 18 A short‐axis view was reconstructed through the middle of the horizontal petrous ICA segment using our PACS software (Carestream, Onex Corporation) for assessing RVPT (Figure S1). The middle was targeted because the RVP is typically asymmetrically thicker at the proximal and distal portions of the horizontal segment where the ICA turns into and out of the canal, creating gaps between the ICA and the bony wall that are filled in by the RVP, and luminal flow artifacts are uncommon here compared with the proximal and distal turns of the ICA. 19 Because RVPT can be uneven along the circumference of the horizontal petrous ICA, we measured the thinnest width based on the assumption that ex vacuo expansion in response to inward remodeling of the ICA should affect the entire circumference, and we wanted to eliminate thickening caused by asymmetric gaps between the ICA and the bony wall. The thinnest segment often occurs along the superior wall, which helped to exclude artifacts from the slow, recirculating flow more commonly encountered along the inferior wall (Figure S2).

In order to confirm vessel wall‐lumen boundaries and exclude flow artifacts on the VWI for RVPT measurements, reconstructions of the VWI through the short axis of the petrous ICA were coregistered with matching reconstructions of the CE‐MRA. Lumen diameters were drawn through the ICA on the arterial phase CE‐MRA reconstructions and copied onto the ICA depicted on the corresponding VWI reconstructions to identify the true luminal margin and exclude flow artifacts before RVPT measurements (Figures S2 and S3). The venous phase CE‐MRA was used to further delineate the RVP between the arterial lumen and surrounding bony wall of the petrous canal (Figure S3). We show computed tomography angiography through the petrous ICA in a patient suspected to have RVP thickening (Figure S4) to further support that the enhanced signal identified on the VWI is not a luminal flow artifact.

Although the ICA wall can be differentiated from the enhancing lumen and surrounding RVP on the venous phase of the CE‐MRA based on the wall's hypoenhancement relative to the adjacent vascular structures, this delineation is obscured on the flow‐suppressed VWI, especially when the RVP enhancement becomes more prominent relative to the thin width of a healthy ICA wall (Figure S3). For this reason, the vessel wall was included in RVPT measurements. RVPT was classified into 4 categories: (1) category 0, no to minimal RVP enhancement; (2) category 1, visually detectable RVP enhancement where the thinnest margin is <1.0 mm; (3) category 2, RVP enhancement where the thinnest margin is ≥1.0 mm to <1.5 mm; and (4) category 3, RVP enhancement where the thinnest margin is ≥1.5 mm. A reference chart was used during the analysis (Figure 1).

The maximum downstream stenosis (eg, distal ICA or M1 segment of the middle cerebral artery) was measured by 2 readers (W.Y. and K.S.) on the maximum intensity projection images from the time‐of‐flight MRA using the Warfarin‐Aspirin Symptomatic Intracranial Disease (WASID) method 20 after reviewing the examination interpretation reported by the senior neuroradiologist (B.A.W., with over 20 years of experience in VWI interpretation). For ICA stenoses distal to the posterior communicating artery (PCOM), a PCOM of sufficient size could allow ICA flow to be diverted and preserve the ICA caliber upstream from the stenosis (Figure 2). Similarly, in the setting of M1 segment stenosis, flow could be diverted through the A1 segment of the anterior cerebral artery or PCOM if of sufficient size and preserve the ICA caliber (Figure 2 and Figure S5). For these reasons, A1 and PCOM segment diameters were measured on the maximum intensity projection images from the MRA by 2 readers (W.Y. and K.S.) on the side identified with the thicker RVP when proximal to the stenosis (ie, the segment could divert flow past the downstream stenosis). If stenosis was detected in the A1 or PCOM segment, the diameter was measured at the most stenotic site. An A1 was considered adequate if its diameter measured >1.5 mm, and a PCOM was considered adequate if its diameter measured >1.0 mm. 21 , 22 A patient was considered to have adequate collateral outflow if either of the following conditions was encountered: (1) ICA stenosis distal to a PCOM >1.0 mm in diameter, or (2) M1 stenosis with an ipsilateral A1 >1.5 mm in diameter or PCOM >1.0 mm in diameter (Figure 2).

A random sample of 155 examinations (76 with downstream stenosis and 79 without) was used to qualitatively assess the asymmetry of RVP thickening between the left and right ICAs based on postcontrast 3‐dimensional VWI, again with readers blinded to vessels above and below the petrous segments. Each case was categorized as either asymmetric RVPT with the thicker side recorded, or symmetric RVPT. CE‐MRA images were available for review when needed to confirm flow artifacts.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using STATA 14.0 (StataCorp LLC). Associations of RVPT with clinical characteristics were evaluated using univariate linear regression. For patients with downstream stenosis without adequate collateral outflow, the association between stenosis degree and RVPT category was assessed using a Spearman rank correlation test. Associations of RVPT category and absence of adequate collateral outflow with degree of downstream stenosis were assessed using multivariate linear regression adjusted for the stenosed vessel segment (distal ICA versus M1). The degree of downstream stenosis in patients with unilateral stenosis or difference in bilateral downstream stenosis was compared between patients with symmetric and asymmetric RVPT using an independent t test.

Results

Study Patients

Of the 284 patient examinations reviewed, 214 (mean age, 55.2±15.2 years; 92 men) were included in this study (see Figure 3 for reasons for exclusions). The most common diagnosis was intracranial atherosclerosis (77 patients). Other diagnoses are shown in Table S1.

RVPT Category and Intracranial Stenosis

Of the 214 patients, 76 (35.5%) showed RVPT category 0, 116 (54.2%) category 1, 15 (7.0%) category 2, and 7 (3.3%) category 3. RVPT was not associated with any cardiovascular risk factors in a univariate model. A total of 142 of 214 (66.4%) patients had no intracranial stenosis downstream from RVPT measurements (Figure 3). For the remaining 72 (33.6%) patients, intracranial stenosis or occlusion was identified in 34 M1 segments and 38 distal ICA segments (4 cavernous, 20 ophthalmic, and 14 communicating ICA segments) downstream from RVPT measurements. Interreader variability estimates for RVPT categorization revealed the greatest discrepancies for categories 0 and 1 (Data S1).

Of the 72 patients with intracranial stenosis, 38 (52.8%) did not have adequate collateral outflow (7 M1 stenoses and 31 distal ICA stenoses) (Figure 4). For these 38 patients, 17 of 18 (94.4%) with RVPT category 0 or 1 (RVPT <1.0 mm) had <50% downstream stenosis, whereas 19 of 20 (95.0%) with category 2 or 3 (RVPT ≥1.0 mm) had ≥50% downstream stenosis (Figure 5, Table S2). RVPT category was highly correlated with the severity of downstream stenosis (γ=0.809, P<0.001). In contrast, when adequate collateral outflow was identified with intracranial stenosis (34 patients, including 27 M1 stenoses and 7 distal ICA stenoses), 94.1% (32 of 34) had an RVPT <1.0 mm despite 82.4% (28 of 34) having ≥50% intracranial stenosis (Figures S6 and S7). For those with intracranial stenosis ≥50%, patients with adequate collateral outflow had more frequent RVPT <1.0 mm than those without adequate collateral outflow (92.9% versus 10.0%, P<0.001). For the 72 patients with intracranial stenosis, a higher degree of RVPT (RVPT ≥1.0 mm versus <1.0 mm) and the absence of adequate collateral outflow were associated with greater downstream stenosis in a multivariate linear regression model (Β=48.66 [95% CI, 35.07–62.24], P<0.001] and Β=34.58 [95% CI, 19.23–49.94], P<0.001], respectively) after adjusting for the stenosed vessel segment (distal ICA versus M1).

Of the 142 patients without intracranial stenosis downstream from the side of the RVP measurements, all had RVPT measurements grouped into either category 0 (44.4%) or category 1 (55.6%). There were 4 of 142 patients who had contralateral intracranial stenosis (3 with ≥50% stenoses and 1 with <50% stenosis) but with adequate collateral outflow and, hence, did not have asymmetrically thicker RVP measurements.

Asymmetry of RVP Thickening and Intracranial Stenosis

Of the 18 patients with unilateral intracranial stenosis without adequate collateral outflow downstream from the RVPT measurements (Figure 3), 9 had symmetric RVPT (between right and left ICAs), with all 9 having <43% stenosis, whereas all 9 of the other patients with asymmetric RVPT had ≥46% stenosis downstream from the thicker RVP and a higher mean stenosis than that for the 9 patients with symmetric RVPT (77.3±22.6 versus 20.9±13.2, P<0.001) (Figure 6A). Of the 13 patients with bilateral stenosis without adequate collateral outflow downstream from the RVPT measurements, 6 had asymmetric RVPT and greater differences between intracranial stenosis measurements compared with patients with symmetric RVPT (37.9±29.9 versus 3.3±4.8, P=0.005) (Figure 6B). The thicker RVP was always ipsilateral to the more stenotic side, and the intracranial stenosis measurement downstream from the thicker RVP was always >50%. The asymmetric group included 1 patient with no difference in stenosis measurements between sides, but this patient had bilateral distal ICA occlusions. Of the 73 patients without intracranial stenosis who were randomly selected to assess RVP asymmetry, 72 had symmetric RVPT.

Discussion

Impact

Our data support the hypothesis that inward remodeling of the conduit ICA occurs in response to downstream stenosis, and this can be detected by ex vacuo expansion of the RVP. In fact, we showed that a minimum RVPT measurement >1.0 mm is strongly associated with downstream stenosis >50%. Furthermore, the presence of a vessel segment that allows the flow to bypass the stenosis, such as an adequate A1 or PCOM for M1 stenosis or an adequate PCOM for ICA stenosis distal to its origin, can maintain enough flow in the ICA to avoid these structural changes. We also showed that visual (qualitative) identification of asymmetric thickening of the RVP can serve as a sign of ipsilateral downstream stenosis of close to ≥50% without necessitating quantitative RVPT measurements, but this falls short in the setting of bilateral intracranial stenosis when differences of stenosis measurements between sides are small. RVP thickening or asymmetry may serve as an important biomarker of hemodynamically significant intracranial stenosis, signaling the need for further evaluation and possible intervention.

Hemodynamic Changes and Threshold for Remodeling

Animal studies have demonstrated that a chronic decrease in blood flow caused by downstream stenosis could lead to a reduction in arterial diameter due to structural remodeling of the vessel wall mediated by endothelial cells in response to low WSS. 1 , 23 , 24 We have extended the results of these studies by showing that arterial caliber reduction, as measured by RVP thickening, occurs in humans in response to downstream stenosis, and its degree relates to increasing severity of stenosis. The venous system is known to be highly variable in size with variations in pressure, 25 and intracranial venous compartment expansion is a well‐known response to a reduction in the surrounding (ie, cerebral spinal fluid) volume within the rigid confines of the skull. 11 , 12 This is analogous to expansion of the RVP, confined by the bony wall of the petrous canal and surrounding the ICA, as the ICA volume decreases.

We observed that inward remodeling is detected when downstream diameter stenosis exceeds a threshold close to 50%. It is noteworthy that a nearly identical threshold was observed using a qualitative assessment of asymmetry to identify RVP thickening, which is presumably a more sensitive assessment of ICA caliber reduction than quantifying RVPT at the thinnest margin on a short‐axis reconstruction image. The 50% stenosis threshold for detecting a caliber reduction signifies a substantial drop in flow that occurs when stenosis reaches this degree. For example, as stenosis develops, the increase in velocity at the throat of the stenosis results in a pressure gradient related to the square of the velocity according to Bernoulli law. The pressure drop (ie, the additional flow resistance) can be considered negligible (relative to the distal microvascular resistance) until the diameter stenosis reaches ≈50%, at which point a steep drop in pressure occurs. 26 Compensatory mechanisms, in particular the reduction in downstream resistance by vasodilatation of the cerebral microcirculation, are exceeded as stenosis increases from this threshold and blood flow begins to seek lower resistance channels such as the contralateral ICA or external carotid artery, 27 resulting in a substantial reduction in flow through the conduit ICA upstream or downstream from the stenosis. 28 Hence, RVP thickening offers a direct measure of the functional consequence of the stenosis.

Even a small increment in stenosis can produce a precipitous drop in flow >50% diameter stenosis. 28 , 29 This reduction in flow lowers WSS, which drives inward remodeling as endothelial cells try to renormalize WSS by downregulating production of nitric oxide. Under the assumption of fully developed blood flow, WSS can be expressed as 32 μQ/D3, where μ is blood viscosity, Q is volumetric blood flow, and D is the diameter of the lumen. In order to maintain WSS, this equation shows that only a small reduction in diameter is needed to compensate for a large drop in flow. To better appreciate this, in our cohort, the mean petrous canal diameter measured from available computed tomography examinations through the middle of the horizontal petrous ICA segment ipsilateral to RVPT measurements was 6.0 mm. For patients with RVPT category 0 or 1, the minimum RVPT averaged 0.7 mm. Conservatively assuming concentric RVPT, this corresponds to a normal petrous ICA lumen diameter of 4.6 mm, which is broadly consistent with angiographic measurements. 30 RVPT values of 1.0 and 1.5 mm (ie, thresholds for categories 2 and 3) would thus correspond to diameters of 4.0 and 3.0 mm, or flow reductions of 35% and 74% to maintain WSS, which are within the range of flow reductions that can be caused by moderate to severe stenoses. 29 Put another way, the detection of RVP thickening, either by RVPT measurement >1.0 mm or by visually identifying asymmetry, is a plausible marker of substantial blood flow reduction. In fact, even without characterizing preexisting collateral anatomy or the autoregulatory function of the cerebral microcirculation, detecting blood flow reduction by RVP thickening suggests exhaustion of these compensatory mechanisms. This makes it a potentially useful nonhemodynamic indicator of a level of flow reduction that might pose potential risk for cerebrovascular ischemic injury.

A caliber reduction of the cervical ICA from a high‐grade stenosis of the extracranial carotid bulb has been described in humans and attributed to the pressure drop across the stenosis. 8 , 31 Although pressure was not measured in our study, there is strong evidence from hemodynamic models that the pressure drop across a stenosis primarily drives the flow reduction that evokes endothelial‐driven adaptive remodeling, whereas changes in luminal pressure itself have less of an impact on vessel caliber than changes in flow. 4 , 29 , 32 This can be seen by noting that the caliber changes occur whether the vessel caliber being measured is downstream to the stenosis where the pressure is reduced, or upstream and proximal to the pressure drop across a stenosis as in our cases. Of note, the threshold of stenosis in the carotid bulb where a caliber reduction was detected in the cervical ICA was reported to be 60%, 8 close to the threshold we identified. The authors used the contralateral cervical carotid caliber as a reference, which can be problematic for higher degrees of stenosis as flow begins to divert to the contralateral carotid (ie, the contralateral reference diameter is not independent of the ipsilateral stenosis). In contrast, the bony wall of the petrous canal is a fixed boundary defining the outer margin of the RVP and serves as a reference for measuring RVP thickening as it expands from inward ICA remodeling, thus avoiding such a potentially confounding factor.

Collateral Outflow Channels

Adequate collateral outflow in our model depends on the resistance of the residual lumen of the stenotic ICA or M1 relative to the resistance of vessel segments capable of diverting flow away from the stenosis (ie, PCOM or A1). Although our definition of adequate collateral outflow was simplistic by using diameter as a proxy for resistance for the purpose of this study, this definition is founded on the inverse relation of resistance and the fourth power of the vessel diameter according to Poiseuille law, and it is simple to implement in clinical practice. Diameter thresholds >1.0 mm for the PCOM and 1.5 mm for the A1 segment are based on reports that define hypoplasia. 21 , 22 Although these measures are small, resistance increases precipitously with diameter (by its fourth power, assuming laminar flow) such that, for example, a PCOM with a 0.8 mm diameter would have a resistance roughly 5 times greater than a PCOM with a 1.2 mm diameter. We also performed a sensitivity analysis by modifying our original PCOM and A1 diameter thresholds for adequacy by 20% in either direction (Data S1). Only 4 cases changed to having adequate collateral outflow when the diameters were reduced by 20%, and 3 cases changed to having no adequate collateral outflow when the diameters were increased by 20%, resulting in little change in associations of RVPT categories with degree of downstream stenosis.

Considering flow reduction caused by a stenosis measuring <50% may not be sufficient to elicit structural remodeling, we repeated our analysis but without excluding cases with adequate collateral outflow when intracranial stenosis was <50% and found no change in associations between higher categories of RVP thickening and stenosis (Data S1), suggesting that the presence of adequate collateral outflow is not important for lower grades of intracranial stenosis.

Imaging Biomarker

RVP thickening observed on contrast‐enhanced skull base imaging or cross‐sectional angiography can signal the need for further evaluation considering that a substantial drop in blood flow would be needed to cause these structural changes. Even when angiography is available, this feature is a functional measure unlike stenosis as it signifies a nonnegligible reduction in flow that exceeds the ability of the intracranial circulation to compensate. It may also prove to be useful to gauge changes in ICA flow between examinations even when flow impairment is not caused by ICA stenosis (ie, monitoring intracranial vasospasm) or validate significant stenosis when image quality limits the confidence of its detection. Although flow through the distal ICA can be more directly measured by imaging techniques such as phase contrast MRI, RVP thickening offers a functional measure of substantial flow reduction that has its own internal reference (ie, bony wall of the petrous canal) and can be assessed on neurovascular imaging and readily applied in clinical practice.

Compared with RVP measurements, asymmetry may be easier and more practical to ascertain with routine contrast‐enhanced imaging. Symmetric RVPT excluded downstream stenosis >50% with high sensitivity (96.6%), but specificity (87.5%) was relatively low because of bilateral stenoses with small differences between sides (Data S1). In contrast, asymmetric RVP thickening detected downstream stenosis >50% with high specificity (96.6%). We studied RVP asymmetry in only 13 patients with bilateral stenosis after excluding all cases with adequate collateral outflow, and this was not a large enough sample to identify a threshold in the difference in stenosis between sides at which point RVP asymmetry can be detected.

RVP thickening may be misinterpreted on contrast‐enhanced VWI as a vasculopathy such as atherosclerotic plaque, vasculitis, or moyamoya disease based on the thickened circumferential enhancement (ie, the RVP) surrounding the ICA (Figures 1C and 1D). 18 Abnormal wall thickening and enhancement associated with downstream stenotic segments were always confined to the stenosis without continuity with the petrous ICA segment, indicating that the petrous ICA caliber reduction is merely a consequence of a stenosis elsewhere rather than involvement by the vasculopathy (Figure 4).

Study Limitations

Study limitations include: (1) Distinguishing between categories 0 and 1 was challenging with 29% discrepant reads between our readers; however, these categories were both strongly associated with <50% intracranial stenosis so distinguishing between these categories is not needed to detect high‐grade stenosis with inward remodeling. (2) The vessel wall was included in RVPT measurements by design. However, RVPT was not associated with any cardiovascular risk factor, suggesting that individual variations in vessel wall thicknesses were not influencing our results. (3) We defined adequate collateral outflow based on the integrity of A1 and PCOM segments, but there may be other low‐resistance paths blood flow can follow to bypass intracranial stenosis.

Conclusions

We present evidence that inward remodeling of the ICA, as detected by RVPT >1.0 mm or RVP asymmetry on contrast‐enhanced VWI, occurs in humans in response to downstream diameter stenosis approaching 50% without adequate collateral outflow (ie, through a PCOM or A1). Hence, RVP thickening may be a novel biomarker signaling a significant reduction in ICA blood flow, and the observation that this occurs in response to downstream stenosis supports that this structural change is primarily the effect of a reduction in flow rather than pressure. Unlike stenosis, this is a functional measure of flow reduction that suggests that the ability of intracranial collateral channels and microcirculatory vasodilatation to compensate has been exceeded. This also offers an explanation for the thick wall of enhancement commonly misinterpreted as a vasculopathy on VWI. The flow reduction needed for inward remodeling of the ICA to be observed is thought to be severe enough to be considered clinically significant, offering new strategies for patient risk assessment. Further studies are needed to validate RVP thickening or asymmetry identified on cross‐sectional neurovascular imaging (eg, computed tomography angiography) as a clinically relevant marker of blood flow reduction.
