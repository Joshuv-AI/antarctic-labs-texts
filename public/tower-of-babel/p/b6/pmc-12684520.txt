Characteristics and Outcomes of Mavacamten Use in 2440 Patients With Obstructive Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy
Authors: Ingy Mahana, Angelica T Mejia, Miriam R Elman, Daniel Kamna, Olives Nguyen, Hailey Volk, Hongya Chen, Ahmad Masri
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOur objective was to assess real‐world outcomes and health care use associated with commercial mavacamten use in patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.MethodsWe used the Symphony Dataverse to study patients receiving mavacamten between May 25, 2022 and June 30, 2024. Safety outcomes were prespecified.ResultsWe included 2440 patients and 18 494 mavacamten dispenses (63% women, median age 66 years [interquartile range {IQR}, 55–73]). Patients were followed for a median of 3037.0 days (IQR, 2808–3221) before mavacamten initiation and for 240 days (IQR, 117–407) on mavacamten. Atrial fibrillation or flutter (AF/AFL) at baseline were present in 536 (22%) of patients, whereas new‐onset AF/AFL requiring therapy occurred in 125 patients (5%) and new heart failure (HF) in 100 patients (4%). During follow‐up, 154 patients (6%) received new dispenses of loop diuretics, 102 (4%) oral anticoagulants, 78 (3%) amiodarone, 17 (0.7%) sotalol, and 6 (0.2%) dofetilide. There was a total of 306 patients (13%) with 428 acute care episodes (most common claims were HF (n=80, 26%), AF (n=74, 24%), and AFL (n=16, 5%)). Predictors of outcomes (new HF, new AF/AFL, ventricular tachycardia, or transient ischemic attack/stroke claim) were age at mavacamten start, obesity, transient ischemic attack/stroke history, and residence in the Western versus Southern United States.ConclusionsOver an 8‐month median follow‐up on mavacamten in patients with obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, new AF/AFL and HF occurred in 5% and 4%, respectively. Further work is needed to better understand the expected incidence of AF/AFL, HF, and other long‐term outcomes of mavacamten treatment in obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.Keywords: mavacamten, obstructive hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, outcomesSubject Categories: Cardiomyopathy

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM) is common, and obstructive HCM (oHCM) constitutes two‐thirds of patients. 1 The underlying mechanisms of dynamic left ventricular outflow tract obstruction are multifactorial, with hypercontractility being 1 of the major mechanisms leading to progressive septal hypertrophy, septal contact, and systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve. 1 , 2 This obstruction causes an increase in left ventricular pressure and impairs diastolic function, which eventually decreases cardiac output and causes heart failure. Patients’ symptoms are typically exertional and vary from chest pain, shortness of breath, palpitations from underlying arrhythmias, and syncope. Until recently, conventional medical treatment for oHCM consisted of β‐blockers, nondihydropyridine calcium channel blockers, and disopyramide. The first cardiac myosin inhibitor, mavacamten, was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2022 and the European Medicines Agency in 2023. Mavacamten is currently indicated as a second‐line therapy for treating patients with symptomatic oHCM to improve symptoms and functional capacity. 3 , 4

Mavacamten underwent multiple evaluations in clinical trials. In EXPLORER‐HCM, mavacamten improved peak oxygen consumption and New York Heart Association class as compared with placebo. 5 However, mavacamten use >30 weeks caused an excessive reduction of left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) to <50% in 5.7% of patients. A second phase III trial, VALOR‐HCM, enrolled patients who were referred to septal reduction therapy, and showed that over 112 to 128 weeks, 15 (13.8%) patients had developed LVEF <50%, including 2 with LVEF <30%, and 1 death occurred in a close proximity to the low LVEF event. The FDA approved mavacamten based on the EXPLORER‐HCM trial results, and due to the decision to not use a mavacamten dose in monitoring patients, a rigorous and restrictive risk mitigation program (REMS) was instituted. 6 Since the start of commercial use of mavacamten, there have been multiple small single‐ or multicenter studies assessing the efficacy and safety of mavacamten. 7 , 8 , 9 However, these studies enrolled small numbers and occurred in experienced centers. In addition, atrial fibrillation and flutter (AF/AFL) are emerging as frequently encountered issues in patients with oHCM on mavacamten. 10 , 11 , 12 In this study, we leveraged a national all‐payers medical and pharmacy claims database to characterize the outcomes of mavacamten use in a real‐world cohort.

METHODS

Data are available through the Symphony Health Integrated Dataverse and cannot be shared directly by the authors or made publicly available.

This population‐based, observational study used claims data from the Symphony Heath Integrated Dataverse, which is a nationally representative, provider‐based claims database containing longitudinal patient medical data for claims submitted to all payer types (commercial, cash, Medicare/Medicaid). We included patients with at least 1 claim for mavacamten between May 25, 2022 and June 30, 2024. In this database, patients aged >80 years are still coded as 80 years. The total period of evaluation of diagnosis and pharmacy claims was from January 1, 2015 through June 30, 2024. The lookback period before mavacamten use allowed us to establish baseline characteristics, comorbidities, and HCM‐specific complications.

The study’s start date was the first claim for mavacamten in the database. Baseline clinical characteristics were assessed at any point before the index (start) date except medication history, obesity, tobacco use, and hyperlipidemia, which were identified in the 90 days before starting mavacamten. Diagnoses, procedures/surgeries, and health care use was defined using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD‐10); Current Procedural Terminology (CPT), and ICD‐10 Procedural Coding System (ICD‐10‐PCS) codes (Table S1). Information on mavacamten, including fill date, days’ supply, and strength, were obtained from fills as were dispenses for other medications/medication classes (Table S2). Follow‐up was defined as the period between the index date and end of clinical activity, based on patients’ last claim in the Symphony database. Episodes of mavacamten use were characterized as consecutive fills with a gap of ≤35 days, using days’ supply to calculate the end date for mavacamten coverage on each claim. Treatment discontinuation was designated as a period of ≥90 days from the end of patients’ first mavacamten episode to the end of clinical activity in the database.

We assessed the characteristics of mavacamten fills as well as patient diagnoses, procedures, and medications in the follow‐up period. For the subset of our cohort with acute care encounters, we manually reviewed diagnosis, procedural, surgical, and medication claims within 14 days of the event to determine potentially related conditions and classify them. In addition to descriptive outcomes, we defined 2 composite outcomes: (1) cardiovascular diagnoses related to mavacamten use including new heart failure, new AF, new AFL, ventricular tachycardia, or transient ischemic attack/stroke diagnosis (cardiovascular diagnoses) and (2) cardiovascular diagnoses related to mavacamten in (listed in 1) as well as new use of loop diuretics, which is used as a surrogate for symptoms of volume overload in patients with HCM. Time from mavacamten initiation to the first of the composite events or end of clinical activity was computed for each composite outcome. The study ethics approval was waived due to the study using a deidentified claims data set. Patient informed consents were not obtained and waived in this observational study.

Statistical Analysis

Patient demographics and clinical characteristics were summarized using frequency with percentage for categorical variables and median with interquartile range (IQR) for continuous variables. Cumulative incidence of first discontinuation of mavacamten and composite events were visualized with Kaplan‐Meier plots. Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify characteristics associated with each composite outcome. The following candidate variables were selected a priori based on clinical knowledge: patient age at mavacamten initiation; sex; US region (West, Midwest, South, and Northeast); weeks present in Symphony database before mavacamten initiation; baseline comorbidities including obesity, hypertension, diabetes, stroke, coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, chronic kidney disease, and sleep apnea; and β‐blockers and calcium channel blockers use before mavacamten. Hazard ratios from univariable and multivariable models are reported with 95% CIs. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) was used for data management, and R 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) for analyses.

RESULTS

From May 2022 to June 2024, 2440 patients received mavacamten and were included in the study cohort. The median age was 66.0 (IQR, 56.0–73.0) years, and 1530 (62.7%) were women. Patients were enrolled and followed for a median of 3037.0 (IQR, 2808.0–3220.5) days before being prescribed mavacamten. Notable baseline comorbidities included hypertension in 1843 patients (75.5%), diabetes in 644 patients (26.4%), coronary artery disease in 969 patients (39.7%), and obesity in 242 patients (9.9%). History of claims reflecting AF/AFL were common and seen in 715 patients (29.3%) (Table 1). The majority of patients were on background medical therapy including 1326 (54.3%) on β‐blockers, 689 (28.2%) on calcium channel blockers, and 129 (5.3%) on disopyramide.

Follow‐Up and Outcomes

Patients were followed for a median of 240.0 (IQR, 117.0–407.0) days following the start of mavacamten. The median dose of mavacamten was 5.0 (IQR, 5.0–10.0) mg, with a median number of fills of 6.0 (IQR, 2.0–12.0). During the follow‐up period, 1075 (44.1%) patients had dose modification, with the majority being down‐titrations as detailed in Table S3. A total of 18 494 fills were captured during the study period. During the follow‐up period, chest pain and angina were common and occurred in 191 (7.8%) patients. New stress‐induced cardiomyopathy was rare, with claims in only 2 patients (0.1%). In addition, new AF/AFL was common and occurred in 125 patients (5.1%), whereas new heart failure occurred in 99 patients (4.1%). Other diagnoses reported during follow‐up are presented in Table 2 as are new medications of interest. There were 154 patients (6.0%) who received new dispenses of loop diuretics, whereas 102 (4.2%) received oral anticoagulants, 78 (3.2%) received amiodarone, 17 (0.7%) received sotalol, and 6 (0.2%) received dofetilide. Over the follow‐up period, there was a total of 306 patients (12.5%) with 428 acute care claims. Heart failure was the most common reason, occurring in 80 patients (26.1% of claims, 3.3% of all patients) after a median of 108.5 days (IQR, 35.0–225.0) from mavacamten initiation. Syncope necessitating acute care occurred in 28 patients (9.2% of claims, 1.2% of all patients). Arrhythmias were another common cause of acute care encounters. There were 74 patients (24.2% of claims, 3.0% of all patients) who had AF at a median of 95.5 days (53.0–191.0), 16 (5.2% of claims, 0.7% of all patients) with AFL leading to acute care, and 23 (7.5% of claims, 0.9% of all patients) with stroke. Acute care encounters also included encounters for atrial arrhythmia management with cardioversion or ablation, and a few patients transitioned to myectomy. Discontinuations

Out of 2440 patients, 412 (16.9%) had a discontinuation after their first mavacamten episode without an additional mavacamten fill in ≥90 days. An additional 85 (3.5%) individuals had a ≥90‐day interruption after an initial episode but restarted mavacamten for at least 1 additional claim. For those who had an acute care encounter, 24 (7.8% of 306) discontinued mavacamten, and 22 (91.7%) of them did not appear to have restarted mavacamten.

Predictors of Adverse Outcomes on Mavacamten

In the model with composite cardiovascular diagnoses, time to the composite event was associated with age at first mavacamten fill (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.01 [95% CI, 1.00–1.02]; P=0.0201) and the comorbidities obesity (aHR, 1.54 [95% CI, 1.11–2.12]; P=0.0093), stroke/transient ischemic attack (aHR, 1.96 [95% CI, 1.45–2.65]; P<0.0001), and the Western region of the United States compared with the South (aHR, 1.38 [95% CI, 1.01–1.87]; P=0.0412) (Figure [A] and Table 4). The inclusion of loop diuretics in the second model resulted in a slight change in the predictors of the composite outcome to include β‐blocker use (aHR, 1.24 [95% CI, 1.01–1.50]; P=0.0350) along with age (aHR, 1.01 [95% CI, 1.01–1.02]; P=0.0015), obesity (aHR, 1.36 [95% CI, 1.02–1.82]; P=0.0370), and stroke/transient ischemic attack (aHR, 1.73 [95% CI, 1.32–2.27]; P<0.0001) (Figure [B] and Table 5). In a model focused only on AF/AFL development, none of the baseline characteristics were associated with development of AF/AFL on mavacamten in the multivariable model (Table S4).

DISCUSSION

Mavacamten is the first‐in‐class cardiac myosin inhibitor. It has significantly influenced the treatment of patients with oHCM. Safety and efficacy of mavacamten were derived from rigorously conducted clinical trials, which usually included core laboratory confirmation of echocardiographic diagnosis of oHCM. 5 However, because commercial use is not subjected to the same rigorous patient selection and monitoring, there is a need to understand the safety of mavacamten in a large, unselected patient cohort. In this current study, patients with oHCM treated with mavacamten had frequent acute care encounters, with the majority related to heart failure and atrial arrhythmias. Patients had significant use of additional pharmacotherapies including oral anticoagulation, antiarrhythmic drugs, and diuretics. In addition, a significant number of patients discontinued mavacamten during follow‐up even when a stringent 90‐day interruption period was used.

Mavacamten was approved by the FDA in 2022 and by the European Medicines Agency in 2023. Due to its potential to cause systolic dysfunction and heart failure, it was required to have an REMS program in the United States or metabolizer status genotyping and monitoring in Europe. 4 The REMS monitoring program is onerous and uses echocardiography as a surrogate to mavacamten drug level, because the drug level was used in the pivotal phase III EXPLORER‐HCM trial, but was not required by the FDA for commercial use. In addition, drug–drug interactions due to mavacamten metabolism pathways require dispensing pharmacies to review patient’s medications and supplements at each dispense. In Europe, a distinct monitoring program is recommended, which also includes Cytochrome P450 genotyping.

The instituted REMS monitoring program is distinct from how the clinical trials were run, and as such, commercial use of mavacamten requires further scrutiny. Despite almost 3 years since the approval of mavacamten in the United States, there is a paucity of large‐scale evaluations of patient outcomes. The single‐center studies are informative, but they suffer from small sample size and selection and reporting biases. 7 , 8 , 9 , 13 In 2023, a real‐world short‐term retrospective cohort study of 659 patients followed for 118 days showed high adherence to mavacamten. 14

Recent data published from the FDA REMS program showed a 6.1% discontinuation rate of mavacamten after the first fill, and 4.6% of patients had LVEF <50%, 1.3% had heart failure hospitalization, and 0.3% had both LVEF <50% and heart failure hospitalization. However, these data do not account for patients who discontinued mavacamten permanently due an adverse event. As such, there are inherent survival and reporting biases to such data, because there is no obligation to report on patients who simply discontinued mavacamten without plans to resume and therefore the need to fulfill the REMS program requirement. 11 A better‐suited database for this question is one that allows for understanding of the events that occur to patients who permanently discontinued or temporarily held mavacamten.

Mavacamten has already been adopted by the American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines as a second‐line therapy for symptomatic oHCM, adjacent to disopyramide and septal reduction therapy, whereas the European Society of Cardiology guidelines recommend it as a second‐line therapy before proceeding with septal reduction therapy. 3 , 4 The EXPLORER‐HCM and VALOR‐HCM trials have shown the ability of mavacamten to improve exercise capacity, symptoms, and avert the need for septal reduction therapy. 8 , 15 However, only the VALOR‐HCM trial based dose titration solely on echocardiographic monitoring, which resembles real‐world practice. In VALOR‐HCM, over 112 to 128 weeks of mavacamten treatment, 15 (13.8%) patients developed LVEF <50%. 3 , 12

In the current study, our goal was not to redefine the incidence of LVEF <50% on mavacamten. We sought to study health care use in acute care settings as well as new diagnoses of interest and discontinuation rates. Another goal is to use pharmacy claims to study disease progression and adverse events. In HCM, symptoms suggestive of heart failure directly overlap with symptoms related to HCM; therefore, other tools are required to study disease evolution in patients. An important surrogate of patients’ well‐being in HCM is the new use of loop diuretics, which signals the transition to a more complex disease state. Although loop diuretics use should not equate heart failure or progressive end‐stage HCM, it is a reasonable surrogate of how the disease is evolving. In our study, loop diuretics were newly introduced in 154 patients (6.0%) after mavacamten had been initiated.

Aside from heart failure, AF and AFL, and their respective treatments, are emerging as significant contributors to morbidity while on mavacamten treatment and beyond. Developing new‐onset AF/AFL mandates lifelong anticoagulation in patients with HCM irrespective of other risk factors, and as such, the morbidity associated with this diagnosis carries over a lifetime. AF/AFL are commonly encountered in HCM, with a prevalence as high as 20% to 30%, and a typical incidence of 2% per year. 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 It remains unclear if there is an association between mavacamten, or cardiac myosin inhibition, and AF/AFL. However, there are some clues that the incidence of new‐onset AF/AFL might be higher than expected, especially with how effective mavacamten is in lowering left ventricular outflow tract gradients and improving left atrial volume. 19 In a study of human atrial tissue, mavacamten inhibited the α‐myosin heavy chain in the atria at a similar rate to the β‐myosin heavy chain found in the ventricle, with resultant reduction in atrial twitch amplitude and possibly depressing atrial contractility. 20 Whether these findings translate into humans with HCM remains to be seen.

Data from clinical trials provide further insights. AF and AFL occurred in 7.7% and 7.1% of patients on mavacamten compared with 6.5% and 0% on placebo in the EXPLORER‐HCM and VALOR‐HCM trials, respectively. 5 , 21 The long‐term extension study MAVA‐LTE showed 33 (14.3%) events of AF/AFL, with 18 (7.8%) being new‐onset over 166 weeks of follow‐up, and the long‐term extension study of VALOR‐HCM showed new‐onset AF/AFL in 11 patients (10.2%) over 128 weeks. 10 , 12 In the current study, new AF/AFL were common and occurred in 125 patients (5.1%), leading to a 7.6% annualized rate of AF/AFL in the first year. The majority of new‐onset AF/AFL episodes required a rhythm control strategy with an initiation of oral anticoagulation. Combined with prior findings from mavacamten trials, these findings represent an urgent need for an in‐depth study of this phenomenon. Further data are needed to understand if AF/AFL episodes are related to duration of mavacamten exposure, metabolizer status, hemodynamic change, effect on atrial myosin, or if these are simply representations of a common arrhythmia that occurs in patients with oHCM.

Finally, discontinuations of mavacamten were substantial. We chose to focus on ≥90 days to have a more stringent and specific criteria. Using this cutoff, 412 (16.9%) patients had discontinued mavacamten after their first episode without an additional fill, whereas an additional 85 (3.5%) individuals had >90‐days interruption after an initial episode but restarted mavacamten for at least 1 additional fill. These longer‐term discontinuations likely signal permanent discontinuation of mavacamten. Although temporary hold of mavacamten typically lasts 4 to 6 weeks for LVEF <50%, we felt that it is difficult to discern these short‐term temporary holds.

Our study has multiple unavoidable limitations. First, using claims data provided access to a large representative sample across the country at the expense of granular phenotypic, laboratory, and echocardiographic data. The goal of this analysis was to quantify patterns of mavacamten use, complications, and acute care use. Despite almost 3 years of commercial availability of mavacamten, no large‐scale real‐world studies have been conducted. Second, although the sample size is large enough for a disease like HCM on a specialty drug such as mavacamten, this database does not capture all patients in the United States who received mavacamten. Third, we are unable to assess mortality in this database. The Symphony database was specifically used to access details of pharmacy claims that provided us with significant insights into complications and morbidity while on mavacamten using fill claims of new medications. In addition, we manually reviewed all acute care claims codes and timelines, classifying them into categories (Table 3). Finally, we did not include a control group due to the inherent heterogeneity of patients who are selected to receive mavacamten versus other traditional therapies for oHCM. The current study is not intended to define outcomes of mavacamten in comparison with other therapies for oHCM or to the natural history of treated and untreated oHCM. It is possible that patients on mavacamten are experiencing fewer adverse events compared with the natural history of untreated oHCM. However, more granular data will be required to conduct such a comparative analysis. Prior studies using claims data have highlighted the significant burden of oHCM, including high rates of AF, heart failure, and hospitalizations for these conditions. 22 , 23 , 24

CONCLUSIONS

Over a median of 8 months, mavacamten use in patients with oHCM was associated with new AF/AFL and heart failure that occurred in 5% and 4% of patients, respectively. Patients also required frequent treatment with loop diuretics, rhythm control, and anticoagulation. Further work is needed to understand the longer‐term outcomes of mavacamten use in oHCM, and whether these findings represent the natural history of oHCM or if they represent particular complications of mavacamten use.
