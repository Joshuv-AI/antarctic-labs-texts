Operational assessment of tattooless breast radiotherapy using AlignRT surface guidance
Authors: Aria Malhotra, Andrew KH Robertson, Ian Sun, Samantha AM Lloyd
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Radiotherapy tattoos pose a negative mental burden on cancer survivors.•Replacing tattoos with surface guidance for breast radiotherapy will not impact clinical operations.•Tattooless breast radiotherapy is feasible with current surface guidance technology.Keywords: AlignRT, Surface guidance, Patient positioning, Radiotherapy, Tattoos

Introduction

Reproducible patient positioning is an important part of radiotherapy (RT) treatments. A standard procedure for ensuring accurate positioning involves tattooing multiple permanent markings across the patient’s body, which are aligned to lasers in the treatment room to position the patient at isocenter.

Many cancer survivors treated with radiation are left with a lasting mental burden from these permanent marks. In a survey across 142 women who had undergone breast radiotherapy, almost 80 % would choose an alternative treatment without tattoos even if it required extra efforts [1]. Opinions on the tattoos are deeply personal and while some women are indifferent and some embrace them as part of their journey, 70 % of cancer survivors have a negative stance on the permanent markings [1]. This can be attributed to the lasting reminder and lack of choice in their cancer care, the pain of receiving the tattoos, or contradiction with personal and religious beliefs [2,3]. While increasing breast cancer survival rates place greater importance on cosmesis during treatment, the continued use of permanent tattoo markings negatively impacts body image and limits clothing choices [3].

Surface guided radiotherapy (SGRT) is an alternative positioning method that uses non-ionizing optical imaging to monitor the position of a patient’s surface relative to isocentre both before and during treatment. SGRT uses near infrared light projections and camera pairs to reconstruct a patient’s 3-dimensional body surface in real time [4]. SGRT can be used throughout all parts of a radiotherapy treatment, for positional setup prior to treatment, and for positional monitoring during treatment.

Patients still typically receive tattoos in preparation for radiation treatment, despite the increasing availability of SGRT technology. Previous works have studied the setup time and accuracy of SGRT-based positioning in breast radiotherapy. Chang et al, Cravo Sa et al, and Hattel et al. compared tattoo-based setups to a combination of using tattoos and SGRT to position patients [5,6,7]. These studies found that incorporating SGRT into the tattoo-based setup procedure reduced the shifts required post verification imaging prior to IMRT treatment. Svestad et al. conducted a positioning and timing study, comparing tattoo-only setup to SGRT-only setup for right sided breast patients, and demonstrated higher accuracy with SGRT positioning and equivalence in setup time prior to IMRT treatment [8].

The work presented here explores SGRT as an alternative positioning modality to tattoos in terms of setup time, positional accuracy, and cost. Tattoos can negatively impact our patients’ quality of life, and in this study, we investigate surface guidance as a replacement on an operational level.

Methods

Study design

Data collection was conducted on two matched TrueBeam treatment units primarily dedicated to breast radiotherapy. Patients were assigned to one unit or the other based on availability at time of scheduling, and all patients were tattooed unless they opted out for personal reasons. One linac is equipped with an AlignRT® Horizon (VisionRT, London, UK) surface guidance system, while the other is not. At the linac not equipped with surface guidance technology, all breast patients were positioned using tattoo-based setup. At the other linac equipped with AlignRT, the tattoos were ignored and the patients were positioned using surface guidance alone (surface guided setup). The setup time and positional accuracy were collected from 337 fractions of radiation therapy for 101 unique patients receiving RT for breast cancer. Due to study staffing constraints, measurements were not performed for every fraction of a patient’s course of treatment. Instead, measurements were taken over consistent time periods each day.

At the time the measurements took place, AlignRT had been in use at the unit for two and a half years and SGRT protocols were well established. All radiation therapists involved in the study were proficient in both surface guidance and tattoo-based patient positioning.

Patients were treated using intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) or volumetric modulated arc therapy (VMAT). Treatment was delivered in 5 or 15 fractions, with boost doses delivered with photons or electrons in 4 or 5 fractions. Both tattoo-based and surface guided positioning are used for electron treatments and boosts, but measurements were excluded from this study due to the small sample size. Free breathing and deep inspiration breath hold (DIBH) techniques were used. Table 1 summarizes the treatment techniques used in this study and the corresponding number of fractions sampled.

Tattoo-based setup

Tattoo-based setup is the current standard of care at our centre. All patients were tattooed unless they declined for personal reasons, regardless of treatment unit assignment or SGRT use. Patients underwent computed tomography (CT) simulation prior to treatment, during which small tattoos (generally 2 to 3 mm) were permanently inked onto the skin to facilitate a reproducible 3-point setup with ipsi-lateral, contra-lateral, and anterior midline markings. Patients unable to raise their contralateral arm above their head received anterior midline and ipsi-lateral tattoos. In the tattooing process, the area is cleaned with an alcohol wipe and then a small drop of ink is placed on the skin. A needle is used to prick the first few millimetres of skin, injecting the ink.

During tattoo-based treatment setup, the couch and patient are moved to align the tattoos with in-room lasers coincident with the treatment isocentre. An automatic couch motion is then used to move the patient from the setup reference position to the treatment position.

Surface guided setup

AlignRT is an SGRT system with three camera pods that each consist of a near infrared light projector and two camera sensors. A light pattern is projected across the patient’s body, and the sensors detect the light’s reflections. Software uses the distortions of the light pattern to generate a real time, 3-dimensional reconstruction of the patient’s surface.

For SGRT-based setups the real-time surface image is compared against a reference surface generated from the external body contour of the simulation planning CT. When using SGRT, patients are positioned in the treatment position and tattoos are not referenced for patient positioning. Tattoos are ignored by the radiation therapists.

Radiographic imaging

For locoregional radiation using a four-field technique, the anterior supraclavicular and medial tangent light fields are checked on the skin to verify appropriate positioning against patient anatomy. This check is not performed for tangent-only or VMAT techniques. Once the patient is positioned in the room, pre-treatment paired kV images are acquired for IMRT treatments and cone beam CT (CBCT) images are acquired for VMAT treatments. Radiographic imaging is registered to the reference planning CT and couch shifts calculated to match the reference treatment position. If calculated couch shifts exceed site specific imaging tolerances, setup process is repeated.

Breathing technique

Patients treated without any restrictions on breathing (free breathing) required no further setup. For patients treated with the DIBH technique, breathing during treatment was monitored using a Real Time Position Management Respiratory Gating external breathing marker box (Varian Medical Systems, Palo Alto, CA) for both tattoo-based setup and surface guided setup. This was done to maintain consistent practice among all treatment units delivering gated RT.

Setup time data acquisition and analysis

The time required to position each patient was manually collected for each treatment. A stopwatch was used to measure the time from the patient first lying down with back and legs on the couch to the time at which the last-person-out chime was activated by the therapist engaging the last-person-out system. Repeat setups were not timed. A Kolmogoroc-Smirnov test was used to establish whether the data followed a normal distribution. A Wilcoxon rank sum test (p < 0.05) was then performed to determine significant differences in setup time between the positioning modalities.

Setup position data acquisition and analysis

Couch shift data from pre-treatment imaging was collected from each fraction asynchronously in an offline imaging review workspace. Shifts in the vertical (VRT), longitudinal (LNG), lateral (LAT), and rotational (RTN) directions were collected. Repeat setups were noted, and in the analysis only the couch shifts required to move the patient into treatment position were analyzed. Tattoo-based and surface guided positional shift data was then tested for statistically significant differences following the same methods as the time analysis (a Kolmogoroc-Smirnov test followed by a Wilcoxon rank sum test). The systematic and random shift errors across the population for both positioning modalities were calculated for all patients with three or more fractions measured [9].

Results

Setup time

The median and interquartile ranges (IQR) of setup time are presented in Table 3. A statistically significant difference in the median setup time between tattoo-based and surface guided setup was identified across all photon treatments (111 vs 100 s, p = 0.04). Stratification of breathing technique emerged through the analysis, and there was a statistically significant difference in median setup time between tattoo based and surface guided setup for free breathing treatments (106 vs 83 s, p = 0.001). DIBH treatment setup time was equivalent for both positioning modalities. The setup times for the first fraction of treatments were often longer than later fractions, however excluding the first day of treatment (n = 51) did not significantly impact the results. Cost to healthcare system

Tattoo costs per patient are less than $1.00 CAD for materials. Each new patient is tattooed by an RT at their CT simulation appointment, which is an estimated 2 min of RT time. The AlignRT item cost can range from $275,000 CAD to $400,000 CAD, depending on the selected configuration. Costs associated with installation include electrical and structural modifications estimated at $40,000 CAD. These are one-time, upfront costs. The annual cost of the service agreement is $49,000 CAD. The system must be commissioned by physicists (5 h) and requires routine quality assurance by radiation therapists (5 min/day), physics assistants (30 min/month) and physicists (2 h/year). For each patient there is an estimated 5 min of RT time for patient preparation in the AlignRT system where the patient surface is imported into the software, the delivery parameters are set, and the region of interest is defined.

Discussion

Setup time

Across the photon beam breast treatment techniques used at our center, SGRT-based setup demonstrated equivalent or improved setup speed compared to tattoo-based setup. Previous studies have reported that SGRT can increase setup time [13], decrease setup time [14], or achieve equivalent setup time [8]. The variation in reported results is likely due to different timepoints being used to define setup time. Svestad et al used timepoints most similar to our study, and their results support our findings [8]. AlignRT setup times were generally shorter than tattoo-based setup in our study, and statistically significant differences were demonstrated across photon treatments (11 s improvement), and specifically for free breathing treatments (23 s improvement). The combination of SGRT and external breathing box technologies for setup and treatment for breath hold could have prolonged the setup time, resulting in equivalent timing for breath hold setups while free breathing setups were significantly faster with AlignRT.

The couch was typically moved into the reference or treatment position immediately once the patient lay down, however, sometimes conversations continued before the couch was moved which increased the measured setup time for some samples. Particularly long conversations were noted thrice during tattoo-based setup, and four times during surface guided setup. Excluding these outliers did not impact the overall findings of this study.

Setup position

AlignRT did not significantly impact the magnitude of shifts during patient setup compared to the standard tattoo-based setup, reflecting the results of previous studies in this area [7,8,13,14,15]. Median differences in translational accuracy between positional modalities were within 0.05 cm, and there were no differences in median rotational accuracy. Systematic and random errors in translational shifts were under 0.32 cm for both modalities. Rotationally the systematic and random errors were under 0.42°.

Cost

The cost analysis in this study was limited to characterizable expenses in breast radiotherapy, conducted in the context of a publicly funded health care system. A surface guidance system’s financial breakdown is dominated by upfront costs, with annual costs attributed to staff salaries for quality assurance and routine maintenance, and the servicing contract. Tattoo item and staffing costs are minimal per patient. Tattoo removal costs are covered by patients and can vary substantially, and some service providers offer to absorb the costs themselves for cancer survivors. However, this translates to operational trade-off costs that are hard to quantify.

A 2024 news article citing the British Columbia Ministry of Health quote that five fractions of radiation therapy cost $3854 CAD [18]. For five fractions of radiotherapy, assuming 8 h work days with 3 breast patients treated per hour, the additional cost of surface guidance per patient borne by the healthcare system is $75 CAD. This is less than the average cost of tattoo removal of $375 CAD carried by our patients. When compared to the total cost of radiotherapy to the healthcare system, the additional financial cost of surface guidance is relatively low and comparable to the cost of other consumables used in radiation therapy which can range from $100 CAD to $300 CAD for each patient (as per our center’s quotes), such as thermoplastic masks.

Limitations

A well-established limitation of both tattoo-based and surface guided setup is skin tone, introducing the issue of equity in cancer care. Tattoos are not easily visible on all skin types, which can result in difficult or inaccurate positioning [3,16]. AlignRT systems offer exposure time adjustments to account for light detection from different skin tones, however the system performs better on light skin tones compared to dark skin tones [17]. While skin tone was not a component of this analysis, it is important to note that this is a limitation of both positioning methods, having an unknown impact on the results of this study. Demographic sub-group analysis of the patients in this study was not feasible due to sample size, and the skin tones of the patients reflected the predominantly European and South/East Asian ethnicities of our local patient population.

This cost analysis attempts to cover quantifiable costs associated with surface guidance. An additional cost not covered in this analysis is the staff training required when introducing new technology. With the introduction of surface guidance, radiation therapists, physics assistants, and medical physicists must attend training sessions, the timings of which will vary between centers and user experience.

Outside of a handful of the largest centers in North America, it is unlikely that a single unit would be dedicated to treating only to breast radiotherapy, thus reducing the number of breast patients per SGRT system per day. However, surface guidance can be used for treatment sites beyond the breast, and can introduce other cost benefits that improve our patient’s experiences but are more difficult to quantify. SGRT could replace gating systems, reduce use of immobilization devices, and reduce the use of anesthesia in pediatric radiotherapy treatments [19,20,21].

Conclusion

Compared to tattoo-based setups, patient positioning with AlignRT can offer equivalent or improved setup time and positional accuracy across photon-based breast radiotherapy treatment techniques. Tattooless breast radiotherapy is an operationally and fiscally feasible option to improve patient experience without sacrificing quality or safety. Cancer centers around the world are adopting surface guidance to support tattooless breast radiotherapy, and this project supports institutional initiatives to keep up with the global standard of care. The authors support the reduction or elimination of tattoo use in breast radiotherapy, allowing benefits beyond operational improvements to be gained through improved patient radiotherapy experiences.
