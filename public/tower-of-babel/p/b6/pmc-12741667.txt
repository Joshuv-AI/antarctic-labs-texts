Ataxin-3 Overexpression via Adeno-associated Viral Vector Injection in the Primate Cerebellum: A Novel Model of Spinocerebellar Ataxia Type 3
Authors: Keonwoo Kim, Aryun Kim, Jinyoung Won, Junghyung Park, Kyung Seob Lim, Chang-Yeop Jeon, Jisun Min, Jee-Hyun Cho, Youngkyu Song, Bon-Sang Koo, Gyu-Seo Bae, Eunsu Jeon, Kang-Jin Jeong, Sung-Hyun Park, Hwal-Yong Lee, Won Seok Choi, Dong-Seok Lee, Youngjeon Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpinocerebellar ataxia type 3 (SCA3) is an autosomal-dominant neurodegenerative disorder caused by an expanded polyglutamine repeat in the ataxin-3 gene. The resulting mutant ataxin-3 protein forms intraneuronal inclusions that lead to neurodegeneration in the cerebellum and other brain regions. This study aimed to develop a novel nonhuman primate model of SCA3 to address the limitations of existing knock-in and transgenic models using an adeno-associated virus (AAV) to deliver the mutant gene. AAV viral vectors carrying mutant ataxin-3 were stereotaxically injected into the cerebellum of monkeys. The animals were monitored over an 8-week period, during which behavioral and neuroimaging assessments were conducted. This was followed by a detailed pathological examination. The AAV vector successfully spread throughout the cerebellum, and the expression of mutant ataxin-3 was confirmed. Neuroimaging revealed a reduction in N-acetylaspartate (NAA) levels, whereas histological analysis showed significant damage to the Purkinje cell layer. Notably, the monkeys exhibited sleep disturbances, a prodromal symptom commonly observed in human patients with SCA3. AAV-mediated delivery of mutant ataxin-3 can effectively replicate the key pathological and clinical features of SCA3 in primates. This approach offers a promising new model for studying disease mechanisms and evaluating potential therapies.Keywords: Spinocerebellar ataxia type 3, Ataxin-3, AAV2, Nonhuman primate, Cerebellum, Sleep disturbance

INTRODUCTION

Spinocerebellar ataxia type 3 (SCA3), also known as Machado–Joseph disease, is a degenerative brain disease that presents with neurodegenerative and behavioral symptoms affecting the cerebellum and related brain regions. The disease develops when the CAG repeat in exon 10 of the ataxin-3 gene is expanded to approximately 51~90 because of a mutation [1, 2]. Patients with SCA3 present with symptoms, including movement disorders, mental illness, and ataxia. There are currently no established treatment strategies for this disease [3, 4].

A high prevalence of pathological alterations in the brain has been observed in patients diagnosed with SCA3. Disease progression is accompanied by cerebellar atrophy. In the initial stages of the disease, atrophy is observed in the cerebellar vermis, hemispheres, brainstem, and middle cerebellar peduncle, accompanied by enlargement of the fourth ventricle [5, 6]. As the disease progresses to its terminal stages, the effects are not limited to the cerebellum, but are also observed in all areas of the brain, including the frontal and temporal lobes. A substantial proportion of individuals with SCA3 display a notable decline in the number of Purkinje cells within the cerebellum, particularly in the vermis. The ataxin-3 protein produced by the mutant ataxin-3 gene is misfolded and accumulates in mutant regions (CAG repeats). This accumulation is observed by immunohistochemistry in the form of intracellular inclusions. These changes affect cellular homeostasis, disrupt cellular physiology, and result in cell loss. Behavioral defects in patients with SCA3 appear during this process [7-11].

Currently, there is no established pharmacological treatment for SCA3; only conservative therapeutic options are available. Recently, numerous studies have been conducted with the objective of reducing the level of the mutant ataxin-3 protein through gene therapy [12-14]. The development of models that exhibit pathologies similar to those observed in human patients with SCA3 is highly important, as it enables the evaluation of treatment strategies in the preclinical stages of the disease. Furthermore, the establishment of SCA3 rodent models that exhibit brain degeneration, ataxia behavior, and inclusion pathology comparable to those observed in patients with SCA3 is of paramount importance [15-17]. In recent years, several SCA3 patient models have been developed through transgenic and knock-in techniques [18-24], as well as by injecting a viral vector containing ataxin-3 Q84 directly into the brain [25-29]. However, although rodent models provide foundational insights and enable high-throughput screening, they are inherently limited by substantial physiological, metabolic, and neuroanatomical differences from humans—including brain size, lifespan, and complexity of motor coordination circuits [30, 31]. Notably, rodents often fail to exhibit the full spectrum of progressive motor and cognitive deficits observed in patients, and their simplified cerebellar and basal ganglia structures hinder the accurate modeling of region-specific pathologies observed in SCA3. These limitations can impede the translation of therapeutic findings to clinical success, particularly for interventions requiring long-term observation or regionally targeted effects.

Nonhuman primates (NHPs) offer a promising alternative because their brains more closely resemble those of humans in terms of both structure and function. NHPs possess a highly developed cerebellum and corticospinal system, similar patterns of aging, and longer lifespans, making them especially suitable for modeling late-onset progressive neurodegenerative diseases, such as SCA3. This anatomical and functional proximity allows for a more accurate recapitulation of disease phenotypes and a more accurate assessment of therapeutic efficacy, including long-term safety and tolerability.

Nevertheless, studies on NHP models of SCA3 remain scarce, with only a limited number of studies using viral vector-based approaches. Therefore, there is a pressing need to develop NHP models that exhibit neuropathological and behavioral features consistent with human SCA3. Such models can serve as critical platforms for evaluating novel treatment strategies at the preclinical stage and bridge the translational gap between rodent studies and clinical applications.

Adeno-associated viral vectors are widely used in biomedical research because of their nonpathogenic nature, neuronal tropism, mild immunogenicity, and efficacy as gene delivery systems [32]. Notably, AAV vectors enable long-term transgene expression, efficient targeting of postmitotic neurons, and region-specific gene delivery depending on the serotype, making them particularly well-suited for modeling neurodegenerative diseases. Direct injection of viral vectors has been employed in animal models of several neurodegenerative conditions, including Alzheimer’s disease [33], Lou Gehrig’s disease [34], and Huntington’s disease [35]. In the case of SCA3, AAV-mediated delivery of mutant ataxin-3 has been reported in rodent models; however, no such approach has yet been applied in NHPs. Here, we establish a nonhuman primate model of SCA3 by stereotaxic delivery of an AAV vector encoding mutant human ataxin-3 with 84 polyglutamine repeats (Q84) to the cerebellum. Animals were monitored for eight weeks with behavioral and neuroimaging assessments, followed by histopathological analyses to characterize cerebellar expression patterns and disease-related pathology. To our knowledge, this AAV-based approach represents the first NHP model of SCA3 and provides a time-efficient platform to investigate disease mechanisms and evaluate potential therapies in a system that closely recapitulates human neuroanatomy and pathology.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Experimental animals and ethics statement

This study used two female cynomolgus monkeys (Macaca fascicularis, C1 and C2) as subjects. The monkeys were 14 years old and had body weights of 3.3 and 3.8 kg. As previously reported, all experimental monkeys were sourced from the National Primate Research Center of the Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology. The cage dimensions were 60×80×80 cm and complied with the standards set forth by the National Institutes of Health (USA). Commercial monkey chow (Teklad 2050TM, Envigo, USA), a variety of fruits, and water were provided ad libitum, and rubber and plastic toys were also supplied for environmental enrichment. The housing conditions were maintained at a temperature of 24±2°C, a relative humidity of 50±5%, and a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. The monkeys were monitored by a veterinarian in accordance with the guidelines of the Weatherall Report on the Use of NHPs. All procedures were approved by the KRIBB Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Approval No. KRIBB-AEC-25036).

Viral vector injection

Two monkeys were included in this pilot study. Monkey C1 received an AAV vector encoding mutant human ataxin-3 with 84 polyglutamine repeats (Q84) under a CMV promoter (titer 8.1×1011 vg/ml) (Fig. 1A). Monkey C2 received an AAV vector of identical backbone without the ataxin-3 insert (empty vector control). Both viral preparations were produced by the IBS Virus Facility (Korea). The vectors were mixed with a gadolinium-based magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contrast agent (5 mM) and stereotactically injected into the bilateral cerebellum to specifically target the deep cerebellar nuclei (DCN). The monkeys were anesthetized by intramuscular injection of 1 mg/kg ketamine and placed in a stereotaxic frame under 2% isoflurane. A scalp incision was made, and an MRI marker was attached to the skull for subsequent imaging. MRI was performed with an MRI marker attached, and the target points were confirmed based on the location of the MRI marker. The distance from the MRI marker to the target points (DCN) was determined using neuronavigation (Tübingen, Germany), after which a hole was drilled to insert the needle into the skull. The needle was positioned over the hole and injected vertically using a stereotaxic device. The viral vector was delivered in a total volume of 30 µl per hemisphere, divided into three tracks (10 µl each) at a rate of 1 µl/min. A dwell time of 5 min was maintained after each infusion before advancing the needle by 0.5 mm. Once the injection was complete, the needle was withdrawn slowly, and the hole was sealed with bone cement. Subsequently, the skin was sutured. MRI was performed using a contrast agent to confirm the injection site, after which the animals were returned to their individual cages (Fig. 1B).

The adeno-associated viral vector carrying ataxin-3 Q84 was injected into the deep cerebellar nuclei. (A) Schematic illustration of the AAV viral vector loaded with ataxin-3 Q84. (B) Viral vector injection via the stereotaxic method and contrast agent diffusion at the injection site confirmed by magnetic resonance imaging.

Circadian activity recording

The physical activity of each monkey was monitored and recorded at 1-min intervals via an Actical Physical Activity Monitor (Pespiro Inc., USA). The device was attached to a silicone collar and worn by the monkeys. The data were then aggregated on a daily basis to assess the overall level of spontaneous motor activity and on an hourly basis to examine diurnal activity rhythms. Actual data were collected for a period of 4 weeks prior to infusion and for 8 weeks following infusion, at 4-week intervals.

Sleep assessment

In the present study, we used an actigraphic approach to assess sleep patterns. This approach has a supportive role in elucidating sleep differences between controls and monkeys with specific diseases [36]. Actigraphy data were collected at 30-s epochs and aggregated into 2-min windows for scoring. Wake was defined when activity was detected in ≥3 epochs per 2-min window, transitional sleep when activity was detected in 1~2 epochs, and relaxed sleep when no activity was recorded within the window. This scoring approach has been validated in previous actigraphy-based studies of NHP sleep [36].

Gait analysis

Gait analysis was performed 4 and 8 weeks after vector injection to assess locomotor function. The evaluation used Social LEAP Estimates Animal Poses (SLEAP), a deep learning-based pose estimation software that enables accurate, markerless tracking of animal joint positions across video frames [37]. Prior to analysis, the model was trained on approximately 2,400 manually labeled video frames to optimize tracking accuracy. SLEAP employs convolutional neural networks to detect keypoints corresponding to specific body parts (e.g., joints) in each video frame and reconstructs the full-body pose by associating these keypoints across time. This allows the precise extraction of locomotor parameters without the need for physical markers. Using the pose estimations generated by the SLEAP, the joint angles of the ankle, knee, and hip were quantified throughout the gait cycle. Additionally, the stride length and walking speed were automatically calculated based on the tracked foot trajectories.

Proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-MRS)

The monkeys were initially anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of a cocktail mixture of ketamine (0.5 mg/kg) and atropine (0.02 mg/kg). During the MRI scan, respiratory anesthesia was maintained with 2.0~2.5% isoflurane and pure oxygen (2 L/min) administered through a mask in the supine position. MRI experiments were performed using a 7T whole-body MR scanner (Achieva 7.0T, Philips Medical Systems, Best, The Netherlands) equipped with a 32-channel receiver head coil and a birdcage 8-channel transmit coil (Nova Medical) at the Korea Basic Science Institute. For brain anatomical reference, a three-dimensional magnetization-prepared rapid gradient echo sequence was used with the following parameters: field of view=150×150×150 mm3, voxel size=0.9×0.9×0.9 mm3, slices=167, repetition time (TR)=5.5 ms, echo time (TE)=2.6 ms, inversion delay time=1,300 ms, flip angle=7°, and sensitivity encoding factor=2. Single-voxel proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H-MRS) was performed to assess changes in total N-acetylaspartate (NAA) (sum of NAA and N-acetylaspartyl glutamate [tNAA]) and creatine concentrations in the brain. Volumes of interest (VOIs) in the cerebral cortex and cerebellum were defined as 25×20×15 mm3 and 20×20×20 mm3, respectively. The sequence and parameters were as follows: semilocalized by adiabatic selective refocusing, TR=5,000 ms, TE=33 ms, spectral bandwidth=4 kHz, samples=2,048, average=64, and acquisition time=5 m 30 s.

The MRS scans were performed consecutively for a total of 10 times—five scans targeting the cerebrum and five targeting the cerebellum. In all spectral acquisitions, variable pulse power and optimized relaxation delays were used for water suppression. Prior to the acquisition of 1H spectra, second-order B0 sheeting by the Philips pencil beam volume algorithm and radiofrequency power optimization on the localized VOI were applied. Following the MRS scans, a brain anatomical reference image was acquired to verify the absence of head motion during scanning.

All MRS data were fitted using the LCModel software [38] and total NAA concentrations were normalized to the ratio of total creatine (creatine + phosphocreatine [tCr]). The basis set used in LCModel was simulated using the Versatile Simulation, Pulses, and Analysis package program [39], and LCModel provided the macromolecule basis set. Cramer–Rao lower bounds (CRLB) were used to evaluate the reliability of the fitting, and all metabolites with CRLB <20% were included for further analysis. The total NAA and creatine spectra were successfully fitted and showed good spectral dispersion with a high signal-to-noise ratio.

Western blot

Total protein lysates were prepared using the PRO-PREP protein extraction solution (Intron Biotechnology, Seongnam, Korea). Equal amounts of protein were electrophoresed on 8~12% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gels and then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (BD Biosciences). The membranes were blocked by incubation in blocking buffer (BD Biosciences) and probed overnight at 4°C with the following antibodies: anti-β-actin and anti-ataxin-3 (Sigma‒Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA). The membranes were washed with Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) and incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling) for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were then washed with TBST, and specific binding was detected using a chemiluminescence detector.

Tissue preparation & immunohistochemistry

Eight weeks after viral vector injection, all cynomolgus monkeys underwent transcardial perfusion with 500 ml of 100 mM phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) under deep anesthesia with an intramuscular injection of a cocktail of ketamine (5 mg/kg) and atropine (0.02 mg/kg). The entire brain was extracted from the skull and washed with cold PBS. The brains were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 week, sectioned at a thickness of 4 mm, and transferred to a 30% sucrose solution at 4°C. The cerebellum tissue was preserved in optimal cutting temperature compound and subsequently sectioned at a thickness of 10 µm via a cryotome. Three distinct antibodies (1H9, 1C2, and 9E10) were used. The slides were then subjected to staining with primary antibodies, namely, the ataxin-3 antibody 1H9 (MAB5360, Millipore, 1:1000, overnight, 4°C), anti-polyglutamine expression disease marker 1C2 (MAB1574, Millipore, 1:1000, overnight, 4°C), and anti-myc antibody 9E10 (ab32, Abcam, 1:500, overnight, 4°C). Next, the slides were washed with PBST thrice for 10 min each and stained with secondary antibodies (mouse or rabbit IgG, according to the primary antibodies). Antibody binding was visualized using a Vectastain Elite avidin and biotinylated enzyme complex kit, followed by application of a 3,3’9-diaminobenzidine substrate kit. After the dehydration, the slides were subjected to a 10-min reaction with xylene, covered with a cover glass, and stored at room temperature. Slide images were acquired using a slide scanner, and the size and number of aggregates present in the images were evaluated. The cerebellum was analyzed via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining [40]. Histopathological assessment was conducted using an Olympus BX51 microscope for direct microscopic observation and a PreciPoint M8 digital slide scanner for high-resolution whole-slide imaging (PreciPoint GmbH, Freising, Germany). For histological quantification, multiple cerebellar sections per animal were analyzed, and section-level replicates were used for statistical comparisons.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software. Because this study was a pilot experiment with two monkeys (C1: ataxin-3 Q84–injected, C2: empty vector control), overall results are primarily presented as per-animal values. For measures in which both animals were assessed across multiple time points, data are presented descriptively to illustrate within-subject longitudinal changes. For datasets that yielded repeated technical or regional replicates within each animal (e.g., multiple strides in gait analysis, repeated voxel acquisitions in MRS, or multiple histological sections), statistical comparisons were performed using these replicate values. Exact p-values are reported for such analyses. In contrast, for measures that provided only a single value per animal, inferential statistics were not feasible; these results are therefore presented descriptively and should be interpreted with caution. A significance threshold of p<0.05 was adopted when statistical testing was feasible.

RESULTS

Expression of ataxin-3 Q84 in SHSY-5Y cells and the cerebellum

The viral vector was transfected into SH-SY5Y cells, and the expression of the vector was subsequently confirmed by Western blotting. The expression of ataxin-3 Q84 was observed in the infected SH-SY5Y cells at 24, 48 and 72 hours post infection (Fig. 2A). The constructed viral vector was observed to express ataxin-3 Q84 in cells. An attempt was then made to express ataxin-3 Q84 protein in tissue via intracerebellar injection. The viral vector was then injected bilaterally into the DCNs in the cerebellum, and immunofluorescence was used to confirm vector expression. ZsGreen, which was labeled with the viral vector, was observed via fluorescence microscopy, which revealed that the vector had spread not only to the injection site but also to the cerebellar cortex (Fig. 2B).

The expression of ataxin-3 by viral vectors in vivo and in vitro was validated. (A) Verification of adeno-associated viral vector expression in SH-SY5Y cells via western blotting. Verification that ataxin-3Q84 is expressed in AAV-infected cells. (B) IF image of the deep cerebellar nuclei and cerebellar cortex. ZsGreen tagged in the viral vector was expressed throughout the cerebellum.

Night activity increase in viral vector-injected monkeys

Following injection, actical monitoring showed no substantial change in total daily activity in either monkey. However, the mutant ataxin-3 Q84–injected monkey (C1) exhibited a progressive increase in nocturnal activity at weeks 4 and 8 compared with its own baseline values. In contrast, the empty vector control monkey (C2) maintained stable nocturnal activity across the same time points. These results are presented descriptively because each condition consisted of a single animal (n=1 per group), precluding formal statistical testing (Fig. 3).

(B) Total activity and light/dark activity ratios of both monkeys (C1: ataxin-3 Q84, C2: empty vector control) across baseline, week 4, and week 8. Data are presented as individual per-animal values (n=1 per group) to illustrate within-subject longitudinal changes; no inferential statistics were applied.

Actigraphy data were subsequently used to evaluate sleep patterns, given that elevated nocturnal activity is often associated with sleep disturbances. After 8 weeks, C1 displayed longer transitional sleep time and increased waking duration relative to its baseline, suggesting the onset of sleep disturbance following viral vector injection. C2 did not show noticeable changes during this period. As only one monkey was available in each condition, these findings are also presented descriptively without inferential statistics (Fig. 4).

Gait analysis via pose tracking in deep learning programs (Social LEAP Estimates Animal Poses)

The application of the SLEAP system (Fig. 5A) for pose tracking demonstrated no significant differences in joint angles or range of motion between the pre-injection and post-injection states (Fig. 5B, C). In C1 (ataxin-3 Q84), there was a trend toward reduced joint angles at weeks 4 and 8 compared with baseline, but this did not reach statistical significance (Hip joint: p=0.1636, Knee joint: p=0.3361, Ankle joint: p=0.4441). C2 (empty vector control) showed stable gait parameters across all time points. Statistical comparisons were based on multiple stride-level repeated measurements within each animal, which allowed for inferential testing but should be interpreted with caution, as the study included only one monkey per condition. No overt clinical gait abnormalities were observed.

Cerebellar N-acetylaspartate decrease detected by magnetic resonance spectroscopy following ataxin-3 Q84 injection

In patients with SCA3, NAA levels are significantly lower than those in the general population [41-43]. NAA is a marker of neuronal loss or impairment, and we found that it was reduced in primates injected with the viral vector. At weeks 4 and 8, the tNAA/tCr ratio decreased in C1 (ataxin-3 Q84), whereas C2 (empty vector control) remained stable (Fig. 6). Each value represents the mean of three repeated voxel acquisitions per session, and statistical comparisons based on these replicates confirmed a significant reduction in C1 (Cerebral cortex: p=0.0005 at week 4; p=0.0002 at week 8, Cerebellum: p=0.0040 at week 4; p=0.0191 at week 8). This result indicates that neuronal loss occurred within the measured area of the cerebellar cortex. To verify cerebellar neuronal loss corresponding to the MRS changes, the monkeys were euthanized and cerebellar tissue underwent histopathological analysis.

(A) Representative spectra obtained from the cerebellar hemisphere at baseline, week 4, and week 8. (B) Quantification of the tNAA/tCr ratio in C1 (ataxin-3 Q84) and C2 (empty vector control). Each value represents the mean of three repeated voxel acquisitions per session (*p<0.05, **p<0.01, ***p<0.001).

Pathologic analyses of monkeys injected with the viral vector

H&E staining was performed to confirm the cell loss of the Purkinje cell layer within the cerebellum. Quantification across multiple cerebellar sections revealed a significant decrease of approximately 20% in the number of cells in the Purkinje cell layer per unit area (1 mm2) in C1 (ataxin-3 Q84) compared with C2 (empty vector control) (p=0.0016). In addition, the mean distance (μm) between cells in the Purkinje cell layer was significantly greater in C1 (p=0.0096) (Fig. 7). These findings are consistent with previous mouse studies [28], which demonstrated pronounced behavioral symptoms, although such symptoms were not evident in our monkey model. The reduction in the tNAA/tCr ratio observed in MRS may be attributable to cell loss in the cerebellum. To verify cerebellar neuronal loss and confirm viral vector expression, immunohistochemistry was performed. Increased staining for ataxin-3 and myc was observed in C1 but not in C2, demonstrating robust expression of the injected viral vector (Fig. 8) (Ataxin-3 positive Cell: p=0.0362, Myc positive cell: p=0.0067). These findings indicate that overexpression of ataxin-3 induced both neuronal loss and pathological protein expression in the primate cerebellum.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we administered an AAV vector encoding ataxin-3 with 84 polyQ to the cerebellum of a monkey (C1). Compared with the empty vector control monkey (C2), a monkey that received the viral vector presented behavioral, radiological, and histopathological differences. These data indicate that the delivery of mutant ataxin-3 to the cerebellum via an AAV vector has the potential to establish a novel primate SCA3 model, overcoming the limitations of previous transgenic models, including prolonged production time and high costs. SCA3 primate models may prove valuable in the preclinical identification of pharmacological or genetic treatments for the cerebellum, which is the region of the brain most severely affected by SCA3.

In total, 60% of patients with SCA3 experience sleep disorder symptoms, irrespective of the severity of the disease [41, 42]. In some SCA3 patients, sleep disorder symptoms manifest prior to the onset of ataxia for a period of 5~10 years [43]. In particular, REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) is one of the most frequently reported conditions and is considered an early non-motor manifestation of the disease [44]. Following viral vector injection, an increase in nocturnal activity was observed in the monkeys, prompting the hypothesis that their sleep patterns were disrupted. To investigate this, sleep assessment was conducted. Compared with the baseline period, sleep assessment revealed an increase in wake and transitional sleep states following vector injection, indicating that the monkeys were not sleeping correctly at night. This altered sleep pattern may reflect RBD-like symptoms, supporting the translational validity of the model. Sleep disorders have also been observed in patients with SCA3, verifying that the model displayed symptoms similar to those observed in actual patients. In total, up to 45~50% of patients with SCA3 experience sleep disturbance symptoms, including restless legs syndrome (RLS) and REM sleep behavior disorder (RBD) independent of disease duration or ataxia severity [45-47]. RBD and other sleep abnormalities in SCA3 patients have been reported using polysomnography [48]. Following viral vector injection, our model showed increased nocturnal wakefulness and transitional sleep states, suggesting disruption of sleep architecture. This phenotype is consistent with clinical manifestations in SCA3, supporting the translational relevance of our primate model. Preclinical studies have also linked cerebellar Purkinje cell degeneration to altered sleep regulation, suggesting a potential mechanistic link between cerebellar pathology and sleep-wake disturbances. More broadly, emerging evidence indicates that sleep and circadian rhythm dysregulation are early indicators of neurodegeneration and may precede overt clinical symptoms in diseases such as Parkinson’s and SCA3 [49].

Gait analysis was performed using SLEAP, a deep learning-based pose tracking algorithm designed to quantify locomotor function in NHPs. The software was used to identify the joint positions and measure the changes in the joint angles throughout the gait cycle. This analysis aimed to detect behavioral abnormalities, such as ataxic gait; however, no motor deficits were observed within the 8-week observation period. The absence of observable symptoms may be due to insufficient viral diffusion into the cerebellar motor circuits or subthreshold expression of mutant ataxin-3 in functionally relevant regions. It is also possible that early-stage deficits were significantly subtle to be detected using the current gait analysis method. Despite its high precision, the pose-tracking protocol may lack sensitivity for capturing mild coordination impairments, which could account for the absence of detectable motor deficits in this study. To address this limitation, we plan to employ complementary assays targeting fine motor skills and hand dexterity, which may detect subtle cerebellar dysfunction. Hand dexterity tasks have already been validated in our previous work using nonhuman primate models of Parkinson’s disease [50], demonstrating their feasibility and sensitivity for detecting subtle motor deficits. In human SCA3, sleep disorders frequently precede motor symptoms such as ataxia. Our model similarly exhibited early sleep disturbances without overt ataxia, suggesting that behavioral phenotypes may emerge after longer observation periods.

MRS was used to detect neurochemical alterations in the cerebellum following injection of a viral vector expressing mutant ataxin-3 (Q84). Among the quantified metabolites, a trend toward reduced levels of NAA, a well-established neuronal marker, was observed. This reduction was particularly reflected in the tNAA/tCr ratio, which indicates neuronal viability relative to energy metabolism. Such a decrease in NAA is typically associated with neuronal loss or dysfunction because NAA is predominantly synthesized in healthy neurons. Therefore, reduced levels may indicate the degeneration of Purkinje cells [51] or impaired mitochondrial metabolism [52], both of which are pathological features commonly observed in SCA3. A continuous decrease in the tNAA/tCr ratio was noted in the cerebellar region from the early post-injection period, which remained suppressed through week 8, suggesting sustained impairment of neuronal integrity. Given that NAA is predominantly localized within neurons, especially in large projection neurons, such as Purkinje cells, the observed reduction is indicative of neuronal dysfunction or loss in the targeted area. These findings are consistent with the neuropathological findings in human patients with SCA3 [53, 54], and the progressive degeneration of Purkinje cells in the cerebellum is a characteristic feature. Therefore, the MRS findings provide in vivo evidence supporting the presence of cerebellar neuronal pathology in this primate model of SCA3 [1, 2].

Impairment of Purkinje cells has been observed in patients diagnosed with SCA3 and in transgenic mouse models [1, 2, 18, 19]. Furthermore, numerous studies have indicated that this impairment is directly associated with motor control, even in the absence of intracellular inclusions [55-57]. To identify whether the observed MRS ratios were a result of damage to Purkinje cells, we conducted histopathological experiments on cerebellar tissue from primates sacrificed 2 months after injection. The number of cells and the distance between cells in the Purkinje cell layer were reduced in subjects who received viral vector injections, a reduction that was also confirmed in models created using conventional viral vector injection or transgenic methods. These findings suggest that mutant ataxin-3 overexpression may trigger Purkinje cell degeneration through mechanisms such as protein aggregation, autophagy impairment, or ER stress—pathways that have been frequently reported in SCA3 rodent models and human patient tissues [7, 20, 21]. Although neuronal changes were observed in the Purkinje cell layer, this study did not include mechanistic investigations such as protein aggregation, autophagy, ER stress, or neuroinflammation. Such analyses are essential to elucidate the pathogenic processes of polyQ-expanded ataxin-3 and will be indispensable in future studies. Elucidating these molecular cascades in future work will be essential for understanding the full pathophysiological impact of mutant ataxin-3 in the cerebellum. Damage to Purkinje cells is a defining symptom of SCA3. The pathological changes observed in our model are consistent with features of SCA3, suggesting that this approach may provide a preliminary platform for future translational studies.

In this study, we injected an AAV vector carrying mutant ataxin-3 directly into the cerebellum of monkeys to induce gene overexpression. We subsequently performed behavioral, radiological, and histopathological analyses of the changes that occurred as a result of this overexpression. Eight weeks after AAV vector injection, an increase in nocturnal activity was observed and a decrease in the tNAA/tCr ratio was confirmed by MRS. Subsequent histopathological experiments demonstrated a reduction in Purkinje cells in the cerebellum and the expression of mutant ataxin-3. Several characteristics reminiscent of SCA3 patients were observed in our model, and this method of modeling by injecting AAV viral vectors has several advantages over conventional transgenic or knock-in models, including a short production time and lower cost, which are highly beneficial for SCA3 studies. In particular, the capacity to monitor functional outcomes such as sleep-wake cycles, behavioral activity, and in vivo neurochemical changes via high-resolution imaging highlights the translational potential of primate models in elucidating SCA3 pathophysiology and evaluating future therapeutics. A limitation of this experiment is that only a period of 8 weeks was observed; thus, the expression of ataxia symptoms, which is the most characteristic feature of SCA3, was not confirmed. Because the use of nonhuman primates is restricted by ethical considerations, the limited availability of aged animals, and regulatory constraints on invasive procedures, this study was conducted as a pilot experiment with only two monkeys (one Q84 and one empty-vector control). With such a small sample size, the potential influence of individual variability cannot be excluded, and some findings may reflect individual-specific characteristics rather than generalizable effects of mutant ataxin-3 expression. Accordingly, the results should be regarded as preliminary, providing proof-of-concept rather than definitive conclusions. Another limitation is that neuronal loss was assessed only within the Purkinje cell layer, and we did not perform co-staining with cell-type–specific markers (e.g., Calbindin, NeuN, GFAP). Thus, while our data are consistent with Purkinje cell vulnerability, cell-type specificity was not directly confirmed.

Future studies incorporating extended longitudinal follow-up, co-labeling with neuronal and glial markers, and larger cohorts will be essential to validate and extend the present findings, to improve reproducibility, and to confirm that the observed changes are generalizable across individuals. In addition, determining the optimal time point and duration of the onset and persistence of ataxic symptoms after injection will be important for understanding disease progression. Neurochemical and pathological alterations were observed in this study. Previous work in SCA3 models and patient tissue has shown that the observed alterations are associated with the accumulation and aggregation of mutant ataxin-3 protein, which can disrupt protein quality-control systems and impair autophagy; persistent protein misfolding also provokes ER stress responses; and activated glial cells contribute to a neuroinflammatory environment. Our findings of sleep disturbances, MRS-detected neurochemical alterations, and cerebellar pathology may likewise reflect the consequences of these processes, although they were not directly examined in this study. Future studies incorporating molecular and immunohistochemical analyses will be necessary to confirm their contribution and to delineate the full pathophysiological cascade in this primate model. The anatomical and physiological similarity of the primate cerebellum to that of humans enables more faithful reproduction of the neuropathological cascade and translational endpoints of SCA3. Thus, this model highlights unique translational value and underscores the importance of its continued development.
