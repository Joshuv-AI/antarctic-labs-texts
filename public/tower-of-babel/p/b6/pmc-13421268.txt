Diagnostic performance of [18F]Tetrafluoroborate PET versus 131I scintigraphy in differentiated thyroid cancer: a prospective study
Authors: Kerim Sonmezoglu, Onur Erdem Sahin, Kerem Saglam, Emre Karayel, Huseyin Pehlivanoglu, Lebriz Uslu-Besli, Haluk Burcak Sayman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeDifferentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) is primarily evaluated using radioiodine (RAI) scanning; however, this approach is limited by the suboptimal imaging properties of 131I, the cost and limited availability of 123I, and restricted access to 124I. [18F]Tetrafluoroborate (TFB), a PET radiotracer targeting the sodium/iodide symporter, has emerged as a promising alternative. This prospective study assessed the diagnostic performance and biodistribution of TFB PET compared with 131I scintigraphy in patients with DTC.MethodsThis prospective study enrolled 58 patients with histopathologically confirmed DTC after total or near-total thyroidectomy. Forty-eight patients underwent both TFB PET/CT and 131I SPECT scintigraphy, while 10 underwent only TFB PET for biodistribution analysis. Patients were stratified into preradioiodine (TFB before 131I; n = 28) and postradioiodine (TFB after 131I; n = 20) subgroups based on recent radioiodine administration (either diagnostic or therapeutic) to evaluate the potential stunning effect of 131I on TFB uptake. Patient- and lesion-based analyses were performed, and agreement was assessed using Cohen’s kappa.ResultsIn patient-based analysis, concordance between modalities showed both TFB and 131I positivity in 20 patients, TFB-only positivity in 1 patient, 131I-only positivity in 5 patients, and negativity in both modalities in 22 patients, yielding substantial overall agreement (κ = 0.75). Agreement was almost perfect in the preradioiodine group (κ = 0.84) but declined to a moderate level in the postradioiodine group (κ = 0.49). In lesion-based analysis (n = 56), overall detection rates were comparable (TFB, 85.7% vs. 131I, 73.2%) but significantly higher for TFB in the preradioiodine group (95.7% vs. 68.1%, p < 0.05). Lesion detection decreased markedly in the postradioiodine group (33.3%), consistent with a stunning effect. Biodistribution analysis showed highest physiological uptake in the stomach, salivary glands, esophagus, nasopharynx and unexpected uterine uptake in Nabothian cysts in 45% of female patients.ConclusionTFB PET demonstrates diagnostic accuracy and lesion detection rates comparable to 131I scintigraphy in DTC. Its diagnostic performance is superior in patients without recent radioiodine administration prior to TFB imaging, whereas lesion detectability decreases after radioiodine administration, likely due to a stunning effect. Therefore, TFB PET represents a promising alternative for disease burden assessment in DTC and warrants further multicenter validation.

Introduction

Differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) is the most common endocrine malignancy worldwide, with papillary thyroid cancer accounting for the majority (85%) of cases followed by follicular subtype and others (15%) [1]. Detecting metastatic or residual disease is essential for guiding management and prognosis in DTC, similar to other malignant neoplasms [2]. Most DTCs retain NIS-mediated iodine uptake, although this may be reduced in Hürthle cell and some other variants. Therefore, radioiodine scintigraphy remains the cornerstone for staging and follow-up, but it suffers from inherent limitations: 131I has poor imaging characteristics (mostly used for therapeutic purposes), 123I is costly and not widely available, and 124I, although suitable for PET, has limited accessibility. Additionally, planar and even SPECT imaging with 123I or 131I may miss small lesions due to relatively low spatial resolution. Even PET imaging with 124I is subjected to the suboptimal image quality by its low positron yield [3]. [18F]Tetrafluoroborate (TFB) is an emerging PET radiotracer targeting the NIS system. It offers several advantages over radioiodine isotopes, including abundant availability of 18F, easy labeling procedure in a small-scale radiopharmacy laboratory, better PET imaging capability due to physical properties of 18F, and favorable radiation safety as shown in several preclinical and human studies [4–10]. However, clinical experience with TFB PET remains limited. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the role of TFB PET imaging in patients with DTC, compare its diagnostic performance with that of 131I (RAI) scanning for detecting residual and metastatic disease, and evaluate its extrathyroidal biodistribution profile.

Materials and methods

This prospective study enrolled 58 patients with histopathologically confirmed DTC who underwent total or near-total thyroidectomy. Forty-eight patients who were scheduled for a possible radioiodine therapy (RAIT) either for ablation purposes or to treat residual or metastatic disease during follow-up underwent both 131I scanning [either post-RAIT survey (Tx-RAI) or pre-RAIT low-activity (185 MBq) diagnostic (Dx-RAI) scan] and TFB PET imaging. The remaining 10 patients had only TFB PET imaging, whose data were used solely for biodistribution analysis.

The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No: E-83045809-604.01.02-93530). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: histopathologically confirmed DTC after total or near-total thyroidectomy, age ≥ 15 years, and endogenously stimulated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) level ≥ 30 mIU/L.

The exclusion criteria included a diagnosis of iodine-refractory metastatic DTC, presence of a secondary malignancy or pregnancy, and age < 15 years.

Patients were categorized into four subgroups according to the order of RAI scanning and TFB PET imaging:

Group A1 (TFB PET & Tx-RAI scan comparison): This group comprises 20 patients (14 female, 6 male) who underwent TFB PET imaging before the first (n = 14) or subsequent (n = 6) RAIT(s). The administered therapeutic RAI activity was 4.68 ± 1.88 GBq (range: 1.85–7.40 GBq). The time between TFB PET imaging and RAI treatment was on average 18.4 ± 11.9 days.

Group A2 (TFB PET & Pre-Dx-RAI scan comparison): This group included 8 patients (5 females, 3 males) with TFB PET imaging performed 3 days before the Dx-RAI scan. All patients received a diagnostic RAI activity of 185 MBq. All TFB PET and Dx-RAI scans were performed approximately 6 months after the last RAIT.

Group B1 (post-Tx-RAI scan & TFB PET comparison): Six patients (5 female, 1 male) were available in this group, 5 of whom underwent TFB PET after initial RAI treatment and 1 after the second RAI treatment. The administered therapeutic RAI activity was 5.71 ± 0.91 GBq (range: 4.63–7.40 GBq). The mean interval between RAI treatment and TFB PET imaging was 2.8 ± 1.3 days (range 1–4 days).

Group B2 (Post-Dx-RAI scan & TFB PET comparison): This group included 14 patients (10 females, 4 males) who underwent TFB PET imaging 4 days after the Dx-RAI scan. All patients received a diagnostic RAI activity of 185 MBq. All TFB PET and Dx-RAI scans were performed 6 months after the last RAIT.

Groups A1 and A2 consisted of patients who underwent TFB PET imaging before a recent RAI (diagnostic or therapeutic activity) administration (preradioiodine groups), in whom RAI appears had no effect on TFB uptake. In contrast, group B1 and group B2 patients underwent TFB PET imaging after a recent radioiodine application within one week (postradioiodine groups), in whom there would be a possibility of RAI stunning effect on the TFB uptake in the lesions.

Imaging

RAI scanning and TFB PET imaging were performed under endogenous TSH stimulation (> 30 mIU/L). PET was acquired 40–60 min after injection of 111–222 MBq TFB, using either a PET/CT (GE Signa DST 710 PET/CT, GE Healthcare, Waukesha, Wisconsin, USA or Siemens Horizon PET/CT, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) or a dedicated PET/MR system (GE Signa PET/MR, GE Healthcare, Waukesha, Wisconsin, USA). All imaging systems were cross-calibrated according to institutional quality control protocols. Imaging covered the body area from the vertex to the mid-thigh with a scanning time of 2 min per bed position. 131I scanning included a whole-body planar (256 × 1024 matrix with a table speed 10–15 cm/min, 364-keV window) and SPECT/CT imaging from the skull base to the upper abdomen (in 128 × 128 matrix with an acquisition time of 30 s/frame over 360 degrees at 32 steps) performed using one of the two SPECT/CT scanners fitted with high-energy collimators (Symbia T6 or T16, Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) after 4–7 days of 131I administration (111–185 MBq for Dx-RAI scan and ≥ 1.11 GBq for Tx-RAI scan). No iv/oral contrast material was used for the CT or MR parts of the imaging studies. Low-dose CT acquisitions were performed and used only for attenuation correction and lesion localization to prevent unnecessary radiation burden to the patients.

TFB was prepared using an automated synthesis module in compliance with in-house magistral radiopharmaceutical synthesis procedure (TRASIS, All-in-One, Belgium). The radiochemical purity (RCP) of TFB was determined using thin-layer chromatography (TLC), and chemical purity analysis was performed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). When the European Pharmacopoeia compatibility was obtained (RCP > 95%, endotoxin, sterility, pH), the product was released for human use.

Images were independently reviewed by two experienced nuclear medicine physicians (KS and OEŞ). Any unusual focal uptake higher than liver parenchyma beyond physiological distribution was considered positive for TFB PET imaging, and any focal uptake more prominent than background activity beyond physiological biodistribution was accepted as positive for RAI scans. The final lesion classification was established by integrating imaging findings with clinical examination data, serum Tg/anti-Tg levels, and other imaging (particularly ultrasound) findings. If an inconsistency exists between the two readers, a third reader (LUB) joins the review process to reach a consensus. The SUVmax and SUVmean values were calculated for each PET-positive lesion using the vendor software.

Extra-thyroidal biodistribution properties of TFB were evaluated in 58 patients by visual inspection and calculating SUVmax and SUVmean values from the physiologically TFB-accumulating organs, including the stomach, parotid glands, esophagus, nasopharyngeal mucosa, choroid plexus, renal parenchyma, left ventricle (blood pool activity), liver parenchyma, and gluteus maximus (muscle activity).

Statistical analysis

Accuracy and sensitivity were calculated for patient-based analyses, whereas detection rates were calculated for lesion-based analyses. Diagnostic performance between TFB PET and RAI scans was compared using the exact McNemar test (lesion-based paired comparisons).

The diagnostic performance of the preradioiodine and postradioiodine subgroups (i.e., TFB-pre vs. TFB-post, and RAI-pre vs. RAI-post) was evaluated using Fisher’s exact test within each imaging modality. Diagnostic agreement between TFB PET and RAI scans was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant within a 95% confidence interval.

Results

Patient-based analysis

Among the 48 patients with comparative results of TFB and RAI imaging, both modalities were positive in 20, TFB PET alone in 1 patient, and RAI scan alone in 5, and both negative in 22 patients. This yielded substantial overall agreement (κ = 0.75). The agreement was almost perfect in the preradioiodine subgroup (κ = 0.84), but only moderate in the postradioiodine subgroup (κ = 0.49). Table 2 presents the patient-based analysis results by groups, including sensitivity and accuracy values for each group and subgroups. No significant association was observed between administered RAI activity and imaging findings or intermodality agreement.

Discordant findings

Six discordant cases were identified. In one patient (TFB+/RAI-), a temporal bone metastasis was visualized on TFB PET but negative on RAI scan, which was confirmed by MRI (Fig. 1). Conversely, four cases (RAI+/TFB-) were observed in the post-therapy group (B1), where the sensitivity of TFB was likely compromised by recent 131I administration. Figure 2 demonstrates a lymph node metastasis with positive RAI uptake and negative TFB PET findings in another patient in pretherapy (A1) group.

Lesion-based analysis

In Group A1 (Pre-Tx-RAI scan TFB PET), a total of 46 lesions were identified in 20 patients, including 1 bone metastasis, 6 lymph node lesions, and 39 remnant/residual lesions or local recurrence. Of these, 29 lesions (63.0%) were detected by both TFB PET and Tx-RAI scan, 15 (32.6%) were detected solely by TFB PET, and 2 (4.3%) were detected exclusively by Tx-RAI scan. The TFB PET detection rate was 95.7%, whereas the Tx-RAI scan detection rate was 67.4%.

In Group A2 (Pre-Dx-RAI scan TFB PET), 8 patients were included. Only 1 lesion, categorized as remnant/residual lesion or local recurrence, was identified. This lesion was detected by both TFB PET and Dx-RAI scan, resulting in a detection rate of 100.0% for both modalities.

In Group B1 (post-Tx-RAI scan TFB PET), 6 patients were evaluated. A total of 5 lesions were identified, all of which were categorized as remnant/residual lesion or local recurrence. Among these, 1 lesion (20.0%) was detected by both TFB PET and Tx-RAI scan, whereas 4 lesions (80.0%) were detected exclusively by Tx-RAI scan. No lesions were exclusively detected by TFB PET. The TFB PET detection rate in this group was 20.0%, compared with 100.0% for the Tx-RAI scan.

In Group B2 (post-Dx-RAI scan TFB PET), 14 patients were evaluated. A total of 4 lesions, all remnant/residual lesions or local recurrence, were identified. Of these, 2 lesions (50.0%) were detected by both TFB PET and Dx-RAI scan, whereas the other 2 lesions (50.0%) were detected exclusively by Dx-RAI scan. No lesions were exclusively detected by TFB PET. The TFB PET detection rate in this group was 50.0%, whereas the Dx-RAI scan achieved 100.0%.

Overall, RAI scans detected 32/47 (68.1%) lesions in the preradioiodine subgroup versus 9/9 (100.0%) lesions in the postradioiodine subgroup (p = 0.094, Fisher’s exact test), and TFB detected 45/47 (95.7%) lesions in the preradioiodine subgroup and 3/9 (33.3%) in the postradioiodine subgroup (p < 0.001, Fisher’s exact test). Overall detection rates were comparable (85.7% for TFB vs. 73.2% for 131I, p = 0.210, Exact McNemar Test) but significantly higher for TFB in the preradioiodine subgroup (95.7% vs. 68.1%, p = 0.002, Exact McNemar Test) in lesion-based analysis (56 lesions). On TFB PET imaging, SUVmax and SUVmean values were calculated from the lesions, and their mean values were 42.12 ± 54.47 and 26.92 ± 36.15, respectively. In addition to disease-related lesions, atypical foci of increased TFB beyond normal biodistribution were found in 19 female patients, 18 of which were within the uterus with a mean SUVmax value of 19.76 ± 10.03 and corresponded to Nabothian cysts, and one lesion with an SUVmax value of 7.21 is located within the breast, compatible with fibroadenoma (Fig. 3).

Biodistribution analysis

Biodistribution analysis showed that the stomach had the highest uptake, followed by the salivary glands, esophagus, and nasopharynx (Table 4; Fig. 4). Additionally, mild to moderate uptake in the kidneys, choroid plexus, and blood pool as well as breast parenchyma in some women. The bladder and ureters also showed intense accumulation because of intense urinary excretion. Furthermore, an unexpected uterine uptake corresponding to Nabothian cysts was observed in 18 (45%) of 40 female patients in this cohort. In addition, mild physiological uptake may be observed in breast tissue in some female patients and in the scrotal region in some male patients.

Discussion

The potential of TFB PET imaging in DTCs has been demonstrated in some preliminary studies. Dittmann et al. (2020) reported that TFB PET detected recurrent or metastatic DTC in significantly more patients than 131I scans (52.0% vs. 12.0%, p = 0.002) [9]. Ventura et al. (2024) demonstrated a strong concordance between TFB PET and 131I scans in patient- and lesion-based analyses, with comparable detection rates [10].

Our study involved a larger cohort of 48 patients and compared the two modalities not only for their ability to detect recurrent or metastatic disease but also for remnant/residual tissue detection, while additionally assessing the potential stunning effect of RAI on subsequent TFB uptake. TFB PET demonstrated significantly higher lesion detection rates than 131I SPECT in patients without prior RAI administration, most likely due to the superior spatial resolution of PET, which enables better detection of small lesions. However, there was a single exceptional case with RAI+/TFB− lymph node metastasis. This suggests that, although rare, discordant findings may occur due to differences in tracer kinetics and organification mechanisms between TFB and radioiodine. Notably, the advantage of TFB was lost in patients with prior RAI administration, as TFB sensitivity declined dramatically in those who underwent RAI before PET, with the lesion detection rate decreasing from 95.7% to 33.3%. While this is often termed ‘stunning,’ the short interval (1–4 days) suggests an acute radiation-induced suppression of NIS function. Clinicians should prioritize TFB PET/CT before any radioiodine administration to ensure optimal lesion detection. In contrast, we did not observe any significant effect of TFB on RAI scans, as there was no significant change in the lesion detection rate of 131I scanning between the preradioiodine and postradioiodine groups. Moreover, patient-based 131I scan sensitivity remained high across both groups (94.7% vs. 100.0%), indicating that organification-independent TFB metabolism does not impair subsequent radioiodine uptake.

The physiological distribution of TFB was consistent with that of other NIS tracers, with the highest uptake in the stomach, esophagus, salivary glands, nasopharynx, and urinary collecting organs. Uterine uptake associated with Nabothian cysts was observed in 45.0% of the female patients. Previous studies have reported a detection rate of 26.3% for Nabothian cysts on radioiodine scans, and radioiodine uptake has also been described in various benign cystic lesions in different organs as well. However, the exact mechanism underlying this uptake remains unclear [11–14]. Given its characteristic anatomical location in the uterine cervix, this finding is unlikely to be confused with metastatic DTC. Because of its biodistribution with intense physiological uptake in various organs, TFB is an imperfect radiopharmaceutical for oncological purposes. However, its biodistribution appears acceptable in the detection of DTC metastasis due to the lack of physiological uptake in the lungs and lymph nodes. The literature data revealed that the radiation burden of TFB to patients is lower than that of RAI scans [6].

The present study has several limitations. First, its single-center design and relatively small sample size within the subgroups (particularly the post-RAI groups), may preclude a more robust statistical analysis. Nevertheless, this cohort represents one of the larger prospective evaluations of TFB in DTC to date. Second, there was a lack of histopathological confirmation for all identified lesions. We attempted to mitigate this by using a composite reference standard integrating clinical examination and laboratory data (Tg and anti-Tg), and ultrasound findings; however, the risk of incorporation bias remains. Third, the use of a combination of PET/CT and PET/MR systems, while cross-calibrated, may introduce minor variations in SUV measurements. Furthermore, logistical constraints regarding 18F procurement affected our workflow. Because of the unavailability of an on-site cyclotron, we obtained the isotope solution from a commercial producer on a bi-weekly basis. This influenced patient scheduling, particularly the imaging order and the time interval between TFB PET and RAI imaging, resulting in non-standardized imaging intervals. Therefore, some otherwise eligible patients were not included in order to avoid delays in their planned RAI treatment. Consequently, our current dataset does not allow definitive conclusions regarding the optimal timing between 131I administration and subsequent TFB PET imaging, and further prospective studies with standardized imaging intervals are needed.

Conclusion

TFB is a promising NIS-targeted PET radiotracer with superior lesion detectability. It appears to be a good alternative to RAI scanning for evaluating patients with DTC with lower radiation burden. Its diagnostic performance is optimal before radioiodine administration but is impaired afterward, likely due to a stunning effect of 131I on TFB uptake. Further multicenter studies are needed to clarify its exact role in the management of patients with DTC and to assess the best imaging time and length of the stunning period caused by prior radioiodine administration.
