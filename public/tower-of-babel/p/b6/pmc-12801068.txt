Pedicled Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction and Lateral Extra-articular Tenodesis Without Additional Implants—“PARLET” Technique
Authors: Piotr Wodzinski, Tomasz Zoraw, Andrzej Wielgus, Boguslaw Sadlik, Szymon Dragan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite constant development of surgical techniques, the outcomes of anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstructions remain unpredictable, and the failure rate remains unacceptably high. Among many factors influencing results, the graft-healing process called ligamentization has recently been highly emphasized. Improvement of healing potential, connected with graft diameter optimization and the addition of lateral extra-articular tenodesis, might lead to superior treatment results. We describe a simple technique of combined anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction with a pedicled hamstring graft of case-specific diameter and lateral extra-articular tenodesis with limited hardware. “PARLET” stands for pedicled anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction and lateral extra-articular tenodesis without additional implants. Three options of graft preparation (3, 4, or 6 strands) enable diameter versatility and optimization during surgery. Preserving the hamstring pedicle and limiting its interference with tibial fixation due to the use of an ACL adjustable cortical button might improve graft healing potential. Fixation of lateral tenodesis with a femoral ACL cortical button solves the problem of tunnel or implant interference.

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction is one of the most common orthopaedic procedures. Unpredictable results, an unacceptably high rerupture rate, and a low return-to-sport percentage encourage constant development and research.1 Previous studies focused mainly on graft strength and mechanical properties. Despite that the mechanical properties of commonly used grafts are equal to or even better than those of the native ACL, the risk of rerupture is surprisingly high. This emphasizes the importance of biology and the healing process, known as ligamentization. Many techniques have been described to improve graft-healing biology. Since significant vascularization of the pes anserinus was presented by Zaffagnini et al.,2 pedicled hamstring grafts have been gaining popularity.3 The difference between complete and partial ACL tears was also highlighted. Sonnery-Cottet et al.4 presented the single anteromedial biological bundle augmentation technique with significant preservation of the ACL remnant (for partial ligament reconstruction), assuming that reinforcing even a partially existing ligament is better than its complete replacement. Some authors also believe that the optimal ACL graft diameter should be case-dependent. Acute partial tears require smaller grafts than chronic or complete ruptures.5 We present our technique of ACL reconstruction with a pedicled, folded hamstring graft fixed with 2 adjustable ACL cortical buttons (cortical button) and lateral extra-articular tenodesis (LET) without additional implants, as presented in Video 1. “PARLET” stands for pedicled anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction and LET without additional implants. Depending on the desired graft diameter, a 3-, 4-, or 6-strand graft can be easily obtained. Our indications for LET are highlighted in Table 1. Advantages and disadvantages of our technique are described in Table 2. Pearls and pitfalls are presented in Table 3.

Surgical Technique

Setup and Arthroscopy

The patient is placed in a standard arthroscopic setup, with an Esmarch tourniquet installed. A high anterolateral arthroscopic portal is created, and a camera is introduced into the joint. An anteromedial portal is created under arthroscopic visualization, and joint inspection is performed. Any concomitant injuries are treated, if required. The ACL injury is assessed, and the optimal graft size is selected. We suggest a graft diameter of 7.5 to 8.5 mm in partial tears with good remnant quality and 9 mm and above in complete ruptures.

Graft Harvesting and Preparation

A standard 2- to 2.5-cm incision over the pes anserinus is performed, and the semitendinosus tendon is harvested (Open-Ended Tendon Stripper; Biomet) with its insertion left intact. The graft is prepared for reconstruction. A marker is placed 4 cm from the tibial insertion (2 cm for the pedicle and 2 cm for tibial tunnel entry) (Fig 1). Using 2 sutures, the graft is bent twice, creating 3 strands, and stretched until the bottom of the graft reaches the level of the marked line (Fig 2). Length and diameter of the tripled part are measured, and the graft type is selected according to the decision tree (Fig 3). Then, the graft is prepared for reconstruction with one of the following options.

Graft preparation, right knee, with the harvesting approach. After harvesting the semitendinosus tendon (St), a colored mark is placed 4 cm from the tibial insertion (4-cm mark).

Graft measurement, right knee. The semitendinosus tendon is bent using 2 sutures to match length until the bottom of the graft reaches the marked line, and then the graft diameter and length are measured (3ST).

Decision tree—graft type choice, depending on length and diameter.

Option 1: Tripled Semitendinosus Graft (Pedicled 3-Strand Graft). The first cortical button (FiberTag TightRope; Arthrex) is sutured, according to the manual, to the free end of the semitendinosus tendon (Fig 4). Then, the second cortical button (Arthrex TightRope RT; Arthrex) is prepared. Passing sutures of the first cortical button are pulled through the loop of the second button, and then the second button is passed down toward the hamstring insertion. Passing sutures of the second cortical button are then pulled through the loop of the first button until the tendon bends, creating a tripled semitendinosus graft. Its length is controlled by pulling each implant until the lower part reaches a marking level. Now the construct consists of a 4-cm single pedicle and tripled graft between 2 cortical buttons (Fig 5). In this configuration, the first cortical button (FiberTag TightRope) is femoral, and the second (TightRope RT) is a tibial button.

Graft preparation, right knee—suturing the free end of the semitendinosus (St) with FiberTag TightRope (Arthrex) implant (FT).

Prepared graft—tripled, pedicled semitendinosus tendon (3ST) graft with FiberTag TightRope (Arthrex) (FT) and TightRope (TR) implant attached, right knee.

Option 2—Quadrupled Semitendinosus Graft (Pedicled 4-Strand Graft). If the next bending is decided, the step of passing the pulling sutures through the loop is repeated, creating a quadrupled graft with a pedicle (Fig 6). In this configuration, the first cortical button (FiberTag TightRope) is tibial, and the second (TightRope RT) is a femoral button.

Prepared graft—quadrupled, pedicled semitendinosus tendon (4ST) graft with FiberTag TightRope (Arthrex) (FT) and TightRope (TR) implant attached, right knee.

Option 3—Tripled Semitendinosus and Gracilis Graft (Pedicled 6-Strand Graft). The gracilis tendon is harvested in a standard manner and prepared for reconstruction. The semitendinosus tendon is shortened to the length of the gracilis tendon. Preparation according to option 1 is proceeded, but with 2 tendons connected with free ends (Fig 7). In this configuration, the first cortical button (FiberTag TightRope) is femoral, and the second (TightRope RT) is a tibial button.

Prepared graft—tripled, pedicled semitendinosus and gracilis tendon graft (3ST3G) (6 strands) with FiberTag TightRope (Arthrex) (FT) and TightRope (TR) implant attached, right knee.

Lateral Approach and Iliotibial Band Preparation for Lateral Extra-articular Tenodesis

The lateral approach is performed—about a 4-cm longitudinal cut slightly posterior to the lateral femoral epicondyle, and the iliotibial band (ITB) is exposed. Starting slightly below the lateral epicondyle, a strip of the ITB is prepared—about 7 cm in length, measured from Gerdy’s tubercle, and 1 cm in width. Then, the strip is cut proximally (Fig 8). The free end is whipstitched. If the “deep to the lateral collateral ligament” technique is preferred, the strip is passed deep to the lateral collateral ligament in a standard manner.

Iliotibial band strip (ITBS) preparation for lateral extra-articular tenodesis—right knee, lateral femoral approach (LA).

Femoral Tunnel Preparation

An outside-in femoral aimer (Footprint Femoral ACL Guide; Arthrex) is introduced through the anterolateral portal and pointed to the anatomic ACL footprint. A drill guide system for the FlipCutter (FlipCutter III; Arthrex) is connected through a lateral approach. After the guide sleeve is pushed to the proximal anterolateral ligament insertion point, slightly proximal and posterior to the lateral femoral epicondyle, the FlipCutter drill is introduced into the joint under arthroscopic visualization (Fig 9). After confirmation of the proper position, the FlipCutter is opened to the measured graft diameter, and a guide sleeve is secured to the bone. Retro-drilling is performed, creating a 2-cm femoral socket. The drill is pushed back to the joint and closed to a 3.5-mm size, then removed. The joint is debrided using a shaver. An ACL shuttling suture is passed through the guide sleeve and femoral tunnel from the lateral approach, with the blunt end of the guidewire inside the joint and retrieved with a suture grasper to an anteromedial arthroscopic portal. A guide sleeve is removed from the lateral approach.

Femoral tunnel preparation for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction—right knee, FlipCutter (Arthrex) drill guide (FLC) in the lateral femoral approach (LA) pointing to the proximal anterolateral ligament insertion (posterior and proximal to the lateral epicondyle).

Tibial Tunnel and Cortical Button Preparation

A tibial aimer (Arthrex) is inserted into the joint through the anteromedial portal and placed in a proper position—inside the tibial ACL footprint. A guide sleeve is connected, and a guidewire is introduced into the ACL remnant under arthroscopic visualization. After confirmation of proper position, the tibial tunnel is drilled with a reamer equal to the graft diameter with preservation of the ACL remnant. The tibial tunnel and the joint are debrided with a shaver. An ACL shuttling suture retrieved previously from the anteromedial portal is now pulled through the tibial tunnel (Fig 10).

Shuttling suture passage—right knee, arthroscopic view. A shuttling suture (ShS) is passed through the femoral tunnel (Fem) for anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, obtained through the tibial tunnel (Tib).

Femoral Cortical Button Passage

Passing sutures of the femoral cortical button are pulled through the loop of the ACL shuttling suture. Free ends of ACL shuttling suture are pulled from the lateral approach, and then button passing sutures are retrieved in the lateral approach. Sutures are pulled with arthroscopic control until the femoral cortical button is above the femoral cortex in the lateral approach (Fig 11).

Passing the femoral endobutton in the lateral femoral approach—right knee. After pulling the shuttling suture, a femoral cortical button (FB) connected to the hamstring graft is passed through tibial and femoral tunnels and obtained in the lateral femoral approach (LA), where the iliotibial band strip (ITBS) is prepared for tenodesis.

Lateral Extra-articular Tenodesis and Femoral Anterior Cruciate Ligament Blocking

Sutures used to whipstitch the LET graft are pulled through the loop sutures of the femoral cortical button with the ITB graft (Fig 12). The knee is placed in full extension, with neutral rotation. Slight tension is placed in a proximal direction on the LET graft sutures. By alternately pulling the cinching shortening sutures of the femoral button, it approaches the lateral femoral cortex using a staple-like method, which allows the LET graft to block and advances the ACL graft into the femoral tunnel (Fig 13).

Lateral extra-articular tenodesis before blocking—right knee, lateral femoral approach (LA). Whipstitch sutures with the iliotibial band strip (ITBS) are pulled through loop sutures (LS) of the femoral cortical button (FB) before blocking.

Lateral extra-articular tenodesis and femoral anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction blocking—right knee in full extension, neutral rotation. With the iliotibial band strip (ITBS) being pulled proximally with slight tension, cinching shortening sutures of the femoral cortical button (FB) are alternately pulled to advance the femoral button through the lateral approach (LA) toward the lateral femoral cortex. This blocks the lateral extra-articular tenodesis while advancing the anterior cruciate ligament graft intra-articularly to the femoral tunnel.

Tibial Anterior Cruciate Ligament Blocking

The TightRope extender (Arthrex) is connected to the tibial cortical button (Fig 14). After arthroscopic confirmation of proper graft placement in the femoral tunnel, with the knee in full extension and neutral rotation, cinching shortening sutures of the second cortical button with the extender are pulled, advancing the button toward the tibial cortex and tightening the graft (Fig 15). When tightening is finished, knee range of motion and stability are assessed. Secondary LET fixation is performed by bending the ITB strip over the femoral cortical button and tying it with its shuttling sutures. The ITB is carefully closed, and the rest of the incisions are closed in a standard manner.

Button extender (Ext) installation to the tibial cortical button (TB)—right knee, near harvesting approach (HA). The blue passing suture of the tibial cortical button is pulled through the hole inside the button extender, and the open end of the extender is passed through the tibial cortical button loop sutures.

Tibial blocking of anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction—right knee, inside harvesting approach (HA). After alternate pulling of cinching shortening sutures, the tibial cortical button (TB) with the connected extender is advanced toward the tibial cortex, blocking the anterior cruciate ligament on the tibial side. Care was taken to place the tibial cortical button on the superior part of the tibial tunnel entrance to leave the hamstring pedicle (Ped) intact and without compression.

Discussion

The presented technique offers easy and predictable solutions addressing factors highlighted earlier. Preserving the insertion of intact tendons may enhance graft vascularization, improve healing, and significantly increase tibial fixation strength as a secondary fixation.6 Use of a cortical button instead of a screw in tibial fixation limits pedicled graft compression, which might increase preservation of vascularization, as presented by Iglesias et al.7 The STABILITY group confirmed that adding LET to ACL reconstruction reduces the graft rerupture rate and residual rotational instability without compromising functional results.8,9 Many extra-articular tenodesis techniques have been described, with different femoral fixation methods. The technique described by Koukoulias et al.,10 adopted in our technique, with fixation of the iliotibial band graft in the modified Lemaire procedure by the femoral ACL cortical button, solves the problem of potential implant or tunnel interference. Choice of graft preparation methods enables obtaining various diameters and lengths and, as a result, more case-tuned ACL grafts. We believe that enhancing biological healing potential, improving mechanical fixation properties, and facilitating versatile graft size preparation will lead to improved surgical outcomes.
