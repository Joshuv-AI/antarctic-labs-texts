Preterm birth and the association with neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) in children born in Pakistan: a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Arsalan Tariq, Abdul Saad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the association between preterm birth and neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs) in children born in Pakistan, aged 2–6 years.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional study analysed medical and developmental records of 2634 Pakistani children aged 2–6 years, born between 2015 and 2020. Neurodevelopmental outcomes were assessed using Ages and Stages Questionnaire-3, Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised and Bayley-III, with Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition confirmation where available. Preterm birth was defined as <37 weeks, with subcategories: extremely (<28), very (28–31) and late preterm (32–36 weeks). Multivariable logistic regression examined factors associated with NDDs, adjusting for sex, maternal age, maternal education, household income and neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission. Poisson regression estimated relative risks, and the interaction between prematurity and socioeconomic status (SES) was assessed.ResultsAmong 2634 children, 730 (27.7%) were preterm. NDD prevalence was higher in preterm compared with full-term children (25.8% vs 13.4%), including autism spectrum disorder (5.8% vs 2.0%), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (7.9% vs 4.7%), cerebral palsy (2.6% vs 0.6%) and developmental delay risk (15.2% vs 9.6%). Prevalence increased with prematurity severity: extremely (55.6%), very (32.1%) and late preterm (23.9%). Multivariable analysis showed that preterm birth (adjusted OR (aOR)=1.91; 95% CI 1.48 to 2.47), low maternal education (aOR=1.44; 95% CI 1.17 to 1.78), male sex (aOR=1.34; 95% CI 1.08 to 1.66), low income (aOR=1.29; 95% CI 1.05 to 1.58) and NICU admission (aOR=1.52; 95% CI 1.18 to 1.95) were independently associated with NDDs. Children who were preterm and from low SES households had the highest odds (aOR=9.43; 95% CI 6.41 to 13.88).ConclusionPreterm birth and low SES were associated with a greater burden of NDDs in Pakistani children aged 2–6 years, with the highest prevalence observed when both were present.Keywords: Low and Middle Income Countries, Cerebral Palsy, Developing Countries, Cross-Sectional Studies, Epidemiology

Introduction

Preterm birth, defined as delivery before 37 completed weeks of gestation, is a leading cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality worldwide, with an estimated global prevalence of 10.6%.1 Despite medical advances improving survival rates, preterm infants remain at high risk for a range of neurodevelopmental disorders (NDDs), including autism spectrum disorder (ASD), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), cerebral palsy (CP) and learning disabilities.2 These conditions often result from disruptions in brain development during critical periods of maturation, compounded by medical complications and environmental stressors.

Numerous studies have shown that the risk and severity of NDDs increase with decreasing gestational age, particularly among those born very preterm (<32 weeks) or extremely preterm (<28 weeks).3 Neonatal complications such as intraventricular haemorrhage, hypoxic-ischaemic injury and sepsis further contribute to adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes.4 Moreover, low birth weight and prolonged stays in neonatal intensive care units (NICUs) are associated with increased cognitive, behavioural and motor impairments later in life.5

Socioeconomic factors significantly mediate the impact of preterm birth on neurodevelopment.6 Infants born to families with limited access to healthcare, poor maternal nutrition and low education levels are at greater risk of poor outcomes due to reduced access to early interventions and supportive services.7 This underscores the role of social determinants in amplifying biological vulnerabilities.

Pakistan ranks among the top 10 countries with the highest number of preterm births, with an estimated 748 100 preterm births annually, representing approximately 14% of all live births.8 The country faces unique challenges, including limited neonatal care infrastructure, high rates of maternal malnutrition and significant disparities in healthcare access, particularly in rural regions. These factors contribute to elevated neonatal mortality and long-term developmental issues.

A study conducted in Pakistan found that preterm birth was significantly associated with poor neurodevelopmental outcomes by the age of two, including cognitive delays and motor dysfunction.9 Additionally, community-based data from low-income urban settlements showed increased prevalence of developmental delays and learning difficulties in children with a history of preterm birth, particularly where early interventions are lacking.10

Preterm birth significantly increases the risk of NDDs such as ASD, ADHD, CP and learning disabilities. While this association is well documented in high-income settings, limited data exist from low- and middle-income countries such as Pakistan, where the burden of preterm births is high and access to follow-up care is limited. Socioeconomic disparities, neonatal complications and inadequate early intervention further worsen outcomes. This study addresses a critical gap by examining the epidemiological link between preterm birth and NDDs, aiming to inform early detection, intervention strategies and healthcare policies in resource-constrained settings.

Materials and methods

This study adopted a retrospective cross-sectional design, using secondary analysis of medical records and developmental screening datasets to assess the relationship between preterm birth and NDDs among Pakistani children aged 2–6 years. The research population included children born between 2015 and 2020, with documented gestational age at birth and available neurodevelopmental assessment data. Participants were selected from both urban and rural healthcare facilities across three major provinces in Pakistan (Sindh, Punjab and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa), ensuring geographical and socioeconomic diversity. The study was conducted from July to October 2024 under the department of public health and physical therapy, The University of Lahore, Lahore Pakistan.

Children between 24 and 72 months (2–6 years) were selected for inclusion because this age range is a critical window for the early detection of NDDs. By age 2, many core developmental milestones in language, motor, social and cognitive domains become assessable using validated tools such as the Ages and Stages Questionnaire (ASQ-3) and the Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers, Revised (M-CHAT-R). Early identification within this period is essential for timely interventions, which are shown to significantly improve long-term outcomes in children with NDDs.11

The upper limit of 72 months ensures inclusion of preschool-aged children, who can also be reliably evaluated using more advanced screening tools or clinical diagnostic criteria for conditions like ASD, ADHD, CP and global developmental delay.12 These disorders were selected due to their high public health relevance, early onset and impact on multiple developmental domains. They also have established diagnostic frameworks and screening protocols suitable for both clinical and population-based research in early childhood.13

Exclusion criteria included children with congenital anomalies or genetic syndromes not associated with prematurity, such as Down syndrome, as well as incomplete records lacking key information about birth history or neurodevelopmental outcomes. Additionally, children without available neurodevelopmental assessment data by the age of 2 years were excluded.

Children were selected using consecutive sampling from hospital and community developmental registers during the study period. Hospital-based records were reviewed from public and private tertiary care institutions in Karachi, Lahore and Peshawar. Community-level data were obtained from national health sources, including the Pakistan Demographic and Health Survey14 and the Lady Health Worker Programme.15 In addition, developmental screening data from early childhood intervention centres supported by provincial health departments and local non-governmental organizations (NGOs) were incorporated. This approach yielded a final sample of approximately 2723 children.

A post hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power (V.3.1) to assess whether the available sample size (n=2723) was sufficient to detect an association between preterm birth and NDDs using logistic regression. The analysis was based on a conservative estimated effect size (OR=1.2), selected in line with recent epidemiological studies reporting modest but significant associations between prematurity and neurodevelopmental outcomes.16 17 A two-tailed alpha level of 0.05 and an anticipated event rate of approximately 20% among preterm children were assumed based on contemporary prevalence estimates in similar populations.1 18 An R² value of 0.1 was included to account for the contribution of additional covariates (eg, sex, maternal education and socioeconomic status (SES)) in the regression model. Under these assumptions, the calculated statistical power was 0.95, indicating that the study was adequately powered to detect a statistically significant association at the specified effect size.

Data were collected using a structured abstraction form, which recorded demographic variables, birth history and developmental outcomes. Demographic and perinatal information included gestational age in weeks, birth weight, type of delivery, presence of neonatal complications such as NICU admission, sepsis and intraventricular haemorrhage, as well as child’s sex, maternal age and education level, family income, occupation and place of residence. SES was classified based on household income, maternal education and housing conditions. It was computed using a composite index that combined maternal education level (no education, primary, secondary or higher), monthly household income (categorised into tertiles) and housing quality (type of housing and access to basic utilities). Neurodevelopmental outcomes were measured using standardised instruments. These included the ASQ-3, a parent-reported tool evaluating communication, motor skills, problem-solving and socioemotional development. Children scoring≥2 SD below the mean on any ASQ-3 domain were classified as at-risk for delay, based on standard cutoffs,19 Third Edition (Bayley-III), used in clinical settings to assess cognitive and motor functioning; and the Modified Checklist for Autism in Toddlers cognitive or motor composite scores<85 (ie, >1 SD below the mean of 100) were considered indicative of developmental delay,20 (M-CHAT-R), used for autism risk screening in children aged 18 to 36 months a score of ≥3 triggered further follow-up screening, while scores≥8 indicated high risk for autism.21 When available, diagnoses were confirmed using the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition criteria, assessed by paediatric neurologists or psychologists. The Gross Motor Function Classification System was used to assess the severity of CP.22 In the absence of formal diagnosis, standardised cutoffs from tools such as ASQ and M-CHAT-R were used to identify children at risk of NDDs.

Preterm birth was defined as delivery before 37 completed weeks of gestation. Subcategories were classified according to standard definitions: extremely preterm (<28 weeks), very preterm (28–31 weeks) and late preterm (32–36 weeks). Full-term birth was defined as delivery at or after 37 weeks and post-term birth as ≥42 weeks.23 Gestational age was primarily based on early ultrasound reports or maternal health booklets when available. These categorisations were used to examine graded relationships between gestational maturity and neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Data were double-entered in Microsoft Excel and cleaned before being imported into SPSS (V.25) for analysis. Descriptive statistics were summarised as means and SD for continuous variables and frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. Group comparisons between preterm and full-term children were performed using independent samples t-tests for continuous variables and Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Multivariable logistic regression was used to identify factors independently associated with NDDs, adjusting for sex, maternal age, maternal education, household income and NICU admission. Adjusted ORs (aORs) with 95% CIs were reported. Given the relatively high prevalence of NDD, Poisson regression with robust standard errors was additionally applied to estimate adjusted relative risks (aRRs). Separate models were also fitted for individual outcomes. An interaction term between preterm birth and SES was included to assess effect modification. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with all variables showing VIF values<2, indicating no evidence of significant multicollinearity. A p value<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To address missing data, records lacking critical variables such as gestational age or neurodevelopmental outcomes were excluded from analysis. For non-critical variables, pairwise deletion was applied. All analyses were conducted using complete-case data.

Other significant factors included male sex (aOR=1.34; 95% CI 1.08 to 1.66; p=0.007), low maternal education (aOR=1.44; 95% CI 1.17 to 1.78; p=0.001), low household income (aOR=1.29; 95% CI 1.05 to 1.58; p=0.014) and NICU admission (aOR=1.52; 95% CI 1.18 to 1.95; p=0.001). To further explore outcome-specific associations, separate multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed for ADHD, risk of developmental delay, ASD and CP (online supplemental table S1). Preterm birth consistently emerged as a strong independent factor associated with all outcomes, with the highest effect observed for CP (aOR=3.10; 95% CI 1.25 to 7.68; p=0.015), followed by ASD (aOR=2.35; 95% CI 1.28 to 4.32; p=0.006), ADHD (aOR=1.72; 95% CI 1.21 to 2.44; p=0.003) and developmental delay (aOR=1.65; 95% CI 1.18 to 2.31; p=0.004). NICU admission and low maternal education were also significantly associated with increased odds of ADHD and developmental delay, while other covariates showed weaker or non-significant associations across outcomes. These findings reinforce the robustness and consistency of the association between preterm birth and multiple neurodevelopmental domains.

Table 4 shows the prevalence of NDDs and risk for developmental delay according to gestational age. Among 2634 children, those born extremely preterm (<28 weeks, n=45) had the highest prevalence of any NDD (55.6%), with 11.1% affected by ASD, 13.3% by ADHD, 6.7% by CP and 24.4% at risk for developmental delay. Very preterm children (28–31 weeks, n=53) showed 32.1% with any NDD, including 7.5% ASD, 13.2% ADHD, 3.8% CP and 17.0% at risk. Late preterm children (32–36 weeks, n=632) had 23.9% with any NDD, 5.2% ASD, 7.1% ADHD, 2.2% CP and 10.6% at risk. Full-term children (≥37 weeks, n=1904) had the lowest prevalence, with 13.4% affected by any NDD, 2.0% ASD, 4.7% ADHD, 0.6% CP and 9.6% at risk. Overall, table 4 highlights a clear inverse relationship between gestational age and neurodevelopmental outcomes, showing that lower gestational age is associated with higher rates of NDDs and developmental risk.

Discussion

This study demonstrates a strong association between preterm birth and increased odds of NDDs in Pakistani children aged 2–6 years. Preterm children showed substantially higher odds of NDDs compared with full-term children. These findings are consistent with international literature, which has consistently reported elevated risks of NDDs among preterm infants.24 In particular, the observed increased risk of ASD and ADHD in preterm children aligns with prior meta-analyses indicating a 2–3-fold higher risk among preterm populations.25

The gradient of risk observed across gestational subcategories in our sample, highest among extremely preterm (<28 weeks), also corroborates earlier research. Studies have shown that early gestational age is associated with increased vulnerability to white matter injury and altered brain connectivity, particularly in areas related to executive function and motor coordination.3 26 For instance, children born extremely preterm exhibited the highest risk of NDDs, mirroring global findings.27

SES emerged as a critical modifier of NDD odds. Our data show a significantly higher prevalence of NDDs among children from low-income households (26.4%) compared with middle (12.3%) and high SES (6.0%) groups. The interaction between prematurity and low SES further compounded the odds, with children who were both preterm and from low SES backgrounds having a 36.9% NDD prevalence. This is consistent with previous study emphasising the cumulative burden of biological and social risk factors.28

Multivariable logistic regression showed that preterm birth (aOR 1.91), NICU admission (aOR 1.52), low maternal education (aOR 1.44), low income (aOR 1.29) and male sex (aOR 1.34) were independently associated with NDDs. These findings are in line with national and global studies that highlight how both biological insults and adverse social environments contribute to developmental vulnerability.29

Additionally, lower domain scores on the ASQ-3, particularly in gross motor and communication skills, indicate early signs of risk for developmental delay that may not yet manifest as diagnosable disorders. This reinforces the importance of early developmental surveillance and targeted interventions, as advocated by Glascoe and Marks,30 especially in low- and middle-income countries where routine developmental screening is often lacking.

Compared with previous studies in Pakistan, which often focused on smaller hospital-based cohorts, our study is among the first to incorporate a large, geographically diverse sample using both hospital and community data sources. This strengthens the generalisability of our findings and provides new insights into how prematurity and social disadvantage affect neurodevelopmental outcomes in early childhood.

Limitation

Although the study included both hospital and community samples from diverse regions, disparities in healthcare access and early intervention availability may have influenced detection rates and outcomes. SES was assessed using parental reports of income, education and occupation, which, while practical, may not fully capture complex dimensions such as neighbourhood deprivation, quality of education or access to developmental support services. Moreover, although common NDDs such as ASD, ADHD and CP were examined, risk of developmental delay was assessed using screening tools rather than clinical diagnosis. Additionally, the study did not capture other important domains such as specific learning disabilities or behavioural disorders, which may emerge later or fall outside the scope of the tools used.

Conclusion

Among Pakistani children aged 2–6 years, preterm birth and low SES were associated with a higher burden of NDDs, particularly among preterm children from low-income households. These findings highlight the importance of early developmental screening, timely intervention and improved follow-up care for high-risk children.
