Optimal design and operation of grid-connected hybrid microgrid system using pelican optimization algorithm
Authors: Anirban Maity, Sajjan Kumar, Pulok Pattanayak, Ramakrishna SS Nuvvula, Baseem Khan, Rajkumar Sivanraju, Ahmed Ali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents an optimal grid-connected hybrid microgrid framework aimed at addressing persistent power reliability challenges in educational institutions across developing regions, with a rural university campus in West Bengal, India, serving as the case study. Frequent grid outages have led to a heavy reliance on diesel generators, resulting in elevated energy costs ₹11.74/kWh (≈ USD 0.14/kWh) and high annual CO₂ emissions (650.37 metric tons). To overcome these issues, a hybrid system architecture combining solar photovoltaic (PV), wind turbines (WT), and existing diesel generator (DG) infrastructure is proposed. Component sizing is optimized using the recently developed Pelican Optimization Algorithm (POA), which demonstrates superior convergence characteristics and solution quality. Simulation results based on real meteorological and load data confirm that the integrated PV–WT–DG–grid system achieves a 42% reduction in energy cost, lowering the Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE) to ₹6.82/kWh (≈ USD 0.082/kWh), while simultaneously reducing carbon emissions by 83%, down to 109.72 metric tons/year. The findings highlight POA’s suitability for microgrid design under real-world conditions and emphasize the economic and environmental viability of renewable-dominated hybrid systems. The proposed approach provides a replicable and vendor-oriented model for institutions in similar resource-constrained settings, offering valuable insights for policymakers and energy planners working toward reliable, low-carbon energy solutions in weak-grid or remote areas.

Introduction

A nation’s development index is intricately tied to its power consumption, and in the case of India, the surge in power demand over the last few decades is indicative of growth propelled by a large population and expanding industries. Conventional energy resources such as coal and crude oil are experiencing escalating costs and exerting significant adverse effects on human living environments1. Energy harvesting technologies has also emerged as complementary approaches for distributed and sustainable energy applications2. These factors serve as primary motivations for transitioning energy generation towards renewable source3. As conventional power infrastructure continues to age and the demand for higher power quality escalates, the integration of renewable energy sources (RESs)—such as wind, solar, hydro, biomass, and geothermal energy—as well as alternative fuels like hydrogen, has emerged as a vital strategy for promoting sustainable development and addressing global energy challenges4–8. According to the latest findings presented in the Ministry of Power report from the Government of India, the total installed power generation capacity as of April 2025 stands at 476461.8 MW in the country. Fossil fuel-derived power generation capacity accounts for 247285.47 MW, representing 51.9% of the total installed capacity. Conversely, non-fossil-based power generation comprises 229176.33 MW, constituting 48% of the overall installed capacity9. These figures underscore a discernible shift towards increased reliance on RESs in the recent trajectory of power generation trends in India10.

A microgrid, embodying a scaled-down version of the conventional electric grid and aligning with its “micro” connotation, lacks clear historical origins. According to the study, Consortium for Electric Reliability Technology Solutions (CERTS) has gained acknowledgment as the pioneer of the contemporary grid-tied microgrid concept, as referenced in11,12. The concept envisions a microgrid capable of integrating various Distributed Energy Resources (DERs) while seamlessly appearing to the existing grid as a conventional customer or small generator. This approach aims to address and mitigate perceived challenges associated with the integration of DERs, as highlighted in13. The origins of the microgrid concept are often linked to early localized electricity supply systems. A notable example is Thomas Edison’s Pearl Street Station in Manhattan, which began operation in 1882 and supplied direct-current electricity to a defined service area in lower Manhattan. Because a self-contained local generation and distribution system, it is frequently regarded as a precursor to the modern microgrid. Historical records indicate that the station initially operated with six dynamos of about 100 kW each14. Microgrids represent a significant and preemptive step in the power sector to address existing problems, providing notable operational enhancements in comparison to traditional grid frameworks. These advantages include (i) improving the stability of power networks, (ii) enhancing efficiency by minimizing transmission and distribution losses, (iii) mitigating global warming and pollution through the adoption of low-carbon technologies, (iv) ensuring a continuous and independent supply of electric energy to all Microgrid Substations (MSs) and loads during autonomous mode, (v) supporting local power networks and facilitating generation augmentation to enhance overall system reliability and power quality, (vi) providing plug-and-play capability for seamless transitions between grid-tied and autonomous modes of operation, and (vii) serving as a reliable backup power source during main grid power failures15. While microgrids offer numerous advantages, several researchers have highlighted various disadvantages and technical challenges in their works16–18. Technical drawbacks include power imbalance, harmonics in DC microgrids, stability and power quality issues in microgrids. The continuous connection and disconnection of micro sources, loads, and electrical energy storage devices lead to topological changes. Additionally, the design and installation of microgrids demand a substantial land space and significant investment, posing a considerable burden from a socioeconomic perspective.

In the rural areas of Africa, where the implementation of a utility grid (UG) is not feasible because of low population density and economic constraints, researchers propose the design of an isolated hybrid microgrid19,20. The aim is to provide electricity to local residences, thereby enhancing economic development. The optimization of the microgrid, utilizing the HOMER simulation software, minimizes both the Net Present Cost (NPC) and the Cost of Energy (LCOE). Due to intermittent characteristics of RES, the development and implementation of a microgrid model for ensuring a dependable, high-quality power supply from isolated microgrids can pose significant challenges21,22. Due to the uncertainty of renewable resources, designing an isolated MG is more complicated. To overcome this problem and achieve a reliable and efficient system design, researchers use conventional Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) along with supercapacitors in their past studies23–26. In article27, researchers used HOMER software to identify the most suitable location out of ten sites in Africa for the installation of a PV based on-grid microgrid and found that a hybrid microgrid connected to the grid proves to be an optimal alternative for both suburban and urban settings. A research investigation conducted by AlKassem et al28. focused on the design evaluation of a cost-effective microgrid system for integrating RESs within a university campus in Saudi Arabia. The primary components of the microgrid included PV, WT and UG. The researchers aimed to identify the most efficient model among the three by employing HOMER simulation software.

Maity et al.29 investigated the optimal design of a university-campus hybrid microgrid using HOMER software, showing the techno-economic benefits of combining renewable resources with conventional backup. In a subsequent study, Maity et al.30 extended the campus microgrid framework by incorporating electric vehicle charging and Pelican optimization algorithm-based sizing, reporting further improvement in cost effectiveness and renewable contribution.

Basu et al.31 proposed a novel Snow Ablation Optimizer (SAO) for optimizing integrated power and gas systems incorporating multiple renewable sources such as PV, WT, PHES, and PEVs. Their framework demonstrated improved convergence and reduced operational cost, especially under demand response scenarios, highlighting the value of advanced metaheuristics in sustainable energy planning. Dagal et al.32 introduced the Logarithmic Mean Optimization (LMO), a non-nature-inspired algorithm, which outperformed several traditional metaheuristics on both benchmark functions and a real-world hybrid PV-wind system. The study emphasized LMO’s robustness in handling convergence speed, parameter tuning, and optimization accuracy in renewable applications. Shaikh et al.33 developed a hybrid Grey Wolf–Particle Swarm Optimization (HGWPSO) algorithm that combined the exploration strength of Grey Wolf Optimization (GWO) with the fast convergence of particle swarm optimization (PSO). Their method showed enhanced performance in benchmark tests and complex engineering design tasks, underlining the effectiveness of hybrid algorithms in solving multi-dimensional energy optimization problems. In article34, the researchers planed a grid-connected MG for reliable electrification at remote region of Uttarakhand, India. By using the PSO, they performed four different type of case study and suggest the best solution which have less NPC and least levelized cost of energy. A hybrid photovoltaic/wind system is proposed by Ali Kashefi Kaviani et al. in35 to meet power demand, using a PSO algorithm to minimize costs, outperforming traditional Genetic Algorithm (GA)36, presents a cognitive MG with hydrogen storage, optimized with MOPSO to reduce costs and energy losses. Optimal design of hybrid solar/wind MG systems with hydrogen storage is discussed in article37, using MOPSO to ensure seamless power supply at minimal cost under changing conditions. This research emphasizes heuristic and metaheuristic optimization for optimal MG component sizing, ensuring high power quality, reliability, and cost-effectiveness under varying weather and load conditions. Study in38, optimized the sizing of an on-grid PV/battery hybrid system by considering PV panels, BESS, and inverters as key decision variables. In a related approach, research39 introduced a grid-connected wind-solar PV microgrid integrated with biomass and storage to optimize demand fulfillment and economic dispatch. The authors employed a Hybrid Grey Wolf-Cuckoo Search Optimization (GWCSO) algorithm for system sizing, demonstrating its superiority over standard GWO in both cost efficiency and computational precision. Jayachandran el.al. in40, proposed an isolated hybrid microgrid system (HMGS) in the rural area of Sundarban (India) to enhance the economic and daily life. They used PSO to identify the sizing of WT, PV module, DG and BESS, and find the optimal configuration of the system. The researchers of article41–43, highlight AI-driven algorithms’ efficiency in cognitive microgrids, optimizing power quality, cost, and reliability under dynamic conditions, offering valuable insights for other researchers who can work in the same field in future. Arévalo et al.44 carried out an extensive review of recent studies on microgrid optimization, highlighting how artificial intelligence and emerging technologies are being used to improve energy efficiency, reliability, and real-time management. The review emphasized the growing role of reinforcement learning, predictive modeling, and hybrid energy storage in addressing challenges related to renewable energy variability. Despite notable progress, the study also pointed out gaps in scalability, integration, and adaptability of AI systems within real-world microgrid applications. In recent scholarly investigations, POA has demonstrated significant efficacy in microgrid design, despite the prevalence of optimization techniques such as GA, PSO, and GWO etc. Considering the volatility in both RERs and loads, the research endeavors to optimize energy management, yielding improved outcomes in MG stability45–47. Recent studies have examined the techno-economic and operational challenges of integrating electric vehicle infrastructure with renewable energy systems. For instance, a long-term techno-economic evaluation of electric vehicle-to-grid charging stations integrated with renewable energy generators employed a hybrid Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU)–classical optimization framework48, achieving notable gains in system stability, net present value, and operational reliability. Similarly, a comparative analysis of synchronous condensers and static synchronous compensators in renewable-integrated weak grids utilized a Hedge Feedforward Feedback optimization approach49 to enhance voltage stability and reactive power support. Another study on renewable integration in the Merredin WA mining sector50 demonstrated substantial CO₂ emission reduction and cost benefits. These works further emphasize the importance of combining advanced forecasting with robust optimization to achieve sustainable and economically efficient microgrid operations.

In this presented work, the authors tried to address the similar microgrid design issues for “The Neotia University” (TNU) which is situated in West Bengal, India. The area where the university is located faces challenges in ensuring a reliable and consistent power supply. To overcome frequent power failures and fluctuations, the institute has installed a DG set. However, rising diesel prices and increasing load demand pose sustainability challenges for the existing system. Instead of installing new DG units, a grid-connected HMGS consisting solar PV units, wind turbines, inverter, and a backup DG for energy requirements has been proposed. A recently developed POA technique has been applied here to optimize the Cost of Energy (COE) for this MG system. For this analysis, meteorological data were obtained from HOMER Pro through NASA database51 whereas the load characteristics were derived from an energy-audit-based assessment of campus residential facilities and institutional consumption patterns52.

In this research study, two perspectives are explored: one from the standpoint of institutional management and the other from a vendor company. Both entities are contemplating the implementation of a microgrid setup at a designated test site, anticipating future benefits. By prioritizing the growth of sustainable energy, the institution’s main goal is to secure a dependable and seamless power supply while also decreasing its annual energy spending. Meanwhile, the vendor company envisions becoming a full-time energy supplier, intending to sell their generated power throughout the year at a specified rate. Notably, the institution incurs no initial investment or maintenance costs, whereas the installation company has yet to address the land space required for installing PV and WT units. The agreement between the institution and the installation company spans 15 years, with a fixed COE that remains constant, contrary to the common practice among distribution companies, which tend to increase their COE by approximately 10% annually. The installing company anticipates profitability for the initial five years from the project’s commencement. As a consultant, the researcher’s role is to provide optimal recommendations to the installing company for determining the most suitable capacity for renewable energy generators and discuss the various technical aspects of sustainable system design in terms of ecology and economy.

It is also important to recognize that the optimal mix of resources is sensitive to load profiles and their evolution over time. Forecasting-aware planning approaches, such as ANFIS-based demand prediction demonstrated in the Ecuadorian context53, highlight the value of integrating learned demand models into microgrid sizing and tariff design. Such methods provide a complementary pathway for long-term renewable integration, and the present study can be viewed as a step toward such forecasting-informed planning frameworks.

This study shows how the Pelican Optimization Algorithm can be used to design and operate a PV–WT–DG–UG microgrid under actual meteorological and institutional load conditions in order to reduce energy cost and emissions. Its novelty lies in the use of site-specific real data, the dual institutional-vendor perspective, and the techno-economic evaluation of a renewable-dominant campus microgrid configuration.

System description

The test site’s power supply is primarily drawn from the West Bengal State Electricity Distribution Company Limited (WBSEDCL) grid network, with a 250 kVA Cummins diesel generator serving as the emergency backup system. This research introduces a novel approach by proposing a grid-connected HMGS to decrease dependence on the utility grid and diesel generator. The primary focus is on achieving maximum renewable energy penetration to minimize energy costs and carbon emissions. The HMGS incorporates multiple units of two different types of RES namely, 10 kW wind turbine and 600 Wp photovoltaic generators, along with a non-renewable DG unit. Leveraging the abundant wind velocity and solar irradiation at the test site throughout the year, this model aims to capitalize on the available resources. The WT generator is equipped with power electronics converters to ensure a stable AC output, while the PV generator employs an inverter to balance peak power demand during daylight hours. The UG is integrated into the main AC busbar through a bidirectional energy meter for calculating net energy flow. Figure 2 illustrates the envisioned HMGS tailored for an educational institute situated in a rural area of West Bengal, India. In the proposed HMGS, black lines represent the power lines, and red lines signify communication signals. The main busbar operates as a three-phase, 440 V, 50 Hz AC system, a parameter maintained by all interconnected sources. All sources and elements including loads are interconnected through ‘CBs’. In Fig. 2, the symbol ‘CB’ represents a composite of different devices such as circuit breakers, relays, and intelligent electronic devices (IEDs) tailored to meet protection needs. Within the Microgrid Central Controller (MGCC), there exists a central protection center (CPC) unit that governs the activation/deactivation of CBs through signal exchange on the communication network. Recognized as the system’s “brain,” MGCC plays a pivotal role in making crucial decisions to enhance the efficiency of energy distribution.

Battery storage has been deliberately excluded in this design due to its high capital and replacement costs, which remain a barrier in the Indian market. In addition, the state net-metering policy allows excess generation from PV and WT to be exported to the utility grid, reducing the need for storage. The existing diesel generator provides backup during grid outages, ensuring reliability. While the addition of a modest BESS or supercapacitor could further smooth intermittency and reduce export, it would raise the system’s LCOE considerably. The present configuration therefore offers a more practical and economically viable solution for the campus.

Meteorological data and load profile

The study site, located in a rural area of South 24-Parganas district (22°15’39"N, 88°11’44"E), demonstrates excellent potential for year-round solar and wind energy generation due to its favorable geographical positioning and climatic conditions. This research endeavours to provide a comprehensive analysis, as depicted in Fig. 3(a), delineating the monthly averages of solar radiation, wind velocity, and atmospheric temperature. Renewable resource assessments reveal monthly averages of 4.84 kWh/m²/day global horizontal irradiance, 4.95 m/s wind speed at 10 m elevation, and 26.06 °C mean ambient temperature—indicating strong hybrid energy potential. Results of the previously conducted energy studies at the test site indicate a clear challenge: it experiences a 20% time (hours) in grid load shedding, as elucidated in Fig. 3(b). This poses a significant hurdle for system designers. To deepen our understanding, Figs. 4(a), 4(b), and 4(c) portray the hourly trends of solar radiation, wind velocity, and peak load respectively. The design of a resilient energy system for TNU is complicated by pronounced variations in hourly electrical load, particularly during university-declared holidays. The demand fluctuates significantly, ranging from a minimum of 15 kWh to a peak of 159 kWh per hour, which adds to the complexity of system planning. The insights obtained from this study are instrumental in crafting effective strategies to overcome the institution’s energy-related challenges.

Microgrid components

This study primarily examines the variability of photovoltaic panels and wind turbines within the microgrid system, while the DG acts as a backup power source. The DG is activated only during renewable energy shortfalls or grid outages to meet excess demand. When renewable generation exceeds load requirements, surplus energy is exported to the main grid via net metering. The proposed microgrid architecture comprises five key components: (1) PV arrays, (2) WTs, (3) DG set, (4) power conversion system, and (5) net metering infrastructure. Detailed specifications of each component are provided in subsequent sections.

Solar photovoltaic panel

India’s geographic location within the Sunbelt region endows it with substantial annual solar irradiance, making it particularly suitable for photovoltaic applications. System modeling incorporates time-resolved solar irradiance data (global horizontal irradiation) and ambient temperature profiles, both critical for accurate PV output estimation. The study site receives an annual mean solar irradiance of 4.48 kWh/m²/day54, a value that satisfies both economic viability and system reliability requirements for hybrid microgrid implementation. Net metering

This study explores the concept of net metering as a mechanism for tracking bi-directional energy flow between the utility grid and the host microgrid system. Net metering functions as an automated billing system, where the net energy usage—or credit—is calculated based on the difference between the electricity drawn from the utility grid and the surplus energy exported back to it from the microgrid. The objective of this section is to analyze how net metering supports real-time energy accounting and facilitates streamlined billing through the mutual exchange of power between centralized and distributed energy systems57,58.

Problem formulation

This study develops a grid-connected hybrid renewable energy system optimized for both economic viability and operational resilience. Critical design parameters include the capacity optimization of photovoltaic arrays and wind turbines, ensuring efficient resource utilization. The following subsections detail the system’s control strategy, optimization objectives, operational constraints, and computational methodology for performance assessment.

Operational strategy

In any hybrid energy system, ensuring system reliability necessitates effective power management. In this context, the hybrid energy system’s operational priority places the diesel generator at a lower preference level, activating it solely when renewable resources cannot fulfil the load demand, and the utility grid is also offline. System modeling assumptions

To reflect the dynamic nature of renewable energy generation and electricity demand, the hybrid microgrid system has been simulated using an hourly resolution over a full year, totaling 8760 data points. This level of granularity helps in accurately analyzing variations in solar and wind energy availability alongside real-time load behavior.

In this study, BESS have been deliberately excluded to highlight the operational dependency on the utility grid and the diesel generator for backup support. Consequently, no assumptions or constraints related to battery charge/discharge cycles have been incorporated into the model.

The DG is operated as a secondary backup unit and is dispatched only when the combined output from renewable sources and grid supply falls short of the load requirement. For realism, the DG operation includes constraints such as a minimum operating duration of one hour and a limited ramp rate to reflect practical deployment.

To address the intermittency of renewable resources without relying on probabilistic models, the system design includes a conservative oversizing strategy. Both PV and wind turbine capacities are planned to exceed the peak load by approximately 10–15%, offering a buffer against typical fluctuations in weather conditions. This deterministic approach, supported by the reliability constraint in Eq. (9), ensures the system remains robust under real-world operational variability.

It may be noted that the model incorporates practical constraints relevant to the site, including a minimum loading threshold for the DG, a 20% converter sizing margin, and grid export limits based on the prevailing net-metering policy. The hourly resolution of the analysis implicitly restricts ramping, and a simplified fuel curve has been applied for DG operation. Sensitivity checks confirmed that these assumptions do not alter the annual techno-economic outcomes, ensuring the robustness of the reported results.

Pelican optimization algorithm (POA): Computational methodology

The Pelican Optimization Algorithm is a nature-inspired metaheuristic introduced by Trojovský and Dehghani61, which mimics the coordinated hunting behavior of pelicans. In the wild, pelicans identify the location of their prey and collaboratively drive it toward shallow water by spreading their wings and diving, thereby facilitating capture. This natural strategy is mathematically adapted in POA to solve complex optimization problems through a population-based approach.

Each algorithm was independently executed ten times, and the mean and standard deviation of the objective values were recorded to capture performance variability.

Results and discussion

This proposed research delves into two perspectives: one from institutional management and the other from a vendor company, both considering the implementation of a microgrid setup at the test site. The institution aims are reliable and cost-effective green energy, while the vendor’s intention is to make profit by continuous selling of all generated energy without any terms and conditions. With a 15-year agreement and fixed costs, the study focuses on advising the installation company on optimal renewable energy generator capacity and sustainable system design. For that purpose, a comprehensive examination through multiple case studies aimed at investigating the cost-effectiveness and reliability of grid-connected microgrid systems. The existing framework relies on the UG as the primary power source, complemented by a DG for backup purposes. Consequently, each envisioned system integrates both sources judiciously. Case-1 focused exclusively on PVs as the renewable energy generator, while Case-2 employed WTs in a similar manner. In a marked departure, Case-3 introduced the HMGS, strategically combining both renewable resources, aiming to deliver superior performance and outcomes. Any surplus energy generated is directed to the main grid through net-metering. The optimization problem is addressed with an assumed cost of ₹4.5/kWh for solar and wind energy. The current energy rate from the grid for bulk load is ₹10.90/kWh, and the selling rate for excess power to the grid is considered as ₹3.00/kWh. LCOE from the diesel generator is very high with compare to others source, it is ₹31.40/kWh. The different case studies give a complete idea which model gives the better results with respect to LCOE and reliable supply. The Pelican optimization algorithm is applied to optimize the system, and the complete optimization results are discussed in the subsequent subsections.

Among the four optimization techniques explored in this study—POA, GWO, PSO, and GA—all successfully generated technically viable configurations for a grid-connected hybrid microgrid system. However, POA stood out for its overall superior performance. It achieved the lowest levelized cost of energy (₹6.8237/kWh) while maintaining a high renewable energy contribution (91.35%), reflecting minimal reliance on diesel generators and grid electricity.

Although GWO yielded a marginally higher renewable factor (91.37%), this came at the cost of increased computational effort, as evidenced by its longer convergence time (36 iterations) compared to POA (17 iterations). PSO and GA also provided feasible designs but exhibited higher total energy costs and greater fuel consumption, indicating reduced integration of renewable resources.

Taken together, these results highlight the POA’s strength in balancing economic and environmental performance with computational efficiency. Its ability to deliver cost-effective and sustainable microgrid solutions positions it as a promising optimization tool for future hybrid energy system planning, particularly in grid-interactive scenarios.

Managerial suggestion

In the context of managerial implications, the findings of this research offer valuable insights for decision-makers and stakeholders involved in the planning and operation of microgrid systems. Managers overseeing university campuses or similar institutions can leverage the results to optimize their microgrid’s performance, achieving a balance between economic efficiency and ecological sustainability. The use of the POA provides a practical tool for managers to enhance the reliability of power supply without necessitating additional financial investments. It is recommended that managers consider adapting and implementing the proposed approach, customizing it to suit the specific characteristics of their microgrid systems. Moreover, staying abreast of advancements in optimization techniques, integrating real-time data, and exploring collaborative opportunities with emerging technologies will be crucial for future managerial strategies aiming at sustainable and cost-effective energy solutions.

Conclusion and future work

This study presents a comprehensive framework for the optimal design and operation of a grid-connected hybrid microgrid system tailored to the energy needs of a rural educational institution in coastal West Bengal, India. Through the integration of photovoltaic arrays, wind turbines, a diesel generator, and utility grid support, the system was able to meet the total electricity demand reliably while achieving significant cost and emission reductions.

The results indicate that the optimized configuration, obtained using the pelican optimization algorithm, led to a substantial 42% reduction in the levelized cost of energy from ₹11.74/kWh (≈ USD 0.141/kWh) to ₹6.82/kWh (≈ USD 0.082/kWh). In parallel, annual carbon emissions were reduced from 650 metric tons to 110 metric tons due to the minimized reliance on diesel generation. POA proved to be an effective optimization tool, consistently identifying near-optimal solutions with fast convergence and robustness against local optima.

The findings confirm that intelligently designed hybrid systems, when configured using efficient metaheuristic techniques, can deliver reliable, affordable, and environmentally sustainable power particularly in settings where access to uninterrupted energy is critical.

Looking ahead, this framework can be adapted to other regions by modifying the input resource profiles and load characteristics, offering a scalable and replicable model for green energy planning. Future work will focus on integrating battery energy storage system and electric vehicle charging infrastructure to support the evolving needs of modern academic campuses. Moreover, incorporating AI-driven forecasting techniques for both meteorological inputs and dynamic load demand can enhance real-time adaptability and long-term sustainability of the system.

This study also introduces a novel perspective by offering a vendor-oriented sizing strategy for renewable energy generators, assuming a fixed cost of energy. In future case studies, the levelized cost of energy will be calculated based on market-driven pricing of renewable components, enabling a more dynamic and economically reflective optimization framework.

This study is limited to a grid-connected hybrid system without battery storage, with optimization carried out under deterministic conditions. Future work could extend the model to incorporate battery-inclusive variants for greater resilience, apply advanced dispatch strategies such as MPC or RL with demand forecasts, and assess the influence of evolving tariff and policy frameworks. In addition, applying the proposed approach to multiple sites would help establish its transferability and scalability for diverse institutional and regional contexts.
