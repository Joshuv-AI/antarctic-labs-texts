Diagnostic discrepancy between patient-reported outcomes and clinical assessment: a critical validation of the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) in Syrian cancer patients
Authors: Ghazal Alidelbi, Thayr Haydar, Maher Saifo, Yosef Latifa, Mostafa Sallom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe escalating psychosocial burden is critically amplified in conflict zones such as Syria, with cancer patients already in need of critical psychological care to distinguish between somatic and psychiatric dimensions of their state, overwhelmed by the extreme environmental pressures of the war. This creates a challenge for both patients and physicians, severely hindering effective diagnosis, treatment, and care.AimsThis study aimed to evaluate the prevalence of anxiety and depression among Syrian cancer patients and validate the compatibility between Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) and Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5)- the gold standard of clinical diagnosis.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted with 101 patients with cancer at specialised centres in Damascus. Participants were assessed using the self-reported HADS, where the researcher was blinded to the participants' scores to eliminate bias. Then followed by independent clinical interviews conducted by another specialist based on the DSM-5 criteria.ResultsThe HADS-D identified a high prevalence of depression (54.5%), significantly exceeding the clinical diagnosis of Major Depressive Disorder (22.8%) via the DSM-5. While the anxiety subscale (HADS-A) demonstrated moderate and acceptable agreement with clinical findings (Cramer's V = .329, P = .001), the depression subscale (HADS-D) revealed a striking systematic discrepancy, indicated by a negative Cohen’s Kappa value of -0.085 (P = .031). High depression scores were significantly associated with older age (> 55 years), whereas no demographic or clinical variables showed significant correlations with independent clinical diagnoses.ConclusionThe findings highlight a "methodological failure" of the HADS-D in differentiating between somatic cancer symptoms and true psychological distress within the Syrian cultural and crisis context. This study cautions against the exclusive reliance on traditional self-report screening tools in such settings and underscores the vital necessity of expert clinical interviews (DSM-5) to ensure diagnostic accuracy in psycho-oncology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05024-0.

Introduction

Cancer represents a growing global health burden, primarily driven by demographic and lifestyle factors such as population ageing and changes in diet and habits [26]. According to a 2020 survey, there were 18.1 million new diagnoses and 9.9 million deaths worldwide. The World Health Organisation estimates that 25.7 million people will require end-of-life palliative care, with advanced cancer accounting for a quarter of these cases [25].

Patients with cancer, particularly in advanced stages, experience a significant symptom burden arising from the disease process, treatment side effects such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy, and co-existing comorbidities [11, 22, 28].The most prevalent symptoms were fatigue (77.8%), urinary incontinence (77.5%), asthenia (66.7%), pain (66.3%), constipation (52.5%), and anxiety (50%). The co-occurrence of these symptoms underscores the critical need for integrated palliative care [28].

As a chronic illness, cancer has multisystem effects, including tissue damage, obstruction, compression, and subsequent alterations in normal organ function, immune response, and hormonal regulation. Beyond physical manifestations, the psychosocial dimension of cancer has gained increasing recognition [16, 23]. Recent research has established that mental health is a vital component of comprehensive care, with psychological support demonstrating efficacy in reducing stress and anxiety, improving sleep quality, and enhancing overall quality of life. Consequently, international clinical guidelines now recommend routine screening for mental health comorbidities, particularly depression and anxiety, in all cancer patients, with an emphasis on those with advanced disease to facilitate early psychological intervention [18, 21].

In light of this evidence, this study aimed to assess the prevalence of depression and/or anxiety among Syrian cancer patients, the influence of key sociodemographic and clinical variables on these psychological states, and to evaluate commonly used psychiatric scales (HADS\ DSM5) [27, 31].

Materials and methods

Study design and population

A cross-sectional study was conducted at two University Hospitals between May 1, 2025, and November 1, 2025. The study cohort consisted of Syrian adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) undergoing cancer treatment at any stage of their treatment schedule. The exclusion criteria included a prior diagnosis of anxiety or depression and current use of antidepressant or anxiolytic medications. Of the 122 patients initially invited, 12 declined to participate, and 9 were excluded based on the medication criteria, yielding a final sample of 101 participants.

Data collection

All eligible participants who provided informed consent were administered a 14-item questionnaire derived from the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). To ensure comprehension, the researcher conducted face-to-face interviews during which the HADS items were explained and slightly simplified to facilitate understanding. Subsequently, a different specialist -blinded to HADS score- independently applied the DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for anxiety and depression to each patient based on clinical observations and interview data.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed to summarise the participants’ characteristics. Categorical variables are presented as absolute and relative frequencies, and continuous variables are summarised using means and standard deviations. Correlations between variables were assessed using Pearson’s and Spearman's rank correlation coefficients, as appropriate. A linear regression model was employed to evaluate the influence of several independent variables—including age, sex, social performance, education level, employment status, time since diagnosis, and treatment duration—on anxiety and depression scores. Furthermore, the efficacy of the HADS as a screening tool was determined by comparing its results with the DSM-5 criteria for predicting major depressive disorder and generalised anxiety disorder.

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki ( revised in 2013). The project was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Faculty of Medicine, Damascus (reference NO: 18,420- June-24–2025). All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. Consent to participate was obtained from all participants.

The remaining cancer types, including osteosarcoma (5%), bladder, prostate, and brain cancers (4% each), were less frequent. The sample was characterised by a low level of formal education: 23.8% were illiterate, 22.8% had attended middle school, and 17.8% had only a primary school education. Attendance at high school and university did not exceed 18.8% and 16.8%, respectively. Reflecting a low-income community with a female predominance, most patients were married (78.2%), and 10.9% were divorced. Community customs and traditions appeared to influence employment status, with 56.4% of patients being unemployed, 24.8% employed, and 19% retired. Most patients (91%) were actively undergoing treatment at the time of the study, while 5% were post-treatment and 4% pre-treatment (Table 1).

The Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) screened positive for depressive symptoms in 54.5% and 18.8% for anxiety symptoms. A further 31.7% and 34.7% of the cases scored in indeterminate\borderline rang for anxiety and depression, respectively. Notably, nine patients (8.8%) screened positive for both Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD) and Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) on the HADS.

Factors affecting DSM5 score

Analysis of the HADS scores revealed several significant associations. An age-based analysis using the sample median indicated that depression was significantly more prevalent in patients older than 55 years (P = 0.033), whereas no significant difference in anxiety distribution was observed across age groups (P = 0.132). A trend towards higher anxiety was noted in female patients, although this finding was not statistically significant (P = 0.066). No sex-based differences were observed in depression.

While no other variables demonstrated a statistically significant effect on HADS outcomes, several demographic groups exhibited notably higher mean scores. The Highest anxiety scores were observed among divorced workers and primary school-educated patients (mean scores: 8.86, 9.32, and 9.11, respectively). The Highest depression scores were found among married, working, and middle school-educated patients (mean scores: 11.1, 11.68, and 11.65, respectively). Furthermore, a longer treatment duration (> 12 months) and post-treatment status were associated with the highest aggregate scores for anxiety and depression (Table 1).

In contrast to the HADS results, none of the demographic or clinical variables demonstrated a statistically significant association with the DSM-5 diagnoses at the P < 0.05 threshold. However, a trend was observed regarding employment status: unemployed patients were more frequently diagnosed with (MDD) (11.9%, n = 12), an association that approached statistical significance (P = 0.075) (Table 1).

The analysis revealed a statistically significant, moderate association between the HADS-A questionnaire and DSM-5-based diagnosis for anxiety (Cramer's V = 0.329, P = 0.001), suggesting that it is a useful screening tool in this clinical context. Discussion

Cancer represents a defining challenge of our era, with escalating global attention corresponding to rising incidence rates and advances in diagnostic and screening technology [4, 23]. The Arab region is no exception. Although cancer prevalence is generally lower than the global average, it is paradoxically characterised by higher mortality rates. The most common cancer types among Arabic-speaking populations largely mirror global patterns, though notable variations exist between individual Arab nations [2].

Contemporary medical literature addresses the multifaceted impact of cancer on society and individuals. Psychosocial aspects have emerged as a critical area of concern, yet receive comparatively less attention in low- and middle-income countries, including those in the Arab world [13]. Evidence suggests that improving psychological health enhances patients' quality of life and may facilitate better adaptation during recovery [17, 30]. Notably, Even after successful oncological treatment, psychological sequelae can persist; in some cases, these may be severe enough to elevate suicide risk, even within conservative religious societal contexts (a Lebanese study reported a 6% rate) [10].

Globally, the estimated prevalence of comorbid psychological distress, such as anxiety and depression, among cancer patients ranges from 30 to 33% [8]. Within the Arab region, estimates vary considerably from 15 to 54% [19]. This discrepancy is frequently attributed to societal norms and religious beliefs. Psychological distress is often stigmatised, viewed as a source of shame, and concealed by patients and their families to avoid being labelled [17]. Furthermore, non-specialist healthcare providers may misattribute psychological symptoms to somatic causes. Research on Arabic-speaking populations globally, including refugee communities, highlights a lack of linguistic and conceptual equivalence for the terms "depression" and "anxiety," compromising the validity of standard diagnostic tools [9]. Many items in widely used instruments, such as the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS), are considered inadequate cross-culturally. Similar challenges often undermine the reliability of personal interviews [10, 17, 29].

Anxiety findings

In this study, the prevalence of anxiety was 18.8% according to HADS-A (cutoff ≥ 8), whereas only 4.0% of patients met DSM-5 criteria for Generalised Anxiety Disorder (GAD). Despite this numerical difference, statistical analysis demonstrated moderate agreement between the two methods (Cramer's V = 0.329, P = 0.001), reflecting some validity of HADS despite comprehension difficulties.

The observed discrepancy can be attributed to two primary factors. First, DSM-5 requires persistent symptoms for at least six months for a GAD diagnosis, a stringent criterion that lowers diagnostic yield compared to HADS-A, which assesses symptoms over the preceding week. Many cases identified by HADS-A likely represent acute adjustment disorders rather than chronic GAD, reflecting the immediate psychological impact of cancer diagnosis and treatment. Second, female patients reported significantly higher anxiety levels than males (P = 0.006), aligning with regional literature [24].

Depression findings

Regarding depression, HADS-D indicated a Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) prevalence of 54.5%, compared to 18.8% per DSM-5 criteria, with a negative correlation. This "methodological failure" is likely attributed to significant overlap between somatic symptoms of malignancy and HADS-D items. With high prevalence rates of fatigue (77.8%) and asthenia (66.7%) in our cohort, HADS-D frequently yielded false positives by misinterpreting physical exhaustion as clinical depression.

Low education levels (23.8% illiterate; 40.6% with only primary/middle school education) led to patient misunderstanding of the questionnaire, supporting our concern against routine reliance on HADS in this population. To address this, clinicians provided standardized verbal clarifications for culturally ambiguous terms (e.g., "butterflies in the stomach"), maintaining semantic equivalence without altering the scale's construct (Supplementary File 1). Despite these efforts, HADS performance remained unsatisfactory, achieving only moderate agreement with DSM-5 diagnoses.

The Syrian context and age-related findings

The unique context of our study population must be considered. Syrian society has endured profound social trauma, displacement, and chronic insecurity. For many, a cancer diagnosis may not be the primary life threat, potentially fostering resilience that alters clinical presentation and self-reporting of psychiatric disorders. HADS – like all self-report screening tools – cannot isolate distress caused by cancer from distress caused by war, displacement, or poverty. This is a known limitation, not a feature. In a population exposed to multiple, severe stressors, reliance on HADS alone may be particularly misleading, strengthening our preference for clinical assessment (DSM-5). Western studies corroborate that concordance between different screening tools (e.g., HADS and PHQ-9) is often only moderate to low [5]. Analysis of patient characteristics revealed a significant correlation between HADS-D and older age (> 55 years) (P = 0.033), which may be attributed to lack of financial aid, retirement, and accumulated psychological trauma among older ages. However, this correlation was not mirrored in DSM-5 clinical diagnosis, suggesting HADS-D may overestimate depression in older Syrian patients. These findings are in contrast with Soqia et al. [24].

This discrepancy may be explained by two key methodological differences: first, Soqia's cohort was exclusively female (breast cancer patients), while our sample included both sexes; second, they employed different screening instruments (GAD-2 and PHQ-2) without clinical validation, whereas we used DSM-5 interviews as the reference standard.

Comparison with regional studies

In Arabic culture, psychological distress is heavily somatised or underreported due to social stigma and religious resilience. Since HADS excludes somatic items to avoid confounding with cancer symptoms, it often misses depressed patients who express distress physically or maintain cultural stoicism – nuances captured only via face-to-face DSM-5 interviews.

Our finding of moderate HADS-DSM-5 agreement (Cramer's V = 0.329) must be interpreted within the broader context of HADS performance in Arab and Middle Eastern populations. Recent meta-analyses support our central argument that HADS cannot be assumed valid without population-specific calibration against clinical interviews.

Rafiei [20] reported substantial variation between screening tools in the Middle East: HADS yielded depression prevalence of 46% and anxiety 47%, compared to PHQ-9 (64%) and GAD-7 (23%). This discrepancy underscores a fundamental problem: prevalence estimates are highly dependent on the choice of screening instrument. Our study extends this observation by demonstrating that even when HADS is used, its agreement with DSM-5 is only moderate, suggesting self-reported prevalence figures derived from HADS alone may be misleading.

More directly relevant to our cancer population, Al-Hussaini [1] conducted a meta-analysis of 83 studies from 14 Arab countries, reporting pooled depression prevalence of 44% and anxiety 47% among cancer patients. HADS was the most frequently used tool. The authors emphasized that different screening tools yield significantly different estimates, and that heterogeneity (I2 > 90%) is partly attributable to instrument selection. Our findings align with this conclusion: moderate HADS estimates do not necessarily correspond to clinically diagnosable disorders.

Cultural and contextual factors further complicate validity as Karam et al. [12] identified war as a major risk factor for mental disorders (PTSD: 24.1% in Lebanon, 34.1% in Palestine) and documented very low treatment-seeking rates (13% in the UAE). Our Syrian sample, exposed to over a decade of armed conflict, represents an extreme case where self-report tools like HADS are most likely to diverge from their performance in Western, stable, literate populations [14, 15] found that only 36% of an Arab sample were willing to see a mental health professional, highlighting the public health importance of accurate screening: misclassification by HADS alone could lead to overtreatment or, more likely, loss of credibility in screening programs.

Summary of HADS limitations

Based on this evidence, HADS has several well-documented limitations: sensitivity to cutoff thresholds [3, 20]; disagreement with other tools [1, 20]; rare validation against clinical interviews [1, 12]; inability to distinguish medical distress from war-related distress [12]; susceptibility to literacy [6, 7] and unproven cultural appropriateness [12, 14, 15].

Implications for global mental health

The present study directly demonstrates the consequence of these limitations: only moderate HADS-DSM-5 agreement. Although conducted in a Syrian cancer population –rarely represented in mainstream journals – our study addresses a universal methodological problem. These findings have direct implications for clinicians working with Arab refugees in Western countries and for global mental health screening in low- and middle-income countries. More broadly, armed conflicts continue to displace millions across Africa, the Middle East, Asia, and Eastern Europe. In every new conflict, clinicians face the same challenge: screening using tools developed in peaceful, Western settings never validated in war-exposed populations.

Future research directions

Future research should focus on developing and validating culturally adapted screening tools that account for somatisation, low literacy, and local idioms of distress in Arab conflict-affected populations. Longitudinal studies are needed to examine how the agreement between HADS and DSM-5 evolves across the cancer care trajectory. Comparative validation studies with Syrian refugee populations in Western host countries would extend the generalisability of our findings. Additionally, future research should include non-cancer control groups to better isolate the confounding effects of war-related trauma from cancer-specific distress, and evaluate the efficacy of culturally adapted psychosocial interventions in conflict-affected settings.

Clinical implication

Clinicians working in conflict zones or cultural contexts similar to Syria are advised to take caution when using the HADS-Depression subscale (HADS-D) as a standalone diagnostic tool, given its inability to reliably differentiate between somatic symptoms of physical illness and psychological distress.

This study underscores the necessity of utilising DSM-5-based clinical interviews, emphasising their essential role in diagnostic accuracy in psycho-oncology within this specific population.

Conversely, HADS-Anxiety subscale (HADS-A) may still be useful as an effective primary screening tool, as its moderate agreement with clinical diagnoses suggests it can aid in identifying patients requiring further psychiatric evaluation.

Study limitations

The study was conducted with only 101 patients at two central oncology hospitals in Damascus. The findings are specific to the Syrian cultural and crisis context and may not be generalizable to cancer patients in stable regions or other cultural settings.

As a cross-sectional study, it captures only a snapshot in time. It cannot determine how the validity of the HADS might change throughout a patient's treatment period or over the course of their illness.

The study excluded patients already taking antidepressants or anxiolytics. This means the findings apply only to untreated populations and may not reflect the screening accuracy in patients already receiving psychiatric care.

The combination of low formal education levels and the lack of linguistic and conceptual equivalence in the HADS items posed a potential barrier to patient comprehension. Consequently, for some patients, the interviewer had to simplify the questionnaire items and explain them in a way more appropriate for the patient's level of awareness. This raises concerns about the accuracy of the reported psychological data.

Conclusion

The findings suggest that the HADS-D lacks sufficient clinical validity to serve as a standalone diagnostic tool in the Syrian oncology setting. We recommend that clinicians exercise caution when utilising self-report screening tools and emphasise the necessity of expert clinical interviews based on the DSM-5 criteria to ensure diagnostic accuracy and avoid the confounding effects of somatic illness symptoms. Future research should focus on developing culturally sensitive screening instruments for this population.
