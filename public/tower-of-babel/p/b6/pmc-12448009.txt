Effect of family caregiving on depression outcome among older European adults
Authors: Sherry Shu-Yeu Hou, Jee Won Park, Arijit Nandi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMost caregiver health studies rely on observational data and traditional regression methods, which fail to account for time-varying confounding, limiting causal inference. This analysis uses inverse probability weighting (IPW) to appropriately account for time-varying confounding in caregiving-depression relationship.MethodsUsing seven waves (2004–2019) of the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe, we estimated the effect of caregiving on probable depression (having 4+ symptoms) comparing daily and some caregiving to no caregiving. We accounted for censoring and potential measured confounding by time-fixed covariates (gender, number of children, country, and education) and time-varying covariates (age, employment, marital status, income, physical limitations, psychiatric medication, receiving help, previous caregiving, and previous depression) using IPCW and IPTW. The product of the two weights was applied to a marginal structural model to obtain the causal estimand on the prevalence difference scale. Confidence intervals were derived from bootstrapping.ResultsAmong 36 346 participants and 67 800 person-waves, compared to no caregiving in the last year, daily caregiving was associated with a 6.7 percentage point (95 % CI: 4.8 %, 8.6 %) increase in the prevalence of probable depression, after accounting for time-fixed and time-varying covariates. Some caregiving was not associated with probable depression (PD = 0.5, 95 % CI = −0.8 %, 1.8 %).ConclusionsOur results support existing findings that high levels of caregiving may increase the prevalence of probable depression, while lower levels of caregiving do not. Accurate documentation of the relationship between caregiving and health outcomes is foundational in creating evidence-based policies to support healthy aging.Keywords: Ageing, Caregiving, Depression, Causal inference, Inverse probability weighting

Background

The proportion of older adults is increasing in many high-income countries, driven by demographic shifts, including the aging of the “Baby Boomer” generation and recent declines in fertility. The overall proportion of the world's population over 60 years will nearly double from 12 % to 22 % between 2015 and 2050 (United Nations et al., 2017). In response to this shift, the WHO is calling for evidence-based policies to support healthy aging (World Health Organization, 2017). As society ages, the demand for long-term and end-of-life care also increases. Currently, the majority of caring for older adults is done by family members or the “informal” social network. Even among those who receive paid or professional services, family caregiving still plays an indispensable role (Home Care across Europe, 2012).

Family caregivers are often defined as people who provide unpaid assistance with everyday activities to a family member or someone within their social network (Schulz et al., 2020). As the proportion of the population 60 or older continues to increase, many adults are taking on the responsibilities of providing personal, daily care to older adult members in their families. In Europe, the prevalence of adult family caregivers over the age of 50 is somewhere between 13.5 % and 25.6 % (Tur-Sinai et al., 2020), while the ratio between individuals who can provide care and individuals who may need care is projected to decrease (Riedel et al., 2011).

The dominant framework has viewed caregiving as a “burden” or “stressor”, but many studies have also documented benefits from providing care, even while caregivers may report the daily tasks of caregiving as stressful (Committee on Family Caregiving for Older Adults et al., 2016). Taking care of a loved one can be fulfilling, and many people choose to provide care to an older family member because of an emotional bond and derive a sense of emotional gratification and self-efficacy from providing care, which could contribute to decreased mortality rate (Fredman et al., 2019; Plothner et al., 2019). Under the stress-process model developed by Cohen and colleagues, if the caregiving demands exceeds the “adaptive capacities”, the caregiver may perceive the situation to be stressful and therefore develop negative emotional responses (Cohen et al., 1997). Yee et al., 2000 further adapts and expands Cohen and colleagues' stress process model to incorporate a gender component, arguing that certain caregiving situations place women at greater risk for developing psychiatric morbidity (Yee et al., 2000). Without adequate support, caregiving responsibilities may experience psychological strain that negatively affect caregivers’ physical and mental health, such as increased depressive symptoms (Schulz et al., 2008). The adverse psychological effect of caregiving includes report of stress, symptoms of depression and anxiety, to impairment to quality of life. The emotional stress and benefits of caregiving often co-occur.

Current evidence concerning the health consequences of caregiving are derived primarily from cross-sectional observational studies. Although this research suggests that under-supported family caregiving activities are associated with poorer mental health outcomes, cross-sectional studies cannot establish temporality and provide limited support for a causal relation between caregiving and mental health (Capistrant, 2016; National Academies of Sciences et al., 2016; Pinquart et al., 2003, pp. P112–P128; Pinquart et al., 2007, pp. P126–P137). More recently, there has been a shift towards modeling the longitudinal relation between caregiving and mental health outcomes using data from prospective cohort studies (Bertogg et al., 2021; Capistrant et al., 2014; Hiel et al., 2015; Kaschowitz et al., 2017). However, these studies have not addressed potential confounding by time-varying characteristics that may be affected by prior levels of exposure (i.e., bias due to an “exposure-confounder feedback loop”), illustrated in Fig. 1(Hernán et al., 2020). In this analysis, previous employment status may influence subsequent caregiving activities and be influenced by prior caregiving roles, with both potentially influencing mental health, making employment status simultaneously a confounder and a mediator of the relation between caregiving and mental health.

In a systematic review published in 2022, all 13 longitudinal studies of family caregiving and mental health addressed time-varying confounding using traditional regression adjustment methods (Ervin et al., 2022). Standard regression adjustment methods, in the presence of a time-varying confounding that is affected by prior exposure, could lead to biased effect estimates for the following reasons: (i) controlling for intermediaries on the causal pathway between caregiving and mental health and/or (ii) opening a backdoor pathway if there are unmeasured common causes, U1, of the time-varying confounder and outcome due to collider stratification bias (Hernán et al., 2020; Mansournia et al., 2017). To properly account for time-varying confounding, we would have to employ one of the three g-methods: IPW, parametric g-formular, and g-estimation.

In this study, we estimated the effect of caregiving on depressive symptomatology using prospective cohort data from eight European countries. To account for potential time-varying confounding, we used an inverse probability weighted marginal structural model (Robins et al., 2000). Since women are more likely to provide care to family members and report depressive symptoms, we also examined differential effects for men and women.

Methods

Data and sample

We used data from the Survey of Health, Ageing and Retirement in Europe (SHARE), a longitudinal survey designed to document the effects of health, social, economic, and environmental policies over the life-course of European citizens. Starting in 2004 with follow-up every two years thereafter, SHARE has collected 8 waves of data through 2022, including 480 000 in-depth interviews of 140 000 people aged 50 or older from 28 European countries and Israel. Individuals are excluded from the sampling frame if they are incarcerated, hospitalized, or out of the country during the entire survey period, unable to speak the country's language(s), or have moved to an unknown address. In each follow-up, additional participants above the age of 50 years are recruited to maintain sample size.

Eligible participants and their spouses or partners living in the same household (regardless of the partner/spouse's age) were invited to provide information regarding their physical, mental, economic, and social circumstances. Participant information was collected during in-person interviews by trained interviewers using a computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) program. For more detailed survey and recruitment methodology, see SHARE methodology (Börsch-Supan and Jügest, 2005). The survey is translated into the official languages of each participating country, and each measure is harmonized across participating countries. If the participant died between waves, an end-of-life interview is conducted with a proxy respondent, usually a family member.

We restricted our sample to countries that participated in the first wave of SHARE and follow-up surveys in waves 2, 4, 5, 6, 7, and 8 (i.e., Austria, Belgium, Switzerland, Germany, Denmark, Spain, France, Italy, Netherlands, and Sweden), shown in Fig. 2. Israel was excluded because subsequent waves differed in timing with other countries. The third wave, the SHARELIFE survey, was not included because it focused on early childhood experiences and was not compatible with the other waves. We further restricted our analysis to individuals over the age of 50 who participated in at least 2 consecutive waves. Respondents were censored if they missed one or more interviews or died, with information on death recorded in the end-of-life interview. For example, if they are lost to follow up or died between wave 5 and 6, then they are considered censored at wave 6 and would not contribute any information after wave 5. For the regression analysis, because the covariates are time-lagged by one wave (e.g., previous marital status at wave 2 would be the marital status collected at wave 1), the status of each covariate at baseline informs the values of the lagged covariates at the first follow-up.

Exclusion criteria applied and the final sample size. Each observation is a person-wave.

Measures

Exposure: caregiving

The time-varying exposure of interest was caregiving in the last 12 months, derived from two questions. First, participants were asked, “In the last twelve months, have you personally given any kind of help listed on card 28 [Appendix A] to a family member from outside the household, a friend or neighbour?”. Those responding affirmatively were asked about the frequency of help given, specifically if it was daily, about every week, about every month, or less often. Second, respondents were asked, “Is there someone living in this household whom you have helped regularly during the last twelve months with personal care, such as washing, getting out of bed, or dressing?”. “Regularly” was defined as daily or almost daily support during at least three months to distinguish it from help during short-term sickness of family members. Based on their responses of caregiving in the last 12 months, we classified the level of caregiving as daily, some, or none. A daily caregiver was defined as providing daily or almost daily help outside of the household (question 1) or regular help within the household (question 2). If the respondent reported less than daily caregiving, they were classified as having provided some caregiving. Otherwise, the respondent was categorized as having provided no caregiving.

Outcome: probable depression

“Current” depressive symptomatology was measured by the EUROD, a twelve-item depression inventory developed through a collaboration of eleven European countries for individuals over 65 years old. Each item is a binary question about a depressive symptom experienced in the last month (e.g., feeling of guilt, trouble sleeping, irritability, etc.) with continuous summary scores ranging from zero to twelve (Prince et al., 1999). The EUROD scale has been shown to be highly correlated with other depression measures, such as CES-D (Courtin et al., 2015). Multiple studies have found Cronbach's alpha value between 0.58 and 0.80 and demonstrated stable and valid estimates of depression symptoms(Gana et al., 2023; Tomas et al., 2022). For this analysis, the outcome was dichotomized, with probable depression defined as self-reporting four or more depressive symptoms, consistent with prior analyses of depression in SHARE (Castro-Costa et al., 2007; Prince et al., 1999).

Covariates

Baseline covariates included gender, number of children, country of residence, age at first interview, and years of education. Time-varying covariates included employment status (employed, retired, homemaker, or other), marital status (married/partnered or not), total household income in Euros in the last year (continuous), any limitations to activities of daily living (ADL) (yes/no), use of medications for depression, anxiety, or sleep problems (yes/no), receiving help from others within social network (yes/no), caregiving status in the previous wave (daily, some, or no caregiving), and probable depression in the previous wave (depressed or not depressed). All time-varying covariates were time-lagged to establish temporality between covariates, caregiving in the 12 months prior to the interview, and probable depression at the time of the interview. For example, employment status was lagged so that employment measured at the previous wave would precede the assessment of caregiving in the prior 12 months and depression caseness, measured at the current wave. With the exception of use of medication, all missing covariate information was imputed by the SHARE team using a combination of hot-deck for variables with less than 5 % of missing and then multiple imputation by Fully Conditional Specification (FCS) for variables with a greater proportion of missing values. The use of medication did not have any missing value. For details on the imputation methodology, please see the SHARE data release guide and methodology (Bergmann et al., 2021; SHARE Release Guide 8, 2022).

Statistical analysis

We aimed to estimate the effect of caregiving, Ait, on the probability of probable depression, Yit, for individual i at wave t using stabilized inverse probability of treatment and censoring weights to account for confounding and censoring, respectively, by measured time-fixed covariates, Zi0, and time-varying covariates, L‾it. For the numerator of the inverse probability of treatment weights (IPTW) [Equation (1)], we used a pooled multinomial logistic regression model to estimate the probability of the level of caregiving reported at survey wave t, conditional on time-fixed covariates and remaining uncensored. Similarly, for the denominator we estimated the probability of the level of caregiving observed conditional on time-fixed covariates, lagged time-varying covariates including prior caregiving status, indicators for survey wave, and remaining uncensored. The numerator and denominator of the inverse probability of censoring weights (IPCW) [Equation (2)] were estimated using pooled logistic regression models, conditional on the same covariates as the IPTWs. Censoring at time t is defined as death or missing at t+1. The final stabilized weights were computed by multiplying the treatment and censoring weights across waves and then taking the product of the weights: SWit=SWitA×SWitC.

We assessed the performance of the weights using visual assessment with box plots, standardized mean or proportion differences, and descriptive statistics. The distribution of treatment and censoring weights were used to assess balance and to identify potential model misspecification or positivity violations (Cole et al., 2008). After adjusting for weights, the weighted distribution of various covariates should be similar across exposure groups. We used box plots to compare the distributions of continuous covariates across treatment groups, before and after applying the inverse probability weights. Standardized mean (for continuous covariates) or proportion (for binary or categorical covariates) differences were used to compare the mean or proportion difference across treatment and censoring groups. Again, well balanced weights should make the weighted standardized mean or proportion more similar across exposure groups than the unweighted mean or proportion (Austin et al., 2015).

Extreme weights suggest that the positivity assumption may be violated and would make certain observations disproportionately influential on the results. Therefore, truncating extreme weights, usually by removing the highest and lowest weights, is often used to address the problem of extreme weights (Chesnaye et al., 2022). In addition, we assessed the performance of unstabilized and stabilized weights. Stabilized weights replaces the numerator, which is 1 when unstabilized, with the probability of exposure. In the case of treatment weights, it would be the probability of exposure given that it is uncensored and controlled for covariates. The mean of stabilized weights should be centered around one (Cole et al., 2008). After assessing the distributions and balance achieved for truncated and untruncated, stabilized and unstabilized IPTW and IPCW, we concluded that stabilized weights truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles performed best.

The marginal structural models (MSM) adjusted for baseline, time-fixed covariates and were weighted by the product of the treatment and censoring weights. To assess potential effect measure modification by gender, we added a cross-product term between caregiving status and gender in the MSM [Equation (3)].

Using the coefficients output from the logistic regression model, we converted the odds to the probability scale and reported the prevalence of probable depression by calculating the predicted probability of probable depression for each exposure group and obtained the prevalence difference by comparing the average effect of daily and some caregiving to no caregiving. Again, we calculated the predicted probability of probable depression by exposure group and gender. The prevalence difference and prevalence ratio of daily and some caregiving were then contrasted with no caregiving for both men and women. The effect estimates on the prevalence difference and ratio scales were presented with 95 % confidence intervals calculated through 500 iterations of bootstrapping.

The SHARE dataset provides variables imputed using a combination of hot-deck and Fully Conditional Specification (FCS), generating five imputations for each observation. To account for uncertainty in the imputation process, we estimated standard errors for the effects of caregiving in each of the five imputed datasets using bootstrapping with 500 resamples. We then used the PROC MIANALYZE command in SAS to obtain corrected standard errors that account for the uncertainty related to imputation (SAS Institute Inc, 2013). All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Sensitivity analysis

Given that a cut-off of 5 or above for probable depression has been proposed (Tomas et al., 2022), we performed a sensitivity analysis using the higher cut-off. Instead of limitations to daily activity (binary), we also used the number of chronic illnesses (continuous) and self-perceived health (excellent, very good, good, fair, and poor) to measure the physical health of the participants, respectively. The results were included in the supplemental material.

Results

Study population

The sample included 36 346 participants and 67 800 observations or person-waves. Out of the entire sample, 1014 person-waves (or about 1.5 %) had incomplete data and were therefore excluded from the regression analysis, leaving a total of 66 786 observations in the regression analysis. The average follow-up time was 2.9 waves or about 4.8 years of participation in the SHARE study. The countries that had the highest proportion of respondents reporting daily caregiving were Italy (16.3 %), Spain (12.9 %), and Belgium (12.2 %). Sweden, Switzerland, and Denmark reported the lowest prevalence of daily caregivers: 5.4 %, 7.0 %, and 7.4 %, respectively. The overall prevalence of daily caregiving, some caregiving, and no caregiving in the last 12 months were 10.4 %, 24.5 %, and 65.1 %, respectively (Table 1). Individuals who reported daily caregiving in the last year were more likely to be female, homemakers, married or partnered, and to report probable depression compared to those who reported no caregiving and some caregiving. Daily caregivers were more likely to report daily caregiving in the previous wave and take medication to address anxiety, depression, or sleep issues.

Inverse probability weights

For the treatment weights, the mean values of the stabilized (truncated and untruncated) weights centered around one, which supports the absence of clear misspecification and non-positivity of the weight model. The weighted box plots showed that the distribution of covariates across treatment levels were more balanced with weights than without weights. The standardized mean and proportion differences between the daily caregiving and no caregiving groups were significantly reduced across most covariates when the treatment weights were introduced [Appendix B Figs. 2 and 3]. The truncated stabilized weights performed the best in the balance diagnostics (Table 2). Comparison of the balance diagnostics among the different weights is in the supplementary figures [Appendix C] (see Table 3).

Marginal structural model

Compared to those who did not report any caregiving in the previous year, the probability of probable depression was 6.7 [95 % CI: 4.4, 8.0] percentage points higher among individuals who reported daily caregiving, after controlling for time-fixed covariates (country, gender, number of children, and years of education) and accounting for time-varying covariates (age, employment status, previous caregiving status, previous depressive status, receiving help, marital status, psychiatric medication use, limitations to daily activities, and income) using inverse probability weighting. The prevalence ratio of probable depression was 1.3 [95 % CI: 1.2, 1.4] for those who reported daily caregiving compared to no caregiving. The prevalence of probable depression was similar among individuals who reported some caregiving and those who reported no caregiving, with a prevalence difference of 0.5 [95 % CI: −0.8, 1.8] percentage points.

Overall, women reported a higher prevalence of probable depression compared to men in all caregiving categories. Among women who provided daily caregiving, the prevalence of probable depression was 7.3 percentage point higher [95 % CI: 4.7, 9.9] than women who provided no care, which was similar to the prevalence difference among men 5.9 [95 % CI: 3.3, 8.5]. The prevalence of probable depression among women who reported some caregiving was 1.3 [95 % CI: −0.6, 3.1] percentage point higher than women who did not provide care, similar to the prevalence difference between men who provided some care and who provided no care −0.4 [95 % CI: −2.1, 1.3]. However, the effects of caregiving on probable depression did not differ substantially across gender on the prevalence difference and ratio scales.

The results from the sensitivity analyses did not noticeably differ from the original analysis. As expected, using a higher cut-off for probable depression (5/6 instead of 4/5) resulted in lower prevalence of probable depression, as fewer people met the definition of probable depression. However, while the magnitude of the prevalence difference was slightly attenuated, the direction remained the same: daily caregiving increased prevalence of probable depression , when the cut-off was set at 5 or more depression symptoms. Similarly, using the number of chronic illnesses and self-perceived health to measure caregivers' physical health did not change the direction of caregiving's effect on probable depression. More detailed results are included in Appendix D.

Discussion

The goal of this analysis was to estimate the causal effect of caregiving on probable depression across 10 European countries using observational longitudinal data and inverse probability weighting. Results show that after accounting for time-varying confounders using inverse probability weights, reporting daily caregiving increased the prevalence of probable depression compared to no caregiving in the last year. Additionally, we found that probable depression did not differ between less-than-daily caregivers and non-caregivers. While prevalence of probable depression among women is higher than men, prevalence difference of probable depression did not differ significantly by gender.

While caregiving can increase the caregivers' confidence and resilience and decrease caregivers' all-cause mortality (Brown et al., 2009; Gibbons et al., 2014; Harmell et al., 2011), much of the caregiving research has focused on the adverse effect on caregivers' health. Our results suggest that daily caregiving increased the probability of depression compared to not providing care; however, this was not the case for lower intensity caregiving reported as less than daily. This supports the idea that caregiving at different levels of intensity could have differential effects on the mental health of caregivers. Using Lazarus and Folkman's definition of stress as a situation “that is appraised by the person as taxing or exceeding his or her resources and endangering well-being” (Lazarus et al., 1984), one can infer that caregivers who have adequate resources and support may be able to tolerate caregiving without experiencing adverse health outcomes. More importantly, this suggests that caregiving per se may not be deleterious to caregivers' health, but that there may be a threshold at which the caregiving responsibility (and the lack of support around caregiving) becomes overwhelming. This suggests that programs that are designed to support family caregivers should take into account the intensity of the care work and reported perceived strain on the caregiver for more targeted intervention.

Women are more likely to become family caregivers and have higher prevalence of depression (Capistrant, 2016). Given differences in cultural expectation of caregiving role between men and women, we expected to see differential effect of caregiving across gender groups. However, while we saw elevated prevalence of depression among women, we did not observe any differential effect from caregiving. Pinquart et al. (2006)'s meta-analysis found increased gender difference in depression outcomes among caregivers compared to general population, and the differences was explained by higher levels of caregiving stressors and lower levels of social resources among women (Penning et al., 2016, p. P33). At the same time, Penning et al., 2016 did not find any gender difference in self-rated mental health among caregivers (Penning et al., 2016). The inconsistency in findings is likely because of the heterogeneity in study population. In our analysis, we did not differentiate the relationship between the caregiver and care recipient. Caring for a child (who may or may not live with a disability), providing end-of-life care for an elderly parent, and attending to a spouse affects caregivers at different stages of their lives and are likely qualitatively different experiences. Future investigations of gender difference in mental wellbeing of caregivers would have to account for these differences in the caregiving dynamics.

Much of the existing literature is based on cross-sectional studies (Ervin et al., 2022), which cannot establish temporality and therefore precludes causal inference. For example, a common issue is reverse causality: healthier individuals are more likely to be able to provide care, possibly resulting in caregiving associated with better health outcomes (Roth et al., 2015), which can be an explanation for studies that find positive health outcomes associated with caregiving. Our analysis accounts for this by lagging time-varying confounders so that caregiving as an exposure always preceded depressive symptoms as an outcome. Other longitudinal studies have relied on traditional regression adjustment to account for potential confounders, which could introduce bias in the presence of time-varying confounding that is affected by caregiving. Using inverse probability weighting, we addressed this issue to reduce bias and strengthen causal inference regarding the relation between caregiving and depression. Our findings are consistent with extant research indicating that high-intensity caregiving can increase the risk of depression (Capistrant, 2016; Schulz et al., 2020).

Limitations of this study include the length of time between measurements, specificity of questions regarding caregiving and care recipient, and information on other mental health interventions. The SHARE survey was conducted every two years. This means that the survey measures are limited in their granularity and may be less responsive to smaller changes to the participants’ lives, resulting in measurement error. The EUROD scale was dichotomized into a binary outcome variable because we felt that it would simplify the model. However, this did result in loss of information in that we were not able to assess the severity of depressive symptoms connected to caregiving responsibilities. We also did not have information on prior diagnoses of mental illness, which could have important confounding effect.

In addition, the exposure of this study was defined as self-reported caregiving activities in the last year. Even though the stated goal of the caregiving questions was to distinguish sustained, regular caregiving from periodic or short-term care (less than 3 months), more detailed information regarding the frequency and length of care (e.g., hours per week) would more accurately capture the intensity of caregiving and reduce potential misclassification. Furthermore, we did not have additional information on the care recipient characteristics or the quality of the relationship between caregiver and recipient, which may confound the relationship between caregiving and the caregivers’ mental health. The SHARE survey did not ask about the use of mental health services beyond medications taken to address depression or anxiety. Therefore, it was possible that caregivers experiencing more stress from caregiving were more likely to seek out mental health services, which could mitigate depressive symptoms, leading to an underestimation of the impact of caregiving effect on depressive symptoms. Finally, while SHARE provides sampling weights to account for the multi-stage sampling methodology, we did not apply the sampling weights out of considerations to keep the model simple. This limits the generalizability of our results in that our findings are not representative of the European population.

Our analysis attempted to establish temporality and address major threats to internal validity when examining the causal effect of caregiving on the mental health of caregivers. Accurate estimation of the caregiving-wellbeing relationship is foundational to creating evidence-based policies to support healthy aging. Further examination of how system-level characteristics of the caregiving environment (e.g., health service use and accessibility to other professional care services) influence caregivers’ mental health can inform strategies for mitigating potentially adverse effects of caregiving on population health; this is particularly relevant given the potential for caregiving roles to become more common as the proportion of older adults increases across higher income contexts.
