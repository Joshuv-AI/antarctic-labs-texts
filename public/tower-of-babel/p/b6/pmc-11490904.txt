Improving complexation of puerarin with kudzu starch by various ultrasonic pretreatment: Interaction mechanism analysis
Authors: Yuheng Li, Chao Zhang, Shuyi Li, Zhenzhou Zhu, Xuehua Wang, Giancarlo Cravotto
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The industrial preparation of kudzu starch (KS) significantly reduces the remaining of flavonoids like puerarin (PU) in the product, weakening its biological activity and making pre-treatments on kudzu crucial. Ultrasonic technique, widely used for modifying biomolecules, can enhance nutrient interactions like those between starch and polyphenols in foods. Thus, a puerarin-kudzu starch (PKS) complex was prepared with the introduction of ultrasonic pretreatment. The results indicated that sonication increased the binding of PU to KS from 0.399 ± 0.01 to 0.609 ± 0.05 mg/g. Particle size analysis and SEM revealed that the particles of the ultrasonic puerarin-kudzu starch complex (UPKS) were larger than those of the untreated complexes. XRD, UV–vis, and FT-IR spectroscopic analyses indicated that hydrogen bonding primarily governs the interaction between PU and KS. Additionally, incorporating PU decreased the starch structure’s orderliness, while ultrasonic treatment altered the helical configuration of straight-chain starch, leading to the formation of a new, ordered structure through the creation of new hydrogen bonds. Additionally, gels formed from UPKS exhibited higher viscosity, elasticity, and shear stress, suggesting that ultrasound significantly altered the intermolecular interactions between PKS. In conclusion, the use of ultrasound under optimal conditions has demonstrated its effectiveness in preparing PKS complexes, highlighting its significant potential to produce high value-added kudzu-based products.

Introduction

Puerarin (PU) belongs to the class of natural isoflavone compounds, renowned for their exceptional cardiovascular protection abilities as well as their health-promoting effects [1]. A range of potential bioactivities of PU, such as alleviating atherosclerosis, inhibiting myocardial hypertrophic growth, enhancing energy metabolism, and reducing inflammation and oxidative stress, have been documented in previous studies [2], [3]. Abundant in PU, kudzu has been regarded as a promising natural source of isoflavones, in line with the global trend of sustainable development. In addition, PU has the potential to treat brain disorders, avoiding the toxicity and single-action problems of most chemical drugs, and is therefore attracting attention as a new drug from natural resources [4]. However, issues related to stability and low bioavailability—both critical factors influencing efficacy, have significantly hindered the commercial development and utilization of PU [5], [6]. Starch is one of the main components of kudzu, a macromolecular polysaccharide polymerized from monosaccharides, which is widely consumed for its energy-providing properties [7]. But the loss of flavonoids during the preparation process makes it difficult for many bioactive substances, such as PU, to exert their effects in deeply processed kudzu products.

Recent studies have shown that advanced technologies such as ultrasound, microwave, and pulsed electric field (PEF) can significantly affect how food nutrients interact [8], [9]. Among these technologies, ultrasound stands out for its potential to enhance the functionality and bioavailability of biologically active constituents, particularly phenols, while minimizing changes in physicochemical and nutritional properties through high-pressure cavitation and collapse-accelerated solvent penetration [10], [11]. Ultrasound has been widely used in food processing for extracting active ingredients, modifying biomolecules, and inactivating microorganisms, leveraging mechanical vibrations, and shear forces [12], [13]. A recent study found that ultrasound disrupts macromolecular chains, breaking monomeric units and influencing the rheological properties of macromolecules [14]. Furthermore, enhancements in mass transfer linked to ultrasound can heighten the likelihood of collisions between reacting molecules or ions [15], as well as interactions between macromolecules, through structural alterations and electrostatic interactions [16]. Ultrasound technology can enhance the emulsification properties of food emulsifiers, including proteins and polysaccharides, while also improving the stability of the resulting emulsions [17]. Our previous research has demonstrated that ultrasound enhances the complexation between starches and polyphenols [10], leading to increased stability and elevated polyphenol content [18], enhanced regulation of intestinal health [19], and increased bioaccessibility [20]. However, the precise mechanism by which ultrasound affects the interaction between PU and KS, along with the physicochemical and processing properties of the resulting complexes, is not yet fully understood.

The formation of starch-polyphenol complexes improves stability and facilitates the controlled release of polyphenols, which in turn boosts their bioavailability, bioaccessibility, and bioefficacy [21]. Moreover, starch-polyphenol complexes possess the capability to withstand digestive enzymes [22]. Starch and polyphenols interact with each other during food processing and thus have received much attention, especially in the study of starch digestion and polyphenol release [23]. On the one hand, forming starch-polyphenol complexes protects the polyphenols, reduces their chemical degradation, improves their stability, and enables the unabsorbed polyphenols to reach the colon where they are released through bacterial activity [24]. It has been found that the combination of starch and polyphenols enhances the processing versatility of polyphenols by increasing their stability, thereby broadening their use in the food industry [25]. These studies suggest that integrating PU with KS can significantly enhance PU's bioavailability and functional benefits, offering promising applications across the food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic sectors. However, detailed information on environmentally friendly methods to enhance starch-polyphenol interactions remains limited, and there are reports of a low efficiency in forming polyphenol-polysaccharide complexes, as well as suboptimal yields of these complexes [9], [26]. Therefore, it is essential to investigate acceptable methods for improving the interaction between PU and KS, aiming to advance the creation of healthier and more nutritious functional ingredients and products.

This study aimed to explore the effects of ultrasound on the interaction between PU and KS, along with the underlying mechanisms. Additionally, the study evaluated the physicochemical and processing characteristics of the resulting complexes to better understand the alterations in their properties. This research offers new perspectives and techniques for advancing the deep processing technology of kudzu.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and materials

Kudzu starch (with starch comprising 83.45 % and amylose accounting for 57.42 %), provided by Hubei Gewa Foods Co., Ltd. (Zhongxiang, China), was prepared from 2021 Zhongxiang kudzu using the water extraction and filtration method. Iodine, potassium iodide, and puerarin were sourced from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All chemicals and reagents are of analytical grade and were obtained from Wuhan Xinshenshi Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. (wuhan, China).

Preparation of puerarin-kudzu starch complex complex

To determine the optimal amount of puerarin (PU) to be added, the effects of various PU concentrations on the complex were evaluated. The concentration that resulted in the highest PU content in the complex was selected for further experimentation. Accurately weighed 5 g of KS was configured with distilled water to form a 10 % starch emulsion, while a certain mass of PU (0 %-4%) was dissolved in anhydrous ethanol, and they were subsequently mixed well. The optimal addition amount was determined by the content of PU in the complex, and the sample prepared under this condition was the puerarin-kudzu starch complex (PKS) complex.

Ultrasonic treatments

To prepare the ultrasonic puerarin-kudzu starch complex (UPKS), 5 g of KS and PU (3 % of the starch mass, as determined by method 2.2) dissolved in anhydrous ethanol were combined and dispersed in 50 ml of diluted water. The fully dissolved solution was then exposed to ultrasonic treatments under various optimal conditions. Optimal parameters were selected to facilitate the formation of UPKS complexes under ultrasonic conditions at 90 W, 5 min (25 kHz, 40 kHz, 55 kHz), 25 kHz, 15 min (80 W, 90 W, 100 W, 110 W), and 25 kHz, 100 W (5 min, 10 min, 15 min). The KS solution with PU that had not undergone any treatment was used as the control.

After incubation, the complex samples were collected via centrifugation (5000 r/min, 10 min) and then washed three times with a 50 % ethanol solution to remove any unbound PU. Some of the complex samples were freeze-dried for PU content analysis, particle size measurement, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction, and scanning electron microscopy. The rest of the samples were kept at 4 °C for further testing.

Iodine binding analysis

Weigh four portions of 100 mg of KS into a beaker, add varying amounts of PU (0 %–16 % of KS), followed by 150 mL of distilled water. Heat for 30 min in a 100 °C water bath, then allow to cool. Take 30 mL of the cooling solution and add 0.2 mL of iodine reagent to let it stand for 5 min, followed by the determination of the full wavelength scan at 400–900 nm.

Preparation of iodine reagent: Weigh 2.000 ± 0.005 g of potassium iodide and add appropriate amount of water to form a saturated solution, followed by 0.200 ± 0.001 g of iodine. Once all the iodine had dissolved, the solution was adjusted to a final volume of 100 mL with distilled water, thoroughly mixed, and stored at 4 °C in a light-protected environment.

Determination of puerarin content in complexes

Accurately weigh 20 mg of PKS or UPKS sample and add 3 mL of dimethyl sulfoxide solvent to fully dissolve the sample. The complex mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min. A 2 mL aliquot of the supernatant was collected, treated with 200 μL of 95 % ethanol, and then diluted to 10 mL with distilled water. Absorbance was measured at 250 nm, and the PU content was quantified using a standard curve (y = 148.31x + 0.0102, R2 = 0.9997).

Particle size analysis

A laser particle size analyzer was used to measure the particle size of the three samples (Mastersizer 3000, Malvern, UK). These samples were dissolved in an appropriate amount of distilled water to completely dissolve. The sample powder was uniformly dispersed in deionized water and introduced into a feeder. Measurements were taken and averaged when the shading rate reached 10 %. Refractive indexes were set to 1.53 for the complex and 1.33 for the water [27].

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR)

FT-IR spectra were recorded in the range of 4000–400 cm−1 using a VERTEX 70 FT-IR spectrophotometer (Bruker, Germany) with the KBr method [28].

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

The dried samples were secured to the sample stage using conductive double-sided tape. After gold spraying, all samples were visualized with a field emission scanning electron microscope (FSEM-1, FEI, Netherlands) at 5.0 kV.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

A suitable quantity of the complex sample was applied evenly in the glass sample holder and positioned on the diffractometer (D8 ADVANCEX, BRUKER, Germany) for analysis, with a scanning range set from 1 to 60°.

Rheological properties

The rheological behavior of kudzu starch and complexes was determined using a multifunctional rheometer (Kinexus pro+, Malvern, UK). The samples were mixed with distilled water to create an 8 % (w/v) starch suspension. Following 30 min of stirring in a boiling water bath, they were placed on a test bench for analysis, using a cone-plate fixture model CP4/40. Dynamic rheological properties were tested using the oscillation mode at a temperature of 25 °C, a fixed stress of 1 % and a frequency of 0.1–10 Hz. The steady state shear rheological properties were measured in steady state mode with a shear rate of 0.1–100 s−1 [29].

Statistical analysis

The experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was assessed using Duncan’s multiple range tests with SPSS software (version 19.0, SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) (p < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Iodine binding analysis

The complexation of starch with iodine can be chromogenic because the iodine molecule uses the hydroxyl group on the exterior of the starch helix as a site of action [30]. This causes the iodine to be drawn to the axial part of the helical cavity of the starch chain, resulting in varying absorbance values based on the number of iodine molecules present in the cavity [31], [32]. Therefore, occupying the helical cavities of starch molecules or hydrogen bonding with starch chain molecules may alter the binding of starch to iodine [33]. As shown in Fig. 1A, different amounts of puerarin (PU) have almost no effect on the absorbance value of iodine at 500–900 nm. The iodine solution exhibits characteristic absorption peaks at 236, 288, and 351 nm. The addition of puerarin causes the characteristic absorption peak of iodine at 350 nm to disappear, and the other two peaks to red-shift to varying degrees depending on the amount of puerarin added. The change in peak intensity indicates that PU can react with iodine, which may be due to the reducibility of polyphenols or the hydroxyl structure of polyphenols interacting with iodine molecules [34].Fig. 1Effect of different additions of Puerarin on the absorbance values of iodine (A), iodine–starch complexes (B).

Effect of different additions of Puerarin on the absorbance values of iodine (A), iodine–starch complexes (B).

Greater absorbance values between 580 nm and 610 nm suggest a higher amylose content [35], [36]. The absorbance of all starch-iodine complexes diminished following the addition of PU (Fig. 1B), with the lowest absorbance (from 1.681 to 0.405) occurring when PU was added at 12 %. This phenomenon suggests that PU may have substituted the iodine molecules, occupied the hydrophobic cavities of amylose, or formed hydrogen bonds with starch molecules, thereby preventing the formation of starch-iodine complexes [33], [37]. Thus, the addition of PU did not completely disrupt the binding between iodine and kudzu starch (KS), but rather inhibited it, suggesting that puerarin can interact with KS without altering its internal structure.

Effect of ultrasonic treatment on the formation of kudzu starch complex

As can be seen in Fig. 2, the content of PU adsorbed in the starch complex was related to the amount of PU added. The levels of PU in the complex increased with the enhancement of PU addition, while the content of puerarin in the complex decreased when the addition of PU reached 4 % of KS (w/w). Therefore, the optimal addition amount of PU was selected as 3 % of KS, and the highest content of PU to KS was 0.399 ± 0.01 mg/g. Fig. 3 presents the PU content in ultrasonic puerarin-kudzu starch complex (UPKS) prepared under various sonication conditions, demonstrating that different sonication factors significantly impact the results. As shown in Fig. 3A, when the ultrasonic frequency reached 25 kHz, the PU content peaked at 0.479 ± 0.02 mg/g. However, a significant decrease in PU content was observed with further increases in ultrasonic frequency. This phenomenon may be due to the fact that at lower frequencies (e.g., 20 kHz), ultrasonic energy is transferred more uniformly to the complex of puerarin and KS, thus facilitating their binding. This finding is consistent with Guo et al., who observed that low-frequency ultrasonication enhanced mass-transfer efficiency and resulted in a denser distribution of NaCl in cured pork tissues, mainly due to the acoustic cavitation effect and the sponge effect [38]. Additionally, previous studies have shown that low-frequency ultrasound improves the arrangement of the crystal structure within starch granules, promoting the formation of more tightly chemically bonded starch and polyphenol complexes [39], [40]. Therefore, the ultrasonic frequency was selected to be 25 kHz in the next step.Fig. 3Effects of ultrasonic frequency (A), ultrasonic power (B), and ultrasonic time (C) on the puerarin content of UPKS. As the ultrasonic power increased, the PU content initially rose, then fell, reaching its peak at 100 W (0.609 ± 0.05 mg/g). The content of PU in the ultrasonically treated complexes was significantly higher compared to the untreated complexes (Fig. 3B). In the presence of ultrasound, the energy of mechanical vibrations and shear forces is absorbed by the molecular chains of starch, which may alter its structure [41]. This process can reveal hydrophobic groups and create more adsorption sites for polyphenols. Previous studies have shown that ultrasonication can change the structure of starch, creating voids and cracks in starch granules, thereby allowing starch and polyphenols to bind more tightly [42]. However, at higher ultrasonic power (110 W), the content of PU showed a decreasing trend. Here are two potential explanations for this phenomenon. Firstly, with excessive ultrasound, the structure of starch undergoes more complex changes, and the generated pores and cracks may become too large and unstable, leading to the fracture and depolymerization of the monomeric units [43]. In addition, it impacted the interaction with polyphenols, leading to a reduction in binding. Liu et al. observed that changes in the composition and structure of polysaccharides affect their interactions with polyphenols, particularly influencing the polysaccharides' structural characteristics [44]. Therefore, the ultrasonic power was selected to be 100 W in the next step. As depicted in Fig. 3C, an increase in ultrasonication time results in a higher PU content. When the sonication time is longer, the sample spends more time in the reaction chamber, thereby increasing the energy input to enhance the binding of PU to KS. Overall, the optimized conditions for ultrasonication were ultrasonic frequency of 25 kHz, ultrasonic power of 100 W, and ultrasonic time of 15 min. Under these conditions, the maximum PU content in the UPKS complex reached 0.609 ± 0.05 mg/g, which was 52.63 % higher than that of the untreated complex. This demonstrates the potential of PKS complexes in polyphenol-rich functional foods, as they can help preserve polyphenols during food processing and improve their bioavailability.

Effect of ultrasonic treatment on the particle size and structure of puerarin-kudzu starch complexes

The particle sizes of kudzu starch and puerarin-kudzu starch complexes were shown in Fig. 4A, with sizes generally within 100 μm. The particle size distribution of the PKS (puerarin-kudzu starch complex) complex was shifted compared to KS, and the average size of the particles became larger, from 15.4 µm to 19.9 µm. This increase in particle size is attributed to the substantial binding of PU to the KS particles. The particle size distribution peak of UPKS also migrated to 17.5 µm, which was in accordance with the results from Fig. 4A, indicating that the content of PU in the complex increased after the ultrasonic treatment. Although the content of PU increased, the displacement of UPKS peaks decreased compared to PKS peaks due to the fragmentation of starch granules by ultrasonication, and the peak areas of PKS and UPKS increased compared to KS, which further proved that there was a combination of substances bound to starch granules, resulting in an increase in the size of starch granules.Fig. 4The particle size distribution of kudzu starch and puerarin-kudzu starch complexes (A), SEM images (B).

The particle size distribution of kudzu starch and puerarin-kudzu starch complexes (A), SEM images (B).

Fig. 4B shows the morphology of the granules of KS, PKS, and UPKS at SEM magnifications of 500×, 5 k×, and 20 k×, respectively. Observations at 500× and 5 k× magnification revealed that the KS granules were primarily elliptical and irregular polygonal in shape, consistent with the morphology of natural starch observed in previous studies [45]. At 20 k× magnification, holes can be seen on the surface of UPKS starch granules, which may be due to the ultrasound breaking the surface integrity of the starch granules, causing the integrity of the granules to be damaged. This structural change may have exposed more hydrophobic groups in the starch, enabling PU to bind more strongly to the starch granules. This explains the increased PU content in the UPKS complex based on granule morphology. A similar phenomenon was observed by Hu et al., demonstrating that ultrasonication can enhance the binding of starch to flavonoids [46].

Effect of ultrasonic treatment on the long-range crystalline order of puerarin-kudzu starch complexes

The X-ray diffractograms and relative crystallinity of the natural and sonicated samples were shown in Fig. 5A. All three samples produced distinct diffraction peaks at 15°, 17°, 18°, and 23°. The puerarin-kudzu starch complex formation did not modify the starch's crystalline type, and high-field ultrasound treatment did not affect the A-type crystalline structure of KS. The X-ray diffraction patterns of the three starch samples showed differences. A sharp dwarf peak appeared at about 2θ = 11° for KS, but disappeared for PKS and UPKS. Additionally, the crystallinity of both PKS and UPKS was higher compared to that of KS. The X-diffraction pattern of starch exhibits a V-shaped structure with distinct diffraction peaks at 7.4°, 13.1° and 20.1°. However, Fig. 5A presented that these characteristic peaks were absent in the puerarin-kudzu starch complexes. It was suggested that puerarin was primarily bound between the straight-chain starch molecules through hydrogen bonding interactions. The incorporation of puerarin led to a modest increase in the crystallinity of KS, and the interaction between PU and KS enhanced the crystallinity order. It was observed that ultrasonication did not disrupt this ordered structure. However, a distinct sharp peak appeared at about 11° for KS, which disappeared in the PKS and UPKS samples. Because of sonication, the crystallinity of PKS and UPKS was increased from 49.96 % to 50.26 % compared to KS. The absence of characteristic diffraction peaks of the V-type structure of starch at 7.4°, 13.1°, and 20.1°, as observed by Lorentz et al. [47], implied that geraniol is most likely bound to straight-chain starch molecules through hydrogen bonding interactions. Effect of ultrasonic treatment on the short-range ordered molecular structure of puerarin-kudzu starch complexes

To explore the impact of ultrasonic treatment on the interaction between PU and KS, we analyzed the FT-IR spectra of KS subjected to various treatments, as depicted in Fig. 5B. Previous studies have classified the FT-IR spectra into four regions: below 800 cm−1, 800–1500 cm−1 (fingerprint region), 3000–2800 cm−1 (C-H stretching region), and 3000–3600 cm−1 (O-H stretching region) [48]. As shown in Fig. 5B, the ultrasonication and physical mixing of the complexes did not result in a significant displacement of the absorption peaks or the disappearance and appearance of the absorption peaks as compared to the natural starch, which indicates that there is no binding through covalent bonds in the complexes. The shift in the –OH stretching vibration peak indicates the presence of hydrogen bonding, with a larger blue shift range indicating stronger intermolecular hydrogen bonding forces. From Fig. 5B, it was evident that the intermolecular hydrogen bonding force follows the order UPKS > PKS > KS. This hierarchy could be attributed to ultrasonication exposing numerous hydrophobic groups in the helical structure of starch [49], thereby enhancing its binding affinity with the –OH groups on PU. This observation further evidenced that ultrasound facilitates the binding of PU and starch by disrupting starch structure and leveraging hydrogen bonding interactions.

Previous studies have found that FT-IR bands close to 1047 cm−1 and 1022 cm−1 correlate with ordered and amorphous structures, respectively, and thus the orderliness of the starch structure can be characterised by the R1047/1022 ratio [50]. As can be seen in Fig. 5C, R1047/1022 of KS was 1.109, which was reduced to 1.024 with the addition of PU, indicating that PU reduced the structural ordering of the starch. Interestingly, the introduction of ultrasound increased R1047/1022 to 1.543, and ultrasound enhanced the linkage between PU and starch chains, favouring the formation of a relatively ordered structure, which is in agreement with Ding et al [43].

Effect of ultrasonic treatment on the rheological properties of puerarin-kudzu starch complexes

Dynamic rheological properties describe how a fluid's viscoelasticity changes under stress, whereas static rheological properties reflect variations in internal viscosity and stress under shear. Both are crucial for guiding processing quality and quality control in products.

G′ represents the elastic component of the sample, and G′′ represents the viscous component. As shown in Fig. 6A, G′ consistently exceeds G′′throughout the frequency scanning process, indicating that elasticity predominates in the starch system [51]. It was demonstrated that even after complexation with puerarin, the system remains a solid-phase-like weak gel [52]. The tanδ values for all three samples are less than 1 (Fig. 6B), indicating that they exhibit predominantly elastic behavior after pasting. The tanδ values increase with frequency, signifying an increase in the viscous component. Following the formation of PKS complexes, the viscous behavior decreases while the elastic behavior increases. The ability of a starch pastes to resist fluidity is referred to as shear viscosity, and its magnitude is quantified by shear viscosity measurements. As illustrated in Fig. 6C, the shear viscosity of the starch paste decreases sharply as the shear rate increases, eventually stabilizing. This behavior can be attributed to the disruption of the network structure [53] of the starch gel under shear, causing more starch molecules to flow and resulting in a decrease in apparent viscosity. When the shear rate reaches approximately 20 s−1, the shear viscosity stabilizes. There is no significant difference in the viscosity among KS, PKS, and UPKS samples.

The starch pastes systems of the three samples exhibited an increase in shear stress with increasing shear rate, which leveled off when the shear rate reached approximately 20 s−1. At first, a significant shear stress is necessary to break down the starch gel's network structure. This disruption allows more starch molecules to transition from the network structure to directional flow, exhibiting liquid-like behavior, which leads to a leveling off of the shear stress [54]. The shear stress of PKS exceeds that of KS, likely because of the increased PU content and the strengthened intermolecular hydrogen-bonding interactions. This highlights the potential of PKS complexes in cosmetics, particularly in products like lotions and creams, which require ease of application while maintaining their texture and performance throughout use.

Conclusion

The study involved the preparation of puerarin-kudzu starch complexes with the introduction of different ultrasonic treatments. The obtained PKS complexes had a maximum binding amount of puerarin (PU) of 0.399 ± 0.01 mg/g, while puerarin-kudzu starch complexes prepared using ultrasonic pretreatment resulted in a higher PU binding amount, with the ultrasonic puerarin-kudzu starch complex (UPKS) complex having a maximum binding amount of PU of 0.609 ± 0.05 mg/g. Iodine binding assay, particle size determination, field emission scanning electron microscopy, and X-ray diffraction were used to investigate the interactions between PU and KS, as well as the effects of ultrasonic treatment. It was found that PU and KS can interact through mixing, but their binding capacity is relatively weak, and no covalent binding occurs. This interaction does not alter the starch's crystalline form and is not a V-type inclusion complex but involves hydrogen bonding. Additionally, ultrasound can promote PU binding to KS by disrupting starch granules and exposing more hydroxyl groups, resulting in tighter hydrogen bonding. Incorporating ultrasound technology can transform KS into a functional food with enhanced physicochemical properties and biological activities, offering new insights into kudzu applications.
