Placebo setting in trials of traditional Chinese medicine nursing techniques: a systematic critical review1
Authors: Lixia Chen, Ying Zeng, Dezheng Zhu, Ting Wang, Min You, Hui Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn the clinical research field of Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) nursing techniques, there are an increasing number of randomized controlled trial (RCTs) involving placebo control settings, but the quality of literature reports remains unclear. This paper aims to assess the reporting quality of placebo-controlled RCTs focusing on TCM nursing techniques.MethodsA comprehensive search was conducted on PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), WanFang Database, Chongqing Weipu (VIP), and China Biology Medicine (CBM) from inception to December 3, 2023. The search included RCTs with a placebo control group related to clinical studies on TCM nursing techniques. The Consolidated Standards for Reporting of Trials (CONSORT) and Template for Intervention Description and Replication-Placebo (TIDieR-Placebo) criteria were employed to assess the standardization of literature reporting.ResultsA total of 39 papers were included, covering 15 TCM nursing techniques with a placebo control group designed for the treatment. Common placebo controls included “fake application of real points,“"real application of fake points,” and changes in the medium. The CONSORT evaluation revealed reporting rates of less than 20% for entries 3b, 6b, 11b, 12b, 14b, 16, 17b, and 18. In the TIDieR-Placebo evaluation, the reporting rates of entries 5, 6, 7, 9, 10, 11, and 13 were all below 20%.ConclusionsThe current literature reporting quality of RCTs incorporating placebo control groups in clinical research on TCM nursing techniques is generally suboptimal. Researchers are advised to conscientiously use CONSORT and TIDieR-Placebo guidelines to enhance the reporting standards of research, fostering greater standardization in clinical research on TCM nursing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12906-025-05125-0.

Background

Since the publication of Prof. Beecher’s seminal paper titled “The Powerful Placebo” in 1955 in The Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) [1], the placebo has remained a focal point of interest and discussion within the medical and psychological research communities. Defined as a preparation mirroring the experimental group in appearance, color, odor, formulation, usage, and route of administration but lacking the therapeutic efficacy of the treatment [2], the placebo serves as a benchmark for assessing the effectiveness of an active intervention. Despite its widespread use as a control in various clinical studies, recent findings indicate a lack of detailed descriptions of placebos, even in papers published in leading journals [3]. Insufficient characterization of placebo controls can compromise the interpretation of intervention efficacy and make it difficult to replicate the trial. Recognizing this, Howick et al. [4] introduced the TIDieR-Placebo in 2020, aiming to guide the comprehensive reporting of placebo measures.

TCM nursing techniques, mainly comprising 18 widely-used, safe, cost-effective, and easily implementable techniques, are predominantly administered by nursing staff in China. These include cupping, Gua Sha, moxibustion, auricular acupressure, acupoint massage, and external applications therapies of TCM endorsed by the State Administration of TCM [5]. Rooted in the TCM theoretical system, they draw upon the meridian theory and qi-blood doctrine established in The Yellow Emperor’s Inner Canon. Their mechanism involves stimulating specific acupoints or body surfaces to regulate qi-blood circulation, embodying the therapeutic principle that “external treatments follow the same rationale as internal treatments.” Contemporary research indicates that these techniques operate through a shared neuro-endocrine-immune regulatory mechanism [6]. Clinical analyses further highlight their primary applicability to two core TCM syndrome patterns: (1) qi stagnation with blood stasis and (2) yang deficiency with cold congelation. Notably, they demonstrate significant efficacy overlap in managing pain-related disorders and suboptimal health states [7].

Internationally, since the release of Development Plan for TCM in the Belt and Road Initiative (2016–2020) of China [8], TCM nursing is currently being carried out in 183 countries and regions worldwide [9]. In China, there are currently over 1.033 million medical healthcare institutions, which range from large-scale TCM hospitals, comprehensive hospitals, and community health service center in various provinces and cities, to county hospitals, TCM clinics, and township hospitals [10]. Under the guidance of the national policy, these healthcare institutions have carried out various TCM nursing techniques. The diverse applications of each TCM nursing technique, coupled with increasing patient satisfaction [11], have positioned them as valuable therapeutic options. Yet, certain techniques, such as cupping and Gua Sha, face skepticism regarding their efficacy, particularly in comparison to widely accepted practices like acupuncture, utilized in over 100 countries. Scholars at home and abroad attribute the effectiveness of these techniques to the placebo effect [12], a specific psychological response observed when patients undergo interventions devoid of therapeutic effect [13].

Although the 18 techniques vary in form, they all qualify as TCM external therapies and are implemented by nursing staff via standardized protocols. This results in shared placebo design challenges, such as simulating herbal odors and ensuring the feasibility of placebo materials. This fully justifies both their grouping under “TCM nursing techniques” and our approach to collectively assess reporting quality. Also crucially, this study focuses not on evaluating the efficacy of individual techniques, but on assessing the methodological quality of placebo control reporting in RCTs across these interventions. The CONSORT and TIDieR-Placebo criteria are universal reporting standards designed to evaluate transparency and replicability of trial methods, irrespective of clinical heterogeneity among interventions [4]. Thus, while TCM nursing techniques vary in form (e.g., auricular therapy vs. moxibustion), their shared dependence on non-pharmacological mechanisms necessitates comparable placebo control strategies—making standardized reporting quality assessment both feasible and methodologically justified.

Implementing a robust placebo control is crucial to mitigating bias arising from patients’ factors, thereby facilitating the production of high-quality evidence in RCTs [14]. The field of TCM nursing is evolving, evidenced by the growing number of studies incorporating placebo controls like ‘fake’ patches and ‘sham’ massages [15]. Nevertheless, the methodological nuances, characteristics, and applicability of placebo control groups remain unclear. This study focuses on the published clinical studies involving placebo groups, employing the CONSORT in conjunction with TIDieR-Placebo to evaluate the reporting quality of placebo-involved RCTs that meet the requisite standards. The evaluation results contribute to a discussion on the current state of placebo utilization in TCM nursing techniques and offer insights as a reference for future trial designs.

Methods

Search strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted from the inception to December 3, 2023 in eight selected databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, Cochrane Library, CNKI, WanFang Database, VIP, and CBM. Adhering to the search guidelines outlined in the Cochrane Collaboration’s workbook [16], the search employed subject terms, by combining medical subject words and free words. For instance, PubMed was searched using the strategy detailed in Appendix 1. Additionally, reference lists of relevant articles and included studies were reviewed, and manual searches for grey literature were also conducted.

Included and excluded criteria

Types of studies

The search targeted RCTs of TCM nursing techniques involving placebo group.

Types of participants

No restrictions were imposed on participant characteristics, including age, gender, disease, symptoms, or ethnicity.

Types of intervention

The State Administration of TCM categorized 18 TCM nursing techniques into three difficulty levels (high, medium, and low) [17]. Yet, after further research in 2023, the standardization of TCM nursing technique management program was refined, yielding 56 TCM nursing techniques across eight categories, including cupping, massage, moxibustion, acupuncture, Gua Sha, TCM hot compress and ironing, TCM fumigation, and Qi Gong [18]. The treatment group employed one of these therapies alone or in combination with medication, while the control group used a placebo instead. Placebo control methods included medium without therapeutic effect but of the same shape, color, formulation, weight, and even odor. The control group utilized the placebo control method.

We excluded publications that met any of the following criteria: (i) Studies not investigating TCM nursing techniques, e.g., acupuncture; (ii) Inadequate description of placebo/control groups; (iii) Unavailability of full texts (including conference abstracts only), duplication, or publications where insufficient data were reported for analysis (e.g., brief reports, letters without primary data); (iv) Non-English/Non-Chinese language; (v) Review articles (systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews).

Data extraction

Two researchers (Chen LX and Zeng Y) conducted the literature search using the established formula and imported the articles into NoteExpress V.3.2.0. for duplicate check. Subsequently, they conducted initial screening by reading titles and abstracts, followed by a detailed examination of full texts. Then they independently extracted the basic study information, including author, publication year, disease/symptom, TCM nursing techniques, sample size, intervention protocol of experimental group, placebo-controlled format and randomization using Microsoft Excel 2016. Arbitration by the third researcher (Shi H) constituted a final and binding decision to resolve discrepancies between the initial reviewers, aligning with the conflict resolution framework recommended by PRISMA 2020 [19].

Assessment of reporting quality

Following the guidelines set forth in CONSORT and TIDieR-Placebo lists [4, 20], two reviewers (Chen LX and Zhu DZ) meticulously evaluated each eligible literature item by item, marking the item “yes” or “no” based on whether it was reported or not. Any discrepancies in literature screening, extraction, or evaluation were reported to a third researcher (Shi H) for resolution.

Results

According to the defined search strategy, 4087 documents were initially identified. Duplicates were removed and 3390 articles remained. Following rigorous screening, we excluded publications that were outside the thematic scope, employed an ineligible study design, lacked a clear description of placebo control, had unavailable full texts, or were reviews/summaries. An additional 7 records were identified through supplementary search methods: five via backward citation tracking of reference lists from the included studies and two via forward citation tracking of the included studies using Web of Science. Following screening against our eligibility criteria, these supplementary records contributed to the final set of 39 included documents [21–59]. Study characteristics

Out of the 39 included papers, 21 [22–24, 27, 28, 30, 32–34, 39, 40, 44–47, 49–52, 54, 59] were in English and 18 [21, 25, 26, 29, 31, 35–38, 41–43, 48, 53, 55–58] in Chinese, originating from 7 countries, with the majority (32) conducted in China [21, 23–26, 28, 29–32, 34–38, 41–46, 48–58]. Besides, 2 studies from Iran [33, 40], and 1 study from Australia [27], Brazil [47], the United Kingdom [22], the United States [39] and South Korea [59], respectively. The year 2022 saw the highest number of publications, totalling 8. The sample sizes ranged from 22 to 334, with a 1:1 allocation ratio in experimental and control group. The TCM nursing techniques covered acupressure, acupoint patch, ear acupressure, Gua Sha, moxibustion, cupping therapy, thumb-tack needle, wrist-ankle acupuncture, TCM moist-heat therapy, TCM wax therapy, TCM hot compress, TCM enema, TCM fumigation, TCM nebulization, and TCM ion introduction therapy, totalling 15 TCM nursing techniques. The current status of placebo group in this area is outlined as follows

Acupressure

5 studies [32, 33, 39, 40, 44] opted for stimulating non-acupuncture points, generally selecting nearby non-acupuncture points or identifying non-acupuncture points based on the acupuncture point chart. The strength, duration, and course of treatment were consistent with the experimental group. Another 3 studies [22, 27, 34] employed non-pressure wristbands at the same acupoints or milder massage strength.

Acupoint application

Placebo patches took various forms, including ointment (comprising honey, beeswax, vegetable oil, caramel, and starch) [53], paste (mixing wheat flour or buckwheat flour with water) [29, 58], and medicinal cake (comprising wheat flour or buckwheat flour with water) [35, 38].

Ear pressure

Sham auricular pressure involved applying blank adhesive patches to the same auricular points [30, 49] or stimulating other ear points without therapeutic effect [50, 54, 59].

Moxibustion

The route of placebo control included applying moxibustion at non-acupoint site [51], reducing thermal radiation [24, 31, 52], and substituting moxa materials with wood powder, sticky powder, and spices [28, 57].

Cupping

Techniques involved puncture cupping, using the handle of an acupuncture needle to stimulate the skin without piercing it [35], and dry cupping, employing a vacuum cupper with a small hole at the upper end and securing the cupping body with double-sided adhesive tape [47, 57].

Gua Sha

Placebo setting was primarily achieved by shortening treatment duration, reducing scraping intensity, decreasing the scraping angle, or altering the scraping medium [36, 41].

Wrist-ankle acupuncture and thumb-tack needle

The placebo effect control involved amputating the tip of the needle to make it blunt, gently stabbing the acupoint without piercing the skin, and covering the site with tape [21, 42].

TCM external application techniques

Placebo control for TCM waxing, TCM hot compress, TCM enema, TCM fumigation, TCM cupping, TCM nebulization, and TCM ion introduction was mainly achieved by preparing a placebo TCM solution or ointment with the same color as the experimental group but lacking therapeutic effects [25, 26, 37, 43, 46, 48, 55, 56].

Discussion

This study encompassed 39 RCTs involving TCM nursing techniques with placebo group, evaluating the quality of the literatures. The analysis utilized the CONSORT statement for assessing overall literature quality and the TIDieR-Placebo checklist for appraising the quality of placebos in the control groups. The findings revealed a broad spectrum of placebo-controlled studies. The field of TCM nursing techniques currently lacks systematic literature quality assessments specifically addressing placebo-controlled trials. As the most validated placebo model in TCM, sham acupuncture offers critical methodological insights for analogous studies. A review of 609 sham acupuncture RCTs revealed that whereas basic procedural parameters (e.g., acupoint selection, treatment frequency, needle retention time) were reported in over 75% of studies, key mechanistic elements exhibited markedly lower reporting rates: needle insertion depth (39.7%), operator training background (12.5%), and blinding efficacy verification (15.8%) [60]. This discrepancy mirrors recurrent methodological flaws in TCM nursing RCTs, including unspecified operator qualifications and incomplete intervention descriptions, reflecting a pervasive trend in TCM placebo research that prioritizes procedural compliance over mechanistic transparency. Notably, two studies consistently identified severe reporting deficiencies—blinding assessment (15.8% vs. 0% in this study) and protocol amendment documentation (0% in both studies). The consistency of these methodological shortcomings across different TCM nursing techniques highlights the urgent need for establishing TCM-specific placebo reporting standards. This is particularly critical given the potential of such standards to improve research reproducibility and methodological rigor. Thus, certain challenges and shortcomings were identified, necessitating enhanced efforts in placebo control methodologies to improve blinding success rates.

Firstly, after a review of the CONSORT statement, several issues were identified:

Trial Design and Outcome Indicator Changes (Entries 3b and 6b): A 0% reporting rate was observed for both trial design changes and outcome indicator changes. While clinical trials should adhere strictly to protocols, exploratory trials may deviate from the initial protocol due to various emerging issues. Detailed reporting of such changes is crucial to maintain trial credibility.

Outcome Indicators (Entry 6a): Approximately 38.46% studies reported primary and secondary outcome indicators. Primary outcome indicators are the most clinically relevant and descriptive, while secondary outcome indicators serve to supplement or address secondary research questions [61]. Clearly delineating both primary and secondary indicators is vital for conveying the overall research questions and findings.

Additional Methods of Analysis (Entry 12b) and Ancillary Analysis of Outcomes (Entry 18): Limited consideration was given to subgroup analyses or corrective analyses of study outcomes. Detailed reporting of predefined and newly attempted analyses is necessary to enhance transparency [4].

Trial Interruption or Discontinuation (Entry 14b): A 0% reporting rate was noted. Clear explanations for trial interruptions or discontinuations are crucial, as early discontinuation based on outcome benefits can lead to unreliable study results [4].

Outcomes and Estimates (Entry 17b): No studies reported absolute effects, and a 0% reporting rate was observed for relative effects (relative risk and odds ratio). The concept of absolute effects is primarily associated with the measurement of risk difference, whereas relative effects are more commonly referenced in terms of relative risk and odds ratio [62]. Reporting both absolute and relative effects is essential to mitigate the risk of overestimating efficacy.

Blinding of Subjects and Healthcare Providers (Entry 11b): A reporting rate of 12.82% indicated limited emphasis on ensuring intervention characteristics’ similarity for successful blinded implementation.

Intent-to-Treat Analysis (Entry 16): A reported rate of 17.95% highlighted the importance of this analysis to avoid bias due to the loss of participants.

Secondly, regarding TIDieR-Placebo evaluation results, three entries did not meet the 10% reporting rate. Challenges were attributed to the list’s availability from 2020 onwards, and many studies were not conducted in recent years. The lack of a standardized reporting process for placebo use contributed to the suboptimal reporting rate. Specific concerns included:

Method of Operation (Entry 6): A 15.38% reporting rate suggested the need for detailed reporting. The specificity of TCM nursing techniques need direct interaction between the researcher and patient. This involves individual interventions on one or multiple patients simultaneously to document the detailed method of operation. It is also important to consider the potential impact of certain delivery factors [63].

Place of Treatment (Entry 7): While the majority described patient recruitment sites, few detailed the intervention delivery location, which could influence patient psychology and outcomes.

Personalized Programs (Entry 9): A 0% reporting rate indicated a lack of detailed reporting for situations requiring a change in intervention. TCM nursing techniques are guided by TCM theory, which emphasizes that treatment should be based on the syndrome differentiation. This results in variations in the selection of acupoints for treating different patients. When a researcher encounters a situation that necessitates a change in the intervention, a comprehensive report is required to facilitate the interpretation of the results.

Protocol Modifications (Entry 10): A 0% reporting rate highlighted the need for detailed reporting if protocol modifications occurred during the trial.

Blinded Assessments (Entry 13): A 0% reporting rate underscored the importance of assessing the success rate of blinding in placebo-controlled trials. The placebo effect, a substance or procedure, aids individuals in altering their psychological and physiological responses [64]. One of its key attributes is the ability to simulate the effects of a treatment [65]. Consequently, the efficacy of blinding in both placebo and treatment groups should be assessed as thoroughly as possible in placebo-controlled trials. Regrettably, this has not been adequately emphasized in most contemporary studies.

Thirdly, a critical appraisal of global research reveals three predominant methodological limitations in placebo-controlled RCTs of TCM nursing techniques: (i) inadequate reporting of core intervention parameters, (ii) insufficient rigor in blinding assessment, and (iii) non-standardized placebo protocols. Our team’s prior scoping review of 36 RCTs encompassing 15 distinct TCM nursing interventions further validated these findings, demonstrating that only 22.2% studies employed effective placebo controls. This control deficiency appears exacerbated by multifaceted confounding factors that complicate placebo effect mitigation in TCM therapeutic contexts [66]. It is crucial that improve the placebo control methodologies, and enhance the quality of reports on the RCTs involving placebo effect of TCM nursing techniques. Initially, in the selection of subjects in the placebo group, selecting patients without treatment experience for “fake application of real acupuncture points” as controls to reduce the risk of breaking the blind. Subsequently, attention should be paid to the selection of placebo devices and intervention methods. For example, improving the control device of moxibustion and placebo moxibustion can use a moxa box made of light reflection material based on the use of heat insulation materials and the replacement of moxa. When using fake cupping as a control, the patient adapts to a suction sensation for 1–2 min before slowly releasing the negative pressure inside the device. For the “true acupoint application”, choosing the non-acupoint located in the remote part of the meridian can avoid the “general acupoint effect” to a certain extent. Ultimately, improving the confusion or similarity of placebo medication. Adding low-dose drugs or odor agents to the placebo formula can improve its smell similarity. For instance, placebo moxibustion materials can be selected with wheat bran, wormwood to improve the quality of placebo control effects.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the TIDieR-Placebo tool, developed in 2020, was used to assess studies dating back to 2010 without considering its applicability to earlier research, which might have led to inaccurate evaluations. Secondly, only Chinese and English literature were included, ignoring other languages and possibly missing relevant evidence. Thirdly, the subjective judgment in evaluating adherence to reporting guidelines could introduce bias and weaken the reliability of the results.

Conclusion

In short, RCTs hold a higher position in evidence-based medicine, as methodological quality and report quality can influence whether medical professionals accept and trust the research, which in turn affects medical decision-making [67]. The successful establishment of high-quality RCTs in this field will contribute to the development of TCM nursing, further improving the quality of evidence and enhancing clinical practice. The study emphasizes the need for meticulous reporting of trial changes, outcomes, and methods of analysis. Researchers should use the CONSORT and TIDieR-Placebo checklists to standardize reporting and promote transparency in clinical research on TCM nursing techniques.
