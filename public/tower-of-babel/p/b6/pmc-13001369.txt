Conceptual pitfalls in the forest carbon debate
Authors: Christian Körner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractForests stock up to 90% of the global terrestrial plant biomass carbon (C). Any rise or fall of that stock, but also its utilization for substituting fossil resources can influence the rate of atmospheric CO2 enrichment. By employing the term ‘C sequestration’, the ongoing debate suffers, however, from an implicit confusion between (1) processes, rates or fluxes of C (e.g. tree growth) with (2) pools, stores or stocks of forest biomass C. Stock formation is driven by turnover, C duration, residence time, or tree demography, and not by the rate of influx of C, including tree growth. Enhanced tree growth must not be treated as a rise in C stock, without accounting for turnover, also removing often assumed benefits of CO2 fertilization for stock formation, should tree growth be C limited, another questionable assumption. A carbon ‘sink’ is a potential volume that can be filled with C, but it does not represent a stock either, without accounting for C residence time. ‘Buying time’ by lengthening rotation has a cost in terms of reduced utilization of forest products for substitution of fossil resources. Finally, management cessation for biodiversity benefits, should be qualified by its conservation value, rather than by making a case for C storage benefits, without accounting for natural forest gap dynamics, and again, without pricing-in the inevitable cost of the cessation of the substitution of fossil C by renewable C. All this calls for a strict separation of the meaning of carbon fluxes and carbon stocks, and avoiding ambiguous terms such as C sequestration and C sink.Keywords: CO2, Carbon sink, Mortality, Sequestration, Substitution, Storage, Tree growth

The meaning of C sequestration

The debate about the role of forests in the regional and global C cycle has attracted a wide research and lay community. Following that discourse over the years, I noticed a continuously unresolved terminology issues and conceptual misunderstandings. Best practice in science starts with agreed upon, well-defined terminology. In my view the terms ‘carbon sequestration’ and ‘carbon sink’ are among the most confusing and misused terms, and are the core of flawed data interpretation and misguided carbon policy.

According to the big encyclopedias and dictionaries (e.g. Mariam-Webster), carbon sequestration is ‘the process of capturing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere…’ (which should better read ‘carbon’ and not CO2). Others such as the US Geological Survey add ‘…and storing it…’. Again, others refer to the process of storing C. Defined as a process, the speed of which is a rate (transfer of mass per unit of time), the term bears no what-so-ever measure of how much carbon has actually been moved from the atmosphere to the biosphere or geosphere. Yet, often this is an implicit assumption and became the mainstream understanding of the term ‘sequestration’ (e.g. [17]; see the next paragraph). The processes of capturing and storing are employed, as if they were synonymous with the size of pools, stocks or stores. While individual trees remain carbon sinks throughout their lives, the carbon balance of entire stands approaches neutrality, if they remain undisturbed [48]; see the excellent reviews by McGarth et al. [40]; Novick et al. [47]. While individual trees might sequester carbon to a forest, other trees might be dying or become harvested at the same time. Hence, the important point is the net change of the carbon stock size over larger areas, accounting for tree turnover. A similar confusion between fluxes and pools can be found in the soil carbon literature as explained in depth by Don et al. [14]; see also ref. [39].

Taking the flux for the pool, is as if one would take money turnover for a change in capital. What matters is the fate of C, once it had been moved from the atmosphere to a leaf or a whole plant, often addressed as turnover (time), carbon duration, carbon transit time [41], or carbon residence time [6]. The residence time of assimilated C may be almost zero, a season long, last a few years, hundreds of years, or thousands of years. It is not the individual C-atom’s residence time, but the net-duration among all carbon in the system that matters. A tree’s growth, as evidenced by the width of its growth-rings, for instance, contributes to the C pool (or stock or capital) only, if no other tree dies at the same time. Tree growth, or the wood production of a parcel, are thus, unsuitable measures of a net gain in the larger ecosystem’s carbon stock. Net carbon storage in trees or an entire ecosystem, results from a demographic process in which birth, growth, decline and death of trees are accounted for (e.g. [9, 32, 63], and recycling of debris, as well as soil C dynamics need to be known. In contrast, even the most prominent forest carbon literature keeps confusing C fluxes with C pools, by considering the process of C sequestration a metric of C storage that can mitigate the rise in atmospheric CO2 concentration (e.g. [4, 19, 54, 60].

A modern approximation of the required net biome carbon gain is the net biome productivity (NBP), a term accounting for all losses, including that by fires [13, 59, 64]. On a given piece of land, the net ecosystem productivity (NEP), as produced by eddy covariance towers, is a reliable measure of that test area’s carbon balance (for definitions and references see ref. [35]. However, there may be mosaics of different ecosystem states of NEP in a landscape, and the gain of one parcel may be balanced or exceeded by the loss of another parcel. An honest treatment of the subject is thus tied to large geographical scales and long enough periods. It remains a miracle to me, why gross primary production (GPP) became so popular. GPP cannot be measured. It results from adding approximations of ecosystem respiration (R) to measured NEP signals. Yet, R is masked through photosynthesis during the day, and differences between day and night (when R measurements are possible) obscure the calculations. Most importantly, GPP is not related to the C balance or the C stock, but reflects a sort of proxy for gross photosynthesis of the system, carbon that becomes largely recycled, before it enters long-term pools. Hence no correlations have been found between GPP and tree growth [12].

In other words, the first step in a fruitful debate of the role of forests in the regional and global carbon cycle, is refraining from confusing rates (carbon sequestration, sink activity) with pools (stocks), accounting for forest dynamics (tree mortality, demography), and adopting the appropriate scales in space and time [8–10, 43]. Appropriate scales in space must include all stages of forest development in a region (commonly > 1 km2) and appropriate time scales need to account for the rates of tree turnover, either that by management or the natural one. The mortality rate applied has to account for tree and forest age [50]. Carbon sequestration refers to a process by which carbon is transferred to a potential storage volume (a sink) that might become a C stock, the size of which is depending on C residence time.

Can ‘buying time’ help mitigating the rise in atmospheric CO2?

In managed forests, which operate below the long-term steady state stock in the sense of Odum [48], management can lengthen turnover, often addressed as ‘buying time’ (e.g. [38]; see below). Associated with the flux vs. pool confusion, the ‘buying time’ idea, assumes that by lengthening rotation, by enhancing growth rates under a fixed rotation regime (Fig. 1), or by abandoning management, atmospheric CO2 accumulation can be slowed. First, this has a time limit because trees have size and age limits, thus, trading a C release wave to the not so far future. Second, and more importantly, allowing the forest stock to ramp up, needs to account for the inevitable ‘cost’ of reduced fossil C substitution by forest products. Obviously, there is a tradeoff between forest C storage and utilizing forest products. While substitution options might diminish as society works toward a C neutral future [22], building with timber and other wood products will remain a mitigation option. Delaying harvest in one place, but harvesting the needed forest products elsewhere, zeros the buying time effect (also termed ‘C leaking’ [1]. The alternative would be a shrinking of the market for forest products, opposite to what the enhanced utilization of renewable products calls for. ‘Utilizing’, includes storage of C in wood products with a mean additional residence time of c. 20 years estimated by Profft et al. [52]. Buying time also relies on forest persistence. Trees are more likely to become affected by bark beetles, drought, wind break and other stochastic disturbances as they get older [43]. When such events do occur, a C certificate loses its value, further questioning C certificate trade related to a buying time strategy (such as delayed harvesting).

Can elevated CO2 concentrations contribute a ‘buying-time‘ effect?

Forests are often assumed to ramp up their C stock faster due to a CO2-fertilization effect - another potential avenue along which a ‘buying time’ effect can come into action. From the above it should be clear that growth acceleration, for what-ever reason, must never be confused with a rise in stocks. Faster growing trees are likely to turnover faster or become harvested earlier [5, 8, 11]. Such a stimulated productivity could, however, feed the timber market (substitution). Faster growth would require an accelerated nutrient cycle and a (transitorily) bigger biomass nutrient stock. How likely is it that soil nutrients permit a CO2 stimulation of tree growth, that is, a steeper ‘buying time’ ramp?

For any organism to make a living, it requires around 22 chemical elements [16, 56], the availability of which has to rise, should there be a CO2 fertilization effect. Results from CO2 enrichment experiments that provide fertile soil either by adding nutrients, disturbing the soil or by starting with widely spaced saplings, are inadequate models for the majority of forests [29, 30, 34]. In industrial or intensive agricultural regions, N is often overabundant through pollution (N deposition) causing P limitation to come into play (e.g. [7, 62]). In the short-term, there are two ways to somewhat escape such nutrient limitation of a CO2 response: (1) by priming (that is, decomposing) recalcitrant soil organic matter (SOM) that ties up nutrients. Low-molecular sugars exuded from roots have been shown to potentially facilitate such microbial priming [25, 49, 55], releasing both carbon and nutrients from SOM. However, such C-rich exudates can also induce nutrient competition by heterotrophic microbes [26, 51]. (2) Another way to escape nutrient limitation is widening the carbon to nutrient ratio in tissues (as for instance shown by Hungate et al. [24, 45], which slows metabolism and produces slower-cycling debris. Eventually this feeds back negatively on stand productivity. In the long run, the carbon cycle is inevitably tied to the nutrient cycle [34]; Fig. 2). Carbon can only cycle faster through a system (trees growing faster) when the nutrients do as well. An unlikely, and in the expectation of enhanced C storage, an unwanted, long-term effect.

The nutrient cycle drives the carbon cycle. For trees absorbing CO2 faster, in a high CO2 atmosphere, essential nutrients need to be available and provided faster, in order to retain plant element stoichiometry, an unlikely long-term option, and a challenge for modelling the forest C cycle. There is no way to predict the C cycle without understanding the nutrient cycle, that of non-N nutrients in particular

Accordingly, the experimental evidence for enhanced forest growth due to elevated CO2 concentrations is weak. The very few (and very expensive) experiments that operated at steady state, and in which the nutrient cycle was not enhanced, do not support the CO2-fertilization idea [30, 31, 34]. Experiments that start during the initial growth of saplings after disturbance (planting), produce a compound-interest-effect that will mask CO2-effects. The results of the hundreds of young, expanding, and fertile test systems, do not permit arriving at a realistic picture of forest CO2-effects (e.g. by meta-analysis). In addition, the actual rise in atmospheric CO2 concentration amounts to 2–3 ppm per year, while experiments cannot avoid exposing trees to a step change by 150–200 ppm, with a possible overresponse to be encountered. The crucial role of the nutrient cycle for forest tree responses to elevated CO2 was demonstrated many times (see e.g. Winter et al. [61], for tropical trees; Hungate et al. [24], for subtropical oaks; Sigurdsson et al. [58], for boreal forest trees). One can switch on or off CO2 fertilization effects, by nutrient addition/withholding. The inclusion of test systems with young, fertile, aggrading stands of saplings, produces a response to a step increase in CO2 concentration that is not seen in steady state forest systems [30, 34].

From the above, C-limitation of forest growth is unlikely, when trees operate under steady state canopy closure and rely entirely on a natural nutrient cycle. The so far best documented evidence comes from a 12-year free air CO2 enrichment experiment (FACE) with yellow poplar in Tennessee, that showed no change of either forest biomass or leaf area index or NPP [45, 46], but higher root turnover that facilitated recalcitrant soil organic matter accumulation, a response unlikely to be sustained because nutrients become locked up in SOM. Eight years of FACE of c. 30 m tall deciduous trees of four species (oak, hornbeam, beech, lime) and of 35 m tall spruce trees (five years), also had no effect on LAI, and the growth rates of stems, fine roots or litter production (Swiss forest FACE; refs. [2, 27, 34]. Similarly, trees in a natural eucalypt forest near Sydney showed no growth stimulation in response to elevated CO2 [15]. In all these cases, nutrient constraints, most likely P limitation, came into action. An old, monospecific oak forest near Birmingham, UK, showed a weak plot-mean of tree growth (p = 0.03) after five years, while NPP did not show a significant response [44]. The signal came, in large, from a sub-fraction of more responsive individuals and depended on the handling of pretreatment growth. In addition, all CO2 experiments are prone to the ‘island effect’, meaning that they miss atmospheric feedback, in case trees lose less water under elevated CO2 (an often-observed stomatal response), and thus, would rise the vapor pressure deficit at landscape scale, which cannot occur within a FACE ring [36]. The results of these five experiments do not provide sufficient evidence for concluding that the gradual increase in atmospheric CO2 stimulates tree growth on a large scale. Yet, if it did, this is no justification for concluding that a rise in C stock occurred in forests due to the steady rise in CO2 levels. For faster growth to rise a forest C stock, trees also need to live longer (Fig. 1), which cannot be explored in FACE experiments. Soil responses at ecosystem level are very hard to quantify, with the delicate interaction of the C and N cycle and the C duration in various SOM fractions holding surprises. One of the most detailed analyses, that in a scrub-oak system under elevated CO2, arrived at no statistically detectable effect of long-term exposure to elevated CO₂ on net ecosystem C uptake [24].

Until recently, the global C balance could not be closed without assuming that a ‘missing fraction’ of c. 30% of the anthropogenic carbon emissions becomes trapped in the terrestrial biosphere every year [21]. Instead of the unjustified assumption of a CO2 fertilization effect, a legacy of former land use could contribute to such a rise in forest biomass as well (secondary forests aggrading toward a new steady state; refs. [28, 53]. A recent exploration of the ‘missing terrestrial C’ by Bar-On et al. [3] arrived at the conclusion that most of the ‘missing C’ is contained in submerged organic debris (in hydroelectrical reservoirs, lakes, rivers, estuaries, coastal waters) and as plastic and other organics in landfills, data that strongly diminish the ‘need’ of a forest biomass C sink to close the global C balance. With the most recent re-calculations by Friedlingstein et al. [20] that arrived at half as much ‘missing’ terrestrial C per year, the application of Bar-On et al.’s [3] findings would suggests a net terrestrial biomass C loss. In any case, these results do not leave a leeway for a significant CO2 fertilization effect on global forest biomass either.

To stock, to use, or re-wild?

Attempts at improving the national carbon budget by an adjusted forest policy, have basically two options: (1) enhancing forest C stocks (more and/or denser forests), (2) utilizing forest products for substituting fossil resources (for a recent review see Anderegg et al. [1]). The limits of enhancing C stocks in forests are obvious: first, there are natural limits of enhancing biomass per unit land area, second, rising these stocks has a price by diminished use of forest products, and third, expanding forest area runs into land use conflicts. Any extra ton of C stored in a forest, is potentially a ton not used for substituting fossil resources [57]. Further, it is often overlooked, that one can stock C only once on a piece of land. From a pure mitigation perspective, utilization is the far more effective measure than storage, and, if done in a sustainable way, it can theoretically be continued forever. A caveat is the energy-side of the substitution options, because these will diminish as the decarbonization of the economy progresses [23], while the substitution in the building sector will remain intact for a long time, with the secondary benefit of extended storage.

Yet, even the most extreme attempts at substituting fossil resources by forest products are comparatively small. Here, an example based on publicly available data. Would the C contained in the entire recorded round wood production of Swiss forestry (30% of Switzerland carries forest), that is, 1.35 Mio t C, be used for a 1:1 replacement of part of the 15 Mio t fossil C consumed in a year (ignoring energy density, transfer losses, process costs, but also unrecorded fire wood use), that is, no other use of timber, this would correspond to a 9% C substitution of the fossil C market [33]. To bring this into perspective, a 9% equivalent of saved gasoline C would mean driving with 6.0 L instead of 6.6 L of gasoline per 100 km. A realistic substitution, still allowing for timber uses other than for energy, would at best be half as large. In Germany, with its larger heavy industry, such an extreme substitution would arrive at less than 5% (again, much less by allowing for other timber uses). As mentioned above, the energy sector will offer declining options for substitution as the decarbonification of transport and heating will proceed. The above example was chosen simply to illustrate the magnitudes of the biological mitigation potential. In the long-run, substitution is still far more effective than storage, but its actual mitigation potential is quite small, even when applying such extreme substitution scenarios. Hence, the substitution potential should not blind us of the overwhelming magnitudes of current fossil C release and the need to cut these. Unfortunately, forests can contribute only marginally to that task, and utilizing forest products is more effective than increasing stocks (not neglecting other ecological benefits of having more forests).

Finally, it is often assumed that leaving forests alone (re-wilding) so that trees can continue accumulating biomass, is a win-win situation, because of biodiversity benefits. Stopping forest management, means releasing a largely even aged tree cohort. This population of trees will initially add on C, because trees are growing beyond their traditional harvest size, while still resembling, demographically, rather artificial stands. The older trees become, the more likely disturbances come in action, inducing partial re-opening of the forests and a gradual transition to natural gap dynamics. Both, the opening and the rising dead-wood stock is highly welcome for biodiversity, however, at the same time, such gaps limit further rises of C stocks. In fact, depending on large browsing animals that can maintain open patches, the C stock may decline. In the long run, the initial rise in stock is unlikely to be sustained, and such re-wilding projects would best be justified by their biodiversity value and not by stressing C benefits that are likely to cease [42]. The abandonment of utilizing forest products for fossil C substitution, particularly the non-energy substitutions, actually adds a negative component to re-wilding forests, in terms of C budgeting [57].

Conclusions

The growth rate of trees, or the local or regional fluxes of C into forest parcels (the C sequestration process or ‘sink activity’) must not be confused with a lasting rise in C stocks. The central issue in carbon accounting is C residence time, requiring models of tree and branch turnover, that is, of tree and branch longevity and mortality. There is no evidence in support of rising forest C storage due to elevated CO2. Such a rise in C stock would require a rise in the availability of scarce nutrients. Any acceleration of growth would be reducing mean C residence time. Terrestrial sinks are finate per unit land area, controlled by biomass turnover and disturbances (fire and pests in particular). The sustainable production and utilization of forest products (rather than ‘mining’ old growth forests) is far more effective for mitigating net fossil C release to the atmosphere than attempts at rising C stocks in forests. Forest expansion incurs land use conflicts, and higher stocks per unit land area bear the risk of depletion by stochastic effects (fire, wind break, pests, active disturbance) with a devaluation of C certificates tied to such stocks. Mining old growth forests, leaves a huge carbon debth that cannot be balanced in relevant time frames by reforestation. In view of these constraints, the potential of both, fossil C substitution and additional C storage in forest biomass is not only limited, but also quantitatively minor, compared to the potential of reducing fossil C consumption [18, 37]. Setting aside managed forests for biodiversity benefits removes the substitution potential (representing a C cost, rather than a C benefit), but may still be wanted for its own sake.
