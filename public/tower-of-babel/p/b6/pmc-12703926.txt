Canine and feline mesenchymal stem cells: an integrative review of direct comparative studies by species and tissue source
Authors: Hyung-Sik Kim, Tae-Hoon Shin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ImportanceMesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) play a pivotal role in advancing veterinary regenerative medicine in dogs and cats and serve as valuable preclinical models for human therapies. Despite their widespread application, fundamental knowledge gaps and the absence of standardized criteria for canine and feline MSCs remain. This review focuses exclusively on direct comparative studies across species and tissue sources to support evidence-based practices in which clinical use often precedes full scientific validation.ObservationsThis review consolidates available comparative studies of canine and feline MSCs, categorizing them by species, tissue origin, and donor factors. We systematically assessed fundamental biological properties of MSCs (yield, morphology, proliferation, differentiation) and functionalities (immunomodulation, angiogenesis, migration). Overall, MSCs from both species exhibit properties comparable to those of human MSCs. However, canine MSCs have been more extensively studied across diverse tissues, whereas feline MSC research remains primarily restricted to adipose tissue and bone marrow. Notable interspecies and tissue-specific differences include proliferation rate, chondrogenic potential, and surface marker variability. Donor factors such as age also influence MSC behavior. Nevertheless, inconsistencies in sampling sites, culture protocols, and surface marker panels across studies continue to pose challenges for achieving a comprehensive understanding through direct comparison.Conclusions and RelevanceEstablishing consistent characteristics of MSCs in companion animals requires well-designed and controlled comparative studies. Standardization of MSC characterization, production, and application has been widely advocated. This review highlights ongoing efforts to address species- and tissue-specific knowledge gaps and emphasizes the need for additional comparative research to strengthen the preclinical and therapeutic utility of MSCs, thereby advancing evidence-based stem cell therapies in veterinary medicine.

INTRODUCTION

Regenerative medicine is a modern therapeutic approach aimed at replacing, repairing, or restoring damaged tissues or organs by harnessing endogenous regenerative mechanisms or applying exogenous biological materials. Stem cells are at the forefront of this paradigm, offering novel strategies for treating various refractory diseases [1]. Based on their fundamental properties, self-renewal and differentiation potential, stem cells are classified into pluripotent stem cells (PSCs), which include embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), multipotent stem cells, such as mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) and hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs), and tissue-specific stem cells with oligopotency or unipotency [2]. Alternatively, they can also be categorized as ESCs or adult stem cells (ASCs), depending on their tissue of origin. Regardless of classification, stem cells are being investigated extensively across biomedical sciences, including drug screening, cell and gene therapy, and organoid development [3].

Among them, MSCs have attracted particular attention as a promising cell source in diverse preclinical and clinical contexts [1]. Compared with ESCs, MSCs are ethically favorable alternatives, with unique stem cell properties and multifaceted functionalities that directly contribute to distinct therapeutic potential [4]. Specifically, MSCs exert their immunomodulatory effects through direct contact with various immune cells or through soluble factors such as nitric oxide (NO), prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), hepatocyte growth factor (HGF), indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-stimulated gene-6 (TSG-6), transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), Galectin-3, interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra), IL-6, and IL-10 [5]. They can also migrate to damaged or inflamed sites, a process known as homing, following systemic administration, stimulated by chemokine receptors such as CXCR4 and adhesion molecules including VCAM-1 and integrins [6]. In addition, MSCs support tissue regeneration through complex trophic effects mediated by the secretion of cytokines, chemokines, and growth factors that promote angiogenesis, anti-apoptosis, and tissue repair [7]. Furthermore, MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (EVs) contain proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and microRNAs that facilitate intercellular communication and modulate recipient cell behavior [8]. As a cell-free modality, EVs offer advantages such as reduced immunogenicity and the ability to cross biological barriers, although standardization remains a significant challenge [9].

In addition to applications in human medicine, there has been growing interest in MSCs for regenerative therapies in companion animals, particularly dogs and cats [10]. Veterinary stem cell therapy not only serves the primary purpose of treating intractable animal diseases but also provides significant value as a preclinical tool for assessing the safety and efficacy of human stem cell therapies [11]. Dogs are often regarded as more suitable model for humans than rodents, largely because of their greater similarities to humans in terms of body size, anatomy, and pathogenesis [12]. However, most applications of MSCs in veterinary medicine rely on real-world use rather than well-established scientific evidence. This trend reflects the limited fundamental knowledge and absence of standardized criteria for defining canine and feline MSCs. Furthermore, several critical questions remain unresolved for the successful clinical translation of MSC therapies, including the optimal tissue source for each condition, donor tissue-specific cell yield, large-scale production, and the optimal dose, route, timing, and frequency of administration [13].

Numerous investigations have been conducted on MSCs derived from dogs and cats to address these gaps. Although the therapeutic safety and efficacy of MSCs have been demonstrated in multiple preclinical and clinical settings, a systematic understanding of species- or tissue-specific differences remains limited. Most studies have examined MSCs from a single tissue source within a single species, likely due to advantages in reproducibility and incremental knowledge accumulation. However, it is virtually impossible to comprehensively review the extensive body of literature published to date. Importantly, comparative analyses based on defined variables, including species, breeds, tissue origins, harvest sites, and donor factors, are critical for optimizing the development and clinical translation of MSC-based therapeutics in veterinary medicine. This review integrates comparative studies performed under controlled conditions using two or more distinct types of MSCs. Based on the findings from direct comparison studies, we aim to provide deeper insights into the inherent biological properties of canine and feline MSCs that may help address two central questions in the field: 1) What is the most appropriate tissue source for MSC therapeutics in dogs and cats? and 2) How can disease-specific standardization be achieved for veterinary stem cell therapeutics?

METHODS

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using PubMed, Google Scholar, and Web of Science with keywords including “canine,” “feline,” “veterinary,” “stem cells,” “stromal cells,” and “comparative” to identify relevant articles describing the fundamental characteristics of canine MSCs (cMSCs) and feline MSCs (fMSCs) for regenerative medicine; last updated 2025-07-20. Studies were included if they provided peer-reviewed, full-text data on MSC characterization, and they were excluded if limited to case reports without scientific analyses. The final selection focused on studies presenting meaningful comparative data on MSC morphology, proliferation, differentiation, or function under defined conditions.

OBSERVATIONS

Overview of canine and feline MSCs

Vertebrate stem cells are broadly defined by their ability to divide, migrate, differentiate into multiple lineages, and organize into multifunctional cell populations. However, stem cell populations are inherently heterogeneous, with individual cells often behaving autonomously rather than uniformly. In particular, MSCs consist of a diverse population of stromal cells, and even among cells derived from the same tissue, they exhibit substantial variability in both phenotype and function [14]. To provide a standardized framework for defining MSCs, the International Society for Cell Therapy (ISCT) announced the minimum criteria in 2006 to reliably define human MSCs (hMSCs) in 2006 [15]: 1) adherence to plastic surfaces under standard culture conditions; 2) in vitro differentiation into adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic lineages; and 3) specific surface marker expression—positive for CD105, CD73, and CD90 (> 95%) and negative for CD45, CD34, CD14 or CD11b, CD79a or CD19, and HLA-DR (< 2%). Although these criteria do not fully capture interspecies or tissue-specific variability, they remain widely adopted for MSC identification and for ensuring data reproducibility across studies.

Based on these human-based standards, MSCs derived from dogs and cats are generally considered comparable in key characteristics and functions. Table 1 summarizes the major tissue sources from which cMSCs and fMSCs have been successfully isolated and characterized to date. Notably, non-expanded autologous cell products, such as bone marrow (BM) concentrates or the stromal vascular fraction of adipose tissue (AD), are frequently used in veterinary clinical practice but are excluded here because of their high heterogeneity and uncertain MSC content [16]. Instead, we focus solely on culture-expanded MSCs obtained under controlled laboratory protocols (Fig. 1). BM and AD are the most prominent MSC sources among companion animals, owing to their ease of access, high tissue availability, and relatively well-defined characteristics. Dogs represent the companion species with the widest range of reported MSC tissue origins, whereas cats have a much more limited diversity of MSC sources, with AD being predominant. Despite sustained efforts in both species, only a few comparative studies are available on cMSC and fMSC biology, and findings from hMSCs may not be directly extrapolated to veterinary contexts. Therefore, further systematic comparisons across species, breeds, tissues, and donor factors are required to clarify the biological and therapeutic implications. Species-based comparison of canine and feline MSCs

Veterinary MSC research originated from preclinical efforts to develop stem cell therapies for human disorders. Although rodents have served as the primary models for human diseases, dogs and cats are gaining attention because of their pathophysiological similarities to humans and their tendency to spontaneously develop human-like diseases. At the same time, the growing interest in regenerative veterinary therapies has driven the characterization and application of MSCs specifically for canine and feline use. Consequently, despite the predominantly human-centred focus of most research, an increasing number of studies have begun to explore species-specific characteristics of MSCs in these animals (Table 2).

Meza-León et al. [17] reported that canine bone marrow-derived MSCs (cBM-MSCs) expressed the distinct marker CD271, similar to hBM-MSCs, but unlike leptin receptor-positive (LepR+) murine BM-MSCs. They further found that all three species shared similarities in marrow histopathology, morphology, distribution and frequency of BM-MSCs. Moreover, age-related changes in canine BM more closely resembled those observed in humans than in mice, supporting the relevance of dogs as surrogate models for stem cell research. However, culture-expanded cBM-MSCs exhibited several distinctions from their human counterparts. Canine BM was prone to clot formation immediately after aspiration, which was associated with a lower MSC isolation yield. cBM-MSCs showed reduced proliferative capacity and an earlier onset of cellular senescence during in vitro expansion compared with hBM-MSCs [18]. These findings suggest that MSC properties may vary even when isolated from tissues with high histological similarity, underscoring the need for species-specific optimization of MSC isolation and culture protocols. Similarly, canine Wharton’s jelly-derived MSCs (cWJ-MSCs) demonstrated relatively poor proliferative capacity and a tendency for accelerated cellular aging, although no significant species-dependent differences were observed in immunogenicity or immunosuppressive abilities [19]. Additionally, cAD-MSCs exhibited angiogenic and neurotrophic effects comparable to those of hAD-MSCs, supporting their potential application in conditions such as spinal cord injury [20]. Collectively, these observations highlight the therapeutic promise of cMSCs in both translational and veterinary contexts.

Compared with cMSCs, fMSCs show greater divergence from hMSCs. In vitro, feline bone marrow-derived MSCs (fBM-MSCs) exhibited morphological changes at passage 4, including loss of the typical spindle shape and irregularities in size and morphology [21], which are not commonly observed in human or canine MSCs. fAD-MSCs were also reported to be inherently smaller than hAD-MSCs [22]. Despite these phenotypic differences, fMSCs retain fundamental characteristics such as differentiation potential, immunomodulatory effects, and gene expression patterns similar to those of hMSC. A direct comparison study between cAD-MSCs and fAD-MSCs revealed that cMSCs outperformed fMSCs in proliferative capacity, trilineage differentiation, and the proportion of cells with a canonical MSC immunophenotype (CD44+, CD90+ and CD34−) [23]. These findings underscore species-specific discrepancies in MSC biology and highlight the importance of direct comparative studies to guide the development of MSC-based therapies tailored to each species.

Morphologically, MSCs are described as adherent, fibroblast-like cells with shapes ranging from cuboidal to elongated. In the case of cMSCs, as well as fMSCs, the morphology generally conforms to this definition, with only subtle differences noted. For instance, compared with cBM-MCSs, which display the classic spindle-shaped morphology, cAD-MSCs tend to be longer, thinner, and more prominently spindle-shaped, while canine MSCs isolated from the amniotic membrane (AM) are typically smaller and more oval in appearance [24]. In cats, placenta-derived MSCs appear more spherical than fAD-MSCs [25]. Such evidence of tissue-specific morphological distinctions highlights the need for donor-matched comparisons and underscores the importance of explicitly reporting tissue origin in MSC research and therapeutic applications. With respect to initial cell yield and proliferative capacity—considerations for clinical use—early comparative studies reported no significant differences between cBM-MSCs and cAD-MSCs [1326]. However, more recent data consistently demonstrated that cAD-MSCs exhibited superior proliferative capacity compared with cBM-MSCs [242728293031], in some cases achieving more than double the proliferation rate of cBM-MSCs up to passage 5 [27]. These findings suggest a practical advantage of adipose tissue as a source of MSCs for clinical applications. Similar trends have been reported in cats, with fAD-MSCs proliferating faster than fBM-MSCs [32], although only a limited number of comparative studies using fMSCs are available. In humans, perinatal tissue-derived MSCs often display superior proliferative capacity and yield compared with adult tissue-derived MSCs, reflecting their more primitive characteristics [33]. However, this pattern may not be directly applicable to dogs and cats.

While canine MSCs derived from perinatal tissues (UC, AM, P) formed a distinct transcriptomic cluster compared with those from adult tissues (AD, BM), cAD-MSCs demonstrated the highest proliferation capacity among all groups [28]. This finding was further supported by evidence showing that cAD-MSCs outperformed cAM-MSCs in yield and proliferation [24]. Among perinatal tissue sources, cP-MSCs exhibited the highest proliferation rate compared with cAM- and cUC-MSCs [34]. However, conflicting evidence has also been reported, with some studies suggesting comparable proliferation rates between cUC- and cAD-MSCs [35]. Canine dental pulp-derived MSCs (cDP-MSCs) displayed a proliferation capacity similar to that of cUC-MSCs [36]. Comparisons between cAD-MSCs and canine synovium-derived MSCs (cSy-MSCs) have yielded inconsistent results: donor-matched study reported similar proliferation capabilities between the two MSC types [29], whereas another found superior proliferation in Sy- and infrapatellar fat pad (IPFP)-derived cMSCs compared with cAD-MSCs [37]. Additionally, cLi-MSCs and cOv-MSCs showed proliferation and senescence profiles comparable to those of cBM-MSCs [38] and cAD-MSCs [39], respectively. Canine endometrial MSCs (cEn-MSCs) exhibited lower proliferation capacity than cAD-MSCs [40]. Collectively, despite the various alternative MSC sources reported in dogs and cats (listed in Table 1), AD-MSCs generally demonstrate superior proliferation and yield compared with BM and other tissue-derived MSCs, supporting their practical benefits for clinical applications. However, variability in study design and methodology should be carefully considered when interpreting these findings and planning subsequent studies.

Tri-lineage differentiation into adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic lineages is a hallmark of MSCs, typically validated by morphological changes and lineage-specific staining. Both cMSCs and fMSCs generally fulfill these criteria, without any species-exclusive features identified thus far. However, unlike human MSCs, which consistently differentiate regardless of tissue source, cMSCs exhibit greater variability. Early studies reported that cAD-MSCs differentiated into osteogenic lineages but failed to undergo adipogenic or chondrogenic differentiation; adipogenesis, however, was restored with the addition of rabbit serum [41], suggesting the need for species-specific protocols. Chondrogenic differentiation appears to be the most variable among the three lineages. For example, cMSCs derived from joint tissues, such as synovium and IPFP, outperformed cBM- and cAD-MSCs in chondrogenesis [37], whereas cLi-MSCs lacked chondrogenic differentiation despite adequate adipogenic and osteogenic potential [38]. Similarly, cDP-MSCs demonstrated enhanced osteogenic potential compared with cBM-MSCs, based on proteomic profiling [36]. Beyond mesodermal lineages, MSCs may also transdifferentiate into ectodermal and endodermal cell types. In dogs, both cAD- and cOv-MSCs have shown potential for neurogenic and endodermal differentiation, including into primordial germ cell precursors [39]. In contrast, feline studies have primarily confirmed mesodermal differentiation of fBM- and fAD-MSCs, with limited cross-tissue comparisons. These findings indicate that differentiation potential should guide source selection for specific therapeutic objectives, rather than serving solely as a criterion for defining MSCs.

According to ISCT criteria [15], the immunophenotype of hMSCs is defined by the presence of the positive markers CD105, CD73, and CD90 and the absence of the negative markers CD34 and CD45. Despite efforts to delineate a more homogeneous MSC population, these markers are not universally applicable across species or tissue types. In dogs and cats, MSC immunophenotyping data vary substantially across studies, with no standardized analysis panel. For instance, CD73 and CD105, typically regarded as positive markers of hMSCs, are often omitted from or expressed at low levels (less than 50%) in companion animal studies. The limited inclusion of CD73 in FACS panels may be due to the scarcity of antibodies that effectively cross-react with canine and feline proteins. CD105 expression, in particular, has shown marked inconsistency. One study reported significantly lower CD105 expression in cBM-MSCs (mean 17.12%) compared with cAD-MSCs (59.84%) and cSy-MSCs (46.16%) [29], whereas another found high expression levels exceeding 90% across all MSC types, including cBM-, cAD-, and cAM-MSCs (99.8%, 99.7%, and 90.5%, respectively) [24]. Other markers, such as CD29, CD44, and CD90, also demonstrated tissue-dependent variability. For example, cBM- and cAD-MSCs consistently expressed higher levels of CD29, CD44, CD90, and CD105 than cAM-MSCs [24]. cMSCs derived from joint tissues, such as Sy and IPFP, exhibited significantly higher CD90 expression than those from AD and BM, while CD44 expression remained similarly high (> 96%) [37]. In cats, AD-MSCs expressed higher levels of CD90 and CD105 than P-MSCs [25]. In the same study, CD44 was proposed as a positive marker for fMSCs, but expression was minimal (< 5%) in both MSC types. Webb et al. [32] reported a consistently higher proportion of CD90+ fAD-MSCs compared with fBM-MSCs, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. Overall, CD29, CD44, and CD90 are frequently used for cMSC immunophenotyping, while CD44, CD90, and CD105 are more commonly applied in fMSCs—primarily to confirm MSC identity rather than to distinguish tissue-specific expression profiles.

Comparative studies of MSCs from different tissues have provided valuable insights into their functional diversity, which is beneficial for regenerative medicine. In dogs, both AD-MSCs and BM-MSCs demonstrated similar immunosuppressive effects, as evidenced by mitogen-stimulated lymphocyte reactions [26]. cSy-MSCs showed comparable efficacy to cAD- and cBM-MSCs in suppressing lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced macrophage activation [29]. Notably, fetal tissue-derived cMSCs, such as AM-, UC-, and P-MSCs, expressed higher levels of key immunomodulatory genes, including IDO, than BM-MSCs under both resting and activated conditions [34]. Although direct comparative studies on MSC immunomodulation in cats are scarce, several cross-species investigations have suggested that fAD-MSCs consistently express major immunomodulatory genes, including PGE2, IDO, and IL-6, at levels similar to hMSCs [22]. With regard to the precise mechanisms of MSC-driven immunomodulation, most evidence has accumulated from independent studies of individual MSC types or specific diseases rather than from single comparative analyses across multiple MSC types (extensively reviewed in [42] and [43]). Beyond immunomodulation, MSCs also exert beneficial trophic effects, including angiogenesis and migration. For example, cBM-MSCs showed the highest expression of vascular endothelial growth factor, a proangiogenic factor, compared with MSCs from AD, UCB, and WJ, even in the resting state [31], whereas cAD-MSCs and cUC-MSCs exhibited comparable migratory capacity toward injured tissue [24]. Collectively, current evidence supports that cMSCs and fMSCs share pleiotropic immunomodulatory and trophic functions with hMSCs, but additional direct comparative studies are required to clarify species- and tissue-specific differences that may influence therapeutic outcomes.

Harvest site–based comparison of canine and feline MSCs

Even within the same tissue type, the biological characteristics of MSCs may vary depending on intrinsic factors such as the anatomical location of tissue collection and the histological substructure targeted for MSC isolation. Investigations examining these relationships have primarily focused on adipose tissue, the most widely used MSC donor source in both dogs and cats. Rashid et al. [44] evaluated cAD-MSCs isolated from four anatomical locations—subcutaneous, perianal, omental (OM), and IPFP— and found that IPFP-MSCs had a smaller cellular size and the fastest proliferation rate. OM-MSCs, in particular, exhibited pronounced adipogenic and osteogenic differentiation potential. In cats, adipose tissues surrounding the reproductive organs provided advantages in tissue weight and initial viability compared with subcutaneous depots, although no significant differences in doubling time or cell viability of MSCs were observed at early passages up to three [45]. For placental MSCs, human studies demonstrated superior proliferation in MSCs derived from fetal components (e.g., UC, AM, and chorionic plate) compared with those from maternal portions, such as decidua parietalis [4647]. In dogs, MSCs derived from the placental matrix exhibited a proliferative advantage over MSCs isolated from other fetal tissues [34]. However, no data are currently available that directly compare placental substructures in dogs or cats. BM represents another source where harvest site should be considered. cBM-MSCs isolated from the humerus demonstrated lower yield and osteogenic potential than those from the tibia, femur, and ilium [21], although comprehensive anatomical comparisons remain limited in both species. Similarly, direct comparative studies of MSCs derived from different intra-articular locations are lacking, despite successful isolation of MSCs from several joint tissues, including the knee and hip.

Impact of donor factors on canine and feline MSCs

The biological properties and functional capacities of MSCs may also vary depending on donor-related factors, including breed, age, sex, and health status. Breed-specific variations represent an important consideration that can affect MSC yield and behavior in both dogs and cats. A comparative study of cBM-MSCs from Border Collie, German Shepherd, Labrador Retriever, Golden Retriever, Malinois, and Hovawart demonstrated that Border Collie MSCs exhibited the fastest proliferation (up to cumulative population doublings, CPD 26) and the lowest proportion of senescent cells (approximately 40% at passage 6). In contrast, German Shepherd and Hovawart MSCs proliferated more slowly (up to CPD 16–17) and contained a higher proportion of senescent cells (approximately 70% at the same passage). The Hovawart breed also demonstrated a significantly lower initial colony-forming capacity from marrow aspirates [48]. Another comparative study of BM-MSCs from Alaskan Malamute, Labrador, American Staffordshire Terrier, Poodle, Eurohound, and Beagle reported similar expression levels of genes associated with differentiation, angiogenesis, neurotrophism, and immunomodulation across all breeds [49]. However, no studies to date have described breed-related discrepancies in cats. Donor age also plays a critical role in determining MSC characteristics. A comparative study of cBM-MSCs revealed that MSCs derived from skeletally mature donors exhibited significantly diminished osteogenic capacity compared to those from immature animals, including a 40% reduction in proliferative ability [50]. Similarly, cAD-MSCs from young donors exhibited 2.4-fold faster proliferation, as well as superior differentiation potential and higher CD73 and CD80 gene expression, compared with those from aged dogs [51]. In cats, comparable findings were reported in a study of fAD-MSCs, in which MSCs from young animals reached passage 2 approximately four days earlier than those from older animals, although no significant differences in immunosuppressive capacity were observed [52]. Investigations of gender- and environment-related influences remain scarce in veterinary MSC research. One comparative study between canine and feline AD-MSCs found no significant sex-related differences within species [23]. Donor health status also significantly affects the biological properties of MSCs. In dogs, MSCs derived from osteoarthritic joints showed reduced chondrogenic but enhanced osteogenic potential compared with those from healthy joints [53]. Likewise, urine-derived MSCs from dogs with chronic kidney disease exhibited faster proliferation without notable changes in phenotype or differentiation ability [54]. In cats, AD-MSCs derived from patients with chronic gingivostomatitis displayed altered exosomal protein profiles, particularly in immune-related pathways, compared with those from healthy animals [55]. Collectively, younger and healthy animals are generally considered optimal MSC donors because of their higher proliferative capacity and functional consistency. Breed-, sex-, and disease-dependent differences remain relatively underexplored but may alter MSC behavior and should be comprehensively evaluated in future studies to enhance standardization and clinical outcomes.

DISCUSSION

While MSCs derived from various human tissues have been extensively characterized through a wide array of experimental comparative studies and comprehensive reviews, the corresponding body of evidence in veterinary species—particularly dogs and cats—remains relatively limited, creating a significant gap in the current understanding of MSC biology and therapeutic standardization. Nevertheless, the direct comparative studies conducted to date, together with independent studies using single MSC types, provide invaluable insights into the unique biological properties of canine and feline MSCs. Although still somewhat fragmented, these findings form a meaningful foundation for identifying species- and tissue-specific differences that are crucial for the rational development of MSC-based veterinary therapeutics across diverse disease contexts.

As summarized in Table 1, MSCs for veterinary therapeutics have been isolated from a wide variety of tissue sources, although the available tissue sources and data remain relatively limited in cats compared with dogs. Table 2 highlights direct comparative studies evaluating species-specific MSCs, either between humans and companion animals or between dogs and cats, using equivalent tissue origins. Unlike human MSCs, for which characterization criteria have been well established [56], no universally accepted criteria are currently available for canine and feline MSCs. Nevertheless, existing studies demonstrate that cMSCs and fMSCs generally fulfill the minimal criteria used to define hMSCs. Specifically, it is widely accepted that they also possess robust proliferative capacity and differentiation potential into mesodermal lineages, similar to hMSCs. Notable differences in surface marker expression, however, suggest species-dependent phenotypic profiles. Among the various surface proteins reported as positive markers of MSCs, CD29 and CD44 appear to be expressed more consistently in dogs than CD73 and CD105, whereas CD90 and CD105 are more commonly reported as typical markers for fMSCs (Table 3). Although these markers may not yet be conclusive for defining canine- and feline-specific MSCs, they cannot be completely ruled out as crucial factors contributing to unique characteristics and functional differences [23]. Therefore, establishing a universally accepted CD marker panel for cMSCs and fMSCs should be prioritized, beginning with methodological standardization based on flow cytometry-based protein expression rather than simple mRNA expression. Another key aspect of species variation is the mechanism of MSC-mediated immunosuppression. In humans and monkeys, MSCs primarily utilize IDO as a central pathway, whereas murine MSCs rely on inducible nitric oxide synthase [57]. A few comparative studies have suggested that cMSCs and fMSCs exert immunosuppressive effects through IDO-dependent mechanisms similar to hMSCs. However, inconsistencies in assay methods—such as LPS-induced macrophage suppression [40] and mitogen-activated PBMC proliferation inhibition [19]—have hampered precise interpretation of immunomodulatory functions under defined microenvironments. Consequently, the exact mechanisms of veterinary MSC therapy in specific disease contexts remain only partially understood. Taken together, these findings underscore the need for a standardized strategy that builds upon the ISCT criteria while incorporating species-specific adaptations. Establishing robust phenotypic marker panels specific for cMSCs and fMSCs, along with functionally validated immunomodulatory assays such as mixed lymphocyte reaction, IDO activity measurement, and PGE2 quantification, should be prioritized to accelerate the development of species- and disease-specific MSC therapies in veterinary medicine.

While it remains unclear which tissue is the optimal source of therapeutic MSCs in dogs and cats, comparative studies summarized in Table 3 suggest that AD is currently the most promising candidate. Similar to observations in hMSC research, AD-MSCs consistently demonstrate significantly higher yield and proliferative capability in both species compared with BM-MSCs [242728293132], which were traditionally considered the gold standard. This property is associated with the advantage of mass production for clinical applications. Intriguingly, in contrast to findings in hMSCs—where perinatal tissues such as placenta, umbilical cord, and amnion exhibit superior functionalities compared with adult tissues like AD—several canine donor-matched comparative studies have reported superior proliferation and delayed cellular senescence in AD-MSCs relative MSCs derived from birth-associated tissues [242831]. Furthermore, AD-MSCs from both dogs and cats displayed favorable immunomodulatory profiles, including high expression of immunoregulatory factors (e.g., IL-10, PGE2) and robust suppression of T-cell proliferation. Considering not only MSC biology but also practical merits such as tissue accessibility and obtainable quantity, AD-MSCs may currently represent the most feasible and effective option for treating immune-related diseases in companion animals. For regenerative applications where direct differentiation of MSCs is essential, however, alternative tissue sources may provide distinct benefits. Sy-MSCs demonstrated significantly greater chondrogenic potential than MSCs derived from BM and AD under equivalent donor conditions [29], whereas BM-MSCs and DP-MSCs showed enhanced osteogenic differentiation [58]. Although it remains uncertain whether in vitro differentiation ability directly translates to in vivo efficacy [59], such data provide valuable guidance for establishing disease-specific MSC standardization strategies. Specifically, Sy-MSCs with superior chondrogenic potential and extracellular matrix production may represent a more appropriate source for treating osteoarthritis, BM-MSCs with strong osteogenic properties may be optimal for bone defects, and MSCs intended for inflammatory bowel disease may require enhanced immunomodulatory capacity, such as inhibition of proinflammatory cytokines and induction of regulatory T cells. These functional attributes are directly related to therapeutic predictability and clinical outcomes. Furthermore, this approach must be accompanied by parallel investigations into the species-specific pathophysiology of canine and feline diseases, particularly in comparison with humans and rodents.

Currently, veterinary MSC therapies are often employed in an off-label manner, largely guided by clinicians’ empirical experience [60]. However, parallel efforts towards industrial commercialization and regulatory-based clinical applications are needed to ensure consistency, safety, and efficacy. Such progress requires a fully integrated standardization framework that incorporates species- and tissue-specific MSC characteristics, validated assay methods, and consistent quality control standards. To this end, establishing robust manufacturing protocols is a top priority to guarantee batch-to-batch consistency and product homogeneity. Although the comparative studies reviewed here have provided valuable data on the biological characteristics of MSCs according to species, tissue origin, and donor factors in dogs and cats, they remain limited in addressing differences arising from varied culture conditions within a single experimental setting. Nevertheless, several studies have provided evidence of changes in MSC behavior depending on cellular senescence (e.g., passage) and specific conditions (e.g., media composition). Given that culture-expanded MSCs are highly sensitive to microenvironmental factors and manipulation methods [61], it is critical to standardize the entire handling process of MSCs, including isolation methods, culture media composition, and cryopreservation procedures, as illustrated in Fig. 1. Future investigations focusing on systematic evaluations of individual MSC populations under diverse culture conditions will be required to establish a more robust foundation for a comprehensive standardization framework.

In conclusion, we emphasize the need for expanded comparative studies to improve understanding of canine- and feline-specific MSC biology. Such studies will facilitate the selection of appropriate tissue sources for defined clinical contexts, thereby enhancing the safety and efficacy of veterinary stem cell therapy, as well as strengthening the preclinical value of companion animals in human regenerative medicine.
