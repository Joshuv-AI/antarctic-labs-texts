Phase 3 Randomized Clinical Trial Evaluating the Safety of Reproxalap in Patients With Dry Eye Disease
Authors: Nathan M Radcliffe, John Sheppard, Blake Simmons, Desiree Owen, Ashley Nguyen, Bill Cavanagh, Todd C Brady

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Dry eye disease (DED) is a common ocular disorder characterized by tear film instability, inflammation, and symptoms including ocular discomfort and visual disturbance [1]. The prevalence of DED increases with age, is higher in female than male individuals, and is rising across all demographic groups [2]. It is conservatively estimated that 10–20% of individuals over the age of 40 years have DED [3]. Quality of life is affected as the disease is associated with declines in visual functioning, activities of daily living, mental health, social and physical functioning, and workplace productivity [4].

DED is typically a chronic condition requiring months or years of treatment and is characterized by episodic flares in disease activity, such as those triggered by wind and low humidity or by prolonged screen time and contact lens use [5]. The therapies currently approved for chronic use in DED may take weeks or months to begin to relieve DED symptoms [6–9]. Therefore, there is a need for new treatments for DED that work quickly and can be safely administered chronically.

Reactive aldehyde species (RASP) are proinflammatory molecules that have been implicated in ocular inflammatory diseases, including DED [10], and are biomarkers of cellular and oxidative stress [11]. RASP covalently bind to thiol and amino groups on various receptors and kinases and activate key mediators in the inflammation pathway, including nuclear factor kappa B (NF-kB) and inflammasomes, upstream of cytokine release [12, 13]. Levels of malondialdehyde (MDA), a well-characterized RASP, are elevated in the tears of patients with DED [14], and the increase in MDA levels in the tears of patients with DED is associated with signs and symptoms of the disease [14].

Reproxalap is a small molecule RASP inhibitor that rapidly and covalently binds RASP [10]. By neutralizing RASP, reproxalap is believed to block NF-kB translocation and the initiation of the cascade that leads to production and release of proinflammatory cytokines [15]. Several reported studies have demonstrated effectiveness of topical reproxalap treatment in alleviating the signs and symptoms of DED [10, 16, 17], including within minutes of administration in a dry eye chamber [17].

The objective of this trial was to evaluate the long-term safety of reproxalap 0.25% ophthalmic solution compared with vehicle in patients with DED. In addition, the effect of treatment on visual acuity and the association between baseline patient characteristics and instillation site irritation were characterized post hoc.

Methods

This phase 3, multicenter, double-masked, randomized, vehicle-controlled, parallel-group clinical trial in patients with DED was conducted at 14 sites in the USA. The trial was conducted from 26 January 2021 to 11 October 2022, in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, International Council for Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice, and applicable regulations. An institutional review board (Alpha IRB, San Clemente, CA, USA) or independent ethics committee approved the protocol, informed consent forms, and recruitment materials at each site before patient enrollment, and all patients provided written informed consent at screening before undergoing any trial-related procedure. The trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov with the identifier NCT04735393.

Patients ≥ 18 years of age with a history of dry eye for ≥ 6 months and a history of use or desire to use eyedrops for dry eye symptoms within the previous 6 months were enrolled. Patients were required to have a score of ≥ 2 on the Ora Calibra® Ocular Discomfort and 4-Symptom Questionnaire (OD4SQ) in at least one symptom, and the Schirmer test score was required to be in the range of 1–10 mm. Key exclusion criteria included clinically significant slit-lamp findings (e.g., active blepharitis, meibomian gland dysfunction, ocular allergies) that require treatment or could interfere with trial parameters; an ongoing ocular infection or active ocular inflammation; use of contact lenses within the previous 7 days (or anticipated use during the trial) or any eyedrops within the previous 2 h; laser-assisted in situ keratomileusis (LASIK) surgery within the previous 12 months; topical cyclosporine or lifitegrast use within the previous 90 days; punctal plug use during the trial unless plug use had been stable during the previous 30 days; and best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) ≥ 0.7 logMAR (20/100 or worse Snellen equivalent) in both eyes. Patients who were unwilling to discontinue all topical ophthalmic prescriptions and over-the-counter solutions, gels, and scrubs for the duration of the trial were also excluded.

At the screening/baseline visit (day 1), eligible patients were enrolled into 6-week and 12-month treatment cohorts (approximately 40% and 60% of patients, respectively) and randomly assigned in a 2:1 ratio to bilateral treatment with reproxalap 0.25% ophthalmic solution or vehicle, with the randomization stratified by treatment cohort (Fig. 1). Neither solution contained preservative. Patients, investigators, and site personnel involved in evaluations were masked to the treatment assignments. Treatment was self-administered bilaterally four times daily (QID) for the first 4 weeks then twice daily (BID) for the remainder of the clinical trial. Follow-up visits were scheduled for both cohorts at weeks 4 and 6, and the 12-month treatment cohort had additional visits scheduled at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months.

Clinical trial design. BID twice daily, QID four times daily

Safety assessments at each visit included adverse events (AEs), BCVA at 4 m, slit-lamp biomicroscopy, dilated fundoscopy, and intraocular pressure (IOP). Central corneal endothelial cell density (CECD) was assessed at baseline and months 6 and 12, and laboratory assessments (clinical chemistry and hematology) were performed at month 12. The severity of AEs was graded by the investigator as mild (transient, not requiring treatment, and not generally interfering with usual activities of daily living), moderate (not transient, interferes with usual activities of daily living, causing discomfort but no significant harm to the patient, alleviated with therapeutic intervention), or severe (interrupts usual activities of daily living, or significantly affects clinical status, or may require intensive therapeutic intervention).

The primary endpoints were the proportion of patients with serious treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) categorized as probably or definitely related to treatment for each of the following: visual acuity decrease (defined as an increase of ≥ 0.22 in logMAR score), IOP increase (defined as IOP > 25 mmHg with an IOP increase from baseline of ≥ 10 mmHg), corneal events detected on slit-lamp biomicroscopy, and retinal events detected on fundoscopy.

Analysis

Baseline patient characteristics and safety parameters were evaluated in the 6-week and 12-month safety populations. The 6-week safety population included all patients randomized to either treatment who received at least one dose and had at least one subsequent safety assessment; patients were analyzed as treated over 6 weeks. The 12-month safety population included all patients in the 12-month treatment cohort who received at least one dose and had at least one subsequent safety assessment; patients were analyzed as treated over 12 months.

Post hoc analysis of the overall change in BCVA from baseline across both eyes and all visits in the 12-month safety population was performed using a mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) model with baseline BCVA as a covariate and visit, treatment, and treatment-by-visit interaction as factors. Overall changes from baseline in IOP and CECD were also evaluated post hoc using similar MMRM models with the baseline value as a covariate. Post hoc analysis of baseline factors associated with instillation site irritation used a multivariable logistic regression model of irritation (yes or no) with age, Schirmer test score, and OD4SQ total score as covariates and sex and race as factors.

Using a two-sided alpha of 0.008375 (assuming a two-sided alpha of 0.0335 for all primary safety endpoints) and assuming a difference of approximately 20% in the proportion of reproxalap-treated and vehicle-treated patients with a specific AE, a sample size of approximately 175 patients completing 12 months of treatment (116 reproxalap and 58 vehicle) was planned to provide at least 80% power to demonstrate a significant difference between reproxalap and vehicle in the proportion of patients with a specific primary endpoint safety event.

Results

A total of 757 patients with DED were enrolled and randomized to treatment with reproxalap or vehicle. The clinical trial was completed by 54.2% of reproxalap-treated patients and 63.6% of vehicle-treated patients. Most patient discontinuations resulted from clinical trial site closure, withdrawal by the patient, or loss to follow-up (Fig. 2). The percentage of patients who discontinued because of AEs was 11.1% with reproxalap and 2.8% with vehicle. The AE most commonly resulting in patient discontinuation was instillation site irritation (8.0% and 9.0% of patients in the reproxalap 6-week and 12-month safety populations, respectively).

Patient disposition

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of patients in the 6-week and 12-month safety populations were well balanced across the treatment groups (Table 1). Most patients were female, and the majority were White. The mean age was 56.0 years in the 6-week safety population and 55.6 years in the 12-month safety population.

Other Safety Findings

No safety concerns were identified in other safety assessments. No reproxalap-treated patient had a shift in fundoscopy findings from normal or abnormal (not clinically significant) to abnormal (clinically significant) that was associated with an AE considered at least possibly related to treatment. Similarly, no clinically significant changes in laboratory assessments were reported as AEs considered at least possibly related to treatment.

Clinically significant changes from baseline on slit-lamp biomicroscopy were reported as AEs at least possibly related to treatment in two patients in the reproxalap 6-week safety population. One patient had abnormal conjunctival and lid findings on day 3 and AE reports of contact dermatitis on the lids, chemosis, and conjunctival injection deemed possibly related to treatment, which resulted in patient withdrawal from the trial. The second patient had abnormal conjunctival findings at the week 6 visit, and an AE of conjunctivitis deemed mild and possibly related to treatment was recorded and resolved the same day; the patient completed the trial.

There were no IOP-related AEs considered at least possibly related to treatment, and post hoc MMRM analysis using the 12-month safety population showed no significant difference between reproxalap and vehicle in the overall IOP change from baseline (Table 4). Further, no AEs related to CECD assessments were reported, and post hoc MMRM analysis using the 12-month safety population showed no significant difference between reproxalap and vehicle in the overall CECD change from baseline (Table 4).

Discussion

Reproxalap was generally well tolerated and had a favorable safety profile in this clinical trial evaluating the safety of reproxalap 0.25% ophthalmic solution in patients with DED who were treated for up to 1 year. No safety concerns were identified on fundoscopy or slit-lamp biomicroscopy, or in BCVA, IOP, CECD, and laboratory assessments. There were no treatment-related severe TEAEs, and the only TEAE that occurred with higher frequency in the reproxalap arms compared with vehicle was instillation site irritation, which most commonly was mild and transient, lasting < 1 min.

Instillation site irritation is a side effect that occurs with many topical ophthalmic medications and can be related to the drug or properties of the formulation (such as pH, osmolarity, and preservative) [1, 18]. Reproxalap (this trial), lifitegrast [19, 20], and acoltremon [21] have been associated with drug-related instillation site adverse effects. An exploratory, randomized, masked, crossover trial in 19 patients with DED evaluated instillation site comfort with reproxalap versus lifitegrast [22]. Patient-reported eyedrop experiences were assessed for up to 1 h after administration of a single bilateral dose of two formulations of reproxalap 0.25% and lifitegrast 5% [22]. Over all assessments, patient ratings of ocular discomfort, as well as blurry vision and dysgeusia, were significantly lower for both reproxalap formulations compared with lifitegrast, suggesting better tolerability of reproxalap [22]. Four ocular adverse events were reported, including two reports of moderate burning upon instillation (both in the same patient) with each reproxalap formulation, one report of mild ocular soreness lasting 30 min with lifitegrast, and one report of mild ocular itching with lifitegrast.

To our knowledge, we are the first to report an analysis of the association between baseline patient characteristics and irritation for a topically administered drug assessed in a long-term safety trial. The incidence of instillation site irritation was similar (approximately 40%) in the 6-week and 12-month safety populations. The likelihood of instillation site irritation was lower for Asian patients, younger patients, and patients with more severe symptoms (a higher OD4SQ total score) at baseline. Patients with a baseline Schirmer test score of ≥ 10 mm were more likely to report instillation site irritation (Table 3), but Schirmer test score was not a significant predictor of irritation in the multivariable logistic regression model (Table 4). The differences in irritation by age and race could potentially reflect differences in reporting behaviors. Further, the lower rate of irritation reporting from patients with higher baseline ocular discomfort could reflect masking of irritation by background symptomatology. Nonetheless, the majority of instillation site irritation events lasted < 1 or 1 min, and most of the affected patients tolerated the irritation and completed the trial.

Previous shorter-term trials have evaluated the efficacy and safety of topical ophthalmic reproxalap treatment in alleviating the signs and symptoms of DED [10, 16, 17]. A phase 2a trial [10] tested three formulations of reproxalap (0.1% and 0.5% ophthalmic solution and 0.5% lipid ophthalmic solution) dosed bilaterally QID for 28 days in patients with DED. Ocular DED symptoms had improved from baseline at both follow-up visits, which were scheduled after 7 and 28 days of dosing [10]. In a phase 2b vehicle-controlled trial [16], reproxalap 0.1% and 0.25% ophthalmic solution were compared with vehicle in patients with DED. Treatments were dosed bilaterally QID for 12 weeks, and signs and symptoms of DED were assessed at baseline and weeks 2, 4, 8, and 12. By week 2, among patients with median or higher symptom scores at baseline, reproxalap 0.25% had a significantly greater effect than vehicle in improving ocular dryness and discomfort scores from baseline [16]. Over all visits, reproxalap 0.25% was statistically superior to vehicle in change from baseline in ocular dryness scores [16]. Finally, a recently reported pivotal, vehicle-controlled, crossover trial tested the efficacy of short-term reproxalap treatment in relieving the symptoms of DED during acute exposure in a dry eye chamber [17]. Patients received four doses of reproxalap or vehicle at a prechamber visit, and on the next day (the chamber visit) received another two doses, the first dose before entering the dry eye chamber and a second dose while in the chamber. Reproxalap treatment was significantly more effective than vehicle in alleviating ocular redness and symptoms of DED over 90 min during dry eye chamber exposure [17]. In the phase 2a and phase 2b trials, the most common TEAEs were transient and related to instillation [10, 16], and mild, transient instillation site irritation was the most commonly reported TEAE in the pivotal trial [17]. The present trial demonstrated a similar tolerability profile of reproxalap during prolonged exposure. In all clinical trials of reproxalap, if the irritation associated with reproxalap exposure were clinically important, we would expect patients to exhibit increased redness and reported increased discomfort. Instead, the clinical trials have indicated decreased symptomatology, in some cases occurring within minutes of reproxalap administration, suggesting that the instillation site irritation is transient and self-limiting.

The observed difference between reproxalap and vehicle in the overall change in BCVA from baseline in the present clinical trial was unexpected. To our knowledge, this was the first demonstration of improved visual acuity in patients treated with a topically administered DED therapy for up to 1 year. The mean gain in BCVA in reproxalap-treated patients was statistically greater than that in vehicle-treated patients, although the difference is of debatable clinical relevance. Nonetheless, the results suggest the possibility that chronic reproxalap administration has potential to improve visual acuity in some patients with DED, possibly by decreasing corneal inflammation that may have led to refractive distortion.

The strengths of the clinical trial reported herein include the double-masked, vehicle-controlled design. As the trial population was both racially and ethnically diverse, the results may be broadly applicable to patients with DED. The favorable safety findings suggest that reproxalap may be a viable option for long-term treatment in patients with DED.

A limitation to consider is that the protocol was not sufficiently powered to detect rare adverse effects that could potentially be associated with reproxalap treatment, and the logistic regression and MMRM analyses were not prospectively statistically powered. Another limitation is that the trial did not include patients treated in the context of a dry eye chamber, which has been utilized in other reproxalap trials. Tolerability profiles could potentially differ depending upon the speed of symptom onset: rapidly in the dry eye chamber and more gradually in environmental protocols. Further, because patients who were unwilling to discontinue ocular therapies (topical ophthalmic prescriptions and over-the-counter solutions, gels, and lid scrubs) were excluded, the trial may have preferentially enrolled patients with relatively mild disease or patients with corneal desensitization (e.g., neurotrophic keratitis). Finally, there was no symptomatic comparison between the excluded patients and those who enrolled. Additional analysis of these subjective factors may yield more information regarding reproxalap tolerability as well as appropriate patient selection for eventual therapy.

Conclusions

The results of the clinical trial reported herein support the safety of long-term topical reproxalap therapy in patients with DED. Reproxalap has potential to be a safe and effective first-in-class new therapy for DED that alleviates inflammation by inhibiting RASP. Chronic therapy for treatable but incurable conditions such as DED or ocular allergy necessitates long-term safety and tolerability as documented in this trial.
