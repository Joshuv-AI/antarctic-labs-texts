Effects of Structural Designs on the Accuracy, Dimensional Stability, and Material Consumption of Digital Light Processing-Printed Models
Authors: Huan Liu, Jiaying Wang, Yanfang Zhao, Yan Zhang, Junli Kou, Qiang Zhang, Kai Li, Yulu Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Structural design significantly influences 3D-printed dental model performance, with variations observed in accuracy, long-term dimensional stability and material efficiency among the 4 structural designs.•The horseshoe-shaped solid (HSS) design delivered optimal performance, achieving high accuracy and long-term dimensional stability while reducing printing time and material consumption compared to the complete palatal solid (CPS) design.•Hollow structures exhibited notable dimensional instability; both complete palatal hollow (CPH) and horseshoe-shaped hollow (HSH) models showed significant deviation starting at 7 days postprinting.•All model designs maintained clinically acceptable accuracy levels (<100 μm), validating the clinical applicability of DLP-printed resin dental models across different structural designs.

Introduction

Dental models play an important role in shaping the performance of prosthodontics. They are widely used in various aspects, including (but not limited to) recording patients’ occlusal relationships; designing restorations; assisting in diagnosis; facilitating communication between dentists and dental lab technicians; and enhancing doctor-patient communication.1,2 Conventional dental models are made from gypsum, which exhibits disadvantages of being easily damaged and difficult to store.3 Compared with traditional gypsum models, digital models obtained via intraoral scanning are easy to store, efficient and cost effective.4 However, the transformation of virtual model data into a physical model is still indispensable for fabricating restorations and for checking the placing, fit and occlusion contact of the restoration in advance.5 Three-dimensional (3D) printing technology has been increasingly applied to fabricate physical models,6 which are expected to be as accurate as possible and to possess adequate dimensional stability.4,7 Recent advances have demonstrated the potential of combining 3D reconstruction techniques with finite element analysis (FEA) to enhance the accuracy of dental model and restoration, highlighting the critical role of precise digital models in prosthetic workflows.8,9

The accuracy of 3D-printed models is crucial for guaranteeing the fit between a prosthesis and the abutment.6 The accuracy of 3D-printed models is reported to be affected by the 3D printer type10,11; printing material12; printing layer thickness13; printing orientation14,15; slicing and support parameter16; and postprocessing procedure.17 After ensuring that the 3D-printed model is within a certain accuracy range, the model needs to maintain dimensional stability for a certain period of time. For example, fixed dental prostheses are typically fabricated within 1 to 2 weeks, whereas the models for implant treatment are required to remain stable for a longer time period. Notably, the dimensional stability of 3D-printed models is influenced by the printing material,18 storage duration and storage conditions.19

In the workflow of fabricating 3D-printed models, the operator can select different types of structural design. However, research on how these designs influence accuracy and stability remains limited. Revilla-León et al. used a coordinate measuring machine to assess the accuracy of material jetting models with 3 different base designs, including solid, honeycomb-structured and hollowed bases.20 Rungrojwittayakul et al. compared the printing accuracy of a solid base design and a hollow base design, both of which were printed by using digital light processing (DLP) and continuous liquid interface production (CLIP) printing techniques.21 Chen et al. used stereolithography (SLA) and DLP technology to print 4 types of design models (including hollow interiors with perforated bases, hollow interiors without bases, fully solid structures and internal support structures with perforated bases) and compared their dimensional accuracy.1 Despite these valuable contributions, the existing literature still presents several important limitations. First, the majority of previous studies focused exclusively on short-term accuracy, with evaluation periods typically limited to less than 7 days after printing.22,23 The long-term dimensional stability (>7 days) of 3D-printed resin models, which is critical for multivisit prosthodontic workflows such as implant-supported prostheses that require 2 to 4 weeks of laboratory fabrication, remains largely unexplored. Second, most studies have concentrated on complete palatal models, whereas data on the accuracy and stability of horseshoe-shaped designs are scarce. This is noteworthy because intraoral scanning of the palate requires additional time and effort, and some clinicians choose to scan only the dentition, thereby generating horseshoe-shaped model data that omits the palate and reduces chairside time by approximately 40%.24,25 The above-mentioned studies of structural design focused mainly on short-term accuracy (<7 days), and investigations into the influence of structural design on the long-term dimensional stability (>7 days) of 3D-printed models are lacking, which is critical for multivisit prosthodontic workflows (such as implant prostheses that require 2-4 weeks of fabrication). Nevertheless, the long-term dimensional stability of such horseshoe-shaped 3D-printed models has not been systematically investigated. Third, no previous study has correlated structural design with material consumption and printing time as integrated indicators of clinical applicability and cost-effectiveness. Such information is essential for dental practitioners when selecting optimal printing strategies in daily practice.

Currently, the mainstream technologies for the manufacturing of 3D-printed models include SLA and DLP.6 According to the literature, the printing efficiency of DLP is higher than that of SLA.1 Additionally, some researchers have recommended DLP as the most commonly used method for fabricating 3D-printed models.7,26 In this study, DLP technology was selected as a representative technology for dental model fabrication. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of 4 model designs on the accuracy, dimensional stability and material efficiency of DLP-printed maxillary resin models. The printed maxillary resin models in this study were intended for use in fixed prosthodontic workflows, including the fabrication of crowns and short-span fixed dental prostheses. In this context, a clinically acceptable accuracy threshold of ≤100 μm was adopted as the primary evaluation criterion, as deviations within this range are generally considered not to compromise the marginal fit or clinical performance of restorations.23

This study is the first to: (1) evaluate long-term dimensional stability (>7 days) of 3D-printed resin models with different structural designs; (2) systematically compare complete palatal versus horseshoe-shaped designs and solid versus hollow configurations over a 28-day period; (3) correlate structural design with material consumption and printing time as practical indicators for clinical decision-making. Thus, the null hypothesis was that the model designs do not affect the accuracy or dimensional stability of DLP-printed maxillary resin models.

Materials and methods

Generation of model’s structural design

The process of this study is shown in Figure 1. A standard maxillary dental model (Rijin Dental Materials Co., Ltd., China) was scanned by using a model scanner (3Shape E4, 3Shape Company, Denmark) (with a manufacturer-reported accuracy of 4 µm) to obtain a digital cast. The digital cast was imported into CAD software (3Shape Dental System 2020, 3Shape Company, Denmark), on which 4 different types of structural designs were constructed: a complete palatal solid (CPS) model, a complete palatal hollow (CPH) model with a wall thickness of 2.0 mm,23,27,28 a horseshoe-shaped solid (HSS) model, and a horseshoe-shaped hollow (HSH) model with a wall thickness of 2.0 mm (Figure 2). Model printing

A 3D printer (Wisdom1, Wisdom1 Medical Information Technology Co., Ltd., China) with DLP technology was used, along with model resin (GV-Model 1, Weishi Medical Information Technology Co., Ltd., China), to print the casts. GV-Model 1 is a methacrylate-based photopolymer. Upon UV exposure, its photoinitiator generates free radicals, triggering chain-growth polymerisation. The conversion of C=C to C–C bonds reduces molecular spacing, causing volumetric shrinkage. According to the manufacturer, the volumetric shrinkage of this resin is approximately 8%. The Wisdom1 printer utilises a DMD-based LED light source with an operating wavelength of 405 nm and a light intensity of 100 lm at the build plane as specified by the manufacturer. Firstly, the 3D printer was calibrated in accordance with the manufacturer's guidelines in advance. To ensure the standardisation of the experiment, all of the casts were manufactured with a 0° build orientation, and the build layer thickness was set to 50 µm. Five specimens were printed for each structural design type, and the sample size was determined based on previous studies related to 3D printing.12,29 To further justify the sample size, a post hoc power analysis was conducted using G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7; Universität Düsseldorf, Germany). Based on the trueness data from the 4 groups on day 1 (mean ± SD: CPS: 57.38 ± 7.56 μm; CPH: 56.56 ± 7.19 μm; HSS: 31.40 ± 4.26 μm; HSH: 49.14 ± 8.02 μm), a one-way ANOVA with 4 groups was assumed. With a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a large effect size (f = 0.68) calculated from the observed data, a total sample size of 20 (n = 5 per group) yielded a statistical power (1 – β) of 0.86, exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.80. Therefore, 5 specimens per group were considered adequate for detecting statistically significant differences among the groups. In this study, a total of 20 casts were printed, and a printing support structure was attached to the bottom of the model. The printing and postproduction procedures followed the manufacturer's instructions and recommendations, which included cleaning with absolute ethanol (Lircon Absolute Ethanol, Lircon Co., Ltd.) in an ultrasonic bath (Renfert, Derui Co., Ltd.) at 28°C for 3 min, followed by processing in an ultraviolet box (envision TEC, Han’s Laser Inc.) with a frequency of 50/60 Hz for 15 min per specimen. The LC-3DPrint Box operates at a wavelength of 405 nm with an output intensity of 8.62 mW/cm² (manufacturer specification). The 15-minute curing time was selected according to the manufacturer's recommendation for the GV-Model 1 resin to ensure adequate monomer conversion. The polymerisation time was determined according to the manufacturer's recommendations for the corresponding resin. All of the printed models were stored in a dry, dark container in a near-constant environment (20°C, 50% humidity). For each group of models, the printing time was recorded; additionally, the models were weighed by using an electronic balance (Double Jie Precision Electronic Balance, Shanghai), and the material consumption for each model type was calculated.

Analysis of trueness and precision

On days 1, 2, 3, 5, 7, 14, 21 and 28 after printing, the 20 printed resin models were scanned by using a model scanner (3Shape E4, 3Shape Company, Denmark), and the scan data were exported in STL format. The digital design files of the models and the scanned files of the printed dental models were imported into Geomagic Control 2014 software (3D Systems Inc.) for editing and registration. In the polygon phase of the Geomagic Control 2014 software, the design model files were used as the reference models, whereas the scanned files of the printed resin model were used as the research models. The research model data were aligned with the reference models via multipoint alignment. To ensure precise superimposition and eliminate potential operator bias, a standardised trimming procedure was applied to remove irrelevant areas. Specifically, the cutting boundary was objectively defined as a plane located 5 mm vertically apical to the gingival margin, following the arch contour. This plane enables consistent removal of all base areas (including support structure attachment sites), while preserving the complete anatomical integrity of the crowns and alveolar ridge. All models were trimmed using the “Section” function in Geomagic Control 2014 software (3D Systems, Inc.). The same cutting plane parameters (a constant 5 mm apical offset from the gingival margin) were applied to all specimens uniformly. This approach does not involve any manual delineation or subjective judgment. Given the small sample size and that the trimming operation could be completed in a single step using the standardised plane, no Python scripts or automation scripts were employed for this step. All operations were performed through the software’s graphical user interface with strictly defined parameters to ensure full traceability. After trimming, the remaining part of the dental arch after clipping was subjected to optimal fitting alignment and deviation analysis.

The evaluation criteria for accuracy can be categorised into trueness and precision.30,31 Trueness is defined as the degree of consistency between the research model and the reference model.30, 31, 32 In this study, the best-fit algorithm in Geomagic Studio 2014 software was used to align the scanned files of 5 printed models from each group that were obtained on the first day with the digital design model data. After deviation analysis was performed on the models, 5 root mean square (RMS) values were generated for each group, and the average RMS value was used to represent the trueness of each research model.

Precision refers to the data deviation between repeatedly printed resin models under specified conditions.31,32 For precision analysis, the scanned data of 5 printed models per group were imported into Geomagic Studio 2014 software and numbered 1 to 5. Model 1 was first designated as the reference model, with models 2 to 5 being designated as floating models; optimal alignment and 3D deviation analysis were performed between the floating models and model 1. Model 2 subsequently served as the reference model, with models 3 to 5 serving as floating models, and another optimal alignment and 3D deviation analysis were conducted. By repeating this process, all of the intragroup and intermodel comparisons were completed, thereby resulting in 10 RMS values per group. The average RMS value was used to represent the precision of the printed resin dental models, with higher RMS values indicating lower precision.

Analysis of stability

In this study, 3D deviation analysis (trueness) was performed by comparing the scanned data of the printed models over a 28-day period with the digital design models to evaluate the dimensional stability of the printed models. Colour maps were generated by using the 3D comparison tool in Geomagic Studio 2014. The maximum and minimum deviation values were set at +500 µm and -500 µm, respectively, with a tolerance range of +100 µm and –100 µm being utilised19,30; moreover, the software automatically calculated the overall RMS values.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed by using IBM SPSS Statistics statistical software (version 25; IBM Corp.). Experimental data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For the trueness and precision data on day 1, One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for intergroup comparisons among multiple groups. If statistically significant differences were detected, pairwise comparisons were further conducted by using the Bonferroni post hoc test. For the dimensional stability data over 28 days, a two-way repeated measures ANOVA was conducted to evaluate the effects of structural design as the between-subjects factor and time as the within-subjects factor. Mauchly’s test of sphericity was examined, and the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied when the sphericity assumption was violated. Bonferroni post hoc tests were performed for multiple comparisons to identify significant differences between groups at each time point and within groups across time points. A P value < .05 was considered to indicate a statistically significant difference.

Discussion

In this study, the accuracy, dimensional stability and material consumption of 3D-printed resin dental models with 4 distinct structures were evaluated through 3D superimposition analysis. Significant differences in trueness and precision were observed among the resin models printed via the use of DLP technology with different structural designs (P < .05), thus rejecting the null hypothesis that no differences exist in the accuracy of 3D-printed models with different structural designs. Camardella et al. also reported that structural differences in 3D-printed resin dental models result in variations in printing accuracy.33 In this study, significant differences in the dimensional changes were observed among the models printed with different structural designs over the research period (P < .05), thereby rejecting the corresponding null hypothesis.

The International Organisation for Standardisation (ISO 5725-1:2023) defines the accuracy of a test object as comprising trueness and precision.31 Previous studies have revealed mean trueness values ranging from 68 µm to 129 µm and mean precision values ranging from 20 µm to 68 µm.31,34, 35, 36 In this study, the trueness values ranged from 31.40 ± 4.26 µm to 57.38 ± 7.56 µm, whereas the precision values ranged from 26.56 ± 4.23 µm to 52.97 ± 6.81 µm. These results are consistent with those of previous research. Shin et al.30 reported that solid models demonstrate greater trueness than hollow models (with wall thicknesses of 1.5 mm and 4 mm). Additionally, Chen et al.1 reported that the trueness of HSS models was greater than that of HSH models, and this trend was consistent with the findings of our study. During the 3D-printing process, the base is the first part to be completed; moreover, the HSH model lacks a full base, which cannot provide adequate support and attachment points for the upper structure.33,37 Furthermore, due to the centripetal polymerisation shrinkage characteristics of the resin during the curing process,37,38 the absence of internal and base support in the HSH structure results in significant model deformation. In contrast, Rungrojwittayakul et al.21 reported that the median values of trueness are not significantly different between hollow and solid models. The discrepancy between their findings and those of this study may be attributed to differences in 3D printers, printing parameters and accuracy evaluation methods; moreover, the underlying mechanism requires further investigation.

The precision of 3D-printed models reflects the stability of repeated printing. Previous studies have revealed that the precision RMS values of 3D-printed models fabricated via DLP technology range from 53.8 µm to 76.0 µm.34 In this study, the precision of the CPS group on day 1 after printing was determined to be the worst among the 4 groups, with an RMS error value of 52.97 ± 6.81 µm (P < .05). The DLP technology employed in this study involves the use of a conventional light source to polymerise photosensitive liquid resin. Unlike SLA, each x-y layer is simultaneously exposed to light via a selectively masked light source.39 By polymerising the resin layer by layer to form the target objects, DLP offers the advantage of a high printing speed.1,26 In this study, the CPS group demonstrated the largest area of resin material per layer; additionally, the deformations generated during the printing and curing process of each layer progressively accumulated, thus resulting in the most significant intergroup deformation of the CPS group among the 4 groups. Although the precision of the CPS model group (52.97 ± 6.81 µm) was lower than that of the other 3 groups, all of the 3D-printed models exhibited clinically acceptable accuracy (<100 µm).9,21

In digital workflows, the accuracy of 3D-printed resin models is closely associated with the accuracy of prostheses. In this study, the printed models were intended for fixed prosthodontic applications (crowns and short-span fixed dental prostheses). For this clinical context, the acceptable accuracy threshold is generally ≤100 μm, as deviations within this range are unlikely to compromise marginal fit.23,40 Over the 28-day observation period, the mean trueness values of all groups remained below this threshold (range: 31.40-97.64 μm). However, notable differences were observed among structural designs. The solid groups (CPS and HSS) consistently stayed well below 100 μm (e.g., HSS: 35.12 μm at day 28). In contrast, the hollow groups (CPH and HSH) approached the threshold after day 7 (e.g., HSH: 97.64 μm at day 28), with individual specimens potentially exceeding this limit at later time points. For comparative purposes, implant prosthodontics tolerates 100 to 150 μm9 and orthodontic treatment accepts up to 200 μm.40 By explicitly defining the intended workflow as fixed prosthodontics, the present findings provide clinically relevant guidance for model selection in this context.

The trueness of the solid groups (HSS and CPS) within 28 days demonstrated no statistically significant difference compared to that on day 1 after printing (P > .05) (Table 3). The 3D-printed resin models with a solid structural design exhibited satisfactory dimensional stability. In contrast, beginning on day 7 after printing, the trueness of the hollow groups (HSH and CPH) significantly differed from that on day 1 after printing (P < .05) (Table 3), thus indicating that their dimensional stability began to decline on day 7 after printing.

Three-dimensional printing and postcuring are processes involving the polymerisation of photopolymer resins, during which resin shrinkage can affect the accuracy of printed models.11 In this study, although the CPS model and the HSS model required more resin, the sufficient thickness of the resin material and the palatal plate structure enhanced the stability of the 3D-printed dental models. The maxillary palate exerted a cross-arch stabilising effect and resisted overall model deformation induced by resin shrinkage during the postcuring process. These results are consistent with the trends reported in previous studies.21,37 The superior performance of the HSS model is attributed to the interplay between resin polymerisation shrinkage and structural support distribution. During DLP printing, photopolymer resins undergo centripetal shrinkage, generating internal stresses that induce deformation in regions lacking adequate support.38 Solid models resist these forces through uniform stress distribution, whereas hollow models (2.0 mm wall thickness) provide limited resistance, explaining their significant deviations after day 7. The HSS design achieves an optimal balance through 2 mechanisms: (1) the horseshoe configuration preserves the palatal bar and alveolar ridge as reinforcement beams, providing cross-arch stabilisation without full palatal coverage; (2) the solid nature ensures uniform stress transmission. This explains why HSS achieved the highest trueness while using only about 70% of the material required for CPS.30,37 While the present study focused on experimental measurements of dimensional changes, finite element analysis (FEA) offers a complementary approach to understanding the biomechanical mechanisms underlying structural design-induced deformation. Recent studies have demonstrated the value of computational simulations in predicting deformation patterns under various loading conditions.8 Future research may integrate experimental accuracy measurements with FEA to quantitatively simulate the polymerisation shrinkage stress distribution in different structural designs, thereby providing deeper mechanistic insights for why the HSS design performs better than other configurations.8

The cloud map demonstrated that the overall trueness of the CPS group was high on day 1 after printing, and shrinkage was observed in certain areas of the posterior occlusal surface from day 2 to day 28 (Figure 4a). The CPH group exhibited high trueness in most areas from day 1 to day 7 after printing. Compared with the HSH group, the CPH group only exhibited shrinkage at the anterior incisal edges and a small area of the left posterior teeth from day 14 to day 28. This result may be attributed to the ability of the intact palatal region to resist overall deformation during postcuring shrinkage (Figure 4b). The HSS group maintained high trueness within 28 days (Figure 4c). In the HSH group, shrinkage was mainly observed on the posterior palatal side on day 1 after printing. Beginning on day 2 postprinting, shrinkage occurred at the anterior incisal edges and cusp tips, whereas expansion was noted on the posterior buccal sides and central fossae (Figure 4d). The opposing deformation directions exhibited by the model may be due to the centripetal polymerisation shrinkage characteristics of the resin during curing,29 as well as the morphological features of these 2 regions. As the storage time increased, the deformation on the posterior palatal side became significant, whereas that in the anterior region remained relatively stable. This effect may be due to the fact that the posterior area lacks transverse support structures, thereby resulting in the most pronounced deformation in the posterior region.33

Additionally, the printing time and material consumption of resin models (which are 2 key factors involved in selecting printing strategies) should also be considered.29 In the 4 model groups included in this study, both the CPS and HSS groups exhibited satisfactory dimensional stability but differed in terms of their printing time and material consumption. The printing time for the CPS group was 80 min whereas that for the HSS group was 65 min. Moreover, the HSS group consumed 29.44 ± 0.13 g of resin, which is approximately 69.96% of the material used by the CPS group (Table 3). When considering the comprehensive factors of accuracy, dimensional stability, printing time and material utilisation, a resin-printed model with a HSS design offers greater advantages.

Based on our findings, we propose scenario-specific recommendations. For fixed prosthodontics (crowns/bridges), the HSS model is recommended as the first choice due to its high trueness (31.40 μm), excellent 28-day stability and material savings (30% less than CPS). For implant prosthodontics, the CPS model may be preferred for multiple-implant cases requiring maximum palatal rigidity and long-term stability (>4 weeks), although HSS remains viable for single-implant restorations. For orthodontic models, the HSH model may be acceptable for short-term use (<7 days) when material efficiency is prioritised, as orthodontic applications tolerate a wider accuracy range (up to 200 μm).40 However, for longer-term storage or multistage workflows, solid designs are recommended. For routine diagnostic models, the HSS model offers the best balance of accuracy, stability and cost-effectiveness. As emphasised by Osman et al., no single 3D-printing configuration is universally superior; rather, parameters should be selected based on the intended use.15 The present study extends this principle to structural design selection for maxillary models.

A limitation of this study is that it focused on the impact of structural design on the dimensional stability of the maxillary model, with only a single type of 3D printer and photopolymer resin being used. To enhance the generalisability of the findings, additional internal designs, 3D printer types and resin materials should be investigated in future studies. Furthermore, factors such as the printing layer thickness; build angle; postcuring time and method; postprinting storage duration; and storage conditions may affect the accuracy and dimensional stability of resin-printed models41,42; thus, further investigations on other parameter combinations are necessary. Regarding the measurement methodology, all specimens were scanned using the same scanner under identical conditions by a single operator. Thus, any systematic error was applied uniformly across all groups and time points, which does not compromise the validity of intergroup comparisons or the assessment of dimensional changes over time. Moreover, the 3Shape E4 scanner has a manufacturer-reported accuracy of ±4 μm. Given that the RMS trueness values ranged from 31.40 μm to 97.64 μm, the inherent scanning error accounted for approximately 4.1% to 12.7% of the measured deviations. This proportion is relatively small, the presence of scanning error does not compromise the conclusion that all models remained within the clinically acceptable range. Nevertheless, the scanner’s accuracy was not independently verified against a certified reference standard. Future studies should consider using coordinate measuring machines or cross-validation with multiple scanners to distinguish printing error from scanning error. In this study, only the RMS values were used for quantitative analysis of manufacturing accuracy. In future research, it is also necessary to consider the average absolute distance and positive and negative average deviations, along with exploring strategies to improve the dimensional stability of resin-printed dental models.
