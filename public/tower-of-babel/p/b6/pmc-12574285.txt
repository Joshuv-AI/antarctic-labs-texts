A novel frameshift variation of PKD1 in familial autosomal dominant polycystic kidney diseases: expanding the clinical phenotype and genetic spectrum of PKD1 disorders
Authors: Qi Chen, Lin Shi, Xiaolan Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAutosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease is a common monogenic hereditary kidney disorder. Mutations in the PKD1 gene lead to the development of fluid-filled cysts in the kidney, liver, and other organs, which can eventually result in end-stage renal failure. Affected individuals have a 50% chance of passing the mutation to each of their offspring. Families with ADPKD are advised to undergo genetic testing to causative variants and would benefit from genetic counseling to reduce the inherited risk.MethodWe performed genetic analysis on a family with ADPKD. DNA samples extracted from the peripheral blood of ADPKD patients were subjected to whole exome sequencing (WES) analysis. Variations were assessed in accordance with the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomic (ACMG) guidelines. Moreover, recent literature focusing on newly identified pathogenic PKD1 mutations and their clinical manifestations was summarized in this study.ResultA novel heterozygous variant in the PKD1 gene [NM_001009944.2, c. 5733_5751dup GGCTGCCGGCTCAGCTGTC (p.Thr1918Glyfs*78)] was identified. The variant was absent in published database and resulted in a frameshift mutation predicted to lead to premature termination of the protein translation. The comprehensive analysis of population frequency, conservation, structural and pathogenicity prediction suggested that this variant was classified as pathogenic.ConclusionThis is the first reported ADPKD case caused by this novel PKD1 variant. This finding expands the known mutation spectrum of the PKD1 genes and provides a foundation for genetic counseling and future preimplantation genetic diagnosis in affected families. Our findings offer valuable genetic and clinical insights for accurate diagnosis and evaluation of disease progression.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12920-025-02248-8.

Introduction

Autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease (ADPKD) is the most common hereditary monogenic kidney disorder, with an estimated prevalence of 1/1000 to 1/2500 individuals [1, 2]. Patients with ADPKD typically present with bilateral renal cysts and diverse extrarenal manifestations, including hepatic and pancreatic cysts [3], intracranial aneurysms [4], abdominal hernias, and/or cardiac valvular disease [5]. However, this systemic condition exhibits significant phenotypic variability in numerous familial cases. Hypertension is very common in adult patients [6]. Other complications arising from cyst growth include acute and chronic pain, gross hematuria, cyst infection, and nephrolithiasis. Progressive loss of kidney function over several decades often leads to end-stage renal failure [7]. Although advances in genetic diagnosis, imaging and laboratory examination have improved our knowledge and understanding of the disease and its management, approximately 7% of ADPKD pedigrees remain genetically unresolved [8].

ADPKD is caused primarily by heterozygous mutations in the PKD1 (chromosome 16p13.3; in 78% of disease pedigrees) or PKD2 (chromosome 4q21; in 15% of disease pedigrees) genes, with other rare cases involving the IFT140 (accounts for approximately 2% of ADPKD cases) [9, 10], GANAB, DNAJB11, and ALG8/9 genes [1, 8, 11, 12]. PKD1 encodes polycystin-1 (PC1), a large multidomain glycoprotein that is cleaved at a G protein-coupled receptor proteolytic site, whereas PKD2 produces polycystin-2 (PC2), a protein belonging to the transient receptor potential family of calcium-regulated cation channels [1]. Genetic testing plays key diagnostic and therapeutic roles. Whole exome sequencing (WES) which enables a comprehensive analysis of almost all exon regions, has become a routine method for mutation detection [13, 14]. Imaging-wise, ultrasonography serves as the primary diagnostic tool [15], with magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and CT providing additional value for assessment, classification, and complication detection, particularly in young individuals [16]. Thus, combined imaging and genetic testing enables effective disease monitoring and the prevention of familial transmission through either prenatal diagnosis (early/mid-pregnancy) or preimplantation genetic diagnosis [17].

Here, we report a family with inherited polycystic kidney and liver disease associated with a novel frameshift duplication variant in the PKD1 gene; this variant has not been previously documented in the general population. To our knowledge, this family represents the first reported case of ADPKD caused by this specific variant. Furthermore, this paper reviews other novel and rare variants from the literature, along with their extrarenal manifestations, are reviewed in this paper.

Materials and methods

Case presentation

A family with inherited PKD was referred to the Reproductive Medical Center, the Fourth affiliated hospital of Zhenjiang, Jiangsu University. His family pedigree was showed in the Fig. 1A. The proband (IV-1) is a 16-year-old young boy with normal creatinine level and normal blood pressure at the time of the investigation. The ultrasound examination showed bilateral polycystic kidney (Fig. 1B-a and -b) and numerous liver cysts (Fig. 1C). Head MRI showed an enlargement of cerebellar medullary cistern and prepontine cistern (Fig. 1D). He was only suffered with polyuria (frequent bedwetting during childhood) and asthma at the time of our investigation. The informed consent from his parents was obtained. The experimental protocol was under the approval of the Ethics Committee of Fourth affiliated hospital of Jiangsu University.

Whole exome sequencing (WES) analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood leukocytes of the patient using the Pure gene Blood Kit (Qiagen, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Genomic DNA (300 ng) was broken into 250–350 bp fragments which were allowed to connect with special adaptor with a “T” base at the 3’ end. After PCR amplification and purification, the target DNA fragment in the PrePCR library hybridizes with a biotinylated probe, and the captured enriched DNA is amplified by PCR to obtain a Post PCR library. Samples were sequenced by MGISEQ-2000 (BGI, China).

All reads were aligned to the human genome reference (GRCh37/hg19) with BWA Multi-Vision software package. The output files from the alignment were used to conduct sequencing coverage and depth analysis of the target region, single-nucleotide variants (SNVs), and INDEL calling. To detect SNVs and indels, we utilized the GATK software. Copy number variations (CNVs) were analyzed using the ExomeDepth algorithm. All SNVs and indels were filtered and evaluated using multiple databases, including NCBI dbSNP, 1000 human genome dataset (phase 3), ClinVar (2024-01-07), ESP6500 (V2), GnomAD (r2.1.1), ExAC (r1), BPGD (V2023Q1, BGI-Phoenix genetic database) and SecondaryFinding_Var* (V2.0.0_20231030, an in-house database of BGI Genomics). The candidate variants were assessed using the multi-sequence alignments and substitution assessment tools: SIFT, MutationTaster, Polyphen2, Splice AI (1.3) and dbscSNV (1.1). Lastly, variant classification was conducted following the American Society for Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines [18], and reported as pathogenic, likely pathogenic, variant of unknown significance (VUS), likely benign, or benign.

Sanger sequencing

To validate the variant, the corresponding gene region surrounding the variations were amplified by PCR and sequenced by Sanger sequencing. To distinguish the sequence of the PKD1 exons 1–33 from the other six pseudogenes, the relevant areas were amplified using PKD1-specific primers that were designed by BGI laboratory. Bidirectional Sanger sequencing was performed after two rounds of PCR amplification for the sequence analysis of the PKD1 gene. For genomic DNA samples, the PCR products were sequenced using ABI Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, United States) following the manufacturer’s protocol.

Results

Clinical evaluation

We investigated five individuals with PKD in this family (Fig. 1A). I1 and I2 had no useful clinical information. II2, 60-year-old, had a polycystic kidney and polycystic liver and renal failure (Cr: 391 µmol/l, eGFR: 10.17 ml/min/1.73m2.) She is undergoing hemodialysis currently. Affected individuals II3 and II4 were died of kidney failure at 65 and 70 years old, respectively. The proband’s mother (38-year-old, III-1) was diagnosed with ADPKD with numerous cysts in the kidney and liver. At her current age, she exhibits a normal creatinine level, normal blood pressure, and an absence of clinical symptoms or complications. The proband’s (IV-1) sister (4-year-old, IV-2) experiences recurrently frequent bedwetting. She is suspected to have ADPKD, but has not undergone further examinations.

Genetic analysis

In this family, we identified a heterozygous variant (NM_001009944.2, c. 5733_5751dup GGCTGCCGGCTCAGCTGTC, p. Thr1918Glyfs*78) in PKD1 as the candidate genetic cause of PKD. Moreover, the other reported mutations identified by WES were shown in the supplementary Table 1. Sanger sequencing revealed that the proband’s mother (III-1) and grandmother (II-2) shared the same variant (c. 5733_5751dup), but not in the father (III-2). The family pedigree exhibits the typical pattern of inheritance seen in families with ADPKD (PP4). The results revealed that the candidate variant co-segregated with typical ADPKD phenotype in this family (PP1).

This variant p. Thr1918Glyfs*78 (c. 5733_5751dup) is a novel frameshift mutation, which has not been reported in the general population as indicated in the ESP6500, EXAC browser, 1000 Genome Project, GnomAD, described in disease databases such as HGMD, ClinVar, or Mayo ADPKD variant Database (PM2). This variant may cause the 1918th amino acid to change from threonine to glycine, leading to the early termination of protein translation. This is a harmful effect on the function of polycistin-1 protein (PVS1). Based on the above results, the novel variant c. 5733_5751dup in PKD1 was categorized as “pathogenic” (PVS1 + PP4 + PM2 + PP1) according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) criteria [18].

Discussion

ADPKD exhibits a high degree of clinical variability among affected individuals, attributable to genetic and allelic heterogeneity, as well as environmental influences. The presence of diverse pathogenic variants and significant intrafamilial phenotypic variability further complicates genotype-phenotype correlations, particularly regarding the rate of progression to end-stage renal failure [1, 2]. The identification of specific genotype-phenotype relationships and the mutation types is crucial for explaining much of the clinical heterogeneity observed in patients with ADPKD. Here, we report a novel frameshift duplication variant in the PKD1 gene (c. 5733_5751dup, p.Thr1918Glyfs*78), identified in a notable family ADPKD presenting with polycystic kidneys and liver diseases. Compared with the wild-type PKD1 gene, this duplicated variant causes the 1918th amino acid to change from threonine to glycine and results in premature termination of translation. This affected family members exhibited significant ADPKD manifestations, consistent with the predicted pathogenic effects of the mutation. According to ACMG guidelines, this novel frameshift mutation in PKD1 is classified as pathogenic and disease-causing.

To our knowledge, most ADPKD cases are caused by mutations in the PKD1 and/or PKD2 genes. Protein-truncating variants -such as large rearrangements, nonsense, frameshift, and splicing variants - that disrupt the coding sequence account for a substantial portion of cases and are uniformly classified as “pathogenic”, exhibiting complete penetrance [12, 13]. In contrast, nontruncating variants like missense and atypical splicing mutations are often designated “likely pathogenic” [12] and frequently show incomplete and variable penetrance [2, 19]. Notably, over 97% of patients with the loss-of-function (LOF) variants displayed ADPKD, whereas only 31.2% of those carrying “likely pathogenic” missense mutations ultimately manifest the diseases, suggesting misclassification or incomplete penetrance [2]. Furthermore, patients with PKD1 mutations typically exhibit more severe phenotypes than those with PKD2 variants. However, even truncating mutations may occasionally be associated with mild kidney disease. For example, one study found that 18% (32/174) of patients with typical image patterns and protein-truncating PKD1 mutations displayed mild clinical and radiological disease [7]. Additionally, the identification of de novo mutations [20–22], which occur with no family history, adds another layer of complexity [23]. For example, one study has screened 90 unrelated families with ADPKD and identified 76 pathogenic or likely pathogenic mutations, including 28 novel PKD1 variants. Notably, four of these novel variants were de novo with no family history [23].

Accurate genetic diagnosis is crucial but challenging, particularly for PKD1 due to its large size (~ 50 kb, 46 exons) and the presence of six highly homologous pseudogenes that share sequence similarity with exons 1–33 of PKD1. To overcome the limitations of standard next-generation sequencing (NGS), methods like long-range PCR and multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MLPA) have emerged as more effective for comprehensive variant detection in patients with ADPKD [5, 24]. For example, in cases where NGS identified no abnormalities, MLPA revealed three heterozygous deletions (PKD1-up-257 nt, PKD1-up-444 nt), PKD1−3 nt–141 nt, and PKD1−18 nt–290 nt in three unrelated patients with ADPKD [20]. Thus, selecting the appropriate genetic methods is essential for accurate diagnosis, classification and clinical management of patients with ADPKD.

Increasing evidence indicates that novel variants in the PKD1 gene contribute to ADPKD pathogenesis in families exhibiting typical phenotypes. The clinical features facilitate the classification of the variants - particularly those initial labeled as uncertain significance (VUS) or likely pathogenic - to a definite pathogenic status (Table 1). For instance, two previously uncharacterized frameshift mutations in the PKD1 gene - c.9484delC (p.Arg3162Alafs*154) [25] and c.9053delT (p.Phe3018Serfs*56) [26] - were confirmed as pathogenic based on strong clinical and familiar ADPKD history. Another suspected pathogenic site c. 151 T >C (p. Cys51Arg) was initially considered likely pathogenic according to ACMG guidelines. After further validation by sequencing technology and pedigree co-segregation evidence, this variant was finally reclassified as pathogenic [20]. However, an in-frame deletion c.2084_2089del (p.Pro695_Ala696del) [27] and a variant of VUS c.12,406 A >G (p.Arg4136Gly) [28] in the PKD1 gene, were classified as likely pathogenic due to insufficient familiar co-segregation evidence. Furthermore, family-based segregation analysis demonstrated a “synonymous” variant c.12444G >A in the PKD1 gene, co-segregated with an ADPKD family and result in aberrant splicing products encoding truncated proteins. This effect is similar to that of another novel variant c.12,441 + 1 G >A, confirming that both are classified as the ADPKD-causing mutations [22]. Thus, families with ADPKD carrying novel variants should be enrolled to facilitate the collection of clinical manifestations across a broader range of cases. Functional analyses of these variants are also essential. These efforts would ultimately enable affected families to benefit from genetic counseling, including prenatal diagnosis or preimplantation genetic diagnosis.

In ADPKD, the age of onset and disease progression exhibit significant heterogeneity and phenotypic variability within pedigrees. Some individuals experience rapid disease progression, while others remain asymptomatic or exhibit only mild symptoms [29]. The predominant manifestation of ADPKD is the development of numerous bilateral renal cysts [15, 16], which is similar to our case. Disease progression involves renal and extrarenal manifestations, including cyst formation in organs such as the liver, seminal vesicles [26, 30], pancreas [31], arachnoid membrane [32], as well as noncystic complications like abdominal herniation, intracranial aneurysms [33], and cardiac abnormalities. Additionally, patients with hepatic cysts commonly experience symptoms including abdominal pain, dyspepsia, dyspnea, early satiety, and gastroesophageal reflux [34]. While rare, severe complications such as portal hypertension accompanied by ascites and pleural effusion may develop [3]. Patients in our cases exhibited both renal and hepatic cysts, with older individuals developing ESKD; two of them ultimately died from renal failure without verification.

In our study, MRI revealed an enlarged cisterna magna and a noticeable widening of the prepontine cistern in the proband. Although no clinical manifestations were observed at the time of our investigation, we doubted that whether this phenotype may be associated with ADPKD. Studies have indicated most intracranial arachnoid cysts are located in the middle cranial fossa, although some occur in the cerebellopontine angle, suprasellar cistern, quadrigeminal cistern, convexity, and posterior fossa/cisterna magna [35–37]. While cyst size and growth patterns vary, most cysts remain clinically silent, with subtle and unpredictable progression; the majority are asymptomatic [36]. In our case, the presence of enlarged cerebellar medullary cistern in ADPKD is rare and may be associated with cystic dilatations in the cisterna magna of the arachnoid cyst type [38, 39]. Although current evidence is insufficient to confirm it as a definite ADPKD-associated manifestation, long-term tracking of the family’s phenotypes may provide further insights.

In conclusion, we identified a novel pathogenic truncating mutation in the PKD1 gene in a Chinese family with ADPKD using WES and Sanger validation. By integrating clinical phenotypes, laboratory findings and cDNA frameshift analysis, we established the causative role of this previously unreported variant in ADPKD pathogenesis. Our findings highlight the critical importance of genetic testing in both establishing molecular diagnosis in prenatal and facilitating early clinical interventions to optimize patient management.
