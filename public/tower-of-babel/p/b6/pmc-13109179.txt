GOLD 2026: Transforming COPD Management with Early Intervention, Multi-dimensional Assessment, and Personalized Care
Authors: Mario Cazzola, Jyoti Bajpai, Luigino Calzetta, Maria Gabriella Matera, Paola Rogliani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The 2026 report from the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) introduces substantial conceptual and practical updates to the management of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. While maintaining the established spirometric definition, the report emphasizes early diagnosis, multi-dimensional assessment, and personalized treatment strategies that move beyond a spirometry-centric approach. Key innovations include formally recognizing disease activity as a therapeutic target, refining the ABE classification with a lower threshold for patients prone to exacerbations (Group E), and integrating blood eosinophil counts to guide inhaled corticosteroid therapy. Nonpharmacologic interventions, such as pulmonary rehabilitation, vaccination, smoking cessation, structured self-management, and post-exacerbation care, are elevated to core disease-modifying strategies. Pharmacological escalation is structured around dual bronchodilation as the preferred initial step, with further intensification to biomarker-guided triple therapy, including inhaled corticosteroids or other anti-inflammatory agents, reserved for selected patients who remain symptomatic or experience exacerbations despite optimized dual therapy. GOLD 2026 also introduces biologics, dupilumab and mepolizumab, as an add-on therapy for exacerbation-prone eosinophilic chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. However, it also highlights ongoing limitations in efficacy, cost effectiveness, and generalizability. Artificial intelligence and emerging digital technologies are recognized as promising adjuncts in the management of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, though their clinical implementation remains preliminary. Overall, GOLD 2026 advances precision medicine in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease by combining structured individualized assessments with early targeted interventions. However, significant uncertainties remain, including biological variability of biomarkers, limited evidence for emerging therapies, and barriers to equitable access to nonpharmacologic and advanced interventions. Careful context-sensitive application and continued validation are essential.

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. It imposes a significant burden on healthcare systems because of frequent hospitalizations, disability, and premature death [1]. Despite decades of mechanistic insights and therapeutic advances, COPD is often diagnosed at advanced stages and remains undertreated. It is also managed in a fragmented manner that frequently fails to account for its biological heterogeneity and frequent multi-morbidity [2]. In this context, successive versions of the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) strategy documents have emerged as the global standard for COPD diagnosis and management [3, 4], with the latest update, the GOLD 2026 report, being notable for its refined clinical algorithms and the introduction of several conceptual innovations pertinent to clinical practice [5].

While the 2026 iteration does not fundamentally redefine COPD, it does reframe the disease paradigm and its clinical management [6, 7]. A principal innovation is the formal adoption of disease activity as a therapeutic target, drawing on paradigms established in other chronic inflammatory conditions. GOLD 2026 encourages clinicians to consider modifiable biological activity that, when mitigated, may slow disease progression and prevent irreversible structural damage. This concept extends management beyond merely controlling symptoms and conventional ‘future risk’ assessment. It informs the updated management cycle, the restructured ABE classification, and the therapeutic decision making process, including escalation, tapering, and the introduction of advanced therapies, such as biologics.

A second major focus of GOLD 2026 is the earlier and more proactive diagnosis of COPD [5–7]. Underdiagnosis remains prevalent, with a substantial proportion of patients unaware of their underlying airflow obstruction. The report distinguishes between targeted case finding, which it endorses, and population screening, which it does not recommend. GOLD 2026 consolidates evidence in support of structured questionnaires, handheld devices, and opportunistic testing in high-risk clinical settings. These recommendations are consistent with emerging epidemiological data indicating that early physiological abnormalities, particularly emphysema and small-airway disease, as detected by computed tomography, precede spirometric decline by several years. This provides a theoretical window for ‘activity suppression’ interventions [8].

Third, GOLD 2026 contextualizes COPD within the broader spectrum of multi-morbidity and frailty [5–7]. An expanded chapter on comorbidities emphasizes that most patients with COPD encountered in clinical settings have more than one condition and highlights the presence of cardiovascular, metabolic, skeletal, and mental health conditions. Alongside conventional pulmonary management, integrated care pathways that address shared risk factors, potential drug–drug interactions, and competing clinical priorities are recommended. This approach aligns with the growing body of evidence suggesting that COPD is best conceptualized as a systemic disorder involving overlapping inflammatory, metabolic, and behavioral pathways, rather than as an isolated airway disease [9]. Furthermore, longitudinal analyses suggest that multi-morbidity clusters, rather than spirometric severity alone, often influence prognosis, resource utilization, and mortality [10].

GOLD 2026 refines the ABE classification by lowering the exacerbation threshold for Group E. This change includes patients who have experienced a single moderate exacerbation in the preceding year. Follow-up algorithms have been restructured to target treatable traits, such as dyspnea and exacerbations, as well as to integrate biomarker-guided escalation, adjunctive therapies, and biologics for select patient groups [11]. Recent real-world studies indicate that even a single moderate exacerbation is associated with an increased risk of future events, accelerated lung function decline, and higher mortality [12]. However, the impact of this lower threshold on potential overtreatment remains to be determined.

Non-pharmacological interventions are emphasized more, and pulmonary rehabilitation, vaccination, smoking cessation, oxygen/non-invasive ventilation (NIV) therapy, and structured post-exacerbation care are considered core disease-modifying strategies rather than optional adjuncts [5–7]. Evidence from controlled trials shows that these interventions reduce readmissions and improve survival, with effect sizes comparable to pharmacological escalation in selected patient groups [13].

The exacerbation chapter has been substantially revised, adopting a physiology-based severity classification system (the Rome proposal) [14] and emphasizing early intervention, structured discharge bundles, and timely follow-up to mitigate recurrent events [15]. GOLD 2026 also introduces a new chapter on artificial intelligence (AI) and emerging technologies, reflecting the rapid integration of digital tools in COPD care [5–7]. Topics include AI-assisted diagnosis, risk prediction, smart inhalers, remote monitoring, telehealth triage, virtual rehabilitation, and chatbot-supported self-management. While evidence remains limited, the report anticipates a gradual shift toward hybrid data-rich care models, contingent on longitudinal multi-modal patient data. However, there are significant concerns regarding validation, generalizability, and algorithmic bias, particularly in under-represented populations [16].

This review provides a critical comparison of GOLD 2026 with the 2025 report, highlighting key updates and conceptual changes. It specifically examines revisions in the ABE framework, the disease-activity paradigm, non-pharmacological strategies, and AI-enabled care, discussing their potential integration into routine clinical practice. While systematically contrasting GOLD 2025 and GOLD 2026, the review goes beyond simple description by explicitly highlighting the authors’ interpretative insights. Drawing on the current literature, we critically evaluate the conceptual innovations, identify potential limitations, and offer our perspective on their practical and theoretical implications. This approach allows us to contextualize emerging concepts, such as the operationalization of ‘disease activity,’ while acknowledging that empirical validation of certain constructs is still ongoing [17–20]. Readers are referred to the original reports for primary evidence and official recommendations.

Diagnosis and Assessment

GOLD 2026 maintains the traditional spirometric definition of COPD, which requires persistent airflow limitation with a post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) <0.70 in a patient with symptoms and risk factors that are consistent with the diagnosis [5], but it clarifies the diagnostic pathway (Table 1). Pre-bronchodilator spirometry can serve as an initial screening for symptomatic individuals. Although normal values typically exclude COPD, continued surveillance is warranted in high-risk patients because early small-airway dysfunction may precede formal obstruction [21]. Post-bronchodilator testing is required to confirm obstruction. Routine bronchodilator reversibility testing to differentiate asthma from COPD is discouraged because of its limited diagnostic and prognostic utility. Significant bronchodilator reversibility (defined as a change in FEV1 ≥12% and ≥200 mL according to the 2005 European Respiratory Society (ERS)/American Thoracic Society (ATS) guideline criteria [23]) has been shown to occur with a similar frequency in patients with asthma (17–19%) and COPD (18–24%), as well as in individuals without chronic airway disease [24]. In 2021, the ERS/ATS Task Force recommended defining bronchodilator reversibility as a change in FEV1 or FVC of more than 10% of the predicted value [25]. A real-life study demonstrated that positive bronchodilator reversibility occurs in fewer than one quarter of treated patients with chronic airway disease [26]. This finding was consistent regardless of the diagnostic criteria used (ERS/ATS 2005 or 2021), and showed no clinical utility in discriminating a physician-assigned diagnosis of asthma from COPD [26]. Furthermore, bronchodilator reversibility in COPD varies over time and is only weakly associated with symptom burden, exacerbation risk, or health status once baseline lung function is taken into account [27]. Therefore, GOLD advises against withholding maintenance inhalers before testing in stable patients.

The report explicitly advises against population-based spirometric screening of asymptomatic low-risk adults because of its limited diagnostic value and poor cost effectiveness. Instead, it recommends targeted case finding in individuals with persistent respiratory symptoms or established risk factors, such as tobacco or biomass exposure, occupational dusts or fumes, recurrent infections, or early-life lung injury. In these populations, spirometry should be used frequently and supplemented with short questionnaires, handheld devices (such as peak-flow meters or microspirometers), and opportunistic testing in settings where it will be most effective. One such opportunity is chest computed tomography performed for lung cancer screening: incidental emphysema or airway wall thickening strongly predicts an accelerated decline in FEV1 and FVC, and increases the likelihood of future obstruction, independently of smoking status or baseline spirometry [28].

Once COPD is confirmed, the GOLD 2026 strategy recommends performing a multi-dimensional assessment that covers five areas: the extent of airflow obstruction, symptom burden and its effect on daily life, history of exacerbations, blood eosinophil count (BEC), which serves as a biomarker of type 2 inflammation and corticosteroid response, and comorbidities. Although FEV1 remains important for prognosis, its correlation with dyspnea and overall health status is modest [29]. Therefore, GOLD grades 1–4 are only one part of a risk assessment, aligning with the broader ‘treatable traits’ approach that emphasizes personalized biological and clinical disease drivers over spirometry alone [9].

To improve symptom evaluations, the report recommends using validated tools such as the modified Medical Research Council (mMRC) dyspnea scale, the COPD Assessment Test (CAT), now renamed Chronic Airways Assessment Test (CAAT), and, when possible, the St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire (SGRQ). St George’s Respiratory Questionnaire thresholds of ≥25, CAAT thresholds of ≥10, and mMRC thresholds of ≥2 reflect clinically meaningful symptoms. These thresholds correlate more reliably with patient-centered outcomes and healthcare utilization than spirometry [30] and enhance discrimination of disease activity states, supporting GOLD’s emphasis on a dynamic rather than static assessment. These metrics inform ABE grouping and guide both initial therapy and subsequent treatment adjustments.

Further physiological testing, including lung volumes, diffusion capacity, and formal exercise assessment, is recommended when symptoms are more severe than suggested by FEV1, or when comorbidities such as pulmonary hypertension, heart failure, or anemia are suspected. New small-airway function tests, such as impulse oscillometry, may offer further insight into the early stages of the disease in select patients [31].

Exacerbation history and blood eosinophil levels remain central. Exacerbations are classified as moderate if they are treated with systemic corticosteroids and/or antibiotics, and severe if they require emergency care or hospitalization. GOLD now categorizes patients as high risk (Group E) after even one moderate or severe event in the previous year, replacing the earlier threshold of two or more events. This revision is supported by real-world data from over 2.7 million patients, for whom a single moderate exacerbation was found to increase the 1-year risk of subsequent exacerbations and all-cause mortality [17]. Furthermore, in the UPLIFT trial, FEV1 decline nearly doubled following the first moderate-to-severe exacerbation (76.5 vs 39.1 mL/year) [32]. Nevertheless, it remains to be seen whether this lower threshold will lead to overtreatment in some patients [5].

Blood eosinophil count is a strong indicator of exacerbation risk in COPD, with elevated levels, particularly ≥300 cells/μL, consistently associated with increased frequency and severity of exacerbations [33]. However, the clinical utility of a single measurement is limited by substantial intra-individual variability in eosinophil levels over time, which can lead to misclassification of risk and inappropriate management decisions [34]. Consequently, repeated measurements obtained during clinically stable periods are required to more accurately characterize a patient’s typical eosinophilic profile and to reliably inform risk stratification. Accordingly, the practical recommendation to obtain two to three BEC measurements during clinically stable periods to better estimate a patient’s typical eosinophilic profile should be supported because averaging repeated values in routine clinical practice may mitigate biological variability and provide a more robust estimate of a patient’s usual eosinophil level [35].

Further refinement of the assessment is achieved through selective imaging. Although routine CT scanning is not required, CT is advised when symptoms, exacerbation burden, or clinical findings appear disproportionate to spirometric impairment, when incidental abnormalities are found, or when a lung-volume reduction or bronchoscopic interventions are being considered. Quantitative CT scanning provides a detailed characterization of emphysema distribution, airway morphology (e.g., airway wall thickness and lumen dimensions), and air trapping, offering additional prognostic information beyond that supplied by spirometry [29]. Evidence from the Genetic Epidemiology of COPD (COPDGene) and the Canadian Cohort Obstructive Lung Disease (CanCOLD) shows that CT-derived markers, such as parametric response mapping and airway morphology metrics, can identify individuals at a higher risk of rapid progression, particularly in moderate COPD [36]. These markers may also support the implementation of earlier personalized therapeutic interventions [29].

Together, these strategies shift the focus of GOLD 2026 from a predominantly spirometry-centric binary paradigm to a structured, quantitative, and multi-dimensional assessment that integrates symptoms, exacerbations, eosinophils, lung function, CT phenotyping, and comorbidities. The overarching objective is to identify high-risk individuals early on and suppress disease activity more effectively, in line with the broader evolution of precision respiratory medicine. In this approach, biological and clinical phenotyping inform individualized care, moving beyond uniform severity-based treatment algorithms [37].

ABE Classification

The GOLD 2026 update consolidates the transition from the traditional ABCD assessment tool to the refined ABE framework, formally integrating this categorization with decisions regarding initial pharmacological therapy. This structure reflects a conceptual shift in COPD evaluation. Symptom burden and exacerbation risk are now deliberately distinguished from each other, in line with evidence showing that, although these two dimensions are related, they follow distinct trajectories throughout the course of the disease and have independent prognostic and therapeutic implications. In parallel, the 2026 GOLD update lowers the threshold for defining exacerbation-prone disease, in line with accumulating evidence suggesting that even a single exacerbation carries significant prognostic implications.

Within this framework, Groups A and B are restricted to patients who have not experienced any exacerbations requiring treatment in the preceding year, and are differentiated solely by symptom burden, as assessed using validated instruments. In routine practice, a CAAT score <10 and an mMRC dyspnea grade <2 categorize patients as Group A, while those with a CAAT score ≥10 or mMRC ≥2 are assigned to Group B. Group E, in contrast, includes any patient with at least one moderate or severe exacerbation in the previous 12 months, regardless of their baseline symptom severity.

Initial Pharmacological Therapy in GOLD 2026

The ABE algorithm is intended specifically for individuals who have not previously received maintenance inhaled therapy. Initial pharmacological decisions are guided by two variables: the ABE group and BEC. For patients in Group A, who are characterized by having minimal symptoms and no exacerbations requiring treatment in the preceding year, initial therapy with a bronchodilator is recommended. Short-acting agents, such as salbutamol or ipratropium, can be used on an as-needed basis for intermittent symptom relief. Escalation to a long-acting bronchodilator is advised when symptoms occur more frequently or when short-acting agents are required more than two to three times per week. GOLD 2026 does not specify a preference for long-acting β₂-agonists (LABAs) or long-acting muscarinic antagonists (LAMAs) at this stage, although LAMAs may improve patient-reported outcomes more effectively [38]. Continuing treatment should depend on clear clinical benefits, underscoring the importance of avoiding unnecessary pharmacological exposure. Although initial dual bronchodilation is not required for patients in Group A, some experts advocate early initiation of LABA/LAMA combinations on mechanistic grounds, as combined autonomic modulation provides greater bronchodilation than monotherapy [39].

Group B comprises patients with a higher symptom burden, but who have not experienced any recent exacerbations. For this group, GOLD 2026 recommends dual long-acting bronchodilation (LABA/LAMA) as the default initial therapy. This recommendation is supported by multiple randomized controlled trials in symptomatic patients at a low risk of exacerbations, which show superior improvements in lung function, dyspnea, health status, and selected exacerbation-related outcomes compared with monotherapy, without an increased risk of serious adverse events. Monotherapy is now considered a secondary option, reserved for circumstances in which combination therapy is limited by cost, accessibility, tolerability, or patient preference.

The most significant change to previous strategies relates to Group E. Under the 2026 GOLD guidelines, any patient with at least one moderate or severe exacerbation in the previous year is considered at high risk and should start treatment with a LABA/LAMA combination. This recommendation is supported by meta-analytic evidence that consistently ranks dual bronchodilation as more effective than monotherapy or LABAs/inhaled corticosteroids (ICSs) combinations at preventing exacerbations. Accordingly, GOLD 2026 explicitly discourages initiating LABA/ICS therapy for COPD in the absence of asthma symptoms, owing to its comparatively weaker efficacy in preventing exacerbations and an increased risk of pneumonia and other corticosteroid-related adverse effects. When ICSs are indicated, escalating directly to triple therapy (LABA/LAMA/ICS) is preferred over stepwise ICS introduction.

Blood eosinophil count serve as a pragmatic biomarker to inform the use of ICSs. Levels of 300 cells/µL or more are strongly predictive of a favorable response to ICS therapy. In exacerbation-prone patients with eosinophils in this range, initiation of triple therapy may be considered even in treatment-naïve patients. However, it should be noted that much of the supporting evidence is extrapolated from step-up trials rather than dedicated de novo trials of triple therapy. Consequently, treatment decisions should rely on individualized clinical judgment, recognizing that blood eosinophils exhibit a continuous, rather than dichotomous, association with therapeutic response. The clinical utility of BECs in COPD is limited by their inherent variability. Substantial intra-individual fluctuations have been documented within individuals over time, during both stable disease and exacerbations, regardless of ICS exposure. This variability is influenced by factors such as infection, corticosteroid exposure, and comorbid allergic disease [40, 41], and appears to be more pronounced during stable phases than during acute events, raising concerns about the reliability of single measurements for guiding long-term therapy [40]. For this reason, it is advisable to obtain two to three BEC measurements before starting a treatment that includes ICS. It is also important to interpret blood eosinophils with caution as a surrogate for tissue-level inflammation. Eosinophilic inflammation in COPD is spatially heterogeneous within the lung with focal microenvironments enriched in eosinophils rather than diffuse infiltration [42].

GOLD 2026 reiterates that patients with clinical or physiological features suggestive of asthma should be managed according to principles focused on asthma treatment, with ICS-containing regimens being prioritized. Conversely, individuals with equivocal spirometric abnormalities or a substantial burden of comorbidities should not be overtreated solely based on categorical classification. Patient preferences, inhaler technique, accessibility, and anticipated adherence must remain central to therapeutic decision making.

Together, the GOLD 2026 recommendations for ABE classification and initial therapy mark a significant shift from the traditional ‘bronchodilator first and escalate slowly’ approach. By recognizing patients as high risk after a single exacerbation, promoting dual bronchodilation as the standard for symptomatic or exacerbation-prone populations, and using eosinophils to guide early identification of corticosteroid-responsive disease, the updated framework encourages an earlier intervention aimed at suppressing disease activity and preventing recurrent exacerbations, key factors in long-term outcomes. In clinical practice, the ABE group should not be viewed as a rigid label, but rather as a structured entry point for personalized shared decision making regarding the most suitable initial inhaled regimen.

Pharmacologic Intensification

Of particular significance is the fact that GOLD 2026 warns against automatic pharmacological escalation beyond dual long-acting bronchodilation. Persistent dyspnea should prompt a reassessment of inhaler choice and technique, drug deposition, eligibility for pulmonary rehabilitation, and any contributing comorbidities, rather than automatic treatment intensification. Cardiovascular disease, anxiety, depression, and metabolic disorders are explicitly recognized as treatable conditions and should be actively addressed within integrated care pathways [43]. This approach has been shown to improve disease control and patient-centered outcomes.

Compared with the 2025 GOLD report, the 2026 update substantially repositions pharmacological escalation beyond dual bronchodilation (Fig. 2). Single-inhaler triple therapy (ICS/LABA/LAMA) is now more prominently integrated into the ABE context and biomarker-guided decision-making processes. GOLD 2026 designates triple therapy as the preferred next step for Group E patients who continue to experience exacerbations despite optimized LABA/LAMA treatment, particularly when BECs are elevated. This recommendation is supported by large phase III trials (IMPACT, ETHOS, TRINITY, and TRIBUTE studies), which collectively suggest a class effect in reducing exacerbations and improving lung function in selected populations. In the 2026 report, the threshold for initiating triple therapy is lower and more explicitly biomarker driven, reflecting an emphasis on earlier intervention in high-risk patients. Several criticisms of triple therapy warrant consideration. In pivotal trials such as IMPACT and ETHOS, more than 70% of participants had previously been exposed to ICS, and up to 40% were already receiving triple therapy upon enrollment, raising concern that ICS withdrawal in comparator arms may have inflated apparent treatment benefits [44]. Post hoc analyses and systematic reviews further suggest that triple therapy is most beneficial for patients with elevated BECs and that ICS withdrawal has the most detrimental effect on this same subgroup [45, 46]. Interpretation is complicated by the limited stratification based on prior ICS use, as well as by the heterogeneity of the trial populations, which predominantly included patients with severe airflow limitation and frequent exacerbations. Consequently, the generalizability to patients with milder disease or infrequent exacerbations, where even one moderate event per year is often considered low risk, remains uncertain [47]. Furthermore, improvements in lung function and symptoms often fall below the established minimal clinically important difference threshold [45].

Cost effectiveness is an additional limitation. Health economic analyses from the UK, Canada and Spain suggest that triple therapy provides only a modest additional benefit compared to optimized dual bronchodilation for patients with infrequent exacerbations [48–50]. The cost per quality-adjusted life-year often exceeds the generally accepted threshold for willingness to pay [51, 52]. These findings reflect small absolute reductions in exacerbation rates combined with an increased risk of adverse events.

Concerns about the risk of ICS-related pneumonia further complicate treatment decisions. Evidence supports a dose-dependent relationship, whereby higher doses of ICSs confer a greater risk [53], particularly in patients with chronic airway infection [54]. Randomized trials often rely on adverse-event reporting rather than standardized diagnostic criteria, which can lead to underestimation of pneumonia incidence [55]. Conversely, observational studies, although more reliant on radiographic confirmation, are susceptible to confounding by indication, as sicker patients are more often prescribed ICSs [56]. Recent population-based analyses also highlight the limited external validity of trial data. They note that study populations tend to be younger and healthier than real-world COPD cohorts. This can distort the observed risk-benefit profile of ICSs [57].

GOLD 2026 introduces a dedicated ‘legacy’ pathway to address the significant number of patients still on LABA/ICS regimens initiated before eosinophil-guided therapy became standard practice. The report recommends reassessing the exacerbation history and prior response to ICSs. If there have been no exacerbations or ongoing indications, switching to a LABA/LAMA regimen should be considered to minimize unnecessary ICS exposure. For patients who are well controlled, with no significant symptoms or exacerbations, continuation of LABA/ICS combinations remains an option, although regular reassessment of symptoms, exacerbation history, and adverse effects is advised. If exacerbations occur, escalation to triple therapy (LABA+LAMA+ICS) is recommended when BEC is ≥100 cells/μL, whereas for BEC <100 cells/µL, a switch to LABA+LAMA is generally preferred. Nevertheless, a BEC below 100 cells/μL should not be regarded as a sole indication to avoid ICSs in patients with COPD. Decisions regarding ICS use should always integrate the patient’s exacerbation history, disease severity, and overall clinical context, rather than relying exclusively on BEC [58]. For instance, patients with a history of frequent or severe exacerbations may still warrant ICS therapy despite low eosinophil counts, whereas those with prolonged stability and low counts may be appropriate candidates for ICS withdrawal [59].

A key development in the GOLD 2026 report is the clearer integration of biologic therapies into the escalation algorithm. The report positions dupilumab, a biologic that inhibits interleukin (IL)-4/IL-13 signaling, as an additional treatment option for patients with COPD, chronic bronchitis, and BECs of at least 300 cells/μL who remain uncontrolled on triple therapy. This recommendation represents a shift from the more cautious approach in the GOLD 2025 report and is supported by the results of the BOREAS and NOTUS trials and subsequent analyses. These studies demonstrated that adding dupilumab to triple therapy reduced moderate-to-severe exacerbations by 30–34% and improved FEV1 in patients with eosinophilic COPD and chronic bronchitis. As highlighted in the GOLD 2026 report table summarizing evidence for biologic therapies in COPD, mepolizumab (anti-interleukin-5) demonstrated lower efficacy. Across the METREX, METREO, and MATINEE trials, mepolizumab reduced exacerbation rates by 18–21%. However, it showed no significant improvements in lung function, symptoms, or quality of life compared to placebo. GOLD 2026 emphasizes that mepolizumab should be considered for patients with a documented BEC of at least 300 cells/μL and does not restrict its use to those with chronic bronchitis only. A structured multi-criteria decision analysis comparing biologics in COPD found that dupilumab demonstrated the most consistent efficacy in reducing exacerbations and improving lung function in biomarker-enriched populations, while mepolizumab showed moderate efficacy limited to exacerbation reduction [60]. Indirect comparisons further suggest that dupilumab may offer a shorter time to prevent one exacerbation and greater improvements in symptoms and lung function, particularly in patients with elevated FeNO, although the magnitude of these secondary benefits did not reach minimal clinically important differences [61].

GOLD 2026 also takes a more sceptical position on biologics in general, acknowledging that most targeted inflammatory pathways in COPD have failed to provide a clinically significant benefit. This is likely because of the heterogeneity of COPD inflammation and the risk of extrapolating information from asthma biology. Outstanding concerns include cost effectiveness, response durability, optimal biomarker thresholds, and generalizability. In particular, the predominantly white populations enrolled in dupilumab trials raise questions about the applicability of the results to populations with greater ethnic heterogeneity [62]. There is also a key criticism of the role of mepolizumab in treating eosinophilic COPD [63]. Mepolizumab reduces the rates of moderate and severe exacerbations, but it does not consistently improve lung function, symptoms, or health-related quality of life. This discrepancy is a main limitation. Mechanistically, IL-5 blockade alone may be insufficient in COPD because of inflammatory redundancy [64] and the persistence of tissue-resident eosinophils, which are maintained through IL-5-independent survival pathways [65]. However, it remains challenging to determine whether one of these two biologics is superior to the other in the absence of a direct comparative trial. Such a study would also be valuable in identifying subgroups of patients with COPD who may respond preferentially to dupilumab versus mepolizumab, and vice versa.

Finally, the GOLD 2026 update revises its position on non-biologic emerging therapies. Ensifentrine, a nebulized dual phosphodiesterase 3/4 inhibitor, showed improvements in FEV1, dyspnea, and exacerbation rates in the ENHANCE trials. While GOLD 2025 acknowledged these findings without taking a clear position, the GOLD 2026 update identifies ensifentrine as an adjunct option for patients with symptoms that are not adequately controlled by monotherapy or dual bronchodilation, particularly as a strategy to delay the initiation of ICS treatment. However, GOLD remains cautious, noting that the effect sizes are smaller than those observed with triple therapy and that active-comparator trials are absent.

Pharmacologic De-escalation

Both the 2025 and 2026 GOLD reports endorse pharmacologic de-escalation, most commonly involving ICSs, when the anticipated risks outweigh the ongoing benefits. The 2026 update places greater emphasis on BECs as a key determinant of relapse risk and explicitly discourages the withdrawal of ICSs in patients with BECs of 300 cells/µL or more. Although this approach is clinically intuitive, it only partially accounts for the biological variability and context dependence of eosinophil measurements, which may complicate interpretation and lead to patient misclassification.

Overall, ICS discontinuation is generally considered safe for patients at low risk of exacerbations who remain on long-acting bronchodilators. However, the optimal timing and criteria for de-escalation are still unclear, and robust clinical trial evidence supporting step-down strategies, particularly those involving bronchodilators or other drug classes, is limited. Observational data further suggest that inappropriate or premature de-escalation may result in adverse outcomes, including an increased risk of exacerbation, especially in patients with recent clinical instability or prior hospitalization [66, 67].

Non-pharmacologic Management

GOLD 2026 substantially strengthens the role of non-pharmacologic interventions, elevating them to foundational components of disease modification rather than supplementary measures. Unlike the GOLD 2025, which merely acknowledged these strategies without operationalizing them, the 2026 report explicitly positions smoking cessation, vaccination, structured patient education, self-management support, and regular physical activity as core interventions to be offered to virtually all patients with COPD. These measures are explicitly positioned as prerequisites for classifying a patient as ‘pharmacologically optimized,’ recognizing that poor outcomes often reflect deficiencies in behavioral and supportive care rather than insufficient intensification of inhaled therapy.

Pulmonary rehabilitation has been given much greater prominence. Whereas the GOLD 2025 report primarily recommended pulmonary rehabilitation for patients who had been hospitalized or who were highly symptomatic, the 2026 update expands eligibility to include all individuals with exertional dyspnea, deconditioning, or recent exacerbations, irrespective of spirometric severity. This shift is supported by the ATS review of 82 randomized controlled trials involving patients with varying degrees of airflow limitation [68]. A recent meta-analysis further supports the early initiation of pulmonary rehabilitation following hospitalization, demonstrating significant reductions in readmissions and clinically meaningful improvements in exercise capacity, health-related quality of life, and dyspnea, without an increase in adverse events [13].

GOLD 2026 acknowledges the growing body of evidence supporting home-based and telerehabilitation programs. When delivered with comparable intensity and supervision, these programs achieve outcomes similar to center-based rehabilitation in terms of functional capacity, quality of life, and dyspnea, with a higher completion rate. However, acknowledging heterogeneity in program design, patient selection, and outcome durability, the report continues to regard supervised center-based rehabilitation as the preferred standard, positioning remote modalities as alternatives for patients unable to access in-person care [68, 69].

There are still persistent limitations, with suboptimal referral, uptake, and completion of pulmonary rehabilitation owing to limited provider awareness, logistical barriers, and inadequate funding contributing to inequities in access [70]. Although telerehabilitation alleviates some of these challenges, concerns remain about protocol standardization, patient selection, and the long-term benefits. Furthermore, the evidence base, which is predominantly derived from stable COPD populations, remains limited about post-exacerbation care, comorbid conditions, and long-term outcomes beyond program completion.

For selected patients, the GOLD 2026 report maintains the established recommendations for long-term oxygen therapy and home NIV. Long-term oxygen therapy is still only recommended for individuals with severe resting hypoxemia, in line with historical survival trials. Home NIV, however, is now also recommended for chronic hypercapnia, particularly following life-threatening exacerbations. This is based on emerging evidence showing that high-intensity strategies applied by experienced teams can reduce mortality and rehospitalization rates.

However, some criticisms must be considered. Long-term oxygen therapy recommendations for patients with persistent severe resting hypoxemia have been criticised for relying on historical trial criteria and for failing to demonstrate benefit in cases of moderate resting or exertional desaturation. A 2022 systematic review found that long-term oxygen therapy does not improve survival, reduce hospitalizations, or provide sustained improvements in quality of life or functional status in these populations [71]. Consequently, the GOLD 2026 criteria may exclude symptomatic patients who experience substantial morbidity despite not meeting strict thresholds. Other concerns include the lack of consensus on the most appropriate oxygen dosage. Recent evidence calls into question the necessity of more than 15 hours per day and highlights the importance of personalized strategies [72]. Furthermore, there is uncertainty regarding the ideal duration of therapy beyond the first year.

Similarly, critiques of home NIV emphasize unresolved issues. There is no consensus on optimal patient selection, thresholds for persistent hypercapnia, the timing of post-exacerbation care, or the prognostic relevance of prior hospitalizations [73]. Evidence for improvements in quality of life is limited and is often derived from small or heterogeneous studies [74]. Implementation is further complicated by technical variability: ventilator settings, pressure targets, modes, and titration protocols lack standardization, although higher inspiratory pressures and back-up rates may be more effective [75, 76]. Real-world adherence is often suboptimal, which compromises the treatment’s overall effectiveness.

For patients with emphysema, lung-volume-reduction procedures, whether surgical or bronchoscopic, should only be performed at specialized centers that provide expert phenotyping, a multi-disciplinary review, and a long-term follow-up. However, this approach presents logistical barriers for rural populations and imposes financial burdens on marginalized groups, potentially limiting their ability to achieve meaningful symptomatic or functional benefits, particularly in the context of newer bronchoscopic techniques that have expanded the therapeutic options [77]. Data from registries and clinical trials indicate that a substantial proportion of referred patients are ultimately ineligible, underscoring the need for more flexible care models that balance safety with accessibility [78].

Exacerbations

GOLD 2026 adopts the Rome proposal to classify COPD exacerbations as mild, moderate, or severe based on clinical features at presentation (Table 2) rather than subsequent treatment or healthcare utilization. This approach integrates symptom worsening, vital sign abnormalities, and required level of care, aligning management with severity (Table 3). Mild exacerbations are managed by increasing short-acting bronchodilator treatment. Moderate exacerbations additionally require a short (5-day) course of systemic corticosteroids alongside this treatment, with antibiotics reserved for patients with purulent sputum, documented prior bacterial infection, or those needing ventilatory support. Severe exacerbations necessitate hospital evaluation, controlled oxygen therapy for hypoxemia, and early NIV for acute or acute-on-chronic hypercapnic respiratory failure guided by arterial blood gas monitoring. Despite its pragmatism, this classification prioritizes simplicity over biological and prognostic granularity. Exacerbations are immunologically and microbiologically heterogeneous, with distinct endotypes (eosinophilic, bacterial, viral, and pauci-inflammatory) that are not captured by symptom- or resource-based schemes [79], thereby limiting opportunities for personalized therapy and accurate risk stratification [80]. Clinical thresholds may underestimate severity in patients with comorbidities or atypical presentations, failing to capture subclinical or unreported exacerbations that are prognostically relevant. Furthermore, management recommendations remain largely unchanged and only partially incorporate emerging evidence supporting the use of biomarkers to guide treatment decisions, particularly regarding corticosteroid and antibiotic use [81].

The GOLD 2026 update highlights the importance of structured discharge bundles, including pulmonary rehabilitation referral, early follow-up within 1 month, and reassessment at 12–16 weeks, to reduce readmissions and optimize maintenance therapy. Triple therapy is recommended following severe exacerbations. However, the routine use of post-exacerbation spirometry is limited by practical constraints and a lack of evidence demonstrating a clear outcome benefit. In addition, the risk-benefit profile of triple therapy remains uncertain in patients with low BECs or a lower risk of exacerbations, underscoring the need for individualized decisions that balance expected benefits against the risk of pneumonia and cost.

Artificial Intelligence and Emerging Technologies

The GOLD 2026 strategy report is a landmark in COPD management, as it is the first to include a dedicated chapter on AI and emerging digital technologies. The report acknowledges the increasing incorporation of AI into standard COPD care and emphasizes its capacity to minimize underdiagnosis, improve diagnostic precision, and refine phenotyping via a sophisticated analysis of electronic health records, spirometry, and imaging data. It also discusses AI-based models for risk stratification, including exacerbation prediction, and the integration of multi-modal clinical, imaging, and laboratory data to support more personalized management.

The report also describes AI applications for remote monitoring and self-management. These include smart inhalers, wearable sensors, tele-rehabilitation platforms, and AI-supported patient interfaces, such as chatbots. Other potential benefits consist of the improved integration of multi-morbidity data within electronic health records, support for care that aligns with guidelines, and the reduction of risks associated with polypharmacy. The report also presents innovative concepts, such as the automated transcription and summarization of clinical encounters (‘keyboard zero’), to enhance clinician-patient interaction.

Despite these prospects, GOLD 2026 takes a deliberately cautious approach. It emphasizes that most AI applications for COPD are still in the early stages of development and should not be implemented in routine practice without robust validation. Key limitations include data fragmentation, a lack of interoperability, limited external generalizability, legal and ethical concerns, and the risk of algorithmic bias, particularly in underrepresented populations. Current models often fail to adequately account for multi-morbidity and contextual factors, which limits their prognostic and clinical utility [82].

Future research should prioritize the development of large, standardized, and diverse datasets, as well as models capable of capturing multi-morbidity interactions to enable meaningful clinical translation. Efforts should also focus on multi-modal integration of clinical, imaging, environmental, and psychosocial data, coupled with rigorous external validation, to enhance the reliability, fairness, and generalizability of AI tools in COPD care. Overcoming these challenges is crucial for advancing AI from a conceptual promise to a reliable, personalized, and clinically actionable tool for COPD management.

Conclusions

The GOLD 2026 update is a significant advancement in COPD management that emphasizes a multi-dimensional assessment, early intervention, and alignment with precision-medicine principles (Fig. 3). By introducing disease activity as a therapeutic target, GOLD 2026 moves beyond a predominantly spirometry-centric approach, encouraging clinicians to address modifiable biological processes that drive symptoms, exacerbations, and disease progression. Currently, BEC is the only validated biomarker to guide ICS use, particularly in patients with frequent exacerbations or T2 inflammation. However, its biological variability and context dependence limit precision. Other candidate biomarkers, such as C-reactive protein, fibrinogen, surfactant protein D, and club cell secretory protein, show prognostic associations, but they lack sufficient validation for routine clinical application. Thus, clinical indicators, such as exacerbation frequency, symptom worsening, and accelerated FEV1 decline, remain the most reliable measures of disease activity. The revised ABE classification, with a lower threshold for Group E, facilitates the earlier identification of high-risk patients. This classification supports targeted pharmacologic escalation, particularly with dual bronchodilation and biomarker-guided triple therapy. In parallel, non-pharmacologic strategies, such as pulmonary rehabilitation, vaccination, smoking cessation, and structured post-exacerbation care, have been elevated to core components of disease modification. These strategies have a substantial impact on morbidity, readmissions, and survival. The expanded focus on multi-morbidity and frailty further reinforces the relevance of clinical aspects, promoting a more holistic, patient-centered approach to care.

Although emerging digital technologies and AI are acknowledged as valuable adjuncts for case finding, phenotyping, risk stratification, and remote monitoring, they are appropriately positioned as supplements to clinical judgment rather than substitutes. Nevertheless, important uncertainties remain. The lack of fully validated biomarkers and longitudinal outcomes complicates the operationalization of disease activity and implementation. The lower exacerbation threshold may increase the risk of overtreatment in patients with infrequent events, and BECs are limited by biological variability. Biologic therapies, particularly dupilumab, show promise for treating eosinophilic COPD, but they are limited by cost, a lack of generalizability, and insufficient long-term evidence. Non-pharmacologic interventions face persistent barriers in referral, uptake, and equity, while advanced interventions such as long-term oxygen, home NIV, and lung-volume-reduction procedures remain restricted by eligibility criteria and logistical complexity.

Overall, the GOLD 2026 update presents a modern and comprehensive approach that focuses on early diagnosis, structured evaluation, personalized treatment, and integrated care. While these innovations offer strong potential to improve outcomes, they must be applied carefully, adapted to specific contexts, and supported by ongoing validation to be practical in real-world care.
