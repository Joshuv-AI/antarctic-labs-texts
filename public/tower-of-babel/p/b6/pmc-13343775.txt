Pediatric acute liver failure in a child with arsenic toxicity and underlying rotor syndrome
Authors: Shivangi Tetarbe, Dhruv Gandhi, Lakshmi Shobhavat, Ira Shah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPediatric acute liver failure can result from infections, metabolic disorders, autoimmune diseases, toxins, or malignancies. It is crucial to consider herbal medicines as an etiological factor in such cases. We report a 7-year-old boy presenting with PALF from arsenic toxicity after consuming herbal medicine. He presented with jaundice, fever, vomiting, and lethargy, with hepatomegaly and signs of raised intracranial tension on examination. He had taken herbal medicine for the jaundice. His toxicology screen revealed high levels of arsenic and lead in serum and urine. Whole exome sequencing incidentally identified pathogenic mutations of the SLCO1B1 and SLCO1B3 genes, suggestive of Rotor syndrome (RS). Despite intensive care, including continuous renal replacement therapy and plasmapheresis, he died from multiorgan dysfunction and sepsis. This case shows the risks of unregulated herbal formulations containing toxins and explores the potential interaction between RS and arsenic toxicity.Keywords: herbal medicine, hepatotoxicity, arsenic exposure, toxin-induced liver injury, liver toxicity

Introduction

Pediatric Acute Liver Failure (PALF) has several causes, including infective, metabolic, autoimmune, malignant, and toxic [1]. Exposure to toxic levels of trace elements like arsenic, copper, iron, and lithium can induce hepatotoxicity and potentially precipitate PALF [2]. Herbal medicine may be beneficial if used appropriately; however, it carries potential risks from incorrect formulations and unregulated ingredients. Certain herbal formulations contain heavy metals that, if consumed excessively or without proper supervision, may result in liver damage [3]. We present a child with PALF from arsenic toxicity with underlying Rotor syndrome (RS).

Case report

A 7-year-2-month-old previously healthy boy presented with vomiting for 8 days, fever and jaundice for 5 days, and irritability and drowsiness for 1 day. He had intentionally consumed herbal medicine indicated for jaundice on the day of onset of jaundice. The name and formulation of this preparation is unknown. On examination, height was 121 cm (50–75th percentile as per Indian Academy of Pediatrics (IAP)) and weight was 20 kg (25–50th percentile as per IAP). He had bradycardia (58 beats/min), hypertension (152/96 mmHg) and was lethargic (Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS): 11/15). On per-abdominal examination, hepatomegaly was present. There were no focal neurological deficits; however, raised intracranial tension signs were present. Other general and systemic examinations were normal. Investigations are depicted in Table 1. Hepatitis A/Hepatitis B/Hepatitis C/Hepatitis E/HIV Elisa were negative. Adenoviral IgG/IgM, Epstein Barr Virus polymerase chain reaction (PCR), and Cytomegalovirus PCR/IgM were negative. Blood and urine cultures did not grow any organisms. Anti-nuclear antibody by immunofluorescence was negative, anti-smooth muscle antibody was positive (1:20), direct Coomb’s test was weakly positive, anti-liver kidney muscle antibody was negative, serum IgG was 1006.44 mg/dl (normal: 504–1560 mg/dl) serum ceruloplasmin was 31.21 mg/dl, and 24-h urinary copper was 236.12mcg/day. Biochemical analysis of herbal medicine found elevated arsenic (5.82 ppm; normal:0.01 ppm) and lead (2.97 ppm: normal:0.1 ppm) content. Serum arsenic and lead levels were 1.24 ppm (normal:0.05 ppm) and 0.44 ppm (normal:0.3 ppm), respectively. Serial urinary arsenic and lead levels post continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) are depicted in Table 2. He was shifted to the pediatric intensive care unit and was started on intravenous (IV) fluids with 3% sodium chloride, injectable vitamin K, N-acetylcysteine infusion (100 mg/kg/day), lactulose, and rifaximin (30 mg/kg/day). Oral sodium benzoate via nasogastric tube (250 mg/kg/day) for hyperammonemia and IV antibiotics were started. He was given fresh frozen plasma, and CRRT was started on day 2 of hospitalization in view of persistent hyperammonaemia. On day 2, due to GCS decreasing to 6, he was intubated and positive pressure ventilation was started. Serial liver function tests are depicted in Table 1. He was diagnosed with PALF and was listed for urgent liver transplantation. On day 3, he required inotrope (adrenaline and noradrenaline) infusions. He had recurrent hypoglycemia requiring glucose infusion (12.5 mg/kg/min). On day 6, he developed pulseless ventricular fibrillation and received cardiopulmonary resuscitation. On day 6, CRRT was stopped, and plasmapheresis along with sustained low-efficiency dialysis (SLED) was initiated in view of coagulopathy and nasal bleeding from high arsenic levels, not responding to CRRT, and due to monetary constraints. Penicillamine was started but was stopped in 24 h due to renal failure. On Day 8, he had spontaneous endotracheal and nasogastric tube bleeding. Brain magnetic resonance imaging was normal, and abdominal computerized tomography with triple-phase angiogram was suggestive of acute liver parenchymal disease with mild ascites. He became anuric by day 11. He eventually succumbed to gram-negative sepsis and multiorgan dysfunction on day 22. Whole exome sequencing with mitochondrial genome sequencing was sent on day 2. A week after the child passed away, the results arrived, which showed 2 contiguous heterozygous deletions on chromosome 12, [chr12:g.(?_20854963)_(21048907_?)del] and [chr12:g.(21131237_21141513)_(21179021_21196945)del], encompassing multiple genes, including SLCO1B3 and SLCO1B1, respectively, pathogenic mutations suggestive of Rotor-type hyperbilirubinemia.

Discussion

Several heavy metals have been found in herbal medications including lead, arsenic, mercury, cadmium, copper, antimony, thallium, and tin [4]. In Ayurveda, certain medications are derived from minerals and undergo a thorough purification process to remove toxicity before being used [5]. While these medications are generally safe, the absence of strict quality control has led to an increasing number of cases of toxicity, particularly from lead, mercury, and arsenic [5]. Saper et al. [6] found that one-fifth of Ayurvedic medicines contained lead, mercury, and arsenic levels exceeding permissible limits. Our patient was prescribed medicines containing arsenic and lead that had higher than recommended levels [5, 6].

Arsenic is also classified as a toxin in Ayurveda, and arsenicosis can be diagnosed by measuring total arsenic levels in serum or tissues using atomic absorption spectrometry, as well as by assessing high urinary arsenic levels [7]. In our patient, urine arsenic levels were elevated. Arsenic exposure is commonly through herbicides, pesticides, herbal medications, contaminated supplements, and drinking water [8]. Acute arsenic toxicity typically causes gastrointestinal symptoms like nausea, vomiting, cholera-like diarrhea, and severe abdominal pain, followed by dehydration and hypotension. Hepatotoxicity may develop within 24–48 h, resembling acute iron toxicity, with hepatocyte necrosis and PALF, with brisk recovery in most cases. Arsenic hepatotoxicity is well-documented, with over 90% of chronic arsenic toxicity patients showing non-cirrhotic portal fibrosis on liver biopsy [5]. The mechanism of arsenic hepatotoxicity is multifactorial, although it remains unclear [9]. In our patient, increased blood lead levels may have played a role in his illness; however, these levels were relatively mild in comparison to his arsenic exposure, and his clinical symptoms were less aligned with lead toxicity. For the ingested herb, the RUCAM score in our case was 7 [10].

Our patient was incidentally found to have RS, a self-limited condition causing hyperbilirubinemia, typically seen in neonates or young children. It is inherited in an autosomal recessive digenic manner, involving mutations in SLCO1B1/OATP1B1 and SLCO1B3/OATP1B3, resulting from the concomitant loss of both proteins. Even one functional protein prevents RS [11]. The condition causes mild intermittent jaundice due to impaired reuptake of conjugated bilirubin by hepatocytes, with typical findings of conjugated hyperbilirubinemia (2-5 mg/dl), elevated urine bilirubin, normal liver enzymes, no hemolysis, and raised urine coproporphyrin levels. Liver histology is normal. RS has a good prognosis, with no progression to liver failure [12]. While RS does affect bilirubin transport, we could not find any sources in the literature that suggest it may also impair arsenic transport and excretion and thereby, possibly contribute to arsenic hepatotoxicity. Thus, in our patient, whether associated RS worsened the arsenic-induced liver failure remains debatable.
