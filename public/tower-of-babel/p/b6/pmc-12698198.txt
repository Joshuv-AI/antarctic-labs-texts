Anishinaabe healthy brain aging: traditional knowledge teachings represented in works of art
Authors: Kristen Jacklin, Melissa Blind, Karen Pitawanakwat, Antonio Paniagua Guzman, Dana Ketcher, Leland Bell, Indira Galeeva, Wayne Warry, Ulla Kriebernegg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDementia is a prevalent and growing concern among Indigenous communities. Research on the lived experience, culture, and context of the dementia journey has improved our understanding of Indigenous Peoples’ experiences. This research study elucidates the deep understanding of healthy brain aging from interviews with seven Anishinaabe/Ojibwe Traditional Knowledge Sharers from Manitoulin Island in Ontario, Canada. Using a community-based participatory research (CBPR) approach and Indigenous methodologies, researchers worked with a Community Advisory Council (CAC) to develop the study. Interviews with Traditional Knowledge Sharers were recorded, transcribed, and shared with a local Anishinaabe/Ojibwe artist for his interpretation and artistic representation of the key healthy brain aging teaching that arose from the interview. The artist developed six paintings based on these teachings for Anishinaabe/Ojibwe people, titled: Perseverance, Anishinaabe Cognition, Presence, Healing Step, Benevolence, and Linkages. A seventh painting was also developed, with the shared teaching of Bebaminojmat—one who goes around and shares their healing gifts. We describe these teachings and the cross-cutting themes that arose from the post-analysis. This study contributes to the field of aging by revealing cultural and philosophical concepts related to healthy brain aging specifically for Anishinaabe/Ojibwe people. The findings can be used to guide healthcare providers in supporting healthy brain aging in culturally meaningful ways and developing culturally safe health education interventions. Artistic analysis and representation proved to be an effective vehicle for exploring cultural values central to the development of programs and education aimed at addressing existing brain health disparities.Keywords: Aging, Art-based methods, CBPR, Dementia, Indigenous

Alzheimer’s disease and Alzheimer’s disease–related dementias (AD/ADRD) are a prevalent and growing concern among Indigenous communities worldwide (Furlano et al., 2025; Suchy-Dicey et al., 2024). Compared to other racial and ethnic groups, Indigenous people have been disproportionately impacted by AD/ADRD due to colonization. Colonization has manifested in a myriad of ways, including disempowerment over health and health care practices (Warry, 1998), exposure to culturally unsafe and thus ineffective care models (Hulko et al., 2025), structural inequalities that result in poorer access to services, increased levels of discrimination and racism (Roh et al., 2024), and forced relocation limiting opportunities to maintain traditional diets, medicine, and lifeways. These manifestations serve as barriers to wellness and healthcare access, contributing to the risk of AD/ADRD (Sehar et al., 2023). Recently, efforts have begun to develop culturally appropriate resources to support Indigenous older adults living with dementia, their families, and caregivers. This includes Indigenous specific dementia fact sheets, clinical intake tools, cultural safety practice frameworks, and Indigenous cognitive screening tools (Chakanyuka et al., 2022; Jacklin et al., 2020, 2024; Webkamigad et al., 2020). Using community-based participatory research (CBPR) and guided by our Community Advisory Council (CAC), we undertook a study aimed at obtaining a deep understanding of traditional Indigenous knowledge concerning healthy brain aging and dementia that the community could use to provide culturally meaningful and safe information to improve healthy brain aging education.

Community-based participatory methods and Indigenous populations

Scholars and communities have used community-based participatory research (CBPR) to facilitate relationship development between academic and nonacademic partners, prioritize local knowledge exchange and collaboration to address inequalities, build community capacity, and empower communities to implement research findings (Hayward et al., 2021; Jacklin & Kinoshameg, 2008; Wallerstein & Duran, 2006; Warry, 1998). CBPR has proven to be effective in Indigenous health research because it seeks to eliminate power imbalances and uplift local knowledge (Christopher et al., 2011; Jacklin & Kinoshameg, 2008; LaVeaux & Christopher, 2009). The combination of CBPR with Indigenous methodologies is especially powerful. Indigenous methodologies are defined as relational and grounded in Indigenous epistemology, while allowing for the use of familiar methods, such as interviews and focus groups (Kovach, 2021), in ways that are appropriate and relevant for Indigenous peoples and that prioritize Indigenous knowledge (Hayward et al., 2021; Jacklin et al., 2020; LaVeaux & ­Christopher, 2009).

In this study, we interviewed Traditional Knowledge Sharers about their knowledge and teachings on healthy brain aging. Indigenous teachings are traditional ways of knowing, being, and behaving that are actively practiced, taught (sometimes indirectly, through storytelling), or learned; these can vary between Nations or cultures. Teachings can also be symbolically represented through objects, such as carvings, beadwork, or, in this case, paintings (Absolon, 2020; Bird, 2014). In this study, the Traditional Knowledge Sharers teachings would come to be interpreted and shared through visual art (paintings)(Hayward et al., 2021; Jacklin et al., 2020; Jacklin & Kinoshameg, 2008; LaVeaux & Christopher, 2009; ­Wallerstein & Duran, 2006).

Dementia and the arts

As an essential part of cultural, traditional, and creative expression, art has been used as a vehicle to study diverse health issues and as a means for interventions in diverse populations (Fraser & al Sayah, 2011). Including artistic practices like poetry, storytelling, writing, and painting which have enhanced the impact of health programs (Beard, 2012). Evidence suggests that art-based interventions improve health outcomes across age ranges and conditions (Emblad & Mukaetova-Ladinska, 2021; Kovach, 2021; Pienaar & Reynolds, 2015).

There have been several important programs and studies that have highlighted the benefits of different art therapies for persons living with dementia. Poetry and spoken word foster self-expression, cognitive performance, and emotional wellness (Paniagua Guzman et al., 2025; Vigliotti et al., 2019); performing arts and dancing are reported to improve symptoms of depression in those with mild cognitive impairment and dementia (Wang et al., 2021), as well as playfulness and sociability (Kontos et al., 2021); and music and singing can increase personal connection and bonding for both the person living with dementia and the caregiver (Bufalini et al., 2022). As a non-verbal art form, visual arts and painting have also been successfully used in interventions in dementia care with studies showing increased independence, quality of life, well-being, and decreased behavioral and psychosocial symptoms of dementia (Emblad & Mukaetova-Ladinska, 2021; González Cabañes et al., 2022). Additionally, visual art and painting-based interventions can benefit caregivers because they provide an opportunity to alleviate feelings of depletion, enhance resilience, restore coping resources, and find a support network (Pienaar & Reynolds, 2015). Important to our study, art-based exhibits on dementia for non-symptomatic older adults proved to promote behavioral changes deemed important for dementia risk reduction (Chandler et al., 2024). While these examples offer just a glimpse into the arts and dementia, they demonstrate an important connection between artistic expression or exposure to the arts and improved social and behavioral outcomes for persons living with dementia and caregivers.

Current study

The study presented here is not an art-based intervention. Our research draws upon Indigenous Elders’ perspectives on healthy brain aging and the artistic expression of their teachings to create artwork that can support culturally safe interventions and education for dementia. Creative expression also enriches the CBPR process by involving community members as partners in discussions about community issues and solutions, fostering cultural identity and resilience while addressing health concerns (Gray et al., 2010; LaVeaux & Christopher, 2009).

In this study, visual art (paintings) emerged from our CBPR approach as an appropriate method and outcome to deeply understand, educate, and curate Anishinaabe/Ojibwe teachings on healthy brain aging. Traditional and Elder knowledge is highly valued and honored in Indigenous cultures, deeply shaping community life, identity, and individual performance (Bird, 2014). Traditional Knowledge Sharers can be referred to in different ways: Traditional Knowledge Keepers, Traditional Knowledge Holders, Elders, and more. Here, we use Traditional Knowledge Sharers as it most closely aligns with what was revealed in this study and is the preference of our Community Advisory Council (CAC). These are highly respected community members who carry and share knowledge with others (Fixico, 2003). Given that wisdom is closely tied to lived experience, Traditional Knowledge Sharers tend to be older adults with deep specialized knowledge of the community’s culture, values, and history. However, in our experience, Elders, Knowledge Keepers, Carriers, and Sharers may be identified at earlier ages as well.

Respecting that there is a distinct Anishinaabe/Ojibwe epistemology regarding health and well-being, we sought the wisdom of recognized Anishinaabe/Ojibwe Traditional Knowledge Sharers to inform a community-based study on the cultural understandings and teachings related to healthy brain aging. Drawing on interviews with Traditional Knowledge Sharers, this article presents cultural teachings from Anishinaabe/Ojibwe Traditional Knowledge Sharers as analyzed and interpreted by local Anishinaabe/Ojibwe artist Leland Bell in ­collaboration with our research team and the CAC.

Methods

This project is part of an ethnographic CBPR study focused on ADRD that was conducted in four stages over 8 years. The overall project was collaboratively designed to study Anishinaabe/Ojibwe community members’ views of healthy brain aging to inform the development of culturally safe health information and interventions. This component of the project involved understanding traditional cultural knowledge about healthy brain aging by conducting interviews with Traditional Knowledge Sharers. The original research plan was to conduct interviews, which would be translated, transcribed, and analyzed thematically by the research team in a typical Western qualitative approach. The CAC requested that instead the analysis and representation of the Traditional Knowledge Sharers, interviews happen through an artistic process. They advocated for the interviews to be interpreted by a local artist and knowledge keeper fluent in Anishinaabemowin.

Phases of the project

Stage 1: formation of the community advisory council (CAC)

An overview of the phases of the project is presented in ­Figure 1. Prior to starting the research project, the research team, in collaboration with the community leadership, developed a CAC to oversee the research process and guide the team throughout the entire research endeavor. Typical of CBPR projects, the CAC played a central role in providing comments and feedback on the approach, identifying the project’s focus and direction, and ensuring that the research would benefit the community. CAC members were compensated $75 per meeting they attended.

Phases of the artistic process.

The research team was committed to Indigenous meth­odologies using a CBPR approach (Jacklin & Kinoshameg, 2008; Wallerstein & Duran, 2006) and Two-Eyed Seeing/Etuaptmumk principles (Martin, 2012). Two-Eyed Seeing/Etuaptmumk is a concept, first introduced by Mi’kmaw Elders Albert D. Marshall and Murdena Marshall, which encourages individuals to view the world through the strengths of both Western and Indigenous knowledge and worldviews.

Stage 2: interviews and data collection

Five individual open-ended interviews and one group interview (with two Elders) were conducted with seven Anishinaabe/Ojibwe Traditional Knowledge Sharers from Manidoo Mnising (Manitoulin Island) in Ontario, Canada. Participants were recruited by a community-based researcher (CBR), who is a community member and a part of the research team (K.P.). Interviews were conducted between October 2016 and September 2017. Five interviews occurred in the Traditional Knowledge Sharers’ homes and one at Laurentian University. All interviews were video and audio-recorded with participants’ consent. Interviews were conducted in the participants’ language of choice1 (Anishinaabemowin or English) and lasted between 60 and 90 min. Participants were given a gift of tobacco, an honorarium of $75, and the original artwork produced by the artist from their interview. Due to the exploratory nature of this study, interviews were based on two central questions:

We would like to get a deeper understanding of Anishinaabe/Ojibwe values, ways of knowing and living, particularly, any activities that would keep your mind healthy as you age.

We would like to get a deeper understanding of Anishinaabe/Ojibwe values, ways of knowing and living, particularly, any activities that would keep your mind healthy as you age.

Stage 3: conceptualization, artistic representation, and analysis

Prior to starting the data collection process, the CAC identified four well-known Anishinaabe/Ojibwe artists who would be suitable for the project. This list of candidates was given to a member of the study team (K.P.), who visited all the artists to learn more about their background, family, engagement with the community, and ideas on the development of this project. K.P. brought this information back to the CAC, who decided to invite Anishinaabe/Ojibwe artist Leland Bell to participate in the project. Bell was born in Wiikwemkoong Unceded Territory, Ontario, Canada, and his work has been nationally and internationally acknowledged for its unique style and its deep connection with fundamental elements of Anishinaabe/Ojibwe culture.

The first step in the analytic process was for the interviewers to take field notes. After each interview with the Traditional Knowledge Sharers, members of the research team (K.P., M.B., W.W.) completed interview summaries that outlined any issues that arose during the interview, the background of the Elder, and key themes discussed during the interview. The research team shared the interview summaries and a copy of the audio/video interview recordings, transcripts, and any other pictures or files related to the interview with Bell on a USB stick. At this stage, the research team took a distant approach to prevent interference with Bell’s artistic process.

As a second step, Bell listened to the interviews and received knowledge from the Traditional Knowledge Sharers as he listened; he took notes and made a list of what he viewed as the most important concepts and values being conveyed, i.e., teachings. Bell then used these lists to sketch out the composition of each painting, determine the symbolic elements to incorporate, and link them to key values related to healthy brain aging that the Traditional Knowledge Sharers highlighted. Based on his own analysis, understanding, and conceptualization of the interview data, Bell produced a total of six original paintings based on data from seven Traditional Knowledge Sharers interviews on 16″ × 20″ canvases with digital and canvas copies for presentation to the research team. The artist described his ­process as follows:

“I listened to tapes and I sat there in my studio. […] So I was sitting there listening to all of it and I listened to everyone as, as best that I can. […] Sometimes, I could see what people are talking about right away. Like I can see what they’re saying right away in my brain. And sometimes, I could see how I should approach it and what would be the best way for me to, uh, how to be able to understand it, what they’re talking about. I put aside all those other things like my judgments and all that. There’s no room for it when you are listening to things. You don’t want to be judging people when they’re, when you’re listening to their tapes or whatever, because I think most of the people that were on there, you know, what you want to do is focus in, on the thing, on their voices and on their language and why they’re saying certain things. And after a while, I could see like a theme going on with all of the presenters.”

As a third step, once the paintings were completed, Bell presented his findings at a CAC meeting that included the entire research team in August 2018. He discussed the messages in the interviews and explained how various aspects of the interviews and central teachings were incorporated into each painting. His presentation was video recorded for later review by the study team.

The fourth step in our process was for the study team and CAC to come to a consensus that Bell’s interpretations corresponded with the data. Understanding that this was an interpretive process, we did not seek to validate the interpretation in the usual sense. We sought general agreement that each interview’s teaching aligned with the paintings’ messages and symbols. This was accomplished by reviewing the fieldnotes and transcripts and re-watching the recording of Bell’s presentation. A consensus was reached that the teachings represented in the paintings aligned with the interviews. This served as a form of community-based validation.

Our final step was to identify a shared teaching. Following the completion of the initial phase of the project, the principal investigator (K.J.) suggested an additional visit with the CAC and Bell to discuss the idea of producing a seventh painting based on his analysis of the collective teachings from the interviews. The meeting with Bell took place in November 2019 and the final painting was created during the COVID pandemic on a 24″ × 40″ canvas. The research team conducted a final interview with Leland Bell to understand his process and analysis of the final painting.

Bell was compensated for the purchase and use of the paintings. This included the original painting, a digital copy, and nontransferable license to use the artwork for nonprofit research and educational activities. The original artwork was purchased for $4.70 per square inch, and $1000 for the licensing fee.

Stage 4: dissemination and knowledge exchange

In June 2024, after delays partially related to COVID, the study team (including the CAC and Bell) was able to hold a free, public exhibition of all the paintings on Manitoulin Island, ON, Canada. The exhibition included the Traditional Knowledge Sharers, their family members, community members, and members of the CAC. The exhibit received local media attention (Erskine, 2025; Garrick, 2024) and was well-received by community members and guests.

Stage 5: final analysis

The final analysis involved articulating the teachings for publication. To convey the results, we created summaries of Bell’s verbal explanations of the paintings. This was an iterative process of writing and reviewing with Bell and the CAC. Bell has reviewed and edited the presentation of the results. For this reason, part of the analysis is presented in a manner consistent with Anishinaabe/Ojibwe communication, intended to reinforce the importance of storytelling as an Indigenous method.

We begin by presenting a conceptual overview provided by the authors, along with quotes from Bell, to help the reader interpret the paintings. This is followed by an explanation of each painting, accompanied by a brief statement that highlights the painting’s connection to healthy brain aging. Following that, we present the results of our overall analysis of the stories to synthesize the key themes that arose from the Traditional Knowledge Sharers’ interviews and Bell’s representation of them, these include (1) connections, (2) colonialism and the resulting trauma, (3) resilience, (4) sharing knowledge, and (5) Elders’ knowledge bundles. This final analysis grounds the results in cross-cutting themes that can be translated into appropriate pathways to healthy brain aging for Indigenous peoples.

Results/findings

Our findings suggest that healthy brain aging for the Anishinaabe/Ojibwe is rooted in their traditional teachings and value systems. The Traditional Knowledge Sharers’ teachings strongly reinforce the detrimental impact of colonization on achieving healthy brain aging, increased rates of dementia, and the unique challenges faced by the Anishinaabe/Ojibwe. Yet, their teachings also provide a roadmap deeply rooted in culture that has fueled resilience and can be applied to living a healthy life that helps preserve cognition.

Leland Bell’s painting series: a brief overview of Anishinaabe/Ojibwe knowledge

According to Bell, Indigenous art is understood as “the ability to talk.” Some of the stories Bell heard were visualized immediately, while others took extra time to conceptualize the teaching. He carefully considered his rationale for sharing certain points of dialogue, as summarized below.The Anishinaabe/Ojibwe life journey starts with many helpers that provide purpose and assist a person in navigating life. Using inherent gifts enables an individual’s connection to everything in our universe. It is up to every person to grasp these opportunities to achieve a heightened level of our existence. This is made difficult by all of the challenges and mistakes we make in life that take energy away from us. This energy is needed to live the experiences intended for our life path. Even the final stage of life is considered a valuable time in our life path, as we maintain purpose and knowledge. Bell stated that,

“Anishinaabe have come through much history, yet we still stand strong. The knowledge we are gaining back is proof of the universal nature of it. From the connections of our spirit’s name and belonging here and now, to surviving all kinds of atrocities the dominant culture has imposed on us, Anishinaabe are still capable of connecting to their vision and using gifts helping one another to heal.”

Even the ancient ones who have gone on their spirit journey are still seen amongst us. Their role is the gift and sharing their thoughts on improving our lives when we believe in them. Colors are meaningful and are based on the four directions. Yellow (East) specifically shows where light comes from and all the knowledge and gifts that can be seen when the light shows them to us. The Anishinaabe/Ojibwe journey is full of learned experiences that are all connected, including knowledge we are only beginning to understand. It is possible to bring all knowledge together through connections and sharing. Bell reminds us that, when there is sharing, “think of what we can accomplish during our physical journey.”

Throughout his artistic career, Bell has developed his own style and visual identity. One of the most important characteristics of his work is the use and combinations of vivid colors close to those found in nature. In this regard he states: “I show the strength of our people by using color […] and the idea that we have a strong heritage. The strength of our heritage is what has kept us in place.”

Painting 1: Perseverance (2018)

This painting shows two Elders on the land (Figure 2). Although they are impacted by colonization and have suffered from intergenerational trauma, they stay strong, working to support their people and to preserve their inner beauty. Maintaining our inner beauty and standing strong with purpose, even in the face of obstacles, contributes to brain health as we age. While working, both characters in the painting have a vision, embodied by the four individuals in the back, one of whom is carrying and playing the drum. These figures are representative of ancestors. This also represents the teachings that the Elders received and the knowledge that they now possess. This scene shows the Anishinaabe/Ojibwe understanding of their relationship with the land, not as its owners, but as part of it, and as a source of life. The relationship with their environment is one of the central values of the Anishinaabe/Ojibwe people and when enacted ensures people actively use their bodies and minds to engage with life.

Perseverance and Anishinaabe Cognition.

Painting 2: Anishinaabe cognition (2018)

The character in red is a young knowledge keeper who visits two Elders to acquire medicine and knowledge (Figure 2). Intergenerational knowledge is central in preserving Anishinaabe/Ojibwe culture throughout time. Knowledge manifests not only when it is transmitted, but also in daily performance and practice. This scene takes place within a lodge, which represents home and family. Behind the two characters on the right, a seven-pointed star is depicted, symbolizing the seven teachings that Anishinaabe/Ojibwe people use to describe the stages of life. The characters in red and orange are holding two cups of tamarack tea, which represents the deep medicinal knowledge they hold. Memory has been essential to the preservation of this knowledge. Learning and teaching are central to aging in a healthy way. Brain health encompasses acquiring knowledge to help others. The young need to learn how to ask elders for this knowledge.

Painting 3: Presence (2018)

This painting represents the interaction between individuals and universal knowledge (Figure 3). This scene embodies the story of creation, which emphasizes that in the beginning of time everything was black. No one could see anything until light appeared and unveiled the world. In Anishinaabe/Ojibwe culture, knowledge and life systems are constant and are intertwined with individual action. However, to acquire and understand such knowledge, it is essential to develop a connection with the universe. The character in the center represents an Elder who has a strong connection with the source of universal knowledge, represented by the rainbow. The colorful circles represent the way this Elder’s character interprets the knowledge they have acquired. This scene highlights the importance of the connection between knowledge and individuals, which becomes stronger as people age and requires people’s presence, mind, and soul.

Presence and Healing Step.

Painting 4: Healing step (2018)

This painting is closely related to the idea of the cycle of life (Figure 3). The four stages of life are represented here: infancy, adolescence, adulthood, and Elderhood. To face the multiple barriers that individuals face throughout life, as well as trauma and the profound wounds that colonization left on Anishinaabe/Ojibwe people, healing is central to succeeding in the process of moving through the stages. The characters are positioned on a medicine wheel, with sacred foods placed in the center and at the four cardinal directions. The background includes rainbow colors, symbolizing knowledge. Food and medicine are essential for achieving a healthy life balance. To age in a healthy way, healing and knowledge sharing through the life course are critical.

Painting 5: Benevolence (2018)

This painting shows a woman greeting a grandmother and her helper (Figure 4). These three characters are embedded in a circle, which is a very important element in the Anishinaabe/Ojibwe culture, as it symbolizes the four directions and the medicine wheel. This scene reflects inclusiveness. Benevolence, then, is based on inclusiveness and sharing. On the right side of the painting, a group of women are talking and exchanging teachings. Most of the characters in the painting have blankets, which symbolize their willingness to help others. The four characters on the right are smudging, gaining strength and health from the four sacred medicines (tobacco, cedar, sage, and sweetgrass). Promoting inclusiveness is crucial to strengthening community ties, preventing isolation, and sharing knowledge, all of which are essential for healthy brain aging.

Benevolence/linkages.

Painting 6: Linkages (2018)

Overcoming trauma is extremely difficult for some people. When someone goes through a traumatic experience, they can get stuck in that stage of their life. They may not recognize the positive and beauty along the trajectory of their life. In Anishinaabe/Ojibwe culture, fire symbolizes home. The two characters on the right are Elders who dedicated their lives to building a circle of knowledge that allowed them to live a full life (Figure 4). Additionally, they have been able to establish a connection between this knowledge and themselves as individuals and their communities. Emphasizing the importance of relationality, the young character in yellow is preparing to receive teachings from the Elders and learn how to establish a connection between them and universal knowledge. “Linkages” represents generations, connections between families, and knowledge being shared. Circles in the sky represent those linkages. These linkages promote the practice of community and family caring as well as the connection to the universal knowledge that allows one to choose a healthy life path.

Painting 7: Bebaminojmat/one who goes around and shares teaching their gifts (2020)

Bell painted this final painting during the COVID-19 pandemic (Figure 5). It offers closure and a way to honor the Elders who inspired the previous paintings. Bell points out that what impressed and influenced him the most from the interviews was the Traditional Knowledge Sharers’ resilience and ability to persevere despite the traumas they and their ancestors had endured. It provides evidence that a person can age well even when faced with significant challenges. In this scene, each Elder has put a bundle on the ground in the middle of the group. These bundles represent all the knowledge they have accumulated throughout their lives that they now offer to their people. Bundles are sacred elements that Anishinaabe/Ojibwe people pass from generation to generation, learning and preserving what Elders have given them. This painting is the seventh of the series and depicts seven Elders, reflecting the significance of this number in Anishinaabe/Ojibwe culture.

Bebaminojmat/one who goes around and shares teaching their gifts.

Cross-cutting themes: healthy brain aging

Each of these teachings represents one story and teaching that can help promote specific pathways to healthy brain aging. The post-analysis also revealed cross-cutting themes interwoven among the paintings. These themes can help guide researchers, policymakers, and health service providers in supporting appropriate actions to address challenges related to healthy brain aging in Anishinaabe/Ojibwe and other communities. These themes are shared below.

Connections

The strong connections to the land and environment (Perseverance), younger generations (Linkages), and to the universe (Presence) are described as central to healthy brain aging. All of the paintings emphasize that connections facilitate the use of one’s gifts and the sharing of knowledge that can promote brain health. The concept of connections is also related to sharing knowledge and inclusivity, explicitly shown in Perseverance, Healing Step, Benevolence, and Linkages. The notion of connectedness presented by these Traditional Knowledge Sharers provides guidance on culturally meaningful ways to prevent isolation. Connections themselves are an energy force that Bell highlights throughout the paintings. The energy force created by the connections is akin to the lifeforce and our desire to continue living life in the face of adversity.

Colonialism and the resulting trauma

Colonization and its role as a significant driver of health inequities for Indigenous peoples, and its relationship to unhealthy brain aging (dementia specifically), are demonstrated powerfully in Healing Step and Linkages. These paintings most directly illustrate this point by showing how a person can become stuck in a particular stage of life or on their path due to trauma. The disconnection from Elder’s knowledge, the values of sharing, and knowledge of traditional food and medicines are important factors in healthy aging that have been suppressed and interrupted through the process of colonization.

Resilience

The teachings in the paintings suggest that there is a purpose at every stage of life, and each stage presents challenges to healthy brain aging that can be addressed through traditional practices and values. The Anishinaabe/Ojibwe are provided with helpers and gifts to assist them through the circle of life, but it is up to the individual to accept them. Occasionally, one will venture off the “good life” pathway of the circle of life, but even when this happens, there is always a purpose. Healing Step emphasizes the importance of sharing knowledge to heal from trauma with a specific focus on knowing the Anishinaabe/Ojibwe foods and medicines necessary for a healthy life balance. Perseverance reminds us that teachings and knowledge have persisted despite obstacles and that people have been resilient. It demonstrates how maintaining our inner beauty and standing strong with purpose, even in the face of obstacles, contributes to brain health as we age. Linkages directs us to Elders who hold the knowledge that can help people move past negative moments and onto the next stage of their life.

Sharing knowledge

The impacts of colonization contributing to unhealthy brain aging can be countered by resilience and connections, but many of the paintings suggest this is best achieved by sharing knowledge. Specifically, it is the knowledge that Elders hold that can aid in healing at all stages of life. Linkages shares that “the Elders know how” to help those who are “stuck” by trauma to get back on a path to a good life. Anishinaabe Cognition suggests the brain is kept healthy by both practicing and transmitting knowledge. Knowledge exists to help people live healthy lives, with an emphasis on teaching younger generations. The knowledge that needs to be shared is sacred knowledge, found in the seven-pointed star, which represents the seven stages of life, the Seven Grandfather Teachings, and the medicine wheel, as depicted in Anishinaabe Cognition, Healing Step, and Benevolence.

Elder knowledge bundles

All of these teachings culminate in reaching Elderhood with a healthy brain leading to Universal Knowledge that will be practiced by the Elders and passed on as knowledge bundles (Bell, 2018). The sacred knowledge in these bundles has the power to help others heal from trauma and preserve Anishinaabe/Ojibwe culture. We are again reminded that knowledge accumulates as we age, with the bundles representing the accumulation of their knowledge. Each Elder’s specific knowledge is represented, or kept, in their knowledge bundles. There is a great emphasis on preserving this knowledge through practice, sharing, and connections.

Discussion

The results demonstrate that the Anishinaabe/Ojibwe have inherent values, practices, and beliefs concerning aging related to their culture that are central to improving dementia outcomes. The approach to the research produced important insights into symbolism, teachings, and Indigenous knowledge that can promote healthy brain aging. The knowledge shared offers a fresh perspective for biomedical researchers and care providers when considering program design, health promotion, instrument development, and care delivery. The consistent messages in the teachings demonstrate that aging is a gift that one must work hard toward. While healthy brain aging is significantly challenged by trauma caused by colonization, the embodiment of Anishinaabe/Ojibwe values can move individuals toward a period of great knowledge. This knowledge is meant to be shared in order to improve both the individual’s health and that of those around them. If these values and teachings are not incorporated into dementia education and awareness materials, these resources may fail to resonate or have an impact in Anishinaabe/Ojibwe communities. Our study also demonstrated that art can be used as an interpretive method and not only as a product of research.

CBPR and indigenizing research

Arts-based research methods are being used internationally to elucidate Indigenous knowledge and teachings and have been successful when incorporated with the principles of CBPR (Hammond et al., 2018). Rooted in Anishinaabe/Ojibwe values and a strong storytelling culture, this project demonstrates the necessity of working in partnership with Indigenous communities and having them lead research through their own epistemologies to create outcomes that result in relevant and culturally appropriate health teachings (Kjerland et al., 2024). The design of this project was guided by CBPR and prioritized Indigenous analysis and perspectives. (Jacklin & Kinoshameg, 2008; LaVeaux & Christopher, 2009) To achieve this, researchers must be prepared to trust and uplift other knowledge systems and give power to partner communities to elevate Indigenous knowledge. It is also imperative that researchers build trust with the community, as they are being entrusted with sacred traditional knowledge (Christopher et al., 2011). This project is an example of how research can take an unexpected but effective turn when led by local advisory groups working with researchers who privilege Indigenous perspectives and sovereignty (Kjerland et al., 2024). It was the CAC who led the study team through the process and conceptualized the idea of creating artistic representations of the Traditional Knowledge Sharers’ teachings. The teachings, analysis, and visual and artistic interpretations all reflect an Indigenous knowledge lens (Absolon, 2020; Bell, 2018) and result in informative, symbolic representations of cultural teachings. With this approach, Indigenous knowledge, values, and beliefs can inform the development of appropriate materials, education, programs, and plans to promote healthy brain aging among Anishinaabe/Ojibwe people.

CBPR and Indigenous methodologies also guided this project in the dissemination of its outcomes, specifically the paintings. These paintings were returned to the Traditional Knowledge Sharers and the community, allowing these teachings to ­continue giving back long after the project was completed. This reflects one of Bell’s important interpretations—namely, the importance of sharing knowledge to maintain balance and health, thereby manifesting reciprocity (Brewer II & Johnson, 2023; Meissner, 2022). Ensuring a public exhibition and ­presentation of this project provided the community with an opportunity to learn more about the Traditional Knowledge Sharers’ teachings and knowledge. Additionally, as material art products that the communities will preserve, Bell’s paintings will also be available to support the intergenerational teachings of healthy aging that the Traditional Knowledge Sharers spoke about extensively. The team has developed a book with the paintings and descriptions shared in this paper as a community resource.

Incorporating culture into care

There are numerous benefits to incorporating art into health interventions, and these are being increasingly explored with Indigenous communities (Motta-Ochoa et al., 2024). In our project, each of the paintings and their descriptions provides direction for us to explore how Western medicine and approaches can support healthy brain aging by integrating culture in meaningful ways into medicine (Jacklin & Walker, 2020; Martin, 2012). This includes acknowledging the impacts of colonization, adopting trauma-informed care approaches, and drawing on cultural teachings to promote behaviors that foster brain health, such as the values of connection and sharing, which can combat the impacts of isolation on cognition (Evans et al., 2019). Importantly, we argue that Western biomedicine must value and center Indigenous experiences of brain aging. A Two-Eyed Seeing approach, in particular, provides a useful framework for balancing the contributions and considerations of Western and Indigenous worldviews (Martin, 2012). While these kinds of culturally focused and integrated healthcare models are few (Ehrlich et al., 2016; Illes et al., 2015), the need is apparent. The results highlight the profound contrast between Indigenous and Western epistemologies, as well as the resulting values and knowledge related to brain aging. Perhaps one of the most striking contrasts is that through deep connections the knowledge of older adults/Elders becomes stronger and more expansive in older age. This is particularly evident in Presence, where the Elder in the painting is connected to the source of universal knowledge. Our CAC shared that universal knowledge is the connection to the ancient ones and the rainbow is one place those ancestors show themselves. The sharing of this knowledge is made possible by following the Seven Grandfather Teachings (Kaagegaabaw, 2023) and living the “good life”, so that the Elder maintains “presence, mind and soul”. The results highlight how cultural connections, understandings, and traditions can enhance health promotion initiatives (Gray et al., 2010).

One of the main threads throughout Bell’s interpretation and representation of the Tradtional Knowledge Sharers’ teachings is the crucial importance of relationality and reciprocity in promoting healthy aging and brain health (Tsosie et al., 2022). Every painting alludes to the power and healing potential of sharing knowledge with others. This stands in sharp contrast to Western biomedical views of healthy aging, which tend to focus on factors associated with an individual’s “intrinsic capacity,” such as genetics and the accumulation of risk factors (Michel & Sadana, 2017). As Tsosie and colleagues describe, “Maintaining the interdependent relationships between the visible and the invisible in the world in reciprocal and respectful ways is conducive to living in balance and harmony with creation (Tsosie et al., 2022).” The invisible (e.g., spirits, ancestors) and visible (e.g., community, land), would not typically show up in a biomedical, Western clinical encounter about brain health and healthy aging. However, these concepts are essential to these Traditional Knowledge Sharers’ cultural understandings of how to age in a healthy way. We echo Brewer and Johnson’s (2023) request that, “for Indigenous and Western Sciences to work together, there must be reciprocity. For there to be reciprocity, Western science must adhere to protocols of Indigenous Peoples’ science” (Brewer II & Johnson, 2023) (pg. 315). In a similar way, for Western and Indigenous approaches to health to work together, Western biomedical approaches must center and adhere to the protocols of Indigenous peoples’ epistemologies. That means understanding the importance of relationships and sharing knowledge, which represents a much more holistic approach to health (­Absolon, 2020).

The use of art to represent cultural values

This project demonstrates that art can be a vehicle for knowledge transmission, preservation, and meaning-making in Indigenous health contexts. This unique approach to artistic representation and analysis can be beneficial for understanding and visually representing important knowledge and teachings that can promote healthy brain aging for future generations. All knowledge systems are profound and rich, but traditional Western approaches to the analysis of Indigenous knowledge and the resulting dissemination are often insufficient at capturing complex epistemological concepts (Absolon, 2020; Bird, 2014). Bird (2014) found that participants with traditional Indigenous knowledge exhibited more efficacy, personal resolve, and greater involvement with existential/spiritual issues. Western approaches to analysis may overlook the cultural narratives that ultimately render these paintings so powerful. Thus, art created within the community is a powerful tool for knowledge representation, with the added advantage that it can become an integral part of the community’s cultural heritage. Due to its creative and expressive nature, visual art can unveil and illuminate areas of knowledge that conventional forms, such as articles, books, and other written materials, cannot.

Limitations

This represents a small study of seven Traditional Knowledge Sharers interviews and may therefore not be wholly representative of all the perspectives on aging and brain health within the communities. It is also worth noting that the paintings mainly reflect one person’s (L.B.) interpretation of interview data, although corroborated by the CAC and research team. However, if other artists had been chosen, the artistic interpretation could have been different. Caution should also be exercised regarding the generalizability of the findings to other Indigenous communities, even to those of Anishinaabe/Ojibwe descent (the origin of the artist and study partner site). Incredible variation exists among Indigenous cultures and Nations, and while there are some points of convergence, researchers should be cautious in applying learnings in a Pan-Indigenous way.

Conclusion

This project builds on essential efforts that researchers and community members have made to clarify Indigenous understandings of healthy aging and relevant dementia prevention strategies. Drawing on CBPR and privileging Indigenous analysis and perspectives, this unique art-based initiative stems from the strong collaboration between academics, community members, the artist, and participants (Traditional Knowledge Sharers). The teachings shared by the Traditional Knowledge Sharers reveal that healthy brain aging and dementia prevention are linked to the practice of Anishinaabe/Ojibwe values such as sharing, knowledge transfer, and connections to others, land, and universal knowledge. The trauma caused by colonization is a central theme, as is the pathway to resilience. Importantly, in their worldview, strength and knowledge grow with age, and the accumulation of knowledge that can be shared is one’s purpose, ideally achieving universal knowledge. The Indigenous knowledge shared through these Traditional Knowledge Sharers’ teachings is often incompatible with biomedical approaches to aging yet provides essential insights into values that can be built into future interventions and health promotion efforts for healthy brain aging. The resulting paintings, based on these teachings, serve as a crucial starting point for future community conversations about aging and brain health, offering an inclusive and Indigenous-centered platform for knowledge translation and dissemination. Future Indigenous health research can benefit from incorporating arts-based approaches to complement the existing efforts of biomedical and social/behavioral researchers in addressing health inequalities in Indigenous contexts worldwide. Importantly, we have demonstrated how honoring Indigenous knowledge can yield findings that are urgently needed to develop culturally appropriate Indigenous health education and programs promoting healthy brain aging among Anishinaabe/Ojibwe individuals.
