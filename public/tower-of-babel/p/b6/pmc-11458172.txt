RNA Sequencing Analysis of Monocytes Exposed to Airway Fluid From Children With Pediatric Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome
Authors: Jocelyn R. Grunwell, Min Huang, Susan T. Stephenson, Mallory Tidwell, Michael J. Ripple, Anne M. Fitzpatrick, Rishikesan Kamaleswaran
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVES:Monocytes are plastic cells that assume different polarization states that can either promote inflammation or tissue repair and inflammation resolution. Polarized monocytes are partially defined by their transcriptional profiles that are influenced by environmental stimuli. The airway monocyte response in pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome (PARDS) is undefined. To identify differentially expressed genes and networks using a novel transcriptomic reporter assay with donor monocytes exposed to the airway fluid of intubated children with and at-risk for PARDS. To determine differences in gene expression at two time points using the donor monocyte assay exposed to airway fluid from intubated children with PARDS obtained 48–96 hours following initial tracheal aspirate sampling.DESIGN:In vitro pilot study carried out using airway fluid supernatant.SETTING:Academic 40-bed PICU.PARTICIPANTS:Fifty-seven children: 44 children with PARDS and 13 children at-risk for PARDS.INTERVENTIONS:None.MEASUREMENTS AND MAIN RESULTS:We performed bulk RNA sequencing using a transcriptomic reporter assay of monocytes exposed to airway fluid from intubated children to discover gene networks differentiating PARDS from at-risk for PARDS and those differentiating mild/moderate from severe PARDS. We also report differences in gene expression in children with PARDS 48–96 hours following initial tracheal aspirate sampling. We found that interleukin (IL)-10, IL-4, and IL-13, cytokine/chemokine signaling, and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype are upregulated in monocytes exposed to airway fluid from intubated children with PARDS compared with those at-risk for PARDS. Signaling by NOTCH, histone deacetylation/acetylation, DNA methylation, chromatin modifications (B-WICH complex), and RNA polymerase I transcription and its associated regulatory apparatus were upregulated in children with PARDS 48–96 hours following initial tracheal aspirate sampling.CONCLUSIONS:We identified gene networks important to the PARDS airway immune response using bulk RNA sequencing from a monocyte reporter assay that exposed monocytes to airway fluid from intubated children with and at-risk for PARDS. Mechanistic investigations are needed to validate our findings.

KEY POINTS

Question: What is the monocyte response to the tracheal aspirate fluid from intubated children at-risk compared with those with pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome (PARDS)?

Findings: Interleukin (IL)-4, IL-13, and IL-10 cytokine/chemokine signaling pathways and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype are upregulated in monocytes exposed to the tracheal aspirate fluid from children with compared with those at-risk for PARDS.

Meaning: A transcriptomic reporter assay following exposure of donor human monocytes to airway fluid from intubated children may be a useful assay to unravel the signaling pathways important to the airway immune response of children with PARDS.

Pediatric acute respiratory distress syndrome (PARDS) occurs in 6% of mechanically ventilated children admitted to a PICU and is associated with 17% overall mortality (1). Monocytes and macrophages are heterogeneous and plastic key innate immune cells with dual roles in acute lung injury (2–5). Resident alveolar macrophages are the first to encounter pathogens in the airways, fight infection, and promote the inflammatory response in the early phase of lung injury (6). This is followed by the recruitment of inflammatory monocytes to the airways whereby monocytes/macrophages begin the process of clearing apoptotic cells and debris (7). During this process, monocytes/macrophages switch from an initial pro-inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory phenotype that helps regulate repair of the lung. Monocytes/macrophages are categorized into three phenotypes, or polarization states, defined by their response to stimulation with certain cytokines (8–10). For example, interferon (IFN)-γ is used to push macrophages into a classically activated macrophages (M1, pro-inflammatory) state, interleukin (IL)-4 is used to push to an alternatively activated (M2a, pro-resolution/pro-fibrotic, alternatively activated) state, and IL-10 is used to push to a different regulatory/alternatively activated (M2c, anti-inflammatory) state (8, 9). While this categorization strategy is overly simplistic, given that these cells may be polarized across a continuous activation spectrum, it does provide a useful framework to describe experimental results.

We have previously shown that airway fluid contains immunologic mediators that stimulate responses from human donor immune cells, such as neutrophils or CD4+ T cells ex vivo (11, 12). Monocytes/macrophages are limited in quantity from tracheal aspirate samples, and bronchoalveolar lavage is rarely performed in intubated children to obtain primary alveolar monocytes/macrophages for analysis. Therefore, to investigate the differences in gene expression profiles of airway-like monocytes in children with PARDS compared with those at risk for PARDS, we used a transcriptomic reporter assay whereby we exposed donor monocytes to airway fluid from tracheal aspirates of intubated children with and at risk for PARDS. The goal of this work was to determine the modulation of normal human monocytes by the airway fluid environment of children with PARDS compared with those at risk for PARDS. We hypothesized that donor monocytes exposed to tracheal aspirate fluid from intubated children with PARDS would have differentially expressed genes characterized by a mixed pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory polarization response from intubated children who were at risk, but never developed, PARDS.

METHODS

Patient Cohort and Ethics Statement

This study is a prospective observational cohort study conducted in the Emory University School of Medicine affiliated Children’s Healthcare of Atlanta 40-bed PICU with participant enrollment between September 2018 and March 2020. The Emory University School of Medicine Institutional Review Board (IRB00034236: Prevalence of Oxidative Stress in Critically Ill Children and Its Relationship to Immune Function; A Pilot Study. Approved: February 14, 2018 and IRB00113035: Airway Immune Response in Critically Ill Children: Precision Medicine in Children at Risk for Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome. Approved: July 29, 2019) approved the study. Trained research coordinators obtained informed consent from a parent or legal guardian prior to enrollment according to the guidance of the Declaration of Helsinki. Children older than 2 days old and with a corrected gestational age of 40 weeks and younger than 18 years old were eligible for enrollment if they were admitted to the PICU and had been endotracheally intubated for less than 72 hours. Children were excluded if they had perinatal related lung disease, chronic respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation via a tracheostomy or RAM cannula, had an immunodeficiency, were on immunosuppression medication for an oncologic process or a hematologic or solid-organ transplant, there was no parent or legal guardian to provide in-person written informed consent, or the attending physician did not wish the patient to participant in the study. Severity of PARDS was classified according to the 2023 Second Pediatric Acute Lung Injury Consensus Conference (PALICC-2) (13, 14) at the time of sample collection. PALICC-2 defines PARDS as the onset of pulmonary edema, not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload, within 7 days of a known clinical insult and with new onset of opacities (unilateral or bilateral) on radiographic chest imaging consistent with acute pulmonary parenchymal disease (and not due primarily to atelectasis or effusion). Children with perinatal lung disease are excluded. Children on invasive mechanical ventilation meet the oxygenation threshold when they have an oxygen index (OI = [mean airway pressure in cm H2O × Fio2]/Pao2 in mm Hg) greater than or equal to 4 or, for those children without an arterial blood gas measurement, as having an oxygen saturation index (OSI [mean airway pressure in cm H2O × Fio2]/Spo2) greater than or equal to 5 if the oxygen saturation (Spo2) is less than or equal to 97%. Stratification of PARDS severity occurs greater than or equal to 4 hours after the initial diagnosis of PARDS with mild/moderate PARDS defined as an OI of less than 16 or an OSI of less than 12 and severe PARDS defined as an OI of greater than or equal to 16 or OSI of greater than or equal to 12. The at-risk for PARDS group has the same age, timing, origin of pulmonary edema, not fully explained by cardiac failure or fluid overload, and chest imaging findings as those with PARDS; however, the oxygenation threshold is the requirement for oxygen supplementation to maintain Spo2 of greater than or equal to 88% but does not meeting the OI of greater than or equal to 4 or OSI of greater than or equal to 5 oxygenation threshold defining PARDS. Severity of illness scores were determined using the Pediatric Risk of Mortality-III, calculated at the time of PICU admission, and Pediatric Logistic Organ Dysfunction-2 score calculated within 24 hours of study enrollment (15, 16).

Tracheal Aspirate Collection and Sample Processing

Tracheal aspirate samples were collected within 72 hours of endotracheal intubation (sample 1) at the time of study enrollment and processed according to published protocols (17–19). Some of the tracheal aspirate samples used in this study have been used in published studies (11, 17–19). In a subset of children with PARDS who remained intubated, a second tracheal aspirate sample was collected 48–96 hours after the first sampling timepoint (sample 2). Up to 5 mL of sterile saline was instilled in the endotracheal tube and the Ballard inline suction catheter connected to a sterile Lukens trap was passed once to obtain a sample. If 50% of the instilled volume was not returned, then a repeat passage of the Ballard was performed. The Ballard suction catheter could be flushed with additional saline to move the aspirate into the Lukens trap. The airway fluid was separated from the cells by centrifugation at 800 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C. Airway fluid supernatant was collected into a fresh tube, centrifuged at 3000 × g for 15 minutes at 4°C, and the resulting cell-free supernatant was stored at –80°C until use.

Cell Culture

Commercially available single donor CD14+ monocytes were purchased from Lonza (Greenwood, SC). CD14+ monocytes (0.5 × 106 in 500 µL) were resuspended in a 1:1 volume:volume mixture of airway supernatant from individual patients in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium with L-glutamine (Corning, Corning, NY), 10% fetal calf serum supplemented with penicillin, streptomycin, and gentamicin and cultured for 12 hours in a humidified 37°C, 5% co2 incubator. A similar transcriptomic reporter assay using neutrophils, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), and monocyte-derived dendritic cells from two separate donors was used to measure the immunogenic activity of plasma from adults’ patients with sepsis compared with uninfected controls (20). Khaenam et al (20) demonstrated that transcriptomic responses measured in neutrophils and PBMC were consistent for both cell donors but not for monocyte-derived dendritic cells. Following overnight culture, CD14+ monocytes were pelleted by centrifugation at 400 × g at 4°C for 15 minutes, resuspended in 500 µL of RNALater, and stored at –80°C until RNA was isolated.

RNA Preparation

RNA was isolated from CD14+ monocytes using the Nucleospin RNA II kit with on-column genomic DNA digestion according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Takara, Mountain View, CA). RNA sizing quantification and quality control was performed in the Emory Integrated Genomics Core on an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer using Pico and Nano Agilent kits and a Tecan optical density plate reader to measure the concentration of the RNA (18).

RNA Sequencing

Complementary DNA was generated using the SMART-Seq v4 Ultra Low Input RNA Kit (Takara Bio, San Jose, CA), and the final RNA sequencing library was generated using the Nextera XT kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA). RNA sequencing libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 with a sequencing depth of 30M PE100 reads per sample. The sequencing results can be accessed at the Gene Expression Omnibus database at: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE250448 with an accession number of GSE250448.

Data Preprocessing

Fastp was used to perform quality control and adapter trimming and the results were filtered by quality. HISAT2 (Version: 2.2.1) (21) was used to map RNA sequencing reads prior to building the index using the human reference genome genome reference consortium human build 38. We then used indices to map our sequential reads to the human genome. We applied featureCounts (Version: 2.0.2) (22) to generate a matrix of row counts of each of the genes in all the samples. Genes were kept in the analysis that had at least ten reads in at least half of the samples.

Differential Gene Expression Analysis

The R package Deseq2 (Version: 1.38.3) (23) was used to determine differential gene expression. We used variance stabilizing transformations for raw data transformation, transformed the data on the log2 scale, and normalized it to library size. We used the Anan empirical Bayes shrinkage methods to detect and correct for dispersion and calculate log2[fold change] for the genes. Differentially expressed genes were defined as having a |log2[fold change]| of greater than 1. The p values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. A false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 0.05 was considered significant.

Pathway Analysis

Reactome (https://reactome.org) was used to obtain the relevant biological pathways compared with different groups derived from the differentially expressed genes (24–29). The report generated is an overrepresentation analysis based on a hypergeometric distribution test that determines whether certain Reactome pathways are enriched in the submitted differentially gene expression data compared with that by chance alone to produce a probability score corrected for a FDR using the Benjamini-Hochberg method. We performed a sensitivity analysis where we excluded the three participants supported by extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO). Only pathways with an FDR of less than 0.05 were used for the analysis. We performed an additional analysis where we excluded children who received systemic corticosteroids as part of their treatment course and compared the differential gene expression of children with PARDS to those at-risk for PARDS.

Three of the seven children with severe PARDS received life-support with ECMO during their hospital course. Three participants with PARDS died. Differentially Expresses Genes in Children With vs. At Risk for PARDS

The volcano plot for differentially expressed genes in donor human CD14+ monocytes cells exposed to airway fluid from children with PARDS compared with those at risk for PARDS is shown in Figure 1. There were 574 upregulated and 23 downregulated genes (Supplemental Digital Content E1, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B408; and Supplemental Digital Content E2, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B409). Transcripts were selected if there was at least a two-fold difference in response to airway fluid stimulation compared with the at risk PARDS group. We compared the differential gene expression in donor human CD14+ monocytes cells exposed to airway fluid from children who did not receive systemic corticosteroids with PARDS (n = 24) to those at risk for PARDS (n = 12) and found four upregulated genes including beta-ureidopropionase—an enzyme involved in pyrimidine degradation, IL-23A—an important cytokine in the inflammatory response to infection and the T helper 17 response, G-protein coupled receptor family C group 5 member C—an orphan retinoic acid-inducible G protein-coupled receptor, and microRNA155 host gene which functions as a post-transcriptional regulator of innate immune signaling pathways and is implicated in the inhibition of viral infections.

Pathway Analysis of PARDS vs. At Risk for PARDS

The top upregulated Reactome pathways in donor human CD14+ monocytes cells exposed to airway fluid from children with PARDS compared with those at risk for PARDS were signaling by IL, cytokines, and chemokine receptors binding chemokines, specifically signaling pathways activated by type 2 inflammatory cytokines IL-10, IL-4, and IL-13 (Table 2). A senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) pathway was also upregulated in donor monocytes exposed to airway fluid from children with PARDS vs. those at risk for PARDS along with recognition and association of DNA glycosylase with site containing an affected purine.

Longitudinal Pathway Analysis in Children With PARDS

There were 76 upregulated Reactome pathways in donor monocytes exposed to airway fluid from sample 2 compared with sample 1 in children with PARDS (Supplemental Digital Content E5, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B412). The top upregulated pathways are shown (Table 3). Most of the significantly enriched functional networks were related to pre-NOTCH transcription and translation/expression and processing, NOTCH signaling, epigenetic modifications (histone deacetylation, acetylation, and methylation), sirtuin 1 negative regulation of ribosomal RNA expression, transcriptional regulation of granulopoiesis, defective pyroptosis, transcriptional regulation of RUNX1 and RUNX1 regulation of megakaryocyte differentiation and platelet function, cleavage of damaged purines/depurination, oxidative stress-induced senescence, and RNA polymerase promoter escape/clearance and transcription (Table 3). There were no downregulated Reactome pathways identified when comparing donor monocytes exposed to airway fluid from sample 2 to sample 1 downregulated genes.

Differentially Expressed Genes and Pathway Analysis in Children With Mild/Moderate vs. Severe PARDS

The volcano plot for differentially expressed genes in donor monocytes exposed to airway fluid from children with severe vs. mild/moderate PARDS is shown in Supplemental Figure 3 (http://links.lww.com/CCX/B417). There were 101 upregulated and 4728 downregulated genes (Supplemental Digital Content E6, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B413; and Supplemental Digital Content E7, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B414). Transcripts were selected if there was at least a two-fold difference in response to airway fluid stimulation in the severe PARDS group compared with the mild/moderate PARDS group.

The top upregulated Reactome pathways in donor monocytes exposed to airway fluid from children with severe PARDS compared with those with mild/moderate PARDS were pathways involving the immune system, neutrophil degranulation, the innate immune system, translocation of zeta-chain-associated protein kinase 70 to immunological synapse, phosphorylation of CD3 and T-cell receptor zeta chains, programmed cell death protein 1 signaling, platelet degranulation, response to elevated platelet cytosolic calcium, generation of second messenger molecules, nuclear signaling by erythroblastic leukemia viral oncogene homologue 4, amyloid fiber formation, and major histocompatibility complex class II antigen presentation (Supplemental Digital Content E8, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B415). There were 56 significantly downregulated Reactome pathways in donor monocytes exposed to airway fluid from children with severe vs. mild/moderate PARDS (Supplemental Digital Content E9, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B416). The most significantly downregulated pathways in donor monocytes exposed to airway fluid from severe vs. mild/moderate PARDS were related to RNA polymerase I promoter opening, DNA methylation, histone deacetylases deacetylate histones, recognition and association of DNA glycosylase with a site containing an affected purine, among others.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we used a transcriptomic reporter assay with bulk RNA sequencing to analyze the gene expression profiles of CD14+ monocytes exposed to airway fluid from patients with PARDS compared with those at risk for PARDS. We found that type 2 inflammatory signaling mediated by IL-10, IL-4, and IL-13, along with the SASP, was upregulated in donor monocytes exposed to tracheal aspirate fluid from intubated children with PARDS compared with those at risk for PARDS. We also analyzed the change in gene expression in a subset of children with PARDS at two sampling timepoints during the course of intubation. We found that NOTCH signaling, which is an evolutionary conserved pathway that determines cell-fate and maintains adult tissue homeostasis, and the machinery regulating epigenetic modifications of DNA that controls gene expression, were upregulated at the second compared with the first sampling time in donor monocytes exposed to tracheal aspirate fluid from intubated children with PARDS.

IL-10 is a pleiotropic cytokine with multiple regulatory immune effects; however, it typically inhibits type 1 pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha, IL-1β, IL-12, and IFNγ (30). The bronchoalveolar lavage fluid collected from adults within the first 3 days of acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) onset was used to drive an “IL-10 like” alternatively activated polarization response in healthy human donor monocyte-derived macrophages (10). Cotreatment of IL-10 driven alternatively activated monocyte-derived macrophages with the pleiotropic cytokine IL-6, commonly associated with pro-inflammatory functions and the pathogenesis of ARDS, enhanced the immunosuppressive phenotype with an increase in the secretion of IL-10 and other markers of alternatively activated macrophages (31).

In addition to the importance of IL-10 and IL-6 signaling on the polarization state and function of monocytes/macrophages (32, 33), the targeting of type 2 inflammation by blocking the IL-13/IL-4 signaling axis has been proposed in adults with severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) ARDS (34). Elevated IL-13 is associated with severe lung injury and the need for mechanical ventilation in two independent cohorts of adults with COVID-19 (35), and patients who were prescribed Dupilumab, the monoclonal antibody blocking IL-13 and IL-4 signaling, while infected with SARS-CoV-2 experienced fewer deaths and lower disease severity without affecting viral load (35). As shown for IL-10, when IL-4 and IL-13 are combined with the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-6, macrophages are polarized to an alternatively activated state that is mediated by the transcription factor signal transducer and activator of transcription-3 (STAT-3) activation (31). IL-10, IL-6, and IL-17 all signal through STAT-3 to elicit an inflammatory response (36–38), and we have previously shown that STAT-3 phosphorylation is upregulated in the airway neutrophils from intubated children with PARDS compared with those at risk for PARDS (18).

Given the diverse cytokine environment of airway fluid, we were intrigued by the upregulation of genes related to the SASP in the monocytes cultured with the airway fluid from children with PARDS compared with those at risk for PARDS. Cellular senescence is a state of irreversible cell cycle arrest triggered by excessive levels of reactive oxygen species and persistent activation of the DNA damage response. Senescent cells secrete a complex mixture of inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, matrix metalloproteinases, and serine protease inhibitors into the surrounding extracellular fluid that can be beneficial or harmful depending on the biological context and duration of the SASP response (39–43). Initially, the SASP is immunosuppressive (e.g., transforming growth factor beta-1) and profibrotic, but progresses to become proinflammatory (e.g., IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8) and fibrolytic (44, 45). Autopsy samples from lungs of adults with ARDS showed increased senescence-associated heterochromatin foci and increased markers of cellular senescence in the endothelium and epithelium from lungs of adults who died from COVID-19 (46–48). Activation of the SASP has not been described in PARDS, and it is not clear whether this response is transient and contributing to the restoration of tissue homeostasis, or the start of a pathologic chronic inflammatory response contributing to impaired stem cell renewal and pulmonary fibrosis associated with poor outcomes.

In a subset of children with PARDS, we examined the donor monocytes transcriptional response to airway fluid at two different time points during intubation. We found that pre-NOTCH and NOTCH signaling were upregulated at sample 2 compared with sample 1. NOTCH signaling upregulates IL-6 in a positive feedback loop in COVID-19 ARDS (49). While limited NOTCH signaling promotes proliferation and transdifferentiation of basal cells in the lung that are required for healing and regeneration of other lung cell types to restore functional airways, excessive NOTCH signaling inhibits this regenerative process (49). Along with a pro-inflammatory cytokine environment promoted by IL-6, continuous NOTCH activation encourages goblet cell hyperplasia, abnormal regeneration of lung epithelium, and an accumulation of basal cells and honeycomb-like structures in the alveolar spaces that contributes to ARDS and disease severity (49). Activation of NOTCH signaling promotes inflammation by inducing a classically activated macrophage phenotype. Conversely, inhibiting NOTCH signaling induces an immunosuppressive alternatively activated macrophage (50–54). Furthermore, NOTCH signaling plays an important role in lung development, regeneration and transdifferentiation of type II to type I airway epithelial cells (50, 55–58). Notch signaling is complex with four Notch receptors having the potential to interact with five distinct ligands. Unfortunately, we cannot differentiate among the Notch ligand-specific signaling pathways, and this can be the subject of future work.

This study is limited to a small sample of patients who were predominantly infants and toddlers with viral lower respiratory tract infections from a single children’s hospital, which may limit generalizability. Some of the findings may be attributable to genetic or epigenetic influences of the donor monocytes; however, variability was limited as all experiments were performed with the same aliquot of donor monocytes. We had a limited number of patient tracheal aspirate samples available at two time points during the intubation course for children with PARDS. Without more data that pairs clinical trajectory with biological sampling, we can only speculate that children with sustained severe PARDS may have a different donor monocyte transcriptional profile after exposure to tracheal aspirate samples compared with those who initially have severe PARDS, but rapidly recover. The receipt of systemic corticosteroids is a confounding factor in our analysis that we cannot control given the observational nature of the study and limited sample size. While systemic corticosteroids were more frequently given to those children with PARDS compared with those at-risk for PARDS, a similar proportion of children with mild/moderate (44%) and severe PARDS (50%) received systemic corticosteroids. We are not able to determine whether differences in gene expression are due to the pathobiology related to the initial acute clinical trigger of PARDS or from ventilator-induced lung injury. Bronchoalveolar lavage in children with PARDS is not routinely performed in clinical practice; therefore, our study relied on the use of tracheal aspirates, which may not reflect the immune system mediators present in the alveolar environment. While we attempted to be consistent in the lavage procedure, we acknowledge that there are no good alternatives to controlling the volume instilled by age and the variability in the amounts of fluid returned with the tracheal aspirate sampling method. Furthermore, we do not know the impact that sample collection outside of a 24-hour window following intubation has on the results. The trajectory of both the airway and systemic immune responses of children with PARDS—to answer why some children are resilient and recover, while others do not recover—is the focus of our future studies.

In conclusion, we report the use of a monocyte transcriptomic reporter assay using airway fluid from children with PARDS compared with those at risk for PARDS. Use of transcriptomic reporter assays may provide a clinically useful strategy to probe complex immune cell responses to heterogeneous environments, such as the airways, of intubated children who are at risk or who have PARDS. Functional responses of monocytes (e.g., phagocytosis) after exposure to airway fluid and the response to conditioned media from the donor monocyte transcriptional assay containing SASP mediators on primary small airway epithelial cells will yield mechanistic insights into the pathobiology of severity over time in PARDS.
