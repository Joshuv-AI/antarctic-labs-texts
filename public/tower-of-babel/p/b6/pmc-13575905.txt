Impact of Smoking, Alcohol Use and Exercise on Underweight after Gastrectomy: A Nationwide Cohort Study of Gastric Cancer Patients in Korea
Authors: Yoon Ju Jung, Kyungdo Han, Sung Geun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/AimsWeight loss following gastric cancer (GC) surgery is a prevalent and significant long-term complication that impacts the patient’s oncologic prognosis and quality of life. The impact of lifestyle changes after surgery with regard to regular exercise, smoking, and alcohol consumption on weight loss have not been fully explored. We studied the factors associated with underweight (body mass index <18.5 kg/m2) after gastrectomy, focusing on variation across sex and age groups.MethodsWe utilized a population-based cohort provided by the Korean National Health Insurance Service, which included cancer screening data. In total, 27,854 patients who underwent GC surgery between 2010 and 2016 were included.ResultsAmong the 27,854 patients in the study cohort, 3,064 (11.0%) were underweight. Female sex (odds ratio [OR], 1.74; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.55 to 1.96; p<0.001) was a significant risk factor for becoming underweight postoperatively. Engaging in regular exercise (OR, 0.73; 95% CI, 0.60 to 0.89; p=0.002) and ceasing smoking (OR, 0.31; 95% CI, 0.13 to 0.78; p<0.001) emerged as substantial preventive factors against becoming underweight, particularly in female patients. Exercise (OR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.65 to 0.89; p=0.001) proved more beneficial for patients aged <65 years.ConclusionsSmoking cessation and regular exercise are associated with a reduced risk of postoperative underweight in GC patients following gastrectomy.

INTRODUCTION

Weight loss is an inevitable consequence of stomach cancer surgery, primarily due to reduced intake and diminished absorption resulting from the bypassed intestine.1,2 This phenomenon has led to the concept of the obesity paradox, where gastric cancer (GC) patients who were obese prior to surgery are more likely to maintain a suitable weight postoperatively.3-5 Underweight following gastrectomy is a significant long-term complication, critically affecting both the oncologic prognosis and the quality of life of patients.6-10

The influence of lifestyle changes after surgery with regard to regular exercise, smoking, and alcohol consumption on underweight remains unclear.11,12 Notably, these changes may vary based on sex and age, potentially affecting body weight differently. However, few studies have stratified these associations by sex and age.8,13

In addition, various clinical indicators are used to identify clinically significant weight loss. Simple changes in body weight may not adequately reflect significant changes in health status. Therefore, multiple indicators, including differences in weight or body mass index (BMI), percentage change from original weight, change in abdominal circumference, or muscle mass, have been used in numerous studies. However, there has been no comprehensive examination of the incidence of each indicator, nor a consensus on which indicators are most suitable for preventive intervention.2,4,11,12 To address this gap, we aimed to evaluate the incidence of underweight using multiple clinical measures and to identify the most suitable indicators and key factors for its prevention.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Study populations

We used a prospective population-based cohort from the Korean National Health Insurance Service, encompassing general health check-up data. In total, 135,701 patients who underwent GC surgery between 2010 and 2016 were initially reviewed. We excluded patients without an insurance claim for GC within 1 year after surgery (n=14,108), those with a history of other cancers prior to gastrectomy (n=6,102), and those without a general health screening within ±2 years of the first gastrectomy claim (n=87,045). In addition, 592 patients were excluded due to missing data, defined as the absence of one or more essential variables, including BMI, weight, waist circumference (WC), lifestyle questionnaires, or key laboratory values such as glucose and lipid profiles. The final cohort comprised 27,854 patients, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

2. Definitions and clinical parameters

GC diagnosis was determined using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD-10) codes C16 and V193, where C16 indicates malignant neoplasms of the stomach, excluding benign neoplasms such as gastric adenoma. Diabetes was defined as a fasting plasma glucose level ≥126 mg/dL, use of oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin, or having an ICD-10 code E11–14. Hypertension was identified as a systolic/diastolic blood pressure ≥140/90 mm Hg, usage of antihypertensive medication, or having an ICD-10 code I10–13 and I15. Dyslipidemia was defined as fasting total cholesterol ≥240 mg/dL, taking lipid-lowering medication, or having an ICD-10 code E78. Medication histories were established based on prescription history within 1 year prior to registration date, defined as the date of GC surgery, which served as the index date for analysis based on claims data.

The questionnaire data included smoking status (binary; nonsmoker, current smoker), alcohol consumption (binary; nondrinker, drinker), and regular exercise (high-intensity: >20 minutes ≥3 times/week; moderate-intensity: >30 minutes ≥5 times/week over the last week, yes/no). Anthropometric data such as height, weight, and WC were measured on the day of the national general health check-up. Systolic/diastolic blood pressure and laboratory data including fasting serum levels of glucose and cholesterol profiles were also collected.

For the purpose of this study, underweight was defined as BMI <18.5 kg/m2, in accordance with the World Health Organization’s globally accepted classification, which identifies a clinically meaningful subgroup at increased risk of malnutrition.14 Given its global clinical relevance and potential implications for postoperative recovery, treatment tolerance, and long-term outcomes, BMI <18.5 kg/m2 was used as the primary outcome measure in subsequent analyses. Ideal body weight was estimated using the Broca Index formula, (height in cm–100)×0.9, a method commonly applied in Asian cohorts for clinical evaluation.15 Postoperative weight change was assessed relative to preoperative weight, and patients were classified into two groups: those with <10% weight loss and those with ≥10% weight loss from baseline. Abdominal obesity was defined as a WC ≥90 cm for males and ≥85 cm for females. Chronic kidney disease was characterized as an estimated glomerular filtration rate <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 in laboratory results. Changes in alcohol and smoking history were analyzed by categorizing them into yes/no, both before and after surgery.

3. Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the College of Medicine, Catholic University of Korea (IRB number: KC22RISI0943), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and good clinical practice. All procedures were conducted in line with the ethical standards of the institutional and national committees on human experimentation and with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its later amendments. Owing to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for written informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board of the College of Medicine, Catholic University of Korea.

4. Statistical analysis

To analyze continuous and categorical variables, Student t test and the chi-square test were utilized, respectively. To assess the independent risk for smoking and alcohol consumption by underweight, a multiple logistic regression model was employed after adjusting for clinically significant factors. Subgroup analyses were performed to explore potential differences by age and sex, rather than adjusting for these variables. All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). Statistical significance was established at p<0.05, and all tests were two-sided.

RESULTS

1. Patient characteristics and univariate analyses

The average time from the preoperative examination to surgery was 154 days, and the period from surgery to the postoperative examination averaged 472 days. In the study cohort of 27,854 patients, 3,064 (11.0%) had a BMI <18.5 kg/m2 (Supplementary Table 1). Defining ideal body weight as (height in cm–100)×0.9, 64.7% of patients had post-surgery weights lower than their ideal body weight. A small proportion (0.8%) experienced a weight loss >10% from their preoperative weight. Notably, considering the lower limit of normal weight for Asians is a BMI of 23 kg/m2, 69.8% fell below this threshold (Supplementary Table 1).

Table 1 presents the clinical characteristics of patients according to postoperative underweight status. The underweight group had a higher proportion of individuals ≥65 years of age, a greater percentage of females, and fewer patients engaged in exercise compared to the non-underweight group (all p<0.001). The underweight group had fewer metabolic diseases such as diabetes, hypertension, or hyperlipidemia (p<0.001). In addition, a larger proportion of the underweight group had undergone total gastrectomy (p<0.001) (Table 1).

Lifestyle habits before and after surgery were also compared. In the underweight group, a higher proportion of patients were either lifelong nonsmokers or continued to smoke. Conversely, the non-underweight group had a higher proportion of patients who smoked before but quit after surgery (p<0.001). Regarding alcohol consumption, the underweight group had more patients who abstained both before and after surgery, while those who drank before surgery were less likely to be underweight, regardless of drinking habits following surgery (p<0.001) (Table 1).

Subgroup analysis of male and female patients revealed differences. In male patients, more underweight cases were observed in older patients aged ≥65 years (p<0.001) (Table 2). By contrast, younger female patients were more likely to be underweight (p<0.001), possibly due to a higher incidence of Borrmann type 4 diffuse advanced GC in this demographic. Regular exercise before surgery was associated with a lower likelihood of being underweight in both sexes (p<0.001 and p=0.001, respectively). Total gastrectomy was related to postoperative underweight in both sexes (both p<0.001). Among males, those who never smoked were less likely to be underweight, while those who continued smoking were more likely to be underweight (p<0.001). Alcohol consumption before surgery correlated with a lower likelihood of being underweight (p<0.001). In female patients, no significant differences were noted between the groups, potentially due to a lower percentage of smoking and drinking history (p=0.212 and p=0.578, respectively) (Table 2).

Using 65 years of age as the cutoff, underweight was less common among patients who exercised (both p<0.001) and more common among those who underwent total gastrectomy (both p<0.001), regardless of age group. The proportion of males was lower in the underweight group (p<0.001 and p=0.020, respectively) (Table 3). Among younger patients, nonsmokers both before and after surgery were more frequently underweight, whereas those who smoked before surgery but quit afterward were less likely to be underweight (p<0.001). In contrast, among older patients, nonsmokers both before and after surgery were less likely to be underweight, and those who smoked before surgery were also less likely to be underweight, regardless of smoking cessation status after surgery (p=0.005) (Table 3).

2. Multivariate analysis

Multivariate analysis, incorporating various factors potentially influencing changes in weight after surgery, was conducted for all patients and for each group categorized by sex and age (Tables 4 and 5). Across the entire patient cohort, female sex was a significant risk factor for becoming underweight (odds ratio [OR], 1.74; p<0.001) (Table 5). For male patients, being <40 or >65 years old, in contrast to the median age, was identified as a risk factor for becoming underweight postoperatively (OR=1.63 and OR=1.31, respectively, p<0.001) (Table 4); however, this was not significant in female patients. Notably, regular exercise had a protective effect against becoming underweight, particularly in female patients (OR, 0.73; p=0.002) (Table 4). Both male and female patients who quit smoking following surgery were at lower risk for becoming underweight, with this effect being more pronounced in female patients (OR=0.79 and OR=0.31, respectively, both p<0.001) (Table 4). In addition, for females, initiating smoking after surgery was a risk factor for becoming underweight (OR, 1.64; p<0.001) (Table 4). Regarding alcohol consumption, patients who continued to drink both before and after surgery—male and female—were used as the reference group; all other groups showed higher odds, indicating that continued drinking was associated with the lowest relative risk of underweight (all ORs >1.00, p=0.001 and p=0.032, respectively) (Table 4).

In age-based subgroup analyses, female sex was a risk factor for becoming underweight in both age groups (OR=1.86 and OR=1.52, respectively, both p<0.001) (Table 5). A protective effect of exercise was observed only in younger patients (OR, 0.76; p=0.001) (Table 5). Smoking cessation after surgery was associated with a reduced risk of underweight in both age groups (OR=0.71 and OR=0.83, p<0.001 and p=0.006, respectively) (Table 5). Regarding alcohol consumption, patients who continued drinking before and after surgery were at relatively lower risk of underweight in both younger and older groups (all ORs >1.00, p=0.001 and p=0.020, respectively) (Table 5). Notably, among older patients, those who did not drink before surgery but initiated drinking afterward demonstrated a protective effect against underweight (OR, 0.89; p=0.020) (Table 5).

DISCUSSION

Exercise and changes in smoking habits were generally associated with lower odds of underweight, particularly among female patients, with exercise showing greater benefit in younger individuals. This effect may be attributable to increased muscle mass. Because females typically have less muscle mass than males, making them more vulnerable to clinically significant underweight after gastrectomy, regular moderate-intensity exercise appears to help increase muscle mass and maintain body weight.8,13 Moreover, sex hormones—particularly estrogen—are known to influence appetite regulation, fat metabolism, and inflammatory responses, which may affect postoperative nutritional recovery.16,17 The use of a uniform BMI threshold across sexes, without consideration of physiological differences, may also contribute to the higher observed prevalence of underweight among females. Combined with behavioral factors such as lower energy intake and heightened sensitivity to gastrointestinal symptoms, these characteristics may render females more susceptible to underweight status after surgery. These findings underscore the need for future research using sex-specific BMI thresholds. Our study provides evidence supporting the development of sex-differentiated criteria for assessing postoperative underweight risk.

In older patients, the impact of exercise on preventing underweight was comparatively limited. Given the decrease in basal energy expenditure and the increase in fat mass with age, older patients may be more prone to weight gain.18 However, age-related declines in digestive function, particularly in stomach cancer patients, makes increasing food intake following surgery challenging. Aging is also associated with a loss of lean body mass, contributing to sarcopenia, which is not easily reversed by exercise alone.19 Exercise in older patients might be more effective for enhancing the muscle mass ratio rather than increasing body weight. Interestingly, a previous study reported that the effect of low BMI on survival in older GC patients was less pronounced than in younger patients.20 This was attributed to the prevalence of age-related sarcopenia, which limits variation in skeletal muscle mass across the geriatric oncology population. These findings suggest that BMI alone may not adequately reflect nutritional or functional status in older patients, consistent with our observation that exercise had only a limited effect on underweight prevention in this group. Combining resistance training with adequate nutritional support may help mitigate sarcopenia and improve clinical outcomes in older patients, although further investigation is needed. Thus, dietary interventions alongside exercise may be required to achieve meaningful improvements in postoperative nutritional status in this population.

Smoking cessation was associated with a lower risk of underweight. A recent study suggested that smoking cessation leads to weight gain, partly due to the elimination of nicotine’s appetite-suppressing effect, a reduction in metabolic rate, and an increase in energy expenditure.21 In a community-based national cohort analysis of 16,663 individuals, those who quit smoking showed a significant increase in body weight and BMI compared with continuous smokers. However, adults who quit smoking had a substantially lower risk for death than those who continued, irrespective of weight and BMI gain. Importantly, this weight gain was not linked to an increased risk for cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, cancer, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, which are often associated with weight or BMI gains. Consequently, the study concluded that encouraging smoking cessation is advisable despite the potential risk for weight gain.21

In addition, a recent meta-analysis of prospective studies indicated that individuals who quit smoking gained weight up to 4.1 kg and BMI up to 1.1.22 Compared to those who continued smoking, the difference in weight gain was up to 2.6 kg. While the study reported no statistical difference between females and males in this regard, it noted that both weight and BMI tended to increase more significantly after quitting smoking in females than in males.22 This suggests that the metabolic effects of smoking might be more pronounced in females, a hypothesis supported by consistent results from animal experiments.

In our study, perioperative alcohol consumption was associated with a lower likelihood of postoperative underweight in both male and female patients, as well as in both young and older groups. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution. Alcohol consumption is often linked to excessive energy intake, which can lead to weight gain in some individuals.23,24 However, due to conflicting results from studies that have investigated various hormones and neurotransmitters related to alcohol, and limitations in addressing biases from factors such as alcohol intake and other lifestyle habits, no definitive conclusions can be made.23,25 Therefore, the recommendation to abstain from alcohol, in conjunction with maintaining a healthy lifestyle that avoids weight gain, remains crucial.

In clinical practice, patients are typically advised to avoid alcohol after stomach cancer surgery for several reasons. First, it can irritate the residual stomach lining, and the carbonation in alcoholic beverages may cause intestinal stretching and abdominal discomfort.26-29 In addition, the tendency to overeat while consuming alcohol complicates the maintenance of healthy eating habits, such as thorough chewing and eating slowly. Alcohol consumption is also linked to “dumping syndrome,” characterized by symptoms such as nausea, diarrhea, fatigue, and palpitations. Late-dumping syndrome, in particular, is caused by a rapid increase in blood sugar levels, which can be triggered by both the alcohol itself and the sugar content in many alcoholic drinks. One study found that consuming wine with a 15% alcohol content significantly increased gastric emptying time, leading to delayed gastric emptying.26 However, the literature provides mixed conclusions regarding the effects of alcohol on gastric emptying.23,30,31 Previous studies on postoperative digestive symptoms have primarily focused on dumping syndrome, while giving limited attention to other common manifestations such as abdominal pain, indigestion, and vomiting, which may result from diverse etiologies, including gastric stasis. Given the multifactorial nature of these symptoms, the findings from such studies are difficult to generalize. Future research using more rigorous and standardized designs is warranted to clarify the complex interplay of factors contributing to postoperative digestive symptoms.

Our study has several limitations. First, important clinical variables such as cancer stage, postoperative complications, and adjuvant chemotherapy were unavailable due to the nature of the data source, which may have influenced patients’ body weight. Although most adjuvant chemotherapy is completed within 6 months to 1 year after surgery, we defined the perioperative period as 2 years before and after surgery to account for this; nevertheless, residual chemotherapy effects cannot be fully excluded. Second, the study design limits the interpretation of correlations between different factors as causal relationships. A potential bias may exist whereby patients who regularly drink alcohol and exercise are inherently healthier and more socially active. Specifically, the observed inverse association between alcohol consumption and postoperative underweight may reflect survival bias rather than a true protective effect. Thus, this finding should be interpreted with caution and does not imply a recommendation for alcohol intake. Third, the binary classification of lifestyle factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption may oversimplify the effects of these behaviors. Although quantitative data on smoking and drinking amounts were available, the complexity of performing subgroup analyses by sex and age led us to defer detailed dose–response analyses to future research. Fourth, underweight status was assessed at a single postoperative time point without considering weight change trajectories or preoperative nutritional status. Patients who were underweight prior to surgery and those who became underweight afterward may differ substantially in clinical outcomes, which was not captured in the present analysis. Future studies should stratify patients by weight change patterns and preoperative nutritional profiles to evaluate more accurately their impact on prognosis and quality of life. Fifth, we did not directly examine the association between postoperative underweight and clinical outcomes such as survival or oncologic prognosis. Therefore, the extent to which underweight status influences long-term outcomes remains uncertain and warrants further investigation. Finally, as previously discussed, potential differences in physical activity and dietary habits between males and females were acknowledged, underscoring the need for further research to clarify sex-related differences. Despite these limitations, the strength of our study lies in being the first to analyze factors associated with underweight after GC surgery, including changes in smoking, drinking, and exercise habits, using extensive, nationwide data. Furthermore, we performed subgroup analyses by sex and age, recognizing the potential differences in physiological responses. The growing importance of sex-differentiated medicine highlights the relevance of such analyses, contributing to precision treatment and management in GC.

In this nationwide cohort study, we identified several modifiable lifestyle factors associated with postoperative underweight in GC patients. Smoking and lack of regular exercise were linked to an increased risk, particularly among younger patients and females, whereas perioperative alcohol consumption was associated with a lower risk. These findings highlight the importance of tailored lifestyle interventions during the perioperative period. Further research incorporating detailed nutritional intake and body composition measures is warranted to refine risk stratification and support personalized postoperative management.
