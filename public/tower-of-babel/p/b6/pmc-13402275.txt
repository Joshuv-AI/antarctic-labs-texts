Hepatitis C Virus Infection at the Start of Hemodialysis and During Early Follow-up: Evidence from Two Dialysis Centers
Authors: Mohammad Abedi Samakoosh, Mohamad Javad Bay, Abolfazl Kolbadinejad, Mehdi Karimi, Lotfollah Davoodi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHepatitis C virus (HCV) remains a concern in patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis due to repeated blood exposure and prolonged contact with dialysis environments. Although direct-acting antivirals have reduced HCV burden in many settings, uncertainty persists regarding early infection dynamics at dialysis initiation and during initial follow-up.MethodsThis observational study was conducted in two hemodialysis facilities in northern Iran (2022–2023). All adult patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis were included using a census-based approach. HCV status was assessed by anti-HCV antibody testing at dialysis initiation and repeated after three months as part of routine surveillance. Demographic and clinical variables, including dialysis duration and transfusion history, were extracted from medical records. Analyses focused on prevalence and associations with clinical characteristics.ResultsAmong 470 patients, six (1.28%) were anti-HCV positive at dialysis initiation. After three months, no new cases were identified; overall prevalence remained unchanged. No significant associations were observed between HCV status and demographic variables, transfusion history, or comorbidities. In contrast, dialysis duration showed a clear association with HCV positivity. Patients with more than ten years of dialysis had markedly higher prevalence than those with shorter durations (P < 0.001).ConclusionsIn this cohort, HCV prevalence at the start of dialysis was low and remained stable during short-term follow-up. Longer dialysis duration was the only factor associated with HCV positivity, supporting its role as a cumulative exposure marker. These findings emphasize the importance of sustained infection-control practices and continued surveillance, even in low-prevalence settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44197-026-00591-2.

Introduction

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) remains an unevenly distributed infection despite the substantial impact of direct-acting antivirals (DAAs) on its global burden. In chronic kidney disease, and particularly in patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis, the risk profile is shaped less by individual behavioral factors and more by repeated vascular access, frequent blood exposure, shared dialysis environments, and the cumulative healthcare-associated risk that accrues over time. Accordingly, contemporary nephrology guidelines emphasize dialysis units not only as sites of life-sustaining therapy, but also as high-priority settings where prevention strategies, structured surveillance, and timely linkage to antiviral treatment must be reliably implemented in routine practice rather than remaining aspirational [1–3].

What makes hemodialysis distinctive is not only a higher background prevalence than the general population but also the persistent risk of within-facility spread (Nosocomial transmission). While a pooled global HCV prevalence has been historically estimated at 24.3% [4], this figure is now considered a broad historical average that masks a significant downward trend. Contemporary data, particularly from the Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study (DOPPS), demonstrate that in many regions, prevalence is declining due to systematic screening and improved infection control [5]. This striking heterogeneity (Geographic heterogeneity) across regions and income strata is precisely what necessitates local epidemiological reporting to identify residual gaps in elimination efforts [6].

Large observational datasets point in the same direction: prevalence is declining in some settings, yet elimination is not guaranteed. Recent Dialysis Outcomes and Practice Patterns Study (DOPPS) data from China reported substantial declines in both HCV prevalence and incidence over the past decade, illustrating what is achievable when screening, staff training, and referral pathways are consistently implemented [7]. At the same time, reports from other health systems continue to document non-trivial burdens, including national dialysis-based estimates in Oman [8], post-DAA experiences across multiple Romanian centers [9], and recent data from Egypt in the context of an elimination program where gaps in treatment uptake can still matter [10].

The treatment landscape has also shifted faster than practice in many places. Evidence syntheses supporting the KDIGO 2022 update underline high DAA effectiveness and acceptable safety in advanced CKD and in patients receiving dialysis [11], while broader clinical guidance continues to emphasize simplified pathways and expanded access [12]. Micro-elimination efforts tailored to hemodialysis populations have reported encouraging real-world results, including nationwide programs in Taiwan [13] and large multi-stakeholder strategies embedded in national eradication plans [14].

Still, unanswered practical questions remain, such as how much positivity reflects imported infection at dialysis initiation versus early acquisition in-unit, and how reliably routine testing detects residual or atypical infection patterns, including occult HCV [15]. Against this background, center-level epidemiology that captures HCV status at the time of hemodialysis initiation and reassesses it during early follow-up can be particularly informative. Such an approach helps distinguish baseline infection burden from early incident acquisition within dialysis settings, while also offering a pragmatic view of how screening practices and infection-control routines perform in routine clinical care rather than under idealized conditions.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This study was conducted as a center-based observational investigation with a cross-sectional framework and a predefined short-term follow-up component. The work was carried out in two hemodialysis facilities in northern Iran: Razi Hospital in Qaemshahr and the Shahrvand Dialysis Center in Sari. Together, these centers provide maintenance hemodialysis services for a large and stable population of patients with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) and follow standardized national infection-control protocols.

Data collection took place over the course of 2022 and 2023 and was embedded in routine clinical care rather than a parallel research-only pathway, allowing the findings to reflect day-to-day practice under real-world conditions.

Study Population and Sampling Strategy

All adult patients receiving hemodialysis at the two participating centers during the study period were initially assessed for eligibility. A census-based approach was utilized to capture the full spectrum of the patient population and minimize selection bias. Patients were included if they required regular maintenance hemodialysis and had documented HCV serology at the time of dialysis initiation. To ensure the cohort strictly represented chronic hemodialysis, patients who recovered renal function (indicating temporary dialysis for acute kidney injury) were excluded. Additional exclusion criteria comprised incomplete baseline medical records, absent initial anti-HCV serology, or loss to follow-up before the three-month reassessment due to facility transfer, transition to peritoneal dialysis, kidney transplantation, or mortality. Applying these criteria yielded a final analytical cohort of 470 patients.

Data sources and Variable Definition

Data were systematically extracted using a standardized collection form (Supplementary File 1) via patient interviews, medical records, and laboratory reports to ensure cross-center consistency. Recorded variables encompassed demographics (age, sex, marital status, educational level, body weight) and clinical profiles, including age at dialysis initiation, dialysis duration, session frequency, transfusion history, and specific comorbidities (diabetes mellitus, hypertension, kidney stone disease, polycystic or congenital kidney disorders, prior transplantation, and immunosuppression). HCV status was established using anti-HCV ELISA at dialysis initiation and at the three-month follow-up. For seropositive patients, records were screened for prior antiviral therapies (interferon-based regimens or direct-acting antivirals); however, no such history was identified. Furthermore, specific behavioral risk factors, notably intravenous drug use (IVDU), were not systematically documented in routine clinical registries and therefore could not be evaluated.

Laboratory Assessment

HCV screening was performed using ELISA for anti-HCV antibodies, following standard laboratory protocols in use at the participating centers. Blood samples were collected via venipuncture before dialysis sessions and processed in certified clinical laboratories. In accordance with local infection-control policies, dialysis machines used in emergency settings or during off-hours were withheld from routine circulation until virologic screening confirmed the absence of transmissible viral infections, including HCV.

Regarding specific unit-level practices, patients with known anti-HCV positivity were dialyzed on dedicated machines, which underwent heat plus peracetic-acid chemical disinfection after each session. While the use of dedicated machines for HCV-positive patients is a debated practice and not explicitly mandated by some international guidelines, it remains a consistent part of our local safety protocol. More broadly, hemodialysis in our center followed WHO/CDC-standard infection-control procedures. Environmental surfaces were disinfected between patients with chlorine-based agents, and all consumables were strictly single-use. Newly admitted patients were screened for anti-HCV prior to initiation of dialysis, and all patients underwent routine serologic surveillance every six months. All staff received structured infection-control training annually, with ongoing onsite supervision to ensure adherence.

Outcome Measures

The primary outcome was the prevalence of HCV infection at the time of hemodialysis initiation and at three months of follow-up. Secondary analyses explored associations between HCV positivity and demographic or clinical variables, with particular attention to dialysis duration as a cumulative exposure marker.

Statistical Analysis

Collected data were coded and entered into a dedicated database and analyzed using SPSS software. Continuous variables were summarized using means and standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate. Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages.

Comparisons between groups were performed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests when expected cell counts were small. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the low prevalence of HCV infection in the cohort, analyses were interpreted cautiously, with emphasis placed on clinically meaningful patterns rather than isolated statistical significance.

Ethical Considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the relevant institutional authorities. All participants were informed about the purpose of data collection and the observational nature of the study. Participation was voluntary, and written informed consent was obtained. Patient confidentiality was strictly maintained throughout data handling, analysis, and reporting, with all results presented in aggregate form.

Methodological Considerations

This study was designed to reflect routine clinical practice rather than an experimental research environment. While serologic testing with ELISA represents standard screening in dialysis units, the absence of systematic HCV RNA testing is acknowledged as a methodological limitation and is addressed in the Discussion. Nonetheless, the approach provides a pragmatic and policy-relevant assessment of HCV burden as detected through standard surveillance pathways.

Results

During the study period, a total of 526 adult patients receiving hemodialysis across the two participating centers were initially assessed for eligibility. Of these, 56 patients were excluded from the final analysis for the following reasons: missing baseline anti-HCV serology or severely incomplete medical records (n = 16), transfer to another facility or transition to peritoneal dialysis (n = 12), all-cause mortality (n = 11), recovery of renal function indicating temporary dialysis (n = 9), and kidney transplantation prior to the 3‑month follow-up (n = 8). Consequently, a final cohort of 470 eligible patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis was included in the analysis. The flow of participant selection is presented in Fig. 1.

Among this final cohort, 6 individuals (1.28%) tested positive for anti-HCV antibodies prior to the initiation of their dialysis treatment. At the three‑month follow‑up, no incident cases were detected, resulting in a stable overall prevalence of 1.28% with zero seroconversions.

Demographic Overview

The cohort consisted of 271 males (57.7%) and 199 females (42.3%). Although five of the six anti‑HCV–positive patients were male, this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.409). None of the six anti‑HCV–positive patients had documented prior antiviral treatment with interferon-based regimens or direct‑acting antivirals.

Age distribution showed that most participants were between 60 and 79 years, which also accounted for half of the HCV‑positive cases. A numerically higher—but statistically non‑significant—prevalence was observed among individuals initiating dialysis before age 40 (4.35%; P = 0.094). Socioeconomic and physical characteristics, including marital status, educational attainment, and body weight, showed no significant association with HCV seropositivity. Clinical Factors and HCV Status

A total of 236 patients (50.21%) had a history of blood transfusion with a median of 3 units received (IQR: 1–5). However, HCV prevalence did not differ significantly between transfused and non‑transfused individuals (1.69% vs. 0.85%; P = 0.686). Similarly, major comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, polycystic kidney disease, and prior kidney transplantation, showed no significant association with HCV status. All six HCV‑positive patients were immunocompetent.

In contrast, dialysis vintage was the only clinical variable demonstrating a strong association with HCV infection. Patients on hemodialysis for more than ten years exhibited a substantially higher HCV prevalence (23.08%; 3/13 patients) compared with those with shorter dialysis durations (P < 0.001). This relationship remained unchanged at the three‑month follow‑up. Dialysis duration distribution is shown in Fig. 2, and relevant statistical comparisons are summarized in Table 2.

Discussion

Hepatitis C virus infection among patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis continues to reflect a combination of biological susceptibility and health-care–related exposures, rather than individual behavioral risks. In the present study, the overall prevalence of anti-HCV antibodies at the initiation of dialysis was notably low (1.28%), and no new cases were identified during the short-term follow-up. Concurrently, the pattern of association observed, particularly the robust link between infection and longer dialysis duration, aligns consistently with the epidemiological dynamics reported across diverse dialysis populations.

For several decades, HCV has been recognized as a critical concern in hemodialysis units. Earlier investigations consistently demonstrated that patients undergoing dialysis carried a substantially higher burden of HCV infection compared to the general population. This excess risk has been attributed primarily to repeated vascular access, frequent blood handling, and the cumulative exposure that accompanies long-term dialysis treatment. Even in geographical settings with otherwise low community prevalence, these facility-level factors have historically facilitated transmission within dialysis environments [16].

International data illustrate the profound heterogeneity of HCV prevalence among dialysis populations. Large-scale observational frameworks, including DOPPS, have reported anti-HCV prevalence rates ranging from single-digit percentages in parts of Europe to substantially higher burdens in several Middle Eastern and Asian nations [5, 17]. Such variation is largely a reflection of discrepancies in background population prevalence, regional transfusion safety standards, the rigor of infection-control practices, and routine screening protocols [18]. Against this backdrop, the low prevalence observed in our cohort likely signifies a combination of effective local infection-control measures and a low underlying population endemicity.

Importantly, the absence of newly detected infections during the follow-up period must be interpreted with caution. Substantial evidence indicates that ongoing transmission can remain undetected for extended periods, particularly when surveillance is exclusively reliant on antibody-based testing (ELISA). The prolonged asymptomatic phase of HCV infection and the immunological window period prior to seroconversion imply that short observation intervals may fail to capture early or subclinical incident cases [16, 19–22]. Therefore, a stable prevalence over a limited timeframe does not definitively equate to the absolute elimination of transmission risk.

Among the clinical variables scrutinized, the duration of dialysis emerged as the sole independent factor unequivocally associated with HCV positivity. In our cohort, patients subjected to hemodialysis for more than ten years accounted for half of all anti-HCV–positive cases, despite representing only a marginal fraction of the total study population. This finding is highly consistent with longstanding epidemiological evidence identifying dialysis vintage as a surrogate marker for cumulative nosocomial risk. Each additional year on dialysis implies repeated exposure to potential, albeit minor, breaches in infection control. Earlier multicenter studies demonstrated higher HCV prevalence among patients with prolonged dialysis histories, independent of their transfusion status, thereby emphasizing the dominant role of facility-level transmission [23–25]. More contemporary analyses continue to replicate these associations, even after adjusting for modern advancements in antiviral therapies [26].

Crucially, our analysis revealed no significant association between a history of blood transfusion and HCV seropositivity, despite half of the cohort (50.2%) having been exposed to blood products. To contextualize this finding, it is imperative to examine the contemporary epidemiological landscape in Iran. First, Iran is characterized as a low-endemicity region, with recent meta-analyses estimating the HCV prevalence in the general population at approximately 0.3% to 0.5% [27]. Second, the risk of transfusion-transmitted infections (TTIs) has reached a historical nadir in the country. Following the implementation of stringent donor selection criteria and advanced screening protocols, including third- and fourth-generation ELISA coupled with nucleic acid testing (NAT) in major centers, by the Iranian Blood Transfusion Organization (IBTO), the pooled prevalence of HCV among Iranian blood donors has plummeted to approximately 0.22% [28]. Third, a quantitative assessment of our data demonstrated that among transfused patients, the “transfusion burden” was relatively light, with a median of only 3 units (IQR: 1–5) received. This reflects a paradigm shift in the management of renal anemia, where the routine clinical adoption of erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) has drastically minimized the frequency and volume of required transfusions. Consequently, in the current era of optimized blood safety and widespread ESA utilization, sporadic blood transfusion is no longer the primary driver of HCV transmission in Iranian hemodialysis units.

Regarding the risk of transmission via dialysis equipment, evidence from the KDIGO 2022 Clinical Practice Guideline clarifies that HCV is not transmitted through the internal fluid pathways of the machine, as the dialyzer membrane and the pressure gradient act as effective barriers. Instead, the machine’s external surfaces, such as control panels and IV poles, serve as potential environmental reservoirs. Transmission in the dialysis unit is primarily attributed to breaches in standard precautions, particularly cross-contamination via the hands of healthcare personnel or inadequate disinfection of these shared surfaces between shifts [1, 29].

Concurrently, the broader epidemiological landscape in Iran has shifted, with intravenous drug use (IVDU) emerging as the predominant behavioral risk factor for HCV transmission. Recent national estimates report an HCV prevalence ranging from 26% to 47% among people who inject drugs [30, 31]. While IVDU is undoubtedly a critical vector, our reliance on routine clinical registries precluded the systematic evaluation of individual behavioral histories. Thus, the potential contribution of IVDU to the HCV burden in our cohort remains an unmeasured confounder and a recognized limitation of this registry-based approach.

Nevertheless, transmission within dialysis units remains influenced by routine intradialytic processes. Shared equipment, environmental surfaces, and repeated contact with blood create opportunities for viral spread when infection-control standards are inconsistently applied. Systematic reviews continue to identify lapses in hand hygiene, inadequate environmental disinfection, and the improper handling of supplies as persistent contributors to HCV transmission in dialysis facilities [32]. Molecular phylogenetic investigations have further demonstrated that outbreaks can often be traced back to specific unit-level operational failures, reinforcing the necessity for sustained vigilance.

Demographic characteristics showed no meaningful association with HCV status in this study. While earlier epidemiological reports occasionally suggested potential relationships with variables such as age, sex, or socioeconomic status, contemporary evidence increasingly indicates that HCV acquisition in hemodialysis settings is driven predominantly by healthcare-associated and environmental exposures rather than individual demographic profiles [33, 34]. Repeated vascular access, shared clinical environments, handling of injectable medications, and the potential for cross-contamination through healthcare personnel or inadequately disinfected surfaces remain the principal mechanisms underlying transmission within dialysis units. This framework also explains why dialysis duration emerged as the strongest correlate of HCV positivity in our cohort, as prolonged dialysis exposure likely reflects cumulative contact with these subtle but repetitive procedural risks. Regardless of demographic background, HCV infection in dialysis populations continues to be associated with adverse clinical outcomes, including increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, reinforcing the importance of sustained infection-control vigilance and routine surveillance [24, 35].

The broader implications of HCV infection in ESRD extend beyond mere prevalence estimates. Infected patients remain at elevated risk for progressive hepatic fibrosis and experience poorer overall survival compared to uninfected peers across multiple settings [6]. These outcomes underscore why HCV remains a central focus of both nephrology and infectious disease guidelines. Current recommendations emphasize regular screening cadence and timely linkage to antiviral therapy. Although antibody testing remains highly practical for routine surveillance, its inherent limitations in detecting early or occult infections should be structurally recognized [16].

The advent of highly effective DAAs has fundamentally reshaped the management of HCV in dialysis populations. High sustained virologic response (SVR) rates across all genotypes now render micro-elimination within dialysis units a highly feasible public health goal. Nevertheless, therapeutic strategies must be rigorously paired with preventive efforts. Antiviral therapy alone cannot sustainably compensate for systemic deficiencies in infection-control practices where environmental exposure risks persist.

From a broader public health perspective, the present findings carry implications that extend beyond the participating dialysis centers. The low baseline prevalence of anti-HCV positivity and the absence of early seroconversion suggest that structured infection-control strategies, when consistently implemented, may substantially reduce ongoing HCV transmission within hemodialysis settings, even in resource-constrained environments [5, 7]. This observation is particularly relevant in the context of the World Health Organization’s goal of viral hepatitis elimination by 2030, which identifies high-risk healthcare environments, including dialysis facilities, as critical targets for intervention [36]. Maintaining low transmission rates in dialysis populations is essential not only for individual patient outcomes but also for reducing the long-term healthcare burden associated with chronic liver disease, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and transplantation-related complications [6, 24, 35].

At the operational level, the findings reinforce the importance of sustained surveillance programs in dialysis units. Although modern direct-acting antivirals have transformed HCV treatment outcomes, therapeutic availability alone cannot replace strict adherence to infection-prevention measures [1, 11]. Continuous staff education, standardized environmental disinfection, routine serologic monitoring, careful handling of injectable medications, and periodic auditing of infection-control compliance remain fundamental components of safe dialysis practice [29, 32, 34]. Importantly, the strong association between prolonged dialysis duration and HCV positivity observed in our cohort supports the concept that cumulative healthcare exposure continues to represent a measurable risk despite contemporary advances in care [5, 23, 26].

The study also highlights several policy-relevant considerations for dialysis systems in low-prevalence regions. First, maintaining universal screening strategies at dialysis initiation and during follow-up remains justified, even when prevalence appears low, because isolated transmission events may still occur and remain clinically silent for prolonged periods [3, 12, 36]. Second, integration of HCV elimination initiatives into nephrology care pathways may facilitate earlier diagnosis and timely referral for antiviral therapy [3, 12]. Third, greater incorporation of molecular testing strategies, particularly HCV RNA–based surveillance in selected high-risk settings, could further strengthen early detection capacities and improve micro-elimination efforts within dialysis populations [13–15]. Future multicenter longitudinal studies incorporating longer follow-up periods and virologic testing would help clarify residual transmission dynamics and guide evidence-based policy refinement.

Several methodological limitations of this study should be explicitly acknowledged. First, the inherently low number of HCV-positive cases constrained the statistical power, potentially obscuring weaker associations with secondary clinical or demographic variables (Type II error). Second, the follow-up period was relatively brief, restricting our capacity to detect delayed seroconversions. Hemodialysis patients, due to varying degrees of uremia-induced immunosuppression, may require several months to mount detectable anti-HCV antibodies post-exposure. Sypsa et al. reported an average seroconversion time of approximately five months in this specific population, suggesting that our three-month window is insufficient to confidently exclude all incident infections [37]. Consequently, the absence of newly detected cases in this study should be interpreted as a lack of evidence for early transmission, rather than an absolute confirmation of zero incidence. Future studies incorporating longer longitudinal follow-ups extending between 6 and 12 months, combined with routine nucleic acid testing (HCV RNA PCR), would yield a more definitive assessment of occult and incident infections.

Despite these constraints, the present findings provide a robust, pragmatic snapshot of HCV epidemiology in routine dialysis practice within northern Iran, ultimately highlighting cumulative dialysis duration as the most persistent and clinically relevant marker of risk.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the prevalence of anti-HCV positivity among patients initiating maintenance hemodialysis in our cohort was low and remained stable during early follow-up, with no detected seroconversions over three months. Dialysis duration emerged as the principal factor associated with HCV positivity, supporting its role as a marker of cumulative healthcare-related exposure. Although these findings suggest effective infection-control implementation within the participating centers, continued vigilance remains essential because short-term serologic stability does not exclude delayed or occult transmission. Sustained surveillance, adherence to standardized infection-prevention practices, and integration of HCV elimination strategies into dialysis care pathways remain critical to maintaining low transmission rates and advancing public health goals for viral hepatitis elimination.
