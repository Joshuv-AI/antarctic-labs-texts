Lipid accumulation product and visceral adiposity index in polycystic ovary syndrome: a systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies
Authors: Mohammadamin Parsaei, Elaheh Karimi, Amirali Barkhordarioon, Mohammadreza Yousefi, Azadeh Tarafdari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a prevalent endocrine disorder associated with reproductive and metabolic dysfunction. Emerging evidence indicates that the lipid accumulation product (LAP) and visceral adiposity index (VAI) could be valuable indicators for evaluating metabolic dysfunction. This study aims to compare LAP and VAI between women with PCOS and healthy controls to evaluate their potential role in metabolic risk assessment.MethodsA structured database search was carried out on March 18, 2025, using PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase. Observational studies comparing LAP/VAI levels between women with PCOS and healthy controls were included. Risk of bias was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklists. A random-effects meta-analysis was carried out to evaluate the pooled differences in LAP and VAI levels between individuals with PCOS and those in the control group. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on the PCOS phenotypes (A, B, C, and D) defined by the Rotterdam criteria and the body mass index categories (lean and overweight/obese).ResultsFifty studies (13,741 women) were analyzed, with 41 included in the meta-analysis. LAP (32 studies: Cohen’s d = 0.597 [0.469–0.726]; P-value < 0.001; I2 = 88.22%) and VAI (28 studies: Cohen’s d = 0.457 [0.378–0.537]; P-value < 0.001; I2 = 65.16%) were significantly higher in PCOS women, with calculated pooled values of 37.1 vs. 23.9 and 3.1 vs. 2.6, respectively. Subgroup analyses revealed that LAP lost significance across all phenotypes (P-value > 0.05), while VAI remained elevated in phenotypes A (P-value < 0.001), B (P-value = 0.001), and D (P-value = 0.001), with marginal significance in C (P-value = 0.051). Stratified by body mass index, LAP was non-significant in lean women (P-value = 0.083) but higher in overweight/obese women (P-value = 0.001). Conversely, VAI was significantly elevated in both lean and overweight/obese groups (P-value = 0.001 each).ConclusionsThis study demonstrates significantly higher levels of LAP and VAI in patients with PCOS compared to controls. These readily calculable indices may serve as practical tools for metabolic risk stratification in PCOS populations. However, further research is needed to establish their diagnostic accuracy and validate their clinical utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02719-y.Keywords: Insulin resistance, Lipid metabolism, Metabolic syndrome, Polycystic ovary syndrome, Endocrinology, Visceral Fat

Background

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most common endocrine conditions among women of reproductive age, with an estimated global prevalence of 9.2% in 2024 [1], characterized by ovarian dysfunction, hyperandrogenism, and polycystic ovarian morphology [2]. In addition to its well-known reproductive symptoms, such as irregular menstrual cycles, anovulation, and infertility, PCOS is strongly linked to metabolic disturbances, including insulin resistance (IR), obesity, and heightened risks of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease [3–6]. Despite its prevalence, diagnosis is often delayed; up to 75% of women in the U.S. remain undiagnosed, and about one in four experience delays of two years or more [7–9].

IR is a key pathophysiologic feature of PCOS, affecting approximately 75% of women, regardless of body mass index (BMI) [10]. The presence of IR, along with the body’s compensatory hyperinsulinemia, disrupts normal ovarian signaling and alters the balance between myo-inositol and d-chiro-inositol. These imbalances contribute to increased androgen production, cycle irregularities, and subfertility [11]. IR is also closely associated with the accumulation of visceral fat, which fuels chronic low-grade inflammation, worsens insulin sensitivity, and increases the overall cardiometabolic burden [12, 13]. Notably, visceral adiposity reflects abnormal fat distribution and shows a strong link to cardiometabolic risk, even among women with a normal BMI [14].

Although widely used, traditional anthropometric measures like BMI, waist circumference (WC), waist-to-hip ratio, and waist-to-height ratio cannot distinguish between visceral and subcutaneous fat or reflect adipose function. To address these limitations, surrogate indices such as the visceral adiposity index (VAI) and lipid accumulation product (LAP) were developed. These indices combine clinical and biochemical data, offering an accessible and efficient way to approximate visceral fat activity and insulin sensitivity. VAI incorporates WC, BMI, triglyceride (Tg), and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol levels, and has been associated with multiple PCOS-related traits, including IR, ovulatory dysfunction, androgen excess, cardiometabolic risk, and chronic inflammation [15–17]. LAP, likewise, reflects underlying metabolic abnormalities and may be especially useful for identifying early IR and cardiometabolic risk in lean women with PCOS [18, 19].

Given the strong overlap between PCOS and metabolic disorders, tools like LAP and VAI have gained attention as possible surrogate markers for metabolic dysfunction. Since they rely on simple, routinely collected parameters, these indices may help screen and identify women with PCOS who are at elevated risk for metabolic complications, particularly in resource-limited settings. However, despite growing interest, no comprehensive meta-analysis has systematically compared LAP and VAI levels between women with PCOS and healthy controls, nor evaluated their variation across different PCOS phenotypes and BMI categories. Therefore, the present study hypothesizes that women with PCOS exhibit significantly higher LAP and VAI values compared to controls, and that these indices can provide additional insight into metabolic risk stratification across phenotypic and BMI subgroups. By addressing this gap, the study contributes novel evidence regarding the potential clinical utility of LAP and VAI as adjunct diagnostic and risk-prediction tools in PCOS.

Methods

Review protocol

This study was performed in strict adherence to the guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 [20] and Meta-Analyses of Observational Studies (MOOSE) [21]. The review protocol was also prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under the registration number CRD420251037083.

Research question

The review question was formulated following the Patient, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome (PICO) framework [22]:

All eligible observational studies with the following designs were included: prospective and retrospective cohorts, cross-sectional, and case–control studies.

Search strategy

To locate eligible publications, systematic searches were undertaken in PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, and Embase on March 18, 2025. The search strategy incorporated keywords related to "PCOS," "LAP," and "VAI," with detailed search syntax and applied filters for each database documented in Supplementary Table 1. To ensure comprehensive coverage, manual screening of reference lists from included studies was performed, and a supplementary search of the first ten pages in Google Scholar using identical search terms was conducted to identify potentially relevant articles not captured in the primary database search.

The exclusion criteria encompassed: Studies that did not measure either LAP or VAI indices, studies that did not include patients diagnosed with PCOS, studies that did not have a control group of women without PCOS, non-human studies, interventional studies, records that were not available in full-text English, and review articles, meta-analyses, case reports, case series, letters, editorials, correspondence, book chapters, or conference abstracts.

Study selection

The retrieved records from each database were exported to EndNote™ version 20.3 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA) for management. Following duplicate removal, preliminary screening was conducted independently by two investigators, who evaluated titles and abstracts against the eligibility criteria. Records meeting exclusion criteria were eliminated, while potentially relevant publications progressed to full-text assessment and were assessed against the eligibility criteria. Subsequently, the research team systematically examined reference lists of all included studies to identify publications potentially missed during database searches. Moreover, both reviewers performed an independent manual search of Google Scholar. Discrepancies in study selection were resolved through iterative discussion, with unresolved conflicts adjudicated by a third senior researcher.

Data extraction

Two reviewers carried out the data extraction process independently; any discrepancies were resolved by consensus with a third reviewer. The extracted data included: first author's name, publication year, country of origin, study design, study duration, PCOS diagnostic criteria, sample size, age, and BMI of cases and controls, matched covariates, and reported LAP and VAI values for each group. Categorical variables were recorded as frequencies, while continuous variables were extracted as mean ± standard deviation. For studies reporting medians with ranges or interquartile ranges, these values were converted to means and standard deviations using validated methods from the literature [25, 26].

Risk of bias assessment

Two independent reviewers assessed the risk of bias in included studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklists for case–control studies [27]. This 10-item tool assesses methodological quality through responses of "Yes," "No," "Unclear," or "Not applicable" for each criterion. Studies were classified as "Include" if they received ≥ 6 "Yes" responses, "Exclude" if they failed to meet this threshold, or "Seek further information" if they contained incomplete data requiring design clarification. To ensure methodological rigor in the meta-analysis, only studies meeting the "Include" threshold were selected for final inclusion.

Data synthesis

Meta-analysis

Two sets of separate meta-analyses were performed to calculate the pooled LAP and VAI values of patients with PCOS and control groups and to compare these values between them. Two independent reviewers conducted comprehensive data extraction using standardized forms, with any discrepancies resolved through consensus or third-party adjudication. For quantitative synthesis, random-effects models were implemented using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis version 3.3.070 (Biostat, Inc., NJ, USA) to generate pooled estimates of LAP and VAI with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each group.

Between-group comparisons were performed in STATA version 18.0 (Stata Corp. LLC, TX, USA), employing a random-effects model, using standardized mean differences (Cohen's d) calculated via restricted maximum likelihood estimation (REML), with statistical significance set at theta P-value < 0.05. Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q and I2 statistics, with I2 > 50% indicating substantial heterogeneity.

Publication bias assessment

Potential publication bias was evaluated using Egger's regression test for small-study effects, with statistical significance (P < 0.05) indicating the presence of publication bias [28]. When evidence of publication bias was identified, the trim-and-fill method was applied. This nonparametric approach estimates and incorporates theoretically missing studies to adjust the pooled effect size [29]. Funnel plots were also generated for visual assessment of asymmetry across included studies, offering complementary insight into potential publication bias.

Sensitivity analysis

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was applied to ensure the stability of findings, with funnel plots constructed to visualize pooled effect sizes and theta P-values after the stepwise removal of individual studies.

Subgroup analysis

A subgroup meta-analysis was planned to compare LAP and VAI levels between different PCOS phenotypes and control groups. PCOS phenotypes were categorized according to the Rotterdam criteria based on the presence or absence of the three diagnostic components: (1) The classic form, Phenotype A, manifests with all three components—hyperandrogenism, menstrual irregularities due to oligo- or anovulation, and polycystic ovaries visible on ultrasound; (2) Phenotype B, exhibiting hyperandrogenism and oligo-/anovulation but without polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound; (3) Phenotype C, demonstrating hyperandrogenism and polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound but with preserved ovulatory function; and (4) Phenotype D, characterized by oligo-/anovulation and polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound but lacking hyperandrogenism [30, 31].

Additionally, LAP and VAI levels between PCOS and control groups were compared after stratifying participants by BMI into lean (BMI < 25 kg/m2) and overweight/obese (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m2) subgroups.

To further explore potential sources of heterogeneity, four subgroup analyses were conducted to compare LAP and VAI levels between PCOS and control groups: (1) studies with and without age-matched participants, (2) studies with and without BMI-matched participants, and (3 and 4) studies stratified by geographical region and publication year.

Results

Search selection

The systematic search identified 498 records initially. Following duplicate removal, 171 unique records underwent title and abstract screening against the predefined exclusion criteria. This process excluded 119 records, leaving 52 articles for full-text evaluation. During full-text assessment, four studies were excluded due to incomplete LAP/VAI data reporting [32–35], and one study was excluded because of sample overlap with another included study [36, 37], resulting in 47 eligible articles [15, 16, 37–81]. Additional manual screening of reference lists and a Google Scholar search yielded three further eligible studies [82–84], culminating in a final inclusion of 50 articles in the review [15, 16, 37–84] (Tables 1 and 2). Study characteristics

Geographical and publication time trend

The geographical distribution of the included studies was as follows: Middle East (n = 16): [44, 46, 50, 52, 53, 55, 62, 64, 66–68, 72, 75, 79, 83, 84], Europe (n = 12) [39, 43, 45, 47, 49, 57–59, 69, 70, 73, 81], South America (n = 11) [15, 37, 38, 40–42, 48, 54, 61, 76, 77], Asia (n = 10) [16, 51, 60, 63, 65, 71, 74, 78, 80, 82], and Africa (n = 1) [56]. Of the 50 studies, 28 were published before 2020 [15, 16, 38–62, 82], while the remaining 22 were published in or after 2020 [37, 63–81, 83, 84].

Designs

All 50 included studies employed a case–control design, with four studies [69, 76, 78, 79] utilizing retrospective medical record review for data collection, while the remaining 46 collected data cross-sectionally [15, 16, 37–68, 70–75, 77, 80–84]. The majority of studies (n = 40) were conducted at single centers [16, 37–43, 45, 47–56, 58–60, 62, 63, 65–70, 73–79, 81, 83, 84], with four being two-center studies [15, 57, 61, 64], one three-center study [71], and five population-based multicenter studies [44, 46, 72, 80, 82].

Populations

The included studies comprised sample sizes ranging from 66 to 2,034 participants, aggregating to 13,741 individuals (7,199 women with PCOS; 6,542 controls). Diagnostic criteria predominately relied on the Rotterdam criteria [85] (n = 44) [15, 16, 37–42, 44–47, 49–51, 53–69, 71, 72, 74–76, 78–84], while others employed: a combination of Rotterdam criteria [85] and 2018 International Guidelines [86] (n = 3) [70, 73, 77], National Institutes of Health guidelines (n = 1) [48], Androgen Excess Society criteria [87] (n = 1) [43], or androgen excess–polycystic ovary syndrome criteria (n = 1) [52]. Regarding sample matching, 23 studies did not report matching methodology [15, 37, 41, 44–46, 48, 52, 54, 56, 60, 61, 66, 68, 69, 71–75, 79, 82, 83], while others matched participants for: age alone (n = 8) [40, 50, 51, 59, 62, 63, 78, 84], BMI alone (n = 3) [47, 76, 77], both age and BMI (n = 14) [16, 38, 39, 42, 43, 53, 55, 57, 58, 64, 65, 67, 70, 80], or age/BMI/ethnicity (n = 2) [49, 81]. Moreover, one included article exclusively studies women affected with type-1 diabetes mellitus with and without PCOS [43].

Outcomes

Among the 50 included studies, 17 exclusively compared the LAP of women with PCOS against controls [38, 40–42, 44, 45, 47, 50, 54–56, 59, 61–63, 65, 79], 12 exclusively assessed VAI [16, 39, 43, 51–53, 68, 69, 74, 81, 82, 84], and 21 evaluated both LAP and VAI [15, 37, 46, 48, 49, 57, 58, 60, 64, 66, 67, 70–73, 75–78, 80, 83].

Review of findings

Among studies that evaluated LAP in PCOS and control groups, 28 reported significantly higher values in PCOS patients [15, 37, 38, 41, 45–49, 55, 57, 59–65, 67, 71, 75, 76, 78–80, 83], while five found no significant differences [42, 44, 58, 70, 77]. Five studies only compared PCOS subgroups with controls: Mario et al. (2012) [40] reported significantly higher LAP levels in anovulatory PCOS patients compared to healthy controls, but no difference was observed between ovulatory PCOS patients and controls. Farhadi-Azar et al. (2022) [72] found no significant differences in LAP values between any PCOS phenotype (A, B, C, or D) and controls. Two studies [50, 66] demonstrated that while LAP did not differ significantly between lean PCOS patients and lean controls, overweight PCOS patients had higher LAP values than overweight controls. In contrast, Dawood et al. (2018) [56] reported that both lean and overweight PCOS patients had higher LAP values compared to lean controls. Kałużna et al. (2022) [73] observed no significant differences in LAP between PCOS and control groups when participants had metabolic syndrome. One study did not perform direct comparisons between PCOS and control groups [54] (Table 1). Detailed information on matched confounders and continuous LAP values for both PCOS and control groups is available in Supplementary Table 2.

Among studies evaluating VAI in PCOS and control groups, 21 reported significantly higher values in PCOS patients [15, 16, 37, 43, 48, 49, 51, 57, 60, 64, 67, 69, 71, 74–78, 80, 81, 84], while eight found no significant differences [46, 52, 53, 58, 68, 70, 82, 83]. Four studies only compared PCOS subgroups with controls: Farhadi-Azar et al. (2022) [72] found significantly higher VAI values in PCOS patients with phenotypes A and B compared to controls, but no differences for phenotype C or D. Similarly, Amato et al. (2011) [39] reported elevated VAI in A, B, and D phenotypes versus controls, with no difference observed for phenotype C. Kałużna et al. (2022) [73] found significantly higher VAI levels in both PCOS and control groups when participants had metabolic syndrome. Finally, Başar Gökcen et al. (2021) [66] demonstrated that while VAI did not differ between lean PCOS patients and lean controls, overweight PCOS patients showed higher values than overweight controls (Table 2). Comprehensive information on matched confounders and continuous VAI values for both PCOS and control groups is provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Risk of bias assessment

To maintain rigorous quality evaluation, the JBI Clinical Appraisal Checklist for case–control studies was applied to all included articles. This standardized tool allowed for a comprehensive evaluation of methodological quality across the studies, considering their designs. Following assessment, 41 studies met the quality criteria and were included in the meta-analysis [15, 37–41, 43, 44, 46–55, 57–63, 65–67, 69–74, 76–78, 80, 82–84], while nine studies were excluded from the meta-analysis due to a high overall risk of bias [16, 42, 45, 56, 64, 68, 75, 79, 81]. The primary sources of bias identified across studies pertained to inadequate baseline comparability between cases and controls, insufficient matching techniques, and failure to properly identify and address confounding factors in their analyses. Meta-analysis

LAP

Pooled data from 32 studies, analyzed using a random-effects model [37, 38, 40, 41, 44, 46, 48–50, 54, 55, 57–59, 61–63, 65–67, 70–73, 76–78, 80, 83], revealed a mean LAP level of 37.1 (95% CI: 34.2, 40.0) in women with PCOS compared to 23.9 (95% CI: 20.4, 27.5) in controls. A comparison of the pooled estimates demonstrated a moderate-to-large effect size for higher LAP levels in women with PCOS (Cohen’s d = 0.597 [95% CI: 0.469, 0.726]; P-value < 0.001), with substantial heterogeneity observed across studies (I2 = 88.22%) (Fig. 2). Egger’s test indicated no significant evidence of publication bias across the included studies (P-value = 0.392). Additionally, a funnel plot was generated to visually assess potential publication bias, as presented in Fig. 3.

Given the significant heterogeneity across the analyzed studies, a leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the robustness of the findings. The results indicated that the pooled effect size (Cohen’s d) remained stable, ranging from 0.553 to 0.618 upon sequential exclusion of individual studies, while maintaining statistical significance (P-value < 0.001) (Fig. 4).

Forest plot for the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis for comparing LAP values between different PCOS and controls

Subgroup analysis on comparing LAP values of different PCOS phenotypes with controls found no significant differences in any of the A (Cohen’s d = 0.043 [95% CI: −0.087, 0.173]; P-value = 0.517, I2 = 13.98%) [47, 58, 80], B (Cohen’s d = 0.345 [95% CI: −0.109, 0.799]; P-value = 0.136, I2 = 78.55%) [47, 58, 80], C (Cohen’s d = 0.000 [95% CI: −0.937, 0.936]; P-value = 0.999, I2 = 75.14%) [46, 47, 58, 80], or D (Cohen’s d = 0.658 [95% CI: −0.497, 1.813]; P-value = 0.264, I2 = 41.39%) [46, 47, 58, 80] phenotypes and controls (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Furthermore, subgroup analysis stratified by BMI categories revealed that LAP levels did not differ significantly between PCOS and control groups in lean populations (Cohen’s d = 0.395 [95% CI: −0.052, 0.841]; P-value = 0.083, I2 = 70.13%) [50, 62, 66, 76]. In contrast, among overweight/obese individuals, LAP levels were significantly higher in the PCOS group compared to controls (Cohen’s d = 0.591 [95% CI: 0.236, 0.946]; P-value = 0.001, I2 = 43.88%) [50, 66, 76] (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Subgroup analyses stratified by matching status demonstrated no statistically significant differences in pooled LAP levels between women with PCOS and controls. The effect sizes were comparable between age-matched studies (Cohen's d = 0.544 [95% CI: 0.416, 0.671], I2 = 92.64%) and non-age-matched studies (Cohen's d = 0.625 [95% CI: 0.412, 0.838], I2 = 65.58%), with no significant between-group heterogeneity (P-value = 0.523; Supplementary Fig. 3). Similarly, analyses stratified by BMI matching status revealed comparable effect sizes between BMI-matched (Cohen's d = 0.597 [95% CI: 0.469, 0.726], I2 = 85.07%) and non-BMI-matched studies (Cohen's d = 0.605 [95% CI: 0.458, 0.752], I2 = 90.93%), with no significant between-group differences (P-value = 0.877; Supplementary Fig. 4).

Geographical subgroup analysis revealed the following pooled effect sizes for LAP levels in PCOS versus controls: Middle East (Cohen's d = 0.469 [95% CI: 0.234, 0.703], I2 = 86.09%), Europe (Cohen's d = 0.600 [95% CI: 0.212, 0.988], I2 = 94.45%), South America (Cohen's d = 0.672 [95% CI: 0.512, 0.832], I2 = 55.18%), and Asia (Cohen's d = 0.658 [95% CI: 0.382, 0.935], I2 = 89.48%). No significant regional heterogeneity was observed (P-value = 0.555; Supplementary Fig. 5). Furthermore, temporal subgroup analysis comparing studies published before 2020 (Cohen's d = 0.682 [95% CI: 0.493, 0.872], I2 = 87.55%) and after 2020 (Cohen's d = 0.481 [95% CI: 0.332, 0.630], I2 = 82.91%) showed no statistically significant differences (P-value = 0.101; Supplementary Fig. 6).

VAI

A random-effects meta-analysis of 28 studies revealed significantly higher pooled VAI values in the PCOS group (3.1 [95% CI: 2.7, 3.5]) compared to controls (2.6 [95% CI: 2.3, 3.0]) [15, 37, 39, 43, 46, 48, 49, 51–53, 57, 58, 60, 66, 67, 69–74, 76–78, 80, 82–84]. The between-group difference showed a small-to-medium effect size (Cohen's d = 0.457 [95% CI: 0.378, 0.537]; P-value < 0.001), with substantial between-study heterogeneity (I2 = 65.16%) (Fig. 5). Publication bias assessment using Egger's test showed no significant evidence of bias (P-value = 0.187), supported by visual inspection of the funnel plot (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Women with PCOS exhibit significantly higher LAP and VAI values than healthy controls. This indicates that these indices could be useful in evaluating metabolic dysfunction, particularly IR, which is a central component of PCOS. Their application may help identify individuals with PCOS who are at greater risk of developing metabolic complications. Notably, although the effect size for the difference in LAP between PCOS and control groups was larger than that for VAI, subgroup analysis, which was stratified by different PCOS phenotypes and BMI categories, revealed that VAI retained its statistical significance in nearly all analyses. In contrast, LAP failed to show significant differences between PCOS and controls, except in the overweight/obese subgroup. Additionally, the results from the leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrated greater consistency for VAI compared to LAP. These findings suggest that while the magnitude of the difference between PCOS and control groups may not be more pronounced for VAI, it may serve as a more reliable biomarker for PCOS across different subtypes and in patient populations with varying metabolic statuses.

The elevation of LAP and VAI in women diagnosed with PCOS emphasizes their clinical usefulness in assessing and categorizing metabolic risk. Particularly noteworthy is VAI's ability to remain a significant marker not only in women with obesity but also in those who are lean, an often-overlooked group when evaluations are based solely on BMI [88]. This broad applicability is reinforced by de Medeiros et al. (2021) [89], who confirmed VAI's reliability in both lean and overweight/obese subgroups, and Oh et al. (2013) [90], who validated VAI as a practical alternative to computed tomography for visceral fat estimation. Similarly, LAP shows robust diagnostic performance, with Wehr et al. (2011) [32] first establishing its role in detecting glucose intolerance, and Hosseinpanah et al. (2013) [44] demonstrating superior IR detection accuracy, and multiple studies confirming its cardiovascular risk prediction value [50, 88, 91].

These findings align with growing evidence linking visceral adiposity to PCOS pathogenesis. As shown by Zheng et al. (2016) [91] and Kabir et al. (2005) [92], PCOS patients typically exhibit greater visceral fat despite comparable total adiposity, driving metabolic dysfunction. Both indices outperform traditional measures by correlating more strongly with key metabolic markers (such as insulin, such as insulin levels, Homeostatic Model Assessment for Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol) [91] and showing superior sensitivity for IR detection [46]. Their clinical relevance is further evidenced by consistent associations with metabolic syndrome features [51, 93–97], inflammatory markers, and early vascular changes [77, 98]. Notably, VAI's ability to identify metabolically unhealthy phenotypes even in normal-weight women highlights its unique value in comprehensive PCOS management [53, 99].

When comparing LAP and VAI to commonly used IR indicators like HOMA-IR or the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, several advantages become evident. While HOMA-IR requires fasting insulin levels, making it less practical for routine testing, TyG indices are easier to compute but do not factor in physical measurements. LAP and VAI integrate both lipid values and body metrics, offering a more holistic view of cardiometabolic health [100, 101]. This practical blend makes LAP and VAI appealing in clinical settings, especially since they can be calculated from routine health data. Recent investigations have rated these indices higher than BMI, WC, or waist-to-hip ratio when it comes to predicting IR and metabolic syndrome in PCOS [46, 88]. Nevertheless, traditional metrics still play a role. Previous studies confirm that BMI remains a strong predictor of cardiovascular mortality on a population level, although it lacks precision in assessing fat distribution [51, 102–104]. WC has also demonstrated strong predictive power for visceral obesity, particularly in young women, and shows comparable accuracy to BMI in assessing body fat [33, 104]. Age- and ethnicity-specific WC thresholds, such as 80.0 cm for younger and 85.0 cm for older women with PCOS, have been proposed, though variability in anatomical measurement sites (e.g., World Health Organization vs. National Institute of Health recommendations) highlights the need for standardized protocols [105, 106]. WC is particularly useful when BMI is below 35 kg/m2 [105]. Similarly, the waist-to-height ratio has gained recognition as a strong predictor of total body fat, visceral adipose tissue, and cardiometabolic risk, often outperforming BMI and WC in both sexes [107, 108]. The prior studies support the waist-to-height ratio cut-offs around 0.59 for visceral obesity prediction [109]. Despite their utility, these traditional indices often fall short of capturing the full metabolic complexity observed in PCOS, further underscoring the added value of composite indices like LAP and VAI [107, 110].

The effectiveness of LAP and VAI in identifying PCOS-related risk appears deeply rooted in the biological mechanisms of metabolic disturbance in this syndrome. Visceral adipose tissue exhibits markedly greater lipolytic activity than subcutaneous fat, resulting in excessive free fatty acid release into circulation [111]. Through portal vein delivery, these free fatty acids drive hepatic lipid accumulation while simultaneously impairing hepatic insulin signaling [111]. The resulting ectopic fat deposition and IR are further compounded by free fatty acids’ inhibitory effects on insulin-mediated glucose uptake in peripheral tissues [111]. In addition, visceral fat acts hormonally by secreting inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α and IL-6, that interfere with insulin pathways and maintain persistent low-grade inflammation [112]. This adipose dysfunction also stimulates hepatic triglyceride overproduction, exacerbating the characteristic dyslipidemia of PCOS [113]. Collectively, these mechanisms represent core pathophysiological metabolic features of PCOS that LAP and VAI effectively quantify [39, 91, 114, 115].

By combining lipid profiles with anthropometric data, LAP and VAI serve as practical reflections of these underlying disturbances. These indices not only quantify fat accumulation but also reveal hormonal imbalances associated with PCOS. Both LAP and VAI have been positively linked to elevated androgens, IR, and inflammatory cytokines, suggesting that they reflect a broader network of endocrine and metabolic dysfunction [24, 88, 116, 117]. VAI, in particular, has shown associations with disrupted menstrual cycles, lower sex hormone–binding globulin levels, and ovulatory dysfunction, even in women without obesity, underscoring its relevance in both metabolic and reproductive domains [49, 74, 115].

The strengths of this study lie in its comprehensive and systematic search across multiple databases, which enhances the reliability of its findings. Additionally, the use of a robust analytic approach, including sensitivity analysis and multiple subgroup analyses, allowed us to examine the consistency of findings across different population groups. Notably, the analysis revealed that LAP's clinical utility appears context-dependent, demonstrating more pronounced effects in populations with higher obesity prevalence. In contrast, VAI demonstrated consistent effects across studies, representing a particularly robust finding.

However, several notable limitations warrant cautious interpretation of the results. Despite observing significant differences in both LAP and VAI between women with PCOS and healthy controls, the strength of evidence remains moderate, primarily because of methodological and population variability across studies. Potential sources of variability were differences in age, BMI, study designs, geographical distribution, and publication year. Although extensive subgroup analyses were performed to account for potential confounders, residual heterogeneity remained high (I2 > 50% in most analyses), indicating that the models did not fully capture all sources of variation. The noted limitations emphasize the importance of conducting uniform, broad-based studies to establish the clinical validity of these indices in diverse groups.

Subgroup analyses based on PCOS phenotypes and BMI categories revealed important insights into the consistency of each index across different PCOS populations. However, the limited evidence base for each subgroup warrants cautious interpretation of these findings. The relatively sparse data in these stratified analyses preclude definitive conclusions, and these results should therefore be considered primarily hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. Furthermore, the potential for Type II errors exists in these subgroup comparisons due to insufficient statistical power resulting from the small number of studies in each category. This limitation particularly affects the ability to detect true differences between subgroups when they may exist.

Additionally, inconsistencies in matching PCOS cases and controls for potential confounders across studies may introduce significant bias into the findings. Given these limitations, further large-scale, high-quality studies with rigorous methodologies are needed to replicate and validate these findings. Additionally, due to limited data, the number of studies included in the subgroup meta-analyses was small, which may reduce the generalizability of these results. Moreover, restriction of inclusion to English-language publications was intended to ensure comprehensive retrieval and accurate assessment of all eligible studies; nevertheless, this strategy could have led to language bias by omitting non-English studies that might have provided important contributions to the findings.

Strengths and limitations

This study compared LAP and VAI values between PCOS and control groups, providing insights into their potential utility as adjunct diagnostic or predictive measures. However, evaluation of diagnostic accuracy was not possible due to data limitations. Risk of bias was assessed using the JBI checklist, which is robust for observational studies but differs in scope from the Revised Tool for the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies (QUADAS-2), specifically designed for diagnostic accuracy research [118]. Sensitivity, specificity, and optimal cut-off values for LAP and VAI in detecting PCOS could not be determined. Therefore, the findings do not establish these indices as definitive diagnostic markers for PCOS. Instead, they highlight their potential role as supplementary biomarkers, warranting further investigation into their diagnostic and predictive utility in rigorously designed studies.

It is noteworthy that while the study focused on LAP and VAI differences between women with and without PCOS, it did not evaluate these indices for distinguishing between PCOS phenotypic subgroups. Previous studies have suggested that these indices may be effective in distinguishing between various PCOS phenotypes and predicting associated metabolic disorders within this population [84, 119–121]. The review results underscore the disrupted lipid metabolism in women with PCOS, as evidenced by elevated LAP and VAI indices. These findings provide a foundation for future investigations aimed at evaluating the performance of these indices in detecting or predicting metabolic disorders in individuals diagnosed with PCOS.

Conclusions

The findings indicate that LAP and VAI levels are substantially elevated in PCOS patients compared with healthy women, supporting their application as complementary diagnostic indices and early screening instruments. These easily calculated indices effectively assess insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction, which are central to PCOS pathophysiology. Importantly, VAI emerges as the more robust biomarker, showing greater consistency across all PCOS phenotypes and metabolic statuses. Their accessibility makes them especially valuable for clinical practice, where they could facilitate early detection of metabolic risks, guide personalized monitoring, and enable more targeted interventions—even in resource-limited settings where advanced testing is not available. In this way, LAP and VAI could complement existing diagnostic criteria and contribute directly to improved risk stratification and management of women with PCOS. From a future perspective, validation in large-scale, longitudinal studies remains essential—not only to establish diagnostic thresholds and predictive values for metabolic outcomes, but also to evaluate their role in monitoring treatment response and tailoring interventions across diverse PCOS subtypes.
