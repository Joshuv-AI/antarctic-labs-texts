Characterization of gut microbiota in wild Asian elephants (Elephas maximus) from five forests across Thailand
Authors: Supika Kantirakoon, Sarisa Klinhom, Chanon Kunasol, Chaiya Huayhongthong, Prapat Sukuprakarn, Weerapong Korawat, Suthipong Kaemthubtim, Thanapong Purintarabal, Wanchai Simmasien, Somporn Pakpien, Boonchu Thongnamchaima, Jirachai Arkajak, Rachaya Arkajak, Nipon Chattipakorn, Siriporn C. Chattipakorn, Chatchote Thitaram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The gut microbiota plays a critical role in mammalian health, yet remains poorly understood in wild Asian elephants (Elephas maximus). This study characterized the gut microbiome of wild elephants using 16S rRNA sequencing of fecal samples collected from five natural habitats in Thailand: Doi Pha Mueang Wildlife Sanctuary (DPM), Khao Ang Rue Nai Wildlife Sanctuary (KARN), Khao Yai National Park (KY), Phuluang Wildlife Sanctuary (PL), and Sublangka Wildlife Sanctuary (SLK), representing distinct geographic regions. Across all sites, Thai wild elephants shared a core gut microbiota dominated by fiber-degrading bacteria. Firmicutes was the most abundant phylum, followed by Bacteroidota, Actinobacteriota, and Proteobacteria. At the family level, Lachnospiraceae predominated, followed by Oscillospiraceae, Anaerovoracaceae, and Christensenellaceae. Environmental variable, including geographic coordinates and minimum elevation, significantly influenced microbial community composition and explained patterns of beta diversity, indicating distinct gut microbiota profiles among elephant populations from different forest regions. These findings establish baseline gut microbiome data for wild Asian elephants and provide a foundation for future ecological and conservation-focused microbiome studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57801-x.

Introduction

The gut microbiome is a complex microbial community inhabiting the gastrointestinal tract of mammals and playing a crucial role in host nutrition, metabolism, and overall health1–3. The composition of the gut microbiome is strongly shaped by diet and host gastrointestinal physiology, as evidenced by distinct microbial communities among dietary groups4–6 and between foregut and hindgut fermenters7. In herbivores, gut microorganisms are essential for the breakdown of complex plant polysaccharides into short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which serve as a major energy source8,9 while contributing to microbial balance and physiological regulation10. Moreover, in herbivorous mammals, dietary intake drives temporal shifts in microbial composition, primarily at the genus and species levels5,11. These microbial communities can respond to dietary changes over both short and long timescales, allowing for metabolic flexibility and enhancing host digestive efficiency and survival during dietary transitions12.

This foundation has driven increasing interest in wildlife gut microbiomes. Unlike captive animals, wild animals inhabit complex ecological environments and consume naturally diverse diets. In addition to host phylogeny and diet, geographic factors influence gut microbiome composition by shaping the availability of food, water, and nutrients13. Previous studies have examined geographic effects alongside other variables, such as host phylogeny14–16 or captivity17, while relatively few have focused on geography as a primary driver. Nevertheless, studies in wild mammals, including house mice13 and western lowland gorillas18, have reported distinct gut microbiome structures across geographic locations, although these patterns are often closely linked to dietary variation in natural habitats. Understanding gut microbiome variation in wildlife has important implications for conservation biology. Evidence from reintroduced species, such as ring-tailed lemurs and Tasmanian devils, demonstrates that gut microbiomes can shift toward wild-like and healthier states following release into natural environments19,20. These findings highlight the potential of gut microbiota as a noninvasive biomarker for monitoring host health, environmental adaptation, and reintroduction success in wildlife conservation19,21,22.

Elephants are monogastric herbivores and hindgut fermenters, with microbial fermentation occurring primarily in the enlarged colon9,23. The gut microbiota of hindgut fermenters, such as horses and elephants, is characterized by high alpha diversity and a high abundance of fiber-degrading bacteria24. Previous studies of captive Asian elephants have investigated gut microbiota variation across life stages25–28, the effects of anthropogenic influences29,30, and cellulolytic enzyme potential31. In contrast, studies of wild Asian elephants have been confined to populations in Yunnan Province, China. These studies suggest that gut microbiota composition differs between wild and captive elephants may vary across geographic regions32,33. Consequently, focused research on wild elephants across three regions in Yunnan identified Firmicutes, Bacteroidota and Proteobacteria as the dominant phyla and found significant differences in alpha and beta diversity across locations, with Proteobacteria showing the highest abundance in the Lincang region34. However, studies of the gut microbiota of wild Asian elephants in natural habitats remain limited. This study aimed to characterize the gut microbiota of wild Asian elephants across five forest regions in Thailand and evaluate the influence of geographic variation on microbial diversity. The five forest complexes in this study exhibit distinct ecological environments distinguished by variation in elevation, plant communities, and habitat heterogeneity35. These environmental variations are likely to affect dietary resources and foraging behaviour in wild Asian elephants, which in turn may shape gut microbiota composition. Although mixed deciduous forest appears consistently across all study sites, other habitat types including dry evergreen forest, deciduous dipterocarp, grassland, bamboo forest, and pine forest are distributed unevenly, reflecting differences in local ecological conditions, and variety of elephant food and water resources. Together, this nature variations provide a framework for investigating how environmental factors shapes diversity and composition of the elephant gut microbiome.

Results

Alpha diversity and beta diversity of gut microbiome in wild Asian elephants

Following taxonomic filtering, the total sequence count across all 30 samples was 4,451,174 ASVs. Per-sample sequencing depth ranged from 139,631 to 161,292 reads, with a mean depth of 148,372 reads. To ensure even sampling for alpha-diversity calculations, the data were rarefied to a minimum depth of 139,631 reads. The corresponding rarefaction curves are provided in Supplementary File 1. The gut microbiota diversity among wild Asian elephants from different forests was assessed using alpha diversity indices including Observed features, the Shannon effective number, Pielou’s evenness, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity (Fig. 1). Overall, elephants from DPM exhibited the lowest microbial diversity across all indices compared to those from KY, PL, and SLK. Specifically, Observed features, Shannon effective number, and Pielou’s evenness indices showed that alpha diversity in elephants from SLK was significantly higher than in those from DPM and KY (q < 0.05; Fig. 1C,D). Additionally, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity revealed that SLK had significantly higher diversity than PL (q < 0.05; Fig. 1B). No significant differences in alpha diversity were observed between elephants from KARN, KY, and PL, or between DPM and KARN. Detailed statistical results for all alpha diversity pairwise comparisons are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Beta diversity was assessed using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and Generalized UniFrac distances to capture complementary aspects of microbial community structure (Fig. 2). PERMANOVA results revealed significant differences in microbial composition among elephants from the five forests (R2 = 0.36, p = 0.001 and R2 = 0.33, p = 0.001 respectively). All pairwise comparisons were significantly different (q < 0.05 via pairwise PERMANOVA). These results, supported by non-significant PERMDISP tests (p > 0.05), reflected that the gut microbiota composition of wild elephants was driven by habitat differences rather than variations in group dispersion. Detailed statistical results for all beta diversity metrics are provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Taxonomic composition of gut microbiota in wild Asian elephants

The taxonomic composition of gut microbiota from wild Asian elephants across five forests is presented as mean relative abundance (%) at the phylum, family, and genus levels (Fig. 3). At the phylum level, Firmicutes was the dominant phylum across all forest sites (Fig. 3A). Bacteroidota was the second most abundant phylum in KY, PL, and SLK (8.46%, 10.18%, and 18.70% respectively). In contrast, Proteobacteria was more prominent in DPM (9.50%), while Actinobacteriota was a notably abundant phylum in KARN (10.61%). At the family level, Lachnospiraceae was dominant in all five forests, followed by the families Oscillospiraceae, Anaerovoracaceae, and Christensenellaceae (Fig. 3B). Planococcaceae showed the highest relative abundance in samples from PL (11.68%). In contrast, Clostridiaceae was more abundant in DPM and KARN (5.39% and 5.41%, respectively) than in the other forest locations. At genus level, the genera Oscillospiraceae UCG-005 (7.19% and 6.49%) and Christensenellaceae_R-7_group (5.82% and 5.59%) were dominant in KARN and KY (Fig. 3C). The genus NK4A214_group was found dominant in DPM and PL (5.01% and 5.75%, respectively), and showed the highest relative abundances in KY (6.07%). In SLK, the gut microbiota consisted of relatively similar abundances of the genera p-251-o5 (4.89%), NK4A214_group (4.72%), Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group (4.55%), and Christensenellaceae_R-7_group (4.41%). Overall, genus-level gut microbiota composition differed among elephants from different forest regions.

Venn diagram was visualized to illustrate the shared and unique ASVs in the gut microbiota of wild elephants from five forests (Fig. 3D). Out of a total of 11,628 ASVs, 1261 were shared across all forest sites. This represents 10.8% of the total ASVs, indicating the presence of a common core gut microbiota of wild elephants. The highest number of unique ASVs was observed in SLK (1876 ASVs; 16.1%), following by PL (1535 ASVs; 13.2%). In contrast, the number of unique ASVs was relatively similar among DPM (1173 ASVs; 10.1%), KARN (1244 ASVs; 10.7%) and KY (1254 ASVs; 10.8%). This distribution reflects both a shared core and region-specific microbial signatures among the five elephant populations.

Differential abundance of gut microbiota in wild Asian elephants

Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) method identified significant differences in microbial taxa among wild elephants from five forest groups (LDA score > 4.0, Kruskal–Wallis q < 0.05; Supplementary Table 3). A total of 40 taxa with significant differences across the five locations are presented in the cladogram (Fig. 4A) and bar plot (Fig. 4B). Elephants residing in DPM forest were associated with higher relative abundance of the genus Clostridium sensu stricto 1. Elephants from KARN forest tended to have higher relative abundance of taxa within the phylum Actinobacteriota, including the family Micrococcaceae, as well as the genera Arthrobacter, Oscillospiraceae UCG-005, Sarcina, Serratia, Carnobacterium, and Pseudomonas. In the KY forest, elephants were associated with higher relative abundance of the class Clostridia, including the families Lachnospiraceae and Oscillospiraceae. The PL elephants showed potential enrichment in the class Bacilli and uncultured genera within the family Planococcaceae. Elephants from the SLK forest were associated with the phyla Bacteroidota and Spirochaetota, including the families Rikenellaceae, Spirochaetaceae, Prevotellaceae, and the genera Treponema and Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group. These differences suggest that habitat-specific factors, such as diet and vegetation type, may shape gut microbial composition in wild elephants.

Correlation between forest types and gut microbiota in wild Asian elephants

Spearman correlation coefficients were used to analyze the relationship between relative area of forest types (%) (Table 1) and gut microbiota composition at the genus level (Fig. 5A). Forest cover was expressed as relative coverage (%) rather than absolute area to standardize comparisons across sanctuaries of varying sizes. However, because these percentage-based forest variables sum to a constant whole (100%), they are inherently compositional and non-independent. Including them simultaneously introduces severe multicollinearity, which limits the use of multivariate regression modeling. Therefore, we applied pairwise Spearman correlations, though the analysis at the forest level did not identify statistically significant associations after multiple-testing correction. Notably, Enterococcus was strongly negatively correlated with regrowth forest coverage (r = − 0.97, p < 0.05), while Bifidobacterium showed a moderate negative association with dry evergreen forest coverage (r = − 0.90, p > 0.05) and Cellulosilyticum was negatively correlated with grassland (r = − 0.87, p > 0.05). These high correlation coefficients suggest potential ecological relationships that may reach statistical significance with a larger number of sampling sites (Fig. 5A).

Geographical factors correlation with gut microbiota in wild Asian elephants

Mantel test was performed to assess correlations between gut microbiota composition at ASV levels and geographical factors, including forest size (km2), forest type abundance (total count of forest types per site), annual average rainfall (mm) along with maximum, minimum, and average height above mean sea levels (MASL; m), and geographic coordinates. Bray–Curtis dissimilarity distances were used for ASV abundance, while geographic variables were represented by Euclidean distance matrices, except for geographic coordinates, which were calculated using Haversine distance. Minimum MASL showed a strong positive correlation with gut microbiota composition (Mantel r > = 0.4, q = 0.01), while other geographic factors exhibited moderate positive correlations (Mantel r = 0.2–0.4, q = 0.01), suggesting that sites with greater environmental dissimilarity tended to harbor more distinct microbial communities (Fig. 5B).

Spearman correlation coefficients revealed significant association among factors. Forest size was positively correlated with forest abundance and annual rainfall while forest abundance showed positive correlation with elevation. In contrast, rainfall was negatively correlated with minimum and average MASL. Furthermore, minimum MASL was positively correlated with maximum and average MASL whereas the maximum MASL correlated only with minimum MASL (Fig. 5B).

Additionally, overall combined geographical factor and geographic coordinate significantly correlated with ASV abundance (Mantel r = 0.41 and 0.51, q = 0.01) highlighting the strong effect of geographical factor and coordinate toward the gut microbiota composition. Statistical data of Mantel test was provided in Supplementary Table 4.

Functional prediction of gut microbiota in wild Asian elephants

Differential abundance analysis of predicted metabolic pathways was conducted using ALDEx2 with the Kruskal–Wallis test to evaluate gut microbiota function among wild elephants from five forests. Out of 289 predicted pathways at Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes metabolic KEGG level 3, only 22 pathways showed statistically significant differences across locations (q < 0.05). This indicate that the vast majority of the predicted metabolic function remains highly conserved regardless of habitat. To examine these few changing features, the log-normalized and 0–1 min-max scaled relative abundances were visualized using a heatmap (q < 0.05; Fig. 6). Among these significant pathways two-component systems (part of signal transduction pathway), galactose metabolism, starch and sucrose metabolism, and butanoate metabolism pathways were potentially highly abundant across all groups. On the other hand, pathways included benzoate degradation, sulfur relay system, limonene degradation, phenylpropanoid biosynthesis, aminobenzoate degradation, sphingolipid metabolism, and nitrotoluene degradation, had lower baseline abundances across all locations. While these pathways reached statistical significance, the narrow range of their normalized abundance shows that the actual size of these differences is small, representing minor variations within a mostly conserved functional core.

Discussion

This study presents the first investigation of the gut microbiota of wild Asian elephants in Thailand. Using 16S amplicon sequencing, we characterized the gut microbiota of elephants across five distinct forest regions and found them to be shaped by geographic variation. Our findings suggest that geographic factors may serve as a useful proxy reflecting environmental and dietary heterogeneity influencing gut microbiota structure. This study highlights the important role of habitat-specific factors in shaping microbial communities in wild elephant populations.

Across all forest sites, the gut microbiota of wild elephants was dominated by the phylum Firmicutes, consistent with previous reports from wild elephants in China32,34 and captive elephants in Thailand27,28. However, the second most abundant phylum varied by location. Bacteroidota predominated in KY, PL, and SLK, whereas Proteobacteria and Actinobacteriota were more abundant in DPM. These phyla are commonly found in Asian elephant gut microbiota, although their relative abundances differ among studies27,28,34,36. The notably high relative abundance of Proteobacteria, as reported in wild elephants from Lincang region34, has been associated with gut microbial dysbiosis or instability in captive elephants with gastrointestinal disorders36. In contrast, the predominance of Bacteroidota observed in wild elephants from Nangunhe33 may reflect a more stable gut microbial community. As Bacteroidota are efficient degraders of complex plant polysaccharides, making it a classic signature of a healthy hindgut fermenter37. The observed variation in the second-ranked phylum across regions suggests that phylum-level ranking may represent a sensitive indicator of gut ecosystem status, with potential applications for preliminary health or environmental assessments. Further investigations are needed to validate the use of these phyla as microbial biomarkers, similar to approaches used in human gut microbiome studies38.

Moreover, analyses of relative abundance and Venn diagrams revealed that wild elephants across all five forests shared a core microbiome. This likely reflects strong host-related constraints, including elephant phylogeny and hindgut fermentation, which require the consistent presence of microbial taxa involved in SCFA production to support host energy metabolism, despite geographical or dietary differences5. The core functional potential of the gut microbiome appears to be driven by dominant bacterial families, particularly Lachnospiraceae, followed by Oscillospiraceae and Christensenellaceae, which are widely recognized as key SCFA producers through fiber degradation39–41. Consistent with this taxonomic pattern, functional prediction analyses indicated that the gut microbiota of elephants from all five forests was potentially dominated by metabolic pathways related to carbohydrate degradation, supporting the presence of a core microbiome enriched in fiber-degrading bacteria. Nonetheless, minor variation in specific predicted pathways was observed among populations, likely reflecting localized environmental conditions and host physiological differences. While the core microbiome provides functional stability, variation at the second-ranked phylum, family and genus levels highlights forest-specific microbial signatures in wild elephants, likely shaped by ecological factors such as diet and geography5. This variation is further supported by beta diversity analyses; both Bray-Curtis dissimilarity and generalized UniFrac distances revealed that the gut microbiota of wild elephants across the five forest types differed significantly in taxonomic composition and phylogenetic lineage structure.

This study suggested that geographic factors significantly influence gut microbiota composition. Mantel test analyses indicated that greater habitat similarity was associated with greater similarity in gut microbiota structure, as measured by Bray-Curtis dissimilarity. Among the examined variables, geographic coordinates exhibited the strongest correlation with microbial composition, followed by minimum elevation. Geographical coordinates reflect not only spatial distance but also variation in elevation, forest structure, and ecosystem composition42. This pattern is consistent with the geographic distribution of the sampled forest regions. DPM represents a mountainous northern forest, whereas KY, PL, and SLK are located within the northeastern plateau region, and KARN is situated in the eastern region adjacent to the plateau. Differences in elevation and forest structure among these regions likely act as environmental filters, indirectly shaping gut microbial communities by influencing forest characteristics and elephant foraging patterns.

In Thailand, tropical forest types and plant composition are structured by climate, soil, and elevation35, providing an ecological basis for the observed regional differences in gut microbiota. Previous studies have indicated that wild Asian elephants preferentially inhabit lowland areas, typically below 1000 m in elevation43,44. Nevertheless, they demonstrate the capacity to adapt to higher elevations45. These higher-altitude forest landscapes typically comprise a mosaic of evergreen and deciduous forest types46–48, with evergreen forests becoming more prevalent at increased elevations49. Consistent with this pattern, KY and PL forests, located at higher elevations (1000–1500 m above sea level), exhibited greater evergreen forest coverage than KARN and SLK, which were located at lower elevations and lacked the hill evergreen and pine forests35. Together, these findings suggest that elevation acts as an indirect driver of gut microbiota composition by influencing forest structure and available forage resources. Previous research on wild small rodents (Apodemus draco) has demonstrated that intermediate elevations (2200–2600 m) provide optimal conditions for gut microbial diversity, exhibiting a hump-shaped distribution pattern where alpha diversity peaks at mid-altitudes50. Conversely, a study on cattle found that individuals inhabiting the highest altitudes (3650 m) possessed the highest microbial richness and a greater abundance of fiber-degrading taxa, likely an adaptation to the high crude fiber content of the available forage and the extreme environmental conditions51. These results support our findings that altitude significantly influences gut microbiota composition. However, rather than a straightforward linear trend, our observations suggest that preferred elevation ranges mediates both microbial shifts and host metabolic adaptations.

Other geographical factors, including annual rainfall and forest type abundance also significantly influenced gut microbiota composition. Rainfall is positively associated with plant diversity and species richness52, and regulates forest productivity through soil water availability53. This ecological complexity suggests that even forests with similar dominant structures can differ functionally, potentially influencing dietary resources and gut microbiota composition54. According to our statistical results, annual rainfall likely shapes the wild elephant gut microbiota indirectly through its effects on dietary diversity and quality. Consistent with this, elephants from SLK exhibited significantly higher gut microbial diversity than those from DPM as measured by Observed features, Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, Shannon effective number, and Pielou’s evenness, despite SLK covering a smaller area and both regions being dominated by mixed deciduous forest. This finding suggests that dominant forest type alone may not fully explain variation in gut microbiota. Similar patterns have been reported in other large herbivores, where overall dietary diversity was not a strong determinant of gut microbiota structure55. Instead, the specific composition and heterogeneity of plant species within habitats appear to play a more critical role than the dominant vegetation type56. Therefore, the higher microbial richness observed in SLK is likely due to higher fine-scale floristic diversity or habitat heterogeneity compared with DPM. However, because geographical variables were averaged for each forest, fine-scale environmental variation may be underestimated, and the results primarily reflect broad patterns in beta diversity rather than alpha diversity. Our results align with ecological patterns where precipitation significantly explains the beta diversity observed in wild geladas and red-fronted lemurs57,58, reflecting distinct seasonal dietary transitions. As grass consumers, geladas exhibit a gut microbiota enriched with cellulolytic taxa during the wet season, coinciding with the increased abundance of aboveground grasses compared to the dry season58. However, a limitation of the Mantel test analyses is that individual-level microbiota data were paired with site-level environmental variables across only five forest sites, which may partially violate the assumption of observation independence. This may lead to potential pseudo-replication. As such, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory. Future studies incorporating a greater number of geographically diverse sites, or applying hierarchical mixed-effects models that account for the nested structure of samples within sites, would enable more robust inference regarding environment–microbiome relationships in wild Asian elephants.

This study observed the potential of correlations between specific forest type coverage and bacterial genera, indicating habitat-specific microbial associations. Regrowth forest was negatively associated with the genus Enterococcus59, a commensal opportunist found in the gut microbiota of a wide range of hosts, including healthy wild mammals60. As a secondary forest undergoing succession and regenerating from anthropogenically disturbed areas such as agricultural land61, regrowth forest likely features shorter canopies and degraded soils from agricultural conversion or burning62, which may reduce ecological support for commensal taxa such as Enterococcus. Similarly, the cellulolytic genus Cellulosilyticum63 was negatively correlated with grassland, likely reflecting insufficient structural vegetation complexity. In contrast, dry evergreen forests are moist and enhance the growth of diverse vegetation, yet they were negatively correlated with the genus Bifidobacterium. This genus is known for its capacity to degrade complex carbohydrates, such as plant glycans64 and starches65. However, the dominant plants in dry evergreen forests are hardwood species47, which may provide a diet higher in complex fiber. Nevertheless, these correlations did not reach statistical significance, therefore, future work should include a greater number of sampling sites to increase statistical power and incorporate more detailed ecological data to elucidate these relationships more deeply.

Differential abundance analysis revealed population-specific enrichment of bacterial taxa, consistent with the ecological complexity and forest heterogeneity across the five habitats. However, LEfSe does not explicitly account for the compositional nature of 16S rRNA sequencing data, where observed abundances reflect relative rather than absolute proportions. Therefore, the taxa identified through this analysis represent potential associations rather than definitive biological differences. Elephants from SLK tend to have higher relative abundance of the genera Treponema, commonly associated with soluble fiber degradation66, and Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group, which had been linked to crude fiber digestion67. In contrast, elephants from KY showed higher relative abundances of Lachnospiraceae and Oscillospiraceae, taxa associated with the breakdown of complex fibers such as cellulose and hemicellulose40. Although these taxa differ in fiber substrate preference, they are broadly involved in fiber-associated metabolic pathways. Variation in fiber-associated taxa may therefore reflect differences in dietary composition among habitats rather than discrete functional specialization. This interpretation is supported by the detection of Treponema in semi-captive individuals30, and the positive correlation between Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group and dietary fiber in other species68. Notably, elephants from KARN appeared to have soil-associated genera, including Arthrobacter, Sarcina, Serratia, and Pseudomonas69,70, Although post-defecation soil contamination cannot be fully excluded, the same sampling methodology was applied consistently across all five forest sites. Therefore, this potential enrichment may reflect genuine ecological or behavioral differences, particularly since geophagy in elephants is often linked to mineral supplementation or neutralization of dietary toxins71. Future studies incorporating of soil would help clarify the contribution of environmental contamination versus true gut-associated microbiota. Moreover, elephants from PL forest showed potential enrichment of an uncultured genus in the family Planococcaceae, which has also been reported in wild elephants from Pu’er34. Members of this family were reported to decrease following translocation30 and have been detected in captive elephants consuming Napier grass28. These patterns suggest that this family may serve as a potential ecological biomarker associated with region-specific dietary exposure or site-related environmental conditions. However, the lack of negative extraction controls is a recognized limitation. Although aseptic procedures were followed throughout, future studies should include such controls to better exclude potential laboratory contamination.

Our study suggests that variation in the gut microbiota of wild elephants is shaped by broad geographical patterns rather than a single environmental factor. These associations help explain the observed differences in beta diversity among the five habitats, with each harboring distinct microbial taxa, while forests with similar geographical characteristics tend to share comparable gut microbiota compositions. This confirms that similar geographical factors may lead to shared gut microbiota compositions. However, this study is limited by the taxonomic resolution of 16S rRNA sequencing and the absence of direct measurements of diet, environmental variable, and microbial activity. Although diet is a primary determinant of gut microbiota composition, direct dietary assessment in wild elephants remains logistically challenging. Future research should integrate comprehensive dietary studies with advanced genomic tools, such as shotgun metagenomic sequencing, to move beyond taxonomic predictions. Incorporating direct functional assessments will be essential to fully elucidate how gut microbes support elephant health and nutrition in varying ecological landscapes.

Conclusion

This study characterizes the gut microbiota of wild Asian elephants across five forest regions in Thailand. While wild elephants shared a conserved core microbiota dominated by fiber-degrading bacteria, gut microbial composition varied significantly among habitats and was potentially influenced by broad geographical factors such as location, elevation, rainfall, and forest structure. Forests with similar geographical characteristics exhibited more similar gut microbiota profiles, highlighting the role of habitat-driven environmental factors in shaping microbial communities. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating environmental data into wildlife gut microbiome studies and support the potential use of the gut microbiota as a non-invasive indicator of elephant health and habitat conditions. Future studies using higher-resolution functional approaches are needed to better resolve environment–host–microbiome interactions relevant to conservation.

Methods

Sample collection and DNA extraction

Samples were obtained from five forests across Thailand (Table 1), with six samples collected per forest, resulting in a total of 30 samples. Each sample was collected from spatially separated fresh dung and was considered to represent a different individual, although individual identity was not genetically confirmed. All samples were processed individually, and no pooling was performed. Approximately 50 g of six fresh fecal samples were collected from inhabited wild elephants in the early morning during November to December in 2021 from five forests across Thailand (Table 1), and then stored at – 20 °C within six hours after collection until DNA extraction. The fecal samples were supposed to be defecated by wild elephants foraging around that area during the nighttime and expected not more than six hours after defecation. Freshness of fecal samples was assessed based on visual and physical characteristics, including well-defined bolus shape, absence of physical disturbance, visible surface moisture, and normal fecal odor. Prior to DNA extraction, indigestible roughage including grass, leaves, fruit seeds, and peels was manually removed using aseptic techniques. The internal core of the fecal bolus was preferentially selected in order to minimize contamination from external environmental material. Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted from 250 mg of prepared fecal pellets using a commercial genomic DNA isolation kit (QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (QIAGEN, Germany)) within a biosafety cabinet, following aseptic techniques.

Elephant foraging locations and habitats

Five forests included Doi Pha Mueang Wildlife Sanctuary (DPM, Lampang: 18° 22′ 12″ N 99° 14′ 07″ E), Khao Ang Rue Nai Wildlife Sanctuary (KARN, Chachoengsao: 13° 24′ 58″ N 101° 52′ 40″ E), Khao Yai National Park (KY, Nakhon Ratchasima: 14° 21′ N 101° 26′ E), Phuluang Wildlife Sanctuary (PL, Loei: 17° 21′ 44″ N 101° 30′ 20″ E), and Sublangka Wildlife Sanctuary (SLK, Lop Buri: 15° 35′ 47″ N 101° 21′ 04″ E). The samples collections sites, ecological information (i.e., forest types, annual average rainfall, height above mean sea level) and number of elephants in each forest were described in Table 1; Fig. 7.

Bioinformatic and statistical analysis

The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified from the extracted bacterial genomic DNA using the primers 341 F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′). Next-Generation Sequencing (NGS) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform. DNA amplification, data quality control methods, and sequencing were conducted by Novogene Co., Ltd. (Singapore). PCR was performed using Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs) under the following conditions: an initial denaturation at 98 °C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 50 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min. After checking PCR product quality, the library preparation was performed by Novogene using the TruSeq® DNA PCR-Free Sample Preparation Kit (Illumina, USA) to reduce PCR-derived bias and artifacts. Paired-end raw sequences (2 × 250 bp) were imported into QIIME 2-2023.772, then denoised and identified Amplicon Sequence Variants (ASVs) by DADA2 plugin73, which included chimera removal. No truncation was applied as quality scores remained consistently high (≥ Q30) across the full 250 bp paired-end reads. The raw sequencing depth per sample ranged from 173,216 to 199,197 reads, which was refined to 140,454 to 163,673 reads after quality filtering. ASVs were aligned with mafft74 and built phylogenic tree with fasttree275. For taxonomic assignment, the ASVs were compared against SILVA database (version 138)76. Feature table output from QIIME 2 was imported into R studio 4.3.1 by the Phyloseq package77 to calculate downstream analysis. Archaeal sequences were retained, while chloroplast, mitochondria, and eukaryote sequences were filtered out. The Kruskal–Wallis test and pairwise Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were used to assess alpha diversity indices, including Observed features, the Shannon effective number, Pielou’s evenness, and Faith’s phylogenetic diversity. For beta diversity, including Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, Generalized UniFrac and, pairwise PERMANOVA was conducted along with the assessment of multivariate homogeneity of group dispersions (PERMDISP). Differential abundance analysis was performed via the Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) method (LDA score > 4.0), using the Kruskal–Wallis test with Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) p-value adjustment. The correlation between forest type (%) and gut microbiota at the genus level was analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation with the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) p-value adjustment method. Geographical factors including annual average rainfall (mm), forest size (km2), forest type abundance (total count), height above mean sea level (m), and geographic coordinates were assessed using the Mantel test based on Spearman’s rank correlation with BH adjustment. Microbial functional prediction was performed using the PICRUSt2 full pipeline within QIIME 2, with metabolic pathway annotation based on KEGG pathways78. Phylogenetic placement of ASVs was conducted using SEPP, and hidden state prediction was performed via the Predictive Information Criterion (PIC) method. Quality control was maintained using a maximum Nearest Sequenced Taxon Index (NSTI) cutoff of 3.0 and a minimum alignment length of 0.05. Annotation and statistical analysis were performed via the ggpicrust2 package79. Significant differences in predicted KEGC pathways across the five forest locations were identified using the ALDEx2 Kruskal–Wallis test, and p-values adjusted using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) method. Pathways were considered significant at q < 0.05 and were converted to relative abundance, log10-transformed, and normalized to a 0–1 scale for heatmap visualization.
