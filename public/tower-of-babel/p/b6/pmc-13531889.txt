Exploring value-creating contexts in the capacity building of nursing managers: a reflective thematic analysis
Authors: Mohammad Azim Mahmodi, Hossein Sadeghnezhad, Ayob Akbari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionBuilding leadership capacity among nursing managers is essential for improving healthcare quality and organizational performance. Although value creation has been widely discussed as an organizational outcome within value-based healthcare, its role as a developmental process underlying capacity building remains poorly understood. This study explored how nursing managers develop leadership capacity through value-creating experiences across personal, social, and professional contexts.MethodsA qualitative study was conducted using Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis. Twenty-six nursing managers from Birjand University of Medical Sciences, Iran, were purposively recruited between April and July 2025. Data were collected through semi-structured, in-depth interviews, transcribed verbatim, and analyzed concurrently using MAXQDA 2020. Analysis continued until sufficient richness had been achieved to construct coherent interpretive themes.ResultsAnalysis generated the overarching theme of “Value-Creating Context as a Developmental Process for Management Capacity Building,” comprising three interrelated themes: family value-creating experiences, community-based developmental factors, and facilitating conditions for value realization in nursing practice. Rather than representing independent contextual factors, these themes described a continuous developmental process in which family experiences established foundational values and self-confidence, community interactions fostered social learning and professional identity, and workplace environments translated these capacities into effective leadership through collaboration, reflective learning, knowledge sharing, and continuous professional development. Together, these findings illustrate how value creation contributes to the progressive development of nursing management capacity.ConclusionThis study proposes a new interpretive understanding of nursing management capacity building by conceptualizing value creation as the developmental mechanism through which leadership capacity evolves across multiple life contexts. The findings suggest that effective leadership development requires organizational strategies extending beyond competency-based training to cultivate value-generating environments that support reflective learning, mentorship, professional identity formation, and continuous leadership development. This perspective offers a broader conceptual foundation for designing sustainable leadership development programs in nursing management.

Background

Healthcare systems worldwide are increasingly expected to improve patient outcomes while ensuring the efficient use of human, financial, and organizational resources. In response, value-based healthcare (VBHC) has emerged as a guiding paradigm that defines value as the health outcomes achieved relative to the resources invested in care delivery [1, 2]. More recently, this concept has expanded beyond patient outcomes to encompass benefits generated for multiple stakeholders—patients, healthcare professionals, organizations, and society. From this broader perspective, value is created through interactions among individuals, teams, and organizations that enhance care quality, strengthen professional capability, and improve organizational performance [3, 4].

For nursing managers, value creation extends beyond organizational efficiency or clinical outcomes alone. It involves establishing the conditions—knowledge, relationships, motivation, and professional capability—that enable nurses and healthcare teams to deliver high-quality care. Value creation is therefore best understood as a relational and developmental process, one in which organizational and professional resources are continuously generated and reinforced through leadership, collaboration, and supportive workplace environments [5]. This framing distinguishes value creation from conventional leadership development: rather than focusing narrowly on individual competency acquisition, it emphasizes the cultivation of sustainable organizational conditions that benefit managers, staff, patients, and the organization simultaneously.

Nursing managers occupy a pivotal position within this process, functioning as key facilitators of value creation. Through leadership, decision-making, resource allocation, and staff development, they shape not only the quality and safety of patient care but also the professional growth and performance of nursing teams. Strengthening their capacity should therefore be understood not merely as an individual educational undertaking, but as an organizational strategy for enhancing value creation across multiple levels of the healthcare system [6].

This positions capacity building and value creation as conceptually interdependent, linked through a reciprocal relationship rather than a one-directional process. On one hand, organizational contexts that promote trust, collaboration, ethical leadership, and learning create the conditions under which managers can strengthen their knowledge, confidence, and relational capabilities—that is, value-generating contexts facilitate capacity building [7]. On the other hand, nursing managers who possess greater leadership capacity are better equipped to foster supportive cultures, empower staff, and improve care processes—that is, enhanced capacity, in turn, generates further value for patients, professionals, organizations, and society. Value creation thus functions both as an antecedent condition for capacity building and as an outcome of it [8].

Prior research supports this contextual view, showing that value creation is shaped by interpersonal relationships, professional collaboration, organizational culture, and the broader social environment of care delivery [9, 10]. Organizational values such as integrity, compassion, accountability, respect, and professional commitment have similarly been identified as foundational to effective leadership and workforce engagement [11, 12]. Snellman (2015), for instance, argued that a value context grounded in integrity, compassion, support, knowledge, and accountability enables optimal individual and organizational performance [12]. Value co-creation frameworks further highlight continuous interaction, mutual learning, feedback, and shared responsibility as the mechanisms through which such value is generated and sustained [13].

Self-leadership and professional responsibility represent a key mechanism linking these two constructs. Jooste and Cairns (2014) demonstrated that limitations in self-direction, confidence, and ownership of professional responsibility constrain nurses’ leadership development and organizational contribution [14]. Their findings suggest that managerial capacity develops not solely through the acquisition of technical competencies, but through workplace environments that reinforce confidence, autonomy, and accountability—environments that simultaneously strengthen managerial capacity and sustain organizational value creation.

Despite growing interest in value-based healthcare and nursing leadership, research examining the relationship between these two constructs among nursing managers remains scarce. Existing studies have predominantly examined value creation in relation to patient outcomes or organizational performance, while capacity-building research has focused largely on leadership competencies and professional development in isolation. As a result, little is known about how value-generating organizational contexts facilitate managers’ capacity development, or how strengthened capacity subsequently sustains further value creation—a gap that limits current understanding of this reciprocal relationship.

Addressing this gap is particularly important given that nursing managers serve as the interface between organizational strategy and clinical practice. Clarifying how value-generating contexts shape their professional development may inform the design of leadership programs and organizational strategies that strengthen managerial capacity while improving healthcare value more broadly. This study therefore aimed to explore nursing managers’ experiences of the contextual factors contributing to capacity building within value-creating organizational contexts. Using Braun and Clarke’s reflective thematic analysis, the study sought to generate an in-depth understanding of how organizational values, interpersonal relationships, and leadership processes interact to strengthen nursing managers’ capacity to create value for patients, professionals, organizations, and the broader healthcare system.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study was conducted using Braun and Clarke’s reflective thematic analysis approach [15]. Reflective thematic analysis was selected because it provides a flexible and theoretically coherent method for exploring patterns of shared meaning while acknowledging the active role of researchers in knowledge production and interpretation. The study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ).

Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Birjand University of Medical Sciences, Iran (IR.BUMS.REC.1404.008). All participants provided written informed consent before participation.

Participants and recruitment

Participants were recruited through purposive sampling from nursing managers affiliated with Birjand University of Medical Sciences. Eligible participants were required to have at least five years of nursing experience and a minimum of one year of managerial experience as a head nurse, supervisor, or matron. Individuals who were unwilling to participate in interviews or declined audio recording were excluded.

Purposive sampling was continued to ensure diversity in managerial position, professional experience, educational background, and gender. Recruitment continued until sufficient depth and richness of data were achieved to address the study objectives.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured, in-depth interviews conducted by the corresponding author between April and July 2025. The interviewer holds a PhD in Nursing and is an Assistant Professor of Nursing with substantial experience in qualitative research and nursing management. To minimize potential response bias, the interviewer had no prior personal or professional relationship with any participant, was not employed at any of the participating hospitals, and held no supervisory, managerial, or evaluative role in relation to the participants. This independent positioning was intended to facilitate open, candid communication during the interviews.

An interview guide was developed based on the study objectives and a review of relevant literature (Table 1). Prior to each interview, demographic information was collected and written informed consent was obtained. Participants were informed of the study’s purpose, the voluntary nature of participation, the confidentiality measures in place, and their right to withdraw at any time without consequence. All interviews were conducted in a private setting, audio-recorded with participants’ permission, and lasted between 40 and 70 minutes.

Throughout data collection, the interviewer maintained field notes and reflexive memos to document contextual observations, critically reflect on personal assumptions, and record emerging analytical insights. This reflexive practice enhanced transparency and helped mitigate the influence of researcher preconceptions on both data collection and subsequent analysis.

Consistent with Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis approach, sample adequacy was determined based on the richness and depth of the data generated rather than the concept of data saturation. A total of 26 nursing managers participated in the study.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted concurrently with data collection, following Braun and Clarke’s six-phase reflexive thematic analysis (RTA) framework [16]. Conducting analysis alongside data collection enabled continuous engagement with participants’ accounts and informed ongoing reflection throughout the research process. All interviews were transcribed verbatim and imported into MAXQDA 2020 for data management and organization.

The analysis followed the six recursive phases proposed by Braun and Clarke: (1) familiarization with the data through repeated reading of the transcripts while documenting initial impressions; (2) inductive generation of initial codes across the dataset; (3) construction of preliminary themes by identifying meaningful patterns across codes; (4) iterative review and refinement of candidate themes in relation to both the coded extracts and the entire dataset; (5) defining and naming themes to capture their central organizing concepts; and (6) producing the final analytic narrative through integration of themes with illustrative participant quotations and relevant literature [16].

Consistent with the principles of reflexive thematic analysis, coding was conducted inductively, allowing themes to emerge from participants’ experiences rather than being imposed by a predetermined theoretical framework. Themes were conceptualized not as objective entities awaiting discovery, but as interpretive patterns constructed through active engagement between the researchers and the data.

Reflexivity was maintained throughout the analytic process. The primary researcher kept reflexive memos to document emerging interpretations, methodological decisions, and personal assumptions that might influence data interpretation. Analysis involved continuous movement between the data, coding, theme development, and theoretical interpretation, reflecting the researchers’ active role in knowledge construction. Regular research team meetings provided opportunities to critically discuss coding decisions, challenge underlying assumptions, compare alternative interpretations, and refine the developing thematic framework, thereby enhancing analytical depth and transparency.

Consistent with Braun and Clarke’s methodological guidance, data adequacy was determined by the richness, diversity, and interpretive sufficiency of the dataset rather than by the concept of data saturation. Recruitment continued until the dataset provided sufficient depth and breadth to develop well-supported, information-rich themes that comprehensively addressed the study aim. The final sample of 26 nursing managers, representing diverse managerial roles and professional experiences, generated a rich dataset that enabled nuanced interpretation of participants’ experiences regarding value-based capacity building.

Reflexivity and trustworthiness

Given the interpretive and reflexive nature of Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis [17], the researchers recognized their active role in constructing meaning throughout the research process. Rather than attempting to eliminate researcher subjectivity, reflexivity was treated as an essential analytical resource. Following each interview, the primary researcher maintained reflexive field notes and analytic memos to critically examine personal assumptions, evolving interpretations, and methodological decisions. Regular discussions within the research team further supported this process, prompting the team to challenge emerging interpretations and consider alternative understandings during theme development.

Research quality was enhanced through sustained engagement with the dataset, systematic documentation of analytic decisions, and transparent reporting of the interpretive process. Throughout the analysis, the researchers moved iteratively between the data, codes, developing themes, and relevant literature to ensure that the resulting themes offered a coherent, insightful, and conceptually meaningful interpretation of participants’ experiences. Representative participant quotations were incorporated to demonstrate how interpretations remained grounded in the data, while acknowledging that themes reflected the researchers’ interpretive engagement rather than objective discoveries. Collectively, these strategies enhanced methodological transparency, analytical rigor, and the credibility of the interpretive account.

Analysis of the interview data generated an interpretive model describing the value-creating context as a developmental process through which nursing managers progressively build leadership capacity. Rather than reflecting isolated contextual influences, participants described value creation as a continuous trajectory: personal values were first formed within the family, subsequently refined through interactions within society, and ultimately translated into leadership practice within the workplace. Capacity building thus emerged not simply from the acquisition of managerial knowledge and skills, but from the ongoing construction, enactment, and reinforcement of professional values across personal, social, and organizational contexts. The interpretive model comprised three interconnected themes and nine subthemes: (1) Family Value-Creating Experiences, (2) Community-Based Developmental Processes, and (3) Facilitating Conditions for Value Realization in Nursing Practice (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Coding tree

Theme 1: Family value-creating experiences—establishing the value foundation of leadership capacity

Participants consistently described the family as the initial environment in which the values underpinning effective leadership were cultivated. Rather than directly developing managerial competence, family experiences provided the ethical, emotional, and psychological foundations that later enabled participants to assume leadership responsibilities. Through early socialization, participants internalized values that became enduring personal resources for professional judgment, interpersonal relationships, and leadership decision-making. Family experiences thus represented the first stage of value creation, in which leadership capacity began with the development of personal character rather than professional expertise.

Internalization of virtues and ethical values

Participants described childhood exposure to values such as respect, empathy, honesty, responsibility, and commitment to helping others as fundamental to their professional identity. These values were perceived not as abstract moral principles, but as enduring behavioral orientations that continued to guide managerial decision-making throughout their careers.From an early age, our family emphasized helping others, respect, kindness, and patience. These values became part of who I am and influence how I manage people today. (Head Nurse)

From an early age, our family emphasized helping others, respect, kindness, and patience. These values became part of who I am and influence how I manage people today. (Head Nurse)

Participants emphasized that these early ethical experiences created a stable value framework that enabled them to approach leadership decisions with fairness, accountability, and compassion.

Spiritual and belief-based Foundations

Beyond interpersonal values, many participants identified spirituality and religious belief as important sources of ethical commitment and professional responsibility. Spiritual beliefs provided an internal moral compass that guided difficult managerial decisions and reinforced integrity when facing organizational challenges.We were taught to work honestly, avoid unethical practices, and remain committed to our beliefs. These principles continue to guide my professional decisions. (Matron)

We were taught to work honestly, avoid unethical practices, and remain committed to our beliefs. These principles continue to guide my professional decisions. (Matron)

These findings suggest that value creation begins with the internalization of deeply held moral beliefs that subsequently shape professional conduct and ethical leadership.

Development of self-confidence

Participants further explained that family encouragement, trust, and early opportunities to assume responsibility fostered self-confidence, independence, and resilience. These psychological resources later facilitated leadership effectiveness by strengthening confidence in decision-making and interpersonal communication.Being trusted with responsibilities from an early age helped me develop confidence in my abilities and strengthened my communication skills. Rather than viewing self-confidence as an individual personality trait, participants described it as a value-based resource that enabled them to lead others, manage uncertainty, and assume accountability within complex healthcare environments.

Theme 2: Community-based developmental processes—transforming personal values into social leadership competencies

While family experiences established the foundation of personal values, participants described the broader community as the context in which these values were refined, challenged, and translated into socially responsive leadership competencies. Interaction with diverse individuals, exposure to different perspectives, and engagement with societal expectations enabled participants to expand their understanding of leadership beyond personal belief. Value creation was therefore understood as an ongoing social process through which individual values evolved into professional competencies capable of responding to complex organizational and societal demands.

Opportunities for broad social interaction

Participants explained that interaction with diverse social groups strengthened communication skills, adaptability, emotional intelligence, and the ability to understand different perspectives—experiences that broadened their capacity to manage relationships within increasingly complex healthcare environments.Interactions within society expose us to different perspectives and teach us how to communicate effectively with various people. (Supervisor)

Rather than simply improving interpersonal skills, these interactions expanded participants’ ability to negotiate differing viewpoints, build collaborative relationships, and respond flexibly to diverse organizational situations.

Learning through role models

Participants frequently described observing respected individuals as a powerful mechanism for leadership development. Both exemplary and ineffective leaders contributed to this learning process by demonstrating behaviors that participants either sought to emulate or consciously avoided.Positive role models showed us how respect, discipline, and commitment can be combined in professional life. Role models thus functioned not only as sources of professional knowledge, but also as mechanisms through which participants translated abstract values into observable leadership behaviors.

Influence of social expectations and cultural norms

Participants also recognized that societal expectations and cultural norms reinforced professional accountability and shaped their understanding of ethical leadership. They described leadership as requiring alignment between personal values and the broader expectations of the communities they served.As nurses, we are expected to align our behavior with the values and expectations of the society we serve. Participants viewed these societal expectations as reinforcing their commitment to professionalism, public trust, and ethical responsibility, further strengthening the value-based foundation of managerial capacity.

Theme 3: Facilitating conditions for value realization in nursing practice—transforming values into leadership capacity

Participants described the workplace as the setting in which personally and socially constructed values were translated into everyday managerial practice. Unlike the family and community contexts, which primarily contributed to the formation and refinement of values, the organizational environment enabled nursing managers to enact, strengthen, and reproduce these values through leadership activity, collaborative learning, and professional development. Participants emphasized that leadership capacity developed not solely through formal managerial training, but through continuous engagement in workplace practices that transformed values into observable leadership behaviors. Value creation thus became both a personal and organizational process, whereby managers not only applied their own values but also fostered environments that enabled others to learn, collaborate, and develop professionally.

Exchange of knowledge and experience

Participants consistently described the workplace as a collaborative learning environment in which knowledge, experience, and practical wisdom were continuously exchanged among managers and staff. These interactions extended beyond information sharing and were viewed as opportunities for mutual learning, mentoring, and the collective development of leadership capacity.I try to create opportunities for staff to learn from experienced colleagues and gain managerial experience through participation and collaboration. (Head Nurse)

I try to create opportunities for staff to learn from experienced colleagues and gain managerial experience through participation and collaboration. (Head Nurse)

Participants explained that collaborative learning strengthened professional relationships, enhanced mutual trust, and facilitated the transfer of tacit managerial knowledge. Through these interactions, individual learning gradually evolved into organizational learning, creating conditions that supported both personal development and collective value creation.

Learning from errors

Rather than viewing errors solely as indicators of poor performance, participants regarded them as valuable opportunities for reflection, learning, and continuous improvement. A psychologically safe environment that encouraged open discussion of mistakes enabled managers and staff to transform adverse experiences into opportunities for professional growth.Mistakes are part of professional development. What matters is learning from them and preventing them from happening again. (Supervisor)

Mistakes are part of professional development. What matters is learning from them and preventing them from happening again. (Supervisor)

Participants emphasized that a culture of learning from errors strengthened accountability, reflective practice, and organizational resilience—illustrating how value creation occurred through the collective interpretation of experience, enabling both individuals and organizations to continuously improve leadership practice.

Continuous educational development

Ongoing educational activities were described as mechanisms enabling nursing managers to sustain and further develop their leadership capabilities in response to changing organizational demands. Participants viewed continuous professional development as more than the maintenance of technical competence; it represented a process of continuously renewing professional values, leadership perspectives, and managerial confidence.Regular educational programs help us remain up to date and support both personal and professional development. (Matron)

Continuous learning therefore reinforced managers’ ability to integrate emerging knowledge with existing professional values, allowing leadership capacity to evolve alongside the changing healthcare environment.

Synthesis of findings: value creation as the developmental mechanism of capacity building

Collectively, the three themes indicate that capacity building is the outcome of an ongoing value-creation process rather than the simple acquisition of managerial competencies. Rather than functioning as independent contextual influences, the themes represent three interconnected developmental stages through which leadership capacity is progressively constructed across the life course of nursing managers: values are first internalized within the family, subsequently refined through social interaction within the community, and finally enacted, reinforced, and reproduced within the workplace through collaborative learning, reflective practice, continuous education, and everyday managerial decision-making.

Importantly, the findings indicate that value creation functions as the underlying mechanism linking these contextual experiences to capacity building. Participants did not perceive leadership capacity as the outcome of isolated educational interventions or the acquisition of technical managerial skills; rather, it emerged through the continuous construction, application, and reinforcement of professional values across personal, social, and organizational domains. As nursing managers enacted these values in practice, they simultaneously fostered learning environments characterized by trust, collaboration, and ethical leadership—thereby generating further value for staff, patients, and healthcare organizations.

This pattern points to a reciprocal relationship between value creation and capacity building: value-generating contexts nurture the development of capable nursing managers, while increasingly capable managers, in turn, strengthen organizational cultures that sustain continuous learning, collaboration, and further value creation. Accordingly, the proposed interpretive model conceptualizes the value-creating context not merely as the setting in which capacity building occurs, but as the developmental mechanism through which managerial capacity is continuously created, enacted, and sustained.

Discussion

The present study offers a new interpretation of capacity building among nursing managers by conceptualizing it as a developmental process embedded within a value-creating context, rather than solely as the outcome of formal managerial education or competency-based training. Although previous research has largely framed value creation as an organizational strategy for improving healthcare quality and performance, our findings suggest that it also functions as a developmental mechanism through which nursing managers acquire, internalize, and enact leadership capacity. Specifically, managerial capacity appears to be constructed through the continuous interaction of three interconnected contexts—family, community, and workplace—which collectively shape the values, attitudes, and professional capabilities required for effective nursing leadership. This process-oriented perspective constitutes the principal theoretical contribution of the study, explaining how value creation contributes to capacity building rather than treating the two concepts as parallel or unrelated phenomena.

These findings extend existing scholarship on value-based leadership in nursing management. Akbari (2023) described value creation as a systematic organizational process involving the identification, development, and sustainability of values within healthcare organizations [18]. While this perspective has substantially advanced understanding of organizational value generation, it has paid comparatively little attention to the developmental origins of leadership capacity itself. The present study broadens this view by showing that organizational value creation is preceded by the gradual development of individual capacities shaped across multiple social contexts. In other words, value is not generated solely through organizational systems or managerial practice; it emerges from leaders whose professional competencies have been progressively shaped by value-generating experiences throughout their personal and professional lives.

The first theme, family value-creating experiences, identifies the family as the earliest developmental context in which future nursing managers begin constructing the personal values that later underpin ethical leadership. Rather than simply transmitting moral principles, family experiences appeared to cultivate enduring dispositions—including responsibility, empathy, integrity, resilience, and self-confidence—that participants consistently drew upon when confronting managerial challenges. These findings align with Swanson (2011), who emphasized the family’s role in fostering learning and personal growth [19], and with López (2005), who identified family support as a fundamental contributor to capacity development [20]. Similarly, Klein (2007) proposed that families generate social and cultural capital that facilitates value creation [21]. Our findings extend this literature by suggesting that family-derived values do more than shape professional identity; they constitute the initial developmental resources upon which managerial capacity is subsequently built. The family therefore functions not only as a source of values, but as the first context in which future nursing leaders begin developing the internal capacities required for value-based leadership.

A particularly notable finding concerns the central role of self-confidence in leadership development. Participants consistently described how early opportunities to assume responsibility, combined with encouragement and emotional support from family members, strengthened their confidence and willingness to make independent decisions—experiences that later shaped their effectiveness as nursing managers. This observation supports Jooste and Cairns (2014), who argued that self-leadership, confidence, and personal responsibility are fundamental prerequisites for effective capacity building in nursing management [14]. The present findings extend this perspective, however, by suggesting that self-confidence should be understood not as an individual trait acquired during professional education, but as a developmental capability that originates in early family experience and continues to evolve throughout a manager’s career. This interpretation reframes leadership development as a life-course process rather than an organizational intervention initiated only after appointment to a managerial position.

The second theme, community-based developmental factors, demonstrates that leadership capacity develops through ongoing interaction with broader social environments. Participants described how engagement with diverse communities, exposure to influential role models, and responsiveness to cultural expectations enhanced their communication skills, social awareness, adaptability, and professional accountability. These findings support previous work highlighting the contribution of communities to knowledge exchange, resource mobilization, and capacity development [22, 23]. However, our findings move beyond framing communities merely as external sources of support; they suggest that community experience constitutes a developmental space in which individuals continuously negotiate professional values, refine interpersonal competencies, and construct leadership identities. Through repeated engagement with diverse social contexts, nursing managers learn to interpret societal expectations, balance competing interests, and develop the relational competencies that later enable them to create value within healthcare organizations.

The third theme, facilitating conditions for value realization in nursing practice, demonstrates that healthcare organizations constitute the context in which previously developed values are translated into observable leadership behavior. Participants consistently emphasized collaborative learning, knowledge exchange, learning from errors, and continuous professional development as mechanisms through which personal values become organizational capabilities. This finding is consistent with previous reports that professional values evolve through workplace experience and organizational culture [24, 25], while Suprato (2021) emphasized the importance of strengthening both professional and personal capacities to improve healthcare delivery [26], and Horton (2007) argued that professional nursing values continuously evolve in response to changing social, technological, and organizational contexts [27]. Our findings extend these observations by suggesting that workplaces do more than reinforce existing competencies: they provide the conditions through which personal values are enacted, refined, and transformed into effective managerial practice. Value realization should therefore be viewed as an active organizational process in which individual capability and contextual opportunity interact to produce leadership effectiveness.

Taken together, these findings support an ecological and developmental model of capacity building in nursing management. Rather than representing three independent categories of influence, the identified themes describe successive, interacting stages within a single continuous developmental process: family experience establishes foundational values and personal capacities; community experience broadens these capacities through social learning and identity formation; and the workplace context translates them into effective leadership practice. Through this ongoing interaction, value creation becomes the mechanism linking personal development to managerial competence. Capacity building should accordingly be understood as the cumulative outcome of sustained value-generating experiences across multiple contexts, rather than the product of isolated educational or organizational interventions. This interpretive model represents the principal contribution of the present study, offering a coherent theoretical account of how value creation shapes the development of nursing managers across their professional journey.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study is its use of Braun and Clarke’s reflexive thematic analysis, which allowed an interpretive exploration of how nursing managers construct leadership capacity across personal, social, and organizational contexts, and enabled the development of an explanatory framework conceptualizing capacity building as a value-generating developmental process. Recruiting participants from diverse managerial positions further enriched the range of leadership experiences captured.

Several limitations warrant consideration. The study was conducted within a single university-affiliated healthcare system and cultural context; consequently, the organizational structures, educational opportunities, and cultural influences shaping participants’ experiences may not be fully transferable to other settings. Consistent with the aims of reflexive thematic analysis, however, the goal was not statistical generalization but the development of contextually grounded interpretations offering theoretical insight applicable to similar settings. Further qualitative and mixed-methods research across diverse organizational and cultural contexts is needed to refine and extend the transferability of the proposed conceptual model.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that value creation should be understood not merely as an organizational outcome, but as the developmental process through which nursing managers’ leadership capacity is constructed. Findings show that this capacity develops through the dynamic interaction of three contexts: family experiences that cultivate foundational values and self-confidence, community experiences that strengthen social learning and professional identity, and workplace environments that translate these capacities into effective leadership through collaboration and continuous professional development.

Capacity building in nursing management therefore extends beyond the acquisition of technical competence, representing instead a longitudinal process in which values are progressively internalized, enacted, and reinforced across multiple life contexts. This interpretive model offers a novel theoretical contribution to the nursing management literature and a conceptual foundation for designing leadership development strategies that may ultimately support stronger nursing leadership and higher-quality patient care.

Implications for nursing management

These findings carry several practical implications. First, leadership development programs should move beyond competency-based training to adopt a developmental perspective, incorporating structured mentorship, coaching, and reflective dialogue that help translate personal values into leadership practice. Second, organizational leaders should cultivate workplace environments—characterized by psychological safety, collaborative learning, and constructive feedback—that support value creation and allow managers to integrate ethical values into decision-making. Third, self-confidence, self-leadership, and personal accountability should be fostered throughout nurses’ careers, beginning well before appointment to managerial roles, through experiential learning and mentorship. Finally, nursing curricula at all educational levels should embed value-based leadership principles alongside technical competencies, equipping future managers to generate value for patients, staff, and healthcare organizations alike.
