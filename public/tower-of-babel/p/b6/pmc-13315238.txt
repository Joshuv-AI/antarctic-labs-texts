New insights into outer membrane vesicles in Gram-negative bacteria from biogenesis to applications
Authors: Bitnara Kim, Yongjun Son, Jihye Yang, Woojun Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNano-sized outer membrane vesicles (OMVs) are lipid-bilayered structures that primarily encapsulate periplasmic components, with minor inclusion of cytoplasmic materials. Rather than passive byproducts of cellular damage, OMVs are now understood as active mediators of bacterial physiology, environmental adaptation, and host interaction. Recent evidence identifies envelope instability as a key mechanistic driver of OMV biogenesis. Disruptions in outer membrane–peptidoglycan connectivity, imbalances in periplasmic homeostasis, and alterations in lipid asymmetry collectively promote vesicle formation as a regulated adaptive response. Under stress conditions, OMVs acquire specialized functional roles, selectively enriching specific cargo and exhibiting surface properties that enable them to sequester host-derived antimicrobial factors. OMV-associated biomolecules further influence vesicle uptake into host cells through distinct endocytic pathways, shaping intracellular trafficking and downstream functional outcomes. Following internalization, pathogen-derived OMVs disrupt host signaling pathways and are exploited to promote immune evasion, whereas commensal-derived OMVs contribute to microbiota homeostasis and immune modulation. In parallel, growing efforts to harness OMVs as vaccine platforms highlight their potential as innovative tools for both therapeutic and prophylactic applications. Collectively, these insights position OMVs as critical mediators of bacterial pathogenicity and as promising targets for anti-infective strategies.Subject terms: Bacterial pathogenesis, Bacteriology

Introduction

Outer membrane vesicles (OMVs), originating from Gram-negative bacteria, are natural nanoparticles ranging from 20 to 400 nm in diameter, making them 5-50 times smaller than bacterial cells and 400-600 times smaller than eukaryotic cells1,2. Thus, OMVs were considered as debris from bacterial membranes of damaged cells, lacking internal bioactive components and not essential for bacterial function3,4. However, OMVs contain specific bacteria-associated adhesins, virulence factors, and gene-regulatory biomolecules, including RNA, proteins, and lipids5–7. It is likely that OMVs exhibit heterogeneity, with significant variations in size and cargo composition in response to dynamic environmental cues and cellular states. However, whether OMV size correlates with the extent of compositional diversity, ranging from cargo-rich to empty vesicles, has not been sufficiently investigated. The mechanisms underlying the incorporation of periplasmic substances into OMVs (e.g., the Tol-Pal complex, β-lactamases, and periplasmic chaperones such as SurA and Skp) are relatively well understood1. In contrast, several proteomic studies have consistently identified cytosolic components, including nucleic acids (DNA and RNA), ribosomal proteins, and cytosolic chaperones or translation-related factors (e.g., GroEL and elongation factor Tu), within OMV preparations, although the mechanisms responsible for their incorporation remain unclear1,6,7. These proteins may be incorporated during vesiculation through transient membrane–cytoplasm interactions, nonselective encapsulation, or inner membrane (IM) leakage that can occur under stress conditions. Interestingly, OMV-mediated gene transfer was demonstrated in clinical strains of carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii ATCC 19606, where OMVs transfer the blaOXA-24 gene and the metallo-β-lactamase-1 (blaNDM-1) gene to carbapenem-sensitive A. baumannii or Escherichia coli JM1098. Packed with proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and metabolites, OMVs are vital for bacterial survival, aiding nutrient acquisition, environmental sensing, and intercellular communication9,10. Siderophores and quorum-sensing molecules in OMVs contribute to bacterial survival in iron-limited environments and play a key role in cell-cell communication11,12. Additionally, their ability to carry immunomodulatory molecules allows OMVs to manipulate host immune systems, aiding bacterial survival and persistence within the host. As such, OMVs are no longer viewed as passive byproducts but as active mediators of bacterial communication and pathogenicity.

Numerous subsequent studies have further elucidated the composition of OMVs derived from pathogenic Gram-negative bacteria, including A. baumannii, E. coli, and Helicobacter pylori12–14. While outer membrane (OM) and periplasmic proteins, such as OmpA, Tol-Pal, and LolA, are commonly found across bacterial OMVs, species-specific cargo loading plays a crucial role in pathogenicity. For example, A. baumannii strains OMVs may carry blaOXA-58, a carbapenemase, and E. coli can contain Shiga toxin 2a and hemolysin15,16. In H. pylori, OMVs are enriched with cytotoxin VacA, which facilitates gastric colonization and immune evasion13. This tailored cargo loading allows each bacterial species to adapt to its specific niche, enhancing its ability to mediate pathogenic effects and manipulate host responses. The gastric cancer-causing H. pylori strain P12 produces OMVs enriched with adhesins (e.g., BabA and SabA), oncoprotein CagA, and the cytotoxin VacA, leading to enhanced bacterial adhesion, immune evasion, and damage to gastric epithelial cells, contributing to the development of gastric inflammation and cancer17,18. Functional analyses of Porphyromonas gingivalis OMVs suggest they contribute to periodontal disease progression by carrying peptidylarginine deiminase (PPAD) and adhesins like FimA and Mfa1, which disrupt amino acid balance and impair the host immune system19.

Although OMVs share a similar surface composition and properties with parent bacterial cells, the proportions of lipopolysaccharide (LPS), porins, and phospholipids (PL) such as phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), phosphatidylglycerol, and cardiolipin (CL) may differ due to the reduction of porins and alterations in phospholipid composition, which causes blebbing and eventual OMV generation20–22. CL enrichment can facilitate membrane curvature owing to its cone-like molecular geometry, inducing membrane bending and contributing to OM remodeling and vesicle formation20,23. However, the extent to which such lipid-driven curvature directly contributes to OMV biogenesis likely varies depending on species and environmental conditions. Despite a low proportion of OM proteins on the surface, OMVs retain the ability to effectively stimulate the immune system, triggering both innate and adaptive immune responses and influencing host-pathogen interaction4. OMVs modulate the immune system through a dual approach; surface components like LPS engage pattern recognition receptors such as TLR4 on the host cell surface to initiate immune signaling, while internalized cargo activates intracellular sensors, leading to synergistic amplification of immune responses. Importantly, OMVs derived from diverse bacteria can stimulate not only intracellular LPS sensors but also TLR4-mediated responses at the host cell surface24,25. OMV-derived LPS triggers an inflammatory response by binding to caspase-11 in the host cytosol after escaping from the early endosome26. While this process has been identified, the mechanisms governing their internalization into host cells and the release of their cargo in association with host-binding proteins remain poorly understood. Immunization of the leukopenic rat with Haemophilus influenzae OMVs extractions using a method simpler than that required for LPS purification has been shown to elicit an immune response comparable to that induced by LPS. This suggests that OMVs may serve as an effective and more easily producible alternative for immunization strategies27. Historically overlooked due to their small size and hollow appearance under electron microscopy, OMVs are now increasingly recognized for their critical role in host-pathogen interactions and immune modulation4,28. Further investigation into the mechanisms and functions of OMVs is essential to advancing our understanding of bacterial pathogenesis and evaluating their potential as targets for vaccine development, immunomodulatory therapies, and targeted drug delivery, all of which may offer significant benefits for human health.

Mechanisms of OMV biogenesis in Gram-negative bacteria

OMVs have been classified into two types based on the underlying mechanisms: explosive cell lysis (lytic OMVs) and OM blebbing (non-lytic OMVs) in Gram-negative bacteria29. The production of lytic OMVs is triggered by antibiotics and genotoxic stress, which activate the expression of prophage-derived endolysin, resulting in the degradation of the peptidoglycan (PG) layer30. The disruption of the PG layer leads to explosive cell lysis, resulting in the formation of scattered membrane fragments that fuse to create lytic OMVs. In contrast, the mechanisms of non-lytic OMV biogenesis have been suggested to involve the instability of OM-PG linkages, an increase in OM curvature, and heightened periplasmic turgor pressures4,31,32. Vesicles generated by these mechanisms can differ in membrane composition. In addition to classical OMVs, bacterial membrane vesicles can include OM-IM vesicles (OIMVs), which contain both OM and IM components. Distinguishing true “non-lytic” OMVs from other vesicle subtypes is challenging; therefore, all vesicles are hereafter uniformly referred to as OMVs. In some cases, OIMVs are formed through non-lytic blebbing, in which the IM protrudes into OM bulges and becomes enclosed within them. In contrast, E-OIMVs arise from explosive cell lysis, during which fragmented OM and IM segments fuse to encapsulate cytoplasmic components. In both processes, portions of the IM and cytoplasmic material are enclosed together with the OM, forming composite vesicles known as OIMVs and E-OIMVs, respectively33. Furthermore, current vesicle isolation methods often rely on bulk purification approaches such as density gradient ultracentrifugation (e.g., sucrose or iodixanol/OptiPrep gradients), which may not effectively distinguish between vesicle subtypes34,35. As a result, co-isolation of heterogeneous vesicle populations, including OIMVs and lysis-derived vesicles, can obscure the interpretation of OMV biogenesis mechanisms, making it difficult to determine how specific vesicle types are formed. In contrast, Gram-positive bacteria, which lack an OM, produce vesicles that are more generally referred to as extracellular vesicles (EVs)6.

As the core molecular mechanisms underlying OMV biogenesis have been comprehensively documented elsewhere, a brief summary of the conceptual models that have emerged for OMV formation (Fig. 1). A major determinant of OMV biogenesis is the weakening of OM–PG tethering (Fig. 1A). The abundant Braun’s lipoprotein (Lpp) and outer membrane protein A (OmpA) are anchored in the OM, where they physically interact with and stabilize the underlying PG layer36,37. Genetic disruption or stress-induced downregulation of these tethers, including σE-regulated small RNAs targeting lpp and ompA, significantly enhances vesiculation38–40. Similarly, perturbation of the Tol–Pal system, which coordinates OM constriction during cell division, results in pronounced hypervesiculation accompanied by envelope instability41,42. Dynamic remodeling of PG synthesis and turnover also plays a pivotal role in OMV production (Fig. 1B). Inhibition of penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs) by β-lactam antibiotics or D-cycloserine leads to imbalanced PG architecture and uncoupling of the OM from the underlying cell wall, promoting outward membrane bulging and vesicle release43,44. Even sub-lethal perturbations of PG biosynthesis are sufficient to induce hypervesiculation, indicating that OMV formation functions as a compensatory response to envelope stress rather than a terminal outcome of cell damage45. Periplasmic crowding represents another major stress axis driving OMV biogenesis (Fig. 1C)46. Accumulation of misfolded or unincorporated proteins under heat, oxidative, osmotic, or envelope stress increases periplasmic turgor pressure, favoring OM protrusion and vesicle release46,47. Loss of periplasmic quality-control proteases such as DegP exacerbates this congestion and triggers robust vesiculation across diverse Gram-negative species48. Alterations in membrane lipid composition profoundly influence OMV biogenesis (Fig. 1D)33. Stress-induced remodeling of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) structure, including changes in O-antigen length or lipid A acylation, modifies membrane rigidity, surface charge, and curvature49. Lipid-modifying enzymes (e.g., PagL) and pH-responsive outer membrane proteins (e.g., PagC) drive vesiculation through asymmetric leaflet expansion and membrane conformational switching. In addition to LPS remodeling, disruption of phospholipid asymmetry represents another key lipid-driven mechanism of OMV biogenesis50,51. Impairment of the Mla (VacJ/Yrb) pathway disrupts OM lipid asymmetry, leading to phospholipid accumulation in the outer leaflet of the OM and then increased vesiculation50. This asymmetric expansion promotes curvature-driven budding and increased OMV production, a mechanism demonstrated in Haemophilus influenzae and supported by conserved hypervesiculation phenotypes in E. coli and Vibrio cholerae. Beyond protein-mediated remodeling, biophysical perturbations of the OM can also be driven by small amphipathic molecules. Small amphipathic molecules such as the Pseudomonas quinolone signal (PQS) further enhance OMV formation by intercalating into the OM and generating interleaflet tension52. Together, these findings suggest that OMV formation reflects a cellular response to envelope stress, emerging from coordinated changes in cell wall dynamics, periplasmic balance, and membrane composition. While numerous recent reviews have extensively addressed the roles of OMVs in interbacterial communication—where OMV-mediated cell–cell interactions support bacterial survival under stressful conditions such as antibiotic exposure, nutrient limitation, and host-associated biofilms—their functions in host-associated contexts remain less well explored1,4,6. Accordingly, this review focuses on OMV-mediated interactions in host–microbe systems.

Stress-associated differences in OMV cargo composition

Pathogens generate stress-dependent remodeling of OMV cargo composition as part of a multifaceted adaptive response53,54. Upon infection, exposure to hostile environments (e.g., antibiotics, iron limitation, acidic pH, oxidative stress) is accompanied by changes in vesicular content, often enriched in factors associated with envelope stabilization, stress mitigation, and nutrient acquisition12,43,55. While often viewed as strategic, these cargo patterns are more likely driven by stress-induced expression changes that remodel the envelope, leading to biased OMV content. Accumulating evidence suggests that stress-associated OMV cargo composition arises from overlapping and often concurrent processes, rather than from a single, discrete mechanism: (i) envelope stress—although the precise mechanisms remain unclear, conditions associated with IM perturbation or increased permeability may permit the aberrant inclusion of cytosolic proteins or drive the mislocalization of periplasmic proteins, facilitating their unintended incorporation into OMVs; (ii) overproduction of specific proteins under stress can drive mass action–dependent enrichment, whereby elevated local abundance near vesiculation sites biases their probabilistic packaging, often in conjunction with membrane perturbations; and (iii) stress conditions frequently induce de novo expression of stress-responsive proteins that, by concurrently engaging the processes described in (i) and (ii), become preferentially incorporated into OMV cargo53–55. Analysis of stress-modulated OMV cargo provides insight into how parent cells respond to defined stress conditions. Enrichment of specific proteins and alterations in OMV surface composition allow vesicles to function as molecular decoys that sequester antibiotics or other toxic compounds, while degradative enzymes packaged within OMVs may further neutralize or inactivate these harmful agents. These properties highlight how stress-conditioned OMVs contribute to bacterial persistence and support pathogen survival during infection. Under meropenem stress, carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae releases OMVs enriched with β-lactamases, as revealed by OMV proteomic analyses; these vesicles directly mediate extracellular degradation of meropenem, which in turn enhances bacterial survival53. Additionally, β-lactamase-positive OMVs released by amoxicillin-resistant strains of Moraxella catarrhalis protect amoxicillin-susceptible M. catarrhalis strains and other cohabiting β-lactam–susceptible pathogens (Streptococcus pneumoniae and Haemophilus influenzae), by extracellularly degrading amoxicillin, supporting interspecies survival under β-lactam stress54. Notably, in A. baumannii, recently characterized lipidated OXA-type β-lactamases (e.g., OXA-23 and OXA-24/40) are covalently anchored to the membrane, promoting their efficient incorporation and stable enrichment in OMVs, where they form a high-density extracellular catalytic surface that hydrolyzes β-lactam antibiotics and protects otherwise susceptible A. baumannii, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa from piperacillin and imipenem56. In Elizabethkingia anophelis, imipenem exposure does not preferentially drive the incorporation of β-lactam–degrading enzymes into OMVs but instead enriches OMVs with OM proteins and nutrient uptake systems, including TonB-dependent receptors and Rag/Sus family proteins57. Although this cargo profile has been interpreted as reflecting rapid envelope remodeling that supports survival under antibiotic stress, the causal link between imipenem-induced OMV remodeling and bacterial survival remains unproven and requires direct experimental validation57.

Because invading pathogens face severe iron limitations imposed by host iron-sequestering proteins, they deploy OMVs to support iron acquisition during infection58,59. Consistent with this role, OMVs isolated from Histophilus somni under iron-limited conditions are enriched in the transferrin-binding proteins TbpA and TbpB, indicating that OMVs can package iron-uptake machinery as an adaptive response to iron restriction in the host environment60. Similarly, Neisseria gonorrhoeae under iron-restricted conditions remodels its OMV proteome to selectively accumulate bacterioferritin, which contributes to metabolic adaptation and iron storage61. By concentrating this essential factor, OMVs act as extracellular survival kits that enhance bacterial fitness amid host-imposed nutritional immunity. Mechanistically, these observations suggest that OMVs may provide an accessible extracellular platform that facilitates the mobilization and delivery of host-sequestered ferric iron (Fe3+), enhancing bacterial survival under iron-limited conditions. Beyond iron limitation, pathogens must navigate a gauntlet of physiological stressors across diverse host niches. Acidic environments are frequently encountered within the stomach, phagolysosomes of immune cells, and the Salmonella-containing vacuole, while reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated by neutrophils and macrophages during the oxidative burst. Furthermore, pathogens must survive the detergent-like effects of bile salts in the gastrointestinal tract62–64. Under phagosome-mimicking acidic conditions, Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium releases OMVs enriched with specific transporters and stress-associated proteins, including the amino acid transporter OppA, the polyamine transporter PotD, and the magnesium transporter MgtA, reflecting an adaptive OMV proteome that might support survival under acidic stress65. Under oxidative stress, the gastric pathogen Helicobacter pylori releases OMVs enriched with the catalase KatA, which function as extracellular antioxidant particles that intercept and detoxify ROS before they reach the bacterial cell surface66. Such OMV-mediated antioxidant protection is not restricted to pathogenic contexts. Beyond host-associated environments, in aquatic systems, OMVs released by Pseudoduganella aquatica under oxidative stress contain the catalase KatA, which detoxifies hydrogen peroxide and protects Microcystis aeruginosa despite its intrinsic sensitivity to ROS67. Beyond simple survival, bile salt stress primes pathogens for enhanced virulence by reshaping OMV cargo. In Campylobacter jejuni, bile exposure induces OMVs enriched in colonization-associated adhesins (CadF and FlpA) and bile efflux components (CmeA and CmeC), along with cytolethal distending toxins (CdtA, CdtB, and CdtC) and the capsule biogenesis factor KpsD, indicating that bile salts act as a key cue for OMV remodeling that promotes colonization, host damage, and immune modulation during intestinal infection68. Hypervesiculation in V. cholerae provides a clear example of OMV biogenesis functioning as an adaptive mechanism during host colonization51. Under iron limitation conditions induced by the iron chelator 2,2′-bipyridyl, transcription of the VacJ/Yrb (Mla) phospholipid transporter is repressed, leading to phospholipid accumulation in the outer leaflet of the OM and then increased vesiculation51. The resulting hypervesiculation accelerates OM remodeling through the accumulation of glycine-modified lipid A and the depletion of OmpT, which is associated with increased resistance to polymyxin B (PMB) and bile salts during host infection. These changes enhance resistance to host-derived stresses and ultimately contribute to improved intestinal colonization in vivo.

Although envelope-stabilizing systems (e.g., the Tol–Pal complex, Lpp/OmpA-mediated tethering, PG remodeling, and lipid asymmetry) are key determinants of OMV biogenesis, their depletion or functional impairment during vesiculation makes their presence within OMVs as functional components unlikely. Nevertheless, OMVs generated under host-associated stresses can function as extracellular decoys that physically intercept antimicrobial factors. Their intrinsic physicochemical properties, including surface charge and lipid composition, mediate interactions with a broad range of antimicrobial agents such as antibiotics, antimicrobial peptides, antibodies, and bacteriophages and confer enhanced bacterial survival under host-associated stresses. In clinical isolates of A. baumannii, decreased levels of the OM–PG tethering protein OmpA are associated with increased OMV production. These vesicles function as extracellular decoys that sequester PMB and enhance bacterial survival69. In V. cholerae, downregulation of the VacJ/Yrb (Mla) system under iron limitation conditions led to increased OMV production, which can counteract host immune defenses, particularly antibodies such as secretory IgA, by acting as extracellular decoys50. Hypervesiculation in the ΔtolR mutant of Salmonella Typhi led to the production of OMVs that act as extracellular decoys by sequestering PMB, enabling survival under otherwise lethal PMB concentrations43. Additionally, these OMVs confer protection to co-cultured susceptible WT cells, allowing their survival under lethal PMB conditions, whereas cells fail to survive in the absence of OMVs. In Campylobacter jejuni, the hypervesiculating ΔmlaA mutant exhibited increased resistance to PMB without changes in membrane permeability, suggesting that elevated OMV production may contribute to resistance by acting as extracellular decoys70. While the precise determinants of OMV formation are not fully understood, numerous studies have reported that OMVs can function as extracellular decoys that protect bacterial cells against a broad range of antimicrobial agents. Exposure of Gram-negative bacteria (e.g., E. coli, Bordetella bronchiseptica, and P. aeruginosa) to sublethal concentrations of host defense peptides, including PMAP-36 and CATH-2, led to increased OMV production, which acted as decoys to sequester antimicrobial peptides, resulting in enhanced bacterial survival71. Extending this concept to bacteriophages, V. cholerae OMVs have been shown to inhibit bacteriophage infection by acting as receptor-bearing decoys72. OMVs reduce phage-mediated killing in a dose-dependent manner, as demonstrated by plaque assays, by binding phage tail fibers and preventing productive adsorption to bacterial cells. Collectively, OMVs promote bacterial survival under host-associated stresses through both cargo-associated functions and their role as extracellular decoys that physically intercept antimicrobial factors. This dual mode of action highlights the synergistic contributions of cargo‑mediated and surface‑mediated defense mechanisms

Entry routes and pathogenic roles of OMVs in host cells

Compared to the direct release of proteins and signaling molecules, OMVs can be considered specialized secretion systems that benefit pathogenic bacteria16,73. By encapsulating biomolecules within the spherical, protective OMVs, host degradation systems may be inaccessible, which enhances the stability and longevity of these biomolecules in the extracellular environment. Furthermore, OMVs can fuse with or be internalized by host cells, leading to the direct delivery of biomolecules into the host cytoplasm, altering host signaling pathways and immune responses to enhance bacterial survival and pathogenesis74. For example, OMVs are involved in the delivery of diverse bioactive factors, including toxins (e.g., Shiga toxin), invasion factors (e.g., phospholipase, hemolysin, and protease), and cytotoxic molecules (e.g., LPS and OmpA)9,16,26,75. Tissue-specific distribution of OMV contents within the host may occur due to the targeted delivery of OMVs to specific host tissues or cell types (Fig. 2)16,76. Although OMVs are well-established toxin shuttles for pathogens, their entry pathways into host cells remain incompletely understood. Recent studies have identified multiple internalization mechanisms, including clathrin- and caveolin-mediated endocytosis, lipid raft-dependent uptake, and macropinocytosis.

Clathrin-mediated endocytosis as a central pathway for OMV uptake

OMV internalization via canonical clathrin-mediated endocytosis (CME) is thought to begin with OMVs binding to unknown host surface receptors (Fig. 2A)77,78. Binding of OMVs to the host receptors triggers the recruitment of the adaptor protein AP-2 to the receptors, which accelerates the accumulation of clathrin triskelions and the subsequent formation of a curved membrane structure79. Clathrin triskelions assemble into polyhedral cages and hexagonal lattices on the membrane, forming a structured coat that deforms the membrane by altering its intrinsic curvature79. As the membrane invaginates and a coated pit forms, the large GTPase dynamin (~96 kDa) constricts the neck of the budding vesicle. Through GTP hydrolysis, polymerized dynamin generates a mechanical force that drives membrane scission at the vesicle neck, resulting in the release of a clathrin-coated vesicle into the cytoplasm77,80. Given that clathrin-coated pits typically measure around 200 nm in diameter, they might accommodate many small OMVs (~20 nm). However, larger OMVs exceeding 200 nm might be expected to rely on other endocytic mechanisms, such as macropinocytosis or caveolin-mediated uptake, depending on their size and surface characteristics77. Because a limited number of unknown host receptors may be engaged by larger OMVs, their binding affinity to the host receptor might not be strong enough to effectively hold and recruit the OMVs through the CME pathway.

It has been documented using dynasore, an inhibitor of dynamin formation, that hemolysin from Edwardsiella tarda, a bacterial pore-forming toxin packaged inside OMVs, can enter Caco-2 cells through dynamin-dependent endocytosis, which is associated with severe intestinal infections75. Upon entry, the integrity of the OMV-containing vacuole is impaired, resulting in the exposure of OMV-derived LPS to the cytosol of the host, which subsequently triggers the activation of the LPS-mediated non-canonical inflammasome pathway. This activation resulted in the release of inflammatory cytokines followed by pyroptosis81. Similarly, studies on P. gingivalis, an invasive bacterium associated with chronic periodontitis, revealed that dynasore also blocked the entry of P. gingivalis OMVs into human oral keratinocytes in a dose-dependent manner82. The cellular uptake of H. pylori OMVs carrying the cytotoxin VacA is linked to the activation of a non-canonical inflammasome pathway, followed by pyroptosis, which correlates with increased micronuclei formation in AGS human gastric epithelial cells. However, the step-by-step processes remain to be elucidated83. Although the CME pathway was inhibited using clathrin inhibitors (chlorpromazine and chloropropyl), micronuclei formation still occurred in a VacA-dependent manner, suggesting the presence of an alternative uptake mechanism for H. pylori OMVs. Unlike the host membrane, Brucella abortus OMVs, which are derived from bacterial membranes and enriched in phosphatidylethanolamine, were efficiently labeled with monodansylcadaverine (MDC)84. Upon internalization into THP-1 monocyte cell lines via CME, a decrease in fluorescence signal was observed, likely due to the reduced accessibility of MDC to OMVs because OMVs are enclosed within clathrin coated-eukaryotic plasma membrane, in contrast to alternative uptake pathways that utilize lipid endosomal compartments. The possibility that the reduced MDC labeling of OMVs might result from caveolin-mediated endocytosis (CvME), rather than CME, cannot be ruled out.

Role of caveolin-mediated and lipid raft endocytosis

Besides the CME pathway, the CvME can take up various small biomolecules, including toxins, signaling molecules, and viruses, by forming caveolin vacuoles ranging from 60 to 80 nm in size (Fig. 2B)85. Thus, small OMVs, but not bacterial cells, may be delivered into the host cytosol through CvME. The CvME proceeds at a rate fivefold slower than CME because caveolae are pre-formed, flask-shaped membrane invaginations with highly stable structures, making their formation and disassembly inherently slower than the dynamic formation and rapid scission of clathrin-coated pits through the CME pathway86. In addition, caveolar vesicles often remain docked at the plasma membrane for extended periods before internalization, further contributing to the slower kinetics of this endocytic pathway87. If various bacterial toxins (e.g., heat-labile enterotoxin, cholera toxin, P. aeruginosa exotoxin A) and OM proteins (OmpU and OmpT) are recognized by different host receptors (e.g., MHC class I and GM1-like gangliosides), lipid rafts enriched in cholesterol and sphingolipids can be remodeled by caveolin-1, driving the formation of caveolae-coated pits88,89. Both caveolin and cavin coordinate to sculpt the membrane into flask-shaped caveolae by the following steps: (i) Caveolin-1 integrates into membrane regions with tightly packed lipids and low fluidity, causing the membrane to bend inward and (ii) then, a cavin-assisted stabilization of membrane curvature occurs. (iii) Similar to the CME pathway, caveolae-coated pits are eventually released from the plasma membrane through dynamin-mediated scission. It is speculated that OMVs derived from bacteria with sphingolipid-enriched OM, such as Sphingobacterium and Bacteroides, might engage in strong interactions with sphingolipids of the host membrane, which may be involved in their internalization through CvME.

The most compelling evidence for OMV internalization through CvME lies in the interaction between specific OM proteins (OmpA and OmpU) and GM1-like gangliosides90. The OM of V. cholerae is highly enriched in OmpT and OmpU, a trait mirrored in its OMVs, which interact with GM1-gangliosides within host lipid rafts, facilitating their entry into host cells via CvME91. Because the presence of OmpT and OmpU in OMVs from other Gram-negative bacteria is not well characterized, the extent to which Omp-mediated CvME contributes to OMV uptake in different bacterial systems is still unclear. The use of filipin, a free cholesterol-binding agent, to disrupt cholesterol-rich lipid rafts partially demonstrated CvME- or lipid raft-mediated uptake of OMVs derived from Moraxella catarrhalis BBH18 into human alveolar and pharyngeal epithelial cells, as the internalization of FITC-labeled OMVs was no longer observed following filipin treatment92,93. Similarly, CvME-mediated OMV entry was not observed in human pharyngeal epithelial cells when OMVs derived from H. influenzae were used with filipin93. Interestingly, heat-labile enterotoxin (LT) from enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC), which functions as both a toxin and an adhesion factor, can be secreted via the Type II Secretion System (T2SS) involving GspCDE94. Once secreted, LT can localize on the surface of its own OMVs by binding to the LPS of the OMVs95. Electrostatic interactions mediate the association of LPS with the B subunit of the LT toxin, displaying the toxin on the OMV surface96. The LT-OMV complex can then bind to GM1-like ganglioside receptors on the host cell membrane, specifically within cholesterol-rich microdomains97,98. Generally, the B subunit of LT toxin is known to bind to the neolacto (Galβ4GlcNAc) core chains of gangliosides on host cells. Contrary to this notion, recent studies suggest that the A subunit of LT toxin may associate with LPS, while the B subunit is predicted to remain available, allowing the binding of B subunit to ganglioside of host cells98. Immunofluorescence microscopy confirmed the colocalization of caveolin and OMV. Additionally, OMVs from LT-deficient strains showed a 60% reduction in host cell association, highlighting the role of specific OMV possessing LT for host entry via CvME pathway99. Unfortunately, the precise role of caveolae in OMV internalization and tissue dissemination remains unclear, as no direct inhibitors of caveolin-1 have been identified. However, the involvement of CvME in OMV uptake can be inferred through treatments with cholesterol and dynamine inhibitors, key lipid raft components, as well as caveolin-1 staining. CvME drives the internalization of ligand-bound receptors within lipid rafts; however, the distinction between lipid raft-mediated endocytosis and CvME remains elusive, as it primarily depends on receptor presence.

Lipid rafts begin to invaginate with one or multiple microdomains rich in heterogeneous cholesterol and sphingolipids, ultimately creating lipid raft vacuoles ranging from 50 to 500 nm in diameter (Fig. 2C)100. Thus, lipid rafts serve as platforms for the internalization of pathogenic bacteria under environmental stress conditions (e.g., Campylobacter jejuni, E. coli, and P. aeruginosa) and a wide range of biomolecules (e.g., signaling molecules, toxins, and OMVs)101. Upon specific binding of an external biomolecule (e.g., LPS and flagellin) to its own receptor (e.g., TLR4 and TLR5) located on the outer leaflet of the OM, the entire lipid bilayer may undergo localized invagination, accompanied by an enlargement of the inner leaflet of the OM, resembling the inward deformation of a compressed balloon102,103. Then, cholesterol is recruited to this region via lateral diffusion, stabilizing the membrane curvature by enhancing hydrophobic cohesion through intrinsic lipid–cholesterol interactions104. It is interesting to note that the depletion of plasma membrane cholesterol by methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MBCD), which sequesters and removes cholesterol from the membrane layer, effectively dismantles lipid rafts105. These findings highlight the essential role of cholesterol in maintaining the structural integrity and stability of lipid rafts. Treatment with MBCD, which depletes membrane cholesterol, reduces P. gingivalis uptake into mouse macrophages by 50%, by blocking its entry through lipid raft-mediated pathways106. Pre-treatment of human intestinal epithelial cells with MBCD prior to C. jejuni OMV exposure significantly reduced the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines IL-8, IL-6, and TNF-α, highlighting the cholesterol dependence of OMV entry and the following inflammatory responses107. While the integrity of lipid rafts and the critical role of cholesterol in pathogen-host interactions are well established, the precise mechanism by which raft domains cluster upon binding to external molecules and undergo membrane invagination for internalization remains elusive.

Macropinocytosis as an alternative system for OMV uptake

Macropinocytosis, primarily involved in the uptake of extracellular fluid materials (e.g., apoptotic bodies, nutrients, and exosomes), is a non-specific form of internalization as it does not rely on a specific receptor (Fig. 2D)108,109. Interestingly, certain bacteria (e.g., Shigella and Salmonella) exploit macropinocytosis to invade host cells, either by hijacking membrane ruffling process or actively inducing it through the injection of effector proteins via the type III secretion system (T3SS) (e.g., IpgB1/2 and IpgD in Shigella; SipA, SipC, SopB, and SptP in Salmonella), which drives their internalization110,111. Because OMVs cannot actively secrete effectors, they may be passively internalized through ongoing macropinocytosis. Macropinosomes formed as a result of macropinocytosis are spacious vacuoles (0.1–5 μm in diameter), which allow the uptake of bacteria, as well as bacterial OMVs during bacterial infection112. Lipid rafts and macropinocytosis might occur at the same location where lipid raft microdomains contain receptors associated with lipid rafts, along with signaling events (e.g., Rac1, Cdc42, PI3K, and Ras) that drive actin cytoskeletal remodeling and membrane ruffling113.

Receptor-mediated endocytosis pathways, such as clathrin- and caveolin-dependent uptake, typically require specific interactions between OMV-associated ligands and host cell surface receptors to initiate internalization. Clathrin-coated pits preferentially internalize small OMVs (≤ 200 nm), whereas caveolin-enriched domains are tuned to accommodate vesicles of even more limited dimensions (~60–80 nm)114,115. In contrast, macropinocytosis is a receptor-independent, non-selective mechanism driven by actin-mediated membrane ruffling, and is capable of internalizing large extracellular structures, including OMVs, some of which are larger than 100 nm116. OMV size appears to be a critical determinant of the internalization route. Although the surface of larger OMVs theoretically have more ligands for potential receptor engagement, their uptake is more likely to proceed via macropinocytosis regardless of receptor interactions, because their size exceeds the structural limitations of clathrin- and caveolin-mediated endocytosis53. This suggests that any ligand–receptor interactions that occur during macropinocytosis are incidental, rather than essential for triggering OMVs internalization. Larger H. pylori-derived OMVs (90-450 nm) were shown to enter human gastric adenocarcinoma and embryonic kidney cells exclusively via a process resembling macropinocytosis without any ligand-receptor interaction, which was proven by using the siRNA techniques for inhibiting clathrin- and caveolin-dependent endocytosis53. A variety of actin polymerization inhibitors (e.g., cytochalasin D, wiskostatin, and amiloride) have been employed to indirectly support the involvement of macropinocytosis in the internalization of OMVs into host cells. The use of actin polymerization inhibitors such as cytochalasin D, which prevents actin filament elongation, and wiskostatin, which inhibits N-WASP and thus blocks branched actin polymerization markedly reduced the uptake of P. aeruginosa-derived OMVs into airway epithelial cells, indicating a critical role for actin dynamics in OMV uptake via macropinocytosis117. Actin polymerization, regulated by Rac1, a key modulator of actin cytoskeleton dynamics, was inhibited by treatment with amiloride, a known Rac1 signaling inhibitor, resulting in a 63.4% reduction in the internalization of Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron-derived OMVs into Caco-2 epithelial cells via macropinocytosis-mediated uptake118.

Through macropinocytosis, internalized bacteria and OMVs are sequestered within macropinosomes, compartments that may be nutrient-poor, weakly acidic, and exhibit a gel-like physical state119,120. These macropinosomes subsequently fuse with lysosomes as part of the endosomal maturation process, leading to progressive acidification, accumulation of hydrolytic enzymes, and degradation of many components121. As a result, OMVs are expected to undergo structural destabilization and breakdown, potentially due to misfolding of OMV-associated proteins or changes in LPS charge under acidic conditions. In contrast to OMVs, Salmonella possesses specialized mechanisms to adapt and reside under intracellular conditions, escaping from macropinosomes122. Through the action of effector proteins, such as SopB, SipA, and SipC, delivered via T3SS, Salmonella induces host membrane remodeling, leading to vacuolar rupture and subsequent escape into the cytosol123. However, the precise mechanisms underlying bacterial escape from macropinosomes remain incompletely understood. It also remains unclear whether OMVs can be produced within macropinosomes and whether Salmonella-derived OMVs contribute to or modulate the escape process, highlighting the need for further investigation. Interestingly, a new model has been proposed in which S. flexneri enters host cells through macropinocytosis, becoming enclosed within a bacterium-containing vacuole (BCV)124. Newly formed macropinosomes, induced by the S. flexneri effector IpgD, subsequently come into direct contact with the pre-formed BCV, promoting its rupture and enabling bacterial escape into the host cytosol124. IpgD, an inositol phosphate phosphatase, converts PI(4,5)P₂ to PI(5)P at the host plasma membrane, enhancing localized membrane remodeling and destabilization at macropinosome–vacuole contact sites.

It should be noted, however, that conclusions regarding OMV internalization pathways derived solely from pharmacological inhibitor studies should be interpreted with caution. Many commonly used inhibitors, including dynasore, filipin, or methyl-β-cyclodextrin, often lack specificity and may exert off-target effects by perturbing multiple cellular processes or membrane properties, making it difficult to attribute OMV uptake exclusively to a single endocytic pathway.

OMVs derived from pathogenic and commensal bacteria in host environments

During host infection, exposure to diverse host-associated stresses is known to increase OMV production compared to non-stress conditions125,126. Gut-derived OMVs have been shown, using single-vesicle analysis combined with OmpA–mCherry–expressing bacteria to trace vesicle origin, to translocate across the intestinal epithelial barrier and enter the bloodstream125. Accordingly, OMVs have been directly detected in the bloodstream at concentrations of ~107–1010 particles per mL126. Although direct quantification of circulating OMVs in humans remains limited, these observations from host-associated models suggest that OMVs may be present at physiologically relevant levels in the human bloodstream, particularly under infection-associated conditions. These OMVs, carrying a diverse range of biomolecules, act as important mediators of bacterial communication and host–microbe interactions across multiple host environments, enabling the rapid dispersal of molecular cargos and dynamically reshaping host-associated microbial communities and immune responses127,128. The host-adaptive properties of bacteria determine whether they are pathogenic or commensal, and these characteristics are mirrored in their OMVs, which, upon entering the host, can modulate host homeostasis in either a beneficial or detrimental manner129,130. Importantly, the distinction between commensal and pathogenic bacteria is not strictly dichotomous but is determined by host physiological and immunological conditions. Bacteria such as Bacteroides fragilis and Pseudomonas aeruginosa may behave as commensals or exhibit limited pathogenicity under homeostatic conditions, but can shift toward pathogenicity when host defenses are compromised, including epithelial barrier disruption, immune imbalance, or pre-existing inflammation.

Detrimental contribution of pathogen-derived OMVs to hyperimmune responses and disease progression

OMVs from bacterial pathogens share pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) with their parental bacteria, triggering immune responses such as cytokine production and inflammasome activation, and contributing to the development of inflammation-associated diseases (Fig. 3)131. As such, OMVs are considered as PAMP delivery vehicles. Upon delivery, the PAMPs contained on or within OMVs, including LPS, PG fragments, and various virulence factors, can be recognized by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and NOD-like receptors on host cells132,133. This interaction represents a key component of the host’s first line of immune defense, leading to cytokine production, ROS generation response, and mitochondria-dependent cell death134. OMVs derived from periodontal (P. gingivalis and Fusobacterium nucleatum) and lung pathogens (A. baumannii and P. aeruginosa) containing the various PAMPs, including lipoprotein Pal, LPS, and PG, are recognized by PRRs, such as TLR2, TLR4, or NOD1 on epithelial cell [e.g., lung epithelial cell (A549), cervical epithelial cells (Hela)] or innate immune cells, leading to the activation of macrophages and dendritic cells33,135–137. In this process, TNF-α enhances the function of antigen-presenting cells (APCs), while an inflammatory milieu rich in IL-6, IL-1β, and TGF-β drives the differentiation of naïve CD4⁺ T cells into Th17 cells. Differentiated Th17 cells secrete a variety of cytokines (e.g., IL-17A, IL-17F, IL-21, IL-22, and GM-CSF), which recruit and activate neutrophils and macrophages at the site of infection and stimulate epithelial and fibroblast cells to produce antimicrobial peptides and chemokines, amplifying the local inflammatory response127,138. Because A. baumannii OmpA interacts with fibronectin on lung epithelial A549 cells, the release of OMVs carrying OmpA may further enhance bacterial adhesion and invasion by increasing fibronectin engagement (Fig. 3A)139–141. OmpA abundant in OMVs activates TLR2 signaling, stimulating macrophages and dendritic cells to produce pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β). Additionally, OmpA containing MHC-I and MHC-II epitopes can elicit antigen-specific CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ T cell responses, therefore bridging innate and adaptive immunity142–144. OMVs from enterotoxigenic E. coli show markedly reduced internalization into intestinal epithelial cells when OmpA or OmpF is absent, indicating that these porins are required for efficient uptake145. This internalization is essential for the induction of proinflammatory responses, as OMV-delivered LPS is subsequently recognized intracellularly, activating caspase- and RIPK2-dependent signaling pathways that lead to NF-κB–mediated inflammatory responses. Surprisingly, OMVs derived from F. nucleatum, implicated in periodontitis and colorectal cancer, contain LPS that activate TLR4 on intestinal epithelial cells, triggering NF-kB signaling and production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-8 and TNF-α) (Fig. 3B)146. Macrophages (M0) exposed to F. nucleatum OMVs are polarized toward an M1 phenotype, which enhances inflammatory responses, and undergo apoptosis in gingival fibroblasts, exacerbating tissue damage147. In particular, the adhesin FadA embedded in F. nucleatum OMVs enters synovial macrophages via endocytosis and simultaneously activates Rab5a localized to endosome. Activated Rab5a induces phosphorylation of YB-1, a key transcriptional regulator of inflammatory mediators Phosphorylated YB-1 subsequently translocates into the nucleus, where it enhances transcription of genes encoding pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6 and TNF-a), leading to an inflammatory response148. In addition to immune activation, OMVs can deliver enzymatically active factors that directly damage host cells. For instance, V. cholerae OMVs transport active proteases such as VesC and hemagglutinin/protease (HAP) into epithelial cells via lipid raft–dependent internalization149. In intestinal epithelial cells (T84), OMV treatment increases IL-8 production, which is significantly reduced in VesC-deficient mutants, indicating that VesC is a key mediator of epithelial inflammatory responses. Moreover, HAP-mediated cellular damage leads to epithelial cell rounding, detachment, and barrier disruption, further amplifying inflammatory signaling.

Unlike PAMPs that require recognition by host pattern recognition receptors, many bacterial toxins delivered via OMVs have dual roles: possessing intrinsic toxin functions and inducing innate immune responses16. When CagA, a major virulence factor of H. pylori, functions as an intrinsic toxin, it becomes active after being phosphorylated by host kinases inside host cells (Fig. 3C)150. Then, it binds to the SHP-2 protein, activating mitogen-activated protein kinase signaling pathways, which disrupt host cellular morphology and epithelial cell-cell junctions, leading to the “hummingbird phenotype”151. CagA, delivered either via the type IV secretion system (T4SS) or through H. pylori OMVs, binds to and inactivates the polarity-regulating kinase PAR1b/MARK2, a critical regulator of microtubule organization and the specialized contact between antigen-presenting cells and T cells13. Under CagA-mediated PAR1b inhibition, this impaired contact inhibits T cell receptor clustering, consequently reducing B cell antibody production. Likewise, because OMVs can carry OMV-associated cholera toxin (CT), their delivery may contribute to epithelial shrinkage, breakdown of the epithelial barrier, and massive fluid loss51,152. Activation of the NOD-like receptor family pyrin-domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome ASC in macrophages is triggered by CT, promoting pyroptotic cell death and exacerbating mucosal injury7. Bacterial OmpA displayed on OMVs induces lung alveolar epithelial A549 cell death by mitochondrial fragmentation through the accumulation of dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) and the evaluation in ROS in mitochondria8. Once activated, Drp1 oligomerizes into a ring-shaped structure and is recruited to the mitochondrial outer membrane, where it mediates mitochondrial fission. The Drp1 ring exerts mechanical constriction on the mitochondria through GTP hydrolysis-driven conformational changes, ultimately severing the organelle and completing the division process153. A similar mechanism has been observed in Neisseria gonorrhoeae, where the porin PorB, like OmpA, localizes to host mitochondria and disrupts mitochondrial function154.

In addition to directly inducing host immune activation and tissue damage mediated by OMVs, OMVs derived from pathogenic bacteria can indirectly drive disease progression by reshaping the host-associated microbiota155. They can suppress the growth of commensal bacteria and drive the expansion of pathobionts, which destabilize gut microbiomes and contribute to the progression of host disease (e.g., neurodevelopment, behavior, immunity, and susceptibility to neurological disorders)156,157. For example, inoculation of OMVs isolated from the gut microbiota of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) patients to APP/PS1 mice resulted in gut dysbiosis, with increased proportion of Acinetobacter and Staphylococcus and reduced levels of commensal bacteria such as the genus Lactobacillus and the family Muribaculaceae (Fig. 3D)158. This dysbiosis results in decreased production of commensal-derived short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) (e.g., butyrate and propionate) that essentially support intestinal barrier integrity and suppress neuroinflammation. The SCFA deficiency leads to increased intestinal permeability, which in turn allows OMVs to access the systemic circulation and penetrate the blood–brain barrier. Ultimately, the phagocytic activity of brain-resident macrophages (microglia) toward amyloid-β is impaired by OMVs-induced overactivation, which contributes to the development of AD. Similarly, oral administration of OMVs isolated from the feces of elderly individuals, in which Paenalcaligenes hominis constitutes a large proportion in the fecal microbiota, was sufficient to induce gut dysbiosis, accompanied by colitis and cognitive impairment159.

Beneficial contributions of commensal bacteria–derived OMVs to immune homeostasis

Accumulating evidence indicates that OMVs released from commensal bacteria act as potent immunoregulatory mediators that help sustain host–microbiota homeostasis (Fig. 4)127. Although the precise mechanisms underlying their beneficial effects remain incompletely understood, recent studies have begun to identify key molecular determinants responsible for these activities. OMVs carrying OmpA from the commensal bacterium Parabacteroides goldsteinii activate Paneth cells, inducing the secretion of IL-22—a cytokine critical for epithelial barrier integrity and mucosal defense. IL-22 stimulates the production of stem-cell-niche factors such as Wnt3, EGF, and TGF-α, as well as antimicrobial peptides including RegIIIβ, RegIIIγ, and β-defensins160. Interestingly, although OmpA is typically considered a virulence factor in pathogens, OmpA from P. goldsteinii exhibits a contrasting, protective immunomodulatory role. In contrast, Salmonella enterica OmpA fails to elicit IL-22 under identical conditions161, underscoring that the functional outcomes of identical structural motifs can diverge depending on bacterial context—reflecting the co-evolution of commensal OMVs with host immunity. Commensal OMVs also influence immune balance through dendritic-cell-mediated pathways. Polysaccharide A embedded in Bacteroides fragilis OMVs engages TLR2 on dendritic cells, promoting regulatory T cell activation and IL-10 secretion—key processes that restrain excessive intestinal inflammation162,163. In dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)–induced colitis models, administration of B. fragilis OMVs alleviates inflammation and restores epithelial integrity (Fig. 5)164. Further mechanistic insights come from RNA profiling of B. fragilis OMVs, which revealed enrichment of mmu-miR-like small RNA miR-5119. Delivery of OMVs containing this microRNA suppressed PD-L1 expression, inhibited GSDMD-mediated neutrophil extracellular-trap formation, and enhanced intestinal stem-cell proliferation—illustrating how bacterial vesicles can exploit host RNA-interference pathways to fine-tune mucosal immunity. Beyond Gram-negative species, Gram-positive commensals such as Bifidobacterium longum also produce EVs with anti-inflammatory activity165. B. longum EVs induce IL-10 production in splenocytes and dendritic-cell/T-cell co-cultures, potentially through abundant solute-binding proteins and ABC transporters that recognize carbohydrates and trigger tolerogenic signaling. These findings collectively highlight that vesicle-mediated immunoregulation is a conserved strategy among gut commensals.

Beyond direct immunomodulation, commensal OMVs shape the gut ecosystem by influencing microbial composition and metabolic output. OMVs derived from Akkermansia muciniphila, B. fragilis, and probiotic E. coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) enhance the growth of beneficial taxa and the generation of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), while suppressing harmful metabolites such as phenol and pentanoic acid, maintaining an anti-inflammatory intestinal milieu127. For instance, A. muciniphila OMVs selectively stimulate the proliferation of Bacteroides acidifaciens, B. thetaiotaomicron, and Lactobacillus spp., restoring microbial balance and enhancing mucosal IgA production in DSS-induced colitis models166. Similarly, B. fragilis OMVs enrich Lactococcus and Romboutsia while reducing pro-inflammatory metabolites that exacerbate epithelial injury167. Oral administration of E. coli Nissle 1917 OMVs to obese or diabetic mice normalized the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio (from ~3.5 to ~1.5) and increased the relative abundance of beneficial families such as Lachnospiraceae and genera such as Oscillospira, leading to elevated levels of SCFAs, particularly butyrate and propionate, which strengthen epithelial tight junctions and exert systemic anti-inflammatory effects168. Notably, inverted inner-membrane vesicles were experimentally generated by high-pressure microfluidization–mediated cell lysis, which induces membrane inversion and vesicle reassembly169. These vesicles, containing HemB-dependent heme proteins-oriented outward, suppressed the growth of oxygen-dependent pathogens (E. coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Citrobacter rodentium) while favoring anaerobic commensals such as Lactobacillus, resulting in the restoration of microbial balance in vivo169. Together, these findings reveal that OMVs from commensal and probiotic bacteria not only act as molecular messengers shaping immune tone but also serve as ecological engineers that restructure microbial communities and metabolic networks. By reinforcing epithelial barriers, promoting regulatory immune pathways, and fostering beneficial microbial consortia, commensal OMVs emerge as key determinants of intestinal resilience and promising candidates for microbiota-based therapeutics.

Recent advances in OMV-based therapeutic applications

Compared with conventional subunit vaccines composed of non-toxic pathogen-derived components such as purified protein antigens or polysaccharides, nanosized OMVs are increasingly recognized as versatile biomedical tools, functioning as both vaccine platforms and delivery vehicles owing to their intrinsic adjuvanticity and native ability to stimulate the immune system170,171. Through genetic engineering, OMVs can be readily modified to display heterologous antigens, efficiently inducing both systemic and mucosal immune responses. This inherent versatility highlights their potential to bridge the gap between classical vaccines and next-generation immunotherapeutics. Nevertheless, several challenges—such as optimizing surface antigen exposure, enhancing yield, ensuring non-toxicity, and achieving durable protection—must still be addressed to realize their full clinical potential. A compelling clinical example is provided by OMV-based vaccines against Neisseria meningitidis. Licensed formulations such as MenBvac, MeNZB, and Bexsero® have demonstrated efficacy against serogroup B meningococcal disease and have been deployed globally for over a decade172,173. In these vaccines, PorA—the most abundant outer membrane protein of N. meningitidis—serves as the principal immunodominant antigen. Detoxified OMVs produced from ΔlpxL mutants were used to minimize endotoxicity while maintaining antigenicity. However, the presence of more than 20 PorA serotypes has limited cross-protection, underscoring a key challenge in OMV-based vaccine design: balancing immunogenicity with antigenic breadth. To address this, the multicomponent Bexsero® vaccine was developed by combining OMVs with additional recombinant antigens—NHBA (Neisserial heparin-binding antigen), fHbp (factor H–binding protein), and NadA (Neisseria adhesin A)—expressed in E. coli, which expands coverage across diverse serogroup B strains174,175. The success of Bexsero® not only validated OMVs as safe and effective vaccine platforms but also demonstrated the value of rational antigen design in overcoming strain variability. Building on this foundation, OMV-based vaccines are being actively developed for other high-priority pathogens such as Shigella flexneri, Shigella sonnei, Campylobacter jejuni, and A. baumannii176. The S. flexneri 2a O-polysaccharide antigen was biosynthesized in Salmonella Typhimurium and enzymatically ligated by WaaL to the Salmonella LPS core–lipid A, resulting in the display the heterologous antigen on the OM177. These OMVs purified from this engineered strain therefore carried the Shigella O-antigen as part of their native LPS structure and conferred protective immunity in mice, resulting in increased survival upon Shigella challenge. Extending this concept, the Shigella Generalized Modules for Membrane Antigens (GMMA) platform, derived from S. sonnei and multiple S. flexneri serotypes, integrates O-antigens with OM proteins and PAMPs such as LPS and lipoproteins, which broadens serotype coverage and enhancing innate immune activation. This formulation is currently under evaluation in Phase 1/2 clinical trials178,179. Similarly, OMV-based vaccines have been developed against C. jejuni by displaying protective glycan antigens on the vesicle surface180. Their heptasaccharide N-linked glycan antigen, normally associated with protein glycosylation in C. jejuni, was expressed in engineered E. coli and displayed on the outer membrane. OMVs presenting the Campylobacter-derived glycans were subsequently purified and conferred protective immunity in chickens, resulting in a substantial reduction in intestinal colonization following C. jejuni challenge. In parallel, OMVs derived directly from clinical A. baumannii isolates (e.g., ATCC 19606 and OXA-23–positive global clone GC2) have elicited robust IgA, IgM, and IgG responses in mouse models, reducing bacterial burden and inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, IL-1β) following infection181,182. These results suggest that even unmodified, native OMVs can act as potent immunogens, leveraging their PAMP-rich surfaces to generate broad, protective immunity. Together, these studies illustrate how the natural immunostimulatory architecture of OMVs can be harnessed for rational vaccine design against antibiotic-resistant pathogens.

Beyond infectious disease prevention, OMVs are being re-engineered as innovative platforms for cancer immunotherapy. Engineered OMVs encapsulating oncolytic adenoviruses have been shown to enhance intratumoral delivery, trigger autophagy, and stimulate antitumor immunity, ultimately promoting tumor regression in preclinical models183. Similarly, OMVs functionalized to display tumor antigens or immunostimulatory molecules, either by genetic fusion or chemical conjugation, serve as self-adjuvanting nanosystems capable of activating antigen-presenting cells and eliciting strong systemic and local immune responses. Multi-epitope OMVs incorporating TRP2 (tyrosinase-related protein 2), Adpgk (colon cancer antigen), and OTI/OTII epitopes from ovalbumin, together with PAMPs as built-in adjuvants, exemplify this dual function as both antigen carriers and immune activators183–185. These findings demonstrate that OMVs can transcend their bacterial origin, functioning as programmable immunological scaffolds with applications extending from infection control to oncology. Interestingly, during bacterial infection, OMVs are naturally released into the bloodstream, where they contribute to systemic inflammation, suggesting their potential as diagnostic biomarkers. In a pneumonia mouse model, use of a polymyxin B–fluorescein (PmBF) probe targeting LPS revealed a rapid increase in fluorescence within 6 h post-infection, indicative of OMV dissemination in circulation125. This observation not only highlights the role of OMVs in mediating systemic inflammatory responses but also suggests that circulating OMVs could serve as early indicators of bacterial infection, offering new opportunities for infection monitoring and rapid diagnosis. Together, these advances highlight the remarkable adaptability of OMVs as multifaceted biological platforms. From vaccines and cancer immunotherapy to diagnostic biomarkers, OMVs exemplify the convergence of microbial physiology and biomedical innovation—transforming what were once viewed as bacterial by-products into versatile tools for next-generation therapeutics and precision diagnostics.

Conclusion

OMVs have emerged as pivotal mediators in the complex interplay between bacteria and host cells, functioning not merely as passive byproducts of bacterial physiology but as active participants in both host-pathogen and host-commensal dynamics. Although OMV production imposes energetic and structural costs, accumulating evidence suggests that these are balanced by significant functional advantages, including alleviation of membrane stress, immune evasion, and membrane remodeling. OMV formation can occur rapidly in response to environmental perturbations, indicating a dynamic process shaped by both physical forces and regulated cellular responses. In some cases, rapidly generated OMVs can contain relatively low levels of protein cargo and arise primarily from acute physicochemical disturbances of the membrane, such as disrupted lipid asymmetry, weakened membrane tethering, or localized lipid domains enriched in saturated or sphingolipid-like lipids that favor curvature stress and membrane budding186. More commonly, OMV production is closely linked to envelope homeostasis through the selective export of deleterious components, including misfolded proteins, aberrant lipids, LPS, and PG fragments that progressively accumulate over successive growth cycles as a consequence of normal envelope turnover. By packaging and removing these potentially toxic or burdensome materials, OMVs often represent the cumulative outcome of stress-relief processes associated with repeated rounds of bacterial growth and division and contribute to the maintenance of membrane integrity and overall envelope quality. Thus, rather than being purely passive, OMV biogenesis represents an emergent, functionally integrated response to envelope stress. Regardless of its initiating trigger, the net outcome is the relief of membrane stress and the re-establishment of envelope homeostasis. This trade-off likely reflects a context-dependent strategy in which OMV production enhances bacterial fitness, particularly under host-associated conditions. Rather than providing definitive answers, the current body of knowledge invites further inquiry into several critical questions: What governs the differential immune outcomes elicited by OMVs derived from pathogenic versus commensal bacteria? How do host cells discern the origin and content of incoming OMVs, and to what extent can this recognition be manipulated for therapeutic purposes? Addressing these questions will require multidisciplinary efforts spanning microbiology, immunology, cell biology, and biomedical engineering. Moreover, the potential to exploit OMVs as delivery vehicles for antigens, immunomodulatory molecules, or therapeutic agents opens new avenues for translational research. However, realizing these applications will necessitate a comprehensive understanding of the molecular determinants that govern OMV uptake, intracellular trafficking, and immunological outcomes in diverse host contexts. In summary, OMVs represent a promising yet complex interface in host–microbe interactions. Research across all disciplines related to OMV-mediated survival strategies of bacterial cells within the host will pave the way for the development of therapeutic strategies against pathogenic bacteria.
