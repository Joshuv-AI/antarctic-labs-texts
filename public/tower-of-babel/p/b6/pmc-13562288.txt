Effects of xanomeline–trospium on the gut–lung microbiota axis and susceptibility to LPS-induced acute lung injury in male and female mice
Authors: Xin Ding, Akifumi Eguchi, Rumi Murayama, Yi Cai, Yong Yue, Jian-Jun Yang, Kenji Hashimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Xanomeline–trospium is a first-in-class muscarinic receptor–based antipsychotic that improves schizophrenia symptoms without dopamine D2 receptor antagonism. Although gastrointestinal (GI) adverse effects have been reported, its broader effects on host microbiota and susceptibility to respiratory injury remain unclear. We investigated the effects of xanomeline and xanomeline–trospium on gut and lung microbiota, host metabolism, and outcomes in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced acute lung injury (ALI) in male and female mice. Adult mice received vehicle, xanomeline, or xanomeline–trospium for 15 days. Body weight was monitored longitudinally, fecal output was measured, gut and lung microbiota were profiled using 16S rRNA sequencing, and untargeted serum metabolomics was performed using UPLC–QTOF/MS. ALI was induced by intratracheal LPS administration, and survival was assessed for seven days. Xanomeline induced weight loss in female mice and constipation in both sexes, and these effects were attenuated by trospium co-administration. Xanomeline–trospium was associated with sex- and region-associated alterations in gut microbiota, with greater remodeling in the cecum and colon, and also altered lung microbiota composition in both sexes. Integrated multi-omics analyses revealed sex-associated links among specific bacterial taxa, circulating metabolites, and host phenotypes. In the ALI model, xanomeline–trospium significantly increased LPS-induced mortality in female mice but not in male mice; however, formal interaction analysis did not support a significant sex-dependent treatment effect. These findings suggest that xanomeline–trospium alters gut–lung microbiota and host metabolic networks and may influence respiratory vulnerability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00406-026-02318-7.

Introduction

Xanomeline–trospium (KarXT; marketed as Cobenfy®) represents a first-in-class antipsychotic strategy that differs fundamentally from traditional dopamine D2 receptor antagonism. This combination therapy pairs xanomeline, a muscarinic receptor agonist with functional selectivity for central M1 and M4 receptors, with trospium, a peripherally restricted quaternary antimuscarinic agent designed to reduce peripheral cholinergic adverse effects and improve tolerability [1, 2]. In Phase 3 EMERGENT trials, xanomeline–trospium demonstrated robust antipsychotic efficacy in patients with schizophrenia while maintaining a favorable metabolic and extrapyramidal safety profile [3, 4]. The subsequent approval of Cobenfy by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration on September 26, 2024, provides clinical validation of a non–D2-based therapeutic paradigm and represents an important expansion of the pharmacological landscape for schizophrenia treatment [5, 6].

Despite these advantages, the safety profile of xanomeline–trospium has been characterized mainly by muscarinic-mediated gastrointestinal (GI) adverse events. Across short-term EMERGENT studies and pooled safety analyses, the most frequently reported treatment-emergent adverse events included nausea, dyspepsia, constipation, vomiting, dry mouth, and, less commonly, diarrhea or abdominal pain [7–9]. These events were generally mild to moderate, occurred early after treatment initiation, often within the first two weeks, and were usually transient [7–9]. In contrast, overt respiratory adverse events have not emerged as a dominant safety signal in clinical trials. Nevertheless, mechanistic vigilance remains warranted because cholinergic GI disturbances, such as nausea, vomiting, gastroesophageal reflux, and altered intestinal motility, may plausibly increase aspiration risk in vulnerable individuals [10–12].

From a pharmacological perspective, xanomeline shares certain mechanistic features with clozapine, particularly muscarinic receptor engagement, although it lacks dopamine D2 and serotonin 5-HT2A receptor antagonism [13, 14]. This comparison is clinically relevant because clozapine, despite its unmatched efficacy in treatment-resistant schizophrenia, has been consistently associated with severe GI hypomotility, constipation progressing to ileus, and a markedly increased incidence of pneumonia, including aspiration pneumonia [15–19]. Genetic susceptibility may further modulate this risk; for example, a retrospective cohort study reported that polymorphisms in the leptin receptor (LEPR) gene are associated with increased pneumonia risk among Taiwanese patients with schizophrenia treated with clozapine [20]. Clozapine-associated pneumonia is thought to arise from convergent mechanisms, including hypersalivation, dysphagia, sedation-related hypoventilation, impaired airway reflexes, and cumulative anticholinergic burden [21]. Although xanomeline–trospium lacks D2 antagonism and incorporates a peripherally restricted antimuscarinic agent, its muscarinic receptor–centered mechanism may still influence respiratory vulnerability indirectly through GI dysfunction, secretion imbalance, altered motility, and aspiration-related pathways [15, 19, 22].

Beyond classical pharmacodynamic mechanisms, accumulating evidence highlights a critical role of host microbiota, particularly gut microbiota, in schizophrenia pathophysiology, antipsychotic efficacy, and treatment-related adverse effects [22–27]. Although trospium was incorporated into xanomeline–trospium to limit peripheral muscarinic actions, GI side effects, including constipation, diarrhea, nausea, dyspepsia, and abdominal pain, remain common in clinical trials [3, 4, 8, 9]. Given the high density and functional importance of muscarinic receptors (M1–M5) throughout the GI tract [28–30], muscarinic modulation may influence not only drug tolerability but also intestinal microbial ecology. Such changes may have broader implications because gut microbial communities and their metabolites can regulate mucosal immunity, systemic inflammation, and gut–lung communication. Thus, xanomeline–trospium may affect respiratory vulnerability not only through aspiration-related mechanisms but also through microbiota-mediated gut–lung–brain interactions [22]. Despite this biological rationale, the effects of xanomeline and xanomeline–trospium on gut and pulmonary microbiota have not been systematically examined in either clinical or preclinical settings.

In the present study, we tested the hypothesis that xanomeline and xanomeline–trospium differentially modulate gut and lung microbiota in a sex-associated manner in adult mice. Animals were treated with vehicle, xanomeline alone, or xanomeline–trospium for 15 days, after which microbial communities in the small intestine, cecum, colon, and lungs were characterized using 16S rRNA sequencing. We also examined whether trospium co-administration attenuates xanomeline-induced constipation and whether these GI effects are associated with alterations in intestinal microbiota. Finally, to explore potential respiratory vulnerability, we assessed sex-specific mortality following lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced acute lung injury (ALI) in vehicle- and xanomeline–trospium-treated mice.

Materials and methods

Animals

Adult male C57BL/6 mice (8 weeks of age; 20–25 g) and age-matched female C57BL/6 mice (18–22 g) were obtained from Japan SLC Corporation (Hamamatsu, Shizuoka, Japan). Animals were housed under standardized conditions in a temperature-controlled facility with a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 07:00 and off at 19:00). Standard laboratory chow (CE-2; CLEA Japan, Inc., Tokyo, Japan) and water were available ad libitum throughout the study.

All experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Chiba University (License Nos. 7–80 and 7–394). Animal handling and experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines, with all reasonable measures taken to minimize discomfort and distress.

Compounds and treatments

A 0.5% carboxymethylcellulose solution (CMC; Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd., Osaka, Japan) was prepared in sterile distilled water and used as the vehicle. The same 0.5% CMC solution without xanomeline or trospium was administered to the control group. Vehicle, xanomeline (2 mg/kg), or xanomeline (2 mg/kg) combined with trospium (1 mg/kg) was administered once daily by intragastric gavage (i.g.) from day 1 to day 15. The doses of xanomeline and trospium were selected based on a previous report [31]. The 15-day dosing regimen was chosen to model short-term repeated exposure during the early treatment phase, when GI adverse events are commonly observed clinically, and to allow detection of early microbiota and host metabolic changes. All treatments were delivered at a dosing volume of 10 mL/kg body weight. Xanomeline (TargetMol; CAS No. 152854–19-8; Lot No. 239317) and trospium (TargetMol; CAS No. 10405–02-4; Lot No. 145996) were freshly prepared each day in 0.5% CMC.

To induce acute lung injury (ALI), lipopolysaccharide (LPS; L-4130, serotype O111; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved in sterile saline and administered at a dose of 5.0 mg/kg, as previously described [32–34]. All other reagents were of analytical grade and obtained from commercial suppliers.

For the ALI survival experiment, we focused on the vehicle + LPS and xanomeline–trospium + LPS groups because xanomeline–trospium represents the clinically approved therapeutic combination. Male and female mice were analyzed separately and assigned to two groups: control (vehicle + LPS) and xanomeline–trospium (xanomeline–trospium + LPS). After 14 consecutive days of treatment with vehicle or xanomeline–trospium, LPS was administered intratracheally on day 15 using a Natsume Aerosol Sprayer (KN-34–700; Natsume Seisakusho Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), in accordance with established protocols [32, 33]. Survival was monitored daily for seven consecutive days after LPS administration.

Measurement of body weight and fecal output

Body weight was measured daily from day 1 to day 15 immediately before drug administration (Fig. 1A). To evaluate gastrointestinal (GI) motility, mice were individually transferred to clean cages on day 16, and fecal pellets excreted during a 60-min observation period were counted. Collection of plasma, BALF, and intestinal contents

Mice were anesthetized with a combined intraperitoneal injection of medetomidine (0.75 mg/kg; Nippon Zenyaku Kogyo Co., Ltd., Fukushima, Japan), midazolam (4 mg/kg; Sandoz K.K., Tokyo, Japan), and butorphanol (5 mg/kg; Meiji Seika Pharma Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), diluted in sterile saline immediately before use and administered at a volume of 10 mL/kg. Following the induction of deep anesthesia, blood was collected via cardiac puncture using a 2-mL syringe and transferred into tubes containing ethylenediamine-N,N,N′,N′-tetraacetic acid potassium salt dihydrate (5 μL, 0.5 M) as an anticoagulant. Samples were centrifuged at 4 °C, and the resulting plasma was isolated and stored at − 80 °C until analysis.

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) was obtained by gently lavaging the lungs with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), followed by immediate centrifugation at 8,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C, as previously described [31, 32, 34]. The supernatant was collected and stored at − 80 °C until further use.

For intestinal sampling, the entire cecum was excised, and luminal contents were collected along with contents from 5–7 cm segments of the small intestine and colon adjacent to the cecum [31, 34–36]. All biological samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C until downstream analyses.

16S rRNA sequencing analysis

Bacterial DNA from BALF samples was extracted using the QIAamp UCP Pathogen Mini Kit (Qiagen; Cat. No. 50214; Lot No. 181025621) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions [31, 34]. Samples yielding DNA concentrations ≥ 0.3 ng/µL were forwarded to BGI Japan (Kobe, Japan) for downstream 16S rRNA gene sequencing.

For intestinal samples, total genomic DNA was isolated from approximately 250 mg of luminal contents using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen; Cat. No. 51604; Lot No. 181023747), following the manufacturer’s protocol [31, 34, 36, 37]. Extracted DNA was quantified, normalized to 10 ng/µL, and submitted to BGI Japan for sequencing.

The V3–V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene were amplified using universal primers (341F/806R) with a dual-indexing strategy, followed by paired-end sequencing. Raw paired-end reads were merged using fastq-join with default parameters. Quality filtering and chimera detection were performed using USEARCH v6.1, retaining reads with a minimum quality score of Q ≥ 20 across at least 99% of bases. High-quality, non-chimeric sequences were taxonomically classified from phylum to genus levels using the Ribosomal Database Project (RDP) classifier with a confidence threshold of 80%. All sequencing data have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) under accession number PRJNA1353641 and will be publicly accessible upon publication.

Microbial α-diversity was assessed using Shannon and Chao1 indices, while β-diversity was evaluated based on weighted and unweighted UniFrac distances. Ordination analyses were performed using principal component analysis (PCA) and principal coordinates analysis (PCoA), and group differences were statistically examined by analysis of similarities (ANOSIM) implemented in the vegan package (R v4.2.2). Comparisons of diversity indices and taxon relative abundances between groups were conducted using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test with false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Differentially enriched taxa were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) with a logarithmic LDA score > 2 and FDR < 0.05 [36, 38]. Visualization of microbial composition and differential taxa was performed using Metware Cloud and OmicStudio platforms (https://cloud.metware.cn/; https://www.omicstudio.cn/) [36, 38].

Functional prediction analysis

Predicted microbial functional profiles were inferred from 16S rRNA sequencing data using PICRUSt2 (KEGG database v115.0) followed by differential abundance analysis and visualization with STAMP (2.1.3). Relative pathway abundances were normalized according to the median-of-ratios method (DESeq2 normalization), and group differences were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test (Mann–Whitney U test), with FDR < 0.1 considered statistically significant. Because these analyses infer functional potential from taxonomic composition, they were interpreted as predicted functional shifts rather than direct measurements of microbial gene content, transcriptional activity, protein expression, or metabolite production.

Untargeted metabolomics analysis of plasma

Untargeted metabolomics profiling of plasma samples was performed as previously described, with minor modifications [36, 37, 39]. Briefly, 100 μL of plasma was mixed with 100 μL of methanol containing internal standards (100 μM N,N-diethyl-2-phenylacetamide and D-camphor-10-sulfonic acid) and centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 5 min to remove precipitated proteins. Subsequently, 400 μL of methanol supplemented with internal standards (25 μM N,N-diethyl-2-phenylacetamide and D-camphor-10-sulfonic acid) and 400 μL of ultrapure water were added to the supernatant. Samples were then homogenized using a BioMasher® II with a PowerMasher® II system to facilitate metabolite extraction.

Plasma metabolomic profiling was conducted using ultra-performance liquid chromatography coupled with quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (UPLC–QTOF/MS), following established protocols [36, 37, 39]. Raw data processing, peak detection, and metabolite annotation were carried out using MS-DIAL software (version 5.3), and downstream statistical analyses were performed in the R environment (version 4.4.1), as described by Takeda et al. [40]. Metabolites were retained for analysis if they were detected in at least 50% of samples, showed a coefficient of variation (CV) ≤ 30% in pooled quality-control samples, and were annotated at confidence level 2 according to the criteria proposed by Schymanski et al. [41].

Statistical analyses

Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) for normally distributed variables or as median with interquartile range (IQR) for non-normally distributed variables, as appropriate. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.4.1 and R statistical software version 4.2.2. Normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variance was assessed using Bartlett’s test.

Longitudinal changes in body weight from day 1 to day 15 were analyzed separately in male and female mice using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA, with treatment group and time as factors, followed by Tukey’s test, as appropriate. Main effects of treatment group and time, as well as treatment group × time interaction effects, were assessed. Survival after LPS administration was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazards regression including sex, treatment, and the sex × treatment interaction term. Male and female mice were analyzed together to formally assess whether the effect of xanomeline–trospium on LPS-induced mortality differed by sex.

Fecal pellet counts were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple-comparisons test. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Volcano plots were generated in R version 4.2.2 using the ggplot2 package. Bacterial taxa were classified as increased when p < 0.05 for intestinal samples or p < 0.1 for lung samples, with log₂ fold change (FC) > 0 in either the control versus xanomeline or xanomeline versus xanomeline–trospium comparisons. Taxa were classified as decreased when the corresponding p-value thresholds were met with log₂FC < 0. For metabolomic analyses, metabolites were classified as increased when p < 0.1 and log₂FC > 0, and as decreased when p < 0.1 and log₂FC < 0, using the same group comparisons. These volcano plot analyses were considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Spearman’s rank correlation analyses were conducted to assess associations among body weight, fecal output, bacterial relative abundances, and plasma metabolites. Correlation matrices and graphical visualizations were generated using GraphPad Prism version 9.4.1.

Results

Effects of xanomeline and xanomeline-trospium on body weight and constipation in male and female mice

Mice received daily administration of vehicle, xanomeline, or xanomeline–trospium for 15 consecutive days, during which body weight was monitored longitudinally (Fig. 1A). In male mice, body weight was significantly lower in the xanomeline–trospium group compared with the xanomeline group (Fig. 1B). In contrast, female mice treated with xanomeline exhibited a significant reduction in body weight relative to the control group. Notably, co-administration of trospium significantly attenuated xanomeline-induced weight loss in females, resulting in higher body weight in the xanomeline–trospium group compared with the xanomeline group and restoring values toward control levels (Fig. 1C).

Consistent with its known GI effects, xanomeline markedly reduced fecal output in both male and female mice, indicative of constipation. Fecal pellet counts were significantly lower in the xanomeline group than in control animals of both sexes. Importantly, trospium co-treatment significantly reversed this effect, as evidenced by increased fecal output in the xanomeline–trospium group compared with the xanomeline group in both males and females (Fig. 1D, E).

Overall, xanomeline administration produced comparable sex-related effects on body weight and constipation, both of which were substantially mitigated by concomitant trospium treatment.

Effects of xanomeline–trospium on gut microbiota composition

We first evaluated α-diversity (within-sample diversity) of the gut microbiota in the small intestine, cecum, and colon of male and female mice across the three treatment groups using the Shannon and Chao1 indices (Fig. 2). In male mice, a significant alteration in α-diversity was detected only in the cecum, where the xanomeline + trospium group exhibited a reduced Chao1 index (reflecting richness) compared with the control group (Fig. 2B). In female mice, Chao1 diversity in the small intestine was significantly increased in the xanomeline + trospium group relative to both the control and xanomeline groups (Fig. 2D). We next assessed β-diversity (between-sample community structure) using weighted and unweighted UniFrac distance metrics. In male mice, both weighted and unweighted UniFrac analyses revealed significant group differences in the cecum (Fig. 2H), whereas only unweighted UniFrac distances differed significantly among groups in the colon (Fig. 2I). In female mice, significant separation among groups was observed in the small intestine with both UniFrac metrics (Fig. 2J), while in the colon, group differences were detected only by unweighted UniFrac analysis, specifically between the xanomeline and xanomeline–trospium groups (Fig. 2L). These findings indicate that xanomeline and xanomeline–trospium treatment altered gut microbial community structure in a sex- and region-dependent manner.

Ordination analyses further supported these observations. In male mice, PCA (based on OTU/ASV abundance) and PCoA (Bray–Curtis distance) showed no clear separation among groups in the small intestine or cecum, despite significant ANOSIM results (Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). In contrast, distinct clustering between treatment groups was evident in the colon (Supplementary Fig. 1C). In female mice, PCA demonstrated significant group separation in the small intestine and colon, whereas PCoA based on Bray–Curtis distances did not show clear clustering in these regions (Supplementary Fig. 1D, F). Conversely, PCoA revealed significant separation among groups in the cecum, while PCA did not (Supplementary Fig. 1E), underscoring methodological sensitivity to both distance metrics and intestinal region.

To identify taxa driving these compositional differences, we performed linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe; LDA > 2, FDR < 0.05) across the three treatment groups. In the resulting cladograms, node color indicates the group in which each taxon was enriched, with concentric rings representing taxonomic levels from phylum to species. For each anatomical site (small intestine, cecum, and colon), the top discriminatory taxa per group are summarized in bar plots (Supplementary Fig. 1G–L).

In male mice, the small intestine of control animals was characterized by enrichment of taxa including f_Bacteroidaceae, g_Bacteroides, g_Lederbergia, f_Streptococcaceae, and g_Megasphaera. The Xan group showed enrichment of g_Terrimonas, g_Marmoricola, c_Cyanophyceae, p_Cyanobacteriota, and g_Stenotrophobacter, whereas the xanomeline–trospium group was characterized by enrichment of f_Lactobacillaceae, o_Lactobacillales, g_Lactobacillus, f_Prevotellaceae, and g_Prevotellamassilia (Supplementary Fig. 1G).

In the male cecum, control-associated taxa included f_Prevotellaceae, g_Prevotellamassilia, p_Pseudomonadota, c_Betaproteobacteria, and f_Neisseriaceae. The xanomeline group was enriched in g_Dysosmobacter, g_Collinsella, f_Coriobacteriaceae, o_Coriobacteriales, and g_Niastella, while the xanomeline–trospium group showed enrichment of o_Anaeroplasmatales, p_Mycoplasmatota, f_Anaeroplasmataceae, c_Mollicutes, and g_Anaeroplasma (Supplementary Fig. 1H). In the male colon, control mice were characterized by f_Muribaculaceae, g_Duncaniella, f_Streptosporangiaceae, g_Nonomuraea, and o_Streptosporangiales, whereas the xanomeline group showed enrichment of o_Eubacteriales, c_Clostridia, p_Bacillota, f_Oscillospiraceae, and g_Flintibacter. The xanomeline–trospium group was enriched in g_Prevotellamassilia, f_Prevotellaceae, o_Anaeroplasmatales, g_Anaeroplasma, and f_Anaeroplasmataceae (Supplementary Fig. 1I).

In female mice, the small intestine of control animals was enriched in g_Phenylobacterium, f_Tepidiformaceae, c_Tepidiformia, o_Tepidiformales, and g_Tepidiforma. The xanomeline group showed enrichment of g_Roseburia, g_Laedolimicola, g_Caproiciproducens, o_Blastocatellales, and p_Acidobacteriota, whereas the xanomeline–trospium group was characterized by g_Desulfovibrio, o_Desulfovibrionales, f_Desulfovibrionaceae, c_Deltaproteobacteria, and g_Sporofaciens (Supplementary Fig. 1 J). In the female cecum, control-associated taxa included p_Bacteroidota, c_Bacteroidia, o_Bacteroidales, f_Prevotellaceae, and g_Prevotellamassilia, while the Xan group was enriched in g_Anaerotignum, g_Acetatifactor, g_Fusobacterium, c_Bacilli, and g_Coprococcus. The xanomeline–trospium group showed enrichment of f_Micrococcaceae, f_Enterobacteriaceae, g_Allomeiothermus, f_Thermaceae, and o_Thermales (Supplementary Fig. 1 K). In the female colon, control animals were characterized by p_Bacillota, o_Eubacteriales, c_Clostridia, p_Pseudomonadota, g_Lawsonibacter, and c_Alphaproteobacteria, whereas the xanomeline group was enriched in g_Brucella, g_Stenotrophomonas, and g_Bacillus. The xanomeline–trospium group was characterized by enrichment of o_Bacteroidales, c_Bacteroidia, p_Bacteroidota, f_Prevotellaceae, g_Prevotellamassilia, and k_Bacteria (Supplementary Fig. 1L).

Collectively, these results indicate that xanomeline–trospium exposure induces sex- and region-specific alterations in gut microbiota composition, with more pronounced effects in female mice and in the cecum and colon of male mice, while exerting relatively limited effects in the male small intestine.

Effects of xanomeline–trospium on mortality rate in LPS-induced ALI

We next examined the impact of xanomeline–trospium on survival in an LPS-induced ALI model in male and female mice. As illustrated in Fig. 4U, mice received daily oral gavage of vehicle or xanomeline–trospium for 14 consecutive days, after which ALI was induced by intratracheal administration of LPS (5 mg/kg). Survival was monitored daily for seven days following LPS administration.

A sex-related difference in LPS-induced mortality was observed. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed that xanomeline–trospium significantly reduced survival in female mice, whereas this effect was not statistically significant in male mice (Fig. 4V, W). However, Cox proportional hazards regression including sex, treatment, and the sex × treatment interaction term did not reveal a significant sex × treatment interaction. Therefore, although xanomeline–trospium was associated with increased LPS-induced mortality in female mice, the combined analysis did not provide sufficient statistical evidence to support a sex-dependent treatment effect.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that xanomeline–trospium exerts sex- and region-associated effects on gut and lung microbiota, host metabolism, and susceptibility to inflammatory lung injury. Although trospium mitigated xanomeline-induced GI hypomotility and weight loss in both sexes, xanomeline–trospium still induced substantial remodeling of intestinal and pulmonary microbial communities. These effects were more prominent and heterogeneous in female mice and in distal gut regions, particularly the cecum and colon. In the LPS-induced ALI model, xanomeline–trospium was associated with worsened survival in female mice, whereas this effect was not statistically significant in male mice. However, formal interaction analysis did not support a significant sex × treatment interaction for LPS-induced mortality. Thus, the mortality findings should be interpreted as a treatment-associated vulnerability observed in female mice rather than definitive evidence of a sex-dependent treatment effect.

Integration of microbiome and metabolomics data revealed coordinated associations linking specific bacterial taxa with circulating metabolites and host phenotypes, including body weight and fecal output. In male mice, several gut-associated species showed positive correlations with phenolic and sulfur-containing metabolites, whereas in female mice, distinct lung and intestinal taxa were associated with metabolites related to choline and carnitine metabolism. These findings suggest that muscarinic modulation by xanomeline–trospium may influence host physiology through microbiota–metabolite networks, in addition to direct pharmacological effects. The anatomical specificity of these associations further supports the presence of a compartmentalized gut–lung axis that is sensitive to cholinergic perturbation.

Although xanomeline–trospium lacks dopamine D2 receptor antagonism and incorporates a peripherally restricted antimuscarinic agent, its muscarinic receptor–centered mechanism may be sufficient to trigger downstream GI and microbial alterations. These changes may secondarily shape pulmonary microbial ecology and immune responsiveness, thereby modifying susceptibility to lung injury. The observed parallels, as well as important distinctions, between xanomeline–trospium and clozapine-associated microbiota and respiratory phenotypes [22, 34] reinforce the concept that muscarinic signaling may represent a shared mechanistic node linking GI dysfunction, aspiration risk, and pneumonia vulnerability, even in the absence of D2 receptor blockade.

Xanomeline–trospium also produced region-specific effects along the GI tract, likely reflecting fundamental anatomical and functional differences between the small intestine, cecum, and colon. The small intestine, characterized by rapid transit, lower microbial density, and strong exposure to bile acids and antimicrobial peptides, exhibited relatively modest and variable microbiota changes, particularly in male mice, suggesting relative resilience to short-term muscarinic modulation [42–44]. In contrast, the cecum and colon, which harbor dense and metabolically active microbial communities central to fermentation, short-chain fatty acid production, and immune regulation, showed more pronounced and consistent alterations following xanomeline–trospium treatment [42–44]. These distal regions displayed shifts in α- and β-diversity, enrichment of specific anaerobic taxa, and changes in predicted microbial functional pathways related to carbohydrate, lipid, and energy metabolism. Such sensitivity is biologically plausible given the high expression of muscarinic receptors throughout the lower gut and the dependence of cecal and colonic microbiota on host motility, secretion, and mucosal signaling [11, 45]. Importantly, the distal gut is a major source of microbial metabolites that influence systemic immunity and lung responses, providing a possible mechanistic link between region-specific microbiota remodeling and downstream lung vulnerability observed in this study [46–50].

The sex-associated differences in microbiota composition, metabolite profiles, and ALI outcomes observed here may reflect a convergence of biological and experimental factors [51–53]. Sex hormones, particularly estrogens and androgens, are known to shape immune responses, gut permeability, bile acid metabolism, and microbial community structure, and may modulate host responses to muscarinic stimulation and inflammatory lung injury. In addition, baseline gut and lung microbiota may exhibit sex-associated configurations that influence metabolite production, mucosal immunity, and susceptibility to dysbiosis following pharmacological perturbation. These pre-existing differences may amplify or buffer downstream responses to xanomeline–trospium in males and females. Furthermore, the relatively short treatment duration may preferentially capture acute or transitional microbiota and immune responses, which could differ between sexes before longer-term adaptive mechanisms emerge. Together, interactions among sex hormones, sexually dimorphic microbiota, and time-dependent host adaptation may contribute to the observed sex-associated patterns in microbiota remodeling and ALI-related outcomes. However, because the formal sex × treatment interaction for LPS-induced mortality was not significant, these findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of a sex-dependent effect of xanomeline–trospium on ALI mortality.

Although the selected doses and 15-day treatment period enabled assessment of early microbiota and host responses to repeated xanomeline–trospium exposure, direct dose equivalence between mice and humans cannot be assumed. Therefore, the present findings should be regarded as preclinical safety signals that require confirmation using clinically relevant pharmacokinetic exposure models and longitudinal clinical microbiome studies.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study is correlative in nature, and causal relationships between specific bacterial species, metabolites, and ALI susceptibility were not directly tested. Second, BALF is a low-biomass sample type, and lung microbiome analyses may be susceptible to contamination from extraction reagents or laboratory procedures. Third, the sample size was relatively small for multi-omics comparisons, and some exploratory analyses used relaxed significance thresholds, increasing the possibility of false-positive findings. Fourth, functional pathway analyses were inferred from 16S rRNA taxonomic profiles and therefore reflect predicted microbial functional potential rather than directly measured metagenomic capacity, transcriptional activity, protein expression, or metabolite production. Fifth, the ALI experiment relied primarily on mortality as an outcome and did not include detailed lung injury phenotyping, such as histopathology, BALF inflammatory cell counts, cytokine profiling, or lung permeability measurements. Sixth, the absence of a xanomeline-alone + LPS group limits our ability to distinguish the relative contributions of xanomeline and trospium to ALI susceptibility. Finally, although sex-associated differences were observed in microbiota and metabolite profiles, the mechanisms underlying these effects were not directly examined, and formal interaction analysis did not support a significant sex-dependent treatment effect on LPS-induced mortality. Future studies using larger cohorts, contamination-controlled lung microbiome workflows, shotgun metagenomics, targeted metabolomics, microbiota transfer experiments, and detailed immunological and histopathological analyses are needed to validate and extend these findings.

Despite these limitations, the present findings have important translational implications. Although xanomeline–trospium represents a promising non–D2-based therapeutic option for schizophrenia with favorable metabolic and extrapyramidal safety profiles, our data suggest that GI and respiratory functions may warrant careful monitoring, particularly in patients with pre-existing GI dysmotility, swallowing impairment, or pulmonary vulnerability. Microbiota-mediated effects may remain subclinical during short-term treatment yet could influence infection risk or inflammatory responses under physiological stress conditions, such as aspiration, systemic inflammation, or acute respiratory insults.

These observations also raise the possibility that adjunctive microbiota-targeted interventions could mitigate treatment-related risks. Strategies such as dietary modulation, prebiotics, probiotics, synbiotics, or postbiotic supplementation may help stabilize gut microbial communities, preserve metabolite homeostasis, and reduce downstream immune or pulmonary vulnerability [46, 48, 54–56]. Although such approaches remain speculative in the context of muscarinic-based antipsychotics, emerging evidence from other neuropsychiatric and inflammatory disorders supports the feasibility of microbiome-informed supportive therapies. Prospective clinical studies will be required to determine whether microbiota modulation can improve tolerability, reduce infection risk, or enhance the overall safety profile of xanomeline–trospium, particularly in biologically vulnerable subgroups.

In conclusion, this study identifies sex- and region-associated alterations in the microbiota–metabolite–lung axis following xanomeline–trospium treatment, linking muscarinic antipsychotic therapy to altered gut and lung microbial ecology and modified outcomes in inflammatory lung injury. Although xanomeline–trospium was associated with increased LPS-induced mortality in female mice, formal interaction analysis did not provide sufficient statistical evidence for a sex-dependent treatment effect on ALI mortality. Future studies should prioritize mechanistic validation using microbiota transfer approaches, targeted bacterial or metabolite interventions, detailed ALI phenotyping, and longitudinal clinical microbiome profiling in xanomeline–trospium-treated patients. Such efforts will be essential for refining safety monitoring strategies and optimizing the long-term clinical use of muscarinic-based antipsychotics.
