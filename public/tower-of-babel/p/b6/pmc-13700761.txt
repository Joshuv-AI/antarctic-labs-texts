Cannabichromene attenuates fracture pain but impairs bone repair in a murine tibial fracture model
Authors: Deepak Kumar Khajuria, Hwabok Wee, Zachary A. Koroneos, Mohamed A. Tantawy, Gregory S. Lewis, Wesley M. Raup-Konsavage, Kent E. Vrana, Fadia Kamal, Reyad A. Elbarbary
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fractures are common musculoskeletal injuries associated with severe pain, and effective analgesia is essential for fracture management. Current therapies such as NSAIDs and opioids have significant limitations, driving interest in alternative analgesics, an avenue where cannabinoids hold great promise. While cannabinoids are increasingly promoted for various indications, robust scientific evidence supporting their use remains limited. We previously demonstrated that cannabidiol (CBD) and cannabigerol (CBG), two non-psychoactive cannabinoids, alleviate fracture pain and promote bone repair. Cannabichromene (CBC), another non-psychoactive cannabinoid, has shown analgesic effects in different disease models, but its impact on fracture pain and healing is unknown. Using a murine tibial fracture model, we investigated CBC’s effects on pain and bone healing. CBC significantly reduced fracture pain, improving mechanical and cold allodynia, thermal hyperalgesia, and gait function. However, bone healing was impaired, with delayed soft-callus resorption, increased bone cell apoptosis, elevated osteoclast activity, and reduced bone formation and mineralization. In vitro, CBC promoted osteoclastogenesis, supporting its resorptive effect. These findings contrast with the reported benefits of CBD and CBG on fracture repair, highlighting that not all cannabinoids are suitable for fracture management. Individual compounds must be carefully evaluated to balance analgesia with bone healing. Caution is warranted when using medical cannabis or cannabinoid oils with variable compositions, as their effects on healing are unpredictable. The divergent effects of CBD and CBG versus CBC may also guide future structure-activity relationship studies for designing synthetic cannabinoids to promote fracture healing.

Introduction

Cortical bone fractures typically heal in three stages: inflammation, repair, and remodeling [1,2]. In the inflammatory phase, immune cells infiltrate the fracture site, releasing cytokines and growth factors that attract additional immune cells, stem cells, and bone progenitors to initiate repair [1,2]. During early repair, a soft fibrocartilaginous callus forms, mainly from proliferating chondrocytes, which later hypertrophy and mineralize, facilitating vascular growth [1,2]. This mineralized callus is gradually replaced by bone, forming a bony callus. Finally, the newly formed woven bone undergoes remodeling to restore the cortical bone’s laminar structure [1,2].

Bone fractures disrupt mechanosensitive nerve fibers, producing the sharp pain typically experienced by patients [3]. At the fracture site, stromal and immune cells release neurotransmitters, growth factors, and cytokines, which drive abnormal nerve sprouting and heighten pain, thereby restricting patient mobility. Since controlled movement-induced loading is essential for healing load-bearing bones like the femur or tibia, inadequate pain control can hinder bone loading and delay recovery [3]. Currently, fracture pain is managed with NSAIDs and opioids [4,5]. However, studies show that NSAIDs can impair bone repair by suppressing the inflammatory phase of healing [6–12]. Optimal dosing and treatment duration for NSAIDs that allows proper pain management without impeding bone healing remain unclear, and many orthopaedic surgeons consider NSAIDs contraindicated in fracture patients [4,6,13–16]. Opioids also present challenges: they impair functional recovery, delay return to work, and have been also recently linked to increased fracture risk and slower bone healing [17–19]. These limitations contribute to poor participation in rehabilitation programs. Consequently, effective pain management in fracture patients remains an unmet clinical need. Phytocannabinoids are gaining attention as potential alternatives, with strong analgesic promise. Nonetheless, limited clinical evidence has restricted their use. Recently, we demonstrated that CBD and CBG provide analgesia comparable to NSAIDs while simultaneously promoting fracture healing [20]. These findings suggest that CBD and CBG could serve as alternatives or adjuvants to conventional analgesics [20]. However, it is still unclear whether these dual analgesic and pro-healing effects are unique to CBD and CBG or represent a broader property of cannabinoids.

Cannabichromene (CBC) is a notable non-psychotropic phytocannabinoid and a significant minor cannabinoid found in Cannabis sativa [21]. It is gaining recognition as a promising candidate for pain relief due to its unique therapeutic properties. Preclinical research indicates that CBC can effectively reduce pain-related behaviors while also exhibiting anti-inflammatory effects [22,23]. Furthermore, CBC has attracted attention for its potential synergistic or “entourage” effects when combined with other cannabinoids [22,24]. In the field of musculoskeletal health, CBC has been shown to decrease joint inflammation and mechanical allodynia in a collagen-induced arthritis mouse model, with its effects further enhanced when co-administered with cannabidiol (CBD), suggesting a potential synergistic benefit in addressing inflammatory musculoskeletal pain [24].

Despite the growing interest in CBC and the broader therapeutic applications of cannabinoids, its analgesic and therapeutic effects have not yet been studied in the context of bone regeneration. In this study, we evaluate the analgesic effects of CBC in a preclinical murine model of tibial fracture and assess its impact on the efficiency of bone fracture healing.

Materials & methods

Animals

Male C57BL/6 J mice (stock #000664) were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) at 14 weeks of age and acclimated for two weeks prior to experimentation. All procedures involving animals were conducted in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) at Pennsylvania State University College of Medicine. Mice were group-housed (five per cage) with unrestricted access to food and water, maintained under a 12-h light/dark cycle, at ambient temperatures ranging from 21.1 °C to 22.8 °C and relative humidity between 30% and 70%.

Fracture surgery, CBC treatment, and tissue harvest

At 16 weeks old, mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg). Sustained-release buprenorphine hydrochloride (0.1 mg/kg) was administered subcutaneously 30 min before surgery. A standardized open mid-diaphyseal fracture was created in the right tibia, stabilized with an intramedullary pin as previously described [2,20,25,26]. Radiographs were obtained postoperatively and at harvest to confirm fracture alignment. CBC (20 mg/kg/day; Cayman Chemicals) was delivered intraperitoneally, starting 24 h after fracture and continuing daily until tissue collection. The dose of CBC was determined based on previous publications [27]. CBC was prepared in a vehicle solution of DMSO, Tween 80, and saline (1:1:18). Control animals received the vehicle alone. Vehicle-treated controls were shared with our previous study [20], as all treatments (vehicle, CBD, CBG, CBC) were conducted concurrently.

At designated endpoints, animals were euthanized, and the fractured limb was dissected from mid-femur to tibiotalar joint, carefully preserving the callus. For day 28 (d28) samples, the intramedullary pin was removed, and bones were wrapped in PBS-soaked gauze and stored at −20 °C for micro-computed tomography (μCT), which was followed by biomechanical testing. Day 21 samples were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, followed by pin removal, μCT scanning, decalcification in 14% EDTA, and paraffin embedding for immunofluorescence (IF) and histological staining. Timepoints were selected based on established healing timelines in this fracture model [2,26,28].

Pain tests

Pain assessments were conducted using the von Frey, acetone drop, and hot plate (52 °C) tests, following our established protocols [20,29]. The von Frey test measured the threshold required to elicit paw withdrawal, while the acetone drop and hot plate tests recorded the duration required to elicit nociceptive behaviors such as paw withdrawal or licking. Each test was performed one hour after administration of the drug or vehicle. To minimize iatrogenic effects, the von Frey test was conducted on day 4 post-fracture, the acetone drop test on day 5, and the hot plate test on day 6. All assessments were carried out by investigators blinded to treatment groups.

Gait analysis

The gait analysis was performed as we described previously [20,29]. Briefly, mice were placed at one end of a three-foot-long dark tunnel lined with blank paper, with their hind paws inked using an ink pad. As the animals traversed the tunnel, their footprints were recorded on the paper. Footprint traces were analyzed by investigators blinded to treatment conditions to quantify four gait parameters: stride length, stance width, sway, and paw print intensity. Paw print intensity was measured using ImageJ software. All assessments were carried out by investigators blinded to treatment groups.

Micro-computed tomography analysis

Micro-computed tomography (μCT) was performed on tibiae collected at days 21 and 28 post-fracture as we previously described [2,20,30]. Samples were scanned using a VivaCT 40 scanner (Scanco Medical AG) at 55 kVp, 145 μA, 300 ms integration time, and 10.5 μm isotropic voxel size, capturing ~600 slices (6 mm) centered on the fracture site. Using Scanco Evaluation software (v6.6), 2D tomograms of the complete fracture callus were manually contoured after Gaussian filter application (sigma = 0.8, support = 1). For each slice, two contours were created: the first tracked the exterior fracture callus perimeter, and the second encircled uninjured cortices. Subtraction isolated the complete callus volume between contours. Mineralized bone tissue was identified using a threshold of 250. Quantitative parameters assessed included bone volume fraction (BV/TV), mean bone mineral density (BMD), trabecular thickness (Tb.Th), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), and connectivity density (Conn.Dn). Three-dimensional reconstructions of the fractured tibiae were generated by importing DICOM image stacks into Avizo 3D software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All assessments were carried out by investigators blinded to treatment groups.

Biomechanical testing

Following μCT scanning, biomechanical testing was performed on day 28 post-fracture samples by investigators blinded to treatment groups, as we previously described [2,28]. Briefly, each specimen was secured at both ends using polymethylmethacrylate dental cement within 6 mm × 6 mm square aluminum tubes. A custom fixture aligned the pots with the bone’s longitudinal axis, maintaining a 7 mm gauge length between them. The potted samples were rehydrated in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and mounted on an MTS/Interlaken servohydraulic axial-torsion load frame equipped with a 177 Nmm load cell. Torsional loading was applied at a rate of 1°/s until failure, and the maximum torque sustained by the specimen was recorded.

Histological and IF staining and TUNEL assay

Following μCT scanning, harvested tissues were decalcified, processed, and embedded in paraffin before sectioning at 5 μm thickness for histological and IF staining, as we previously described [2,20,25,26]. Sagittal sections encompassing the callus center were deparaffinized and stained with Hematoxylin/Safranin-O/Fast Green [2,28]. Adjacent sections were subjected to tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) staining to quantify osteoclast numbers per bone surface, following established protocols [20,28]. Osteoclasts were identified as multinucleated, TRAP-positive cells. Histomorphometric analyses were performed using the OsteoMeasure system (OsteoMetrics Inc.). Apoptotic cells were detected in situ by TUNEL assay using the Invitrogen™ Click-iT™ Plus TUNEL Assay Kit (Catalog No. C10619, Fisher Scientific).

IF staining for Collagen I (Col I) using a biotinylated primary antibody (ab24821, Abcam) and for the proliferation marker Ki67 (ab16667, Abcam) was performed as we previously detailed [20,26,31]. Mounting and nuclear counterstaining were carried out using ProLong™ Gold antifade reagent containing DAPI (Invitrogen). For Col I, the stained area was quantified and normalized to the total callus area, while for Ki67, the number of Ki67-positive cells was normalized to the total number of cells in the callus (identified by DAPI). Images were acquired with a Zeiss Axio Observer 7 upright wide-field microscope (Carl Zeiss Microscopy GmbH) and analyzed using Zen Blue advanced image analysis software.

In vitro osteoclastogenesis

In vitro osteoclastogenesis was conducted following established protocols [32,33]. Briefly, bone marrow (BM) cells were isolated from the hind limbs of 8-week-old mice and cultured in α-MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 24 h, non-adherent cells were collected and seeded in medium containing 30 ng/ml macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF) for 3 days. Subsequently, cells were cultured for an additional 3 days in medium supplemented with 30 ng/ml M-CSF, 100 ng/ml receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL), and either 1 μg/ml CBC or vehicle control. Following treatment, cells were harvested for RNA isolation using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and expression of osteoclastogenesis markers was quantified by TaqMan gene expression assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific) as described previously [34,35].

Study design and statistical analysis

Sample sizes were calculated using the pwr2 package in R, with statistical power set at 0.8 and significance level at 0.05. The estimated sample sizes were 5–6 mice/group for μCT and IF staining analyses, 8 mice/group for biomechanical testing (expected effect size of 30%), and 3 samples/experimental condition for in vitro osteoclastogenesis assays (expected effect size of 50%). Experimental groups were assigned accordingly. Statistical comparisons between two groups were performed using Welch’s t-test, while analyses involving more than two groups utilized either one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Significance levels are indicated as follows: *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; ****P < 0.0001.

Results

CBC effectively ameliorates bone fracture pain

To evaluate the analgesic effects of CBC on fracture-induced pain, we performed a series of pain behavioral assays. We first assessed mechanical nociception using the von Frey test, and vehicle-treated fractured mice exhibited significant mechanical allodynia, demonstrated by a reduced paw withdrawal threshold compared to sham-operated controls (Fig. 1B). CBC treatment significantly increased the mechanical threshold in fractured mice, restoring it to levels comparable to the sham group (Fig. 1B). We also assessed sensitivity to cold and hot stimuli using the acetone test and the hot plate test, respectively. Vehicle-treated fractured mice showed marked cold hypersensitivity and thermal hyperalgesia, with approximately a threefold decrease in latency to nociceptive responses relative to sham controls (Fig. 1C, D). CBC administration significantly prolonged response latencies in fractured mice, normalizing them to sham levels (Fig. 1C, D). Collectively, these reflexive pain assessments indicate that CBC effectively elevates response thresholds to mechanical, cold, and heat stimuli following fracture.

We further investigated pain-associated motor dysfunction through gait analysis. Tibial fracture significantly impaired gait parameters, including reductions in stride length, stance length, sway distance, and weight-bearing on the injured limb compared to sham mice (Fig. 2A–D). CBC treatment significantly improved stride length, stance length, and weight-bearing (Fig. 2A, B, D), but did not improve sway distance (Fig. 2C). These findings demonstrate that CBC not only alleviates sensory hypersensitivity but also largely restores motor function impaired by fracture. In summary, CBC exhibits pronounced analgesic effects that effectively mitigate fracture-induced pain and associated motor deficits.

CBC substantially inhibits fracture healing

To evaluate the impact of CBC treatment on fracture healing, we analyzed fracture calli collected from vehicle- and CBC-treated mice at 28 days post-fracture. At this stage, the repair phase is typically complete, with the fracture gap bridged by newly formed woven bone undergoing remodeling [2,20,25,26,28]. μCT analysis revealed that CBC treatment adversely affected the healing process, as indicated by significant reductions in BV/TV, BMD, Tb.N, and Tb.Th, accompanied by an increase in Tb.Sp compared to vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 3A–G).

Complementing these structural findings, biomechanical torsional testing demonstrated that CBC-treated mice exhibited significantly reduced bone strength relative to vehicle-treated animals (Fig. 3H). Together, these results indicate that CBC substantially impedes fracture healing by compromising both bone microarchitecture and mechanical integrity.

CBC delays bony bridging of the fracture gap and inhibits fibrocartilaginous callus resorption

To further elucidate the mechanisms underlying CBC-induced delays in fracture healing observed at 28 days, we analyzed fracture calli from vehicle- and CBC-treated mice at an earlier time point (21 days post-fracture). Consistent with previous reports [2,20,25,26,28], histological evaluation revealed that the fracture callus in vehicle-treated mice was predominantly composed of newly formed woven bone, with extensive resorption of the fibrocartilaginous callus (Fig. 4A, B; Supplementary Fig. 1). In contrast, CBC-treated mice exhibited delayed resorption of the fibrocartilaginous callus and markedly reduced bony bridging within the callus compared to controls (Fig. 4A, B; Supplementary Fig. 1).

IF staining for collagen I (Col I) provided additional evidence of impaired osteogenesis in CBC-treated animals, showing diminished new bone deposition relative to vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 4C, D). These histological findings were corroborated by μCT analysis, which showed that CBC treatment significantly reduced BV/TV, BMD, and Tb.Th compared to vehicle controls (Fig. 4E–J).

In summary, these results demonstrate that CBC substantially delays bony bridging across the fracture gap by inhibiting both fibrocartilaginous callus resorption and its replacement by newly formed woven bone.

CBC induces osteoclastogenesis

To gain further insight into the mechanisms by which CBC inhibits bone formation, we performed a TUNEL assay on day-21 fracture calli. This analysis revealed that CBC treatment induced apoptosis of cells within the woven bone region (Fig. 5A, B). Additionally, IF staining for the proliferation marker Ki67 demonstrated that CBC significantly suppressed cellular proliferation in the same region (Fig. 5C, D).

Importantly, CBC substantially increased the number of osteoclasts within the callus (Fig. 5E, F), suggesting that bone resorption is abnormally elevated and may contribute to the diminished bone formation observed in CBC-treated mice (Figs. 3A–G, 4E–J). To corroborate these in vivo findings, we conducted in vitro osteoclastogenic differentiation assays, which showed that CBC markedly upregulated Nfatc1 (nuclear factor of activated T cells, cytoplasmic 1; master transcription factor for osteoclastogenesis), Dcstamp (dendrocyte-expressed seven transmembrane protein; fusion and multinucleation marker), Car2 (carbonic anhydrase 2; resorptive activity and matrix degradation marker), and the canonical osteoclast markers Acp5 (acid phosphatase 5, tartrate resistant, which encodes TRAP) and Ctsk (cathepsin K). Together, these in-vitro results confirm that CBC significantly induces osteoclastogenesis.

Collectively, the promotion of osteoclast formation, induction of apoptosis, and inhibition of proliferation within the woven bone area represent key mechanisms underlying the negative effects of CBC on fracture healing.

Discussion

With NSAIDs contraindicated in fracture pain management due to their negative impact on healing [6–12], and the abuse potential associated with opioids [17–19], there is a critical need for safer analgesics for fracture patients. Promising options from pre-clinical murine models include anti-nerve growth factor antibodies, which alleviate fracture pain without impairing healing [36,37]. Cannabinoids have demonstrated analgesic effects in various pain models, which motivated us to investigate their pain-relieving properties in murine fracture models. Our previous studies showed that both CBD and CBG exert powerful analgesic effects in a tibial fracture model [20]. In the present study, we demonstrate that CBC similarly provides effective analgesia. However, a striking difference emerged regarding their effects on fracture healing: while CBD and CBG significantly promoted bone formation and accelerated healing, CBC substantially inhibited the healing process. These divergent outcomes highlight the complex and cannabinoid-specific effects on musculoskeletal repair.

Mechanical loading of the fractured limb is a critical determinant of healing trajectory. Notably, CBD, CBG, and CBC all mitigated fracture-associated motor deficits and restored gait function, thereby increasing mechanical loading of the fracture site [20] (Fig. 2). However, only CBD and CBG promoted bone healing, while CBC impaired it—a divergence that cannot be explained by locomotor recovery alone. Thus, improved loading insufficiently explains CBD/CBG’s pro-healing versus CBC’s anti-healing effects. These outcomes suggest cannabinoid-specific pharmacological actions driving distinct biological responses.

The distinct biological activities of CBC, CBD, and CBG can be attributed in part to their differing chemical structures and receptor binding profiles. All three cannabinoids originate from the precursor cannabigerolic acid (CBGA). Decarboxylation of CBGA, through heat and drying, produces CBG; while CBC and CBD are produced through enzymatic conversion of CBGA and subsequent decarboxylation of acidic precursor molecules. This process yields structurally unique molecules with a common cannabinoid backbone but varied ring configurations and functional groups [38]. These structural differences might influence their affinity and activity at cannabinoid receptors and other molecular targets. CBD exhibits low direct affinity for the canonical cannabinoid receptors CB1 and CB2, but acts as an allosteric modulator of the two receptors [39–42]. CBD also signals through CB1 and CB2 via indirect mechanisms; for instance, CBD inhibits fatty acid amide hydrolase (FAAH), leading to increased anandamide levels, an endogenous ligand with high CB1/CB2 affinity [43–45]. Additionally, CBD activates transient receptor potential (TRP) channels such as TRPV1 [46] and modulates several orphan G-protein-coupled receptors [47]. CBG displays clearer direct activity at canonical cannabinoid receptors than CBD. It acts as a partial agonist at CB2 and modulates CB1 signaling, although the mechanisms remain unclear [48]. It also interacts with non-cannabinoid receptors, including α2-adrenoceptors and 5-HT1A receptors [49]. In contrast, CBC’s receptor profile is less well characterized. It reportedly acts as a CB2 agonist, while its activity at CB1 is controversial [22]. CBC’s biological effects appear to be mediated through activation of TRP channels, particularly TRPA1 and TRPV1 [22]. These differences in receptor affinity and signaling pathways likely underlie the opposing effects on fracture healing, with CBD and CBG promoting osteogenesis, increasing the abundance of callus progenitors, and supporting callus mineralization [20], and CBC delaying soft-callus resorption, enhancing osteoclastogenesis, and impairing bone repair (Figs. 3–5). These receptor-binding profiles also underscore the promiscuous pharmacology of cannabinoids and their affinity for a wide range of non-cannabinoid receptors in a tissue- and context-dependent manner. This complexity challenges receptor-specific hypotheses for cannabinoid biological functions. However, understanding these structural and functional differences is an important step that will guide future structure-activity relationship studies aimed at designing synthetic cannabinoids that promote fracture healing or target specific callus cell populations within the fracture callus.

Interestingly, CBD, CBG, and CBC have demonstrated anti-inflammatory and analgesic properties in cancer models, often exhibiting entourage effects when combined [22,24,50,51]. However, our findings reveal that in the context of fracture healing, CBC exerts opposite effects to those of CBD and CBG. This suggests that the entourage effect is highly context-dependent, influenced by tissue-specific receptor expression and downstream signaling pathways. The contrasting effects of CBC compared to CBD and CBG on bone repair underscores the need for careful evaluation of cannabinoid combinations in therapeutic applications.

This study focused on outcome measurements at days 21 and 28 to capture CBC’s cumulative effects, revealing late-healing-phase phenotypes the included inhibited bone formation, reduced biomechanical strength, impaired soft callus resorption, induced osteoclastogenesis, promoted apoptosis, and reduced cellular proliferation (Figs. 3–5). These late-phase impairments may stem from CBC’s influence on earlier healing stages. While CBC’s early anti-inflammatory effects merit consideration as potential contributors to the overall inhibition, the multifactorial nature of fracture repair and cannabinoids’ broad impact across diverse cell types suggest additional mechanisms. These include inhibited recruitment, proliferation, or osteogenic differentiation of stem/progenitor cells, as well as CBC’s direct effects on late-stage cell types (e.g., osteoblasts and chondrocytes). Indeed, our in-vitro data demonstrate that CBC directly promotes osteoclastogenesis (Fig. 5E–G). These compelling mechanistic questions necessitate extensive future studies.

Crude cannabis preparations feature compositions that vary by cannabis source, extraction method, cultivar, and processing conditions. On the other hand, purified, single-cannabinoid formulations (e.g., CBC, CBD, or CBG) enable precise dosing and reproducible outcomes and are gaining growing research interest. Our work focuses on purified CBC to establish foundational insights into its impact on pain and healing. Unlike CBD—which has an FDA-approved formulation (Epidiolex) with well-characterized human pharmacokinetics—CBC and most minor cannabinoids lack defined pharmacokinetic profiles, reference plasma values, or clinical biodistribution data. No measurements exist for human serum CBC levels following administration of purified CBC at measured doses, rendering physiologically relevant concentrations undefined. CBC levels reported in medical marijuana or over-the-counter oils are inapplicable, as these preparations contain only trace amounts of this minor cannabinoid amid complex mixtures. In the absence of human reference ranges, we selected a CBC dose commonly used in preclinical pain studies, mirroring translational scenarios. A key cautionary finding of this study is that this dose—which demonstrates potent analgesic effects—impairs fracture healing, highlighting the risks of using CBC in managing bone fracture pain.

Given the increasing popularity and accessibility of over-the-counter cannabinoid oils and medical marijuana products—often containing such complex and variable mixtures—our findings raise a cautionary red flag. The unregulated use of such products, without thorough characterization of their cannabinoid composition or understanding of their effects on bone healing, poses potential risks for patients with fractures or, possibly, other musculoskeletal injuries. It is imperative that future research rigorously investigating the pharmacological profiles and healing impacts of individual cannabinoids and their mixtures is required before clinical recommendations can be safely made.

In conclusion, while CBC shows promise as an analgesic and anti-inflammatory agent, our data highlight a critical limitation: its substantial negative effect on fracture healing. This warrants caution in considering CBC for clinical use in musculoskeletal injuries and calls for further mechanistic studies to delineate its pathways of action. Future drug development efforts should focus on designing synthetic cannabinoids or formulations that retain analgesic efficacy without compromising tissue repair.
