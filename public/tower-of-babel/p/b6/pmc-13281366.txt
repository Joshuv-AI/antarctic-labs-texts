Evaluation of the accuracy, quality, and readability of large language models on local anesthesia and general anesthesia-sedation in dentistry
Authors: Semanur Özüdoğru, Taibe Tokgöz Kaplan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aims to compare the responses provided by commonly used artificial intelligence-based chatbots such as ChatGPT-3.5, ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 2.0 Flash, and DeepSeek-R1 about dental local anesthesia, sedation, and general anesthesia in terms of accuracy, reliability, and readability.MethodsSixty questions were created from the American Dental Association (ADA) and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) guidelines. Thirty were patient questions, thirty professional questions. Each group contained ten open-ended, ten multiple-choice, and ten true/false questions. The questions were submitted to ChatGPT-3.5, ChatGPT-4o, DeepSeek-R1, and Gemini 2.0 Flash. Four blinded pediatric dentists evaluated the answers with a modified global quality scale. Clinical safety and risk analysis were evaluated using a 3-point Likert scale. Readability was measured by Flesch Reading Ease Score (FRE) and Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL). The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) tested inter-rater reliability. Significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsDeepSeek-R1 demonstrated the highest overall accuracy and inter-rater agreement, providing the most accurate and reliability responses across all question types (p < 0.001). It was followed by Gemini 2.0 Flash, ChatGPT-4o, and ChatGPT-3.5. In terms of readability, Gemini 2.0 Flash consistently produced the most accessible responses, while DeepSeek-R1 was significantly less readable (p = 0.012). GPT-3.5 showed variability by question type, with MCQs being easier to read than open-ended ones (p = 0.027). No significant readability differences were observed across question types for ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 2.0 Flash, or DeepSeek-R1 (p > 0.05).ConclusionChatbot performance depends on both question type and evaluation criteria. DeepSeek-R1 excelled in accuracy and quality. Gemini 2.0 Flash produced the clearest, patient-friendly responses. AI chatbots can support communication in dental anesthesia. Choosing the right model may improve education, assist clinical training, and guide professional decisions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03934-5.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a multidisciplinary field comprising systems capable of performing tasks that require human intelligence. Today, AI is revolutionising numerous healthcare fields, including image processing, natural language processing, and clinical decision support systems. In dentistry, AI plays a crucial role in processes such as diagnosis, treatment planning, patient monitoring, and behaviour management [1, 2]. Large language models (LLMs) developed in recent years have the potential to support patient-doctor communication, provide rapid access to information, and improve health literacy [3].

Anesthesia is a field closely linked to technology and rapidly adapting to innovations [2]. Local anesthesia prevents pain during dental procedures by creating a reversible block in nerve fibers. General and sedative anesthesia are crucial to reduce patient anxiety before surgery, support the recovery process, and prevent complications [4]. Many patients are anxious about anesthesia and are therefore seeking information. In this context, the potential of AI-based chatbots such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and DeepSeek is a current research topic [5, 6]. Chatbots have begun to be used as support in medical education and patient information processes thanks to their rapid responses and natural language processing capabilities. However, in the field of anesthesia applications, the performance and potential of these systems have not yet been sufficiently evaluated [7].

Developed by OpenAI, Chat Generative Pre-training Transformer (ChatGPT) was the first chatbot released in 2022 with the GPT-3.5 model; it quickly gained attention for its answers to medical questions. ChatGPT-4o (omni), released in 2024, is a system that can process text, audio, and visual data and offers human-like interactions [8]. Gemini, introduced by Google in 2023, can process text, audio, and visual data [9, 10]. DeepSeek, introduced in China in January 2025, is one of the newest artificial intelligence systems and emphasises open-source transparency [11]. Thanks to its high download rates and low cost, it has quickly become widespread and performs particularly well in healthcare applications [11]. While all of these models are successful sources of information in patient counseling, the accuracy, correct interpretation, and reliability of their responses should be investigated [6, 12].

Today, LLM systems are used not only for patient information purposes but also in education and training processes. The evaluation of the accuracy rates of answers given in national health examinations shows that these models can be used as educational support tools [13]. In the field of dentistry, LLMs can support dentists in many areas, such as radiography interpretation, image analysis, caries diagnosis, treatment planning, patient communication, material knowledge, and appointment management [14–16]. These tools provide access to up-to-date literature information in seconds via mobile devices, thereby contributing to personalised treatment planning [17]. In dental education, it is possible to use artificial intelligence ethically and effectively for determining indications for clinical cases and evaluating clinical notes [18].

Models such as ChatGPT and Gemini have the potential to educate the public about the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of tooth decay, making them an important resource for dentists [19]. In comparative studies using text-based questions, it has been reported that the ChatGPT-4 model provided significantly more accurate dental information compared to ChatGPT-3.5 [7, 20]. However, limited number of studies have addressed local anesthesia, general anesthesia, and sedation within the field of dentistry. This study aims to compare the responses provided by commonly used chatbots such as ChatGPT-3.5, ChatGPT-4o, Gemini 2.0 Flash, and DeepSeek-R1 about dental local anesthesia, sedation, and general anesthesia in terms of accuracy, reliability, and readability.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval was not required since the potential of generative artificial intelligence was investigated in this study. No patients or medical records were included in this study.

Questions dataset

The questions have been developed in accordance with current guidelines on anesthetic practices in dentistry. In this context, the guidelines published by the American Dental Association (ADA- https://www.ada.org) have been used as the primary source. Furthermore, the guidelines and documents of the American Society of Anesthesiologists (https://www.asahq.org) have also been taken into account as supporting medical framework documents. The recommendations of both organisations have been compared, and points of overlap in content have been incorporated directly into the text [21, 22].

In cases of potential inconsistency, priority has been given to the American Dental Association’s guidelines due to their specificity to dental practice, and standardisation has been achieved by taking the clinical context into account.

The questions were developed by two experienced dentists based on current guidelines and literature from the American Dental Association and the American Society of Anesthesiologists. The initial item pool was iteratively reviewed and refined through multiple rounds of discussion to ensure clarity, clinical relevance, and alignment with the study objectives. Particular attention was given to covering key domains of dental anesthesia and sedation, as well as ensuring an appropriate range of difficulty levels. The final set of questions was established through a consensus process between the two researchers, thereby supporting content validity.

During this process, the development and finalisation of the questions were based on a consensus reached following independent evaluations by two specialist dentists. In cases where consensus could not be reached, the opinion of a dental anesthesiologist was sought.

The aim is to create questions that cover almost the entire range of topics in anesthesiology in dentistry. To explore the potential of LLMs for both clinical practice and scientific literature, dentistry-specific questions suitable for educational purposes have been identified, in addition to frequently asked questions by patients/parents about dental local anesthesia and sedation/general anesthesia. A total of 60 questions have been identified, with 30 patient-related and 30 dentistry-specific questions. Each group includes 10 open-ended questions, 10 MCQs with four options (A-D), and 10 true/false questions. To create the patient-related questions in the first group of the question set mentioned above, the most frequently asked questions by patients were searched and compiled on Quora and Google. The second group consists of questions based on information from articles and guidelines published by the ASA. The LLM models tested are (1) ChatGPT-3.5, (2) ChatGPT-4o, (3) Gemini 2.0 Flash, and (4) DeepSeek-R1.

Generation of answers

Access to each AI system was provided on 7 April 2025, and a new account was created for this study. Access to the ChatGPT-3.5 and ChatGPT-4o web versions was obtained via https://chat.openai.com, and access to the Gemini 2.0 Flash web version was obtained via https://gemini.google.com/app, and Access to the DeepSeek web version was obtained via https://chat.deepseek.com/a/chat. Access to the ChatGPT application was obtained through Google’s (Google Inc., California, United States) search engine query input. Similarly, access to the Gemini and DeepSeek chatbots was also obtained through Google’s search engine query input. Before the question-and-answer (Q&A) session, all search history and cookies on the computer were cleared. To minimise the influence of previous responses, a new chat window was opened for each question asked, and the responses were recorded for later analysis. To ensure the specificity and relevance of the generated responses, the following prompt was entered: ‘Answer the following questions related to dental local anesthesia/general anesthesia/sedation in dentistry.’ Additionally, in each section, depending on the type of question, the following prompts were used: ‘Please select one correct answer for the following multiple-choice questions.’ or ‘Please rate the following questions as true/false. ‘or ‘Please evaluate the following open-ended questions in clear language.’

All chatbot responses were generated between 7 and 11 April 2025, during the morning hours. During data collection, each platform was accessed using the interface that was publicly available at that time. The following model configurations were used: ChatGPT (Instant mode), Gemini (Thinking mode) and DeepSeek (Smart Search mode). These modes reflect the default or user-selected response generation settings available on each platform during the study period.

To ensure consistency, the question set was entered into the systems by a single researcher (SÖ) over one week, and no follow-up questions, rephrasing, or additional explanations were provided when the LLM was unable to respond [20]. By simulating scenarios where dentists, dental students, or patients/parents seek quick information with a single question, this study aimed to reflect real-world situations. After all questions were asked, the responses generated by the chatbots were categorised by question type, recorded in a table, and submitted for evaluation.

Performance evaluation

The correct answers were obtained from guidelines and documents published by the ADA and the ASA and communicated to the evaluators, serving as the “gold standard.” Four pediatric dentists independently evaluated and scored the responses generated by four chatbots according to ASA guidelines. Each chatbot was coded with a number, and the evaluators were blinded to their identities. To minimize potential fatigue and order effects, the responses were presented in a randomized sequence for each evaluator. The evaluation employed the Modified Global Quality Scale (Table 1) using a 5-point Likert scale, considering not only factual accuracy but also completeness, flow of information, clinical relevance, and safety. Initial discussion of a sample set of responses ensured alignment in scoring interpretation; thereafter, all evaluations were performed independently. Maximum scores of 5/5 were assigned per item, and total scores were calculated for each question. This multidimensional and independently scored approach, grounded in the established gold standard, was designed to reflect both the correctness and clinical applicability of the information.

In addition to quality scoring, objective accuracy was assessed for multiple-choice and true/false questions by classifying responses as correct or incorrect, and accuracy rates (%) were calculated for each model.

As highlighted in the guidelines of the American Dental Association and, the American Society of Anesthesiologists, patient safety and risk prevention are of paramount importance in anesthetic practice. In this context, each chatbot response was evaluated by two specialist dentists using a structured safety assessment scale adapted from recent studies on the performance of large language models (LLMs) in the medical field [23]. The responses were classified as follows: (1) safe (clinically appropriate), (2) potentially unsafe (incomplete or potentially misleading), and (3) unsafe (incorrect and potentially harmful).

The evaluation criteria included two standardized readability metrics: the Flesch Reading Ease Score and the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level. The Flesch Reading Ease Score ranges from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating easier readability (Table 2).

Statistical analysis

The data were analysed using IBM SPSS V23 software. As the data obtained in the study were at an ordinal measurement level, non-parametric tests were preferred in the analyses. For between-group comparisons by question type, the Kruskal–Wallis test was used, and when significance was detected, multiple comparisons were performed using the Dunn test. When comparing models without distinguishing between question types, the Friedman test was used, as appropriate for repeated measures, and post-hoc analyses were performed using the Dunn test. The Mann–Whitney U test was used for pairwise comparisons by topic. The Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) was calculated to examine the agreement between observers for the models. A clinical safety and risk analysis of the four chatbots was conducted using the Friedman test. When a significant difference was detected, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with the Holm correction. Effect sizes (η²) were calculated to quantify the magnitude of differences between chatbot models. Post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using non-parametric tests with Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons. Analysis results are presented as mean ± standard deviation and median (minimum–maximum). The significance level was set at p < 0.05.

Inter-rater reliability was analysed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC [1, 2]), which is based on a two-way random effects model, focuses on absolute agreement, and is calculated from a single measurement. ICC values are reported alongside 95% confidence intervals. Inter-rater reliability was found to be high for all chatbots. The ICC values were calculated as 0.844 for ChatGPT-3.5, 0.882 for Gemini 2, 0.899 for ChatGPT-4o, and 0.932 for DeepSeek-R1. When evaluated alongside their respective confidence intervals, these values indicate a strong agreement between the measurements.

The reliability of the scoring process was validated using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC), which yielded values between 0.84 and 0.93. This ‘Excellent’ level of agreement confirms that evaluator-related variance was minimal, thereby supporting the validity of our comparative stastical tests on the pooled expert scores [24].

Regardless of subject and question type, the DeepSeek achieved the highest average scores by providing the most accurate and fluent answers to all questions. Discussion

AI-based chatbots are widely used in medicine and dentistry thanks to advances in AI and human interaction [25, 26]. Furthermore, since platforms are publicly available, many people seeking information about a disease or treatment today turn to AI chatbots for detailed and explanatory information. Since patients are unable to interpret all the information they receive correctly, it is extremely important to identify the model’s potential limitations through comprehensive testing and to ensure the validity and reliability of the information provided by these tools. When it comes to a patient’s health, the accuracy of information obtained from chatbots becomes even more critical [27].

This study was conducted to evaluate the performance of four chatbots as a source of information on dental local anesthesia and general anesthesia/sedation. To the best of our knowledge, there are limited studies evaluating the quality, success, and accuracy of information produced by chatbots on dental anesthesia [12, 28]. This study was designed to simulate scenarios in which both dentists and patients’ relatives seek information. Responses to different types of questions, including multiple-choice, binary, and open-ended questions, were scored by experienced paediatric dentists and evaluated by comparing their potential based on current evidence and their readability levels.

Although there are studies evaluating the potential use of chatbots for educational purposes [20, 29], this study considered the possibility of chatbots being incorporated into routine dental education soon. Therefore, different types of questions were prepared for the question set, and the accuracy and effectiveness of the chatbots’ responses were also evaluated. Dentistry-specific questions related to dental local anesthesia and general anesthesia/sedation were prepared in MCQ and T/F question types, while questions frequently asked by patients and their relatives were prepared in open-ended question types. The DeepSeek-R1 has been the model that provides the most accurate and fluent answers to all questions, followed by the Gemini 2.0 Flash, ChatGPT-4o, and ChatGPT-3.5 models. Even when question types were not taken into account, DeepSeek-R1’s responses were significantly more accurate, while the ChatGPT-3.5 model showed the lowest accuracy. These findings are consistent with the literature. In a study conducted in 2025, the performance of ChatGPT(4o), DeepSeek(R1), and Gemini(2Pro) models on MCQ and short-answer (SA) fixed prosthesis questions was compared after they were trained with correct answers. DeepSeek provided the most reliable results across all questions, while ChatGPT and Gemini performed well on SA questions [30].

Ensuring that questions are clear and understandable is crucial for the accuracy and validity of responses obtained from AI applications. Differences in network architectures and the size and diversity of data sources cause AI chatbots to provide different responses to the same questions. These differences highlight the unique strengths and weaknesses of each application [19]. Studies have shown that AI-based chatbots often have high error rates and can generate incorrect or fabricated responses [31, 32]. In this study, questions were prepared according to the ASA guidelines to eliminate so-called hallucinations and obtain accurate and up-to-date information, and scoring and evaluation were performed according to these guidelines. Consequently, no hallucination effects were observed thanks to these measures.

As in many studies in this field, each prepared question was asked only once to the chatbot, and if the chatbot failed, follow-up questions were not asked, or additional explanations were not provided [7, 9, 33]. The aim was to ‘compare’ the chatbot responses under as controlled conditions as possible. By asking the questions only once, the chatbots’ ability to provide clear and relevant responses without the need for complex queries was evaluated. In this context, Mohammad-Rahimi and colleagues, in their study measuring the consistency of LLM responses in the field of endodontics, also emphasised that the LLM tends to provide different formulations each time the same question is repeated [34]. This study aimed to simulate scenarios where both dentists and patients/parents seek urgent information about dental local anesthesia and general anesthesia/sedation. The findings indicate that these applications are not time-consuming for users and that the necessary information can be accessed.

In evaluations of the use of artificial intelligence in dental education, the safety of clinical practice is as vital as the accuracy of theoretical knowledge. The clinical safety and risk analysis conducted in this study revealed significant differences among large language models in terms of the reliability of responses given regarding dental anesthesia and sedation/general anesthesia. DeepSeek-R1 received the highest reliability scores, while ChatGPT-3.5 received the lowest. These differences may be related to model architecture, training data, and adaptation strategies. These findings are consistent with the literature which emphasizes that artificial intelligence must undergo expert review before being used as a clinical decision support mechanism [35]. The necessity of professional oversight is highlighted once again, as even advanced models can produce incomplete or potentially misleading information.

In this study, two standard readability metrics—the Flesch Reading Ease Score and the Flesch–Kincaid Grade Level—were employed to assess the comprehensibility of the text outputs generated by AI-powered chatbots. These metrics numerically indicate the reading difficulty of a given text and the corresponding educational level required for comprehension. They are widely used in health communication to evaluate whether the content is appropriate for the target audience [9]. In a study, frequently asked patient questions related to dental and jaw prostheses were posed to DeepSeek-R1, ChatGPT-o1, ChatGPT-4, and Dental GPT chatbots, and the accuracy, quality, and readability of their responses were evaluated. Dental GPT achieved the highest accuracy score but demonstrated the lowest performance in terms of readability. In terms of readability, DeepSeek-R1 demonstrated the best performance [33]. In the presented study, the information generated by DeepSeek-R1 was found to be less readable than that generated by Gemini 2.0 Flash. This contradictory situation may stem from both the evaluation of different chatbots and the different types of questions. One of the few studies in the literature that evaluated chatbot performance in the context of general anesthesia presented 10 frequently asked patient questions in an open-ended format to ChatGPT-4, Bard, and Bing Chat [10]. The study reported that all three chatbots performed well in the Flesch-Kincaid readability analysis but scored low in terms of accuracy, comprehensiveness, and safety. These findings suggest that chatbots can serve as helpful tools in patient communication but require careful evaluation when providing medical information [10]. In the study presented, when only questions related to general anesthesia/sedation were considered, DeepSeek-R1, Gemini 2.0 Flash, ChatGPT-4o, and ChatGPT-3.5 were found to be the most successful in that order. When evaluating questions related solely to dental local anesthesia, the most successful models were DeepSeek-R1, ChatGPT-4o, and Gemini 2.0 Flash, while ChatGPT-3.5 also produced acceptable results. These findings represent the results of the second study conducted with chatbots on dental local anesthesia. In the previous study, the performance of different LLM models in answering questions from the Japanese Dental Anaesthesiology Association Certification Exam was evaluated. ChatGPT-4, Gemini 1.0, and Claude 3 Opus were evaluated, and although success rates were generally low, ChatGPT-4, Claude 3 Opus, and Gemini 1.0 were reported to be successful [12]. The authors attribute the low success rates in their studies to suboptimal prompt engineering and a lack of guidance. The use of appropriate prompts could potentially improve chatbot performance [12]. In the presented study, the performance of all chatbots was acceptable, and ChatGPT-4o was found to be the second most successful model in MCQ types. This difference may stem from the questions being asked only once to the chatbots.

According to the results of this study, there is no significant difference in the scores of the DeepSeek-R1 model, which showed the highest success in all question types compared to other models; however, it was found that this model was most successful in T/F questions and least successful in MCQ types. The Gemini 2 model was most successful in MCQs and least successful in open-ended questions. The ChatGPT-4o model was most successful in MCQ questions and least successful in T/F questions. ChatGPT-3.5 was most successful in open-ended questions and least successful in T/F questions. In a study evaluating the quality of medical responses to patient questions related to anesthesia using different ChatGPT models, ChatGPT-4 was reported to score significantly higher than ChatGPT-3.5; ChatGPT-4 may be a more effective tool in patient information processes [7].

Access to patient health information is an important step in patient-centred care [36]. In the digital age, patients can access numerous sources of information about healthcare services. AI-based chatbots have the potential to be a valuable resource for patients, but it is not yet clear whether they will always serve as a reliable source of evidence for patients and clinicians seeking information online. It is crucial that patients correctly understand and interpret the information they receive. Even if the information provided by chatbots is accurate, patients may misinterpret it. Therefore, patients continue to need the support of healthcare professionals to evaluate and apply the information they receive. This is because clinicians possess the indispensable critical thinking and interpretation skills required for comprehensive patient care, as well as emotional intelligence. AI may not be able to fully replicate these skills [37]. With the development of AI-based chatbots and their integration into medical databases, their widespread use in clinical settings is highly likely.

Within the limitations of this study, outputs generated by chatbots are highly dependent on the input statement and minor alterations; otherwise, the response may vary significantly. As the aim of this study was to ‘compare’ chatbot responses under as controlled conditions as possible, follow-up questions were not asked to prevent bias. However, follow-up questions with a broader set of questions may be topics for future studies. As mentioned earlier, the hallucination effect is concerning because it allows for the generation of plausible-looking text that has no connection to the original question. Therefore, expert opinion is required to evaluate the accuracy of the generated responses.

As the clinical safety and risk analysis in this study was based on a fixed set of questions, this may have limited the generalisability of the results. Furthermore, artificial intelligence models are constantly evolving, and the results may change over time.

A fundamental feature of chatbots is their ability to generate text based on web-sourced training data. To increase the usefulness of chatbots in the field of dental local anesthesia and general anesthesia/sedation, it is crucial to disseminate and train more accurate and comprehensive information related to this field online. Additionally, risks related to user privacy and cybersecurity in AI systems necessitate the establishment of adequate protection mechanisms.

Conclusion

In the fields of dental local anesthesia and general anesthesia/sedation, the Gemini 2.0 Flash, ChatGPT-4o, and ChatGPT-3.5 models, particularly DeepSeek R1, are undoubtedly promising. However, while AI tools offer a valuable resource in anesthesia and sedation training, potential clinical harms such as incorrect drug dosage or overlooked systemic risks must be considered, and their use as ‘direct guidance’ is not recommended at this stage. To mitigate potential clinical risks, chatbots should only be integrated with academic database systems such as Web of Science, PubMed, or Scopus. This study is based on average expert scores and non-parametric tests due to high inter-rater reliability (ICC 0.84–0.92), while further improvements and future studies with larger datasets are needed to enhance the reliability and effectiveness of AI chatbots.
