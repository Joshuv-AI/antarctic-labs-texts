Development of a PRAME pMHC targeted T cell engager for solid tumor therapy
Authors: Katarzyna Skrzypczynska, Kristin Schimert, Heather Stephenson, In Kyoung Mah, David Mortenson, Kelli Boyd, Timothy Hardman, Nikolai Novikov, Elbert Seto, Sabrina Lu, Randy Yen, Brian Lee, Min Wang, Don Kang, Ying Huang, Xinchao Yu, Magdeleine Hung, Sheng Ding, Nathan Thomsen, Nicole Schirle Oakdale
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBispecific T cell engager (TCE) therapies have demonstrated transformative clinical success in the treatment of hematological cancers, but the lack of antigens that are sufficiently selective for malignant cells has hampered the success of TCEs in the solid-tumor space. To overcome the on-target, off-tumor toxicities that result from the expression of even low levels of tumor-associated antigens in healthy tissues, we sought to identify a TCE target with highly tumor-restricted expression patterns. Here, we characterize cancer-testes antigen Preferentially Expressed Antigen in Melanoma (PRAME) as a highly selective tumor antigen and identify a proteasomal degradation peptide PRAME425–433 (PRAME425) presented in the context of major histocompatibility complex I (MHCI) as an attractive TCE target. We designed a TCR-mimic (TCRm) antibody screening cascade that prioritizes screening anti-PRAME pMHC binders in off-target T cell dependent cellular cytotoxicity assays in a potent TCE format, rather than relying solely on traditional pMHC binding assays, to determine specificity. Using this screening cascade, we discovered antibodies that selectively bind PRAME425 pMHC without over-recognition of off-target peptides or MHCI via a TCR-like binding geometry. We further solved the first structure of an anti-PRAME425 pMHC TCRm antibody in complex with PRAME425/HLA-A *02:01 using cryo electron microscopy to confirm the TCRm antibody binds in a TCR-like binding geometry and specifically recognizes the PRAME425 peptide. By formatting these novel TCRm antibodies into potent TCEs, we demonstrate PRAME425 pMHC-specific killing of tumor cells, representing a new class of anti-PRAME pMHC biologics.KEYWORDS: PRAME, TCR-mimic (TCRm) antibody, T cell engager, solid tumor, Cancer testes antigen, pMHC complex, immunotherapy, diabody

Introduction

T cell engagers (TCEs) are bispecific antibodies that induce T cell-mediated tumor cell killing through simultaneous engagement of a tumor antigen on cancer cells and the CD3 subunit of the T cell receptor (TCR) on T cells. TCEs for the treatment of hematological cancers have shown immense clinical benefit and complete responses, demonstrating the transformative promise of this therapeutic modality.1 By definition, the abundance of solid tumor-associated antigens (TAAs), such as EpCAM, EGFR, CEA, or HER2, is increased in malignant cells compared with healthy tissues. However, TAA expression in normal cells is often present at levels sufficient to result in cytotoxicity in healthy tissues. On-target, off-tumor toxicity has long been an obstacle hindering the development of TCEs that offer the same deep and durable antitumor responses in solid tumors as those attained against hematological malignancies.2 In response to these clinical outcomes, the search for solid tumor TAAs that offer improved safety and widened therapeutic window has turned toward targets such as cancer testis antigens, which have improved tumor specificity.

Cancer testis antigens (CTAs) are a category of over 200 proteins whose expression is restricted to the testes and placenta, but which become aberrantly expressed in cancers as a result of dysregulated epigenetic and transcriptional processes in malignant cells.3,4 CTA expression is most commonly found in melanomas, bladder, lung, and hematological malignancies, and with lower frequency in kidney and colon cancers.3 CTAs, including MAGE, NY-ESO-1, and PRAME, have been implicated in promoting tumor growth and progression by impacting mechanisms associated with cell division, evasion of apoptosis, regulation of epithelial to mesenchymal transition, metastasis, and modulation of the tumor microenvironment, making these attractive therapeutic targets.3

Most CTAs are intracellularly expressed and are therefore inaccessible as surface antigens to TCEs except as self-peptides presented on the cell surface in the context of the major histocompatibility complex (MHC). Peptide-MHC (pMHC) complexes are traditionally recognized by TCRs on T cells and can drive an immune response when an immunogenic peptide is presented and recognized by T cell expressing a cognate TCR. This mechanism of pMHC recognition by T cells has been exploited to develop cancer therapies through CTA peptide-based vaccination approaches that can elicit antitumor humoral responses, or through chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CAR-T) engineered to express TCRs (TCR-T cells) that specifically recognize CTA peptides.5,6 However, cancer vaccines have been slow to show clinical benefit and current autologous TCR-T therapies have limited manufacturing capacity due to the need to engineer cells from individual patients.

Tebentafusp (KIMMTRAK®), a recombinantly expressed and affinity-matured gp100-targeted TCR fused to an anti-CD3 binding domain (ImmTAC), has been approved for the treatment of metastatic uveal melanoma and represents the first in a new class of off-the-shelf biologic TCE therapies targeting CTAs.7 However, recombinant TCRs present challenges in the drug development process due to their poor thermostability, heavy glycosylation, and generally micromolar affinities.8 While there have been developments introducing stabilizing mutations and improving affinities to picomolar ranges, manufacturing remains a challenge.8–12 TCR-mimic (TCRm) antibodies are an alternative approach to circumvent the complexities typically associated with recombinant TCR development. Antibodies are generally thermostable, lack variable domain glycosylation sites, have nanomolar binding affinities and can be manufactured using traditional processes. A growing number of TCRm antibodies are being used to target pMHC CTAs.13–18

Here, we describe the characterization of PRAME as a prevalent, highly cancer-selective solid tumor target and the discovery of TCRm antibodies that recognize PRAME425 pMHC. Given the need for exquisite selectivity to PRAME425 pMHC and the potential for severe toxicity resulting from recognition of off-target peptides, we developed an extensive antibody screening cascade to identify highly selective antibodies. First, antibody antigen-binding fragments (Fabs) were selected for their binding preference for on-target PRAME425, but not off-target MAGE-A4230 pMHC antigens or those that bound only the N- or C-terminus of the PRAME425 peptide, using peptide-pulsed T2 cell binding assays.19–21 We next formatted hits as potent diabody-Fc TCEs and screened for off-target killing of PRAME-negative cell lines to remove nonselective binders that either bind off-target peptides or disproportionately recognize MHC.22 In this way, we were able to identify selective antibodies in a high-throughput and highly sensitive T cell dependent cellular cytotoxicity (TDCC) assay in the intended therapeutic format, rather than relying on traditional binding approaches to select the top molecules. Antibodies that mediate only PRAME-positive cell killing were further evaluated in an X-scan peptide-pulsed T2 cell binding assay to identify antibodies with selective binding profiles.23 Finally, we solved the cryo electron microscopy (cryoEM) structure of a selective TCRm bound to the PRAME425/HLA-A *02:01 complex and demonstrated that this TCRm antibody binds with a “head-on” geometry, similar to the binding geometry of endogenous TCRs.24 Taken together, we demonstrate the utility of anti-PRAME425 pMHC antibodies as TCE therapeutics that specifically recognize and mediate the killing of PRAME-positive tumor cells, but not PRAME-negative cells.

Results

Characterization of PRAME expression in solid tumors and normal tissues

We performed a bioinformatic analysis of the RNA transcript abundance of six well-described CTAs (PRAME, NY-ESO, ADP, KK-LC-1, MAGE-A4, MAGE-A10) across breast, lung, head and neck, and hematological indications. Among these, only PRAME showed consistently high expression in all indications evaluated and was the most abundant CTA in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), diffuse-large B-cell lymphoma (DLBC), and breast cancer (BRCA) with little to no expression in normal tissues (Figure 1(A), Supplemental Figure S1). This is consistent with literature reports suggesting that PRAME is highly expressed in a variety of solid tumors.25–28 To confirm the RNAseq expression analysis, a panel of 44 squamous cell carcinoma non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), 80 adenocarcinoma NSCLC, 55 triple negative breast cancer (TNBC), 67 DLBCL, and 52 head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) patient-derived samples were selected to characterize PRAME expression by immunohistochemistry (IHC). Patient tumor samples were stained with an anti-PRAME antibody to determine the percentage of tumors that express PRAME and to assess the heterogeneity of PRAME across tumor samples, as PRAME-negative portions of the tumor could be resistant to an anti-PRAME therapy and lead to tumor resistance (Figure 1(B)). Consistent with the RNAseq analysis, 35–70% of tumors stained were positive for PRAME expression (Figure 1(C)). However, expression heterogeneity varied from indication to indication, with PRAME expression being most homogeneous in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) samples and most heterogeneous in HNSCC samples (Figure 1(D)). PRAME-positive samples from NSCLC, TNBC, and HNSCC were also evaluated for HLA-A expression by IHC. All PRAME-positive samples expressed HLA-A in the tumor cells, stroma, or both. More than 50% of all tumor samples expressed HLA-A in both tumor and stroma, with LUSC having the fewest number of samples (53.3%) and HNSC having the greatest number of samples (93.3%) co-expressing PRAME and HLA-A (Supplemental Figure S2). A tissue microarray (TMA) composed of normal tissue was also stained for PRAME expression to understand the potential target-related toxicities in healthy tissues. PRAME expression was limited to lymph node, adrenal gland, and strong staining in the reproductive tract (testis, endometrium, and ovary) (Supplemental Figure S3). The high levels of PRAME expression in solid tumors combined with limited expression in normal tissue make PRAME pMHC a promising solid tumor target with low risk of on-target, off-tumor toxicity.

Several PRAME peptides that bind to HLA-A *02:01 have been identified in the literature and/or have been targeted by clinical-stage therapeutics.13,29–33 Thus, we sought to compare the peptide abundance of several literature-described or clinically targeted PRAME peptides at the cell surface. We subjected a panel of nine HLA-A *02:01-positive cancer cell lines derived from solid tumor malignancies with varied levels of PRAME and HLA expression to quantitative mass spectrometric analysis of peptides following MHC immunoprecipitation and elution of surface-presented peptides. The abundance of six PRAME peptides (PRAME100, PRAME142, PRAME300, PRAME371, PRAME394, and PRAME425) was quantified relative to stable isotype-labeled peptide standards and a corresponding surface expression density was determined for each PRAME peptide (Figure 1(E), Supplemental Table S1). One of the peptides, PRAME142, was not detected in any of the cell lines tested. PRAME peptides PRAME100, PRAME300, and PRAME371 were detected only at low abundance (< 20 peptide MHC per cell) in one to three of the nine cell lines assayed. However, both the PRAME394 and PRAME425 were detected in at least six of the nine cancer cell lines tested and were observed in varying levels of abundance from ~10 to 500 pMHC per cell. On average, the surface density of the PRAME425 peptide was higher than PRAME394 and so the PRAME425 pMHC was chosen as the therapeutic target.

Identification of highly selective anti-PRAME pMHC antibodies

Highly selective anti-pMHC antibodies are difficult to discover since only the nine or ten amino acids of the peptide differentiate the pMHC of interest from all other off-target pMHCs. We, therefore, anticipated that the majority of antibodies discovered would likely bind primarily to MHC and would not be peptide selective. To maximize sequence diversity and increase the hit rate for peptide selective antibodies, a variety of species, including Lewis rats, wild-type C57BL/6 mice, wild-type BALB/c mice, Trianni humanized mice, and Alloy humanized mice were immunized with purified recombinant complexes of human HLA-A *02:01 MHC and PRAME425 peptide (PRAME425 pMHC). We used microfluidic single B cell screening to analyze antibody binding to PRAME425 pMHC and an off-target peptide MAGE-A4230 loaded into the same human HLA-A *02:01 MHC (MAGE-A4230 pMHC) to identify and eliminate binders that were not peptide selective. From 1.6 million B cells screened, we recovered ~250 unique antibody sequences that bound only the on-target PRAME425 pMHC. The resulting sequences were analyzed for complementarity-determining region H3 (CDR-H3) diversity based on 75% identity to classify clonal families and unique antibodies. A total of 131 unique antibodies were formatted as Fabs and purified for additional screening.

To evaluate the specificity and potency of each Fab in a cellular context, we next carried out multiplexed on- and off-target binding assays using the T2 lymphoblast cell line. T2 cells express HLA-A but are deficient in antigen processing and presentation, which facilitates exclusive presentation of exogenous peptides of interest.20,21 T2 cells were pulsed with PRAME425 peptide (on-target) or an unrelated MAGE-A4230 peptide (off-target) and co-incubated with beta-2-microglobulin (B2M) to form the pMHC complex. The 131 unique Fabs were incubated with peptide-pulsed T2 cells and analyzed by flow cytometry to assess on- vs. off-target cell binding (Figure 2, Supplemental Table S2, Supplemental Figure S4, Supplemental Figure S8A). Of the 131 Fabs screened, 27 did not bind at all or bound weakly with EC50 >11 nM (Figure 2(B)) to PRAME425 peptide-pulsed T2 cells. Another 27 Fabs were considered nonselective because they bound the MAGE-A4230 peptide-pulsed T2 cells with an EC50 <200 nM (Figure 2(C)). The majority of the Fabs screened, 77, were considered selective because they bound potently (EC50 < 11 nM) to on-target PRAME425 peptide-pulsed T2 cells and with >20-fold EC50 window between on- and off-target MAGE-A4230 peptide-pulsed T2 cells (Figure 2(D)).

To elucidate the approximate binding footprint of the 77 PRAME425-selective Fabs, we performed an alanine scan by incubating Fabs with T2 cells that were pulsed with PRAME425 peptides bearing either the parental PRAME425 sequence or single alanine mutations at each position to identify and eliminate binders most likely to be nonselective in TDCC assays when formatted as TCEs (Figure 3(A)).19 Ideally, a highly selective antibody would recognize PRAME425 pMHC in a “TCR-like” geometry, similar to the anti-NY-ESO-1157 pMHC Fab 3M4F4 (Protein Data Bank (PDB) ID: 3GJF), by binding to the center of the peptide and making the majority of binding contacts across several peptide residues (Supplemental Figure S5).16 We therefore sought to exclude “end-binders,” or those similar to the WT1126 pMHC binding antibody ESK1 (PDB ID: 4WUU), that make contacts only to the N- or C-terminal residues of the peptide. “End-binder” antibodies may bind with an overcontribution of contact to MHC or have minimal peptide residue contacts, which may increase the risk of binding peptides from the human proteome that share sequence similarity with PRAME425 (Supplemental Figure S5). Indeed, high-affinity binding of the ESK1 antibody was driven primarily by MHC interactions and the ESK1 antibody could recognize peptides from the human proteome with homologous N-terminal amino acids.34 As such, an anti-PRAME425 pMHC Fab was considered to be an “End-binder” if a single alanine mutation reduced antibody binding to <60% of wild-type PRAME425 peptide at only the first four N-terminal or last four C-terminal amino acid positions (Figure 3(B)). Likewise, a Fab was considered nonselective if it was sensitive to alanine mutation at <4 positions. Consistent with this, an antibody discovered from a previous campaign, PR-19, was included in our TDCC assay as a control molecule because it was demonstrated to have an “End-binder” geometry in the alanine scan assay and mediated off-target killing of a PRAME-negative cell line, U87MG (Figure S6). A Fab was considered to have a TCR-like binding footprint if it was sensitive to alanine mutation at ≥4 positions spanning the middle four residues (positions 3–6) (Figure 3(C), Supplemental Figure S7). However, Fabs sensitive to <4 positions were additionally included if their binding EC50 was <1 nM, alanine mutations at >2 TCR-facing positions (P1/P4/P5/P8) reduced binding by >60%, and they did not bind off-target MAGE-A4230 peptide. Of the 77 hits screened in the alanine scanning assay, 37 met the criteria for a TCR-like binding footprint with 25/37 making ≥4 contacts and 12/37 making <4 contacts (Figure 3(D)).

While we expected the alanine scan assay to enrich for selective binders, we anticipated that not all antibodies classified as having a TCR-like binding geometry in our assay would harbor the highly selective properties we sought in a potential TCE targeting arm. Indeed, literature suggests that even selective binders or binders with a “TCR-like” binding geometry may demonstrate off-target reactivity when formatted as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T cells.22 Thus, binding alone may not be sensitive enough to discriminate between truly selective and nonselective binders. We therefore sought to screen the resulting 37 anti-PRAME425 pMHC antibodies in a TCE format in off-target killing assays to eliminate nonspecific binders, followed by on-target killing assays with high and low density PRAME target cell lines to identify those binders with ideal binding geometry and selective killing profiles. The diabody-Fc TCE has been shown to enable potent killing of low receptor density targets relative to a traditional bispecific IgG format by bridging a tighter immunological synapse.35 Given the low density of PRAME pMHC on target cells, we reasoned that traditional bispecific antibody formats may not be potent enough to drive off-target killing and the 37 binders were reformatted to diabody-Fc TCEs for screening in TDCC assays (Supplemental Table S3). Of the 37 TCEs screened, the majority mediated potent killing of PRAME-negative, homozygous HLA-A *02:01 U87MG cells at 2:1 effector-to-target (E:T) ratio, while only six (HU-08, HU-38, HU-19, HU-58, BC-03, and B6–22) did not demonstrate any appreciable killing (Figure 4(A)). These six antibodies were further screened for their ability to kill NCI-H1755 (high density; ~105 PRAME425 pMHC per cell), SAOS-2 (low density; ~20 PRAME425 pMHC per cell), and U87MG (PRAME-negative) cancer cell lines at a higher 4:1 E:T. The NCI-H1755 and SAOS-2 cell lines were chosen to represent potential diversity in PRAME expression in patient tumors and enable us to rank order our lead candidates for their ability to mediate killing of tumors with a variety of PRAME expression levels. Four of the six antibodies screened (HU-08, HU-38, HU-19, and HU-58) mediated potent killing of both PRAME-high (NCI-H1755) and PRAME-low (SAOS-2) expressing cell lines while demonstrating no off-target killing of PRAME-negative cells (Figure 4(B–E), Table 1) and were carried forward for further peptide specificity screening. Two antibodies, BC-03 and B6–22, demonstrated both on- and off-target killing at higher E:T and were deprioritized (Figure 4(F–G), Table 1). Taken together, the potent diabody-Fc format enabled high-throughput TDCC selectivity screening of the discovered binders in the modality of the intended therapeutic, thereby limiting the number of time-consuming reformatting steps and facilitating quicker identification of lead molecules.

To determine the peptide recognition profile of the selective diabodies, we pulsed T2 cells with peptides bearing single amino acid substitutions to all other 19 amino acids at each position for an X-scan binding analysis.23 The four antibodies with no detectable off-target killing were incubated with peptide-pulsed T2 cells at the binding EC80 of each antibody to wild-type peptide and assessed for their ability to bind each peptide in the X-scan (Figure 5, Supplemental Figure S10). Binding to each X-scan peptide was normalized as percent binding relative to wild-type peptide to give the probability of antibody binding to any amino acid at each position. Each antibody had a unique binding footprint, with HU-19 (Figure 5(B)) and HU-38 (Figure 5(C)) having the most selective binding profile to TCR-facing residues at positions 4, 5, and 8. The normalized binding scores for each PRAME X-scan peptide were then used to derive a list of peptides of concern from the human proteome. Consistent with the X-scan binding data, HU-19 and HU-38 were found to have the least number of in silico predicted off-target peptides, with 16 and 26 predicted, respectively, for each. However, the HU-19 diabody-Fc TCE had a strong propensity to aggregate and low purification yield (Supplemental Table S4). We therefore deprioritized the HU-19 diabody-Fc TCE and proceeded with HU-38 as a lead candidate.

Structural analysis of lead antibody HU-38

To understand the requirements for pMHC recognition by the HU-38 clone, we determined the structure of the HU-38 Fab/PRAME425 pMHC complex by cryo-EM to 2.72 Å resolution (Figure 6, Supplemental Figure S11, Supplemental Table S5). The Fab:pMHC complex was purified by size exclusion chromatography (SEC) and then complexed with excess anti-Kappa VHH to increase molecular mass and rigidify the Fab. In the cryoEM structure, pMHC and CDR residues at the interface are well-resolved in the density, allowing for analysis of specific contacts. HU-38 binds the PRAME pMHC with linear or “TCR-like” geometry (Supplemental Figure S12), and forms contacts with multiple residues of the PRAME425 peptide. The HU-38 antibody contacts with the PRAME peptide are formed primarily by the heavy-chain CDRs (CDR1 and CDR3), while light-chain CDRs form most of their contacts with the MHC (Supplemental Figures S13, S14). Heavy chain CDR1 residue Y32 interacts with peptide residues Q4 and H5 via hydrogen bond and packing, respectively. Heavy chain CDR3 residue E97 forms direct hydrogen bonds to peptide residues Q4 and L6, while residues I98 and H99 form through-water contacts with the peptide. Additionally, multiple residues from heavy chain CDR3 form packing contacts with the peptide (e.g., CDR3 H99 with peptide I7). In addition to forming direct contacts with the PRAME peptide, heavy-chain residue E97 forms a salt bridge interaction with MHC residue R65, as observed for some other TCRm antibodies (Supplemental Figure 15).16,36

Complex overview and detailed view of HU-38 Fab:PRAME425 pMHC complex interface. A) HU-38 engages the PRAME425 pMHC in a linear or “TCR-like” orientation, allowing for formation of contacts with multiple PRAME425 residues. B) HU-38:PRAME425 contacts are mediated largely by residues from heavy chain CDR1 and CDR3. Contacts include direct and water-mediated hydrogen bonds as well as hydrophobic/packing interactions.

Interestingly, only a single residue from the HU-38 light-chain CDRs forms a direct contact with the PRAME425 peptide: W32 from light chain CDR1 packs against peptide residue G8. Light chain CDR1 is 100% conserved among the four lead antibodies and contact between W32 and peptide residue G8 likely accounts for the strict preference for glycine at this peptide position (Figure 5). Another light chain CDR residue, F94, is positioned near peptide residue I7, but does not form a direct contact. However, F94 interacts directly with heavy chain CDR3 residue H99, which directly contacts peptide residue I7. These two CDR residues may account for the strict preference of HU-38 for isoleucine at this peptide position. While the CDR-H3 sequence varies among the four lead antibodies, light chain CDR-H3 residue F94 is conserved, and may contribute to the different but nonetheless strict preferences toward peptide position 7 among the four lead antibodies. Overall, “TCR-like” binding observed in the HU-38 complex structure is consistent with the X-scan profile for this lead, which shows peptide sequence selectivity at multiple positions across the length of the PRAME peptide.

Discussion

Differences in protein expression levels, proteasomal degradation of intracellular antigens such as CTAs, and peptide affinity for MHC drive varied display of peptide antigens at the cell surface.37,38 We used targeted quantitative mass spectrometry to determine the abundance of six PRAME peptides eluted from MHC precipitated from cancer cell lines and found that they were displayed at variable densities across cell lines, with the PRAME394 and PRAME425 peptides being displayed with the highest abundance in the cancer cell lines evaluated. Thus, careful selection of a pMHC target with high antigen density may aid the design of an optimally potent TCE. Using a similar approach of mass spectrometric analysis of peptides presented by pMHC immunoprecipitated from both cell lines and primary samples representative of multiple solid tumor indications, Ribeiro et al. corroborate our findings in their report describing the predominance of the PRME425 peptide.31 While T cells can mediate killing of target cells through recognition of only 2–10 pMHC per cell, killing potency increases as antigen density increases.11 Indeed, we found that the killing potency of anti-PRAME425 pMHC TCEs against cells displaying ~100 PRAME425 peptide per cell (NCI-H1755) were ~10-fold more potent than cells displaying only ~20 PRAME425 peptide per cell (SAOS-2).

Although CTA pMHC targets are generally associated with improved safety due to their highly restricted expression patterns in normal tissues, the risk of severe adverse events resulting from binding to unrelated pMHC complexes can be difficult to predict and highlights the need for thorough off-target screening. In 2013, a clinical trial involving a CAR-T directed against the EVDPIGHLY peptide of the CTA MAGE-A3 was terminated as a result of fatalities in two patients due to off-target activity against cardiac tissue.19 While MAGE-A3 protein was not detected in the heart, it was later found that the expression of the muscle protein, A1-Titin, detectable only in beating cardiomyocyte cell culture derived from induced pluripotent stem cells, was the source of the similar peptide ESDPIVAQY, resulting in molecular mimicry and recognition of Titin pMHC peptides by the MAGE-A3 CAR-T.39,40 In an effort to predict such potentially devastating off-target interactions, a complex screening cascade was designed to identify only highly selective anti-PRAME425 pMHC leads. Binders that make few peptide contacts or bind only the N- or C-terminus of the peptide (e.g., “end-binders”) are associated with a higher likelihood of off-target binding by over-recognizing the MHC.22,34,41 Consistent with this, the PR-19 “end-binder” mediated off-target killing of PRAME-negative cells when formatted as a TCE. We assessed antibody epitope using an alanine scan binding assay to enrich for antibodies more likely to have selective killing profiles, such that binders that make few peptide contacts or bind only the N- or C-terminus of the peptide were excluded from further evaluation. We expected that even antibodies that appeared to have TCR-like binding footprints in low sensitivity alanine scan assays would still mediate off-target killing in higher sensitivity TDCC assays, suggesting these antibodies still have an over-contribution to MHC-binding despite having a “TCR-like” binding footprint. Indeed, from a total of 37 identified PRAME pMHC antibodies with selective binding profiles, only four had no propensity for off-target killing. The four lead antibodies were further evaluated in an X-scan epitope mapping assay to determine binding promiscuity, which confirmed the TCR-like binding mode of the antibodies.

The optimal potency of a TCE is dependent on multiple additional factors, including target epitope, the affinity and format of the targeting arm(s), and molecule valency.35,42 For abundant, surface-expressed TAAs such as CEA, a 2 + 1 TCE format consisting of two CEA-binding scFvs and one scFv CD3-targeting arm was shown to enhance potency compared with a monovalent (1 + 1) scFv–scFv format and 2 + 1 and 1 + 1 Fab-based TCEs.43 This increased potency can likely be attributed to avidity effects driving an enhanced functional affinity of the two CEA-targeting arms, as well as reduced interdomain distance between the smaller scFv CD3 and CEA binders, resulting in increased proximity of the effector and target cells. In contrast, CTA peptide-MHC (pMHC) antigens are much less abundant on the cell surface compared with conventional TAAs, observed at frequencies ranging from tens to a few hundred peptides per cell (Supplemental Table S1). In such cases, an avidity-based TCE may be less effective due to the reduced antigen density, which results in greater spatial separation between individual target molecules. In an evaluation of different TCE formats incorporating Fab-based mono- and bivalent TCEs against the pMHC antigen WT1, Walseng et al. determined that the ability of a TCE to induce the formation of an artificial immunological synapse through geometric conformation between the anti-pMHC and anti-CD3 arms and not avidity-driven target engagement was responsible for optimal potency against this target class.44 Consistent with this, we compared multiple 1 + 1 and 2 + 1 TCE formats and found that the 2 + 1 formats yielded no additional potency benefit relative to 1 + 1 formats targeting the PRAME425 peptide (data not shown).

Structural characterization of the complex formed between HU-38 and its target pMHC confirmed an optimal binding interaction localized to amino acids Q4–G8 in the center of the PRAME425 peptide. TCRs typically engage pMHCs with “head-on” or “linear” binding geometry, ensuring a broad contact surface with presented peptides.45–47 TCR binding geometry is thought to be constrained by co-evolution with the pMHC and/or structural compatibility with signaling complex formation.48 However, no such constraints are operative for pMHC-binding antibodies. In the screening cascade described here, lead antibodies with TCR-like binding were discovered via binding experiments with on- and off-target pMHCs, and this binding mode was confirmed by cryoEM for the lead HU-38. The four highly selective leads, HU-08, HU-19, HU-38, and HU-58, have 88% overall sequence identity, have the same CDR-H3 length, and share the same heavy (IGHV4–59) and light (IGKV1–12) germline usage. The light chains from these four antibodies bear even higher sequence identity, with 92.5% overall sequence identity and only a single amino acid difference (either F96 or Y96, no MHC contact) across all light chain CDRs. While “head-on” antibody:pMHC binding is likely necessary to achieve selective pMHC engagement, the precise nature of contacts with peptide residues will also play a key role in pMHC binding selectivity. Despite high sequence homology, the four leads still varied in their binding promiscuity, with HU-19 and HU-38 demonstrating the least potential for binding to homologous peptide sequences in the human proteome. The majority of amino acid contacts to the PRAME425 peptide are made by CDRs H1 and H3 of HU-38. The main differences between HU-08, HU-19, HU-38, and HU-58 lie in CDR-H3, where they share less than 75% sequence homology. Thus, the peptide promiscuity observed within this set of antibodies is likely driven by CDR-H3.

In summary, we demonstrate that PRAME is highly cancer-selective because it is expressed in many liquid and solid tumors with expression in normal tissues limited to only non-essential organs. Due to the intracellular expression of PRAME, we further quantified the surface expression density of several PRAME-derived MHC peptides and chose the PRAME425 peptide as a TCE anti-tumor target due to the increased abundance relative to other PRAME-derived MHC peptides. Finally, we discovered four selective anti-PRAME425 antibodies that mediate selective killing of only PRAME-positive cancer cells when formatted as a bispecific TCE. Due to the risk of MHC-binding modalities, these molecules need to be further evaluated for safety through additional assays, such as primary cell staining to test for off-target reactivity and B-lymphoblastoid cell line staining to assess alloreactivity. While several anti-PRAME biologics are under clinical evaluation, these therapies are derived from affinity-matured recombinant TCRs and our antibodies represent a novel class of anti-PRAME pMHC biologics.49,50

Materials and methods

Gene expression analysis

The RNASeq raw counts of TCGA tumor and GTEx healthy normal tissues were extracted using recount R package.51 The edgeR package was used to normalize the raw counts together to generate transcripts per kilobase million (TPM) gene expression levels.52 To compare PRAME expression between tumor and normal tissues, or to other CTA genes, the TPM values were log2 transformed (log2TPM) by adding 1 pseudo-value. All statistical analysis was performed in R version 4.0.3.

IHC staining

Human formalin-fixed, paraffin embedded (FFPE) tumor tissues were acquired from commercial vendors in indications of high unmet need. Human FFPE normal tissue TMAs (catalog # 69574031–3139, catalog# 69574031–3140) were obtained from TriStar Technology group. IHC staining for PRAME (Cell signaling Technology, catalog# 56426) was performed on the Discovery Ultra autostainer (Ventana Medical Systems, Roche Tissue Diagnostics) using the Discovery ChromoMap DAB kit (Roche Tissue Diagnostics, catalog# 760–159). Five um tissue sections were incubated with 0.5 ug/ml of PRAME antibody for 40 minutes at room temperature, then incubated with Discovery anti-Rabbit HQ (Roche Tissue Diagnostics, catalog# 760–4815), and discovery anti-HQ HRP (Roche Tissue Diagnostics, catalog# 760–4820) for 32 minutes each, followed by detection with the Discovery ChromoMap DAB kit. Slides were then counterstained with Hematoxylin (Roche Tissue Diagnostic, catalog# 790–2208) for 4 minutes and permanently mounted with Dako Mounting Medium (Agilent Technologies, CS70330–2) on an automated Dako coverslipper (Agilent Technologies, CR10030). IHC staining for HLA-A (Abcam, catalog# ab52922) were conducted on the Leica Bond RX autostainer (Leica Biosystem) using the Bond Polymer Refine Detection kit (Leica Biosystems, catalog# DS9800). The tissue sections were incubated with 0.075 ug/ml of HLA-A antibody for 15 minutes at room temperature. Whole slide Images were acquired using the Aperio AT2 slide scanner (Leica Biosystems). Scoring for staining of tumor tissue sections was performed by a pathologist. Expression data is shown from translational cohorts with >25% tumor cells expressing PRAME including non-small cell lung adenocarcinoma, non-small cell lung squamous cell carcinoma, TNBC, and HNSCC. Data is not shown from indications with <25% tumor cells expressing PRAME, renal cell carcinoma, prostate cancer, and colorectal cancer.

Quantitative mass spectrometry and pMHC antigen density determination

Approximately 200 million cells from each cell line were grown according to ATCC specifications, washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), harvested by scraping from the plate surface, pelleted, and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. The samples were transferred to MS Bioworks (Ann Arbor, MI) for MHC peptide quantification. Cell pellets were thawed on ice and resulting cell lysates were clarified via centrifugation, filtered, and immunoprecipitated with a pan-HLA antibody, W6/32. Peptides were eluted from the immunoprecipitated MHC and PRAME peptides were evaluated by mass spectrometry. For quantitative mass spectrometry, stable-isotope labeled peptide standard controls corresponding to PRAME100, PRAME300, PRAME425, PRAME142, PRAME371, PRAME394, and NY-ESO-1157 were spiked into peptide eluents at known concentrations. The number of peptides per cell was then determined by taking into account the number of input cells in the pellet and volume of cell lysate analyzed.

Recombinant peptide HLA-A *02:01 MHC production

MHC class I heavy chain and hβ2m preparation, purification, and folding were performed based on published protocols.53 Proteins were produced in E. coli BL21(DE3) cells. Cells were sedimented and lysed by microfluidizer in lysis buffer (50 mM Tris·Cl pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA, and 1 mM TCEP). Inclusion bodies were harvested by centrifugation, washed with detergent buffer (50 mM Tris·Cl pH 8.0, 1% Triton X 100, 5 mM EDTA, and 1 mM TCEP), followed by lysis buffer and then dissolved in 8 M urea and 20 mM tris pH 8 for 16 h at 4°C. Following high-speed centrifugation at 40,000 × g for 20 min, the soluble denatured protein in the supernatant (concentrations usually about 15 mg/ml for hβ2m, 7 mg/ml HLA) was stored at −80°C until use.

To fold the heavy chain-hβ2m-peptide complexes, 3 µM of heavy chain and 6 µM of β2m, and 10 μM of peptide as indicated were diluted into 250 ml of refolding buffer (100 mM Tris·Cl pH 8, 0.5 M arginine, 2 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM oxidized glutathione, 5 mM reduced glutathione), and the reaction was incubated at 4°C for 3 days, then centrifuged at 40,000 × g for 30 min to sediment aggregates. The concentration of soluble protein was then determined using the Bradford assay. The refolding mixture was concentrated in a Centricon-20 and loaded onto a Superdex S200 column in 20 mM Tris pH 8 100 mM NaCl. The peak corresponding to the MHC complex is analyzed by SDS-PAGE before being pooled. The presence of all three pMHC complex components was confirmed by mass spectrometry.

MHC/Fab complex generation

MHC complex was mixed with individual Fabs in a 1:1.2 ratio and injected into a Superdex-200 16 60 column equilibrated with a buffer containing 20 mM Tris pH 8, 100 mM NaCl. The UV profile of the mixture was superimposed with those of MHC and Fab to compare the elution volume.

Antibody discovery

Antibody discovery was carried out at AbCellera. Briefly, Lewis rats, wild-type BALB/c & C57BL/6 mice, Trianni humanized mice, and Alloy humanized mice were immunized with recombinant PRAME425-HLA *02:01 pMHC. Lymph nodes, spleen, and bone marrow were harvested and plasma cells were enriched for single B cell microfluidic screening. B cells producing antibodies that selectively bound fluorescently labeled beads coated with PRAME425 pMHC, but not fluorescently labeled beads coated with unrelated MAGE-A4230 pMHC were sequenced. From 1.6 million B cells screened, ~250 unique antibody sequences were identified. The sequences were analyzed and clustered by CDR-H3 at 75% identity to group clonal families. A total of 131 representative member(s) covering each clonal family were selected to screen in downstream assays.

Protein purification

Fab proteins were expressed in Expi293 cells and the harvested cell culture media were loaded onto an immobilized metal affinity chromatography column (Cytiva HisTrap HP) equilibrated with Buffer A (25 mM Tris pH 8.0 and 300 mM sodium chloride). After loading the column was washed for 5 column volumes with the equilibration buffer prior to protein elution using Buffer B (25 mM Tris pH 8.0, 300 mM sodium chloride, and 500 mM imidazole) in 5% and then 30% step elution where the majority of the protein desorption occurred. The pool was dialyzed to PBS pH 7.4 and 0.2 µm filtered. Analytical SEC was performed on the buffer exchanged Fab which determined the protein to be >95% monodispersed.

Diabody-Fc proteins were expressed in either Expi293 or ExpiCHO. The harvest cell culture media were clarified and 0.2 µm-filtered prior to loading on a Protein A column (Cytiva MabSelect SuRe or Cytiva Fibro PrismA) that was equilibrated using 25 mM Tris pH 7.5, 25 mM sodium chloride. The harvested cell culture fluid was then loaded onto the column and washed with the equilibration buffer. This was followed by a high salt wash using 25 mM Tris pH 7.5, 500 mM sodium chloride and then another equilibration buffer wash. Protein desorption was induced using 100 mM acetate pH 3.7 and the elution pool was immediately neutralized with 6% (by volume) of 1 M Tris pH 8.0 where some precipitation occurred. The pool was clarified using centrifugation at 5k ×g for 10 minutes and 0.2 µm filtration before moving to the next purification step on a CEX column (Cytiva SP Sepharose HP). The CEX column was equilibrated with Buffer A (25 mM MES pH 5.5, 20 mM sodium chloride) and eluted with an increasing gradient using Buffer B (25 mM MES pH 5.5, 1 M sodium chloride). If further polishing was required, the CEX pool was concentrated and then injected on an SEC column (Cytiva Superdex 200). All material was buffer-exchanged to 20 mM histidine pH 5.8 with the addition of 9% sucrose and 0.05% Tween 80 for long-term storage if needed. Samples were analyzed and determined to be >95% monodispersed by SEC-HPLC, SDS-PAGE (non-reduced and reduced), and mass spectroscopy (intact and reduced).

On- vs. Off-target peptide-pulsed T2 cell binding assays

Peptides for on and off-target screening were generated at Genscript (PRAME425 sequence: SLLQHLIGL; MAGE-A4230 sequence: GVYDGREHTV). T2 cells were incubated with 50 µg/mL peptides and 5 μg/mL β2 microglobulin (BD Biosciences Cat. # 551089) for 2–4 hours at 37°C and then cryopreserved and stored at −150°C. On the day of the assay, antibodies were serially diluted in 384-well plates in 10-point dose response in fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (PBS +2% fetal bovine serum) using a Biomek FXP liquid handler (Beckman Coulter). Peptide-pulsed T2 cells were thawed, washed with serum-free IMDM (Gibco Cat. #12440046), and resuspended in PBS. Cells were then incubated at room temperature for 20 minutes with Fc block (BioLegend Cat. # 422302) plus LIVE/DEAD stain (Invitrogen Cat. # L34965, Near-IR for on-target and Violet for off-target) for viability gating and to enable multiplexing into the same well. The cells were washed three times in FACS buffer, mixed, and dispensed to 384-well polypropylene v-bottom plates using a cassette on a BioTek EL406 washer dispenser (Agilent). Antibodies were added to cells and mixed using a Bravo liquid handler (Agilent), followed by incubation for 1 hour at 4°C. Cells were washed using a centrifuge to pellet cells, an EL406 to dispense and aspirate FACS buffer, and a MixMate (Eppendorf) to vortex cells. Goat anti-human IgG (H+L), PE (Invitrogen Cat. # PA1–86078) secondary antibody was added and incubated for 30 min at 4°C. Cells were washed before fixation with PBS + 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (ThermoFisher Scientific Cat. # AAJ61899AP) for 15 minutes at room temperature. The cells were washed a final time and resuspended in PBS for acquiring data on an iQue Screener PLUS flow cytometer (Sartorius). Mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of live, single cells was measured and dose–response curves were fit with nonlinear regression (four-parameter logistic) to derive EC50 values for off-target/on-target window assessment. EC80 values were also extracted to determine each antibody concentration to use in alanine or X-scan (described below). To ensure accurate EC80 recapitulation between the binding assay measuring EC80 and the peptide mutagenesis assay to assess footprint (run at each antibody’s PRAME425 peptide binding EC80), identical staining and assay conditions were used between the pairs of assays for Fabs and diabody-Fcs, respectively.

Alanine scan and X-scan peptide-pulsed T2 cell binding assays

Peptides for alanine scan and X-scan were generated at Genscript. T2 cells were washed with serum-free IMDM (Gibco Cat. #12440046), resuspended in PBS, and incubated with 50 μg/mL peptides and 5 μg/mL β2 microglobulin (BD Biosciences Cat. # 551089) for 2–4 hours at 37°C in 96-well v-bottom polypropylene plates. The peptide-pulsed cells were then transferred in duplicate (alanine scan) or quadruplicate (X-Scan) to 384-well v-bottom plates using a Biomek FX liquid handler (Beckman Coulter) and incubated with Fc block (BioLegend Cat. # 422302) plus LIVE/DEAD stain (Invitrogen Cat. # L34965) at room temperature for 20 minutes. The cells were washed with FACS buffer and each antibody of interest was added at its EC80 concentration measured from WT peptide binding (using identical assay conditions) and mixed using a Bravo liquid handler (Agilent). Cells and antibodies were then incubated for 1 hour at 4°C. Cells were washed and Goat anti-Human IgG (H+L), PE (Invitrogen Cat. # PA1–86078) secondary antibody (to detect the Fabs in the alanine scan) or Rat anti-human IgG Fc PE, Clone: M1310G05 (BioLegend Cat. #410708) secondary antibody (to detect the diabody-Fc format in the X-Scan) was added and incubated for 30 minutes at 4°C. Cells were washed before fixation with PBS + 4% PFA (ThermoFisher Scientific Cat. # AAJ61899AP) for 15 minutes at room temperature. The cells were washed a final time and resuspended in PBS for acquiring data on an iQue Screener PLUS flow cytometer (Sartorius). MFI was measured for each antibody-peptide pair and normalized to a percentage of binding relative to the mean MFI of multiple WT peptide controls on each plate for that antibody. If the normalized percentage of WT binding generated a negative number, it was set to 0.

In silico prediction of off-target peptides

In order to predict potential off-target peptides that could be theoretically recognized by each antibody, the raw binding score for each peptide was converted to a percentage relative to the target PRAME425 peptide sequence. The product of the percent binding value of each amino acid in a given peptide within the X-scan was determined to derive a list of all possible peptides with high binding scores to each antibody. A score of 0.1 corresponds to ~10% binding and would yield an ~100-fold window between on- and off-target binding. Peptide sequences that a scored >0.1 would therefore likely have a < 100-fold window between on- and off-target binding and were considered as potential off-target sequences. High scoring peptides were blasted against the human proteome to identify those peptides that are found in the human proteome.

T cell dependent cytotoxicity assays

Target cell lines were stably transduced with the nuclear-restricted fluorescent protein Nuclight Red (Sartorius, Cat#4476) per the manufacturer’s instructions. Target cells were seeded and allowed to adhere overnight. The next day, human CD8+ T cells were added at an effector to target (E:T) ratio as indicated in figure legends for 72 h at 37°C and 5% CO2 in the presence of anti-pMHC diabodies or negative control diabody with an irrelevant TAA-targeting arm. Specific lysis of target cells was determined by quantification of cell nuclei using Incucyte imaging software. Percent normalized killing was determined by determining the percent decrease in the number of nuclei detected compared with untreated control wells at each timepoint.

Cryo-EM

For the preparation of cryo-EM grids, HU-38/PRAME425 pMHC complex at 0.75 mg/mL total protein was mixed with a 2-fold molar excess of CaptureSelect LC-kappa VHH (ThermoFisher, cat# 1033270500). Grids were prepared using a VitroBot Mark IV (ThermoFisher) by spotting 3 μL of sample on glow-discharged Quantifoil UltrAuFoil grids (R1.2/1.3, Au 300 mesh) and blotting with 10 s wait time/2 s blot time before plunging into liquid ethane. The VitroBot was equilibrated at 10 degrees C and 100% humidity during sample freezing. Images were recorded on a 300 keV Titan Krios microscope with a K3 detector (Gatan). In total, 7341 movies were collected in.tif format and with a total exposure dose of ~50 e/Å2.

Data processing was carried out in CryoSPARC.54 Following patch motion correction and patch CTF estimation of movies, particles were initially picked via Gaussian blob picking and subjected to one round of 2D classification. A set of 15 2D classes were selected for a round of template-based particle picking, the output of which was filtered by 2D classification. All extracted particles were Fourier-cropped to a pixel size of 1.3 Å, and blob- and template-picked particles were then merged into a set of 1,574,628 unique particles. Three 3D classes were generated ab initio, and a single 3D class containing 555,081 particles was re-extracted to a pixel size of 0.65 Å and subjected to local and global CTF refinement, yielding a 2.86 Å map. Following reference-based motion correction and 3D refinement, the map was improved to a final resolution of 2.72 Å.

The HU-38/PRAME425 pMHC complex model was built in Coot, and refinement was carried out against a sharpened map in phenix.real_space_refine.55,56 The anti-Kappa VHH sequence was assigned based on PDB entry 7O31.57 Validation of protein geometry was carried out with MolProbity58 Structure figures were generated in PyMol (The PyMOL Molecular Graphics System, Version 2.5.7 Schrödinger, LLC). The structure of the HU-38/PRAME425 pMHC complex has been deposited in the PDB under accession code 9PKV.
