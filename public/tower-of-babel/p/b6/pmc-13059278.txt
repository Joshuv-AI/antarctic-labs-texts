Effectiveness of virtual nurse interventions in chronic disease self-management: a systematic review of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Tsuei-Wun Chang, Hsin-Yi Chen, Shiouh-Chu Shieh, Yu-Chi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic diseases require ongoing self-management, but patients often struggle with knowledge, behavioral adherence, and emotional well-being. Virtual nurse interventions, which refers to digital systems simulating nurse–patient interactions, offer a potential strategy to support self-management. This review aimed to evaluate their effectiveness in improving health knowledge, behavioral outcomes, psychological well-being, and patient satisfaction.MethodsA systematic search of the Cochrane Library, PubMed, CINAHL, MEDLINE, and EMBASE was conducted for randomized controlled trials published between 2014 and April 2025. Studies involving adults with chronic diseases and virtual nurse interventions reporting outcomes on knowledge, behavior, psychological well-being, or patient satisfaction were included. Methodological quality was appraised using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist. Data were synthesized narratively using the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) framework due to substantial heterogeneity in intervention modalities and outcome measures.ResultsSix trials with 533 participants were included. Virtual nurse interventions consistently improved disease-specific knowledge and psychological well-being, and were associated with high patient satisfaction. Evidence for behavioral adherence and clinical outcomes was limited and often not statistically significant.ConclusionsVirtual nurse interventions show potential to enhance disease-related knowledge and psychological support among adults with chronic diseases. However, given the small evidence base, their long-term effects on sustained behavioral change and clinical outcomes remain inconclusive. Future research should prioritize rigorous, long-term trials and consider cultural adaptations and digital health literacy to optimize real-world implementation.Clinical trial numberNot applicable. Introduction

Chronic diseases remain the leading cause of mortality and disability worldwide, imposing substantial burdens on patients, families, and healthcare systems [5, 20]. The aging global population has increased the prevalence of conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, and chronic kidney disease, all of which require continuous self-management and long-term support [20, 21]. This rising health burden highlights the need for scalable, effective, and sustainable care models applicable across diverse healthcare systems [6, 34].

Patient education is fundamental to chronic disease management, providing and empowering patients with the knowledge and skills necessary for self-care, adherence to treatment regimens, and early detection of potential complications. However, education alone does not constitute self-management; rather, it serves as a foundational mechanism that supports the broader process of ongoing self-management, which involves sustained behavioral, cognitive, and emotional regulation to maintain health outcomes and quality of life [12, 21]. Nurses play a central role in delivering this education; however, traditional methods—such as face-to-face sessions and printed materials—are often constrained by limited time, workforce shortages, and lack of individualized tailoring [1, 16]. These challenges are particularly pronounced for patients requiring continuous, personalized support in home-based care environments.

Digital health innovations, particularly AI-driven technologies, have emerged as promising adjuncts to traditional nursing care [3, 36]. Virtual nurses—AI-based agents simulating nurse–patient interactions—provide personalized education, medication reminders, symptom monitoring, and emotional support through mobile apps, web platforms, and virtual reality [17, 26]. Unlike generic chatbots, virtual nurses are designed with anthropomorphic features such as facial expressions, vocal intonation, emotional recognition, and adaptive feedback to foster more engaging and empathetic interactions [2, 21]. Evidence suggests these interventions can enhance patient motivation, health literacy, and behavior change, particularly in home-based or remote care settings [6, 10, 13, 38].

However, the implementation of virtual nursing involves substantial challenges, including barriers for patients with limited digital literacy, especially older adults or socioeconomically disadvantaged groups [4, 11], and ethical considerations such as data privacy, algorithmic bias, informed consent, and transparency [3, 18]. These issues underscore the importance of inclusive design and rigorous evaluation to ensure safety, efficacy, and equity in clinical practice.

Although previous systematic reviews have examined chatbots and AI applications in healthcare, few have specifically evaluated the clinical effectiveness of anthropomorphic virtual nurses in chronic disease self-management. Moreover, the impact of design factors—such as interaction frequency, delivery modality, and personalization—remains underexplored. Therefore, this systematic review aims to evaluate the effectiveness of virtual nurse interventions in supporting chronic disease self-management, focusing on health knowledge, behavioral outcomes, psychological well-being, and patient satisfaction, factoring in design characteristics, user experience, and implementation considerations.

Methods

Study protocol

Due to the heterogeneity of intervention designs and outcome measures (Table S1), this systematic review adhered to the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) [9]. Reporting followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [30]. The review protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO).

Design

The review was structured around the following PICO framework: Among adults with chronic diseases (Population), do virtual nurse interventions (Intervention), compared to usual care or alternative educational interventions (Comparison), improve outcomes (Outcome), specifically disease-related knowledge, self-management behaviors, psychological well-being, physiological indicators, patient satisfaction, and acceptability?

The study population included adults aged 18 years or older diagnosed with chronic conditions such as diabetes, heart failure, or atrial fibrillation. The interventions encompassed virtual nursing systems featuring avatars, relational agents, or AI-based digital characters exhibiting human-like attributes (e.g., facial expressions, voice modulation, and emotional responsiveness). The comparison group consisted of usual care or other educational interventions delivered by healthcare professionals or digital platforms. The primary outcome was improvement in disease-related knowledge and awareness. Secondary outcomes included self-management behaviors, physiological indicators, psychological well-being, patient satisfaction, and acceptability.

Eligible studies for inclusion were randomized controlled trials assessing virtual nursing systems designed to facilitate patient education, coaching, or chronic disease self-management. The synthesis compared intervention delivery formats, outcome domains, and patient-reported measures to ascertain overall effectiveness.

Search method

A comprehensive literature search was conducted using five electronic databases: the Cochrane Library, PubMed, CINAHL, MEDLINE, and EMBASE. The search covered studies published between January 2014 and April 2025 and was limited to peer-reviewed articles published in English and Chinese.

Keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) were derived from the PICO framework and included: “virtual nurse,” “avatar,” “relational agent,” “embodied conversational agent,” “digital health,” “self-management,” “chronic disease,” and “patient education.” Boolean operators (“AND” and “OR”) were used to optimize both the sensitivity and specificity of the search strategy. The detailed search strategies for each database are provided in Table S2.

All studies identified for inclusion were cross-referenced with those included in a larger systematic review by the authors (unpublished) of all risk assessment scales. Additionally, the reference lists of systematic reviews found in the search were screened to identify potential articles for inclusion.

Inclusion and/or exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were: (1) RCTs; (2) adult participants (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with chronic diseases; (3) interventions involving virtual nurse or avatar-based systems; and (4) reporting of relevant outcomes related to patient education or self-management.

Reviews, protocols, conference abstracts, clinical guidelines, and studies without outcome data were excluded. The reference lists of all the included studies were manually screened for additional relevant articles. When full-text articles were not accessible, the corresponding authors were contacted.

EndNote reference software was used for article screening and data extraction. Duplicates were removed and two reviewers independently assessed the study titles and abstracts to identify potentially relevant studies meeting the inclusion criteria. Studies that failed to satisfy the inclusion criteria were excluded and the reasons were recorded. Following the title and abstract screening, full-text screening was performed independently by two reviewers to assess eligibility. Any disagreements were discussed and resolved by a third reviewer, as necessary.

Search outcome

A total of 54 unique citations were initially identified through database searches. Following screening, eight articles were selected for full-text eligibility assessment. Upon detailed review, two articles were excluded: one because it was not a RCT, and the other for lacking relevant outcome data. Thus, six studies were included in the final analysis. Quality appraisal

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist for Randomized Controlled Trials (2020). The checklist consists of 13 items, each scored as “Yes” (1 point), “No,” “Unclear,” or “Not applicable” (0 points), with a maximum score of 13. Studies with scores > 7 were considered methodologically adequate [27].

Two independent reviewers performed the evaluations. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion and consultation with a third reviewer when consensus could not be reached.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data were independently extracted and summarized using a standardized template. The extracted information included author(s), publication year, country, target population, disease type, sample size, description of the virtual nurse intervention, delivery platform, and measured outcomes. Two reviewers independently conducted the data extraction. Disagreements regarding the extracted data were resolved through discussion between the reviewers, and, where needed, a third reviewer was consulted to resolve discrepancies.

Due to the heterogeneity in intervention designs, durations, and assessment tools, a meta-analysis was not deemed appropriate. Synthesis without meta-analysis was conducted to categorize the interventions and summarize the outcomes across five domains: health knowledge, behavioral changes, physiological indicators, psychological well-being, and patient satisfaction.

Results

Characteristics of included studies

The review included six RCTs. A summary of these studies is presented in Table 1, detailing disease focus, delivery platforms, and outcome domains. All six studies were conducted between 2014 and 2022, primarily in the United States or Australia [17, 19, 24, 31, 35, 36]. A total of 533 participants were enrolled across the studies, all of whom were adults (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with chronic conditions that included type 2 diabetes mellitus, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and acute coronary syndrome.

Virtual nurse interventions varied in design and platform, encompassing embodied avatars, relational agents, and AI-driven digital features. These interventions were delivered in a range of formats, including tablet-based applications (e.g., Nurse Cora [19] and Tanya [24]), mobile health applications (e.g., My Diabetes Coach [17]), virtual reality platforms (e.g., Second Life [31] ]), and web-based systems [35, 36]. Most studies reported positive effects in at least one domain, particularly in improvements in health knowledge [35, 36], quality of life [17, 24], and patient satisfaction [19, 36]. All studies clearly described their randomization procedures and participant characteristics, with most demonstrating baseline comparability, consistent intervention delivery, the use of reliable outcome measures, and appropriate statistical analyses.

However, important sources of bias were identified across all studies. Allocation concealment and the blinding of participants and outcome assessors were either not reported or not feasible due to the interactive and visually distinguishable nature of virtual nurse interventions. While randomization procedures were generally described, the absence of blinding raises concerns regarding performance and detection bias, particularly for outcomes relying on patient self-reporting. These methodological limitations were therefore considered in the interpretation of outcome-specific findings rather than treated as minor reporting issues.

Technology formats and functions of virtual nurses

All included studies assessed the participants’ digital literacy or device access prior to enrollment to ensure the feasibility of using virtual nurse technologies. Overall, the interventions were well received and demonstrated positive effects on health knowledge, self-management behaviors, and selected physiological and psychological outcomes (Table 3). The technological formats and functional characteristics of virtual nursing systems vary along two primary dimensions discussed below.

Interaction modes and interface types

Virtual nurse interventions are delivered via mobile apps, tablets, websites, or virtual reality environments. Most systems employ human-like avatars that interact through text-, voice-, or video-based communication to simulate natural nurse–patient conversations. For example, Rosal et al. [31] implemented a virtual-world group education model using Second Life; Gong et al. [17] used a voice-based agent named Laura to deliver diabetes education; Magnani et al. [24] employed a computer-animated relational agent named Tanya to provide atrial fibrillation education; and Wonggom et al. [36] developed a tablet-based avatar application to educate patients with heart failure (Table 1).

Educational content, duration, and delivery flexibility

The educational content of the interventions was aligned with evidence-based clinical guidelines and focused on core self-management areas, including symptom monitoring, medication adherence, dietary and lifestyle modifications, and emotional well-being [17, 19, 24, 31, 35, 36]. Several multimedia elements, such as videos, infographics, and interactive quizzes were incorporated [24, 35, 36]. The study duration ranged from 30 days to 12 months, with interaction frequencies varying from daily to weekly [17, 19, 24, 31]. Several systems have enabled self-paced learning and provided adaptive feedback based on user input, thereby enhancing personalization and flexibility [17, 35, 36].

Outcome indicators

All included studies primarily verified participants’ device access or prior exposure to app use (e.g., smartphone/tablet ownership). No study employed validated digital literacy instruments or assessed higher-order competencies, such as navigating AI-driven interfaces, interpreting automated feedback, troubleshooting technical issues, or critically evaluating AI-generated information. Outcomes were synthesized narratively across five key domains: health knowledge, self-management behaviors, physiological indicators, psychological wellbeing, and user satisfaction. A cross-study comparison of the outcomes is summarized in Table 3.

Health knowledge and disease awareness

All included studies reported significant improvements in disease-specific knowledge. Tongpeth et al. [35] reported marked increases in symptom recognition among patients with acute coronary syndrome, rising from 58.35% to 83.55% (p < .001), along with gains in symptom knowledge (p = .001), attitudes (p = .009), and beliefs (p = .001) (study-specific, non-validated instruments). Acute Coronary Syndrome Response Index scores remained elevated at both 1 and 6 months (p < .01), with 85% of the participants expressing greater confidence in recognizing and responding to symptoms (single-item self-report, non-validated). Wonggom et al. [36] demonstrated improved heart failure knowledge, with a 22.2% increase in the intervention group compared to 3.7% in the control group (p = .002, validated Self-Care of Heart Failure Index). Gong et al. [17] did not report direct knowledge scores but showed high user engagement, with 1,942 chatbot sessions, (engagement data, non-validated), suggesting effective educational delivery. (See Table 1 for an outcome summary of the studies).

Self-management and behavioral change

Positive behavioral outcomes were observed in all six studies [17, 19, 24, 31, 35, 36]. Rosal et al. [31] reported an 18.4% increase in physical activity in the virtual group compared to a 22.5% decrease in the face-to-face group (p = .10, self-reported, non-validated), while diabetes self-efficacy improved in both groups (p < .001, validated), with no significant between-group difference (p = .268).

Guhl et al. [19] identified higher self-reported anticoagulant adherence at 30 days in the intervention group vs. control, with an adjusted difference of 16.6% (p < .001, single-item, non-validated). Magnani et al. [24] found MMAS-8 adherence scores increased from 7.3 to 7.7 (p = .01, validated), while PAM scores remained unchanged (p = .33, validated). Wonggom et al. [36] reported improved self-care in the intervention group at 30 and 90 days (Self-Care of HF Index, partially validated) with no significant differences in hospital readmissions. (See Table 3 for a comparative summary of behavioral changes.)

Physiological outcomes

Modest reductions in HbA1c were observed in patients with diabetes [17, 31]. Rosal et al. [31] reported a 3.2% reduction in the virtual group (p = .186) and a 4.9% reduction in the face-to-face group (p = .019), with no significant between-group difference (p = .52, validated laboratory measures). Gong et al. [17] reported a 0.33% reduction in the intervention group vs. 0.20% in the control group (p = .83, validated laboratory measures).

Regarding blood pressure, Rosal et al. [31] found no significant changes in systolic or diastolic BP in either group (validated clinical measures, all p > .23). These objective outcomes are less susceptible to bias than patient-reported measures, supporting relatively higher confidence in physiological results. (See Table 3 for the glycemic and blood pressure outcomes.)

Psychological wellbeing and emotional support

Virtual nursing systems provided psychological benefits. Gong et al. [17] reported reductions in HADS anxiety scores (− 0.89, p = .04, validated) and improvements in HRQoL utility (Δ = 0.04, p = .04, EQ-5D, validated). Rosal et al. [31] observed depressive symptom reduction in both groups, with marginally greater improvement in the face-to-face group (p = .051, CES-D, validated). Guhl et al. [19] reported increased emotional support (self-report, non-validated) while maintaining QoL scores (AFEQT, validated). Magnani et al. [24] found AFEQT scores improved from 64.5 to 76.3 (p < .01, validated).

These results highlight that virtual nursing interventions may enhance both psychological support and QoL, though measures and instruments vary in validation status [17, 19, 24, 31]. (See Table 3 for depression and quality of life outcomes.)

Patient satisfaction and acceptability

User satisfaction was consistently high across studies [19, 24, 35, 36]. Guhl et al. [19] reported median satisfaction of 6/7 and ease of use of 1/7 (survey, non-validated), noting some content repetition issues. Wonggom et al. [36] found 91.3% of participants satisfied with the app (non-validated survey). Magnani et al. [24] highlighted positive perceptions of daily monitoring, and Tongpeth et al. [35] reported complete satisfaction with an avatar app (single-item survey, non-validated).

Technical usability issues were reported in several studies, including interface complexity and limited interactivity, underscoring the need for user-centered design improvements in future virtual nurse systems. (See Table 3 for detailed satisfaction outcomes.)

Discussion

This systematic review explored the potential role of anthropomorphic virtual nurse interventions in supporting chronic disease self-management, with a focus on health knowledge, self-care behaviors, psychological well-being, and user satisfaction. Overall, the available evidence suggests that such interventions may be feasible and acceptable, and may support patient education and engagement in selected populations and settings. Across the included studies, improvements in disease-specific knowledge and patient satisfaction were frequently reported, indicating potential educational value. However, changes in clinical outcomes—such as glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels and blood pressure—were generally modest and often lacked statistical significance, suggesting that meaningful physiological improvements may require longer-term, more intensive, or more integrated interventions.

Given the limited number of included trials and substantial heterogeneity in intervention designs, disease populations, and outcome measures, these findings of this review should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. With only six randomized controlled trials available, this review does not establish clinical efficacy. Instead, it identifies emerging patterns and preliminary signals of potential effectiveness across heterogeneous contexts, primarily at the levels of knowledge, engagement, and psychosocial outcomes, warranting further rigorous investigation.

Educational functions and delivery models

Virtual nursing systems aim to simulate elements of nurse–patient interactions by delivering personalized education, behavioral prompts, and emotional support. Most systems in this review employed humanlike avatars with voice-, text-, or video-based interactions, enabling real-time or asynchronous communication. Wonggom et al. [36] and Tongpeth et al. [35] reported statistically significant improvements in disease-specific knowledge and symptom recognition, highlighting the value of interactive and multimodal learning environments.

These findings are consistent with prior evidence showing that repetitive, self-paced digital education can enhance health literacy and knowledge retention [10]. Relational agents that incorporate narrative dialogue or simulated scenarios may further increase emotional relevance and engagement, thereby supporting deeper understanding [12, 13, 16]. Across the included studies, the format and frequency of virtual nurse interactions varied substantially, ranging from daily app-based monitoring [17] to weekly group-based virtual education sessions [31]. As noted in prior eHealth research, including chronic kidney disease monitoring, optimal intervention intensity must balance user engagement with burden to minimize attrition [15].

Design challenges and considerations

Although virtual nursing systems demonstrate strong educational potential, their design and implementation present several challenges. A major concern is the lack of standardization across interventions, particularly regarding content scope, interaction frequency, and feedback mechanisms, which limits comparability and generalizability across studies. While some interventions employed high-fidelity relational agents and sophisticated interfaces, many lacked adaptive features that tailor content to patients’ needs, responses, or levels of digital health literacy. Designing effective virtual nurse systems therefore requires balancing technological sophistication with accessibility. Early involvement of target users through co-design processes has been shown to enhance clinical relevance, usability, and sustainability by ensuring intuitive interfaces and minimizing user burden [18]. Such participatory approaches are particularly important given the diversity of patient populations targeted by virtual nurse interventions.

Digital health literacy represents another critical but under-addressed design consideration. In line with contemporary frameworks, digital health literacy should be viewed not merely as a binary access issue, but as a dynamic, multidimensional competency involving functional, interactive, and critical skills to engage with AI-driven feedback [28, 32]. Most included studies did not assess participants’ digital competence across these dimensions, limiting understanding of how user readiness may have influenced engagement and outcomes. Prior research indicates that older adults and socioeconomically disadvantaged populations are more likely to experience difficulties using digital health tools due to limited access, skills, or confidence [4, 14]. To mitigate these barriers, virtual nurse systems should incorporate adaptive designs catering to varying literacy levels, such as low-burden interfaces, multilingual support, onboarding assistance, and, where feasible, structured digital health literacy training alongside intervention delivery [8, 11, 37]. Addressing these user-level factors is essential to prevent the unintended widening of digital health disparities.

In addition, alignment with clinical practice guidelines is a key design requirement. Evidence suggests that AI-generated health education content may deviate from established clinical standards if not carefully supervised [1]. Active involvement of nurses and clinical experts in content development, validation, and quality assurance is therefore essential to ensure accuracy, safety, and credibility, and to maintain clinical integrity alongside educational effectiveness.

While this review demonstrates the potential effectiveness of virtual nurse interventions on selected clinical and behavioral outcomes, effectiveness observed under controlled trial conditions does not necessarily predict successful real-world implementation. Notably, none of the included studies systematically reported implementation outcomes, such as intervention reach, adoption by eligible patients or clinicians, reasons for non-enrollment, dropout patterns, technical difficulties, or experiences integrating virtual interventions into routine workflows. From an implementation science perspective, critical questions remain unanswered. Using the RE-AIM framework, evidence is lacking regarding intervention reach, adoption, fidelity, and maintenance. Similarly, constructs emphasized in the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR)—such as organizational readiness, compatibility with existing workflows, and perceived intervention complexity—were not systematically examined. Consistent with the findings of Shankar et al. [32], our review confirms that organizational readiness and workflow integration remain significant gaps in the digital health transition for chronic care. These gaps highlight the need for future research to integrate effectiveness and implementation outcomes, explicitly consider digital literacy and user competency, and guide the translation of virtual nurse interventions into sustainable, real-world chronic care delivery.

Cultural and geographic considerations in virtual nurse interventions

Most included studies were conducted in high-income countries, primarily the United States and Australia, highlighting the need to consider cultural and contextual influences when interpreting generalizability. Factors such as cultural norms, healthcare system organization, family caregiving structures, and digital health literacy patterns may shape how virtual nurse interventions are perceived, adopted, and translated into health outcomes.

The current evidence base reflects English-speaking Western healthcare systems, which emphasize individual autonomy, self-directed disease management, and direct patient–provider communication. These assumptions may not hold across diverse cultural contexts and may partly explain variability in intervention effectiveness. For example, Rosal et al. [31] found that a virtual intervention for Non-Hispanic Black women with type 2 diabetes yielded less favorable outcomes compared with face-to-face education, suggesting that cultural expectations regarding interpersonal interaction, relational trust, and the perceived legitimacy of digital care may moderate intervention effects.

Asian healthcare contexts, in particular, present distinct challenges and opportunities. Many Asian societies are characterized by collectivist values, strong family involvement in health decision-making, and family-centered caregiving models [23]. Virtual nurse systems designed primarily for individual self-management may need adaptation to support family participation or shared engagement mechanisms. Intergenerational differences in digital health literacy are also salient; widespread smartphone access does not necessarily equate to digital competence or confidence [29], and older adults may require additional guidance to navigate AI-driven interfaces effectively [32].

Attitudes toward AI in healthcare vary across cultural settings. In some contexts, AI-enabled care may be seen as innovative and supportive, whereas in others it may be perceived as impersonal or undermining valued human relationships in cultures that prioritize “high-touch” care [22]. Hybrid care models positioning virtual nurses as complements rather than substitutes for human nurses may be particularly relevant in these contexts to maintain the humanistic core of nursing while leveraging technological efficiency [7].

Future research should move beyond general calls for studies in low- and middle-income countries and delve deeper to examine how cultural values, family structures, digital literacy, and healthcare system characteristics interact with virtual nurse design and implementation. Culturally and digitally responsive adaptations are essential to ensure interventions are equitable, acceptable, and clinically meaningful across diverse global settings.

Effectiveness variability and future research

To aid interpretation of the observed variability in effectiveness, this review post hoc conceptually categorized the included interventions by interaction modality and delivery format (as mapped in Table 2). These groupings—ranging from group-based virtual environments to voice-based agents—represent distinct models of patient engagement that may help contextualize observed outcome patterns.

This review found consistent improvements in disease-specific knowledge, psychological well-being, and user satisfaction, but effects on behavioral and physiological outcomes—such as HbA1c and blood pressure—were less conclusive. For example, Gong et al. [17] and Rosal et al. [31] reported HbA1c reductions that did not reach clinically relevant thresholds. These findings align with broader eHealth literature, which suggests that knowledge acquisition alone rarely leads to sustained behavioral or physiological change without motivational, social, and environmental reinforcement [2, 25, 26].

Heterogeneity across interventions likely contributes to outcome variability. Although all studies involved anthropomorphic virtual nurses, interaction and educational models differed. Group-based avatar education in immersive environments [31] incorporated synchronous interaction and peer support, whereas voice-based conversational agents [17] provided brief, individualized exchanges focused on self-monitoring and reminders. Differences in interaction intensity, social context, and relational continuity may partly explain why group-based interventions showed modest HbA1c improvements while brief task-oriented interventions did not.

Similarly, animated relational agents with frequent interactions [19, 24] emphasized emotional engagement and continuity, which may account for consistent effects on psychological well-being and user satisfaction. In contrast, tablet-based avatar applications [35, 36] focused on structured educational content, leading to stronger effects on knowledge acquisition but limited physiological impact. These observations highlight the need to systematically examine which design features—interaction modality, frequency, personalization, and social support—most strongly influence specific outcomes.

Digital literacy may further moderate intervention effectiveness. Variability in behavioral and psychological outcomes may partly reflect differences in participants’ prior technology experience and higher-order digital competencies. Future research should assess digital health literacy using validated instruments, distinguishing between basic access and advanced skills, to guide tailored intervention design and equitable implementation.

Measurement heterogeneity represents an additional, cross-cutting challenge. Outcome measures varied widely across studies, particularly for behavioral adherence and health knowledge, limiting meaningful cross-study comparison. This concern aligns with recent methodological work on patient-reported outcome measurement, which emphasizes that without rigorous evaluation of measurement properties and harmonization of outcome instruments, evidence synthesis across heterogeneous digital interventions remains fundamentally constrained [33]. Together, these considerations underscore the need for consensus on validated core outcome sets to advance the evaluation of virtual nurse interventions.

To advance the field, future studies should prioritize long-term RCTs with extended follow-ups, comparative evaluations of interaction modalities, and hybrid models combining human and virtual support. Incorporating culturally tailored content, adaptive feedback, and emotionally responsive interfaces may reduce disparities in engagement and outcomes. Standardization using validated outcome measures and core outcome sets will enable cross-study comparisons and strengthen conclusions regarding effectiveness.

Finally, evaluating cost-effectiveness and scalability is essential to determine broader impact. Well-designed virtual nurse systems may reduce hospital readmissions, improve self-care adherence, and alleviate healthcare personnel burden [6, 26]. Future research should explore strategies for embedding virtual nurses within health system reforms and digital health policies to support sustainable, person-centered chronic care delivery.

Implications for nursing practice, research, and policy

The findings of this review suggest that anthropomorphic virtual nurse interventions can complement, rather than replace, traditional patient education when implemented under appropriate conditions [17, 19]. These systems offer scalable educational support beyond clinical settings; however, safe and effective use requires careful design, validation, and integration rather than rapid deployment [1].

Active nurse involvement in co-design, evaluation, and implementation is essential to ensure clinical relevance, patient safety, and alignment with nursing values [18]. Future-oriented nursing education and training programs may be needed to prepare the workforce to critically appraise and appropriately integrate AI-driven tools into routine care [3].

Given the limited number of randomized controlled trials, the current evidence remains exploratory. Future research should focus on long-term outcomes, cost-effectiveness, and real-world implementation while considering cultural context and digital literacy [4, 31]. From a policy perspective, supporting pilot programs and implementation studies—rather than immediate large-scale rollout—allows health systems to assess feasibility, safety, and equity, while addressing digital divides through inclusive design and workforce development [8, 11].

Equity considerations are critical in the future development and evaluation of virtual nurse interventions. Implementation research is needed to examine how these interventions can be effectively integrated into existing chronic care frameworks and clinical workflows, while accounting for patients’ digital health literacy, cultural contexts, and individual preferences [32]. As most existing trials have been conducted in high-income countries, further research in low- and middle-income settings is warranted, where scalable digital solutions may offer substantial benefit but where digital access and capacity constraints are more pronounced [33].

Addressing the digital divide through inclusive design, infrastructure investment, and workforce training will be essential to prevent the exacerbation of existing health inequities [8, 11]. In this context, nurses, as trusted healthcare professionals, should play a central role in shaping these research and policy agendas to ensure that AI-enabled innovations remain evidence-based, ethical, and patient-centered.

Study limitations

This review has several limitations. First, the small number of included studies (n = 6) limits the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Second, substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity—across target populations, virtual nurse designs, intervention intensity, and outcome measures—precluded meta-analysis and limited direct cross-study comparisons. Third, methodological weaknesses in several trials, including lack of participant and assessor blinding and insufficient reporting of allocation concealment, may have increased the risk of bias, particularly for self-reported outcomes. Fourth, publication bias cannot be ruled out, as studies reporting null or unfavorable effects may be underrepresented.

Additionally, most studies were conducted in high-income countries, predominantly the United States and Australia, raising concerns regarding the transferability of findings to other geographic, cultural, and healthcare contexts. Factors such as healthcare infrastructure, cultural norms, family involvement in care, and levels of digital health literacy may substantially influence both implementation feasibility and intervention effectiveness. Future research in low- and middle-income countries is needed to clarify how these contextual factors shape outcomes and to support more equitable global translation of virtual nurse interventions.

Conclusion

This systematic review synthesized current randomized evidence on anthropomorphic virtual nurse interventions for chronic disease self-management. The findings identify preliminary signals that such interventions may support disease-related knowledge acquisition, psychological well-being, and high levels of user satisfaction among adults with chronic diseases. However, the small number of available trials and substantial heterogeneity in intervention designs, populations, and outcome measures limit the ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding clinical efficacy or long-term clinical benefit.

At the current stage of evidence, virtual nurse interventions should be regarded as an emerging and exploratory approach rather than a practice-ready solution. Future research should prioritize rigorously designed, adequately powered randomized trials with longer follow-up periods, standardized outcome measures, and explicit assessment of behavioral and physiological endpoints. Evaluation of cost-effectiveness, scalability, and the role of digital health literacy is also essential. With careful design, appropriate clinical oversight, and attention to equity and digital inclusion, virtual nurse technologies hold promises as complementary tools within chronic disease self-management research.
