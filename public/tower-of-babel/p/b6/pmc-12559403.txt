Modulation of the blood–brain barrier permeability by patient-derived glioblastoma stem cell secretome
Authors: Francesco Saverio Sica, Michela Patrucco, Martina Giambra, Martina Ghizzi, Silvia Sesana, Barbara Vergani, Biagio Eugenio Leone, Andrea Di Cristofori, Carlo Giorgio Giussani, Gabriella Nicolini, Angela Bentivegna, Francesca Re
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The blood–brain barrier (BBB) is a highly selective barrier that strictly controls the passage of substances and cells into the brain, protecting it from potential harm while preserving homeostasis. It is composed of specialised endothelial cells (ECs), along with surrounding cells, such as pericytes and astrocytes. In glioblastoma (GBM), the most prevalent primary malignant brain tumour in adults, the BBB is heterogeneously dysfunctional. In the tumour microenvironment, regions enriched with glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs) are protected by an intact BBB. However, the influence of GSCs on BBB function remains largely unexplored. In this study, the impact of patient-derived GSC (PD GSC) secretomes on human brain capillary ECs has been investigated in vitro. Results showed that secretomes decrease the BBB permeability, leading to an increase of transendothelial electrical resistance and of tight junction protein claudin-5 (CLDN5) levels. Moreover, the receptor for advanced glycation endproducts (RAGE), which is involved in cancer and chemotherapy resistance, modulates CLDN5 expression by activating the pERK/ERK signaling pathway and influences junctional organization. These findings suggest a functional pathway through which PD GSC secretomes can modulate BBB permeability, potentially impacting therapeutic efficacy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21272-3.

Introduction

The blood‒brain barrier (BBB) is a complex and highly regulated structure that is essential for proper brain function. The BBB tightly regulates the passage of substances between the bloodstream and brain tissue. The brain is composed of specialised endothelial cells (ECs) that line capillaries within the brain, as well as surrounding cells such as pericytes and astrocytes1. These components work together to create a selective barrier that protects the brain from potentially harmful substances, such as toxins, pathogens, and large molecules, while allowing essential nutrients and molecules to enter. Only small (< 500 Da and < 400 nm), lipid-soluble molecules such as oxygen, carbon dioxide, and some hormones can diffuse freely across the BBB, whereas larger molecules and charged ions require specific transport mechanisms to pass through, thus limiting therapeutic options2. As a result, the BBB helps to maintain the delicate internal environment required for proper neuronal function. It is also involved in neurovascular coupling, which refers to the coordination between neuronal activity and blood flow regulation3.

BBB dysfunctions have been implicated in various neurological disorders, highlighting their importance in maintaining brain health and integrity4. In the case of glioblastoma (GBM), the most prevalent primary malignant brain tumour in adults5, the BBB is heterogeneously dysfunctional6,7, and at the tumour region, its integrity is altered, forming the so-called blood–tumour barrier (BTB)4. These modifications are mainly ascribed to the tumour cell secretome and soluble and/or insoluble factors released or secreted by cancer cells4. The BTB exhibits unique characteristics due to the presence of tumour cells and associated vasculature alterations. In particular, the BTB exhibits diminished tight junction (TJ) expression, greater permeability, irregular pericyte coverage, an increased presence of activated astrocytes accompanied by restriction of astrocyte endfeet, and degradation of the basal membrane. Notably, these alterations depend on the stage of the disease and on the histological/molecular subtypes of GBM; moreover, the BTB is not distributed uniformly, but some tumour regions are protected by a healthy BBB, as demonstrated by Gadolinium accumulation-based magnetic resonance imaging in GBM patients8,9. This phenomenon is particularly important for tumour niches, anatomically distinct regions within the tumour microenvironment where there is an interplay between glioblastoma stem cells (GSCs) and ECs10. GSCs constitute a rare subset of cancer stem cells within GBM with high expansion capacity, the ability to generate new tumours, the ability to sustain self-renewal and significant resistance to chemo- and radiotherapy11. Although BBB integrity is lost at the tumour core, capillaries supplying nutrients to the tumour niches and adjacent to normal tissue are intact12,13. These features can hinder the delivery of therapeutic agents to brain tumours, limiting the effectiveness of chemotherapy and targeted therapies13,14. Strategies to overcome the BTB and enhance drug delivery to brain tumours include the use of specialised drug delivery systems, such as nanoparticles or liposomes, which can bypass or penetrate the barrier more effectively4,15. Therefore, considering that the five-year survival rate for relapsed GBM patients is only 6.9% 16, understanding these changes in the BBB is crucial for improving treatment outcomes or for designing new approaches for GBM patients and for increasing the body of knowledge on GBM.

In this context, GBM-induced molecular, structural and functional changes in ECs lining the BBB have been reported. These include alterations in TJs and multiprotein junctional complexes involved in sealing the paracellular pathway. Among them, the most well-known alterations are (i) elevated occludin phosphotyrosine levels17,18 due to the VEGF-dependent activation of the protein kinase C signaling pathway; (ii) degradation of claudin-5 (CLDN5), zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and occludin by matrix metalloproteinases; and (iii) reduced expression of ZO-1, CLDN5 and vascular endothelial cadherins. These changes lead to a reduction in transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER) and an increase in BBB permeability19.

To date, the effect of GSCs on BBB properties has been poorly studied. Here, we investigated the effect of the secretome of three patient-derived GSCs (PD GSCs) on human brain capillary ECs in vitro. The results showed that the PD GSC secretome decreased BBB permeability via the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE)-dependent signaling pathway. RAGE, a multiligand pattern recognition receptor, is implicated in cancer20 and has recently attracted increased interest in GBM21. Increased levels of RAGE in cancer cells have been associated with cell proliferation, angiogenesis, inflammation and resistance to chemotherapy22. Herein, by using human cerebral microvascular endothelial (hCMEC/D3) cells transfected to overexpress RAGE, we demonstrated the occurrence of crosstalk between RAGE and CLDN5, suggesting the potential to discover new therapeutic targets and strategies to prevent drug resistance.

Materials and methods

Donors and human glioblastoma stem cells

Human GSCs were isolated from tumour biopsies of three GBM patients diagnosed following the 2021 WHO Classification of CNS tumours recommendations23. Patients were enrolled at the Neurosurgery Unit of the Fondazione IRCCS San Gerardo dei Tintori (Monza Brianza, Italy) and after informed consent was signed, samples were collected during craniotomy. This study was approved by the ethics committee “Comitato Etico Monza e Brianza” of the Fondazione IRCCS San Gerardo dei Tintori (Approval No: 0031436—GLIODRUG-V, approved on 3 January 2020), and performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and legal regulations on human body material. The protocol for patient-derived cell culture isolation and characterization in terms of stemness and genomic profiling were described by Giambra et al.24. Briefly, using the Oncomine™ Precision Assay (OPA) hotspot regions affected by somatic variants associated with neoplasms in 45 clinically and diagnostically relevant genes were analysed by the Ion Torrent™ Genexus™ System Platform (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The results converge for the classical subtype for all three patient-derived GSCs, although gene expression analyses were not performed. In addition to the absence of IDH mutations, the three patient-derived GSCs showed high level of EGFR amplification and chromosome 7 amplification, two peculiar characteristics of the classic subtype.

PD GSCs were cultured in selective medium composed of DMEM F-12, Neurobasal 1:1, B-27 supplement without vitamin A (Life Technologies Italia, Milan, Italy), 2 mM L-glutamine (Euroclone S.p.A., Milan, Italy), 10 ng/mL recombinant human bFGF, 20 ng/mL recombinant human EGF (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergish Gladbach, Germany), 20 UI/mL penicillin and 20 µg/mL streptomycin (Euroclone S.p.A., Milan, Italy).

Cell lines

Immortalized human cerebral microvascular endothelial cells (hCMEC/D3) were provided by Prof. S. Bourdoulous (Institut Cochin, Inserm, Paris, France) and were used as a model of the brain capillary endothelium. Cells at passages 27–35 were seeded in tissue culture flasks pretreated with rat tail collagen type I (0.05 mg/mL). The cells were grown in complete culture medium (EBM-2 supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum FCS, 1% chemically defined lipid concentrate, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 10 mM HEPES, 5 µg/mL ascorbic acid, 1 ng/mL bFGF, and 1.4 µM hydrocortisone) and maintained at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The culture medium was changed every 2 days25,26.

hCMEC/D3 cells overexpressing RAGE (hCMEC/D3RAGE+) were engineered in the laboratory of Dr. S. Bourdoulous (Institut Cochin, Inserm, Paris, France). To this purpose, the gene encoding human RAGE was cloned and inserted into the lentiviral expression vector pCDH-CMV-MCS-EF1α-GFP-Puro under the control of the CMV promoter. This vector also expresses a puromycin resistance marker and GFP from the human elongation factor alpha (EF1α) promoter. Lentiviral particles were produced in 293T cells, and the viral titer was measured as previously described27. hCMEC/D3 cells were transduced with viral particles at a concentration corresponding to a multiplicity of infection of 5. Positive cells were selected using 5 µg/mL puromycin (Sigma‒Aldrich) (Fig. 1).

In vitro model of the blood‒brain barrier

hCMEC/D3 or hCMEC/D3RAGE+ cells were used to establish the in vitro model of brain EC monolayers. The hCMEC/D3 and hCMEC/D3RAGE+ seeding densities on day 1 were optimized at 50,000 cells/cm2 to obtain monolayers with optimal barrier properties, as determined by the TEER (> 30 Ω cm2) and low endothelial permeability (EP) to fluorescent dextrans (FITC-dextran, 40000 Da, EP 6.15 × 10−6 cm/min; TRITC-dextran, 4400 Da, EP 1.71 × 10−4 cm/min). hCMEC/D3 cells at passages 27–35 were seeded onto collagen-coated (150 µg/mL rat tail collagen type 1; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific) transwell filter inserts (12-well polyester, 0.4 μm pore size, 1.12 cm2 surface area translucent membrane inserts; Greiner BioOne) at a constant seeding density of 50,000 cells/cm2 (Fig. 2A). The upper and bottom compartments of the transwell system were made of complete EBM-2. After 3 days of culture, the media were changed to EBM-2 supplemented with 5% FCS, 1% chemically defined lipid concentrate, 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 10 mM HEPES, 5 µg/mL ascorbic acid, 0.01 M LiCl and 1.4 µM hydrocortisone. BBB models were established between 7 and 10 days after seeding26,28.

TEER was measured using a Volt-Ohm-Meter (World Precision Instruments, Sarasota, FL, USA Millicell ERS-2, STX4 electrode). To calculate the net resistance in Ω cm2 of the cell monolayers, the TEER value of the filters without cells was subtracted from each measurement, and the TEER values in Ω were multiplied by the surface area of the filters as follows: TEER (Ω cm2) = (cell monolayer resistance − empty transwell filter resistance) × surface area (cm2)29.

The permeability of the EC monolayers to TRITC-dextran (4400 Da, Sigma‒Aldrich) was performed as previously described30. Briefly, the culture medium in the bottom compartment was replaced with Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline w/o calcium or w/o 1x magnesium (PBS, Euroclone, D8537) minimizing interference in fluorescence detection. Then, TRITC-Dextran was added to the upper compartment of the transwell inserts at a concentration of 1 mg/mL in PBS. Samples containing fluorescent tracers that had passed across the EC monolayers were collected from the bottom well at different time points for a total of 60 min, and the fluorescence intensity was measured with a spectrofluorometer (FP-8500, Jasco). Clearance (cm/min) was calculated as described previously31.

The formation of tight/adherens junctions was evaluated by transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM).

For TEM analysis, cells were grown on p35 Petri dishes, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and 2% glutaraldehyde, and post fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide. Following dehydration through graded alcohol solutions, the specimens were embedded in Epon 812. The bottom of the Petri dishes was then removed with liquid nitrogen, and blocks of epoxy resin containing cells were reoriented. Ultrathin sections obtained with an Ultracut E Reichert-Jung ultramicrotome were doubly stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate and examined by a Philips transmission electron microscope (FEI, Eindhoven, The Netherlands).

For CLSM analysis, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilised with 3% BSA and 0.1% Triton X-100 in 1× PBS, and tight/adherens junctions were visualised with an inverted point scanner confocal microscope (Airyscan LSM170, Zeiss, 63x immersion oil objective) using the primary antibodies listed below (Table 1). Cell nuclei were visualised with DAPI (1:5000; Sigma–Aldrich). The experiments were performed in triplicate for each condition.

Effect of the PD GSC secretomes on EC monolayers

The PD GSC medium was changed every three days. The conditioned media, containing the secretome of PD GSCs, were collected and stored at −20 °C for further analysis.

At 7–11 days after the seeding of hCMEC/D3 cells onto transwell filter inserts, the bottom samples were replaced with 1 mL of the PD GSC secretome and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h or 48 h. Complete EBM-2 or complete PD GSC media were used as controls. After incubation, the TEER was measured as described above, and the protein levels of junctional and non-junctional proteins were measured by immunoblotting.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism 8.3 using one-way or two-way ANOVA and Student’s t test. p < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance. Data are reported as mean value ± SEM of at least three replicates.

Results and discussion

To examine the effect of the PD GSC secretome on human brain capillary ECs, we utilised hCMEC/D3, a human endothelial cell line derived from cerebral microvessels that retains most of the structural and functional features of human adult brain ECs32. When grown on transwell filters (Fig. 2A), hCMEC/D3 cells exhibited TEER values ranging from 35 Ω cm2 to 50 Ω cm2, starting from day 3 to day 11 after seeding, with the highest values occurring on day 7 after seeding (Fig. 2B). On Day 7, the endothelial permeability (EP) to fluorescent dextran (DX) probes of 4.4 kDa and 40 kDa was 1.7 × 10−4 cm/min and 6.15 × 10−6 cm/min, respectively (Fig. 2C). The transport of markers across the in vitro BBB is likely to occur via the paracellular route (i.e., size-dependent transport through TJs)33. In addition, the EP coefficient ratio between the 40 kDa probe and the 4.4 kDa probe was 27, indicating that the BBB model is effective at identifying molecules with different molecular weights. These results are in agreement with the values reported in the literature32,34,35.

The ultrastructures of the hCMEC/D3 monolayers were analyzed via transmission electron microscopy imaging. The results (Fig. 2D) revealed electron-dense contact elements between adjacent ECs separated by interspaces of 50–200 nm, suggesting the formation of junctional complexes between ECs, as previously reported36.

Then, the hCMEC/D3 cells were exposed to the PD GSC secretomes. The hCMEC/D3 cells were grown on transwell filters for 7–11 days, after which the culture medium in the bottom chamber was replaced with a secretome, after which the cells were incubated for 24 h to 48 h. The results (Fig. 3A) revealed a variation in the TEER of the hCMEC/D3 monolayers after incubation with PD GSC secretomes, which was dependent on the differences in tumour cell states37. Indeed, while the PD GSC23 secretome did not induce any TEER variation, the PD GSC44 and PD GSC7 secretomes caused a significant increase in TEER after 24 h of incubation compared to that of untreated hCMEC/D3 cells (Fig. 3A, inset).

Interestingly, while the effect of the PD GSC7 secretome was transient, hCMEC/D3 cells exposed to the PD GSC44 secretome had the highest TEER, which was maintained over time, after up to 48 h of incubation. These results agree with one of the most significant features of GBM, which is the heterogeneity of the tumour cells. A recent review by Eisenbarth et al. highlighted the extensive diversity of cell states, cell compositions, and phenotypical and molecular characteristics, which also complicate the accurate classification of GBM37.

To verify that the bioelectrical changes measured in hCMEC/D3 cells after incubation with the secretomes of PD GSCs were consistent with the structural properties of the monolayers, the protein levels of CLDN5, the most enriched TJ protein38,39, were assessed by immunoblotting (Fig. 3B). Exposure of hCMEC/D3 cells to the PD GSC44 secretome for 48 h induced a 2-fold increase in CLDN5 protein levels, whereas no significant changes were detected upon incubation with the secretomes derived from the other PD GSCs tested (Fig. 3C). No changes were observed 48 h after incubation with PD GSC7 or PD GSC23 secretome, even though CLDN5 levels were found increased after 24 h of incubation with PD GSC7 secretome (Fig. S1). These results are consistent with the measured changes in TEER (Fig. 3A).

Secretome-dependent modulation of BBB permeability can be attributed to different patterns of soluble factors and/or extracellular vesicles released/secreted by PD GSCs, as shown by SDS–PAGE analysis of secretomes (Fig. S2). This topic deserves more in-depth investigation, particularly because, despite the similarity in protein patterns between PD GSC7 and PD GSC23, the same electrophoretic band may exhibit different levels of protein enrichment.

Moreover, we assessed the protein level of RAGE because it is highly expressed by peripheral and brain ECs40 and can contribute to the regulation of vein endothelial cells during inflammation41.

The incubation of hCMEC/D3 cells with the PD GSC44 secretome for 48 h induced an ~ 2-fold increase in RAGE levels. No significant changes were detected upon incubation with the secretomes derived from the other PD GSCs tested (Fig. 3B, C). Interestingly, no changes in RAGE levels were founded after 24 h incubation with PD GSC7 (Fig. S1), which showed an increase of CLDN5 levels, suggesting that a long-lasting modulation of CLDN5 requires the over-expression of RAGE.

Considering that most investigations concerning the brain endothelium RAGE in Alzheimer’s disease involve β-amyloid- and inflammation-dependent TJ protein disruption42,43, we questioned whether increased levels of RAGE might play a direct role in the regulation of brain EC permeability. To address this point, we engineered hCMEC/D3 cells to overexpress RAGE. hCMEC/D3 cells were stably transduced with lentiviral vectors expressing GFP alone (control) or with GFP and RAGE (RAGE+) (Fig. 1), and RAGE expression was assessed via Western blot and immunofluorescence analysis. The results (Fig. 4A) showed that full-length transmembrane active isoform of RAGE44 was strongly overexpressed with the presence of glycosylated and oligomeric forms of the protein (between 40 and 70 kDa)45 and potential degradative products (< 35 kDa)46. Immunofluorescence analysis (Fig. 4B) showed that RAGE is localized in the plasma membrane, demonstrating the correct localization and trafficking of the protein.

We next characterised the effect of RAGE overexpression on the bioelectrical, functional and structural features of hCMEC/D3 cells. hCMEC/D3RAGE+ cells grown on a transwell plate had TEER values within 40–80 Ω cm2, which are greater than the TEER values of hCMEC/D3 cells. The highest value of 75 ± 10 Ω cm2 was observed on day 7 after seeding (Fig. 5A). On day 7, the EP to 4.4 kDa fluorescent dextran probe was 6.95 × 10−5 cm/min (Fig. 5B), 2.5-fold lower than that of the hCMEC/D3 cells. No changes in TEER values were detected for hCMEC/D3 cells expressing GFP alone.

We then examined the localisation of VE-cadherin, a key component of adherens junctions that controls EC permeability, and the localisation of occludin and ZO-1, which contribute to TJ barrier function. As shown in Fig. 5C, while similar continuous staining for VE-cadherin was observed in both cell lines, the accumulation of occludin and ZO-1 at cell junctions was greater in the hCMEC/D3RAGE+ cells than in the control hCMEC/D3 cells (hCMEC/D3 GFP+).

Additionally, immunoblot analysis revealed that RAGE overexpression was associated with increased levels of CLDN5 (Fig. 5D). These results strongly suggest that RAGE overexpression can play a role in BBB integrity by regulating TJ protein expression and organisation at cell-cell junctions. It has been shown that cell lines express a 70 kDa RAGE isoform47, justifying the difference in the MW of full-length RAGE observed between hCMEC/D3 (cell line) and hCMEC/D3RAGE+ (overexpressing the 55 kDa isoform)46 (Fig. 5D).

Considering that in hCMEC/D3RAGE+ the level of RAGE is 4-fold higher versus a CLDN5 increase of + 30%, while in hCMEC/D3 incubated with PD GSC44 secretome the level of RAGE is 2-fold higher versus a CLDN5 increase of + 100%, it is possible to speculate that the increase of RAGE expression is necessary but not sufficient to exert an effect on the barrier phenotype.

Considering that RAGE activates various intracellular signaling pathways, including mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK), which in turn activate downstream transcription factors22,48, pERK/ERK levels were also assessed by immunoblotting.

The results showed that the levels of pERK/ERK were increased in hCMEC/D3RAGE+ cells compared to untreated hCMEC/D3 and, more interestingly, the incubation of hCMEC/D3 with PD GSC44’s secretome strongly increased the pERK/ERK levels (Fig. 6A).

Treating hCMEC/D3RAGE+ cells with Pimasertib, a known inhibitor of the MAPK signaling pathway49, pERK/ERK levels were reduced; as expected and notably, a ~ 30% reduction in CLDN5 expression was detected, without affecting RAGE levels (Fig. 6B).

Taken together, these results demonstrated that the increase in RAGE expression induced by the PD GSC secretome could play a pivotal role in controlling brain EC permeability by regulating the expression and subsequent localisation of junctional proteins. In particular, our results suggest that RAGE signaling leads to the activation of the ERK pathway, which in turn may influence downstream effectors that can phosphorylate transcription factors that modulate the expression of CLDN5, leading to changes in BBB permeability. We can speculate that the transcription factor involved could be CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein alpha (CEBPα), whose phosphorylation can trigger its translocation from the cytoplasm to the nucleus, thus promoting the expression of CLDN5. This assumption is supported by the findings of Kakogiannos N. et al., who showed that JAM-A, a junctional adhesion molecule belonging to the RAGE superfamily, promotes the expression of CLDN5 via CEBPα 50. Further investigations are needed to deepen the hypothesized signaling pathway.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the results indicated that PD GSC secretomes enhance BBB tightness by reducing its permeability, increasing transendothelial electrical resistance, and elevating the levels of the tight junction protein claudin-5 (CLDN5). Additionally, the receptor for advanced glycation end products (RAGE), known for its role in cancer and chemotherapy resistance, regulates CLDN5 expression through activation of the pERK/ERK signaling pathway and affects junctional organization. This axis could play a role in regulating BBB integrity and function, particularly in the context of brain tumours. Understanding the molecular crosstalk between RAGE and TJ proteins could provide insights into the pathogenesis of BTB dysfunction and offer potential therapeutic targets for brain diseases. Given that BBB modulation appears to be patient-specific, expanding the number of human samples is crucial to better correlate its effects with the clinical and molecular characteristics of different patients. Moreover, in vivo studies are required to elucidate the dynamic interactions and regulatory mechanisms of these proteins in the context of the BBB and GBM pathology, while further characterization of the secretome could offer additional insights and support a more comprehensive interpretation of the findings.

These findings will contribute to understanding the role of PD GSCs in modulating BBB permeability, which could have implications for therapeutic outcomes.
