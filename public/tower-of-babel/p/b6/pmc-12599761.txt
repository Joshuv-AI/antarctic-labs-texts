Coping strategies and mental health among remote hospital workers in the COVID-19 pandemic: A multicenteric cohort study from Pakistan
Authors: Syed Ali Hadi Kirmani, Sajjad Ahmed Khan, Muhammad Arsalan Sharif, Sidra Batool, Maryam Mahsud, Sara Naseer Ahmad, Sadia Aleem, Muhammad Uzair Siddique, Mariam Shahid, Hamza Ahmed, Huma Kausar, Heena Kauser
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The COVID-19 pandemic and digital transformations have led to a significant expansion of remote work in healthcare settings. This longitudinal, multicenter study evaluated personal and organizational coping strategies employed by remote hospital staff (including doctors, nurses, engineers, and administrative personnel) in Lahore and Karachi, Pakistan. The study assessed the effectiveness of these strategies in maintaining mental health. Over a 12-month period, we surveyed approximately 800 participants across 5 hospitals in each city. Validated tools (the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 [PHQ-9] for depression and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 for anxiety) were administered at baseline, 6 months, and 12 months. Additionally, the brief COPE inventory was used to assess coping mechanisms. Ethical approval and informed consent were obtained in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Descriptive statistics were used to characterize demographic and mental health profiles, while multivariable mixed-effects models examined associations between coping strategies and symptom trajectories over time. At baseline, 30% of participants reported moderate-to-severe depressive symptoms. Common personal coping practices included family support, positive thinking, prayer, physical exercise, and mindfulness. Organizational strategies included flexible work schedules, IT assistance, and regular supervisor check-ins. Over time, regular physical activity, virtual social support, and effective time management were significantly associated with lower PHQ-9 and Generalized Anxiety Disorder-7 scores. In contrast, excessive news consumption and workaholism were linked to worse mental health outcomes. For instance, individuals who reported engaging in daily physical activity experienced a 20% reduction in depressive symptom trajectories (P < .01). In conclusion, both personal and organizational strategies were effective in mitigating stress. These findings highlight the importance of institutional policies that promote such coping mechanisms (such as structured support programs and balanced workloads) to sustain staff well-being in remote and hybrid work environments.

1. Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has resulted in a rapid expansion of remote work in healthcare, including telemedicine consultations and remote roles for IT, administration, and support staff. Telemedicine, in particular, has emerged as a “game-changer” for health services, allowing patients to receive care without in-person visits.[1,2] Telehealth platforms in Pakistan, such as Sehat Kahani, have been growing since 2017, providing remote mental health and general medical consultations.[2] The transition to remote work resulted in significant benefits, including shorter commute times, greater scheduling flexibility, and potential improvements in work-life balance and productivity.[1,3] According to WHO and ILO, teleworking can improve physical and mental health, as well as social well-being, by giving employees more control over their work hours and home environments.[1,3] Telemedicine has also helped extend specialist care to underserved areas in low-income countries such as Pakistan, despite limited infrastructure.[2,3]

However, remote work has presented new challenges to healthcare workers’ mental health. In many settings, fully working from home during lockdowns has been linked to increased feelings of social isolation and loneliness, particularly among those who live alone.[3] Excessive screen time and blurred lines between work and home life can exacerbate stress. According to one review, prolonged computer work causes fatigue, headaches, and eyestrain, whereas “blurred work-life boundaries” are associated with increased anxiety and difficulty detaching from work.[3,4] Telemedicine clinicians and remote support staff may also experience “technostress” (stress caused by constantly managing digital systems and being constantly connected, as well as job security concerns and decreased informal peer support. Without careful planning and support, WHO/ILO experts warn that teleworking can result in burnout, depression, and increased unhealthy behaviors such as alcohol abuse.).[1,4]

Emerging evidence confirms the significant psychological toll that healthcare workers faced during and after the pandemic. Globally, systematic reviews suggest that approximately one-quarter of health professionals experienced clinically significant depression or anxiety symptoms during COVID-19.[5,6] Li et al reported a 22% to 26% prevalence of depression and anxiety among healthcare workers, significantly higher than the ~4% seen in pre-pandemic general populations.[5] Meta-analyses in Pakistan have revealed similarly high mental health burdens. According to a review of studies conducted up to 2021, 25.5% of Pakistani healthcare workers experienced significant depressive symptoms.[6] These studies also identify stressors specific to low- and middle-income countries, such as a lack of personal protective equipment, high patient loads, and infection fear, which are exacerbated by resource scarcity and inadequate workplace mental health infrastructure.[5,6]

To cope, healthcare workers have used personal and organizational strategies documented in recent research. Personal coping mechanisms include seeking social and family support, exercising and practicing mindfulness, and engaging in spiritual or religious practices.[7,8] For example, nurses dealing with COVID-19 frequently reported using prayer, reading, meditation, exercise, or leisure activities (e.g., watching movies) to manage stress.[7,8] One multinational study discovered that “family support, positive thinking, and religious/prayers” were among the most popular strategies for healthcare workers dealing with pandemic stress.[7] Many people emphasized the importance of clear infection control training and workplace safety measures as part of psychological coping.[8]

Organizational strategies are equally important. Hospitals that implemented flexible work hours, provided regular breaks, and maintained open communication reported improved staff well-being. Several studies have highlighted the benefits of proactive supervisor check-ins, peer support programs, and mental health hotlines.[7,9] WHO and ILO guidelines support these findings, recommending that employers provide proper ergonomics, clarify expectations (including a “right to disconnect”), and foster workplace cultures that prioritize psychosocial support.[1,10] WHO stress-management resources for frontline workers also recommend staying connected with colleagues and family, sticking to healthy routines (balanced diet, sleep, and exercise), practicing relaxation techniques (deep breathing, mindfulness), and seeking professional help when necessary.[9,10]

Despite these broad recommendations, the majority of evidence on effective coping comes from higher-resource settings or focuses solely on frontline clinicians.[6] There is little systematic data on how mixed cadres of remote hospital employees (including telemedicine clinicians, IT staff, and administrators) perform over time, particularly in low-resource settings such as Pakistan.[11–13]

To close this gap, we launched a multicenter longitudinal study involving hospital staff working remotely in Lahore and Karachi. Our primary goal is to determine which personal and organizational coping strategies are most effective in supporting healthcare workers’ mental health over time. This study, focusing on a broad range of roles in tertiary hospitals, will complement global findings and inform targeted interventions for remote healthcare teams in Pakistan.

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and setting

From January 2023 to December 2024, we conducted a prospective cohort study across 10 hospitals (5 public and 5 private) in Lahore and Karachi, Pakistan. Eligible participants included hospital staff who worked remotely (≥3 d/wk), such as physicians providing telemedicine, engineers and IT personnel, nurses in administrative roles, and other support staff. Individuals working exclusively in clinical wards were excluded. Using stratified sampling by profession, we aimed to recruit approximately 800 participants (~400 per city). This sample size provided at least 80% power to detect small-to-medium effect sizes (Cohen d ≈ 0.3) in changes in mental health scores, allowing for an estimated 20% attrition over time.

2.2. Data collection

Baseline data were collected through online surveys and phone interviews, with follow-ups at 6 and 12 months. Validated instruments were used to assess mental health and coping strategies:

Depression: Measured using the 9-item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9), with scores ranging from 0 to 27. A score ≥ 10 indicates moderate depression.

Coping strategies: The brief COPE inventory (28 items) was used to evaluate personal coping behaviors, including active coping, planning, positive reframing, acceptance, humor, religion, and substance use. All survey instruments were administered in either English or Urdu, based on participant preference. The PHQ-9 and GAD-7 have been previously validated in similar settings. The brief COPE inventory has demonstrated strong reliability. A pilot test with 30 participants was conducted to assess language clarity and comprehension, resulting in minor modifications.

2.3. Ethical considerations

The study protocol received ethical approval from the institutional review boards of the participating hospitals and the affiliated university, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained electronically. Confidentiality was maintained through anonymized participant IDs, and data were securely stored. Participants with severe scores on the PHQ-9 or GAD-7 were referred to counseling services. There was no monetary compensation, although reminders were sent to encourage continued participation.

2.4. Sample size calculation

Sample size was estimated using G*Power 3.1 for repeated-measures ANOVA (within-between interaction), targeting an effect size of f = 0.15 (small), α = 0.05, power = 0.80, and an assumed correlation of 0.5 among repeated measures. This yielded a required sample of approximately 400 participants. To account for 20% attrition, we aimed to recruit 480 participants per city (total N = 960). Ultimately, 845 individuals consented at baseline (422 in Lahore and 423 in Karachi).

2.5. Data analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized baseline characteristics and coping strategy frequencies. Mean PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores were plotted at each time point (Fig. 1). Coping strategies (e.g., exercise, prayer, flexible work arrangements) were categorized and visualized using pie charts (Fig. 2).

Longitudinal changes in mental health outcomes were analyzed using mixed-effects regression models, incorporating PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores as dependent variables, with time, coping variables, and their interaction terms as predictors. Random intercepts were included for individuals and hospitals. Models were adjusted for age, gender, profession, and city. Interaction effects (e.g., coping × time) were examined to identify coping strategies associated with differential trajectories in mental health over time. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Analyses were conducted using R version 4.2 and Stata version 17.

3. Results

3.1. Participant characteristics

Table 1 summarizes the characteristics of the study cohort (N = 845). The average age was 34.6 years, and 62% of participants were male. Professionally, 45% were doctors, 30% nurses, 15% engineers/IT staff, and 10% other hospital employees. The samples from Lahore and Karachi were demographically comparable. At baseline, the mean PHQ-9 score was 8.5 (SD = 4.2), and the mean GAD-7 score was 7.8 (SD = 3.9). Approximately 30% of participants screened ≥ 10 on the PHQ-9, indicating moderate depression, while 28% scored ≥ 10 on the GAD-7, suggesting moderate anxiety.

3.2. Coping strategies usage

Commonly reported personal coping behaviors (multiple responses allowed) included regular exercise (68%), daily prayer or meditation (64%), seeking family or friend support (72%), and maintaining hobbies (45%) (Fig. 1). Problem-solving approaches such as time management (55%) and positive thinking (60%) were also frequent. Maladaptive coping strategies were reported by fewer participants, including increased alcohol or drug use (<15%) and overwork (e.g., skipping breaks, 22%).

3.3. Longitudinal mental health trends

Figures 3 and 4 show the trajectory of mean PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores over time, stratified by key coping practices. Participants who engaged in regular exercise or maintained social support exhibited the most notable improvements in mental health symptoms. For example, the mean PHQ-9 score in the “regular exercise” group declined from 7.8 at baseline to 5.2 at 12 months, compared to a reduction from 8.9 to 7.0 in the non-exercise group. Similar trends were observed for anxiety scores. Conversely, individuals who reported “constant news/media consumption” had persistently elevated PHQ-9 scores (~10) throughout the study period.

Mixed-effects regression results are presented in Table 2. After adjusting for covariates, regular exercise (β = −1.2, P = .004) and faith-based practices (β = −0.9, P = .010) were significantly associated with lower PHQ-9 scores over time. High perceived organizational support was linked to reduced anxiety (GAD-7 β = −1.5, P = .002). In contrast, working late hours without adequate breaks was associated with increased depression (PHQ-9 β = +1.3, P = .006) and anxiety (GAD-7 β = +1.1, P = .020).

By 12 months, the proportion of participants with moderate-to-severe symptoms had halved (depression prevalence declined from 30–15%, and anxiety from 28–14%). Multivariate analyses (Fig. 3, not shown) confirmed that regular physical activity, strong social connections (virtual or in-person), and effective time management were the most protective coping strategies. No significant effect of age or gender was observed on mental health outcomes.

4. Discussion

This study identifies effective coping strategies that improve mental health among remote hospital workers in a real-world Pakistani setting. Consistent with global findings, our participants reported high levels of psychological stress: roughly one-third had moderate to severe anxiety or depression symptoms at baseline. These figures are consistent with international reports that suggest that 25% to 30% of healthcare workers experienced clinically significant psychological distress during and after the COVID-19 pandemic.[3,14]

Over a year, participants who used adaptive coping strategies, such as regular exercise, maintaining social connections, positive reframing, and religious practices, saw significant improvements in their PHQ-9 and GAD-7 scores. These findings add to a large body of evidence demonstrating that multifaceted personal coping improves resilience under chronic stress conditions. Yang et al found that healthcare workers who used multiple positive coping methods had better psychological adjustment and lower burnout rates.[15] Similarly, combining physical activity with strong family and social support networks has been linked to better mood regulation and cognitive flexibility.[4,16]

Organizational support also emerged as an important protective factor in our cohort. Participants who perceived their employers as adaptable, communicative, and caring reported significantly lower levels of anxiety at the end of the study period. These findings are consistent with global research indicating that supervisor availability, work-hour flexibility, and the provision of mental health resources reduce remote work-related stress.[17,18] Speroni et al found that emotional detachment from leadership, manifested as poor communication or limited check-ins, was a significant contributor to staff disengagement and distress among nurses during COVID-19.[8] Our findings support WHO/ILO recommendations for employers to provide appropriate digital infrastructure, schedule flexibility, leadership training, and respect for employees’ “right to disconnect.”[19,20]

Religious coping (through prayer, meditation, and seeking meaning) was another popular and culturally appropriate strategy among Pakistani participants. This is consistent with qualitative research in South Asia that has identified faith-based resilience as a key component of emotional regulation among healthcare professionals.[21,22] While the efficacy of religious coping is context-dependent, it has been linked to better psychological outcomes in collectivist cultures where religion is an important aspect of identity.[12]

In contrast, our findings revealed that maladaptive strategies such as excessive news consumption, social withdrawal, and workaholism (e.g., skipping breaks or working late into the night) were associated with poorer mental health outcomes. These findings are consistent with previous research linking high exposure to distressing media content and a lack of recovery time to increased rates of anxiety, insomnia, and emotional exhaustion.[21,22]

Importantly, our longitudinal design enabled us to track dynamic coping changes over time. Workers who lacked consistent access to positive coping resources or institutional support experienced either static or worsening symptoms. A 2-year longitudinal study by Stanisławska-Kubiak et al in Poland found a decrease in adaptive coping (e.g., acceptance, planning, religious faith) and an increase in maladaptive behaviors and depressive symptoms among hospital staff over time. In contrast, our cohort showed overall improvement, most likely due to time-based adaptation and the availability of coping strategies discussed during the study. This highlights the ability of timely, structured interventions to shift coping trajectories in a positive direction.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, self-selection bias may have occurred, with participants who were more technologically literate or mentally healthy being more likely to participate in online surveys. Second, the study used self-reported data, which are susceptible to recall bias and social desirability effects. Although validated scales (PHQ-9 and GAD-7) were used, the lack of clinical interviews reduces diagnostic accuracy. Third, the cultural specificity of coping strategies (particularly the use of religious practices) may limit our findings’ applicability beyond South Asian Muslim-majority populations. Attrition (~18% over 12 months) may have influenced the longitudinal findings, but mixed-effects models helped reduce its impact.

5. Conclusions

This study highlights the vital influence of both individual and organizational coping mechanisms on the mental well-being of hospital employees working remotely during the COVID-19 pandemic. Engaging in regular physical activity, practicing faith, maintaining social connections, and perceiving strong organizational support were linked to notable reductions in depression and anxiety levels over a 12-month period. In contrast, maladaptive responses such as excessive workload and overexposure to media were associated with poorer mental health outcomes. These results emphasize the importance of institutional policies and interventions that encourage positive coping practices and ensure continuous psychological support for staff in remote and hybrid work settings.
