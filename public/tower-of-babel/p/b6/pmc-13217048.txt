Comparison of clinical characteristics between patients with single mutation and co-mutation in hereditary renal cancer: a retrospective analysis of 115 patients with von Hippel-Lindau syndrome
Authors: Tao Liu, Haode Liu, Ruiyi Deng, Qiu Jianhui, Zedan Zhang, Chuandong Wang, Yuhang Bao, Xiaolin Chen, Zheng Song, Tianyi He, Lin Cai, Yizhou Wang, Jingcheng Zhou, Kan Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposevon Hippel-Lindau (VHL) syndrome-related renal cell carcinoma (RCC) is the most prevalent hereditary RCC and exhibits clinical heterogeneity, complicating patient management. While VHL gene inactivation is the primary driver, the impact of co-occurring germline mutations in other renal cancer predisposition genes on clinical phenotype remains poorly understood. This study aimed to investigate whether such co-mutations define a distinct clinical and molecular subgroup of patients with VHL syndrome.Methods115 patients with VHL syndrome were enrolled in this study. All patients underwent germline sequencing of a 39-gene panel for hereditary RCC. Pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants of the VHL gene were identified, and patients were stratified into a VHL single-mutation group and a VHL co-mutation group. Clinical characteristics, tumour manifestations, renal lesion growth kinetics and age-related tumour risks were compared.Results42.6% of patients harboured germline co-mutations. The co-mutation group exhibited a greater proportion of symptomatic patients (95.9% vs 78.7%, p=0.012) and increased penetrance of RCC (79.6% vs 57.6%, p=0.013) and pancreatic cystic tumours (77.6% vs 48.5%, p=0.002). This group had a lower incidence of positive family history (40.8% vs 69.7%, p=0.002). Co-mutation status is an independent predictor for RCC development (HR 1.798, p=0.016), and their RCC lesions exhibited significantly faster linear (0.376 cm/year vs 0.225 cm/year, p=0.022) and volumetric (3.132 cm3/year vs 1.167 cm3/year, p=0.049) growth metrics.ConclusionsGermline co-mutations are prevalent in VHL syndrome and define a clinically distinct subset of patients characterised by higher disease penetrance and a more aggressive RCC phenotype. Our findings suggest that co-mutation status is a critical determinant of clinical heterogeneity and a powerful biomarker for risk stratification.

Introduction

Hereditary renal cell carcinoma (RCC) constitutes a clinically and genetically distinct category of kidney cancers caused by germline mutations in tumour susceptibility genes.1 2 Compared with sporadic RCC, hereditary forms often develop at an earlier age, manifest as bilateral or multifocal lesions and are commonly associated with a family history of malignancy.3 Several hereditary RCC syndromes have been characterised, including von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) syndrome (OMIM 193300), Birt–Hogg–Dubé syndrome (OMIM 135150), hereditary leiomyomatosis and RCC (OMIM 150800) and hereditary papillary RCC (OMIM 164860).47 In recent years, with the implementation of large-scale population-based genetic sequencing, an increasing number of studies have demonstrated that, in addition to the classical renal cancer syndrome genes, other genes may also be associated with hereditary renal cancer, such as mismatch repair-related genes and DNA damage response-related genes.8 9 Among these, VHL syndrome is the most prevalent, accounting for the largest proportion of hereditary RCC worldwide. Patients with VHL syndrome face a markedly increased lifetime risk of developing clear cell RCC (ccRCC), a risk that often necessitates repeated surgeries or ablative treatments and long-term surveillance.10 11 Given its prevalence and significant clinical burden, VHL syndrome-associated RCC serves as a crucial paradigm for understanding hereditary kidney cancer.

The VHL gene, located on chromosome 3p25, encodes a tumour suppressor protein that forms part of an E3 ubiquitin ligase complex responsible for degrading hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF)-α subunits under normoxic conditions.12 13 Loss-of-function mutations in VHL lead to constitutive stabilisation of HIF, transcriptional activation of hypoxia-responsive genes and promotion of angiogenesis, glucose metabolism and tumour cell survival.1416 This mechanism explains why VHL mutations are regarded as the hallmark of both hereditary and sporadic ccRCC. However, accumulating evidence suggests that in hereditary RCC, VHL mutations may co-occur with pathogenic alterations in other susceptibility genes such as FANCD2, SDHA, FLCN or CHEK2.1719 This raises the possibility that tumourigenesis in VHL syndrome is not always driven by VHL mutation alone, but may be influenced by additional germline or somatic events. Such multigene alterations are hypothesised to be key modifiers that help explain the profound clinical heterogeneity observed in VHL disease, including variability in age of onset, tumour aggressiveness and treatment response. Yet, this phenomenon remains poorly characterised, largely limited to individual case reports and lacking systematic investigation in large patient cohorts.

To address this critical knowledge gap, we systematically compared the clinical characteristics of patients with VHL syndrome with single VHL mutations against those harbouring co-mutations in other hereditary RCC predisposition genes. We hypothesise that co-mutations contribute to a more aggressive phenotype, including accelerated tumour growth. By delineating the distinct clinical profiles between these two patient subgroups, we aim to uncover the functional impact of co-mutations on disease presentation and progression. Ultimately, our findings provide a foundation for personalised management of VHL syndrome and offer novel perspectives into the molecular pathogenesis of hereditary kidney cancer.

Methods

Study population and clinical assessment

This study was based on a retrospectively maintained institutional database. A total of 115 patients were enrolled in this retrospective cohort study from April 2021 to April 2025. Inclusion in this analysis required a definitive VHL diagnosis based on genetic testing. Clinical characteristics were systematically collected through medical history inquiry and previous medical records. The phenotypic data were provided in online supplemental figure S1. Age at onset was defined as the age at which the first VHL-related symptom or sign was documented.

Germline genetic analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from peripheral blood samples from all participants. Targeted next-generation sequencing was performed using a custom panel of 39 genes associated with hereditary renal cancer according to guidelines recommendations and literature review (online supplemental table S1). Sequence variants were identified and subsequently classified according to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) and the Association for Molecular Pathology guidelines. For this study, only variants classified as ‘Pathogenic’ (P) or ‘Likely Pathogenic’ (LP) of the VHL gene were considered clinically significant. Patients were then stratified into two cohorts for comparison: the ‘single-mutation group’, comprising individuals with only a P/LP variant in the VHL gene, and the ‘co-mutation group’, comprising those with a P/LP variant in VHL plus at least one additional variant in genes on the panel, regardless of the co-mutated genes’ current pathogenic classification. The mutation details and ACMG classifications of all the participants were presented in online supplemental tables S2 and S3.

Renal lesion growth rate measurement

For patients with available serial imaging, tumour growth rate was calculated by measuring the change in size of the same lesion between two consecutive contrast-enhanced CT scans as previously described.20 21 All consecutive follow-up CT images were independently reviewed by two experienced radiologists in Peking University First Hospital. In this process, each radiologist separately measured the target lesions according to RECIST 1.1 criteria while remaining blinded to the other’s assessment. To further minimise bias, the radiologists were not informed whether scans were from baseline or follow-up timepoints, nor were they provided with patients’ clinical details. In cases where initial measurements differed by more than 10%, the two radiologists jointly re-evaluated the relevant lesions to reach a consensus, and all subsequent growth rate analyses were based on this finalised dataset. The linear growth rate (LGR), defined as the annual increase in the maximal tumour diameter (cm/year), was calculated to represent unidimensional tumour expansion. The volumetric growth rate (VGR) represents the volumetric change rate of a tumour over time between two consecutive imaging assessments.

Statistical analysis

All clinical features of patients in the single mutation group and the co-mutation group were summarised and analysed. The Student’s t-test was applied to compare mean onset age between two groups, and the Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the renal tumours’ LGR and VGR. For categorical variables, including gender, family history, mutation type, mutation region and VHL syndrome-related disease, were compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Kaplan-Meier analysis was performed to assess the age-related cumulative risk of major VHL-associated lesions. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to identify risk factors, with patients categorised by sex, family history and mutation type. Data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.22.0 and GraphPad Prism 10. A two-tailed p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical and genetic characteristics of patients with VHL syndrome with single-gene mutation or co-mutation

Of the 115 patients with VHL syndrome enrolled, 66 (57.4%) harboured only a P/LP variant in the VHL gene (single-mutation group), while the remaining 49 (42.6%) carried at least one additional variant (co-mutation group).

Baseline clinical and genetic features are shown in table 1 and online supplemental figure S1. The co-mutation group had a significantly higher proportion of male patients (69.4% vs 50%, p=0.037). Most notably, patients with co-mutations reported a significantly lower rate of positive family history (40.8% vs 69.7%, p=0.002). Clinically, the co-mutation group exhibited a more severe phenotype, with a higher proportion of symptomatic individuals (95.9% vs 78.7%, p=0.012). Specifically, the incidences of RCC (79.6% vs 57.6%, p=0.013) and pancreatic cystic tumours (PCT) (77.6% vs 48.5%, p=0.002) were significantly elevated in this group. In contrast, no significant differences were observed in types or location of VHL gene mutation, onset age and the proportions of those with central nervous system hemangioblastomas (CHB), retinal angiomas (RA) or pheochromocytomas (PHEO).

VHL gene mutation spectrum and co-mutation gene analysis

To explore the genetic underpinnings of these clinical differences, we first analysed the VHL mutation spectrum. Although overall mutation types did not differ (table 1), their distribution varied. In the single mutation group, point mutations clustered at known hotspots (eg, bases 163, 194 and 481 correspond to codons 55, 65 and 161), consistent with previous reports.22 23 In contrast, point mutations in the co-mutation group were more diffusely distributed and involved rarer sites (figure 1A,B). The frequency of large deletions was similar between the two groups (figure 1C). These findings, coupled with the lower rate of positive family history, strongly suggest that the mutational events in the co-mutation group may predominantly arise from de novo processes rather than familial inheritance.

Next, we characterised the co-mutation landscape in 49 patients. In addition to a single P/LP VHL variant carried by each patient, we identified 62 distinct variants across the other 29 genes. The most frequently co-mutated genes were TSC2 (12.2%), MLH1 (12.2%), BRCA2 (10.2%) and RECQL4 (8.2%) (figure 1D). A striking finding emerged when comparing this germline co-mutation profile to the somatic landscape of 172 VHL-mutant ccRCC cases from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. The most common somatic co-mutations in TCGA were in PBRM1 (51%), SETD2 (15%) and BAP1 (12%) (figure 1E), genes rarely affected in our germline co-mutation cohort. This stark divergence highlights fundamentally different molecular trajectories between hereditary and sporadic VHL-driven tumourigenesis.

Age-related tumour risks of single mutation and co-mutation patients

To account for age as a confounding factor, we performed Kaplan-Meier analysis to model the cumulative lifetime risk of developing VHL-associated lesions. The results revealed that the co-mutation group had a significantly higher age-adjusted cumulative risk of RCC (p=0.014). No significant differences were observed for the overall disease risk (p=0.064) or the risks of CHB (p=0.287), PCT (p=0.112), RA (p=0.447) or PHEO (p=0.981) (figure 2). This finding aligns with the gene panel used, which was enriched for RCC predisposition genes.

Given that the TSC family genes were the most frequently co-mutated, along with the prevalence of short arm of chromosome 3 (3p) deletion in both hereditary and sporadic RCC,24 we further performed subgroup analyses within the co-mutation group. We found no significant differences in tumour risk between patients with or without tuberous sclerosis complex (TSC)-family mutations (online supplemental figure S2). However, patients with co-mutations on chromosome 3p (n=7) showed a lower overall disease penetrance compared with those with non-3p co-mutations (n=42; p=0.038), though RCC-specific risk was not significantly different (online supplemental figure S3). These subgroup findings should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample sizes.

Co-mutation is an independent predictor of RCC and PCT development

To determine if co-mutation status was an independent risk factor, we performed univariate and multivariate Cox proportional-hazards regression (table 2). The multivariate analysis demonstrated that the presence of a co-mutation was a significant and independent predictor of RCC (HR 1.798, 95% CI 1.113 to 2.894, p=0.016). Interestingly, co-mutation status was also independently associated with an increased risk of developing PCT (HR 1.654, 95% CI 1.016 to 2.695, p=0.043).

Co-mutations are associated with accelerated RCC growth kinetics

The clinical guidelines for VHL syndrome explicitly recommend regular imaging surveillance to monitor tumour progression.10 25 Finally, to assess tumour biology directly, we analysed the growth rates of 49 renal lesions with adequate serial imaging (19 lesions in 14 single-mutation patients and 18 lesions in 12 co-mutation patients). As depicted in representative images (figure 3A,B) and summarised in figure 3C, RCC lesions in the co-mutation group grew significantly faster than those in the single-mutation group, both in linear and volumetric terms, with a mean LGR of 0.376 cm/year vs. 0.225 cm/year (p=0.022) and a mean VGR of 3.132 cm3/year vs. 1.167 cm3/year (p=0.049). These data provide direct evidence that co-mutations are associated with more aggressive tumour behaviour.

Discussion

This study provides the first systematic delineation of the clinical and genetic landscape of patients with VHL syndrome harbouring additional germline mutations in hereditary RCC predisposition genes. Our findings reveal that co-mutations are not rare events but rather a common feature defining a distinct subgroup with a more aggressive clinical course. These insights have profound implications for understanding RCC pathogenesis, refining risk stratification and guiding personalised therapeutic strategies for this patient population.

A major observation is that nearly half of the enrolled VHL cohort carried pathogenic co-mutations, revealing a degree of genetic heterogeneity far greater than previously appreciated. The co-mutation group demonstrated significantly higher disease burden, particularly a greater penetrance and accelerated growth rate of RCC. These results strongly support a multi-hit oncogenic model in which co-mutated genes cooperate with VHL loss to potentiate renal tumour initiation and progression.18 19 While VHL inactivation drives HIF stabilisation and promotes angiogenesis and metabolic reprogramming,14 26 concomitant alterations in genes such as TSC2, MLH1, BRCA2 or RECQL4, which were frequently detected in the present cohort, may further disrupt mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR) signalling, DNA damage repair and genomic stability,2729 thereby conferring additional selective advantages to tumour progenitor clones. This synergistic interplay could mechanistically account for the more aggressive phenotypes observed among co-mutation carriers. Among these co-mutated variants, we noted that some mutations were benign/likely benign or variations of uncertain significance and these types of mutations currently lack sufficient evidence to cause diseases; for instance, benign mutations in the TSC1/2 gene did not lead to TSC syndrome.30 31 However, in this study, all patients already harboured a pathogenic variant in the VHL gene. Against this background, it is meaningful to investigate the synergistic effects of co-occurring mutations. A strong line of evidence supporting this concept comes from studies on Hirschsprung disease, in which mutations in the RET gene, acting as a proto-oncogene, play a primary pathogenic role.32 In this context, additional mutations in genes such as EDNRB and GAP43 have been shown to exacerbate the disease phenotype, whereas mutations in these genes alone are insufficient to cause the disorder.33 34 Our study of co-mutational patterns suggests that a similar phenomenon may also exist in VHL syndrome.

Marked distinctions were also noted in the mutational spectra between germline VHL-associated RCC and sporadic RCC with somatic VHL mutations. Analysis of the TCGA-kidney renal clear cell carcinoma cohort demonstrated that somatic VHL mutations were most frequently accompanied by PBRM1, TTN, SETD2 and BAP1 alterations, whereas these genes were rarely co-mutated in the germline setting. In contrast, germline co-mutations primarily involved canonical hereditary cancer predisposition genes with potential relevance to RCC, spanning diverse functional pathways, with minimal overlap with the somatic mutational landscape. This divergence implies fundamentally distinct evolutionary trajectories between hereditary and sporadic RCC. Sporadic tumourigenesis likely arises from clonal selection within an otherwise genomically stable background, whereas hereditary tumourigenesis emerges within a context of constitutive genomic instability, in which co-inherited defects in genome maintenance facilitate early neoplastic transformation. This insight has clinical implications, as therapeutic strategies derived from studies in sporadic RCC may not be directly applicable to hereditary RCC cases characterised by distinct molecular drivers.35 Furthermore, identification of the affected signalling pathways (eg, TSC/mTOR, mismatch repair and homologous recombination) could inform the rational application of pathway-targeted or synthetic-lethal therapeutic strategies, such as mTOR inhibitors, poly ADP-ribose polymerase inhibitors or immune checkpoint blockade in selected patients with hereditary RCC, an approach not addressed by existing clinical guidelines.272936

In addition, the co-mutation group displayed distinctive clinical profiles, being predominantly male and exhibiting a lower prevalence of positive family history compared with the single-mutation group. This pattern suggests that many co-mutation cases may represent de novo germline events rather than classic vertical inheritance. Supporting this notion, the VHL mutation sites in the co-mutation group were more widely distributed and included rare variants, whereas the single mutation group harboured recurrent hotspot mutations consistent with long-standing familial transmission. These observations indicate that conventional genetic counselling paradigms for VHL syndrome may require refinement to account for the possibility of sporadic germline mutational convergence, particularly in apparently isolated cases without family history.17

Risk factor analysis further demonstrated that the presence of co-mutations constitutes an independent predictor of RCC and PCT development, even after adjustment for sex and family history. This finding underscores the necessity of incorporating co-mutation status into future risk stratification frameworks for VHL syndrome. Current surveillance recommendations are largely age-based and do not consider genetic modifiers. The present data suggest that individuals with co-mutations may warrant earlier initiation and/or shorter intervals of imaging surveillance, as well as lower thresholds for nephron-sparing interventions in view of the observed faster tumour growth rates.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size of the co-mutation subgroup was relatively small, which limited statistical power for rare gene-specific analyses, and subgroup comparisons (eg, TSC family vs non-TSC family, chromosome 3p vs non-3p mutations) were likely underpowered. Second, functional studies to directly validate the synergistic oncogenic effects of specific co-mutations were not performed and will be critical for future investigations. Additionally, although the gene panel encompassed 39 canonical hereditary RCC genes, structural variants, deep intronic alterations or yet-undiscovered predisposition genes may not have been captured. Larger multicentre cohorts, comprehensive whole-genome sequencing and mechanistic modelling using patient-derived systems will be required to fully elucidate the biological consequences of co-mutations in VHL syndrome.

The findings of this study may provide clinically relevant information for genetic counselling in individuals with VHL syndrome. This is particularly applicable to patients without a known family history who present with VHL-associated clinical manifestations, especially renal tumours. In such cases, multigene germline mutation tests may be informative for identifying additional genetic variants that could contribute to phenotypic variability. For individuals found to harbour germline variants in more than one gene, enhanced follow-up may facilitate earlier detection of tumour progression and enable timely clinical management.

In summary, this study demonstrates that a substantial proportion of patients with VHL syndrome harbour pathogenic germline co-mutations. These co-mutations define a clinically and genetically distinct subgroup associated with higher disease penetrance and a more aggressive RCC phenotype. Co-mutation status emerges as a critical determinant of clinical heterogeneity in hereditary kidney cancer and represents a promising biomarker for risk-adapted surveillance and individualised therapy. Integration of comprehensive germline genomic profiling into routine clinical practice will be essential to translate these insights into improved outcomes for patients and usher in a new era of precision oncology for hereditary kidney cancer.
