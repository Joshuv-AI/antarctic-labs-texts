Left frontal activity measured by fNIRS during a verbal memory task is modulated by predictability in cognitively healthy older adults
Authors: Yumi Song, Prawesh Dahal, Hyoung-ju Kim, Minkyung Kim, Yong Seo Koo, Jin Hyung Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFunctional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) is widely used to measure cognitive load during working memory tasks. However, little is known about how task structure modulates load-dependent neural responses.ObjectiveWe examined how working memory load measured by a verbal memory task is represented in fronto-parietal hemodynamic responses during the encoding phase in cognitively healthy older adults and whether these responses are modulated by the predictability of task difficulty.MethodsOxygenated hemoglobin (HbO) responses were measured using fNIRS while participants performed digit span trials with load levels presented in either increasing order or randomized sequences.ResultsMixed-effects modeling across all participants and fronto-parietal regions of interest revealed a significant main effect of load levels, with HbO responses during the encoding phase increasing linearly with increasing memory load. However, load sensitivity differed by task structure: the random group showed a significantly steeper load-related increase in HbO responses than the non-random group, driven primarily by the left frontal cortex. Behavioral accuracy remained stable across load levels.ConclusionsThese findings indicate that fronto-parietal encoding-phase hemodynamic responses reflect working memory demands in a context-dependent manner, revealing neural sensitivity beyond overt behavioral performance.

Introduction

Over the past several decades, neuroimaging techniques such as positron emission tomography (PET), magnetoencephalography (MEG), and functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) have been widely used to examine human brain function (Lindquist et al., 2025). However, these methods are often limited by their invasiveness, high cost and sensitivity to motion artifacts which can constrain their use in task-based experimental settings (Pinti et al., 2020). In this context, functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) has emerged as a low-cost, non-invasive imaging modality for studying cortical activity (Yücel et al., 2025). The method measures hemodynamic changes in the brain associated with cortical activation using low levels of near-infrared light transmitted through the scalp (Villringer and Chance, 1997; Ferrari and Quaresima, 2012; Ayaz et al., 2022). Increases in neural activity elevates metabolic demand, leading to characteristic increases in oxyhemoglobin (HbO) and decreases in deoxyhemoglobin (HbR) concentrations, analogous to responses measured with fMRI (Buxton, 2013; Scholkmann et al., 2014). These hemodynamic signals provide an indirect measure of neural engagement and have been widely used to examine cognitive processes, including task engagement and modulation by working memory load (Llana et al., 2022). Importantly, the validity of fNIRS-derived hemodynamic measurements shows strong spatial and functional correspondence with fMRI findings (Huppert et al., 2006; Ferrari and Quaresima, 2012), supporting the use of fNIRS for investigating cortical dynamics during working memory tasks.

Working memory is a core component of higher cognitive functions that supports the temporary maintenance and manipulation of goal-directed behavior (Cowan, 2014). Rather than serving as a static storage system, working memory requires active processes that monitor, update and selectively prioritize information over short time scales as task demands change (Baddeley, 1986; Baddeley, 2012). These processes critically depend on the integrity of the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and its interactions with the posterior brain regions (Miller and Cohen, 2001). Consistent with this framework, fMRI studies using verbal memory tasks have repeatedly demonstrated engagement within the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) and posterior parietal cortex, with a significant left-hemisphere lateralization in the prefrontal cortex (see meta-analysis, Owen et al., 2005). These fronto-parietal networks are therefore commonly studied using experimental paradigms that systematically manipulate cognitive loads.

Working memory load is commonly manipulated using span-based paradigms, such as the N-back task or digit span tasks, that probe verbal working memory and require sustained attention (Hilbert et al., 2015; Jonides et al., 1997). In the N-back task, participants continuously monitor a stream of stimuli and compare the current stimulus to stimulus presented N items earlier, with increasing N placing greater demands on working memory (Conway et al., 2005; Owen et al., 2005; Meidenbauer et al., 2021). Similarly, digit span tasks can manipulate cognitive load by varying the length of digit sequence that must be encoded, maintained, and subsequently recalled, such that longer span length is associated with increasing task difficulty (Hilbert et al., 2015). Prior fNIRS studies using span-based working memory tasks have demonstrated load-dependent changes in HbO and HbR across areas of prefrontal cortex and parietal regions (Kuruvilla et al., 2013; Fishburn et al., 2014; Sato et al., 2013; Ayaz et al., 2012).

Although task difficulty and working memory load are typically manipulated as parametric variables, the structure by which difficulty levels are presented across trials has not been systematically studied. In many experimental paradigms, task difficulty is often presented in predictable or structured sequences across blocks (e.g. 1-back → 2-back → 3-back in N-back tasks) allowing participants to anticipate upcoming demands from prior trials (Braver et al., 1997; Lefebvre et al., 2005; Nagel et al., 2013). In contrast, randomized presentation of difficulty levels across trials (e.g. 2-back → 1-back→ 3-back) removes predictability in trial order (Meidenbauer et al., 2021; Han et al., 2022). Moreover, such randomization does not change the magnitude of working memory load at any given difficulty level but instead alters the sequential structure in which increasing task demands are encountered. Therefore, it remains unclear whether and how task structure modulates load-dependent neural responses within fronto-parietal networks known to support working memory.

To address this gap, we examined how working memory load is represented in fronto-parietal hemodynamic responses measured with fNIRS during the encoding phase of a digit span task, and whether load-dependent neural scaling differs as a function of task structure, reflecting the predictability of load progression. Participants performed digit span trials of 4, 5, or 6 digits, with load levels presented in either random or non-random order. We hypothesized that fronto-parietal encoding-phase HbO responses would increase linearly with increasing working memory load and that this load-dependent scaling would differ according to task structure. Specifically, we expected that unpredictable task structure would be associated with greater load-related HbO modulation relative to predictable task structure. Using linear mixed-effects models, we first tested whether fronto-parietal encoding-phase hemodynamic responses increase linearly with working memory load across participants, pooling across task groups and regions of interest. We then examined whether the magnitude of this load-dependent change varies as a function of task structure (random vs. non-random load presentation). Finally, we conducted regional follow-up analyses to assess whether task structure–dependent load effects exhibit regional specificity across frontal and parietal cortices.

Methods

Participants

Twenty-nine cognitively healthy older adults participated in the study. All participants were aged 60 years or older (mean age = 69.9 ± 6.2) and met the inclusion criteria for normal cognitive functioning, defined as a Global Deterioration Scale (GDS) score of 1 (Reisberg et al., 1982). Individuals with a history of neurological or psychiatric disorders were excluded. Participants were assigned to either a random group (n = 17) or a non-random group (n = 12) according to enrollment order. This prospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Asan Medical Center, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to participation, and the study was conducted in accordance with institutional ethical guidelines. (IRB No. 2025-0450).

Task and experimental design

Participants performed a verbal digit span working memory task while fNIRS data were recorded (see Fig. 1). Experimental design of the working memory task.

Each participant completed a single run consisting of 15 digit span trials (five trials per load condition), preceded by a 5-min resting-state recording. Each trial followed a block paradigm comprising a 10-s encoding period and a 10-s retrieval period, each interleaved with 10-s rest periods. Trial load order was either sequential (non-random group) or randomized across trials (random group).

Each participant completed a single experimental run consisting of fifteen digit span trials, with five trials for each working memory load level (4-, 5-, and 6-digit conditions). No additional experimental runs were performed. At the beginning of the session, a 5-min eyes-open resting-state recording was acquired, during which participants were instructed to remain still and relaxed.

The task phase followed a block design. Each trial began with a 10-s encoding period, during which participants memorized a visually presented 4-, 5-, or 6-digit sequence. The encoding period was followed by a 10-s rest interval. Participants then completed a 10-s retrieval period, during which they verbally recalled the previously encoded digit sequence, followed by an additional 10-s rest period before the onset of the next trial. Participants were explicitly instructed not to vocalize or rehearse the digit sequence aloud during the encoding period.

In the non-random group, trials were presented in a fixed sequential order, consisting of five 4-digit trials followed by five 5-digit trials and then five 6-digit trials. In contrast, in the random group, trials from the three load levels were interleaved and presented in a pseudorandomized order across the run, while maintaining an equal number of trials per load level. This pseudorandomized presentation was implemented to reduce potential order effects associated with sequential load progression.

Task performance was quantified as accuracy, defined as the proportion of correctly recalled trials within each load level. Accuracy values were calculated at the subject level by averaging across the five trials per load level.

fNIRS data acquisition

fNIRS data were acquired using a continuous-wave system (Brite24, Artinis Medical Systems). The wavelengths of emitted light in this system were 757 nm and 851 nm, and data were recorded at a sampling frequency of 50 Hz using Oxysoft software (version 4.0).

The optode configuration consisted of 10 sources and 8 detectors, forming a total of 22 source–detector pairs (channels) covering bilateral frontal and parietal cortices (see Fig. 2). Optodes were mounted on a standard EEG 10–20 cap, in which additional perforations were made to accommodate the fNIRS optodes. In accordance with established guidelines recommending optode placement based on the international 10–20 system (Yücel et al., 2021), this setup allowed optode placement to follow established 10–20 system landmarks while ensuring consistent source–detector geometry across participants.

Four cortical regions of interest (ROIs) were defined a priori: left frontal, right frontal, left parietal, and right parietal. Frontal ROIs were defined to cover the middle-to-inferior frontal cortical regions, whereas parietal ROIs covered the postcentral-to-inferior parietal cortical regions, including the supramarginal gyrus. Assignment of fNIRS channels to ROIs was based on the predefined probe geometry and the correspondence between optode positions and underlying cortical regions as determined by the international 10–20 cap layout. Each ROI comprised an equal number of fNIRS measurement channels, with six channels assigned to each frontal ROI and five channels assigned to each parietal ROI, resulting in a total of 22 channels included in the ROI-based analyses.

fNIRS data preprocessing

fNIRS data were analyzed using MNE (version 1.11.0) and MNE-NIRS (version 0.7.1). Raw light intensity signals were first converted to optical density. Channels with poor scalp coupling were identified using the scalp coupling index and excluded from further analysis if the index was below 0.7 (Pollonini et al., 2014). Motion-related artifacts were corrected using the temporal derivative distribution repair (TDDR) method (Fishburn et al., 2019). After motion correction, optical density signals were converted to changes in hemoglobin concentration using the modified Beer–Lambert law with a partial pathlength factor of 0.1 (Luke et al., 2021). The resulting hemoglobin concentration signals were band-pass filtered between 0.02 and 0.3 Hz to attenuate slow drifts and high-frequency physiological noise. Filtered signals were then segmented into epochs spanning from 2-s before to 15-s after encoding onset. Baseline correction was applied to each epoch by subtracting the mean signal value during the −2 to 0 s pre-onset period. To further exclude residual artifacts, individual trials were rejected if the peak-to-peak amplitude of the HbO signal exceeded 80 μmol/L. For each subject, a minimum of two valid trials per load level was retained for subsequent analyses. After trial rejection, the final number of valid epochs retained for analysis was as follows. In the non-random group (n = 12), 56, 52, and 54 epochs were retained for the 4-, 5-, and 6-digit conditions, respectively. In the random group (n = 17), 78, 80, and 73 epochs were retained for the corresponding load levels. HbO feature extraction

Within each ROI, HbO signals were averaged across channels. Mean HbO amplitude was computed for each epoch within a time window from 4 to 8 s after encoding onset, corresponding to the expected peak of the hemodynamic response (Pinti et al., 2020). This specific interval has been validated in previous literature as an effective feature for characterizing task-related cortical hemodynamic responses while minimizing the impact of physiological noise (Naseer and Hong, 2015; Muñoz et al., 2023). To minimize contamination from speech- and movement-related physiological activity, fNIRS analyses focused exclusively on encoding-related HbO responses, with feature extraction restricted to a 4–8 s window following encoding onset and prior to the verbal retrieval phase. This temporal separation was a deliberate design decision intended to isolate encoding-related hemodynamic responses from overt motor or speech-related responses. For the primary analyses, mean HbO amplitudes were averaged across valid epochs within each load level, yielding a single HbO estimate per subject, ROI, and load level. In addition, trial-level mean HbO amplitudes were retained to allow modeling of trial-wise effects in supplementary analyses.

Statistical data analysis

Subject characteristics and group comparisons

Demographic characteristics and overall task performance scores were compared between the random and non-random groups. Sex was compared using Fisher's exact test, whereas age, years of education, and overall task performance scores were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Missing data were present for years of education (n = 2) and handedness (n = 1) and were handled using pairwise exclusion.

Task performance analysis

Load-related effects on task accuracy were assessed using nonparametric Friedman tests. Analyses were performed for the full sample and separately within the random and non-random groups. Bonferroni correction was applied to control for multiple subgroup comparisons.

Statistical analysis of HbO responses

HbO responses were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models with subject-specific random intercepts to account for repeated measurements across load levels and ROIs. Random intercept models were selected to account for baseline between-subject variability while maintaining model stability given the small sample size. Working memory load was modeled as a continuous variable (0, 1, and 2 corresponding to 4, 5, and 6 digits, respectively) to test linear trends in HbO responses with increasing task demands.

To examine load-dependent effects across ROIs, we first fitted models including working memory load, ROI, and group as fixed effects. These models were used to examine (i) the main effect of load across ROIs and (ii) the Load × ROI interaction to assess regional variation in load-related slopes.

To evaluate whether load-related changes differed as a function of task structure, we next tested a Load × Group interaction while controlling for ROI effects. To examine whether Load × Group interaction effects could be explained by sequential progression across trials (time-on-task effects), a sensitivity analysis was conducted by including trial index as an additional covariate in the mixed-effects model. Trial index was defined as the chronological order of memorization trials within each subject and was centered within subjects prior to inclusion in the model to reduce collinearity with load progression. The Load × Group interaction was re-evaluated while adjusting for the trial index.

To address concerns regarding the influence of consistently low-performing participants, participants who scored fewer than three correct responses across all load conditions were excluded, and the analyses of the Load × Group interaction and ROI-specific load effects were repeated using the reduced sample. Results of this analysis are reported in the Supplementary Materials.

To further characterize task structure–dependent effects at the regional level, ROI-specific mixed-effects models were fitted separately within the random and non-random groups to characterize group-wise load-related slopes. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons across ROIs. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons between load levels were conducted using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests within each group and ROI, with p values corrected for multiple comparisons across the three pairwise contrasts using the Bonferroni method.

All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed in Python (version 3.10) using the scipy. stats (version 1.15.2) and statsmodels (version 0.14.6) libraries.

Specifically, HbO responses in the random group exhibited a steeper increase with increasing working memory load (slope: β = 2.48), whereas no systematic load-related change was observed in the non-random group (slope: β = 0.01). These findings indicate that load-dependent increases in HbO responses depended on task structure rather than reflecting a uniform effect across subjects.

To further evaluate the robustness of the Load × Group interaction, we conducted a sensitivity analysis including trial index as a covariate. The Load × Group interaction remained statistically significant (β = 1.51, SE = 0.73, z = 2.09, p = 0.037), whereas the trial index itself was not significant (p = 0.165), indicating that the Load × Group interaction was not attributable to sequential trial progression. Additional sensitivity analyses excluding low-performing participants yielded comparable results (see Supplementary Materials).

To determine whether task structure–dependent load effects differed across regions, we further evaluated a Load × Group × ROI interaction. The interaction did not reach statistical significance, suggesting that the magnitude of the group difference in load-related slopes did not differ reliably across ROIs. Together, these results demonstrate that task structure modulated load-dependent encoding-phase HbO responses, with the most robust and statistically reliable group difference observed in the left frontal cortex.

Discussion

In summary, fronto-parietal encoding-phase HbO responses measured by fNIRS increased linearly with working memory load, as indexed by digit span length. Moreover, the magnitude of this load-related increase differed as a function of task structure, with stronger load sensitivity observed in the random group and a region-specific group difference most evident in the left frontal cortex. These neural effects were observed in the absence of corresponding load-related changes in behavioral accuracy.

Our findings demonstrate a linear increase in HbO responses across the fronto-parietal cortex with increasing working memory load. The absence of a significant Load × ROI interaction suggests that load-related changes in HbO responses were broadly comparable across the frontal and parietal ROIs, rather than being confined to a single cortical area. This global recruitment pattern is consistent with previous fNIRS research by Fishburn et al. (2014), which reported that bilateral frontal activation scales linearly with working memory load and that functional connectivity, particularly within the fronto-parietal and interhemispheric networks, increases as task demands escalate. In addition, Meidenbauer et al. (2021) demonstrated reliably greater fronto-parietal activation during a 2-back task compared with a 1-back task, indicating that fronto-parietal hemodynamic responses measured by fNIRS are sensitive to graded differences in cognitive load. Evidence from a recent multimodal EEG–fNIRS study further showed that increasing working memory load is associated with enhanced fronto-parietal hemodynamic responses and strengthened functional connectivity, alongside load-dependent modulation of electrophysiological activity, suggesting coordinated neural and vascular responses within the working memory network (Wang et al., 2024). Furthermore, a meta-analysis of 42 fMRI studies demonstrated that verbal working memory consistently recruits a widely distributed network encompassing frontal and parietal cortices, as well as subcortical and cerebellar regions (Emch et al., 2019). The lack of regional differentiation in load-related slopes in the present study aligns with this network-level account, suggesting that increasing task demands are consistent with engagement of a large-scale fronto-parietal system rather than isolated regional specialization (Owen et al., 2005; Fedorenko et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2024). Together, these coherent load-related increases in encoding-phase HbO responses across the fronto-parietal cortex likely reflect the integrated engagement of executive control and attentional resources and further underscore the utility of fNIRS as a sensitive modality for capturing the distributed hemodynamic dynamics associated with increasing cognitive effort (Izzetoglu et al., 2004; Ayaz et al., 2012; Herff et al., 2014; Pinti et al., 2020; Geissler et al., 2020; Chenot et al., 2021).

Beyond load magnitude, our findings indicate that load-related changes in fronto-parietal encoding-phase HbO responses depend on task structure, specifically the predictability of difficulty progression. Although both groups performed the same digit span task with identical load levels, load-related increases in HbO responses were observed only in the random group, with the clearest group difference evident in the left frontal region. This pattern suggests that unpredictability in task structure alters how neural resources are allocated as cognitive demands increase. Converging evidence from electrophysiological studies similarly suggests that the neural benefits of predictability are load-dependent, with unpredictable task contexts eliciting greater neural responses and increased control demands under higher cognitive load (Lui et al., 2025). Notably, these neural differences emerged despite comparable behavioral accuracy across groups. From a control-strategy perspective, the random task structure may have relied on a more reactive, trial-by-trial scaling of cognitive control due to the lack of advance information about upcoming task difficulty (Braver, 2012). In contrast, the predictable task structure in the non-random group may have enabled anticipatory or proactive allocation of cognitive resources through top-down biasing signals that facilitate processing of anticipated task demands, thereby attenuating the need for incremental neural scaling across loads (Miller and Cohen, 2001; Braver, 2012). Such a dissociation between neural responses and overt performance suggests that fNIRS-derived hemodynamic measures are sensitive to latent differences in control strategies and neural effort not captured by accuracy alone. This interpretation is consistent with theoretical frameworks distinguishing proactive and reactive control modes, in which predictable task contexts allow top-down regulation and resource optimization, whereas unpredictable contexts necessitate more immediate, stimulus-driven adjustments. The persistence of the Load × Group interaction after controlling for trial index further suggests that the observed effect cannot be explained solely by simple time-on-task or sequential progression across trials. Although this additional analysis supports the interpretation that task structure contributes to load-dependent encoding-phase hemodynamic responses, the fixed-order structure of the non-random condition limits the ability to completely separate task structure effects from potential time-dependent influences such as habituation or cognitive fatigue.

In addition, several methodological considerations related to the physiological specificity of fNIRS signals should be acknowledged. Because short-separation channels (∼1 cm) were not available in the optode configuration, superficial physiological signals associated with autonomic or vascular responses may have contributed to the observed HbO patterns (Gagnon et al., 2012). This issue may be particularly relevant in the present study because differences in task structure and unpredictability could potentially influence systemic physiological states in addition to task-evoked cortical hemodynamics (Yücel et al., 2021). Although several preprocessing procedures were applied to improve signal quality and reduce motion-related and low-frequency physiological noise, these approaches cannot fully dissociate cortical activity from extracerebral contributions in the absence of short-separation regression (Zhou et al., 2020; Klein et al., 2023). Therefore, the present findings should not be interpreted as direct evidence of cortical-specific neural activity, but rather as task-related hemodynamic response patterns observed under the current experimental conditions. Future studies incorporating short-separation channels and additional physiological monitoring will be important for more specifically isolating cortical responses associated with working memory load during encoding and task predictability.

The present analyses focused on encoding-related hemodynamic responses and therefore did not capture retrieval-phase hemodynamic responses that may also exhibit load-related modulation. This analytical choice was made to reduce potential contamination from overt speech production and motion-related artifacts during the verbal recall phase, thereby providing a more conservative estimate of task-evoked hemodynamic modulation under the present experimental conditions (Zhang et al., 2022). Nevertheless, because working memory demands may extend beyond encoding into retrieval-related processes, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting strictly encoding-phase hemodynamic responses rather than the full spectrum of working memory processing. Future studies incorporating experimental designs optimized for retrieval-phase analysis and speech-related artifact correction may help more comprehensively characterize the neural and hemodynamic dynamics associated with verbal working memory performance.

The observation that task structure–dependent load effects were most evident in the left frontal region invites further functional interpretation. Given the absence of a significant Load × Group × ROI interaction, the following ROI-specific patterns are interpreted descriptively rather than as evidence of differential regional effects. In the mixed-effects analysis, a significant Group × Load interaction was observed only in the left frontal ROI, indicating that the magnitude of load-related HbO changes differed between groups specifically in this region. Complementary group-wise analyses further showed that, within the random group, positive load-related slopes were observed in both the left frontal and left parietal regions after correction for multiple comparisons. These findings suggest that although the clearest between-group divergence emerged in the left frontal region, load-dependent scaling within the random condition extended to adjacent left-lateralized regions.

Given the verbal nature of the digit span task, which places demands on phonological storage, articulatory rehearsal, and executive control, left-lateralized frontal engagement is consistent with established models of verbal working memory (Paulesu et al., 1993; Baddeley, 2012; Rottschy et al., 2012; De Tollis et al., 2021). Prior neuroimaging studies have similarly shown that verbal working memory preferentially recruits left-hemispheric frontal and parietal regions, reflecting the integration of language-related processing (Nagel et al., 2013). Within this framework, the heightened left frontal sensitivity to task unpredictability observed here may reflect increased engagement of frontal control processes associated with verbal working memory when difficulty progression cannot be anticipated. Although the frontal ROI encompassed regions adjacent to classical language areas, the spatial resolution of fNIRS precludes precise localization or attribution of the observed effects to language-specific mechanisms. Rather, the enhanced sensitivity of the left frontal region to randomized task structure likely reflects engagement of a broader verbal working memory control network integrating executive monitoring, updating, and rehearsal processes (Baddeley, 2012; Paulesu et al., 1993). Taken together, these findings suggest that task unpredictability is associated with greater load-related neural activity, highlighting the need to consider task structure when interpreting neural markers of cognitive load.

The present results highlight the utility of fNIRS for sensitive cognitive neurophysiological assessment by demonstrating its ability to capture context-dependent encoding-phase hemodynamic responses that are not reflected in overt behavioral performance. Consistent with prior work showing that fNIRS-derived prefrontal activity can effectively quantify mental workload and neural efficiency (Causse et al., 2017), the observed hemodynamic patterns indicate that fNIRS can reveal subtle variations in neural effort during encoding as a function of task structure and predictability. By detecting load-related changes in encoding-phase neural activity under conditions of stable behavioral accuracy, fNIRS provides a complementary perspective on neural demands of working memory encoding beyond performance-based measures. This methodological sensitivity supports the use of fNIRS for assessing neural efficiency, with potential relevance for diverse applications such as training optimization and the assessment of cognitive reserve in clinical populations (Curtin et al., 2019; Liao et al., 2020).

The present study was designed to examine relative differences in load-dependent HbO modulation associated with task structure within a healthy older adult cohort, rather than to characterize age-related neurovascular mechanisms. Because a younger comparison group was not included, the present findings cannot establish whether the observed hemodynamic response profiles are specific to cognitively healthy older adults or reflect more general patterns of working memory–related HbO modulation. Aging is known to influence neurovascular coupling and baseline hemodynamic characteristics, which may influence the magnitude and temporal dynamics of fNIRS responses (D'Esposito et al., 2003; Fabiani et al., 2014; Causse et al., 2019; Turner et al., 2022). Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted specifically within the context of cognitively healthy older adults. Future studies incorporating younger comparison groups and longitudinal designs may help clarify whether the observed task structure–dependent HbO modulation reflects aging-related adaptive responses or more general mechanisms of working memory encoding.

Focusing on cognitively healthy older adults allowed us to characterize load-related hemodynamic modulation in the absence of overt cognitive impairment. The observed dissociation between stable behavioral performance and robust hemodynamic scaling suggests that fNIRS captures subtle variations in neural resource allocation that remain undetected by performance measures alone. Consistent with this observation, a related fNIRS study by Ranchod et al. (2023), which compared young adults (18–27 years) and older adults (64–84 years), reported age-related differences in prefrontal hemodynamic responses despite comparable task accuracy across age groups. These findings suggest that fNIRS provides complementary information about task-related neural engagement beyond that captured by behavioral metrics. Establishing such response patterns in cognitively healthy older adults provides an essential reference for future studies examining how load sensitivity may be altered in pathological aging (Llana et al., 2022).

The between-subjects design was selected to avoid order and carryover effects that would arise if participants were exposed to both task structures. A within-subject design would risk contaminating the manipulation of interest in this study. Nevertheless, this design increases sensitivity to inter-individual variability, and the small and unequal group sizes (n = 12, n = 17) limit statistical power and the stability of group-specific load effect estimates. To address this, linear mixed-effects models with subject-specific random intercepts were used, such that the estimated group difference reflects load-related slopes within individuals rather than absolute between-subject differences in HbO amplitude. The present findings should be nonetheless interpreted cautiously and validated in future studies using larger and more balanced cohorts.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that fronto-parietal encoding-phase hemodynamic responses measured by fNIRS increase linearly with working memory load in cognitively healthy older adults, even in the absence of corresponding changes in behavioral accuracy. Load-related increases in HbO responses were observed across frontal and parietal regions, consistent with a distributed fronto-parietal engagement during the encoding phase of the verbal working memory task. Importantly, the magnitude of load-dependent neural responses was modulated by task structure. Randomized presentation of memory load was associated with steeper load-related HbO increases during encoding, with the most robust group difference observed in the left frontal region. These findings indicate that fNIRS is sensitive to context-dependent modulation of encoding-phase neural engagement that may not be captured by behavioral performance measures alone.
