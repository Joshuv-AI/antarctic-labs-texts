Open surgical management of multiple mycotic superior mesenteric artery aneurysms following staphylococcal infective endocarditis
Authors: Mohammed M Chowdhury, Ali Navi, Andrew Winterbottom, Manjit S Gohel, Simon M Kreckler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMycotic superior mesenteric artery (SMA) aneurysms are rare but carry a substantial risk of rupture, bowel ischemia, and mortality. Their management is particularly challenging in the setting of multifocal disease involving major mesenteric branches. We present the case of a 32-year-old man with a history of intravenous drug use and recurrent infective endocarditis who developed multiple mycotic SMA aneurysms secondary to Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia. Following emergency mechanical aortic valve replacement, left ventricular outflow tract abscess repair, and prolonged antimicrobial therapy, surveillance imaging demonstrated three persistent patent SMA aneurysms. The patient underwent open surgical repair through a midline laparotomy. Two distal jejunal and ileal branch aneurysms were excised and ligated. The proximal complex aneurysm involved multiple jejunal branches and the right colic artery and was reconstructed using autologous internal iliac artery patch repair. This report outlines the operative strategy and multidisciplinary decision-making involved in the management of these challenging multifocal mycotic SMA aneurysms. Visceral artery aneurysms are uncommon, with superior mesenteric artery (SMA) aneurysms accounting for approximately 5% to 8% of all visceral aneurysms. Mycotic SMA aneurysms remain particularly rare but are associated with significant morbidity and mortality because of the risk of rupture, distal septic embolization, and bowel infarction. Infective endocarditis remains one of the principal underlying etiologies, particularly in patients with intravenous drug use.

The management of mycotic SMA aneurysms is complex and must consider aneurysm morphology, branch involvement, ongoing infection, patient comorbidity, and the risk of mesenteric ischemia. Endovascular techniques including embolization and covered stent placement have increasingly been described; however, open surgical repair remains essential in patients with multifocal disease, extensive branch involvement, or concern regarding persistent infection.

In this report, we present the operative management of three mycotic SMA aneurysms in a young patient with recent staphylococcal infective endocarditis requiring mechanical aortic valve replacement.

Case report and operative technique

A 32-year-old man was transferred to the tertiary vascular center for management of multiple mycotic SMA aneurysms. His past medical history included tricuspid valve infective endocarditis in 2023 managed medically, recurrent deep venous thrombosis previously treated with apixaban, borderline personality disorder, depression, opioid dependence, and previous drug overdose. He had a significant history of intravenous drug use and smoked both cigarettes and cannabis.

He initially presented to his local hospital with fever and a right groin abscess in the context of Staphylococcus aureus bacteremia. Subsequent investigations demonstrated aortic valve infective endocarditis requiring emergency mechanical aortic valve replacement, left ventricular outflow tract abscess repair in 3 months before referral and review in the tertiary vascular unit.

Computed tomography angiography performed during this admission demonstrated multiple saccular aneurysms involving the SMA and its branches (Fig 1). Following cardiac surgery, the patient completed 6 weeks of intravenous antimicrobial therapy consisting of daptomycin, fosfomycin, and rifampicin as guided by the local microbiology team. Anticoagulation was transitioned from treatment dose low-molecular-weight heparin to warfarin because of the mechanical valve prosthesis.

The patient underwent repeated multidisciplinary discussion involving local vascular surgery, cardiothoracic surgery, microbiology, and interventional radiology. Positron emission tomography-computed tomography demonstrated no definitive evidence of metabolically active septic emboli. Endovascular embolization was initially considered due to the presence of a single feeding vessel to one aneurysm sac. However, interval imaging demonstrated persistent multifocal patent aneurysms with extensive branch involvement, and concerns remained regarding mesenteric perfusion following exclusion of the aneurysms. The patient was therefore referred and transferred to the tertiary vascular unit for consideration of definitive open surgical management.

Operative technique

Following induction of general anesthesia, a standard midline laparotomy was performed. Exploration confirmed three separate mycotic aneurysms involving the SMA distribution, correlating with preoperative imaging.

The first and largest aneurysm arose from the proximal SMA below the level of the pancreas and extended approximately 4 to 5 cm, involving multiple jejunal branches and the right colic artery. Two additional distal aneurysms involved SMA branches supplying the distal jejunum and mid ileum, respectively (Fig 2).

Intraoperative view of the two distal superior mesenteric artery (SMA) aneurysms before resection.

The right internal iliac artery was exposed from the iliac bifurcation to its distal divisions and harvested as a potential autologous conduit for mesenteric reconstruction. Autologous venous conduit was considered unsuitable because the superficial veins of both the upper and lower limbs were of poor caliber with evidence of previous superficial thrombophlebitis and scaring, with additional evidence of previous deep venous thrombosis. The internal iliac artery was therefore selected because of its favorable size match and reduced risk of reinfection within a contaminated field.

Alternative conduit options were also considered intraoperatively, including the use of bovine pericardium as a prosthetic patch or tubular conduit; however, in view of the infected field and the preference to minimize prosthetic material where possible, an autologous arterial option was prioritized.

The vessel was harvested following ligation with Ethibond sutures (Ethicon) and vascular clips. The ureter was identified and protected throughout the dissection. Although initially considered as a potential tube interposition conduit, the internal iliac artery was ultimately used as a patch for reconstruction rather than a tubular graft, due to the geometry of the defect and the need to incorporate multiple branch vessels into a single reconstructive unit.

Attention was first directed toward the two distal aneurysms. These were sharply dissected, with proximal and distal control achieved before aneurysm excision. Dissection was undertaken tightly on the aneurysm through one side of the mesentery, thus preserving as many vessels and collaterals as possible. Only the immediate inflow and outflow vessels feeding the aneurysm were ligated and the mesenteric defects repaired using 3-0 Vicryl sutures.

The proximal SMA aneurysm was approached through a lateral exposure, below the level of the pancreas. The duodenum was mobilized caudally and the ligament of Treitz divided to facilitate exposure of the proximal SMA. The inflow vessel was dissected and controlled, followed by identification of the distal outflow and associated jejunal branches. The right colic artery was ligated.

Systemic intravenous heparin was administered before clamping the inflow and outflow vessels. The aneurysm sac was opened and hemostasis secured. Reconstruction was performed using the harvested internal iliac artery as an autologous patch to reconstruct the SMA defect while incorporating the jejunal branch vessels and distal outflow as a single patch repair (Fig 3).

Intraoperative view of proximal superior mesenteric artery (SMA) aneurysm with associated branches (left) and view post-internal iliac artery patch reconstruction (right).

Following restoration of mesenteric perfusion, a short segment of small bowel corresponding to the territory supplied by the second excised aneurysm appeared transiently ischemic, although the remaining bowel appeared well perfused. After a further 20 minutes of observation, all bowel appeared viable and no bowel resection was required.

Samples from the aneurysm sacs were sent for microbiological analysis. Abdominal closure was performed using looped Polydioxanone for the fascia and 3-0 Monocryl to skin. Rectus sheath catheters were placed for postoperative analgesia.

Postoperatively, the patient was admitted to the high-dependency unit for close hemodynamic and metabolic monitoring because of the risk of mesenteric ischemia. Nasogastric decompression was maintained with gradual reintroduction of oral intake. The long-term intravenous antimicrobial therapy was continued following multidisciplinary microbiology review due to the underlying infective endocarditis and mechanical prosthetic valve. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 12, with normal bowel function and no further surgical intervention.

Discussion

Mycotic aneurysms involving the SMA are rare vascular pathologies but remain clinically significant because of the associated risks of rupture, sepsis, and catastrophic mesenteric ischemia. SMA aneurysms account for approximately 5% to 8% of all visceral artery aneurysms, with infective endocarditis representing one of the principal underlying etiologies of mycotic degeneration. Septic embolization to the mesenteric circulation can result in arterial wall destruction, pseudoaneurysm formation, and progressive aneurysmal degeneration.1,2

The management of mycotic SMA aneurysms remains controversial and must be individualized according to aneurysm morphology, branch involvement, patient comorbidity, and ongoing infective burden. Endovascular techniques, including coil embolization and covered stent placement, have increasingly been described, particularly in hemodynamically unstable patients or those unsuitable for laparotomy.3,4 However, these strategies may be limited in the setting of multifocal disease, extensive branch involvement, or concern regarding persistent infection.

In the present case, the largest aneurysm involved a long proximal SMA segment incorporating multiple jejunal branches and the right colic artery. Endovascular exclusion would therefore have carried a substantial risk of bowel infarction due to compromise of mesenteric branch perfusion. Furthermore, the patient had recently undergone emergency surgery for staphylococcal infective endocarditis with mechanical valve replacement, raising additional concerns regarding residual infected tissue and the long-term durability of prosthetic material within a contaminated field.

Open reconstruction therefore offered several important advantages, including direct debridement of infected tissue, complete aneurysm excision, preservation of mesenteric perfusion, and avoidance of prosthetic graft material. Autologous internal iliac artery was selected as the reconstructive conduit because of its favorable size match, resistance to reinfection, and ability to facilitate complex patch reconstruction incorporating multiple branch vessels. Autologous conduit use has previously been advocated in infected vascular fields because of reduced reinfection risk compared with synthetic reconstruction.5,6 The choice of using the internal iliac artery was based on its availability and the requirement for a short patch. For longer tube reconstruction, alternative autologous grafts like the great saphenous vein could be considered.

A significant challenge in the operative management of these lesions is accurate assessment of bowel viability following mesenteric reconstruction. Extensive multidisciplinary planning involving vascular surgery, intestinal transplantation, microbiology, cardiothoracic surgery, and interventional radiology teams was therefore essential in this case.

The role of multidisciplinary discussion was central to the management in this patient. Initial consideration had been given to embolization following vascular multidisciplinary team review, particularly as one aneurysm demonstrated a single feeding vessel. However, interval imaging confirmed persistent multifocal patent aneurysms with complex branch involvement, ultimately favoring definitive open reconstruction.

We believe that open surgical repair remains an important treatment modality for complex mycotic SMA aneurysms despite increasing endovascular experience. Careful patient selection, aggressive antimicrobial therapy, meticulous preservation of mesenteric perfusion, and multidisciplinary operative planning are critical to achieving favorable outcomes in these technically challenging cases.

Conclusions

Multiple mycotic SMA aneurysms represent a rare and technically challenging vascular pathology associated with substantial risks of rupture and mesenteric ischemia. This case highlights the importance of multidisciplinary decision-making and careful operative planning in patients with complex branch involvement following infective endocarditis. Although endovascular techniques may be appropriate in selected cases, open surgical excision and autologous reconstruction remain valuable treatment strategies where preservation of mesenteric perfusion and eradication of infected tissue are paramount.
