The non-scaffold effects of natural polysaccharides in promoting organ regeneration: a review
Authors: Sheng-ping Lei, Pan Chang, Li Li, Yan Niu, Jun Yu, Jian-li Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Natural polysaccharides are known for their scaffold-based roles in tissue engineering, but their non-scaffold bioactivities in promoting organ regeneration remain poorly understood.This review aims to systematically summarizes the latest research advances in natural polysaccharides promoting organ regeneration through non-scaffold mechanisms, exploring their multifaceted actions, including creating a pro-regenerative microenvironment (e.g., immune regulation, extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, and gut microbiota-mediated short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)), regulating stem cell proliferation and differentiation, promoting angiogenesis, and other effects like epigenetic regulation, antioxidant activity, and mitochondrial function modulation. Also outline their broad application potential in the regeneration and repair of multiple organ systems, including skin, heart, nerves, liver, and intestine. Furthermore, we discuss current challenges and future development directions, aiming to effectively exploit the therapeutic potential of natural polysaccharides in regenerative medicine and provide novel strategies for addressing organ repair challenges.

Introduction

Organ regeneration refers to the process by which a biological organism, following organ damage or partial loss, achieves complete restoration of the damaged organ’s structure and function through cellular proliferation, differentiation, and tissue remodeling. This process distinctly differs from ordinary wound healing, which is typically accompanied by scar formation and fails to restore original functionality [1]. In nature, regenerative capacity varies significantly across species and organ types: lower animals, such as planarians and salamanders, can achieve complete regeneration of limbs or organs, whereas mammals (including humans) exhibit limited regenerative potential, primarily relying on mechanisms such as stem cell activation, cellular differentiation, or compensatory hyperplasia [2]. Natural polysaccharides are a class of high-molecular-weight carbohydrates derived from plants, animals, microorganisms, or marine organisms, including chitosan, alginate, hyaluronic acid, heparin, fucoidan, and cellulose [3]. These polysaccharides possess excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, low immunogenicity, and properties that mimic the ECM, rendering them pivotal in the field of organ regeneration [4].

The mechanisms by which natural polysaccharides contribute to organ and tissue regeneration primarily manifest in two forms: scaffold effects and non-scaffold effects. The scaffold effect involves their use as biomaterials to construct three-dimensional physical support structures that simulate the natural ECM, providing a microenvironment for cell attachment, migration, proliferation, and differentiation [5]. In contrast, non-scaffold effects refer to their application in soluble forms (e.g., solutions, nanoparticles, coatings, or injections) to modulate cellular signaling, immune microenvironments, and tissue remodeling without macroscopic scaffolds [6]. Emerging evidence shows that, even without acting as physical scaffolds, polysaccharides can actively promote repair through multiple non-scaffold mechanisms (Fig. 1), including immune regulation of cell signaling [7], antioxidant activity [8], ECM remodeling [9], gut microbiota-mediated SCFAs [10], epigenetic regulation [11] and pro-angiogenesis [12]. Potential workflows could integrate protein-focused predictors for protein–glycan complexes with carbohydrate-specialized builders and emerging glycan ML models (e.g., GLYCAM/CHARMM-GUI and recent machine-learning glycan frameworks). Although multiple reviews have examined natural polysaccharides as scaffold materials for tissue engineering, fewer have explicitly examined soluble (non-scaffold) bioactivities, their molecular mechanisms, and the degree to which preclinical results have advanced to clinical evidence. Therefore, this review is deliberately scoped to soluble, non-scaffold applications (e.g., injections, coatings, oligosaccharides, nanoparticles and other non-macroscopic forms) and asks: Which soluble natural polysaccharides show reproducible non-scaffold regenerative effects, what are the dominant molecular mechanisms, and how strong is the preclinical versus clinical evidence? To answer this we carried out a targeted literature search across PubMed, Web of Science and Scopus for articles published up to December 1, 2025 using keywords including “polysaccharide”, “non-scaffold”, “soluble”, “regeneration”, “angiogenesis”, “immunomodulation” and organ-specific terms.

Literature search

Although this review is narrative in scope, we conducted and report a transparent literature search to improve reproducibility and enable readers to evaluate coverage and potential bias. We searched PubMed (MEDLINE), Web of Science and Scopus for articles published between January 1, 2000 and December 1, 2025. Searches combined controlled terms and free text for polysaccharides and regeneration; an example PubMed search string was:

(“polysaccharide”[Title/Abstract] OR “polysaccharides”[Title/Abstract] OR “polysaccharide”[MeSH Terms] OR “glycan”[Title/Abstract] OR “oligosaccharide”[Title/Abstract])

AND

(“regeneration”[Title/Abstract] OR “regenerative”[Title/Abstract] OR “tissue repair”[Title/Abstract] OR “organ regeneration”[Title/Abstract] OR “wound healing”[Title/Abstract])

Where relevant we added specific polysaccharide names as secondary terms (for example: chitosan, hyaluronic acid, alginate, fucoidan, β-glucan, heparin). Searches were performed between 01 November and 05 December 2025. Reference lists of included reviews and relevant primary studies were hand-searched to identify additional records.

Inclusion criteria: (1) original research (in vitro, in vivo, clinical) or reviews that report non-scaffold (soluble, nanoparticle, coating, injectable or biochemical signaling) effects of natural polysaccharides on processes relevant to organ/tissue regeneration (for example: immunomodulation, angiogenesis, stem cell regulation, ECM remodeling, antioxidant/mitochondrial effects); (2) articles in English; and (3) publication within the stated date range.

Exclusion criteria: (1) studies that discuss polysaccharides only as macroscopic structural scaffolds (scaffold-only materials) without reporting non-scaffold biochemical or cellular effects; (2) editorials or commentaries without original data or mechanistic discussion; and (3) articles not available in full text. If a key non-English article was identified (by title/abstract), we sought translation or English summaries; none met inclusion criteria.

Of the 278 records initially identified, 18 were excluded due to inaccessible full texts. Based on the information available in their titles and abstracts, we assessed the potential relevance of these records: six were rated as having high potential relevance, primarily concerning the immunomodulatory mechanisms of polysaccharides; seven were considered of moderate potential relevance, touching upon related but non‑core topics; and five were deemed of low potential relevance, showing weak association with the main theme.

Sources of natural polysaccharides

In the process of drug development and biomolecular design, active drug metabolites or synthetic intermediates are occasionally discovered serendipitously or identified during the isolation of natural products. Natural compounds have attracted considerable scientific interest owing to their ready availability and diverse therapeutic properties. Polysaccharides, which are ubiquitous in natural compounds, can be categorized into homopolysaccharides and heteropolysaccharides based on their monomeric composition. Homopolysaccharides consist of a single monosaccharide type, while heteropolysaccharides comprise multiple monosaccharide varieties [7–9].

These polysaccharides exhibit molecular weights ranging from 2.6 kDa [10] to 8854 kDa [11], with most being branched heteroglycans featuring varied architectures, such as mixed α/β glycosidic linkages, terminal xylose residues, and acidic moieties. Non-scaffold effects and its mechanisms of natural polysaccharides

Construction of pro-regenerative microenvironment

The engineering of a pro-regenerative microenvironment by natural polysaccharides hinges on the synergistic interplay of multiple mechanisms, including immune microenvironment modulation, ECM remodeling, and metabolism regulation mediated by the gut microbiota-mediated SCFAs, which collectively foster a favorable milieu capable of suppressing inflammation, supporting stem cell function, and promoting functional tissue reconstruction.

Immune microenvironment modulation

Evidence summary: Immunomodulatory/macrophage effects

Increasing evidence demonstrates that these polysaccharides exert immunomodulatory effects by restoring the balance between pro-and anti-inflammatory mediators through multi-target modulation [46]. Their therapeutic potential has been noted across various inflammatory disorders, including acute liver injury and inflammatory bowel disease. One representative example is heparin and its analogues: surface plasmon resonance and binding studies demonstrate that heparin binds HMGB1 with nanomolar affinity and changes HMGB1 conformation, thereby reducing HMGB1 receptor binding and downstream cytokine induction in cultured macrophages and in experimental models [47, 47–50]. In experimental models of acute liver injury, heparin administration not only reduced serum aminotransferases and histopathological liver damage, but also markedly decreased TNF-α levels, while promoting IL-10 secretion [51–54]. Similarly, in dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced ulcerative colitis (UC) models, fucoidan supplementation decreased disease activity scores by more than 50%, alleviated colonic inflammation, and improved comorbid anxiety- and depression-like behaviors [55, 56]. In addition, marine-derived fucoidan has been shown to inhibit the activation of the NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome, reducing downstream processing and secretion of IL-1β and IL-18, two central mediators in intestinal inflammation and neuroimmune dysfunction [57, 58]. Taken together, natural polysaccharides dual regulatory capacity distinguishes them from many conventional single-target anti-inflammatory drugs [46].

ECM remodeling

Natural polysaccharides, such as HA, chitosan, alginate, and chondroitin sulfate, play a pivotal role in organ regeneration by regulating the activity of enzymes associated with ECM remodeling metabolism, inhibiting pathological fibrosis, and promoting functional matrix deposition. Evidence summary: ECM remodeling/antifibrosis

Although the application of natural polysaccharides in ECM remodeling holds broad promise, it still faces several challenges, including insufficient biocompatibility and controllability of degradation rates, which may lead to inconsistent in vivo outcomes; barriers to clinical translation, such as manufacturing complexities and regulatory requirements; and limited understanding of precise molecular mechanisms, which can trigger unintended inflammatory responses [69]. Future directions should focus on chemical modifications and composite material designs, such as incorporating growth factors or nanoparticles to optimize enzyme regulation and fibrosis inhibition; integrating with 3D printing and stem cell technologies to develop personalized regenerative strategies; and conducting large-scale clinical trials to validate safety and efficacy, thereby advancing the transition from basic research to clinical applications [65].

Evidence summary: gut microbiota/SCFA-mediated regeneration

Despite the promising prospects of natural polysaccharides as prebiotics in gut microbiota modulation and regeneration, several challenges remain. These include inter-individual variability in microbiota responses leading to inconsistent therapeutic outcomes, difficulties in standardizing dosage and bioavailability due to structural complexity, limited understanding of long-term safety and potential side effects (e.g., bloating or microbiota dysbiosis in susceptible populations), and translational barriers from preclinical to clinical settings constrained by regulatory requirements for purity and efficacy validation [77]. Evidence summary: stem cell proliferation & differentiation/epigenetics

Astragalus polysaccharide (APS) and chitosan oligosaccharide (COS) play significant roles in modulating stem cell proliferation and differentiation, with emerging evidence highlighting their therapeutic potential in regenerative medicine. APS enhances the proliferation and osteogenic differentiation of human bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (hBMSCs) by modulating cell cycle regulators (e.g., cyclin D1, p21) [90]. It also promotes neural stem cell differentiation into oligodendrocytes, mitigates neuroinflammation, and demonstrates neuroprotective potential [91]. Furthermore, APS restores the function of bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) and hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) by modulating the Hippo pathway, correcting immune dysfunction in damaged microenvironments. It also aids gastrointestinal recovery by promoting the regeneration of intestinal stem cells (ISCs) after irradiation [92].

Similarly, COS enhances the proliferation and survival of ovarian germ stem cells (OGSCs), improving ovarian function and immune response after chemotherapy [93]. It maintains intestinal homeostasis by promoting ISC proliferation and differentiation [94]. In wound healing, COS accelerates tissue repair by stimulating fibroblast activity, extracellular matrix remodeling, and epithelial cell migration, particularly in diabetic models [95]. Additionally, COS supports the viability and differentiation of human adipose-derived mesenchymal stem cells (ADSCs) in hydrogel scaffolds for tissue engineering [96]. These polysaccharides, through their immunomodulatory and growth-promoting properties, offer promising avenues for stem cell-based therapies in diseases involving impaired cellular regeneration.

Evidence summary: angiogenesis

Polysaccharides influence adhesion molecule expression, promoting endothelial cell migration and invasion, which are critical steps in angiogenesis. APS2 polysaccharide enhances endothelial cell migration and vascular repair [98]. Dual-modified glucomannan induces macrophage secretion of nitric oxide (NO), supporting antibacterial activity and a tissue repair environment conducive to angiogenesis [97]. Certain polysaccharides reduce inflammatory responses, creating a microenvironment favorable for angiogenesis. Algae polysaccharides suppress NF-κB activation, lowering pro-inflammatory cytokines, indirectly promoting angiogenesis [91].

The challenges of natural polysaccharides in promoting angiogenesis mainly include issues with bioavailability and stability, inadequate mechanical properties, and incomplete understanding of their mechanisms. The angiogenesis-regulating mechanisms of certain polysaccharides are underexplored, and clinical translation remains difficult. Future developments will focus on enhancing polysaccharide stability through technologies like nanoparticles, developing stimulus-responsive materials, and further exploring structure-activity relationships, especially for less-studied polysaccharide sources such as algae and bacteria. Additionally, designing novel polysaccharide-based hydrogels to better mimic the microenvironment and advancing clinical trials and multifunctional composite materials will position natural polysaccharides as a strategy for angiogenesis therapy [62, 92, 93].

Other mechanisms

Furthermore, the non-scaffold effects of natural polysaccharides extend far beyond macroscopic microenvironment engineering to encompass precise intracellular regulation. These include maintaining cellular homeostasis via robust antioxidant capacity, and ensuring energy supply and cellular viability through modulation of mitochondrial function.

As described in Sect. “Regulation of cell proliferation and differentiation”, polysaccharide formulations (often nanoparticle forms) can alter DNA methyltransferase expression and global methylation in cell models [100, 101]. Furthermore, polysaccharides regulate chromatin structure by influencing histone acetylation or methylation (such as H3K9ac and H3K27me3), promoting the expression of antioxidant genes (such as Nrf2) and anti-inflammatory genes, thereby improving the stem cell microenvironment and supporting organ regeneration [102, 103]. In specific applications, HA enhances the osteogenic differentiation capacity of bone marrow mesenchymal BM-MSCs in bone regeneration through epigenetic modifications, promoting bone matrix deposition [104–106]. Similarly, chondroitin sulfate regulates histone deacetylase (HDACs) activity to inhibit the expression of inflammation-related genes, alleviating neuronal aging in neurodegenerative disease models and promoting neural regeneration [107, 108].

Polysaccharides also interact with long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) or miRNAs involved in epigenetic regulation, further refining the gene expression regulatory network [109–112]. Additionally, polysaccharides indirectly affect epigenetic modifications by modulating oxidative stress and inflammatory microenvironments, enhancing stem cell functions in liver, skin, and cardiovascular tissue regeneration [113, 114].

Natural polysaccharides exhibit significant potential in epigenetic regulation, yet their application faces several challenges [105, 115]. First, their mechanisms of action remain incompletely understood. The specificity of different polysaccharides toward target genes varies, which can lead to inconsistent therapeutic outcomes [116, 117]. Second, issues with bioavailability and in vivo stability may limit their long-term epigenetic effects, especially in complex disease environments [118]. Additionally, the dynamic and reversible nature of epigenetic modifications complicates the prediction of long-term efficacy and raises concerns about off-target effects or unintended changes in gene expression [119, 120]. Finally, clinical translation is hindered by regulatory hurdles, including difficulties in standardizing polysaccharide purity, dosage, and assessing long-term safety [121, 122]. Future efforts should focus on developing more targeted delivery systems, such as functionalized nanocarriers, to achieve precise epigenetic modulation. Deeper mechanistic insights can be gained by integrating single-cell sequencing and epigenomic profiling to elucidate polysaccharide-epigenome interactions. Furthermore, multicenter clinical trials are needed to validate the long-term benefits of polysaccharides in stem cell aging and organ regeneration [121–123]. Combining polysaccharides with gene-editing tools like CRISPR/Cas9 could also enhance the precision and specificity of these interventions, advancing the development of personalized regenerative therapies [124].

In normal cells, natural polysaccharides maintain mitochondrial integrity by preventing morphological alterations, stabilizing mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP), and reducing mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening, which prevents apoptosis and preserves energy metabolism [130]. For example, mycelium polysaccharides restore mitochondrial structure in models of ulcerative colitis [131], while Maca polysaccharides increase F0F1-ATPase activity to enhance ATP production [132]. In contrast, in cancer cells, these polysaccharides promote mitochondrial dysfunction by stimulating mPTP opening, elevating ROS levels, and inducing apoptosis, as observed with acetylated mannan in colorectal cancer cells [133]. Overall, these effects modulate the electron transport chain (ETC) and calcium homeostasis, ensuring mitochondrial function under stress [130].

These mechanisms are influenced by polysaccharide structure, including molecular weight, degree of polymerization, and hydrophilicity, which affect their metabolic processing and efficacy [115]. Polysaccharides activate Nrf2, upregulating antioxidant enzymes such as HO-1 and NQO1, while downregulating Keap1 [116]. LBP, for instance, increase Nrf2 and HO-1 expression to combat oxidative stress [117]. This pathway regulates lipid peroxidation and energy metabolism; HCP reduce lipid peroxidation products, enhancing mitochondrial resilience [118]. Activation protects mitochondria from oxidative damage, as seen with APS attenuating mitochondrial dysfunction [130]. Down-regulation of NF-κB reduces inflammation and protects mitochondrial RNA; Momordica charantia polysaccharides decrease NF-κB activity in gastric ulcer models [119, 120]. In cancer cells, polysaccharides activate caspase cascades, releasing cyto-C and increasing Bax/Bcl-2 ratios, as demonstrated by Ganoderma atrum polysaccharides [121, 122].

Despite promising findings, several challenges hinder the advancement of research on natural polysaccharides for antioxidant and mitochondrial protection. The synthesis of these compounds is constrained by their high molecular weight and complex structures, limiting scalability and standardization [123]. Additionally, the mechanisms of digestion and metabolism, particularly for plant-derived polysaccharides, remain poorly understood, necessitating more in vivo studies to elucidate their bioavailability and pharmacokinetics [124]. Enhancing stability without compromising bioactivity poses another hurdle, as natural polysaccharides are prone to degradation, which affects their potential in functional products [134].

Looking ahead, future research should prioritize comprehensive in vivo investigations to clarify metabolic processes and precise signaling interactions [135]. Targeted studies blocking specific pathways could isolate the effects of individual polysaccharides, addressing the complexity of pathway crosstalk [136]. Moreover, a deeper understanding of the structure-function relationships will facilitate industrialization, enabling the development of novel therapeutic applications, such as targeted drug delivery systems or nutraceuticals for oxidative stress-related diseases.

Applications and challenges

Integration of non-scaffold mechanisms in tissue repair

The therapeutic effects of natural polysaccharides in tissue repair arise from the integration of multiple non-scaffold biological mechanisms. In skin repair, for instance, chitosan contributes to efficient wound healing through a multi-target synergistic mode of action [137]. Preclinical studies indicate that its ability to induce M2 macrophage polarization not only reduces TNF-α levels but also accelerates granulation tissue formation by upregulating PDGF-BB expression [46, 138, 139]. While the liquid absorption capacity of chitosan-based dressings is a well-established material property stemming from the protonation of free amino groups [140-]165], advanced in vitro and animal models demonstrate that the antibacterial efficacy of chitosan-silver ion gels against MRSA relies on a synergistic membrane-disruption effect [166–171]. However, the complexity of the chronic wound microenvironment presents material challenges: 1) Degradation kinetics mismatch between the material (e.g., 3–7 days for standard chitosan) and the prolonged healing cycle of diabetic ulcers (>14 days), which strategies such as glutaraldehyde crosslinking or enzyme-responsive chitosan-polycaprolactone copolymers aim to address by extending functional duration [172–178]. 2) The evolutionary pressure from drug-resistant bacteria, where Pseudomonas aeruginosabiofilms can reduce chitosan’s antibacterial efficiency, necessitating the exploration of combination strategies like bacteriophage-encoded lysins in nanocapsules for a dual “penetrate-lyse” attack [179–185].

In myocardial repair, preclinical evidence suggests the angiogenic capacity of β-glucan originates from Dectin-1 receptor activation, triggering cardiac macrophage reprogramming. This process not only elevates VEGF secretion but also inhibits cardiomyocyte apoptosis via an IL-10-mediated miR-21/PDCD4 pathway [49] 186–188]. A key translational bottleneck remains delivery inefficiency, severely limiting its therapeutic potential and highlighting targeting as a primary challenge [189–194]. Similarly, in neural regeneration, experimental studies show that lentinan activates the SIRT1/PGC-1α pathway, increasing mitochondrial ATP production [195–200]. Its application faces significant central delivery barriers: 1) BBB penetration limits, with free drug brain concentration being extremely low, driving the development of targeted systems like transferrin receptor antibody-conjugated chitosan nanoparticles; and 2) Potential neuroexcitotoxicity at high doses, requiring careful design of smart delivery systems such as MMP-9 enzyme-responsive “silent-activation” platforms [201, 202].

These applications exemplify the integration of the previously discussed non-scaffold mechanisms -including immunomodulation via macrophage polarization, angiogenesis promotion through growth factor modulation, and mitochondrial regulation for energy support. By leveraging these interconnected mechanisms, natural polysaccharides address specific tissue repair needs and create synergistic effects that enhance regenerative outcomes, bridging mechanistic understanding from preclinical models to potential clinical translation.

Pharmacokinetics and safety evaluation of natural polysaccharides

The pharmacokinetic profile of natural polysaccharides in regenerative medicine is influenced by factors such as molecular weight, charge, and chemical modifications, which collectively affect their absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion [203]. High molecular weight polysaccharides often exhibit limited oral bioavailability due to poor gastrointestinal absorption, necessitating the use of alternative delivery routes (e.g., intravenous or local administration) for targeted organ regeneration in both preclinical and clinical settings[204, 205]. Strategies like nanoparticle encapsulation have been shown in experimental models to enhance bioavailability and prolong circulation time, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy in tissue repair [204].

Regarding safety, most natural polysaccharides exhibit good biocompatibility and low immunogenicity at therapeutic doses. Preclinical safety assessments, including cytotoxicity assays, hemocompatibility tests, and animal model evaluations, are standard for monitoring potential adverse effects [141] 206]. For example, polysaccharides like alginate and chitosan have demonstrated a favorable safety profile in clinical studies for wound healing and tissue engineering applications, with minimal adverse effects reported [141] 206]. It is important to note that variability in source material and purification processes can impact batch-to-batch safety and efficacy, highlighting the need for standardized manufacturing and quality control protocols in their therapeutic development.

Future directions

To overcome existing bottlenecks, next-generation technologies may necessitate cross-integration across three major directions: 1) Intelligent precise structural modification: As a speculative research direction, one future avenue is to integrate emerging AI/ML approaches with carbohydrate-specific modeling to inform rational sulfation patterning (for example, targeted 3-O-sulfation in sulfated chitosan). At present, however, such an integrated polysaccharide structure–activity prediction platform is not yet mature: protein predictors (e.g., AlphaFold) are powerful for proteins and protein–ligand complexes, but de-novo, high-confidence prediction of long, flexible carbohydrate polymers and their sulfation effects generally requires carbohydrate builders, force-field based modelling and experimental structural validation. Therefore, any design proposed from in-silico pipelines should be regarded as hypothesis-generating and requires experimental validation (e.g., by NMR, MS and functional assays) [142–146]. 2) Upgrading the clinical adaptability of intelligent delivery systems: This conceptual framework includes: for myocardial infarction, ROS/pH dual-responsive hydrogel loaded with β-glucan, with triggered release in the acidic environment of the infarct area, while simultaneously clearing excess ROS through built-in MnO2 nanozyme [147, 148]; in stroke treatment, magnetic field-guided Fe3O4-lentinan complex,intended to cross the blood-brain barrier, with an external alternating magnetic field to enhance accumulation in the brain [149, 150]. 3) Paradigm innovation in combination therapy: Stem cell enhancement function, with chitosan-modified mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) overexpressing CXCR4, combined with HIF-1α gene editing to augment angiogenesis capability [151]; As a conceptual framework for CRISPR-polysaccharide synergy, with chitosan-encapsulated SaCas9/sgRNA complex for targeted knockout of the TGF-β1 gene in the infarct area, synchronous release of β-glucan to promote vascular reconstruction, achieving “microenvironment remodeling - regeneration promotion” dual-module therapy [152]. These strategies remain largely conceptual or in early exploratory stages and require extensive further validation.

Clinical translation and regulatory considerations

Translation of polysaccharide-based therapies to first-in-human (FIH) trials requires a coordinated data package covering quality (CMC), nonclinical safety, pharmacology, and immunogenicity, and sponsors should follow relevant ICH/EMA/FDA guidance when planning studies.Chemistry, Manufacturing and Controls (CMC). A cGMP manufacturing process with clear raw-material specifications, validated purification steps and defined release tests (identity, purity, potency, endotoxin and microbial limits) is essential for an IND/CTA submission. A validated quantitative potency (bio)assay and an internal reference standard for lot-to-lot comparison are recommended and will typically be requested by regulators.Non-clinical safety and toxicology. Sponsors should conduct risk-based GLP single- and repeat-dose toxicology studies in appropriate species in accordance with ICH M3(R2) and related guidance, and include safety-pharmacology endpoints as indicated by the route and exposure. Biodistribution and PK/ADME studies (radiolabeled or otherwise traceable material) are important for interpreting toxicokinetic margins and selecting safe starting doses.Immunogenicity and immunotoxicity. Preclinical immunogenicity screening (anti-drug antibody assays, neutralization testing), human whole-blood cytokine assays and complement activation studies are recommended to identify immune-activation risks and to inform clinical monitoring plans.Impurities, viral and microbial safety. Early consultation with regulators (e.g., pre-IND or scientific advice meetings with FDA/EMA or national authorities) is strongly encouraged to agree on product classification (drug/biologic/device/combination), nonclinical requirements and CMC expectations.

Conclusion

Natural polysaccharides represent a promising class of bioactive materials in regenerative medicine, particularly through their non-scaffold effects that actively modulate organ regeneration. In this review, we have systematically elucidated the multifaceted mechanisms underlying these effects, including immunomodulation and anti-inflammatory actions via macrophage polarization, cytokine network balancing, and complement system regulation; regulation of cell proliferation and differentiation through epigenetic modifications and signaling pathways; promotion of angiogenesis by stabilizing growth factors and activating endothelial pathways; antioxidant properties and mitochondrial protection to mitigate oxidative stress; ECM remodeling for balanced matrix deposition and fibrosis inhibition; microbiome regulation as prebiotics to enhance mucosal repair and immune homeostasis; and epigenetic modulation to delay stem cell aging and optimize gene expression. These mechanisms highlight the transition of natural polysaccharides from mere structural supports to dynamic orchestrators of healing, with applications spanning skin, heart, nervous system, liver, gut, and beyond. By mimicking endogenous repair processes, they offer advantages such as biocompatibility, low toxicity, and multi-target synergy, potentially circumventing limitations of traditional therapies like organ transplantation.

Despite these advances, challenges persist, including incomplete understanding of structure-activity relationships, bioavailability issues, long-term safety concerns, and practical barriers to clinical translation (manufacturing, impurity control, immunogenicity risk and bioassay standardization), limitations and regulatory considerations are discussed in Sect. “Limitations”–“Clinical translation and regulatory considerations”. Future directions should emphasize technological innovations, such as integrating omics technologies with AI-driven design for rational polysaccharide engineering; developing advanced delivery systems like targeted nanoparticles; and fostering interdisciplinary collaborations for scalable biosynthesis and personalized therapies. Large-scale clinical trials will be essential to validate efficacy and safety, paving the way for natural polysaccharides to become integral components in regenerative strategies for organ failure and tissue damage. Ultimately, harnessing these non-scaffold effects has the potential to advance regenerative medicine, but realization of this promise will depend on rigorous mechanistic studies, standardization of polysaccharide preparations, and well-designed clinical trials to demonstrate safety and efficacy.
